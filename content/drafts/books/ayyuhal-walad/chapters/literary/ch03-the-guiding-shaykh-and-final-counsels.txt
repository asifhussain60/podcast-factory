# The Guiding Shaykh and the Final Counsels

## Section 19 — The Role of the Guiding Shaykh

The traveler on this path cannot navigate it alone. I say this not as a counsel of humility, though humility is required. I say it as a structural observation about what the path actually is.

The path runs through the self — through the dense and resistant territory of your own character, with its ingrained habits and defensive self-justifications and the surprisingly resilient capacity of the soul to arrange your understanding of things so that your existing preferences are preserved. A guide is needed not because you are incapable, but because you cannot see your own blind spots from inside them. The shaykh who is fitted for this role is the one whose training in his own character has progressed far enough that he can recognize in another what he has already worked through in himself — like a skilled farmer who knows exactly where the thorns are rooted and how deeply, not from theory but from long seasons of clearing.

But I want to be precise about what qualifies such a shaykh, because the title has been claimed by people who have not earned it.

He is one who has removed himself from love of the world and love of rank. He has traced his formation to a discerning teacher who traced his own formation back toward the Prophet. He has disciplined himself in scarcity — less food, less sleep, less speech — and in abundance of prayer and charity and fasting. He has cultivated the qualities that make a person genuinely reliable to be around: endurance, gratitude, trust in God, generosity, contentment, tranquility of soul, humility, truthfulness, modesty, gravity, quietness. Not one or two of these in the way that people develop reputation for a virtue — but the whole constellation of them, worked into daily habit.

Such a person is rare. More precious than red sulphur, which is to say: you should not expect to find one easily, and when you do, you should not take it lightly.

If you are fortunate enough to find a shaykh of this description, and he accepts you, honor him outwardly and inwardly — and the difference between these matters enormously.

Outward honor means not disputing with him in argument, not placing your prayer carpet before his except at the time of prayer, not multiplying supererogatory practices in his presence, and doing what he commands within your capacity.

Inner honor means not denying in your heart what you accept from him outwardly. Hypocrisy is born from the gap between the two — from performing an outer compliance while the inner life continues unchanged. If inner agreement is not possible, it is better to leave his companionship until it is, rather than to maintain the performance.

Being a Sufi has two characteristics: uprightness with God, and good conduct with people. Uprightness means offering the pleasures of your soul as ransom for your soul's sake. Good conduct means not burdening people according to your own preferences, but bearing what their needs require of you — provided this does not violate the sacred law.

Devotion comprises three things: careful observance of the commands of the sacred law; satisfaction with God's decree and fate and apportionment; and abandoning your own preferences in order to seek His pleasure.

Trust is fortifying your belief in what God has promised. What He has fated for you will reach you without fail, though all the world attempted to prevent it. What is not written for you, you will not attain, though all the world tried to help you reach it. This is not fatalism. It is the particular freedom of a person who has stopped trying to manage what cannot be managed, and can therefore direct all their energy toward what actually calls for it.

Sincerity is that all your works be done for God — your heart neither resting content at people's praise nor despairing at their censure. Hypocrisy is born from valuing people's opinion as if it had power over your condition. The cure is to see clearly that people are moving under God's decree as completely as any object is moved by its nature — that they are, in the final analysis, unable to grant you what you need or withhold it. When you genuinely see this, hypocrisy loses its appeal, because you no longer need what hypocrisy was trying to secure.

## Section 20 — The Unwritten Knowledge

The remainder of your questions — some are covered in my writings. Go there and look.

But setting down others in writing is forbidden. Not forbidden in the sense that they are being withheld from you as a punishment or a test. Forbidden in the sense that writing them would not transmit them. The medium is wrong for the content. These things are known by arriving at them, not by reading about them.

Work by what you already know. What you do not yet know will be revealed to you as you work. This is the only sequence that functions. The revelation does not precede the practice; it follows from it.

## Section 21 — The Tongue of the Heart and Patience

After today, ask me what is obscure by the tongue of the heart rather than the tongue of the mouth.

God said: *Had they waited patiently until you came forth to them, it had been better for them.* Patience here is not passive endurance — the gritted teeth of someone waiting out an unpleasant situation. It is the active orientation of a person who understands that the time of revelation has its own structure, and that trying to force an early arrival distorts rather than accelerates.

Khidr said to Moses, when Moses asked to follow him: *Do not ask me about anything until I mention it unto you.* Moses, who had come with urgent questions, was asked to wait until the answers were ripe. And every time Moses could not contain himself — when the boat was damaged, when the boy was killed, when the wall was built without payment — Khidr said: *Did I not tell you that you could not bear with me patiently?* Three times. Three failures of patience. And only after agreeing to part did Khidr explain, and the explanation made the apparent contradictions coherent.

You will not reach the understanding you are after by rushing toward it. The signs will be shown when you have traveled far enough to read them. *Have they not traveled through the earth, and seen?* Traveling is not metaphorical here. It is the actual motion of a life being lived in accordance with what you know, which creates the conditions in which more can be known.

## Section 22 — The Sacrifice of the Spirit

If you travel, you will see wonders at every station.

Dhu al-Nun al-Misri said to one of his students: *If you are able to give your spirit without reserve, come. If not, do not busy yourself with the idle practices of mysticism.*

The abruptness of this counsel is its precision. It does not say: come, and we will determine together how much you can give. It does not say: start where you are and we will adjust the demands to your capacity. It says: the core of this matter is in applying your spirit abundantly. If you cannot do that, the rest is decoration — a vocabulary of spiritual experience attached to a life that has not actually been given over.

I am aware this is demanding. I am not softening it.

## Section 23 — Eight Admonitions: Four to Avoid, Four to Do

I want to leave you with eight specific counsels. Four to avoid. Four to practice. Receive them from me lest your knowledge become your adversary at the final reckoning.

Of what to avoid, the first is argument.

Do not argue with anyone about anything, as far as you are able. The mischief in argument is great, and its evil almost always exceeds its benefit, because argument is the soil in which hypocrisy and envy and pride and malice and enmity and boasting grow most easily. These qualities are not incidental byproducts of argument. They are what argument, in most of its forms, is actually doing — competing, establishing hierarchy, managing how one is seen.

There is an exception. If a question arises and your genuine purpose is that truth should appear and not be lost — not that you should appear and not be diminished — then discussion is permissible. Two signs will tell you which it is. First: it makes no difference to you whether the truth is stated by your tongue or by the tongue of your interlocutor. Second: you prefer to have the discussion in private rather than in public. If those two conditions are present, proceed. If they are not, be silent.

Now here is a distinction that requires care. Disputing is different from answering questions asked in genuine need. The diseases of the heart that bring people to ask questions vary considerably. Some questions arise from envy and hatred — and when you answer such a question with the clearest possible answer, the only result is increased rage. You cannot cure this. Leave that person with their disease. The Quran says: *Withdraw from whoever turns away from Our remembrance and desires nothing except the present life.* Envy, the Prophet said, eats up good deeds the way fire eats wood. Do not add your energy to someone else's fire.

Some questions arise from a particular kind of stupidity — the person who has learned a little and thinks that what is obscure to him must also be obscure to the most learned. He is not asking out of need; he is performing the asking. You cannot cure this either.

Some questions come from a person genuinely seeking but constitutionally unable to receive what they are asking for — dull in understanding, unable to arrive at the truth of things even when it is placed in front of them. The Prophet said: *We, the company of prophets, have been commanded to speak to people according to their understanding.* Speaking above someone's understanding is not generosity. It is a form of carelessness.

But there is a fourth kind — the intelligent person, free of envy and anger and the love of position, seeking the straight road. His questions arise from need. He can receive what is given. This is the person you answer. Not merely answer — you are obligated to answer. His question is a disease presented to a physician, and withholding the medicine would be a betrayal of the physician's role.

The second thing to avoid is becoming a preacher and warner. The mischief in this is great.

## Section 24 — The Dangers of Preaching and Worldly Association

Two characteristics undermine preaching and must be avoided.

The first is affectation in speech — the elaborate expressions, the allusions, the exaggerations, the performed eloquence. God is not drawn to those who affect. And the person who affects has already revealed something about their inner state: they have organized their speaking around the impression it creates rather than the truth it carries.

What warning and admonition actually are — what they are *for* — is this: the worshipper remembers the fire of the hereafter. He remembers his own soul's shortcomings in the service of his Creator. He reflects on the past life that has been wasted in what did not concern him. He contemplates what lies ahead: the uncertainty of whether his faith will be intact at the moment of death, the manner of his encounter with the angel of death, whether he will be able to answer what he is asked in the grave, what his condition will be on the Day of Resurrection, whether he will cross safely or fall. These thoughts continue in his heart, disturbing his complacency. The heat of these interior fires, the grief of these contemplated losses — this is called warning. Communicating these realities to others in such a way that the heat touches the people in the gathering, so that they regret time spent in other than God's obedience — this is called admonition.

Think of what it would mean if you saw a flood approaching someone's house while they and their family were inside. Would you stop to craft your warning with elegant expressions and clever allusions? Would your heart desire to deliver the news with affected rhetoric? No. You would say: *Beware! Flee! The flood is here!* The urgency of the reality would strip your speech of everything that was not the warning itself.

The preacher who has forgotten this has forgotten what they are for.

The second characteristic to avoid in preaching is making the measure of your success the agitation of your audience — their ecstasy, their torn garments, their visible emotion. The moment you begin to aim for that response, you have shifted from serving the truth to managing an effect. The goal is not that people should feel something in your presence. The goal is that the realities of the hereafter should touch them enough that they change how they live.

These are the final counsels.

I have written what can be written. Work by it. What cannot be written will come to you in the working.
