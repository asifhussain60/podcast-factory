# Commuter Benefits and Dependent Care FSA: The Benefits You're Probably Overlooking

When people talk about tax-advantaged benefits, the conversation almost always starts — and often ends — with HSAs and FSAs. Those are the flagship accounts, the ones that feature prominently in open enrollment presentations and benefits fairs. But sitting quietly alongside them, often buried in the fine print of a benefits package, are two tools that address two of the largest recurring costs in many Americans' lives: getting to work every day, and paying for someone to care for your children or aging parents while you are there.

Commuter benefits and the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account — the DCFSA — are underutilized, underappreciated, and genuinely valuable. Understanding them is not complicated. Using them is even simpler. And the savings they produce, year after year, are real.

---

## Commuter Benefits: Turning Your Commute into Tax Savings

There is something deeply ordinary about the daily commute. You take the subway, or the bus, or park your car in a garage, and the money flows out of your account without a second thought. For many urban and suburban workers, commuting costs are a significant monthly expense — $150 in transit passes, $200 in monthly parking, or both. Across a year, that can easily reach $3,000 to $5,000. And almost none of it, under normal circumstances, receives any tax treatment at all.

Commuter benefits change that. Offered through your employer, commuter benefits allow you to set aside pre-tax dollars from your paycheck — each month, before income taxes are calculated — to pay for eligible transit and parking costs. The pre-tax deduction reduces your taxable income, and you spend the money on the same commuting expenses you were already paying. The only difference is that you pay less tax.

The eligible expenses for a transit account include train, subway, bus, ferry, and vanpool costs. The eligible expenses for a parking account include parking at or near your workplace, or at a facility from which you commute to work by mass transit. These are the most common daily transportation costs for working Americans, and both categories are covered.

For 2026, the IRS monthly limit for both transit and parking is $340 per month, per category. Members who use both transit and parking can contribute the combined maximum — up to $680 per month total. At $340 per month for transit alone, the annual contribution potential is $4,080. At a 30 percent effective tax rate, that represents more than $1,200 in annual tax savings. For workers with both transit and parking costs, that savings figure can reach $2,000 or more per year — simply by redirecting money they were already spending through a pre-tax account.

HealthEquity's commuter benefits platform offers several convenient ways to use the funds. You can arrange direct payments to your transit provider or parking garage, eliminating the need to track and submit receipts. You can have monthly transit passes or tickets mailed to your home address. You can submit requests for reimbursement — called "Pay Me Back" — for parking or transit expenses you have already paid out of pocket. Or you can use the HealthEquity Commuter debit card, loading a chosen amount each month and using the card directly to pay for transit fares and parking.

One of the most attractive features of commuter benefits — in contrast to the healthcare FSA — is flexibility. You can enroll at any time, without waiting for open enrollment. You can pause your contributions, change your election amount, or cancel entirely whenever your circumstances change. If you switch from taking the subway to working from home, you can stop contributions immediately. If your commuting costs change, you adjust your election to match. There is no open enrollment window to wait for, no annual planning commitment required. The account flexes with your life.

Unused commuter funds also do not automatically expire at year end the way FSA funds do — as long as you remain employed, your balance carries forward. This makes the commuter benefit particularly low-risk: there is no penalty for estimating your contributions conservatively.

HealthEquity's own illustration puts the savings in concrete terms: a member spending $150 per month on transit and $125 per month on parking — $3,300 per year total — saves $990 per year in taxes at a 30 percent effective rate. That is nearly $1,000 per year in tax savings on money that was already going to leave your pocket. You are not taking on any additional financial commitment. You are simply reorganizing spending you were already doing.

## Dependent Care FSA: Making Childcare and Eldercare More Affordable

The cost of caring for dependents — children, disabled spouses, or aging parents who require day care while you work — is one of the heaviest financial burdens facing working families in the United States. Full-time daycare for an infant can cost $15,000 to $25,000 per year in major metropolitan areas. After-school programs, summer camps, elder care facilities: these costs add up with relentless consistency, and they are paid entirely with after-tax dollars in the absence of a Dependent Care FSA.

The DCFSA is the solution HealthEquity offers for this specific category of expense. Like the healthcare FSA, it is an employer-sponsored account funded with pre-tax payroll deductions. But where the healthcare FSA covers medical costs, the DCFSA covers the cost of caring for qualifying dependents — so that you can go to work.

The IRS definition of a qualifying dependent for DCFSA purposes covers three categories: a child under age 13, a disabled spouse, and an older parent in eldercare. The defining criterion across all three is that the care must be provided during your working hours — the purpose of the DCFSA is specifically to enable you to work, not to subsidize care provided at other times. A parent in a full-time residential facility whose care costs are unrelated to your work schedule would not qualify; a parent in a daytime eldercare program while you are at the office typically would.

Eligible expenses include daycare centers, preschools, babysitters, nannies, summer day camps (for children under 13), and before- and after-school care programs. Eligible expenses do not include overnight summer camps, private school tuition, or expenses for children who have aged out of the under-13 eligibility window.

The contribution limit for DCFSAs in 2026 is $7,500 for individuals and for married couples filing jointly — a significant increase from the 2025 limit of $5,000, driven by the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which raised DCFSA limits to help families keep pace with rising care costs. For married couples filing separately, the limit is $2,500 per spouse.

At $7,500 in annual DCFSA contributions and a 30 percent effective tax rate, the annual tax savings are $2,250. For a family spending $30,000 per year on childcare — not an unusual figure in high-cost cities — $7,500 of that through the DCFSA means $2,250 is effectively returned to them through tax savings. It does not solve the full cost of childcare in the United States. But it meaningfully reduces it.

HealthEquity's DCFSA illustration is instructive: a member spending $300 per month on daycare, $500 on summer camp, and $50 per month on an after-school program — $4,500 in total annual spending — saves $1,350 per year in taxes. That is $1,350 that could go toward next year's camp, toward savings, toward anything. The tax savings are not abstract. They are dollars.

The rules of the DCFSA are similar to the healthcare FSA in key respects. Elections are made at open enrollment and are generally locked in for the plan year, with changes permitted only for qualifying life events such as a change in employment status, a change in marital status, a change in number of dependents, or a daycare provider closure. The DCFSA is also subject to use-it-or-lose-it rules — unused funds are forfeited to the employer at the end of the plan year, though some employers offer a grace period extension. Unlike the healthcare FSA's advance funding feature, DCFSA funds are generally available only as payroll contributions accumulate, not in full at the start of the year.

The DCFSA does not impact HSA eligibility, which means members can hold both simultaneously — using the HSA for medical costs and the DCFSA for dependent care costs. It can also be combined with a standard healthcare FSA for comprehensive coverage of both medical and caregiving expenses.

Taken together, commuter benefits and the DCFSA represent potentially thousands of dollars per year in tax savings for working Americans who look past the headline HSA and FSA accounts to the full range of benefits available to them.
