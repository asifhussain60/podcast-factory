# What is HealthEquity?

Think about the last time a medical bill caught you off guard. Maybe it was an explanation of benefits that arrived in the mail a month after your doctor's visit, with a number in the "amount you owe" column that didn't match what you'd expected. Or maybe it was a pharmacy run where the total came up and you did a quiet mental calculation — do I have enough in checking to cover this, or does something else slip this week? Or maybe it was the moment you actually looked at your pay stub and noticed what your health insurance premium costs every month, and realized you had no idea whether you were spending that money well or just spending it.

You are not alone in that feeling. Millions of Americans carry financial anxiety into healthcare spaces — into pharmacies, into waiting rooms, into the back-and-forth of insurance paperwork — and most of them have never had a single conversation about the tools that exist to make this less painful. The tools are real. They are built into your employer's benefits package. And for most people, they are almost entirely unused.

HealthEquity was built to change that. The company was founded in 2002 in Draper, Utah, by a physician named Stephen Neeleman who had spent years watching patients make healthcare decisions based on what they could afford rather than what they needed. His conviction — the one that drove him to spend years building a company around it — was that connecting health and wealth could transform ordinary people's lives. Not wealthy people. Ordinary people: the ones who work, who pay into health insurance every month, and who often have no idea that the benefits system includes powerful savings tools they've never activated.

HealthEquity's mission, stated plainly on everything the company publishes, is to save and improve lives by empowering healthcare consumers. That sentence is worth sitting with. Not "administer benefits efficiently." Not "deliver compliance solutions." Save and improve lives. It is the organizing principle behind every product the company offers and every decision it makes — and when you understand what the products actually do, the claim turns out to be accurate.

---

The turning point in HealthEquity's history came in 2003, when Congress created the Health Savings Account as part of a larger Medicare reform law. For the first time, Americans enrolled in a high-deductible health plan could open a dedicated account to save money specifically for healthcare costs — and that money would never be taxed going in, never taxed while it grew, and never taxed when you spent it on healthcare. It was a policy breakthrough disguised as a technical benefits rule change, and HealthEquity was positioned from day one to be the custodian of those accounts.

In 2006, the U.S. Treasury granted HealthEquity a Non-Bank Trustee license — a designation that meant the company could hold HSA assets directly, with a level of accountability and specialization that most banks don't match. By its tenth anniversary in 2012, HealthEquity held $1 billion in managed HSA assets. It had moved from early adopter territory to national institution.

In 2014, the company went public on the NASDAQ under the ticker HQY. Then came the moment that made HealthEquity the company it is today: in 2019, HealthEquity acquired WageWorks, one of the country's largest administrators of FSAs, HRAs, COBRA, and commuter benefits. That acquisition wasn't just a growth move. It was a strategic transformation. HealthEquity went from being the best HSA custodian in the country to being the only company that could offer every major consumer health benefit — HSA, FSA, HRA, COBRA, commuter, and Dependent Care FSA — through a single integrated platform.

The result is what HealthEquity calls a Total Solution: one login, one debit card, one member services number, one complete picture of all your health-related accounts in one place. For you as an employee, that means you don't have to juggle three different apps or call two different companies to figure out where your money is.

---

Today, HealthEquity administers benefits for more than 17 million accounts across the country, working with over 120,000 organizations — from small businesses to Fortune 500 companies. In fiscal year 2025, the company held $32.1 billion in total HSA assets across 9.9 million accounts. That 9.9 million figure grew 14 percent in a single year, which tells you something about how fast awareness of these tools is spreading.

But numbers don't fully capture what this means. Those 17 million accounts belong to people — individuals and families — who have access to a smarter, more financially efficient way to pay for their healthcare. The accounts don't reduce the cost of a prescription or a doctor's visit at the source. What they do is change who pays the taxes on the money you use for healthcare costs. The answer, when you use these tools correctly, is: nobody. You keep the tax money. You use it for the same healthcare spending you were going to do anyway, and you just don't give the government a cut of it.

HealthEquity's audacious goal is to make Health Savings Accounts more common than 401(k)s by 2030. Right now, about 72 million Americans have a 401(k). The company's bet is that once people truly understand how the HSA works — not just as a spending account, but as a triple-tax-advantaged (meaning you never pay income tax on the money going in, while it grows, or when you spend it on healthcare) savings and investment vehicle — the demand will be undeniable. The HSA is, by many measures, the most tax-efficient financial account available to working Americans today. More on that in the next chapter.

The company's culture is organized around what they call "purple" — not just a brand color, but a commitment to genuinely remarkable service. That commitment shows up in places like the launch of Purple with Purpose, a community-giving initiative where full-time team members get two paid days off each year to volunteer, and where HealthEquity's Community Foundation supports organizations focused on financial literacy, community wellness, disaster relief, and basic human needs. It also shows up in the 2020 commitment to keep all member services staff based in the United States — so when you call with a question about your account during a stressful moment, you talk to someone trained to explain, not just to process.

---

The chapters that follow are each about a specific product in HealthEquity's portfolio: the Health Savings Account, the Flexible Spending Account, the Health Reimbursement Arrangement, COBRA coverage, commuter benefits, the Dependent Care FSA, and finally a look at what all of these tools can mean together across a working life.

Understanding any one of them in isolation is useful. Understanding how they fit together — and why the decisions you make at your next benefits sign-up season could echo financially for decades — is the point of this series.

You don't need to be a financial expert to use these tools well. You need about twenty minutes of careful attention during benefits sign-up season, a basic sense of what your healthcare costs look like in a typical year, and the understanding that the system has been designed, specifically and intentionally, to let you keep more of your money if you know where to look.

That's what HealthEquity was built to make possible. That's what these chapters are here to explain.
