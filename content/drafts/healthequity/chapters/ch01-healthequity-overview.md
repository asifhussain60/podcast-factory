# What is HealthEquity?

Every year, millions of Americans sit across from a doctor — and the weight they carry into that room is not only about their health. It is about money. It is the fear of a bill they cannot pay, a procedure they will delay, a prescription they will split in half because the full dose costs too much. The gap between the care people need and the care they can actually afford has been widening for decades, and for a long time, there was no dedicated institution built specifically to close it.

HealthEquity was built to close that gap.

Founded in 2002 and headquartered in Draper, Utah, HealthEquity is the nation's leading non-bank Health Savings Account custodian and consumer-directed benefits administrator. But that description undersells what the company actually does. HealthEquity's mission, stated plainly and returned to constantly across everything the organization publishes, is to save and improve lives by empowering healthcare consumers. Those are not words chosen for a marketing brochure. They are the organizing principle behind every product, every partnership, and every employee who answers a member's call.

The origin story begins not in a boardroom but in a doctor's office. Founder Stephen D. Neeleman, MD, is a physician who spent the mid-1990s watching patients navigate a system that felt designed to confuse and exhaust them. In 1996, when HIPAA passed and began paving the way for wider use of medical savings accounts, Neeleman saw an opening. He believed deeply that connecting health and wealth — allowing ordinary Americans to save money specifically for healthcare, the way they save for retirement — could transform people's lives. He spent years developing that conviction before assembling a startup team in Draper in 2002.

The turning point came in 2003. President George W. Bush signed the Medicare Modernization Act into law, and buried within that legislation was the creation of the Health Savings Account — the HSA. For the first time, Americans enrolled in high-deductible health plans could open a dedicated, tax-advantaged account to save for medical expenses. The HSA was not just a product feature. It was a policy breakthrough. HealthEquity was positioned from the very beginning to be the custodian of choice for these accounts, and the company onboarded its first client and first health plan partner that same year.

Growth came steadily, then rapidly. In 2006, the U.S. Treasury granted HealthEquity a Non-Bank Trustee license — a significant designation that meant the company could take direct custody of HSA assets, a distinction most banks and financial institutions did not hold in the same way. By 2011, a series of strategic acquisitions had brought HealthEquity's managed HSA assets to $415 million. By 2012 — its tenth anniversary — the company reached $1 billion in managed HSA assets. It was a milestone that signaled HealthEquity was no longer a niche custodian serving early adopters. It was becoming a national institution.

In 2014, HealthEquity went public on the NASDAQ, beginning to trade under the ticker HQY. The IPO marked a maturation point — outside capital now available to accelerate growth, a public accountability structure in place, and a broader profile in the financial services world. The company joined the NASDAQ Financial 100 Index and the S&P SmallCap 600 Index the following year.

The most transformative moment in HealthEquity's history came in 2019, when the company acquired WageWorks — one of the country's largest administrators of consumer-directed benefits including FSAs, HRAs, COBRA, and commuter benefits. The acquisition was not merely an expansion of headcount. It was a strategic merger that gave HealthEquity the ability to offer what it calls a Total Solution: a fully integrated suite of healthcare and financial benefits, administered through a single platform, serving both employers and individual members. Where the company had once been the best HSA custodian, it became the most comprehensive consumer health benefits company in the United States.

Today, HealthEquity and its subsidiaries administer benefits for more than 17 million accounts across the country, in partnership with over 120,000 organizations — from small businesses to Fortune 500 corporations. It serves thousands of registered benefits advisors and maintains integrations with health plans, retirement providers, and third-party administrators. In fiscal year 2025, the company reported revenue of $1.2 billion, up 20 percent year over year, and held $32.1 billion in total HSA assets. The number of HSA accounts alone reached 9.9 million, a 14 percent increase in a single year.

Those numbers represent something more concrete than financial performance. They represent 17 million people — individuals and families — who have access to a smarter, more tax-efficient way to pay for the healthcare they need.

The culture HealthEquity has built around this mission is captured in the color purple. Purple is not just HealthEquity's brand color. The company describes it as a way of life — a commitment to remarkable service, to genuine care, to going beyond what is expected. This culture is visible in the 2015 launch of Purple with Purpose, a community-giving initiative that supports organizations focused on disaster relief, medical research, community wellness, financial literacy, and advocacy for consumer-driven healthcare. Full-time team members receive two additional paid days off to volunteer, and since the program launched, employees have collectively contributed over 5,800 hours of community service. The HealthEquity Community Foundation, an extension of this effort, supports 501(c)(3) organizations whose work addresses basic human needs — safety, housing, food security — in communities across the country.

HealthEquity's ambition for the future is audacious but specific. By 2030, the company plans to make Health Savings Accounts more common than 401(k)s. In 2026, approximately 72 million Americans have a 401(k). The vision is that within a few years, even more Americans will have an HSA — not because they were required to, but because they understand how powerful the tool is, and because HealthEquity made the experience simple enough that choosing one became obvious.

That vision depends on education. It depends on employers offering the right benefit packages. It depends on health plans partnering with platforms that make the member experience seamless. And it depends on a company willing to make the argument, loudly and consistently, that the connection between financial health and physical health is not abstract — it is the difference between a person getting a mammogram or postponing it, between filling a prescription or halving the dose, between a retirement that includes adequate healthcare funding or one that is derailed by unexpected medical costs.

What follows in the chapters ahead is a detailed look at each product in HealthEquity's portfolio. But understanding why these products matter — understanding the urgency behind the mission — begins here, with the story of a physician in 1996 who believed that the American healthcare financing system could be remade, one savings account at a time.
