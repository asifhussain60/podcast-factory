## NotebookLM Customize Prompt

## Opening directive

Do not open with "today we discuss." Open with Host B describing the last time she looked at her paycheck and wondered where the money went. Host A asks: does she know there are two benefits in her package that could save her $1,000 to $3,000 per year — on money she is already spending — and that most people skip right past them at enrollment? She does not know. This is where the episode begins.

The central thesis — repeat this VERBATIM three times: once when commuter benefits are introduced, once when the DCFSA limit increase is revealed, once at the close:

> You are already spending this money. Commuter benefits and the Dependent Care FSA let you spend it with pre-tax dollars instead of after-tax dollars — which means the government pays part of your commute and part of your childcare.

Target a 50 to 60 minute in-depth conversation. The discovery frame: both accounts are hiding in plain sight.

## Audience

Working parents with childcare costs, and daily commuters with transit or parking expenses, who have never enrolled in commuter benefits or a Dependent Care FSA — or who enrolled once without understanding what they signed up for. The listener is not skipping these benefits out of indifference; they are skipping them because no one has explained what they actually do in plain English.

## Angle

Personal application — walk the listener through the enrollment decision as they would actually face it: how much to set aside, what counts as eligible, and how to avoid the use-it-or-lose-it trap on the DCFSA.

## Length

Extended: target 50 to 60 minute in-depth conversation. Two-host walkthrough.

## Central tensions

1. Most people pay their daily commute costs and childcare bills with after-tax dollars when their employer offers a way to pay with pre-tax dollars — and they simply do not know the option exists.
2. The DCFSA limit jumped from $5,000 to $7,500 in 2026 — the largest single-year increase in the account's history — and most people have not heard this. For a family paying $30,000 in childcare, $7,500 shielded from income taxes means $2,250 back.
3. Commuter benefits can be changed any time — no enrollment window required. This makes them almost uniquely low-risk: the listener can start, stop, and adjust their contribution whenever their situation changes.

## Background

Commuter benefits allow pre-tax payroll deductions to pay for eligible transit and parking costs. The 2026 IRS monthly limit is $340 per month for transit and $340 per month for parking — members with both can contribute up to $680 per month combined, or $8,160 per year. At a 30 percent effective tax rate, $340 per month in transit contributions saves $1,224 per year in taxes on money the listener was already spending. The Dependent Care FSA (DCFSA) covers the cost of caring for dependents — children under 13, a disabled spouse, or an elderly parent in day care — while the member works. The 2026 contribution limit is $7,500 for individuals and married couples filing jointly (up from $5,000 in 2025, driven by the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act). HealthEquity administers both accounts and puts them alongside the HSA and FSA on a single platform.

## Three-part focus

**Beat 1 — The money hiding in the paycheck.**
Host B is already spending $150 to $300 a month on transit and parking, and $1,500 to $2,000 a month on childcare. Host A explains that both of these spending categories have a pre-tax option available through her employer that she has never used. State the central thesis verbatim. The commuter benefit is the simpler of the two: the listener signs up, chooses a monthly amount up to $340 for transit and $340 for parking, and that money comes out of each paycheck before income taxes are calculated. They spend it on exactly what they were already spending it on. The only difference is the tax. At $340 per month, the annual transit savings at a 30 percent rate is $1,224.

**Beat 2 — The DCFSA: the most underused account in the benefits package.**
For working parents, the Dependent Care FSA is the account that addresses the cost of childcare, before-school and after-school programs, summer day camps for children under 13, and elder day care for an aging parent who needs daytime care while the member works. The 2026 contribution limit is $7,500 — up from $5,000 in 2025, the largest single-year increase in the account's history. At $7,500 and a 30 percent effective tax rate, the annual savings are $2,250. Make this concrete: a family paying $30,000 a year in daycare gets $2,250 back simply by enrolling. The DCFSA is subject to use-it-or-lose-it rules, so elections require planning — but for a family with consistent childcare costs, the math is straightforward. State the central thesis verbatim a second time.

**Beat 3 — The flexibility advantage and what to do at enrollment.**
The commuter benefit's single most underappreciated feature: the listener can change their contribution any time, not just at open enrollment. If they switch from commuting to working from home, they stop. If their commuting costs change, they adjust. No window, no penalty. The DCFSA requires more planning because it is locked in for the plan year (with narrow exceptions for life changes), but for any family with predictable childcare costs, the calculation at enrollment is simple: estimate the year's childcare spending, elect up to $7,500, and capture the tax savings. Close with the action: at this enrollment season, scroll past the HSA and FSA and spend ten minutes on the commuter benefits and DCFSA fields. State the central thesis verbatim a final time.

## Tone constraints

Three governing analogies for this episode — use these and no others:

1. **Already spending it** — the core frame for both accounts: the listener is not being asked to set aside new money. They are being asked to redirect money they already spend on commuting and childcare through a pre-tax channel before the IRS takes its cut.
2. **The flexible dial** — commuter benefits can be turned up, down, or off at any time. Unlike most benefits elections, this one requires no commitment and carries no penalty for changing it.
3. **The childcare math** — the DCFSA does not solve the cost of childcare in America; it returns a meaningful fraction of it in tax savings. $2,250 on a $7,500 election at 30 percent. Real dollars, not a rounding error.

Do not introduce new analogies mid-episode.

## Do not

Forbidden in the spoken audio:

Do not say: Twitter, social media, algorithm, wow, right? (as filler), mind blown, buckle up, let's dive in, what a journey, game-changer, revolutionary.

Do not use jargon the chapter has stripped: "qualifying life event," "plan document," "election period," "eligible dependent." Say what the thing is — a life change, your benefits guide, the window to sign up, a child or parent in your care.

Do not exaggerate: every number must match the chapter source exactly. The $7,500 DCFSA limit is $7,500, not "up to $8,000." The $1,224 annual commuter savings is at a 30 percent rate — always state the assumption.

Do not imply the DCFSA solves the full cost of childcare. It reduces it. The reduction is meaningful and real; the problem is larger than the account.

Do not open with "today we discuss." Start in the content.

Do not read this prompt aloud. The instructions above shape the conversation but are never spoken.
