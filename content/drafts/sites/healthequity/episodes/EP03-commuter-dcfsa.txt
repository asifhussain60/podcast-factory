## Audience

Working parents with childcare costs, and daily commuters with transit or parking expenses, who have never enrolled in commuter benefits or a Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account — or who enrolled once without understanding what they signed up for. The listener is not skipping these benefits out of indifference; they are skipping them because no one has explained what they actually do in plain English.

---

## Angle

Personal application — walk the listener through the enrollment decision as they would actually face it: how much to set aside, what counts as eligible, and how to avoid the use-it-or-lose-it trap on the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account.

---

## Central tensions

1. Most people pay their daily commute costs and childcare bills with after-tax dollars when their employer offers a way to pay with pre-tax dollars — and they simply do not know the option exists.

2. The Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account limit jumped from $5,000 to $7,500 in 2026 — the largest single-year increase in the account's history — and most people have not heard this. For a family paying $30,000 in childcare, $7,500 shielded from income taxes means $2,250 returned through tax savings.

3. Commuter benefits can be changed any time — no enrollment window required. This makes them almost uniquely low-risk: the listener can start, stop, and adjust their contribution whenever their situation changes.

---

## Background

Commuter benefits allow pre-tax payroll deductions to pay for eligible transit and parking costs. The 2026 IRS monthly limit is $340 per month for transit and $340 per month for parking — members with both can contribute up to $680 per month combined, or $8,160 per year. At a thirty percent effective tax rate, $340 per month in transit contributions saves $1,224 per year in taxes on money the listener was already spending. The Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account covers the cost of caring for dependents — children under 13, a disabled spouse, or an elderly parent in daytime eldercare — while the member works. The 2026 contribution limit is $7,500 for individuals and married couples filing jointly (up from $5,000 in 2025, driven by the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act). HealthEquity administers both accounts alongside the Health Savings Account and Flexible Spending Account on a single platform.

## Beat 1 — The money hiding in the paycheck (15 to 18 minutes)

Host B is already spending $150 to $300 a month on transit and parking, and $1,500 to $2,000 a month on childcare. Host A explains that both of these spending categories have a pre-tax option available through her employer that she has never used.

State the central thesis verbatim (first time).

Walk through how commuter benefits work: the listener signs up through their employer's benefits portal, chooses a monthly contribution amount up to $340 for transit and $340 for parking, and that amount comes out of each paycheck before income taxes are calculated. They spend it on exactly what they were already spending it on. The only difference is the tax.

Walk through three concrete scenarios with numbers. First: Maria spends $150 a month on subway passes. At a thirty percent effective tax rate, enrolling in commuter benefits saves her $540 a year — on money she was already spending. Second: David pays $200 a month for parking near his office. Enrolling saves him $720 a year. Third: Priya spends $330 a month on both transit and parking combined. Enrolling saves her $1,188 a year. None of them are spending new money. All of them are spending the same money with fewer taxes taken out.

Cover how to use the funds: direct payment to transit provider, monthly pass mailed to your home, "Pay Me Back" reimbursements for expenses already paid, or the HealthEquity Commuter debit card for direct payment.

## Beat 2 — The Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account (18 to 20 minutes)

For working parents, childcare is often the largest recurring expense after housing. Full-time daycare for an infant runs $15,000 to $25,000 a year in major metro areas. After-school programs, summer camps, and elder day care add more. All of it paid with after-tax dollars — in the absence of a Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account.

Explain what it covers: childcare, preschool, babysitters, nannies, summer day camps for children under 13, before- and after-school programs, and daytime eldercare for an aging parent who needs care while the member works. The care must be work-enabling — its purpose is to allow the member to work.

The 2026 contribution limit is $7,500 — up from $5,000 in 2025, the largest single-year increase in the account's history. At $7,500 and a thirty percent effective tax rate, the annual tax savings are $2,250. Walk through the worked example: a family paying $24,000 a year in daycare elects the full $7,500. They save $2,250 in taxes on that portion. For a family in a high-cost city paying $30,000 a year in childcare, the savings are the same $2,250 — because the account caps at $7,500, not at the full childcare bill.

State the central thesis verbatim (second time).

Cover the use-it-or-lose-it mechanics: unlike the Health Savings Account, unspent Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account funds at year-end are forfeited (some employers offer a grace period). The planning strategy: estimate conservatively. If annual childcare spending is reliably $24,000, electing $7,500 carries essentially no forfeiture risk. If spending is unpredictable, elect less.

Explain what does not qualify: overnight summer camps, private school tuition, care for children 13 and older.

## Beat 3 — The flexibility advantage and what to do at enrollment (12 to 15 minutes)

The commuter benefit's single most underappreciated feature: the listener can change their contribution any time, not just at open enrollment. Month-to-month adjustment, no penalty, no waiting period. Switch from commuting to remote work: stop contributions immediately. Commuting costs increase: adjust upward the same month. This flexibility makes commuter benefits essentially risk-free to start.

The Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account requires more planning. The contribution is set at enrollment and locked for the plan year (with narrow exceptions for major life changes). For any family with predictable childcare costs, the calculation at enrollment is straightforward: estimate the year's spending, elect up to $7,500, and capture the tax savings.

Show the combined picture: a working parent who is also a commuter and who enrolls in both accounts can realistically save over $3,000 a year in taxes. On the HealthEquity platform, both accounts sit alongside the Health Savings Account — one app, one login, one place to manage all of them together.

Cover the four reasons most people skip enrollment: they do not know the accounts exist; they assume enrollment is complicated; they are afraid of the use-it-or-lose-it rule; they think the amounts are too small to matter. Address each one directly.

State the central thesis verbatim (third time).

The action at enrollment: scroll past the Health Savings Account and Flexible Spending Account sections and spend ten minutes on the commuter benefits and Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account fields. The commuter benefit can be started any time — not just during open enrollment. The Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account requires this enrollment window. Do not let it close without looking.

## Tone constraints

Three governing analogies — use these and no others:

1. **Already spending it** — the listener is not being asked to set aside new money. They are redirecting money they already spend on commuting and childcare through a pre-tax channel before the IRS takes its cut.
2. **The flexible dial** — commuter benefits can be turned up, down, or off at any time. No commitment, no penalty.
3. **The childcare math** — the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account does not solve the cost of childcare; it returns a meaningful fraction in tax savings. $2,250 on a $7,500 election at thirty percent. Real dollars.

Do not introduce new analogies mid-episode.
