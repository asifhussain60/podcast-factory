# Health Savings Accounts: The Triple Tax Advantage

Here's a scenario that plays out millions of times every fall during benefits sign-up season. You sit down with your employer's benefits materials. You see a health plan option described as a "high-deductible health plan" — and the word "high-deductible" stops you cold. You look at the deductible number, which is the amount you pay before insurance kicks in. It's $1,700, or $2,000, or more. You think: that's a lot of money I'd owe before insurance kicks in. So you go with the more familiar plan, pay the higher monthly premium (the monthly cost of your health insurance), and move on.

What you probably didn't notice — because it was described in two sentences of fine print, if it was mentioned at all — is that the high-deductible plan came with access to a Health Savings Account. And that account, if you had used it the way it's designed to be used, would have been the most powerful savings tool you've ever had access to.

Most people who have heard of Health Savings Accounts think of them as a healthcare spending account — a way to set aside money before taxes and use it to pay doctor bills. That's accurate, but it's roughly like describing a Ferrari as "a way to get to the grocery store." It's technically true and almost completely misses the point. The Health Savings Account, used fully, is arguably the most tax-advantaged financial account available to working Americans today — meaning you never pay income tax on the money you put in, the money it earns, or the money you take out for eligible health costs. It beats a 401(k) on pure tax terms. It beats a Roth individual retirement account. Nothing in the American tax code offers what it offers — and it's sitting in your benefits package, underfunded or entirely unopened, for millions of people right now.

This chapter is going to explain exactly why. Not in the language of benefits administration, but in the language of what it actually means for your financial life.

---

**First: how the account works**

To open a Health Savings Account, you need to be enrolled in a high-deductible health plan. In 2026, a plan qualifies as high-deductible if it has a minimum deductible of $1,700 for just you, or $3,400 if you're covering your family. The out-of-pocket maximum — the most you could ever owe in a year before insurance covers everything — is capped at $8,500 for yourself or $17,000 for a family.

The trade-off with a high-deductible plan is real: you pay more before insurance starts covering costs. But these plans almost always have significantly lower monthly premiums than traditional plans. The strategy HealthEquity teaches is to take the difference in what you'd pay in monthly premiums between a traditional plan and the high-deductible option, and put that money directly into your Health Savings Account. Many people find that premium savings alone come close to covering typical out-of-pocket costs in an average year — and the Health Savings Account keeps growing for the years when healthcare expenses are heavier.

The contribution limits for 2026 are $4,400 for just yourself, or $8,750 if you're covering a family. If you're 55 or older, you can add another $1,000 on top of that. You can contribute through pre-tax payroll deductions — meaning the money comes out before income taxes are calculated, lowering the amount of your paycheck that the IRS taxes. Or you can make a personal contribution at any point during the year, up until the tax filing deadline, and deduct it from your taxes.

**A side-by-side enrollment example: seeing the real numbers**

Abstract explanations of the high-deductible health plan trade-off only go so far. Let's look at a concrete example so you can see the math in real terms.

Suppose your employer offers two plans this fall. The traditional preferred provider organization plan costs $450 per month in premiums — the amount deducted from your paycheck each month for health coverage. Its deductible is $500, meaning you pay the first $500 of medical bills each year before insurance begins covering costs. The high-deductible health plan costs $280 per month in premiums. Its deductible is $1,700.

On paper, many people stop right there. $1,700 sounds frightening compared to $500. But let's look at what's actually happening with your money.

The premium difference between the two plans is $170 per month — $450 minus $280. Over twelve months, that's $2,040 in premium savings simply by choosing the high-deductible plan. Now here's the critical step: if you redirect that $170 per month into your Health Savings Account, you've contributed $2,040 by year's end — from money that was already leaving your paycheck. You haven't reduced your take-home pay at all. You've redirected spending that was going to the insurance company into an account that belongs to you.

Now compare the deductibles. The worst-case additional exposure with the high-deductible plan is $1,700 minus $500 — a difference of $1,200. But you've already saved $2,040 in premiums, which you've deposited into your Health Savings Account. Even if you hit your full deductible in a bad health year, you have more than enough in the account to cover it — and you still come out ahead by $840 relative to the traditional plan.

In an average year — where most people spend a few hundred dollars on prescriptions, routine visits, and perhaps a specialist or two — the high-deductible plan plus Health Savings Account combination is often substantially cheaper. In a bad year, you're protected. And in the years where you stay healthy and barely touch the account, that $2,040 in contributions stays in your Health Savings Account, rolls over, and compounds for the future.

This is the core of the strategy HealthEquity teaches: the premium savings aren't lost when you switch plans. They become the seed money for an account that will serve you for decades.

**The triple tax advantage — and why it matters more than you think**

Here is where the Health Savings Account separates itself from every other financial account.

The first advantage: money you put into your Health Savings Account is never subject to federal income tax. In most states it's exempt from state income tax too. If you contribute $4,400 in a year and you're in a combined 30 percent tax bracket, you've effectively gotten $1,320 for free — money that would have gone to taxes, now sitting in your account working for you.

The second advantage: your Health Savings Account balance grows completely tax-free. If you invest the money in your account — and more on why you should in a moment — your gains are never subject to capital gains tax. The interest, the dividends, the growth: none of it gets taxed, ever, as long as it stays in the account.

The third advantage: when you spend Health Savings Account money on eligible health costs — a prescription, a dental cleaning, an eye exam, a hearing aid, a doctor's visit — not one dollar of that withdrawal is taxed either. You put it in without paying tax. It grew without paying tax. You took it out without paying tax.

No other account in the American tax system does all three. A traditional 401(k) gives you the tax break when you put money in, but taxes every dollar when you take it out. A Roth individual retirement account taxes you going in but not coming out — two advantages, not three. The Health Savings Account is the only account that gives you all three tiers of tax protection simultaneously, and it does it specifically for healthcare spending, which is one of the largest and least predictable expenses in most people's lives.

To make this concrete: on $4,000 of annual medical spending — a realistic figure when you add up prescriptions, dental work, vision care, and a few doctor visits — someone in a 30 percent combined tax bracket saves $1,200 per year just through the Health Savings Account's tax treatment. Over a decade, that's $12,000 in tax savings before any investment growth. With investment growth, the number is much higher.

**The retirement angle — the part most people have never heard**

Here is the piece of the Health Savings Account story that genuinely changes how people think about it.

After age 65, you can use your Health Savings Account for anything. Not just healthcare. Anything. If you withdraw for a non-medical expense after 65, you pay regular income tax on it — exactly the same as withdrawing from a 401(k). But for medical expenses, even after 65, your withdrawals are completely tax-free.

Think about what that means. You are building an account that, before 65, gives you tax-free access to healthcare costs. After 65, it gives you the same access as a 401(k) for general expenses — plus continued tax-free access for healthcare costs. It's a retirement account that doubles as the best healthcare-cost vehicle ever created, and it does so at the stage of life when healthcare costs are highest.

Here's the math on saving $4,400 per year — the 2026 individual limit — and investing it at a 7 percent average annual return for 20 years. You'd end up with approximately $180,000. Every dollar of that is tax-free if you use it for healthcare. Every dollar was put in tax-free. Every dollar grew tax-free. That is the triple tax advantage working over time, and that's what HealthEquity means when it says the Health Savings Account is the most powerful consumer health benefit in existence.

The Health Savings Account also has two advantages over a 401(k) that most financial conversations overlook entirely. First, Health Savings Account contributions made through payroll are also exempt from Social Security and Medicare taxes — something a 401(k) contribution doesn't give you. That's an extra 7.65 percent in savings on every dollar you contribute through payroll. Second, the Health Savings Account has no required withdrawals at any age. A 401(k) forces you to start taking money out at age 73. Your Health Savings Account can sit and compound indefinitely, without the IRS forcing distributions.

The conclusion many financial experts have reached: if you have access to a high-deductible health plan with a Health Savings Account, you should be maxing the Health Savings Account before or alongside your 401(k) — not instead of it, but before it, because the tax math is simply better.

**Using the Health Savings Account alongside a 401(k): the sequencing argument**

Most financial conversations about retirement savings treat the 401(k) as the first and primary vehicle, with everything else secondary. The Health Savings Account changes that calculus — when you have access to one, the optimal savings sequence looks different.

Here is the order that many financial planners now recommend for someone with access to a high-deductible health plan and a Health Savings Account through work.

Step one: contribute enough to your 401(k) to capture your employer's full match. If your employer matches the first four percent of your salary, contribute four percent — no more, no less. That match is a guaranteed 100 percent return. Never leave it on the table.

Step two: max out your Health Savings Account. For 2026, that's $4,400 for an individual or $8,750 for a family. The triple tax advantage makes this the most efficient place to grow money after capturing the 401(k) match. Every dollar going in here saves more in taxes than the same dollar going into a 401(k), because the Health Savings Account exempts contributions from Social Security and Medicare taxes as well as income tax — something the 401(k) does not do.

Step three: if money remains after maxing the Health Savings Account, direct additional contributions into your Roth individual retirement account, up to the annual limit. The Roth's tax-free growth compounds well over time, and withdrawals in retirement are tax-free, making it an excellent complement to both the Health Savings Account and the traditional 401(k).

Step four: continue maximizing your 401(k) contributions if you have additional capacity after the above.

The reason this sequencing matters: the Health Savings Account functions as a stealth retirement account when you invest the balance rather than spending it down. By following this order, you're building three tax-advantaged pools simultaneously — the employer-matched 401(k), the triple-tax-advantaged Health Savings Account, and the Roth — rather than stuffing money into a single 401(k) and forgoing the Health Savings Account's superior tax treatment.

For a 35-year-old contributing $4,400 to a Health Savings Account annually and investing it at a 7 percent average annual return, the account reaches approximately $180,000 by age 55 — a pool of money that covers the healthcare costs of early retirement or bridges the gap between leaving the workforce and Medicare eligibility at 65. That gap is one of the largest financial risks for early retirees, and the Health Savings Account is purpose-built to fill it.

**The investment angle**

HealthEquity makes it straightforward to invest your Health Savings Account balance through a lineup of low-cost Vanguard mutual funds, including index funds and target-date funds. The idea is simple: keep enough cash in the account to cover your near-term healthcare costs, and invest the rest. The invested portion grows tax-free and builds toward the long-term healthcare fund you're going to need.

The strategy for how much to keep in cash and how much to invest is straightforward: maintain three to six months of your expected annual out-of-pocket medical costs in the cash portion of your Health Savings Account. This gives you a buffer to cover unexpected medical bills without having to liquidate investments at an inopportune time. Everything above that buffer goes into investments.

Here's what that looks like in practice. Suppose your expected annual out-of-pocket medical costs — prescriptions, routine visits, dental work — run about $2,400 per year, or $200 per month. Three to six months of that is $600 to $1,200. Keep that amount in the cash portion of your Health Savings Account as your medical expense buffer. Every dollar above $1,200 should be invested. If your account balance is $8,000, you have roughly $6,800 invested and $1,200 in cash — growing in the market, compounding tax-free, building toward your long-term healthcare fund.

This approach runs counter to how most people instinctively use the account. The natural impulse is to treat the Health Savings Account like a checking account for medical expenses — fund it, spend it down, repeat. That approach captures the tax deduction on contributions but discards the tax-free growth and the tax-free withdrawal advantage that accumulate over years. The invested approach is what transforms the Health Savings Account from a good short-term tax tool into a genuinely exceptional long-term financial vehicle.

The members who have taken this step are building real financial reserves. As of fiscal year 2025, 753,000 HealthEquity members had investment portfolios in their Health Savings Accounts — a number that grew 23 percent in a single year. That growth reflects a shift in how people are beginning to understand the Health Savings Account: not as a debit card for copays, but as a tax-advantaged investment vehicle with a specific purpose.

**What you can spend it on — and why the list is bigger than you think**

The list of eligible health costs is more expansive than most people know. HealthEquity maintains a searchable version at healthequity.com/learn/hsa. It includes prescriptions, doctor's office fees, urgent care visits, emergency room costs, dental cleanings, braces, orthodontia, eye exams, eyeglasses, contact lenses, LASIK surgery, hearing aids, flu shots, immunizations, acne medication, allergy medicine, ibuprofen and other over-the-counter medications, menstrual products, birth control, breastfeeding supplies including breast pumps, face masks, hand sanitizer, sunscreen, and much more.

The list was significantly expanded in 2020 and has continued to grow. For most people's day-to-day healthcare spending, the Health Savings Account covers it.

A few categories that frequently surprise people: mental health therapy sessions, smoking cessation programs, fertility treatments, weight-loss programs prescribed by a doctor, and service animals. Chiropractic care and acupuncture also qualify in many circumstances. Costs for a guide dog — including veterinary care and food — can qualify as well, since the animal is medical equipment.

The receipt-keeping strategy is worth understanding in detail, because it is one of the most underutilized features of the Health Savings Account. The IRS does not impose a time limit on when you must reimburse yourself for an eligible medical expense. You can pay a medical bill out of pocket today, save the receipt, and reimburse yourself from your Health Savings Account years or even decades later — as long as the expense occurred after you opened the account.

This creates a powerful compounding opportunity. Every time you can afford to pay a medical bill out of pocket, you can let the equivalent amount stay invested in your Health Savings Account, growing tax-free, rather than withdrawing it immediately. The receipts become a running ledger of future reimbursements you're entitled to pull from the account whenever you want — including in retirement, when you may prefer to leave your 401(k) untouched to minimize taxable income.

In practical terms: keep a dedicated folder — physical or digital — for every receipt from an eligible medical expense you pay out of pocket. Note the date, the amount, and what it was for. Over ten years of paying modest medical costs out of pocket while your Health Savings Account grows, you might accumulate $15,000 to $20,000 in documented reimbursements you can claim at any time, completely tax-free. The account has been invested and growing the entire time.

**Common objections — answered**

The Health Savings Account is not right for everyone in every situation. But three objections come up so frequently that they're worth addressing directly.

"What if I have a bad health year?"

The fear is real: you choose the high-deductible plan, you fund the Health Savings Account, and then in January you need unexpected surgery. Your deductible is $1,700, and you haven't built much cushion yet.

A few things mitigate this risk. First, the premium savings you've redirected into the Health Savings Account start immediately — even in January of your first year, you've usually accumulated at least one to two months of premium savings in the account. Second, your employer may also contribute to your Health Savings Account at the start of the year, which is common with high-deductible plan offerings. Third, even in a worst-case year where you exhaust your deductible and owe the maximum out-of-pocket, the total cost exposure is defined and capped. The out-of-pocket maximum for a self-only high-deductible plan in 2026 is $8,500. That is a painful bill, but it is a finite one — and Health Savings Account funds cover it tax-free.

The more important point: you are not comparing the high-deductible plan in isolation to some imaginary worst-case year. You are comparing it to the traditional plan, where you're already paying $2,040 more per year in premiums regardless of your health. Over five years on the traditional plan, you've paid an extra $10,200 in premiums — money that's gone forever. Over five years on the high-deductible plan, that $10,200 is sitting in your Health Savings Account, growing.

"What if my employer switches plans and I can no longer contribute?"

Health Savings Account eligibility requires enrollment in a qualifying high-deductible health plan. If your employer changes plans or you change jobs, and your new coverage is no longer a qualifying high-deductible plan, you lose the ability to make new contributions. But you do not lose the account or the balance. Every dollar already in the account continues to belong to you, continues to grow tax-free, and can still be used for eligible medical expenses at any time. The account simply stops accepting new contributions — it does not disappear. If you later find yourself enrolled in a qualifying plan again, contributions resume.

"What if I retire early — before Medicare eligibility at 65?"

Early retirement is one of the most compelling use cases for a well-funded Health Savings Account. Between leaving the workforce and Medicare eligibility — often a gap of five to ten years for early retirees — health insurance is one of the largest and most unpredictable expenses. The Health Savings Account can be used to pay COBRA premiums (the continuation coverage from your prior employer), marketplace insurance premiums, and, once you're on Medicare, Medicare Part B and Part D premiums. Combined with the accumulated balance from years of investing, the account can meaningfully cover this coverage gap without forcing you to tap taxable retirement accounts early.

**What most people miss**

The most common Health Savings Account mistake is treating it like a use-it-now account — spending every dollar the same year you contribute it, the way people treat a Flexible Spending Account. That approach captures one of the three tax advantages (tax-free contributions) and discards the other two (tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals later in life).

The more powerful approach is to pay healthcare costs out of pocket when you can afford to, let the Health Savings Account balance accumulate and invest, and reimburse yourself years later. The IRS doesn't set a deadline for reimbursements — if you pay a $200 dental bill today and keep the receipt, you can reimburse yourself from your Health Savings Account five years from now, after the account has grown. The reimbursement is still tax-free.

Your Health Savings Account is also fully yours. It doesn't belong to your employer. It doesn't expire if you change jobs. It doesn't disappear at the end of the year. Whatever you put in is yours permanently, rolls over without limit, and follows you anywhere.

If you have access to a high-deductible health plan through your employer and you haven't opened a Health Savings Account — or you opened one but have been leaving it nearly empty — you are paying more in taxes than you have to, every single year. The tax advantage doesn't accumulate in the background waiting for you. It requires a decision during benefits sign-up season: choose the high-deductible plan, open the Health Savings Account, and start contributing.

The people who made that decision ten or twenty years ago and have been consistently contributing and investing their Health Savings Account balances have accounts worth tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars — money that will cover the healthcare costs of retirement, completely tax-free. The people who made the other decision have a standard health plan and a zero balance in an account they never opened.

The window is still open. The question is what you do with it.
