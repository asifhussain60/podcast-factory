# Commuter Benefits and Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account: The Benefits You're Probably Overlooking

When people talk about benefits that save you money on taxes — meaning you never pay income tax on the money you put in — the conversation almost always starts — and often ends — with Health Savings Accounts and Flexible Spending Accounts. Those are the flagship accounts, the ones that feature prominently in benefits sign-up season presentations and benefits fairs. But sitting quietly alongside them, often buried in your benefits guide, are two tools that address two of the largest recurring costs in many Americans' lives: getting to work every day, and paying for someone to care for your children or aging parents while you are there.

Commuter benefits and the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account are underutilized, underappreciated, and genuinely valuable. Understanding them is not complicated. Using them is even simpler. And the savings they produce, year after year, are real.

---

## Commuter Benefits: Turning Your Commute into Tax Savings

There is something deeply ordinary about the daily commute. You take the subway, or the bus, or park your car in a garage, and the money flows out of your account without a second thought. For many urban and suburban workers, commuting costs are a significant monthly expense — $150 in transit passes, $200 in monthly parking, or both. Across a year, that can easily reach $3,000 to $5,000. And almost none of it, under normal circumstances, receives any tax treatment at all.

Commuter benefits change that. Offered through your employer, commuter benefits allow you to set aside pre-tax dollars from your paycheck — each month, before income taxes are calculated — to pay for eligible transit and parking costs. The pre-tax deduction reduces the income the IRS taxes, and you spend the money on the same commuting expenses you were already paying. The only difference is that you pay less tax.

The eligible expenses for a transit account include train, subway, bus, ferry, and vanpool costs. The eligible expenses for a parking account include parking at or near your workplace, or at a facility from which you commute to work by mass transit. These are the most common daily transportation costs for working Americans, and both categories are covered.

For 2026, the IRS monthly limit for both transit and parking is $340 per month, per category. If you use both transit and parking, you can contribute the combined maximum — up to $680 per month total. At $340 per month for transit alone, the annual contribution potential is $4,080. At a 30 percent effective tax rate, that represents more than $1,200 in annual tax savings. For workers with both transit and parking costs, that savings figure can reach $2,000 or more per year — simply by redirecting money you were already spending through a pre-tax account.

HealthEquity's commuter benefits platform offers several convenient ways to use the funds. You can arrange direct payments to your transit provider or parking garage, eliminating the need to track and submit receipts. You can have monthly transit passes or tickets mailed to your home address. You can submit requests for reimbursement — called "Pay Me Back" — for parking or transit expenses you have already paid out of pocket. Or you can use the HealthEquity Commuter debit card, loading a chosen amount each month and using the card directly to pay for transit fares and parking.

One of the most attractive features of commuter benefits — in contrast to the healthcare Flexible Spending Account — is flexibility. You can enroll at any time, without waiting for benefits sign-up season. You can pause your contributions, change how much you choose to set aside, or cancel entirely whenever your circumstances change. If you switch from taking the subway to working from home, you can stop contributions immediately. If your commuting costs change, you adjust how much you set aside to match. There is no benefits sign-up window to wait for, no annual planning commitment required. The account flexes with your life.

Unused commuter funds also do not automatically expire at year end the way Flexible Spending Account funds do — as long as you remain employed, your balance carries forward. This makes the commuter benefit particularly low-risk: there is no penalty for estimating your contributions conservatively.

**Three concrete commuter scenarios: seeing the savings in real numbers**

The easiest way to understand commuter benefit savings is to see them applied to real spending patterns. Here are three scenarios that reflect common commuter situations.

Scenario one: the urban subway commuter. Maria works in Manhattan and takes the subway to the office five days a week. Her monthly subway pass costs $150. She sets aside $150 per month through her employer's commuter benefit account. At a 30 percent effective tax rate, her annual tax savings are $540 — on $1,800 in annual transit spending that was already leaving her account. She has not changed her commute in any way. She has simply routed $1,800 per year through a pre-tax account, and the IRS never sees that income.

Scenario two: the suburban parking commuter. David drives to work and parks in a garage near his office at $200 per month. He sets aside $200 per month through the parking account. At a 30 percent effective tax rate, his annual tax savings are $720 — on $2,400 in annual parking costs that he was paying anyway. Same garage, same spot, same commute. The only change is that $2,400 per year now flows through a pre-tax account before it reaches the garage.

Scenario three: both transit and parking. Priya drives to a commuter rail station, parks in the station's monthly parking lot, and takes the train into the city. Her transit pass costs $200 per month and her station parking costs $130 per month — a combined $330 per month, or $3,960 per year. She sets aside the full $330 per month across both accounts. At a 30 percent effective tax rate, her annual tax savings are $1,188. If her costs were slightly higher and she used the full combined maximum of $680 per month — $340 each for transit and parking — her annual tax savings would be $2,448.

Taken together, these scenarios illustrate a consistent pattern: commuter benefits deliver $500 to $2,400 or more in annual tax savings depending on commuting costs, with no change to the commuter's actual transportation behavior. The IRS limit of $340 per month per category is generous enough to cover the realistic transit and parking costs of most working Americans — and the flexibility of the account means you contribute only what you actually spend.

HealthEquity's own illustration puts the savings in concrete terms: someone spending $150 per month on transit and $125 per month on parking — $3,300 per year total — saves $990 per year in taxes at a 30 percent effective rate. That is nearly $1,000 per year in tax savings on money that was already going to leave your pocket. You are not taking on any additional financial commitment. You are simply reorganizing spending you were already doing.

## Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account: Making Childcare and Eldercare More Affordable

The cost of caring for dependents — children, disabled spouses, or aging parents who require day care while you work — is one of the heaviest financial burdens facing working families in the United States. Full-time daycare for an infant can cost $15,000 to $25,000 per year in major metropolitan areas. After-school programs, summer camps, elder care facilities: these costs add up with relentless consistency, and they are paid entirely with after-tax dollars in the absence of a Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account.

The Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account is the solution HealthEquity offers for this specific category of expense. Like the healthcare Flexible Spending Account, it is an employer-sponsored account funded with pre-tax payroll deductions. But where the healthcare Flexible Spending Account covers medical costs, the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account covers the cost of caring for qualifying dependents — so that you can go to work.

The IRS says a dependent qualifies for a Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account if they are one of three things: a child under age 13, a disabled spouse, or an older parent in eldercare. For all three, the key rule is that the care must happen while you are working — the purpose of the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account is specifically to enable you to work, not to help pay for care provided at other times. A parent in a full-time residential facility whose care costs are unrelated to your work schedule would not qualify; a parent in a daytime eldercare program while you are at the office typically would.

The contribution limit for Dependent Care Flexible Spending Accounts in 2026 is $7,500 for individuals and for married couples filing jointly — a significant increase from the 2025 limit of $5,000, driven by the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which raised Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account limits to help families keep pace with rising care costs. For married couples filing separately, the limit is $2,500 per spouse.

At $7,500 in annual Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account contributions and a 30 percent effective tax rate, the annual tax savings are $2,250. For a family spending $30,000 per year on childcare — not an unusual figure in high-cost cities — $7,500 of that through the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account means $2,250 is effectively returned to them through tax savings. It does not solve the full cost of childcare in the United States. But it meaningfully reduces it.

**A worked annual planning example**

The most useful way to understand the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account is to walk through a concrete annual planning exercise.

Suppose you and your spouse both work, and your child is three years old. Full-time daycare at your provider costs $2,000 per month, or $24,000 per year. During benefits sign-up season in November, you elect the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account for the following calendar year.

The maximum contribution is $7,500. You elect $7,500, spread evenly across 26 biweekly pay periods — approximately $288 per paycheck. This comes out before income taxes are calculated, reducing your taxable income by $7,500.

At a 30 percent combined effective tax rate — federal and state income taxes combined — your annual tax savings from the $7,500 contribution are $2,250. In concrete terms: $2,250 of the $24,000 you were going to spend on daycare is now returned to you through lower taxes. Your net daycare cost drops from $24,000 to $21,750.

By the end of the year, your $7,500 in Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account funds has been reimbursed against $7,500 worth of eligible daycare invoices. The remaining $16,500 in daycare costs you paid with after-tax dollars — but you've protected the first $7,500 from income tax entirely.

The math works similarly for other dependent care scenarios. If you're paying $15,000 per year for after-school care and summer camp for two elementary-school-age children, electing $7,500 in the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account saves $2,250 in taxes while covering half of that cost pre-tax. If you're paying for a nanny and your annual nanny cost exceeds $7,500, you elect the maximum and apply it to the first portion of the year's payments.

**What qualifies as dependent care — the full list**

The list of eligible Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account expenses is more specific than many people expect — it's broader than daycare but narrower than all child-related costs. Understanding what qualifies helps you plan accurately.

For children under 13, eligible expenses include: licensed daycare centers and preschools (note: preschool qualifies as dependent care, not education, if the primary purpose is childcare while you work — a nuanced but important distinction); babysitters and nannies who care for your child while you work; before-school care programs operated by schools or independent providers; after-school care programs, including those offered at school facilities; and summer day camps, including specialty camps for sports, arts, or science, as long as they are day programs and the child is under 13 at the time of the camp.

For eligible expenses related to children, expenses that do not qualify include: overnight summer camps (the cost of sleeping away is categorically excluded), private school tuition for kindergarten and above (the IRS treats this as education, not dependent care, even if it frees you to work), and costs for children who have turned 13 during the year for any expenses incurred after their 13th birthday.

For disabled spouses and elderly parents in elder day care, eligible expenses include: adult day care centers that provide daytime supervision and programming while you work; home care aides or companions who care for the dependent in your home during your work hours; and similar programs that allow you to maintain employment while your dependent receives care. Eligible elder care must be provided while you are at work — overnight or full-time residential care facility costs are generally not eligible, since the care is not specifically enabling your employment.

A note on nannies and babysitters: the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account reimburses payments to individual caregivers, including nannies and babysitters you hire privately. However, using the account for a caregiver you employ creates tax-filing obligations on your end — you may be what the IRS calls a household employer, with associated payroll and tax requirements. If you pay a nanny $2,400 or more per year, consult a tax advisor about household employer obligations before relying on Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account reimbursements.

**The use-it-or-lose-it risk and how to plan conservatively**

The Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account's most significant downside is the use-it-or-lose-it rule. Unlike the Health Savings Account, which rolls over every dollar indefinitely, any balance remaining in your Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account at the end of the plan year — or at the end of a grace period your employer may offer — is forfeited back to your employer.

This is a real risk, and it creates an important planning discipline. The goal is to elect an amount you are confident you will spend, not the maximum amount you theoretically could spend.

A conservative planning approach works like this. Start with your known, recurring dependent care costs — the monthly daycare invoice, the after-school program tuition, the nanny's weekly rate. Multiply by twelve to get your annual baseline. Subtract any weeks when care will not be provided (holidays, vacations, weeks when your child is sick and not attending). Compare the result to the $7,500 maximum.

If your known annual dependent care costs reliably exceed $7,500 — which they will for most families using full-time daycare in a major city — elect the maximum with confidence. The $7,500 will be spent before the plan year ends.

If your annual dependent care costs are closer to $5,000 or $6,000 and you're unsure whether you'll reach $7,500, elect $5,000 or $5,500 and accept a smaller tax benefit in exchange for lower forfeit risk. The tax savings on $5,000 at 30 percent are $1,500 — still a meaningful benefit — and you avoid the forfeiture of any amount you fail to spend.

A few things that can reduce your dependent care spending mid-year, creating forfeiture risk: a change in your child's school schedule that reduces afterschool care needs; a caregiver who changes pricing or availability; one spouse reducing work hours or leaving the workforce; your child aging out of eligibility at 13 mid-year. When any of these changes occur, check whether your employer allows a mid-year election change — most plans permit this when you experience a qualifying life event. Reducing your election promptly limits the amount at risk.

**The rules of the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account**

The rules of the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account are similar to the healthcare Flexible Spending Account in key respects. You decide how much to set aside during benefits sign-up season, and that amount is generally locked in for the year. You can only change it if you have a major life event, like a change in jobs, getting married, having a baby, or if your daycare provider closes. The Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account also has use-it-or-lose-it rules — meaning any money you don't spend by the end of the year goes back to your employer, though some employers offer a grace period extension. Unlike the healthcare Flexible Spending Account's advance funding feature, Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account funds are generally available only as payroll contributions accumulate, not in full at the start of the year.

The Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account does not impact Health Savings Account eligibility, which means you can hold both simultaneously — using the Health Savings Account for medical costs and the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account for dependent care costs. It can also be combined with a standard healthcare Flexible Spending Account for comprehensive coverage of both medical and caregiving expenses.

HealthEquity's Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account illustration is instructive: someone spending $300 per month on daycare, $500 on summer camp, and $50 per month on an after-school program — $4,500 in total annual spending — saves $1,350 per year in taxes. That is $1,350 that could go toward next year's camp, toward savings, toward anything. The tax savings are not abstract. They are dollars.

Taken together, commuter benefits and the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account represent potentially thousands of dollars per year in tax savings for working Americans who look past the headline Health Savings Account and Flexible Spending Account accounts to the full range of benefits available to them. **Why these benefits are chronically underused — and what to do about it**

Despite the clear financial case for commuter benefits and the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account, participation rates remain significantly lower than for Health Savings Accounts and standard health insurance. The reasons are consistent and predictable — and each one has a practical answer.

The first reason is invisibility. During benefits sign-up season, most employer communications lead with health insurance, then the 401(k), then perhaps the Flexible Spending Account. Commuter benefits and the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account are often mentioned briefly or relegated to the last page of a benefits guide. Many employees complete enrollment without realizing either account was available to them. The fix is simple: read every section of your benefits guide before submitting enrollment elections, not just the sections you already know about.

The second reason is confusion about what qualifies. Many workers assume their specific transit pass doesn't qualify, or that their nanny payments aren't eligible, or that summer camp is excluded. In most of these cases, the assumption is wrong — the eligible expense lists are broader than intuition suggests. HealthEquity maintains searchable eligibility tools at healthequity.com for both commuter benefits and the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account, and member support specialists can answer eligibility questions directly before you commit to an election.

The third reason is fear of the use-it-or-lose-it rule. Workers who have heard about forfeitures in healthcare Flexible Spending Accounts sometimes assume the same risk applies to commuter benefits — it doesn't, since unused commuter balances carry forward. For the Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account, the use-it-or-lose-it rule is real, but it's manageable through conservative planning. The appropriate response to forfeiture risk is careful estimation, not non-participation.

The fourth reason is the sense that the savings aren't worth the administrative effort. For a worker paying $150 per month in transit costs, the annual tax savings are $540. That sounds modest. But $540 per year for a five-minute enrollment decision that lasts indefinitely — and doesn't require changing anything about how you commute — is an extremely good return on administrative effort. When combined with Dependent Care Flexible Spending Account savings of $2,250 and Health Savings Account savings of $1,320, the combined picture looks very different: over $4,000 in annual tax savings from three enrollment decisions made during the same benefits sign-up session.

The underlying logic of all three pre-tax accounts is the same. Money you were going to spend on healthcare, commuting, and dependent care was already leaving your paycheck. Routing it through a pre-tax account before it reaches those expenses reduces the income the IRS taxes. The care your child receives is identical. The subway fare is identical. The doctor's bill is identical. Only the tax treatment is different — and the difference is thousands of dollars per year.
