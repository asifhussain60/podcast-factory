## Glossary

**Sun Wukong** — The Monkey King, the trickster-hero protagonist of *Journey to the West*. A stone-born immortal with shapeshifting powers; often calls himself "old Monkey" the way a swaggering hero might say "yours truly."

**Jade Emperor** — Supreme ruler of Heaven in Chinese mythology and Daoist religion, presiding over a celestial bureaucracy of gods. Functionally a Chinese Zeus, but running Heaven like an imperial court rather than a stormy household.

**Great White Planet of Venus** (Taibai Jinxing) — An elderly star-deity who serves as the Jade Emperor's diplomat and envoy. The conciliator who keeps proposing compromises rather than fights.

**Southern Gate of Heaven** — The main gateway between the mortal world and the celestial palace, guarded by heavenly soldiers. Think of it as the front door of Heaven.

**Water-Curtain Cave** — Sun Wukong's stronghold on the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit, hidden behind a waterfall. His home base and throne room.

**Mountain of Flowers and Fruit (Flower-Fruit Mountain)** — The paradisal island-mountain where Wukong is king over the monkeys and allied demon clans.

**BanMaWen (Keeper of the Horses)** — A real-sounding Chinese bureaucratic title invented as a joke: the lowest "unranked" post in Heaven, responsible for stable duty. The whole plot hinges on Wukong realizing he's been given a humiliating non-job.

**Heavenly King Virūḍhaka** — One of the Four Heavenly Kings of Buddhist cosmology, guardian of the south. Here he commands the gate-soldiers who block Wukong's first approach.

**Pagoda-Bearing Heavenly King Li Jing** — A senior Daoist-Buddhist warrior-god who carries a miniature pagoda as his signature weapon (the pagoda magically imprisons demons). Father of Nezha; a kind of celestial general-in-chief.

**Nezha** — A famous child-deity warrior, third son of Li Jing. Depicted as a boy with extraordinary powers who can manifest three heads and six arms, each gripping a divine weapon — a martial prodigy roughly equivalent to a young Heracles crossed with a Hindu Kartikeya.

**Three heads and six arms** — A classic divine transformation showing a deity's full battle-power, borrowed from Buddhist iconography. Visual shorthand for "now I'm fighting at full strength."

**Mighty Magic Spirit (Juling Shen)** — A heavenly general who serves as Li Jing's vanguard, wielding a great battle-ax. Mostly here to be defeated and break his weapon.

**Great Sage Equal to Heaven (Qitian Dasheng)** — The grandiose self-given title Wukong demands, claiming rank equal to the Jade Emperor himself. The defining boast of the Monkey King's career.

**One-Horned Demon Kings** — Lesser demon-lords from the surrounding caves who flatter Wukong with a royal robe and plant the "Great Sage" idea in his head. The instigators of the upgrade.

**Russet-yellow robe** — A reddish-gold ceremonial robe. In Chinese court symbolism yellow is the imperial color, so wearing it is itself a political claim.

**As-You-Will Golden-Banded Cudgel (Ruyi Jingu Bang)** — Wukong's signature weapon: an iron staff that grows or shrinks at his will, normally tucked behind his ear. The Monkey King's equivalent of Thor's hammer.

**Garden of Immortal Peaches** — A celestial orchard whose peaches grant immortality. Setting up a major plot device for later chapters — and a Chinese cousin of the Garden of the Hesperides.

**Grand Monad elixirs / elixir-refining furnace** — Daoist alchemical pills and the furnace that produces them, said to grant immortality. The pharmaceutical wing of Heaven.

**Jade Hare / Golden Crow** — Mythic animals of the moon (the rabbit pounding elixir) and the sun (the three-legged crow). Stand-ins for moonlight and sunlight in classical poetry.

**Vaiśravaṇa** — One of the Four Heavenly Kings of Buddhism (guardian of the north); named here as a celestial palace.

**Star Lord of Wuqu / Star Officer of the Wood Virtue / Star Lords of the Five Bushels** — Stellar deities in Daoist cosmology, each governing a constellation or cosmic function and serving as celestial officials. Heaven's mid-level civil service.

**Heavenly Master Zhang** — Zhang Daoling, the legendary founding patriarch of religious Daoism, here serving as a senior court official in Heaven.

**Bull Demon King, Flood-Dragon Demon King, Roc, Lion-Camel, Macaque, Lemur-Monkey** — Wukong's six sworn brothers, powerful demon-lords of other mountains who together with him form the "Seven Great Sages." The Bull Demon King especially returns as a major figure later.

**Kowtow** — The ritual prostration of bowing head-to-floor before a superior. Wukong's refusal to kowtow to the Jade Emperor is the central rudeness of the chapter.

**"One day in Heaven is a year on earth"** — A standard convention of Chinese myth: celestial and mortal time run on different scales. Explains why Wukong's two weeks of stable duty are twelve years back home.

**Yaksha** — A class of nature-spirit / minor warrior-demon imported from Indian Buddhist tradition. Here, a general in Li Jing's army.

**Dayuan horses / Chitu / the eight chargers** — Legendary breeds and named steeds from Chinese history and myth (Chitu was Lü Bu's red warhorse in the *Three Kingdoms*). The list is meant to dazzle: every famous horse you've ever heard of is in Wukong's stable.

**Numinous Empyrean Treasure-Hall (Lingxiao Baodian)** — The main throne hall of the Jade Emperor, the highest chamber in Heaven. The "Oval Office" of the celestial court.

**Dipper and Bull** — Constellations (Big Dipper and the asterism around Altair) used as poetic shorthand for "the whole sky." To "stir up Dipper and Bull" is to throw all of Heaven into uproar.

## Host Asides

> Aside: The Jade Emperor isn't quite a Zeus — he's more like Heaven's emperor with a vast civil service. So every snub Wukong delivers isn't just impiety; it's also breaking bureaucratic protocol, which makes it funnier.

> Aside: That title "BanMaWen — Keeper of the Horses" is the joke of the whole chapter. It sounds impressive in court Chinese but it's the celestial equivalent of "assistant to the regional manager." Wukong only finds out at the office party.

> Aside: Nezha sprouting three heads and six arms isn't just a special effect — it's a real iconographic pose you'll see on temple statues across East Asia. Worth picturing: a child-warrior with six weapons, fighting a monkey who instantly copies the trick.

> Aside: "One day in Heaven is a year on earth" is a recurring rule in Chinese myth — same logic as the Irish fairy mounds where you come back and everyone's grown old. It's why his monkeys think he's been gone twelve years.
