## Glossary

**Pangu** — The primordial giant of Chinese creation myth who split apart the formless cosmic egg, separating the "pure" (sky/yang) from the "impure" (earth/yin). Functionally similar to Ymir in Norse myth or the Babylonian Tiamat — the body whose breaking opens the world.

**Twelve Earthly Branches (Zi, Chou, Yin, Mao, Chen, Si, Wu, Wei, Shen, You, Xu, Hai)** — A traditional Chinese duodecimal system used to mark hours of the day, months, years, and cosmic epochs. Here they index the twelve great ages in a 129,600-year world-cycle, the way Hindu cosmology uses yugas.

**Yang and Yin** — Complementary cosmic principles: yang is bright, active, masculine, hot; yin is dark, receptive, feminine, cool. Not opposed like good-vs-evil, but interdependent like daylight and dusk.

**Master Shao Kangjie (Shao Yong)** — An eleventh-century Song-dynasty Neo-Confucian philosopher famous for cosmological diagrams of cyclical time. Quoted here as an authoritative voice on when "the heart of heaven" stirs.

**Qian and Kun** — Two of the eight trigrams in the *Book of Changes (Yijing/I Ching)*: Qian is pure heaven, the creative; Kun is pure earth, the receptive. The cosmic father and mother of all hexagrams.

**Book of Changes (I Ching)** — Ancient Chinese divination and philosophy text built around 64 hexagrams of yin/yin lines; foundational to Daoist and Confucian cosmology.

**Five Forms** — Water, fire, mountains, stone, soil — the basic constituents of the formed earth in this cosmogony (distinct from the more familiar "Five Phases / wuxing": wood, fire, earth, metal, water).

**Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors** — Legendary culture-hero rulers of remotest Chinese antiquity, credited with founding civilization (writing, agriculture, marriage, medicine). Roughly analogous to the mythic kings of early Rome or the biblical patriarchs.

**Four Continents (Spiritual Excellence / Cattle-Offering / Jambu / Kuru)** — A Buddhist cosmological scheme: four great landmasses around the world-mountain Sumeru. Jambudvipa (Southern) is where humans live in classical Buddhist geography; the others are mythic.

**Aolai** — A small kingdom on the easternmost edge of the Eastern Continent; the local realm beneath Flower-Fruit Mountain. Not a real historical place — a mythic frontier.

**Flower-Fruit Mountain (Huaguo Shan)** — The magical island-mountain where the Stone Monkey is born; the ancestral root of all mountains in this cosmology. Think Olympus crossed with Eden.

**Zhang / chi / cun / li** — Traditional Chinese units of length. A zhang ≈ 10 feet, a chi ≈ 1 foot, a cun ≈ 1 inch; a li ≈ a third of a mile. The stone's dimensions are coded numerology, not random measurements.

**Nine Palaces and Eight Trigrams** — The Nine Palaces are nine sectors of a magic-square cosmogram; the Eight Trigrams (bagua) are the eight three-line symbols of the *I Ching*. Together they map heaven's geometry — the stone's openings literally mirror the universe.

**Twenty-Four Solar Terms** — The traditional Chinese agricultural calendar's division of the solar year into 24 fortnightly periods (Spring Begins, Grain Rain, etc.).

**Four Images** — Sun, moon, stars, constellations — the four luminous bodies of the sky in this cosmology.

**Jade Emperor (Yu Huang)** — Supreme ruler of Heaven in Chinese folk religion; head of a vast celestial bureaucracy of gods. Functionally like Zeus, but presiding over a heavenly civil service rather than a quarrelsome family.

**Cloud Palace of the Golden Gate / Spirit-Mist Treasure Hall** — The Jade Emperor's celestial throne-room complex.

**Palace of the Dipper** — The constellation of the Big Dipper, conceived as a literal palace in the sky inhabited by star-deities.

**Thousand-Mile Eye and Down-the-Wind Ear (Qianliyan and Shunfeng'er)** — Two of the Jade Emperor's surveillance officers; one sees a thousand miles, the other hears anything carried on the wind. Heaven's reconnaissance team.

**Southern Gate of Heaven** — The main ceremonial gate of the celestial palace, opening downward toward the mortal world.

**King Yama (Yanluo / Yama)** — King of the dead and judge of souls in the underworld; rules the realm where the deceased are evaluated and assigned their next rebirth. Borrowed from Indian Buddhism into Chinese religion; analogous to Hades or Pluto, but specifically a judicial figure.

**The Way (Dao)** — In Daoism, the underlying pattern or path of all reality; in this novel, also the discipline by which one cultivates immortality.

**Buddhas, Immortals, and Holy Sages** — The three categories of beings who have escaped death and rebirth: Buddhas (enlightened ones of the Buddhist tradition), Immortals (xian — Daoist adepts who have transcended mortality), and Holy Sages (shengren — Confucian/legendary perfected beings).

**Wheel of Rebirth (samsara)** — The Buddhist cycle of birth, death, and reincarnation, driven by karma. Escaping it is the goal of enlightenment.

**Jambudvipa** — In Buddhist cosmology, the southern continent — the inhabited human world. Indian Sanskrit term, transliterated into Chinese.

**Great Sage Equal to Heaven (Qitian Dasheng)** — The title Sun Wukong will later claim for himself, brazenly equating his rank with the Jade Emperor's. The text foreshadows it here.

**Common-Backed Gibbon (tongbei yuan)** — A specific kind of long-armed ape whose "linked-shoulder" anatomy was thought to give it special powers; in folklore, a wise and uncanny creature.

**Cinnabar (dan)** — A red mineral (mercury sulfide) used in Daoist alchemy to make the "elixir of immortality." "Cinnabar's completion" means the alchemical attainment of the deathless state.

**Penglai** — One of the legendary islands of the immortals in the Eastern Sea; the Chinese equivalent of the Isles of the Blessed or Avalon.

**Yellow Court (Huangting Jing)** — A foundational Daoist meditation scripture concerning inner alchemy and the body's internal deities.

**Fragrance Fills the Courtyard (Manting Fang)** — A classical Chinese song-lyric tune-pattern (cipai). Here, the title of the woodcutter's song.

**Filial Piety (xiao)** — The Confucian virtue of devoted service to one's parents; in classical China, the cardinal moral duty.

**Mountain of the Spirit-Terrace and the Square-Inch / Cave of the Slanting Moon and Three Stars** — Coded names for the Patriarch's dwelling: "Spirit-Terrace" and "Square-Inch" are classical metaphors for the heart-mind; the "slanting moon over three stars" is a visual riddle for the character 心 (xin, "heart"). The location is the seeker's own heart.

**Patriarch Subodhi (Puti Zushi)** — The Daoist–Buddhist master who will teach Sun Wukong. His name fuses "Subhuti" (a disciple of the Buddha in Mahayana sutras) with the Chinese honorific "Patriarch." Functionally a syncretic Daoist–Buddhist sage.

**Dharma-name** — A religious name given to a disciple upon initiation, marking entry into a spiritual lineage; common to Buddhist monastic practice.

**Sun Wukong** — "Monkey Awakened to Emptiness." Sun comes from removing the "beast" radical from the character hu (狲, monkey), leaving child + small. Wukong (悟空) joins "awakened" + "emptiness" — the Buddhist insight into the voidness of all phenomena.

**Kowtow** — A formal prostration: kneeling and touching the forehead to the ground; the deepest gesture of respect in Chinese ritual etiquette.

**Three yang** — A reference to the three yang lines of the Qian trigram in the *I Ching*, signifying pure creative force.

## Host Asides

> Aside: The Jade Emperor is the supreme ruler of Heaven in Chinese mythology — basically Zeus, but presiding over a vast celestial civil service complete with paperwork, ranks, and inter-departmental memos. Keep that bureaucratic image in mind — it pays off massively when Monkey starts wreaking havoc in it later.

> Aside: When the text says "the Way," it means the Dao — the underlying pattern of reality in Daoist philosophy, but also a discipline a practitioner cultivates to become immortal. "Seeking the Way" here is closer to "joining a mystery cult to defeat death" than "becoming philosophical."

> Aside: King Yama is the judge of the dead, imported into Chinese religion from Indian Buddhism. He's less a grim reaper and more a celestial magistrate — souls get processed, weighed, and assigned to their next life. Monkey's fear of him is what kicks off the entire quest.

> Aside: The name of the Patriarch's mountain is a riddle — "Spirit-Terrace, Square-Inch, Slanting Moon, Three Stars." Those are all classical metaphors for the heart-mind, and the "slanting moon over three stars" literally draws the Chinese character for "heart." The novel is telling us, with a wink, that Monkey's real journey is inward.
