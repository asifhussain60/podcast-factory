## Glossary

**Handsome Monkey King / Sun Wukong** — The novel's trickster protagonist, a stone-born monkey who has mastered Daoist magic. "Sun Wukong" is his religious name, meaning roughly "Monkey Awakened-to-Emptiness." Think of him as a Chinese Loki-Hercules hybrid with cosmic-scale ambitions.

**Mountain of Flowers and Fruit / Water-Curtain Cave** — Wukong's home mountain and the cave behind a waterfall where his monkey tribe lives. It functions as his kingdom and base of operations throughout the novel.

**Somersault Cloud** — Wukong's signature flying technique: a single somersault carries him 108,000 li (roughly 33,000 miles). A magical mode of travel, not a vehicle.

**Aolai** — A fictional human kingdom east of the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit, near enough to raid for weapons.

**Body-dividing magic** — A standard Daoist trick in the novel: Wukong plucks hairs from his body, chews them, and spits them out as duplicates of himself or other creatures. His all-purpose labor force.

**Catty (jin)** — A traditional Chinese unit of weight, roughly 1.3 pounds (600 grams). 13,500 catties is around 17,500 pounds — the cudgel is impossibly heavy by design.

**Crystal Palace / Dragon King of the Eastern Sea (Aoguang)** — The undersea palace of Aoguang, ruler of the Eastern Sea. In Chinese myth the Four Dragon Kings (Aoguang east, Aoqin south, Aorun west, Aoshun north) govern the seas and rains — celestial bureaucrats more than monsters.

**Yaksha** — A class of nature spirit borrowed from Indian Buddhism, here serving as undersea guards and patrollers for the Dragon King. Closer to "warrior-attendant" than the demonic yaksha of horror tales.

**Great Yu** — Legendary sage-king who tamed China's primordial floods. Comparable to Noah crossed with a civil engineer; his flood-control tools are sacred relics.

**Heavenly River** — The Milky Way, conceived in Chinese cosmology as a literal river flowing through the sky. Yu's iron rod once measured its depth.

**As-You-Will Gold-Banded Cudgel (Ruyi Jingu Bang)** — Wukong's iconic weapon: a black-iron staff with gold bands that obeys mental commands to grow, shrink, or multiply. The single most famous magical object in Chinese fiction.

**Phoenix-winged cap, chain-link golden armor, cloud-walking shoes** — Wukong's divine outfit, extorted from the Dragon Kings. Standard "celestial warrior" regalia in Chinese iconography.

**Region of Darkness / Hall of Judgment / Ten Kings of the Underworld** — The Chinese afterlife bureaucracy. Souls pass through ten courts (King Qinguang, King Yama, King of Mount Tai, etc.) for judgment and sentencing before rebirth. Like Dante's hell, but run as a government ministry.

**Yama** — Originally the Indian/Buddhist lord of death; in Chinese folk religion, one of the Ten Kings overseeing the underworld courts.

**Registers of Life and Death** — The ledger books recording every creature's allotted lifespan. Striking your name out makes you immortal — the cosmic equivalent of hacking the social security database.

**Ox-headed and horse-faced demons** — Two iconic underworld guards (Niu-tou and Ma-mian) who escort souls to judgment. Roughly the Chinese counterparts to grim reapers.

**Three Realms and Five Elements** — The Three Realms = Heaven, Earth, Underworld (the whole cosmos). The Five Elements (wood, fire, earth, metal, water) are the constituent forces of all matter. To "rise above" them is to be enlightened beyond ordinary existence.

**Qilin and Phoenix** — Mythical creatures that govern the four-legged beasts (qilin) and the birds (phoenix) respectively. Wukong fits neither category — a running joke about his anomalous nature.

**Bodhisattva Kshitigarbha (Dizang)** — A Buddhist savior figure who vowed to empty the hells of suffering souls. Here he is the highest spiritual authority over the underworld — a compassionate overseer above the bureaucratic Ten Kings.

**Jade Emperor** — Supreme ruler of Heaven in Chinese folk religion. Equivalent to Zeus, but presiding over a vast celestial bureaucracy of immortal officials.

**Hall of Divine Mists / Cloud Palace of the Golden Gate** — The Jade Emperor's throne room and palace complex in Heaven.

**Six sworn brothers (Bull Demon King, Serpent, Roc, Lion-Camel, Macaque, Marmoset Kings)** — Other powerful demon-kings Wukong allies with through a sworn-brotherhood ritual — a binding fictive-kinship oath common in Chinese epic. The Bull Demon King will become a major recurring figure.

**Great White Planet of Venus (Taibai Jinxing)** — Personification of the planet Venus as a wise, elderly immortal minister. The Jade Emperor's go-to diplomat — the celestial court's mediator.

**Thousand-Mile Eye and Down-the-Wind Ear** — Two Heavenly officers with supernatural sight and hearing. Heaven's surveillance system.

**Edict of amnesty and recruitment (zhao'an)** — A real imperial Chinese policy of pardoning rebels in exchange for state service. Heaven offers Wukong a government job rather than fighting him.

**Five-Phoenix Tower** — A grand ceremonial gate-tower of imperial palaces; mentioned here as poetic shorthand for "the king's palace."

**Thirty-Third Heaven / Eighteenth Hell** — The vertical extent of the Buddhist cosmos: 33 ascending heavens above, 18 hells below. The cudgel can span the whole universe.

**Dream of the Southern Branch** — A classical literary allusion to a Tang-dynasty tale in which a man dreams a whole lifetime under a locust tree, only to wake and find it illusion. Shorthand for "it was all a dream."

## Host Asides

> Aside: The Jade Emperor is the supreme ruler of Heaven in Chinese myth — basically Zeus, but instead of throwing thunderbolts he reads memos and signs edicts. Heaven in this novel runs like the imperial Chinese civil service.

> Aside: The cudgel weighs 13,500 catties — roughly 17,500 pounds. The point isn't realism; it's that no one *else* could pick it up. It's the Chinese version of Thor's hammer, except it also shrinks down to fit behind his ear.

> Aside: When Wukong scratches his name out of the Registers of Life and Death, he's not committing murder — he's hacking the cosmic spreadsheet. The Chinese underworld is a bureaucracy, and he's just edited the database.

> Aside: That "edict of amnesty and recruitment" at the end is a real imperial-Chinese tactic — pardon the bandit and give him a government job. Heaven decides it's cheaper to hire Wukong than fight him. Spoiler: that plan goes about as well as you'd expect.
