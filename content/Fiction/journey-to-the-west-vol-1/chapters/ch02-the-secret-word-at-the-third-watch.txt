# The Secret Word at the Third Watch

*Six or seven years of sweeping, sweeping and gardening pass in the cave of the Patriarch Subodhi before the new disciple is tested. Offered four different gates of the Way — Arts, Schools, Stillness, Action — Sun Wukong refuses every one because none of them grants eternal life. The Patriarch loses his temper, strikes him three times on the head, claps his hands behind his back, and walks into the inner rooms — and Wukong alone reads the hidden message: come at the third watch, by the back door. There, in secret, the Patriarch passes him the wondrous Way of the Golden Elixir. Three years on he is warned of the Three Calamities — thunder, fire, wind — and given the seventy-two Earthly-Fiend transformations and the Somersault Cloud that covers a hundred and eight thousand miles in a single leap. A pine-tree transformation performed in jest gets him driven from the cave forever. He returns to the Water-Curtain Cave to find his troop bullied by a fiend, the Monstrous King of Havoc, and there he tests his new arts for the first time — including the Body-Outside-the-Body method, in which a single fistful of his own hair becomes two or three hundred little monkeys to swarm an enemy.*

## Six or seven years of sweeping

Now the Handsome Monkey King, having at last received both a surname and a religious name, was so overjoyed that he leapt and capered with delight, then bowed before the Patriarch Subodhi to give his thanks. The master at once ordered the assembled disciples to lead Sun Wukong out beyond the second gate, where they were to teach him how to sprinkle and sweep, how to answer when spoken to, and the proper manners of coming and going, advancing and withdrawing. The immortals obeyed and led him out. Once outside the gate, Wukong bowed again to all his elder brothers in the faith and arranged himself a sleeping place within the corridors of the cloister.

Early the next morning he set about learning speech and courtesy from his elder brethren, discussing the scriptures and debating the Way, practicing his brushwork and burning incense. So passed each day. In his idle hours he swept the grounds and hoed the garden, tended the flowers and pruned the trees, gathered firewood and tended the flames, drew water and carried it in buckets. Of all the chores that wanted doing, there was none he left undone. And so, scarcely noticing the passage of time, he dwelt in that cave for six or seven years.

## The lecture and the dance

One day the Patriarch mounted his lecture platform, took his lofty seat, and summoned all the immortals to hear him expound the Great Way. Truly it was a marvel:

> Heaven's blossoms came tumbling down,
> golden lotuses welled up from the earth.
> Subtle, he laid out the teaching of the Three Vehicles,
> in fine detail, the ten thousand methods complete.
> A slow wave of the whisk, and pearls and jade came spraying;
> the sound rolled like thunder, shaking the Ninefold Sky.
> A while he spoke of the Way, a while he discoursed on Chan —
> the three traditions joined as one, as is their nature.
> Open but a single word, and you grasp the principle of return and faith;
> follow his pointing finger, and you fathom the mystery of the deathless, deep nature.

Sun Wukong, listening from the side, was so delighted that he scratched his ears and rubbed his cheeks, his brows fairly blooming and his eyes laughing; he could not keep his hands from waving nor his feet from stamping in a dance. All at once the Patriarch caught sight of him and called out: *Sun Wukong! There you stand among the ranks — why do you leap and dance like a madman instead of listening to my lecture?*

*Your disciple was listening with all his heart,* said Wukong, *and when he reached the place where the old master's wondrous voice grew most marvelous, he was so beside himself with joy that, before he knew it, he fell into this capering. I beg the master's pardon.*

*Since you can recognize the wondrous voice,* said the Patriarch, *let me ask you this: how long have you been in this cave?*

*Your disciple was always a dull-witted creature,* said Wukong, *and cannot reckon how many seasons it has been. I only remember that whenever there was no fire under the stove, I would go off behind the mountain to gather kindling. There I found a fine hill of peach trees, and there I have eaten my fill of peaches seven times over.*

*That hill is called Ripe Peach Mountain,* said the Patriarch. *If you have eaten there seven times, then it must be seven years. And now what manner of the Way do you wish to learn from me?*

*That rests with my honored teacher's instruction,* said Wukong. *If there is but a breath of the Way's true essence in it, your disciple will learn it gladly.*

## Three hundred sixty side-doors

The Patriarch said: *Within the gate of this one word, 'Tao,' there are three hundred and sixty side-doors, and every side-door can lead to a proper fruition. I wonder — which of them would you study?*

*That is as my honored teacher pleases,* said Wukong. *Your disciple will listen and obey with his whole heart.*

*Suppose I teach you the Way that lies within the gate of the word 'Arts,'* said the Patriarch. *How would that suit you?*

*And what does the Way of the Arts gate mean?* asked Wukong.

*Within the gate of the Arts,* said the Patriarch, *are such things as summoning immortals and consulting the planchette, casting fortunes and divining with yarrow stalks — the lore by which one may know how to seek good luck and shun misfortune.*

*And by such means,* asked Wukong, *can one win eternal life?*

*No, no,* said the Patriarch.

*Then I won't learn it, I won't learn it,* said Wukong.

The Patriarch went on: *Suppose I teach you the Way within the gate of the word 'Schools.' How would that be?*

*And what is the principle within the Schools gate?* Wukong asked again.

*Within the gate of the Schools,* said the Patriarch, *are the Confucians and the Buddhists, the Taoists, the Yin-Yang masters, the Mohists and the Physicians — some who pore over the scriptures, some who chant the Buddha's name, and others who summon the true and call down the holy.*

*And by such means,* asked Wukong, *can one win eternal life?*

*If it's eternal life you want,* said the Patriarch, *then this is like setting a pillar inside a wall.*

*Master,* said Wukong, *I am a plain, honest fellow, and I don't follow the cant of the marketplace. What do you mean, 'setting a pillar inside a wall'?*

*When folk build a house,* said the Patriarch, *and want it to stand firm, they set an upright pillar in among the walls. But the day the great hall begins to topple, that pillar is sure to have rotted away.*

*By that account,* said Wukong, *it does not last long. I won't learn it, I won't learn it.*

The Patriarch said: *Suppose I teach you the Way within the gate of the word 'Stillness.' How about that?*

*And what proper fruition lies within the Stillness gate?* asked Wukong.

*This,* said the Patriarch, *is fasting from grain and guarding the body's emptiness, pure quietude and non-action, sitting in Chan meditation, holding silence and keeping abstinence; some practice their merit lying down, some standing up, along with entering trance and sealing oneself in retreat.*

*And can such practices win eternal life as well?* asked Wukong.

*They are like an unfired brick of clay on a kiln,* said the Patriarch.

Wukong laughed: *The master is full of riddles! Just now I told you I can't follow the marketplace cant, and here you go again. What do you mean, 'an unfired brick of clay on a kiln'?*

*It is just like a brick or tile of clay shaped upon the kiln,* said the Patriarch. *Though it has already taken its form, it has not yet been tempered by water and fire; and when one day the rain comes pouring down in torrents, it is sure to dissolve back into mud.*

*That does not last long either,* said Wukong. *I won't learn it, I won't learn it.*

The Patriarch said: *Suppose I teach you the Way within the gate of the word 'Action.' How about that?*

*And what then is the Way of the Action gate?* asked Wukong.

*This is the path of doing and contriving,* said the Patriarch: *drawing on the yin to nourish the yang, drawing the bow and treading the crossbow's lever, massaging the navel and circulating the breath, compounding elixirs by formula, firing the cauldron over a grass blaze, ingesting red lead and refining autumn stone, and such things as drinking a woman's milk.*

*And by such means can one win eternal life?* asked Wukong.

*To seek long life this way,* said the Patriarch, *is like fishing for the moon in the water.*

*There the master goes again,* said Wukong. *What do you mean, 'fishing for the moon in the water'?*

*The moon hangs in the vast sky,* said the Patriarch, *and its reflection lies in the water below. Though you can see it plainly, there is nothing there to grasp or seize; in the end it comes to nothing at all.*

*Then I won't learn that either, I won't learn it,* said Wukong.

## Three blows upon the head

When the Patriarch heard this, he gave a sharp cry — *Tcha!* — and sprang down from the high platform. Grasping his ruler-rod, he leveled it at Wukong and said: *You ape! This you won't learn and that you won't learn — what is it you're waiting for, then?* He strode forward and struck Wukong three times upon the head. Then, clasping his hands behind his back, he walked off into the inner quarters, shut the middle gate, and left the whole assembly standing there.

This so startled all those who had come to hear the lecture that every one of them was seized with fear, and they fell to scolding Wukong: *You wretched monkey! You have no manners at all. The master would have handed you the methods of the Way — why wouldn't you learn them? Why must you talk back to him? Now that you've provoked him, there's no telling when he'll come out again!* One and all they reviled him, heaping reproach upon him and despising him utterly. But Wukong was not the least bit vexed; his whole face was wreathed in smiles. For the truth was that the Monkey King had already cracked the riddle hidden in the master's words and kept it secret in his heart — and that was why he would not quarrel with the others, but held his peace in silence. The Patriarch's three blows upon his head meant: *be mindful at the hour of the third watch*; and the clasping of his hands behind his back as he walked into the inner rooms and shut the middle gate meant: *enter by the back door, where in secret the Way would be passed to him.*

## The hour of the Rat

That day Wukong made merry with the rest before the Cave of the Three Stars, watching the sky and impatient for the dark, which seemed as though it would never come. When dusk at last fell he went to bed with the others, but only feigned to close his eyes, steadying his breath and gathering his spirit. In the mountains there was no watchman's drum or water-clock to mark the hours, so he could not tell the time by any instrument; he only regulated the breath that passed in and out through his own nostrils. When it drew near the hour of the Rat, he rose ever so softly, put on his clothes, stealthily opened the front door, slipped away from the sleepers, and went out. He lifted his head and looked about, and this is what he saw:

> The moon shone bright, the clear dew cold,
> and to the eight far quarters, no speck of dust.
> Deep in the trees the hidden birds were roosting,
> and at its source the water purled and flowed.
> The flitting fireflies scattered their drifting glow,
> and passing wild geese ranged in script across the clouds.
> Just at the moment of the third watch's depth —
> the very hour to seek the truth of the Way.

Watch him now, following the old familiar path to the place behind the rear gate, where he found the door standing half open and half closed. Wukong rejoiced: *So the old master truly means to pass me the Way — that is why he has left the door ajar.* With long, quiet strides he drew near, turned himself sideways, and slipped inside, going straight on until he stood beneath the Patriarch's sleeping couch. There he saw the master lying curled up, asleep with his face to the wall. Wukong dared not disturb him, but knelt down before the couch and waited. Before long the Patriarch awoke, stretched out both his legs, and murmured to himself:

> *Hard! Hard! Hard! The Way is most profound —*
> *do not treat the Golden Elixir as a trifling thing.*
> *Unless you meet a perfected man to pass the wondrous secret,*
> *you weary your mouth with empty words and dry your tongue!*

At this Wukong answered aloud: *Master, your disciple has been kneeling here in waiting a long while.* The Patriarch, hearing that the voice was Wukong's, at once rose and pulled on his robe, sat cross-legged, and barked: *You ape! Why aren't you sleeping out in front? What are you doing here behind?*

*Master,* said Wukong, *yesterday at the lecture platform, before all the others, you promised your disciple that at the hour of the third watch you would pass me the Way by the back door. That is why I have made so bold as to come straight here and kneel beneath your couch.*

When the Patriarch heard this he was greatly pleased, and thought secretly to himself: *This creature is indeed something engendered by Heaven and Earth — how else could he have cracked the dark riddle hidden in my words?*

*Here there are no other ears to hear,* said Wukong, *none but your disciple alone. I beg the master in his great mercy to grant me the Way of eternal life, and I shall never forget the kindness.*

*You have an affinity now,* said the Patriarch, *and I too am glad to speak. Since you have read the hidden riddle, come closer and listen carefully, and I shall pass to you the wondrous Way of eternal life.*

## The oral formula of the Golden Elixir

Wukong kowtowed his thanks, washed out his ears, fixed his whole mind upon it, and knelt beneath the couch. The Patriarch chanted:

> The true and wondrous secret, manifest and hidden, perfect and all-pervading,
> guards nature and life — there is nothing else to say.
> It all comes down to essence, breath, and spirit:
> hold them firm, lock them deep, let nothing leak away.
> Let nothing leak away, but store it in the body,
> and the Way I pass to you will of itself grow great.
> Keep the oral formula in mind — much good it brings;
> banish lewd desire, and win the cool serenity.
> Win the cool serenity, the light pure and bright,
> and turn toward the elixir-terrace to savor the shining moon.
> The moon hides the jade hare, the sun hides the crow;
> of themselves the tortoise and serpent coil and entwine.
> Coiled and entwined, nature and life stand firm —
> and you can plant the golden lotus within the very fire.
> Gather and reverse the Five Phases in their working,
> and when the work is done, become a Buddha or an immortal as you will.

In that moment the very root and source were laid bare. Wukong, his spirit quickened and his good fortune arrived, fixed the formula firmly in his heart. He bowed to the Patriarch in deep gratitude, then went out by the back door and looked about him. The east was just beginning to pale with white, and along the western road a great golden light shone forth bright. Following the old path, he came round to the front door, pushed it softly open, went in, and sat down in his usual sleeping place. Then he deliberately shook the bedstead so that it rattled and called out: *It's light! It's light! Time to get up!* The rest were still fast asleep, never knowing that Wukong had won this fine thing. That day he went about and mingled with the others as usual, secretly nurturing his attainment, regulating his own breath morning and evening, before noon and after.

## The Three Calamities

In no time three more years had slipped by, and the Patriarch once again ascended his treasure-seat to expound the Dharma to the assembly. He spoke of the public cases and parables, and discoursed on the outer forms and the husks that wrap them. Suddenly he asked: *Where is Wukong?* Wukong came forward and knelt down: *Your disciple is here.*

*What sort of Way have you been cultivating all this while?* asked the Patriarch.

*Of late your disciple's spiritual nature has grown fairly clear,* said Wukong, *and the root and source are gradually growing firm.*

*Since you have penetrated the nature of the Dharma and grasped the root and source,* said the Patriarch, *and have already settled your spirit-body, you must now guard yourself against the perils of the Three Calamities.*

Hearing this, Wukong brooded for a long while, then said: *Master, surely your words are mistaken. I have always heard that one of lofty Way and abundant virtue is coeval with Heaven itself; that when water and fire are brought into harmony, no illness can arise. How then can there be these perils of the Three Calamities?*

*This,* said the Patriarch, *is no ordinary Way. It robs Heaven and Earth of their very work of creation; it usurps the hidden workings of the sun and moon. Once the elixir is achieved, ghosts and spirits cannot abide it. Though it keeps your youth and lengthens your years, yet after five hundred years Heaven will send down a thunder-calamity to strike you. You must see your nature and make your mind clear, and dodge it beforehand. If you can dodge it, your life span will equal Heaven's; if you cannot, there your life is cut off. After another five hundred years Heaven will send down a fire-calamity to burn you. This is no fire of Heaven, nor any common fire; it is called the yin-fire. It kindles up from the Bubbling Spring point beneath the soles of your own feet, drives straight up to the Niwan Palace in the crown of the head, reduces the five viscera to ashes and the four limbs to rot, so that a thousand years of bitter cultivation are all turned to illusion. After yet another five hundred years a wind-calamity will be sent down to blow upon you. This is no wind of east or south or west or north, no mild or scorching wind off the metal autumn, nor any wind through flowers and willows, pines and bamboo; it is called the Mighty Wind. It blows in through the crown of the head into the six bowels, passes the elixir-field and pierces the nine apertures; flesh and bone melt and disperse, and the body of itself comes undone. All of these, therefore, must be dodged.*

When Wukong heard this his hair stood on end and a shudder ran through him; he kowtowed and bowed, saying: *I beg the old master to take pity and pass me the method for dodging the Three Calamities. To the very end I shall never dare forget the kindness.*

*This is not so hard,* said the Patriarch. *Only, you are different from other men, and so it cannot be passed to you in the usual way.*

*But I too have a round head reaching up to Heaven and square feet treading the Earth,* said Wukong. *I have the same nine apertures and four limbs, the five viscera and six bowels. How am I different from other men?*

*Though you have the likeness of a man,* said the Patriarch, *you are short in the cheeks compared with others.* For the truth was that this monkey had a hollow-cheeked, pointed-jawed face, a sunken visage and a sharp snout. Wukong put up his hand, felt about his face, and laughed: *The master hasn't reckoned right. True, I'm short in the cheeks — but I have more than other men in this gullet-pouch of mine, and that may serve to make up the difference.*

## The Earthly Fiends and the Somersault Cloud

*Very well, then,* said the Patriarch. *Which sort would you learn? There is one set after the number of the Heavenly Ladle, which comprises thirty-six transformations; and there is one set after the number of the Earthly Fiends, which comprises seventy-two transformations.*

*Your disciple would rather cast a wider net,* said Wukong. *Let me learn the Earthly Fiends' transformations.*

*In that case,* said the Patriarch, *come forward and I shall pass you the oral formula.* Then he put his mouth to Wukong's ear and murmured low — who knows what wondrous method he spoke? But this Monkey King was one in whom, once a single aperture opened, all hundred apertures opened together; and there and then he learned the formula, cultivated and refined it himself, and mastered all seventy-two transformations entire.

Then one day the Patriarch and his disciples were amusing themselves before the Cave of the Three Stars, enjoying the cool of the evening. The Patriarch said: *Wukong, have you accomplished it yet or not?*

*By the master's boundless grace,* said Wukong, *your disciple's labor and fruit are complete, and I can already rise on the rosy mists and soar aloft.*

*Try flying up for me to see,* said the Patriarch.

Wukong displayed his skill: he hunched his body, gave a tumbling somersault, sprang up some fifty or sixty feet from the ground, trod the clouds and mists for about the time it takes to eat a meal, came and went, and covered not quite three miles before dropping down again before them. He folded his hands and said: *Master, that is what they call soaring on the clouds!*

The Patriarch laughed: *That can't be counted as cloud-soaring — it can only be called cloud-crawling. As the old saying goes: 'An immortal roams the Northern Sea at dawn and reaches Cangwu by dusk.' But you, in half a day, have gone not even three miles; that can't even be reckoned as cloud-crawling.*

*What does it mean,* asked Wukong, *to 'roam the Northern Sea at dawn and reach Cangwu by dusk'?*

*Those who soar upon the clouds,* said the Patriarch, *rise in the early morning from the Northern Sea, roam across the Eastern Sea, the Western Sea, and the Southern Sea, and come round again to Cangwu. Now Cangwu is the common name for Lingling on the Northern Sea. To traverse the whole expanse beyond the Four Seas in a single day — that may be reckoned as true cloud-soaring.*

*This is hard indeed, hard indeed,* said Wukong.

*In all the world there is no thing too hard,* said the Patriarch; *only fear a man of resolve.*

When Wukong heard these words he kowtowed and bowed, and said: *Master, when one does a thing for another, one should see it through to the end. Of your great mercy, be generous and pass me this method of cloud-soaring as well, and I shall surely never dare forget the kindness.*

*When all the immortals soar upon the clouds,* said the Patriarch, *they rise by stamping off their feet — but you are not like that. Just now I watched you go: you had to scramble up by tumbling somersaults. Working from this very posture of yours, I shall pass you the Somersault Cloud.* Wukong bowed again and begged earnestly, and the Patriarch passed him this further formula: *For this cloud, make the secret hand-sign, recite the true words, clench your fists tight, give your body a shake, and leap up — and with one somersault you'll cover a hundred and eight thousand miles.*

When the assembly heard this, every one of them tittered and laughed: *What luck for Wukong! If he can work this trick, he could hire himself out as a courier-runner, carrying documents and delivering dispatches — wherever he went, he'd never want for a meal.* Master and disciples then dispersed, each to his own quarters, as the sky grew dark.

That night Wukong worked his spirit and refined the method, and mastered the Somersault Cloud. From then on, day by day, he was free of all restraint, at ease and roaming as he pleased — and this too was one of the joys of eternal life.

## The pine-tree transformation

One day, when spring was passing into summer, the assembly had been gathered a long while beneath the pine trees, discoursing together. They said to him: *Wukong, what affinity from a former life had you cultivated to deserve this? The other day the old master put his mouth to your ear and whispered low, and passed you the methods for dodging the Three Calamities and the transformations. Have you really mastered them all?*

Wukong laughed: *I'll not deceive you, elder brothers. For one thing it was the master's teaching, and for another it was my own diligence night and day — and I have mastered every one of those several skills.*

*Now is a fine time,* said the assembly. *Try giving us a demonstration — let us have a look.*

Hearing this, Wukong roused his spirit and prepared to show off his arts: *Elder brothers, set me a subject. What would you have me change into?*

*Change into a pine tree, then,* they said. Wukong made the secret sign, recited the spell, gave his body a shake, and changed into a pine tree. Truly it was:

> Lush and dense, breathing mist through all four seasons,
> rising straight above the clouds in a fine, upright form.
> Not the least trace of the goblin-monkey about it —
> nothing but boughs that have braved the frost and borne the snow.

When the assembly saw it they clapped their hands and roared with laughter, all crying out: *What a fine monkey! What a fine monkey!* Their clamor and uproar, before they knew it, disturbed the Patriarch. He came hurrying out of his door, dragging his staff, and demanded: *Who is making this racket here?* At his call the assembly hastily checked themselves, straightened their robes, and stepped forward. Wukong too resumed his true form and, lost among the crowd, said: *May it please the honored master: we were gathered here in discourse, and there were no outsiders making any noise.*

The Patriarch shouted in anger: *Bawling and shouting like this — you do not in the least resemble men of cultivation! A man who cultivates the Way, when he opens his mouth, lets his spirit-breath disperse, and when he stirs his tongue, breeds right and wrong. How is it you are roaring with laughter here?*

*We dare not deceive the master,* said the assembly. *Just now Sun Wukong was performing his transformations for sport. We told him to change into a pine tree, and a real pine tree it was. Your disciples were all praising and applauding him, and so our loud voices startled and offended the honored master. We beg your pardon.*

*Off with you, all of you,* said the Patriarch. Then he called: *Wukong, come here! What sort of spirit were you working? What manner of pine tree were you changing into? Is this a feat to be showing off in front of others? Suppose you saw someone else with such a skill — wouldn't you beg him for it? And when others see that you have it, they will surely beg it of you. If you fear the harm that would follow, you will have to teach them; and if you do not teach them, they will surely do you injury — and then you cannot keep your own life safe.*

Wukong kowtowed and said: *I only beg the master to pardon me.*

*I lay no blame on you,* said the Patriarch, *but you must simply go.*

When Wukong heard these words, his eyes brimmed with tears. *Master, where would you have me go?*

*Wherever you came from,* said the Patriarch, *back there you must go.*

All at once Wukong understood, and said: *I came from the Water Curtain Cave on the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit, in the Aolai Country, in the Eastern Continent of Superior Body.*

*Hurry back there,* said the Patriarch, *and preserve your life. To remain here is by no means to be permitted.*

Wukong accepted his fault and pleaded with the honored master: *I have been away from home a full twenty years now. Though I long indeed to look upon my children and grandchildren of old, yet when I think how I have not yet repaid the master's deep kindness, I dare not go.*

*What kindness or obligation is there to speak of?* said the Patriarch. *Only see that you stir up no trouble and do not drag my name into it — that is all I ask.*

Seeing there was no help for it, Wukong could only bow his farewell and take his leave of the assembly. The Patriarch said: *On this going of yours, you are bound to come to no good. Whatever trouble you stir up or evil you commit, you are forbidden to say that you are my disciple. Let half a word of it out, and I shall know it at once — and I will flay this ape's hide and grind his bones, and banish his spirit-soul to the Nine Hells, so that through ten thousand ages he shall never turn over again!*

*I will never dare to breathe a word of the master,* said Wukong. *I'll only say that I learned it all by myself, and there's an end of it.*

## The Somersault Cloud home

Wukong gave his thanks and then drew himself together, made the secret sign, gave a tumbling spring, mounted the Somersault Cloud, and headed straight back to the Eastern Continent. In less than an hour he could already see the Water Curtain Cave on the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit. The Handsome Monkey King, glad in his own heart, said to himself in secret:

> When I set out, my mortal bones and frame were heavy;
> now that I've won the Way, my body's light, my whole self light.
> In all the world there's none who'll set his will to it —
> set your will to cultivate the mystery, and the mystery clears of itself.
> When I went out, the waves were hard to press across;
> today, returning, how easy the journey is!
> The parting words of admonition still ring in my ears —
> who would have thought I'd see the Eastern Sea in a single breath?

Wukong pressed down his cloud-head and came straight to the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit, picking his way along. Suddenly he heard the cry of cranes and the wail of apes — the cranes' cries piercing beyond the clouds of heaven, the apes' wailing mournful and full of grief. At once he called out: *Children, I have come home!* From beneath the cliffs and rock-clefts, out of the flowers and grasses and the trees, monkeys great and small came leaping out by the thousands and tens of thousands, surrounding the Handsome Monkey King in their midst. They kowtowed and cried: *Great King! How carefree you must have been! How could you stay away so long? You left us stranded here, longing for you as for food and drink. Lately we have been bullied and oppressed by a fiend who insists on seizing our Water Curtain Cave for his own dwelling. We have fought him to the death, heedless of our lives. All this while that wretch has plundered our household goods and carried off many of our young, so that day and night we cannot sleep but must keep watch over our home. Happily the Great King has come! Had the Great King stayed away another year or two, we and the whole mountain cave would have fallen into another's hands!*

When Wukong heard this his heart blazed with rage. *What fiend is this, that dares behave so outrageously? Tell me the whole of it, and I'll go and hunt him down to avenge you.*

The monkeys kowtowed. *We report to the Great King: the wretch calls himself the Monstrous King of Havoc, and dwells away to the due north.*

*How far is it from here to his place?* asked Wukong.

*When he comes, it's on cloud,* said the monkeys; *when he goes, it's on mist — now wind, now rain, now lightning, now thunder. We have no idea how far it is.*

*If that's so,* said Wukong, *then never fear; just go on with your play, and let me go and hunt him out.*

## The Monstrous King of Havoc

Splendid Monkey King! He drew his body together, leapt up and away, and went tumbling somersault after somersault straight to the north to look about him. There he saw a high mountain, truly steep and perilous in the extreme. A fine mountain:

> Brush-tip peaks standing sheer, winding ravines sunk deep.
> The brush-tip peaks stand sheer, piercing the empty sky;
> the winding ravines sink deep, leading down to Earth's own gate.
> On the two cliffs, flowers and trees vie in their splendor;
> in many a place, pine and bamboo contend in their green.
> On the left, the dragons — tame and docile;
> on the right, the tigers — crouching low and still.
> Here one often sees the iron ox at plough,
> and there are ever golden coins for the planting.
> The hidden birds sing sweet and clear;
> the cinnabar phoenix stands facing the sun.
> The rocks lie jagged, the waters lie clear —
> strange and uncanny, fierce and forbidding indeed.
> Famous mountains in the world are past all counting,
> where the flowers bloom and fade and crowd again in throngs;
> but how can they match this scene that endures forever,
> through the eight festivals and four seasons utterly unmoved?
> Truly it is the Source-Pit Mountain of the Three Realms,
> the nourishing Water-Viscera Cave of the Five Phases.

The Handsome Monkey King was silently taking in the scenery when he heard the sound of someone speaking, and went straight down the mountain to seek it out. There, before the sheer cliff, was the Water-Viscera Cave. Outside its gate several little imps were dancing about; the moment they saw Wukong they took to their heels. *Stop!* said Wukong. *Lend me the speech of your mouths, that I may carry word of what's in my heart. I am the Lord of the Water Curtain Cave on the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit, in the due south. What is this Monstrous King of Havoc of yours, who has so many times bullied my children and grandchildren? I have come on purpose to seek him out and settle accounts with him face to face.*

When the little imps heard this they rushed pell-mell into the cave to report: *Great King! Disaster!*

*What disaster?* asked the Fiend King.

*Outside the cave,* said the imps, *there's a monkey-head who calls himself the Lord of the Water Curtain Cave on the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit. He says you have bullied his children and grandchildren time and again, and he's come on purpose to seek you out and have it out with you face to face.*

The Fiend King laughed. *I've often heard those monkey-spirits say they had a Great King who'd gone off to leave home and cultivate the Way. So he's come back at last. How is he got up? What weapons does he carry?*

*He has no weapon at all,* said the imps. *His head is bare; he wears a single robe of red, with a yellow sash bound about him, and on his feet a pair of black boots. Neither monk nor layman, and not like a Taoist or an immortal either — bare-handed and empty-fisted, he's shouting outside the gate.*

When the Fiend King heard this he said: *Fetch me my armor and weapons.* The little imps brought them out at once.

The Fiend King donned his armor and helmet, grasped his saber in his hand, and came out the gate with his horde of imps. At once he cried out in a loud voice: *Which of you is the Lord of the Water Curtain Cave?* Wukong opened his eyes wide and looked, and beheld the Fiend King:

> On his head an iron-black helmet, gleaming in the sun;
> over his body a black gauze robe, fluttering in the wind.
> Below he wore black iron armor, bound tight with leather thongs;
> on his feet, patterned pleated boots — mighty as a great commander.
> Round the waist, ten spans broad; in stature, three rods tall.
> In his hand he gripped a saber, its edge most keen and bright.
> Styled the Monster of Havoc — a bold and savage figure.

## Body-Outside-the-Body

The Monkey King roared: *You wretched fiend, with eyes so big — and yet you can't see Old Monkey!*

The Fiend King looked at him and laughed: *You're not four feet tall, you're not thirty years old, and you've not so much as a weapon in your hand. How dare you be so wild and reckless as to come seeking me out to have it out?*

Wukong cursed him: *You wretched fiend, so you're blind after all! You reckon me small, but to make myself big is no hard thing. You reckon me without a weapon, but these two hands of mine can hook down the moon at the sky's edge! Never fear — just take a punch from Old Monkey!* With a spring he leapt up and struck straight at the fiend's face. The Fiend King put up his hand to ward it off and said: *You're such a runt, and I'm so big and tall; if you mean to use your fists while I mean to use my saber, cutting you down with my blade would only set folk laughing. Let me lay down the saber and try a round of fists with you.*

*Well said,* replied Wukong. *Now there's a brave fellow — come on, then!* The Fiend King let go his guard and struck out, and Wukong bored straight in, grappling and trading blows. The two of them punched and kicked, charging and ramming. A long reach is hollow and unwieldy, but short clinching blows are firm and sure. The Fiend King found Wukong digging at his short ribs and ramming into his groin, and after a few cracks at his joints he was badly battered. He dodged aside, snatched up his broad steel saber, and slashed straight down at Wukong's head. Wukong whipped his body away, and the stroke cut empty air. Seeing how fierce the fiend was, Wukong now used the Body-Outside-the-Body method: he plucked a fistful of hairs, threw them into his mouth and chewed them to bits, then spat them out into the air with a cry of *Change!* At once they became two or three hundred little monkeys, clustering all around.

For when one attains the immortal body, his spirit issues forth in transformations without measure. Now since this Monkey King had won the Way, there were upon his body eighty-four thousand hairs, and every single one of them could transform, answering to the need at the bidding of his mind. Those little monkeys, sharp-eyed and nimble at leaping, dodged the saber when it came and could not be wounded by the spear when it thrust. Watch them now, springing forward and leaping back, boring in and swarming up, surrounding the Fiend King: some hugged him, some hauled at him, some ducked into his groin, some grabbed at his legs, kicking and pummeling, plucking his hairs, gouging at his eyes, pinching his nose, hoisting him up and tumbling him about, beating him into one writhing heap. In that moment Wukong managed to wrest away the fiend's saber, parted his little monkeys, and brought it down full on the crown of the fiend's head, cleaving him into two halves. Then he led his band charging into the cave and wiped out every last fiend, great and small. He shook his hairs and gathered them back onto his body — and then he saw that some could not be gathered back, and these were the little monkeys the Fiend King had carried off from the Water Curtain Cave.

## The whole nest of Suns

*How did you come to be here?* asked Wukong. There were some thirty to fifty of them, and they all said through their tears: *After the Great King went off to cultivate immortality, that wretch made trouble these past two years and snatched us all away here. And aren't these the household goods from our own cave? Our stone basins and stone bowls were all carried off by him.*

*If they are our household goods,* said Wukong, *then move them all out.* Then he set a fire blazing in the cave and burned the Water-Viscera Cave dry to a cinder, reducing it all to ruin. He said to them: *All of you, follow me home.*

*Great King,* said the monkeys, *when we came, we only heard the wind whistling in our ears, and we drifted hazily to this place; we never learned the road. How are we to get home again?*

*That's just a magic art he worked,* said Wukong. *What's hard about it? Now that one aperture in me is open, all hundred apertures are open, and I too can work it. Close your eyes, all of you, and don't be afraid.*

Splendid Monkey King! He recited the words of a spell, summoned a wild gale, and brought his cloud-head down to earth. *Children, open your eyes,* he called. The monkeys set their feet on solid ground, recognized it for their own homeland, and every one of them rejoiced, all racing for the old path to the cave-mouth. The monkeys that had stayed in the cave came crowding out together to meet them, and they all went in, ranked themselves in order of age, and bowed in homage to the Monkey King. They set out wine and fruit to welcome him back and offer congratulations, and asked about the matter of subduing the fiend and rescuing their young. Wukong told them the whole of it in full detail, and the monkeys praised him without end, saying: *Great King, who would have thought that, going off to that far place, you would return with such powers!*

Then Wukong said: *When I parted from you in those days, I followed the waves and drifted with the current, blown across the great Eastern Ocean to the borders of the Southern Continent of Jambu. There I learned to take on human form, put on these clothes and wore these boots, and swaggered and roamed about the clouds for eight or nine years and more, yet never found the Way. Then I crossed the great Western Ocean to the borders of the Western Continent of Cattle-Gift, and after long inquiry I had the good fortune to meet an old patriarch, who passed me the true and fruitful art of being coeval with Heaven, the great gateway of deathless eternal life.*

The monkeys offered their congratulations, all saying: *Such fortune is not to be met with in ten thousand ages!*

Wukong laughed again: *Little ones, here's another happy thing — every one of this clan of ours now has a surname.*

*What is the Great King's surname?* asked the monkeys.

*My surname now is Sun,* said Wukong, *and my religious name is Wukong.*

When the monkeys heard this they clapped their hands in delight, crying: *The Great King is the Elder Sun, and we are all the second Suns, the third Suns, the little Suns, the small Suns — a whole family of Suns, a whole nation of Suns, a whole nest of Suns!* And they all came forward to wait upon the Elder Sun, with great basins and little bowls of coconut wine and grape wine, immortal flowers and immortal fruits — truly the whole household made merry together. Ah!

> Threaded through into a single surname, the self returns to its root,
> awaiting only the glorious rise — a name on the immortal rolls.

But after all, there is no knowing how it will all turn out, nor how his sojourn in this realm will end from first to last. Listen to the explanation in the next chapter.
