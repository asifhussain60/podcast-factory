# The Great Sage Plunders the Peaches and Steals the Elixir

*Idle in his new mansion beside the Garden of Immortal Peaches, the Great Sage Equal to Heaven swears brotherhood with every star and constellation of the upper realm, until the Perfected Man Xu Jingyang warns the Jade Emperor that an unoccupied monkey-spirit will breed mischief. The Jade Emperor sets him over the Peach Garden as warden — three thousand six hundred trees, ripening once in three thousand, six thousand, and nine thousand years — and Wukong, alone among the leaves, eats his fill of the choicest fruit. When the Queen Mother sends her seven robed maidens to gather peaches for the grand Festival of Immortal Peaches, they find him shrunk to a two-inch manikin asleep at the very top of a tree; the jolt of a plucked branch wakes him in fury. He fixes the maidens where they stand with a body-binding spell, gulls the Barefoot Great Immortal away from the feast with a counterfeit summons to the Hall of Universal Brightness, takes the Immortal's likeness, and steals into the empty Jade Pool. There he conjures sleep-insects upon the wine-officers, gorges on the hundred-flavored delicacies, drinks deep of the jade-fluid and jasper-nectar, and — staggering homeward — blunders by mistake into the Tushita Palace, where he crunches down all five gourds of Lord Lao Tzu's refined golden elixir like roasted beans. Sobered by the elixir and certain of doom, he slips through the Western Gate by invisibility and goes down to Flower-Fruit Mountain, where he tells the whole tale to the Four Mighty Generals and the seventy-two cave-kings and brings back stolen jars of immortal wine for his clan. The seven maidens, freed at last from the spell, report to the Queen Mother; the wine-officers report the plundered feast; Lord Lao reports the stolen elixir; the Barefoot Immortal reports the gulling; and the Inspecting Numinous Officer traces the whole disaster to the Great Sage. The Jade Emperor, sore wroth, musters the Four Great Heavenly Kings, Pagoda-Bearing Heavenly King Li, Prince Nezha, the Nine Luminaries, the Twenty-Eight Constellations, and a hundred thousand heavenly soldiers to spread the eighteen frames of heaven-and-earth net about the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit. The Nine Luminaries are beaten back; the Four Heavenly Kings and the Twenty-Eight Constellations join battle from the hour of the Dragon until sundown, and the seventy-two cave-kings are taken captive; but Wukong, plucking a handful of hairs and crying "Change!", scatters a thousand and a hundred Great Sages across the field and routes the heavenly princes into their evening camp. The cave-mouth holds; tomorrow's battle waits for the dawn.*

## An Idler Among the Stars

Now then — for all his grand title, the Great Sage Equal to Heaven was at bottom nothing but a monkey-spirit. He neither understood the ranks and gradations of office nor cared whether his stipend ran high or low; so long as his name was on the rolls, he was content. In the offices beneath his Equal-to-Heaven residence the two bureaus of immortal clerks — the Bureau of Peace and Quiet and the Bureau of Tranquil Spirit — waited upon him morning and night, while he himself knew only this: three meals by day, one bed by night, no cares to entangle him, free and at ease in every way.

In his idle hours he visited his friends and roamed the celestial palaces, forging bonds and swearing brotherhoods. When he met the Three Pure Ones he addressed them as *Venerable*; when he encountered the Four Emperors he said *Your Majesty*. With the Nine Luminaries, the Generals of the Five Quarters, the Twenty-Eight Constellations, the Four Great Heavenly Kings, the Twelve Branches of Time, the Five Elders of the Five Regions, and all the starry hosts of heaven and the divinities of the River of Stars — with every one of them he stood as brother to brother, hailing them all as equals. Today he wandered east, tomorrow he roamed west, drifting with the clouds, coming and going as the whim took him, his comings and goings never fixed.

One day, as the Jade Emperor held his morning audience, the Perfected Man Xu Jingyang stepped forth from the ranks, bowed low, and made his report. *This Great Sage Equal to Heaven, having nothing to occupy him, idles his days away and consorts with all the stars and constellations of heaven, calling high and low alike his friend. I fear that, with nothing to do, he will one day breed mischief in his idleness. It would be better to set him over some single charge, so that no other trouble may arise.*

The Jade Emperor heard this and at once issued a summons. The Monkey King came tripping in, all delight, and said, *Your Majesty — for what advancement or reward have you summoned Old Monkey?*

*Seeing that you are idle and unoccupied,* said the Jade Emperor, *I give you a duty. Take charge for the present of the Garden of Immortal Peaches, and mind it well, morning and evening.*

The Great Sage, overjoyed, gave thanks for the favor, made his bow before the throne, and withdrew. He could scarcely wait, and at once went into the Peach Garden to inspect it.

## The Garden of Three Thousand Six Hundred Trees

At the garden gate a local earth-god barred his way and asked, *Where does the Great Sage go?*

*I am sent by the decree of the Jade Emperor,* said the Great Sage, *to take charge of the Peach Garden, and I have come now to inspect it.*

At once the earth-god made his bow and summoned the whole company — the laborers who hoed the roots, the laborers who carried water, the laborers who tended the trees, the laborers who swept the grounds — to come and kowtow before the Great Sage, and they led him within. And there he beheld:

> Luxuriant and glowing, bough on bough, tree upon tree.
> Luxuriant and glowing, the blossoms crowd the branches;
> bough upon bough, the fruit weighs heavy on the limbs.
> The fruit upon the boughs hangs down like brocade pellets;
> the blossoms on the trees are clustered thick as rouge.
> Now flowering, now fruiting, ripened in a thousand years —
> without a summer, without a winter, slow-maturing through ten thousand ages.
> The early-ripened wear a flushed and wine-drunk face;
> the still-green keep their stems and pale unripened skins.
> The misty flesh shows green beneath;
> against the sun they bare their crimson glory.
> Beneath the trees rare flowers and strange herbs,
> through all four seasons never fading, every hue in even rank;
> to left and right, the towers and halls,
> their heights forever veiled in cloud and rainbow.
> No common stock of the mortal world is this —
> the Queen Mother of the Jade Pool planted it with her own hand.

The Great Sage gazed and reveled long, then asked the earth-god, *How many trees are there in this garden?*

*There are three thousand six hundred,* said the earth-god. *The twelve hundred in front bear small blossoms and little fruit; they ripen once in three thousand years, and a man who eats of them becomes an immortal and attains the Way, his body hale, his frame made light. The twelve hundred in the middle bear layered blossoms and sweet fruit; they ripen once in six thousand years, and a man who eats of them ascends in rosy mists and flies aloft, deathless and unaging. The twelve hundred behind bear fruit with purple veins and pale yellow stones; they ripen once in nine thousand years, and a man who eats of them lives as long as heaven and earth, of one age with the sun and moon.*

Hearing this, the Great Sage was beside himself with joy. That very day he made his count of the trees, looked over the pavilions and lodges, and returned to his residence. From that time on he came every three or five days to enjoy the sight, and he neither sought out his friends nor wandered elsewhere.

## A Manikin Among the Leaves

One day he saw that on the boughs of the older trees the peaches were more than half ripe. He had a mind to taste a few and try the new fruit — but the earth-god, the laborers, and the immortal clerks of his own residence followed him too closely for comfort. Of a sudden he hit upon a scheme.

*Step outside the gate, all of you, and wait there,* he said. *Let me rest a little while upon this pavilion.*

The whole company duly withdrew. Then the Monkey King slipped off his cap and robes, climbed one of the great trees, picked out the largest and ripest peaches, plucked a great many, and there among the branches ate his fill at his leisure. When he had stuffed himself, he leapt down, set his cap on, donned his robes, and called his attendants and retinue back to the residence. And two or three days later he went again and contrived to steal the peaches, indulging himself to his heart's content.

Now one morning the Queen Mother prepared a banquet, threw open her precious halls, and made ready in the Jade Pool the grand Festival of Immortal Peaches. She sent the immortal maidens — robed in red, in green, in white, in black, in purple, in yellow, and in blue, seven maidens each bearing a flower-basket — to the Peach Garden to pluck the peaches and lay the feast. The seven-robed maidens came straight to the garden gate, and there they found the earth-god, the laborers, and the two bureaus of immortal clerks from the Equal-to-Heaven residence all keeping watch at the gate.

The maidens drew near and said, *By the gracious command of the Queen Mother, we have come to pluck the peaches for the banquet.*

*Wait, fair immortals,* said the earth-god. *This year is not as years gone by. The Jade Emperor has appointed the Great Sage Equal to Heaven to oversee this place, and we must inform him before we dare open the garden.*

*Where is the Great Sage?* asked the maidens.

*He is within the garden,* said the earth-god. *Being weary, he has gone off by himself to sleep upon the pavilion.*

*If that is so,* said the maidens, *let us go and find him without delay.*

The earth-god went in with them. They searched all the way to the flower-pavilion and saw no one — only his cap and robes lying there, with no telling where he had gone; and search as they might in every quarter, there was no finding him. For the truth was that the Great Sage, after sporting awhile and eating his fill of peaches, had changed himself into a little manikin no more than two inches tall and fallen asleep beneath the dense leaves at the very top of one of the great trees.

*We came at the Queen Mother's command,* said the seven-robed maidens, *and we cannot find the Great Sage. How dare we go back empty-handed?*

An immortal attendant standing nearby said, *Since you fair maidens come by command, you need not hesitate. Our Great Sage is much given to idle wandering and has likely gone out of the garden to visit his friends. Go and pluck your peaches; we will answer for you.*

The maidens did as he said and went down among the trees to pluck. From the front trees they took two baskets; from the middle trees, three; but when they came to the rear trees they found the blossoms and fruit grown sparse, with only a few green-skinned, hairy-stemmed peaches remaining. For all the ripe ones the Monkey King had already eaten. The seven maidens looked this way and that, and saw on a southward bough but a single peach, half red and half white. The green-robed maiden pulled the branch down with her hand, the red-robed maiden plucked the fruit, and then they let the branch spring back.

Now it so happened that the Great Sage, transformed, lay sleeping upon that very branch, and the jolt woke him. At once he resumed his true form, drew the gold-banded cudgel from within his ear, shook it until it grew thick as a bowl, and roared out, *What sort of monsters are you, that you dare so boldly to steal and pluck my peaches?*

Terrified, the seven maidens fell to their knees together and cried, *Stay your wrath, Great Sage! We are no monsters, but the seven-robed maidens sent by the Queen Mother to gather the immortal peaches for the grand opening of her precious halls and the holding of the Festival of Immortal Peaches. We came here and first met the earth-god and the other spirits of this garden, but could not find the Great Sage. Fearing to be late, we could wait no longer, and so plucked the peaches here. We beg you to forgive our fault.*

At these words the Great Sage turned from anger to delight. *Rise, fair maidens,* he said. *This banquet the Queen Mother holds — who is invited?*

*The upper assembly has its old established custom,* said the maidens. *Those invited are the Buddha of the Western Heaven, the Bodhisattvas, the holy monks, the Arhats; the Guanyin of the South Pole in the south; the Sage Emperor of Exalted Grace in the east, and the immortal sages of the Ten Continents and Three Islands; the Mystic Numen of the North Pole in the north; and the Great Immortal of the Yellow Horn at the center — these being the Five Elders of the Five Quarters. There are also the Star Lords of the Five Bushels; in the upper Eight Caves the Three Pure Ones, the Four Emperors, the Grand Unity and the celestial immortals; in the middle Eight Caves the Jade Emperor, the Nine Mounds, and the gods of sea and peak; in the lower Eight Caves the Lord of the Dark Underworld and the earthbound immortals who watch over the world; and the great and lesser honored gods of every palace and every hall — all alike will attend the splendid Feast of Peaches.*

The Great Sage laughed and said, *And am I invited?*

*We have not heard you mentioned,* said the maidens.

*I am the Great Sage Equal to Heaven,* said he. *Why should Old Monkey not be invited to sit in the place of honor?*

*That is the old custom of the upper assembly,* said the maidens. *What may be done at this present feast, we cannot say.*

*You are right,* said the Great Sage, *and no blame to you. Stand here awhile and let Old Monkey go ahead and find out the news — whether or not he is to be invited.*

## Body-Fixing Spell and Barefoot Gull

Good Great Sage! He made the magic sign, recited a spell, and said to the maidens, *Stay! Stay! Stay!* This was a body-fixing spell, and it left the seven-robed maidens, every one of them, standing rooted beneath the peach trees, eyes glazed, blank-faced and stiff.

Then the Great Sage mounted an auspicious cloud, sprang out of the garden, and set off straight along the road to the Jade Pool. As he traveled, he saw, off to one side:

> Heaven all bright with auspicious haze, light wavering and aglow,
> five-colored clouds of blessing flying without cease.
> The white crane's cry resounds through the Nine Marshes;
> the purple fungus blooms in glory, parting a thousand leaves.
> And in their midst appears an immortal,
> his features heaven-formed, his bearing all his own.
> Spirit-dancing on the rainbow, he sweeps across the starry sky,
> at his waist a precious scroll, deathless and undying.
> The Barefoot Great Immortal is his name,
> come specially to the Peach Feast to add to the years of life.

The Barefoot Immortal came face to face with the Great Sage. The Great Sage lowered his head and laid his scheme: he meant to gull the true immortal so that he might go secretly to the feast in his stead, and so he asked, *Venerable Sir, where do you go?*

*By the Queen Mother's gracious invitation,* said the Immortal, *I go to the splendid Feast of Peaches.*

*Venerable Sir, you do not know,* said the Great Sage. *Because Old Monkey's somersault cloud is so swift, the Jade Emperor has charged me to go by all five roads and invite the worthies, that they may first perform the rites of ceremony below the Hall of Universal Brightness, and afterward go on to the feast.*

Now the Barefoot Immortal was an open and upright man, and he took the Sage's lie for truth. *In years past,* he said, *the rites of thanksgiving were performed at the Jade Pool itself. How is it that this year we first go to the Hall of Universal Brightness to perform the rites, and only then to the Jade Pool for the feast?* But there was no help for it, and so he turned his auspicious cloud about and made straight for the Hall of Universal Brightness.

The Great Sage, riding his cloud, recited a spell, shook himself, and changed into the very likeness of the Barefoot Immortal, then sped on to the Jade Pool.

## Plundering the Jade Pool

Before long he arrived at the precious halls, stayed his cloud, and softly, step by careful step, walked within. And there he saw:

> Rare incense curling, auspicious haze in profusion.
> The jasper terrace spread with colored knots,
> the precious halls awash in fragrant mist.
> Phoenixes soaring, simurghs in flight, their forms half-glimpsed;
> golden flowers and jade calyxes, their shadows floating, sinking.
> Set out above: the nine-phoenix screen of rosy cloud,
> the eight-jeweled stools of purple rainbow,
> the five-hued tables traced with gold,
> the thousand-flowered basins of green jade.
> Upon the tables, dragon-liver and phoenix-marrow,
> bear's paws and the lips of apes.
> Rare viands of a hundred flavors, each one delicious;
> strange fruits and choice morsels, each fresh in its own color.

Everything was laid out in perfect order — but not a single immortal had yet arrived.

The Great Sage had not taken in half of what there was to see when of a sudden a gust of wine-fragrance came stealing to his nose. He turned his head, and there beneath the long gallery on the right-hand wall were several wine-making immortal officers and dreg-turning laborers, leading a few water-carrying Daoists and fire-tending lads, washing the vats and scrubbing the jars — for they had already made the jade-fluid and jasper-nectar, the fragrant brew and the choicest vintage.

The Great Sage could not keep the drool from the corners of his mouth, and longed to go and drink — but all those people were there. So he worked a piece of magic: he plucked a few hairs, tossed them into his mouth, chewed them small, spat them out, and recited a spell, crying, *Change!* At once they became several sleep-insects, which flew onto the faces of the company. And look — that whole crew went limp at the hands, drooped at the head, knit the brow, and closed the eyes; dropping their charges, every one of them drifted off to doze.

Then the Great Sage helped himself to the hundred-flavored delicacies, the choice morsels and rare dishes, walked into the long gallery, and there beside the vats and against the jars he opened his gullet and drank deep — a long and hearty bout. When he had drunk a good long while, he was thoroughly fuddled with wine. He felt and fumbled at himself and said, *This is bad — this is bad! In a little while, when the invited guests arrive, will they not lay the blame on me? If I am caught here, how shall I fare? Better get home early and sleep it off.*

## Five Gourds of Cinnabar Elixir

Good Great Sage! Reeling and swaying, propped up by the wine, he blundered along just as the fancy took him. Soon he lost the road — and instead of arriving at the Equal-to-Heaven residence, he came to the Tushita Palace. The sight of it brought him to his senses with a start.

*The Tushita Palace stands above the Thirty-Three Heavens,* he said, *in the Heaven of Parted Sorrows, the dwelling of Lord Lao Tzu, the Supreme Patriarch. How have I blundered here? Well, no matter — I have long meant to call on this old gentleman and never managed it. Now that my idle steps have brought me, I may as well pay him a visit.*

So he straightened his clothes and barged in — but Lord Lao was nowhere to be seen, nor was there a soul in sight. For the truth was that Lord Lao and the Ancient Buddha Dipankara were expounding the Way upon the Vermilion Mound Cinnabar Terrace in the three-storied high hall, and all the immortal lads, immortal generals, immortal officers, and immortal clerks stood attending to left and right, listening. The Great Sage went straight into the elixir-chamber but, seek as he might, found no one. He saw only, beside the elixir-stove, the fire still glowing in the brazier; and to left and right of the stove stood five gourds, and within the gourds was all the refined golden elixir.

*This stuff is the supreme treasure of the immortal folk!* the Great Sage said in delight. *Ever since Old Monkey attained the Way, I have grasped the principle that inner and outer are one and the same, and I too have meant to refine some golden elixir to succor mankind, but never had the leisure once I got home. Today fortune favors me, and I have stumbled on the very thing. While the old gentleman is away, let me eat a few pellets and try them.*

So he tipped out all the gourds and ate the lot, crunching them down as one cracks roasted beans.

In a little while the elixir had cleared the wine from his head, and again he reckoned with himself. *This is bad — this is bad! This is a calamity bigger than the sky itself. If the Jade Emperor learns of it, my life is forfeit. Go — go — go! Better to go down to the world below and be a king there.*

So he ran out of the Tushita Palace, and not by the old road — slipping instead through the Western Heaven Gate by a spell of invisibility, he made his escape.

## Home to Flower-Fruit Mountain

He pressed down his cloud and returned to the bounds of the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit, where he saw banners flashing and halberds gleaming, for the Four Mighty Generals and the seventy-two cave-kings of monsters were drilling at their martial arts.

*My little ones, I am back!* the Great Sage called out in a loud voice. The whole horde of monsters threw down their weapons and fell to their knees. *How easy in heart the Great Sage is!* they cried. *You left us so long and never came to look in on us.*

*It was no long time,* said the Great Sage. *No long time at all.*

Talking and walking, he passed straight into the deep recesses of the cave-heaven. The Four Mighty Generals swept and made ready his resting place, kowtowed and paid their respects, and when that was done they all asked, *The Great Sage has been a hundred years and more in heaven. What office did you truly hold?*

The Great Sage laughed. *By my reckoning it was but half a year,* he said. *How can you speak of a hundred years and more?*

*One day in heaven,* said the Generals, *is a year in the world below.*

*It is well, then,* said the Great Sage, *that this time the Jade Emperor took such a liking to me. He did indeed enfeoff me as the Great Sage Equal to Heaven, raised up an Equal-to-Heaven residence for me, and set up the two bureaus of Tranquillity and Spirit-Repose, each with immortal clerks and guards. Later, seeing I had nothing to do, he set me to mind the Garden of Immortal Peaches. Now of late the Queen Mother held her grand Feast of Peaches and never invited me. So I did not wait for her invitation, but went first to the Jade Pool, where I stole and ate the whole of her immortal delicacies and immortal wine. Coming out of the Jade Pool, staggering and lurching, I blundered into Lord Lao's palace by mistake, and there I stole and ate the golden elixir from his five gourds as well. But fearing the Jade Emperor would charge me with the crime, I made my way out of the heavenly gate just now.*

At this the whole horde of monsters rejoiced greatly. At once they set out wine and fruit to welcome him, and brimmed a stone bowl with coconut wine and offered it up. The Great Sage took one sip and at once grimaced and bared his teeth. *It has no taste — no taste at all,* he said.

The two generals Beng and Ba said, *The Great Sage has been drinking immortal wine and eating immortal fare in the heavenly palace, so coconut wine is little to his liking. As the old saying has it, "Sweet or not sweet, it's the water of home."*

*And you,* said the Great Sage, *are "kin or not kin, they're the folk of home." When I was reveling this morning at the Jade Pool, I saw beneath the long gallery a great many bottles and jars, all full of jade-fluid and jasper-nectar — and none of you has ever so much as tasted it. Wait while I go and steal a few bottles back, and each of you shall drink half a cup, and every one of you shall be deathless and unaging.*

The monkeys were overjoyed beyond measure. The Great Sage went out of the cave-mouth, turned a somersault, and by a spell of invisibility went straight to the Peach Feast and into the palace of the Jade Pool. There he found the wine-makers, dreg-turners, water-carriers, and fire-tenders still snoring and not yet awake. He seized the largest jars, tucked two under his arms and carried two in his hands, then turned his cloud about and went back. Gathering the monkeys in the cave, he held a feast of immortal wine; each drank a few cups and they made merry — but of that we say no more.

## The Reports Come In

Now those seven-robed maidens, once the Great Sage had laid his body-fixing spell upon them, could not break free until a full day and night had gone round. Each took up her flower-basket and went back to report to the Queen Mother, saying, *The Great Sage Equal to Heaven worked his magic to bind us fast, and that is why we are so late in returning.*

*How many peaches did you pluck?* asked the Queen Mother.

*Only two baskets of small peaches and three of middling,* said the maidens. *When we came to the rear trees, there was not half a large peach to be found — all eaten, we think, by the Great Sage. And as we were searching, he himself came out and fell upon us in a fury, then asked who was invited to the feast. We told him the whole matter of the upper assembly, whereupon he fixed us where we stood and went off, none knows where. Only now have we come to ourselves and returned.*

When the Queen Mother heard this, she went at once to the Jade Emperor and laid the whole affair before him. Scarcely had she finished when the company of wine-makers came in with their immortal officers and reported: *Someone — we know not who — has thrown the grand Feast of Peaches into confusion and stolen and drunk the jade-fluid and jasper-nectar; the eight delicacies and hundred flavors have all been stolen and eaten as well.*

Then came four Grand Celestial Masters to report: *The Supreme Patriarch of the Way has arrived.* The Jade Emperor went out with the Queen Mother to greet him. When Lord Lao had finished his salutations, he said, *In this old Daoist's palace I had refined some Nine-Cycle Golden Elixir against the day Your Majesty should hold the Grand Elixir Assembly — but it has been stolen away by a thief, and I come expressly to make it known to Your Majesty.* The Jade Emperor heard the report with dismay.

In a little while the immortal clerks of the Equal-to-Heaven residence came kowtowing and said, *Sun the Great Sage has neglected his charge. Since yesterday he has gone off roaming and to this hour has not returned, and there is no knowing where he is.* This added still more to the Jade Emperor's misgivings.

Then the Barefoot Great Immortal bowed low and reported: *By the Queen Mother's summons, your servant went yesterday to attend the feast, and by chance met the Great Sage Equal to Heaven, who told me that Your Majesty had decreed he should invite us all to perform the rites of ceremony first at the Hall of Universal Brightness, and only then proceed to the feast. Trusting his word, your servant returned at once to the foot of the Hall of Universal Brightness, but saw nothing of Your Majesty's dragon-carriage and phoenix-chariot, and so hastened here to wait.*

The Jade Emperor was more alarmed than ever. *This wretch has counterfeited a decree and gulled our worthy minister!* he said. *Quick — set the Inspecting Numinous Officer to track down the trail of this wretch.*

The Numinous Officer received the command, went out from the hall, and made inquiry everywhere; and having learned the whole matter in full detail, he returned and reported: *He who has thrown the heavenly palace into confusion is none other than the Great Sage Equal to Heaven.* And he related the whole affair from beginning to end.

## A Hundred Thousand Heavenly Soldiers

The Jade Emperor was sore wroth, and at once dispatched the Four Great Heavenly Kings, together with the Pagoda-Bearing Heavenly King Li and Prince Nezha; and he mustered the Twenty-Eight Constellations, the Nine Luminary Star-Officers, the Twelve Branches of Time, the Revealers of the Five Quarters, the Four Duty-Gods of the Watches, the stars of east and west, the deities of south and north, the gods of the Five Sacred Peaks and the Four Great Rivers, and all the starry hosts of heaven — a hundred thousand heavenly soldiers in all — to spread eighteen frames of heaven-and-earth net and descend upon the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit, there to surround and besiege the wretch and seize him without fail for punishment.

The host of gods raised their army at once and departed the heavenly palace. And as they went, behold:

> Yellow wind rolling, rolling, darkens the very sky;
> purple fog surging, surging, blots the earth in gloom.
> All because a monkey-monster has flouted the High God,
> the assembled saints come down into the dust of the world.
> The Four Great Heavenly Kings, the Revealers of the Five Quarters:
> the Four Great Kings hold the supreme command,
> the Five Revealers marshal the many troops.
> Li, the Pagoda-Bearer, in the central host gives the signals;
> fierce Nezha leads the vanguard.
> Rahu the star takes the muster as chief;
> Ketu the star presses on towering behind.
> The star of the Moon's Essence is full of dash and vigor;
> the star of the Sun shines clear and bright.
> The stars of the Five Elements excel in valor;
> the Nine Luminary stars love best to strive.
> The Branch-stars of midnight, noon, dawn, and dusk —
> each and every one a mighty heaven-warrior.
> The Five Plagues and Five Peaks ranged east and west,
> the Six Ding and Six Jia marching left and right.
> The dragon-gods of the Four Rivers split above and below,
> the Twenty-Eight Constellations in dense and serried ranks.
> Horn, Neck, Root, and Room lead all the rest;
> Stride, Bond, Stomach, and Mane are practiced in the tumult.
> Dipper, Ox, Maid, Void, Roof, House, and Wall;
> Heart, Tail, and Basket — every star a champion.
> Well, Ghost, Willow, Star, Bow, Wing, and Axletree —
> brandishing spears and waving swords, they show their awesome might.
> Halting the clouds and lowering the mist, they came down to the mortal world,
> and pitched their camp before the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit.

A poem says:

> The heaven-born Monkey King, of myriad transformations,
> stole the elixir, stole the wine, made merry in his mountain den.
> Only because he threw the Peach Feast into confusion,
> a hundred thousand heavenly soldiers spread their net for him.

## The Nine Luminaries at the Iron-Plank Bridge

Now the Heavenly King Li gave his orders, bidding the host of heavenly soldiers strike camp and ring the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit so close that not a drop of water could leak through; above and below they spread the eighteen frames of heaven-and-earth net. Then he sent the Nine Luminaries, those fierce stars, out to give battle first.

The Nine Luminaries led their troops straight to the cave-mouth, where they saw monkeys great and small leaping and sporting at their play. The star-officers cried out in stern, loud voices: *You little fiends! Where is that Great Sage of yours? We are the heavenly gods dispatched from the realm above, come to subdue this rebel. Tell him to come quickly and submit — and if he so much as breathes a word of refusal, you shall all be put to the sword together.*

In alarm the little fiends rushed the word within: *Great Sage — disaster! Disaster! Outside there are nine fierce gods who call themselves heavenly gods sent down from the realm above, come to subdue the Great Sage.*

The Great Sage was just then sharing out the immortal wine with the seventy-two cave-kings and the Four Mighty Generals. Hearing this report, he paid it no heed at all. *Today there is wine, today get drunk,* he said. *Never mind what's right or wrong at the door.*

Scarcely had he spoken when another band of little fiends came leaping in: *Those nine fierce gods are at the door with their foul and abusive talk, hurling challenges to battle!*

The Great Sage laughed. *Pay them no mind,* he said. *With poems and wine I'll take my pleasure for today; as for rank and fame, ask not when they'll come to pass.*

But the words were hardly out of his mouth when yet another band of little fiends came running to report: *Father! Those nine fierce gods have broken down the door and are fighting their way in!*

The Great Sage flew into a rage. *These vile hairy gods! What gross effrontery! I had meant to take no quarrel with them — how dare they come to my very door to bully me?* At once he ordered the One-Horned Demon King: *Lead forth the seventy-two cave-kings to give battle. Old Monkey will follow after with the Four Mighty Generals.*

The Demon King swiftly led his fiend-soldiers out of the gate to meet the foe — but the Nine Luminaries fell upon them all together and pinned them down at the head of the iron-plank bridge, so that they could not break out.

In the thick of the uproar the Great Sage arrived, crying out, *Make way!* He whipped out his iron cudgel, shook it once till it was thick as a bowl and a good twelve feet long, threw himself into his stance, and came striking forth. Not one of the Nine Luminaries dared withstand him, and in a moment he beat them back.

The Nine Luminaries planted their battle-line and called out, *You reckless, death-defying Protector of the Horses! You have committed the ten unpardonable crimes — you stole the peaches, you stole the wine, you threw the grand Feast of Peaches into confusion, you filched Lord Lao's immortal elixir, and you have stolen the imperial wine to make merry here. Crime heaped upon crime — do you not know it?*

The Great Sage laughed. *These several matters — they are true, all true. But what of it now?*

*We bear the golden decree of the Jade Emperor,* said the Nine Luminaries, *and have led our host here to subdue you. Submit at once and spare these living creatures their lives — if not, we will trample this mountain flat and overturn this cave of yours!*

*What power have a pack of paltry hairy gods like you,* the Great Sage roared in fury, *that you dare utter such wild words? Stand and take a taste of Old Monkey's cudgel!*

The Nine Luminaries leapt forward all together — but the Handsome Monkey King, not in the least afraid, swung up his gold-banded cudgel, parrying left and warding right, and battered the Nine Luminaries till their sinews ached and their strength failed, and one by one they trailed their weapons and fled the field in rout, hastening into the central command-tent. There they said to the Pagoda-Bearing Heavenly King, *That Monkey King is fierce and brave indeed. We could not stand against him, and have come back beaten.*

## The Melee from the Hour of the Dragon

Then the Heavenly King Li sent forth the Four Great Heavenly Kings and the Twenty-Eight Constellations to march out in one body and give battle. And the Great Sage, plainly unafraid, called out the One-Horned Demon King, the seventy-two cave-kings, and his Four Mighty Generals, and drew them up in battle-line outside the cave-mouth. Behold this melee — a terrible sight indeed:

> Cold wind whistling, eerie fog hanging dark.
> On that side, banners flying their colors;
> on this side, halberds glittering bright.
> Helmets gleaming, rank on rank;
> armor flashing, fold on fold.
> Rank on rank of gleaming helmets caught the sun
> like silver chimes battering heaven;
> fold on fold of flashing armor walled the cliffs
> like icy peaks bearing down upon the earth.
> Great broadswords flew like cloud and lightning;
> white-tasseled spears pierced fog and cloud.
> Square-heaven halberds, tiger-eye whips, ranged thick as a hemp-grove;
> green bronze swords, four-bright shovels, set close as a forest.
> Curved bows and stiff crossbows, eagle-feathered arrows;
> short cudgels and serpent-lances that snatched away the soul.
> The Great Sage's single As-You-Will cudgel,
> turning and overturning, fought the gods of heaven.
> They battled till in the sky no bird flew over,
> and through the mountains tigers and wolves fled;
> flung sand and rolling stones blackened heaven and earth,
> whirling earth and flying dust darkened all the universe.
> Only the clash and clatter, startling sky and ground;
> the fierce and awful fury, shaking gods and ghosts.

This battle was arrayed from the hour of the Dragon, and the slaughter raged on until the sun went down behind the western hills. By then the One-Horned Demon King and the seventy-two cave-monsters had all been taken captive by the host of heavenly gods. Only the Four Mighty Generals and the troop of monkeys got away, hidden deep at the bottom of the Water-Curtain Cave.

The Great Sage, with his single cudgel, held off the Four Great Heavenly Gods, Li the Pagoda-Bearer, and Prince Nezha, all in the middle air; and after a long bout of slaughter, seeing that the day was drawing to its close, he plucked out a handful of his hairs, threw them into his mouth, chewed them small, spat them out, and cried, *Change!* At once they became a thousand and a hundred Great Sages, each one wielding a gold-banded cudgel, and they beat back Prince Nezha and routed the five heavenly kings.

## Three Sobs, Three Laughs

His victory won, the Great Sage gathered in his hairs and hastened back into the cave. There at the head of the iron-plank bridge the Four Mighty Generals met him, leading the host, and bowed in welcome; and the whole company wept three sobbing, choking sobs, and then laughed three loud, hearty laughs.

*You see me again,* said the Great Sage, *and you both weep and laugh — why is that?*

*This morning,* said the Four Mighty Generals, *when we led the host to do battle with the heavenly kings, the seventy-two cave-kings and the One-Horned Demon King were all taken captive by the host of gods, and we ourselves escaped only with our lives — and that is why we wept. Now that the Great Sage has returned victorious and unhurt, that is why we laughed.*

*Victory and defeat are the common lot of soldiers,* said the Great Sage. *The ancients say, "Slay ten thousand of the foe, and lose three thousand of your own." Besides, those captains who were carried off are creatures of the tiger and leopard, wolf and reptile kind — badgers and roebucks, foxes and raccoon-dogs — and of our own monkey-kin not one was harmed. Why should we fret? Though I beat them back with my body-dividing magic, they will still pitch camp at the foot of our mountain. Let us keep close watch and ward, eat our fill, sleep with easy hearts, and nurse our strength. Tomorrow at daybreak you shall see Old Monkey work a mighty magic, take these heavenly captains, and avenge our host.*

So the Four Generals and the troop of monkeys drank a few bowls of coconut wine and went to their rest with quiet minds — but of that we say no more.

As for the Four Great Heavenly Kings, they withdrew their troops and broke off the battle, and each reported his merits: some had taken tigers and leopards, some had taken lions and elephants, some had taken wolves and reptiles, foxes and raccoon-dogs — but not a single monkey-spirit had been caught. Then they pitched their barracks afresh and laid out a great encampment, rewarded the generals who had won merit, and gave their orders to the soldiers of the heaven-and-earth net; and each man shook his bell and called his watchword, ringing the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit about and waiting only for the great battle of the morrow. Each held to his command and kept strict guard at his post. Truly:

> The monkey-monster's rising shook both heaven and earth;
> they spread their net and kept their watch by day and night.

And how, after the dawn broke, the matter was dealt with — that you shall hear told in the next chapter.
