# The Four Seas and a Thousand Mountains Bow in Submission

*Back on the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit, the Handsome Monkey King drills his troop with bamboo spears and wooden blades until the worry strikes him that play may turn to earnest and no human king or Lord of Beasts will spare a band armed only with sticks. So he leaps to the city of Aolai on his Somersault Cloud, raises a sand-flinging wind to clear its streets, and helps himself to its entire armory by way of his body-dividing magic. Forty-seven thousand monkeys are mustered, and seventy-two caves of monster-kings on every quarter of the mountain swear allegiance and pay tribute. The common weapons still do not suit his hand, so the four old monkeys send him down beneath the iron-plank bridge to the Crystal Palace of Aoguang, Dragon King of the Eastern Sea. There he turns up the Heavenly-River-anchoring rod that the great Yu once used to measure the floods — a black-iron pillar bound in gold that shrinks and grows at his thought, the As-You-Will Gold-Banded Cudgel of thirteen thousand five hundred catties. He bullies the four Dragon Kings into a phoenix-winged purple-gold cap, chain-link golden armor, and cloud-walking shoes of lotus thread to match it. He swears brotherhood with six other demon-kings of the mountains, and one night, drunk after a banquet, his soul is dragged down to the Region of Darkness. Wukong wakes to himself there, smashes the soul-takers, climbs into the Hall of Judgment, and strikes the name of every monkey out of the Registers of Life and Death. Two memorials race up to the Jade Emperor — Aoguang of the Eastern Sea complaining of armed robbery beneath the waves, King Qinguang of the underworld pleading for divine troops to restore the order of birth and death. The Great White Planet of Venus proposes an edict of amnesty and recruitment instead — let the immortal be gathered in by the way of the Way — and descends to invite the Monkey King up to Heaven.*

## Bamboo spears and wooden blades

Now, the Handsome Monkey King had returned in glory to his native mountain. Having destroyed the Demon of Havoc and carried off a great broadsword, he spent his days drilling his followers in the arts of war. He set the little monkeys to cutting bamboo for spears and whittling wood for blades; they fashioned banners and pennants, blew whistles for signals, advanced and retreated on command, pitched camp and raised stockades, and amused themselves at this for a long while.

Then one day, sitting quietly, the King fell to brooding. *Here we are at our games,* he said to himself, *but I fear that play may turn to earnest. We may yet disturb some human king, or some Lord of Birds or Lord of Beasts may take offense and say that we are mustering troops in rebellion. They would raise an army and come to slay us — and with your bamboo poles and wooden swords, how could you meet such a foe? We must have keen swords and sharp halberds before we are safe. What is to be done?*

When the monkeys heard this, every one of them was alarmed. *Your Majesty sees far ahead,* they said, *but there is nowhere we can obtain such weapons.* As they spoke, four old monkeys stepped forward — two bare-rumped roan-apes and two long-armed gibbons — and came before him. *Your Majesty,* they said, *to procure sharp weapons is not so difficult a thing.* *How is it not difficult?* asked Wukong.

*East of our mountain,* answered the four, *across two hundred miles of water, lies the kingdom of Aolai. In that land there is a throne, and within its walls are soldiers and citizens beyond counting — surely there must be smiths who work in gold and silver, copper and iron. If Your Majesty were to go there, you might buy or commission whatever weapons you please; then you could train us properly and guard our mountain stronghold. Truly that would be the way to lasting peace and security.* Wukong was filled with delight. *You stay here at your sport,* he said, *while I go and return.*

## The wind upon Aolai

Good Monkey King! He leapt at once onto his somersault cloud and in an instant had crossed two hundred miles of water. There indeed he saw a walled city, with six avenues and three markets, ten thousand households and a thousand gates, the people coming and going freely beneath the open sky. Wukong thought to himself, *There must be ready-made weapons here. I could go down and buy a few — but it would be better still to use a little magic and simply help myself.*

So he made the magic sign and recited a spell, drew in a breath toward the southeast quarter, and blew it out in one great puff. At once a wind arose, flinging sand and rolling stones — a fearsome thing indeed:

> Where the cannon-clouds rise, heaven and earth are shaken;
> Black mists and gloomy fogs darken all the land.
> Waves churn the rivers and seas till fish and crabs cower;
> Trees snap in the mountain woods, and tigers and wolves take flight.
> In every trade no merchant or traveler stirs;
> In every walk of life not a soul is seen.
> The king upon his hall withdraws to his inner court;
> Before the steps, civil and martial officers scatter to their offices.
> The age-old treasure-thrones are all blown over,
> And the lofty Five-Phoenix Tower rocks to its very roots.

Where the wind blew, it scattered the king of Aolai in terror; through the three streets and six markets, every household slammed its gates and barred its doors, and no one dared walk abroad. Only then did Wukong bring down his cloud and stride straight in through the palace gates, making his way directly to the armory and weapons-stores. He threw open the doors and looked inside — arms beyond number met his eyes: sabers and spears, swords and halberds, axes and battle-axes, pikes and sickles, whips and rakes, bludgeons and maces, bows and crossbows, forks and lances — every kind complete.

Delighted at the sight, he said, *How many can one man carry? Better to use my body-dividing magic and move the lot.* Good Monkey King! He plucked out a handful of his hairs, chewed them to bits in his mouth, and spat them out, reciting a spell and crying, *Change!* At once they became hundreds upon hundreds of little monkeys, all snatching and grabbing in a great tumult — the strong ones taking five or seven pieces, the weaker ones two or three — until they had cleared the place out entirely. Then he mounted his cloud, worked a summoning spell, called up the wild wind, and led his little monkeys back to their home.

## Forty-seven thousand and seventy-two caves

Now the monkeys great and small of the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit were just then sporting outside the cave entrance when suddenly they heard the wind howling and saw the air filled with a dense, boundless swarm of monkey-spirits swooping and tumbling overhead. Frightened, they all scattered helter-skelter and hid. In a moment the Handsome Monkey King set down his cloud, gathered up the mists, shook his body, and recovered his hairs. The weapons he heaped in a great pile before the mountain and called out, *Little ones, come and take your arms!*

When the monkeys looked and saw Wukong standing alone on the open ground, they all came running to kowtow and ask what had happened. Wukong told them the whole story of how he had raised the wild wind and carried off the weapons. When the monkeys had finished thanking him, they all rushed to grab sabers and seize swords, to snatch axes and fight over spears, to draw bows and bend crossbows — shouting and bawling, they made sport of it the whole day long.

The next day they formed up the camp as before. Wukong assembled the whole host of monkeys, and they numbered more than forty-seven thousand. This roused all the monstrous beasts of the mountain — wolves and serpents, tigers and leopards, river-deer and roe-deer, muntjac and elk, foxes and wildcats, badgers and raccoon-dogs, lions and elephants, lion-mastiffs and orangutans, bears and stags, wild boars and mountain oxen, antelopes and blue rhinoceros, cunning hares and divine hounds — every sort of monster-king. There were seventy-two caves of them in all, and they came one and all to pay homage to the Monkey King as their sovereign. Year by year they offered tribute, and four times a season they reported for muster. Some drilled with the ranks; some levied grain by the seasons. Trim and orderly, they made of the whole Mountain of Flowers and Fruit something like an iron cauldron — a fortress of gold. The monster-kings of every quarter, moreover, brought gongs and drums, bright banners, helmets and armor; in a busy bustle they practiced their drills and mustered their forces day after day.

## A saber that does not suit the hand

The Handsome Monkey King was rejoicing in all this when he suddenly said to the assembly, *You are now well-versed in bow and crossbow and skilled with your weapons. But this saber of mine is truly clumsy and ill-suited to my hand. What is to be done?* The four old monkeys came forward and addressed him: *Your Majesty is an immortal sage, and common weapons are not fit for your use. We wonder, though — can Your Majesty travel beneath the water?*

*Since I came to know the Way,* said Wukong, *I have mastered the seventy-two earthly transformations, and my somersault cloud has boundless power. I am skilled in the arts of vanishing and concealment, in raising spells and summoning spells. To Heaven there is a road for me, and into the Earth there is a gate; I walk under sun and moon and cast no shadow; I pass through metal and stone without hindrance; water cannot drown me, fire cannot burn me. Is there anywhere I cannot go?*

*If Your Majesty has such powers,* said the four monkeys, *then beneath this iron-plank bridge of ours the water flows clear through to the Dragon Palace of the Eastern Sea. If Your Majesty would go down there, seek out the old Dragon King, and ask him for a proper weapon — would that not be just what you wish?* Wukong was overjoyed to hear it. *Wait here while I go and return,* he said.

## Down to the Crystal Palace

Good Monkey King! He sprang to the head of the bridge, worked a water-parting spell, made the magic sign, and with a splash plunged into the waves. Cleaving a path through the water, he went straight down to the floor of the Eastern Ocean. As he traveled along, he suddenly came upon a sea-patrolling yaksha who barred his way and demanded, *What divine being is this, pushing the waters aside? Speak plainly, that I may go and announce you.*

*I am Sun Wukong,* said Wukong, *the Heaven-born Sage of the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit — your old Dragon King's near neighbor. How is it you do not know me?* When the yaksha heard this, he hurried back to the Crystal Palace and reported: *Your Majesty, outside there is one Sun Wukong, the Heaven-born Sage of the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit. He calls himself Your Majesty's near neighbor and is even now arriving at the palace.*

Aoguang, Dragon King of the Eastern Sea, rose at once and came out with his dragon sons and dragon grandsons, his shrimp soldiers and crab generals, to receive him. *Come in, honored Immortal, come in!* They went straight into the palace, and when Wukong had been seated in the place of honor and served tea, the Dragon King asked, *When did the honored Immortal attain the Way? What magic arts did you receive?*

*From the very time of my birth,* said Wukong, *I left my home to practice cultivation, and I have won a body that is neither born nor perishing. Lately, because I have been training my children and grandchildren and guarding our mountain caves, I find I have no proper weapon. I have long heard that my worthy neighbor enjoys jeweled halls and coral palaces, and must surely have some divine arms to spare; so I have come expressly to beg one of you.*

Hearing this, the Dragon King could hardly refuse. He ordered the perch marshal to fetch a great long-handled saber and present it. *Old Sun does not know how to wield a saber,* said Wukong. *I beg you, give me something else.* The Dragon King then bade the eel commandant lead the cuttlefish strongmen to carry out a nine-pronged fork. Wukong leapt down, took it in his hands, ran through a few passes, and set it aside. *Light, light — far too light! And it doesn't suit my hand either. I beg you, give me something else.*

The Dragon King laughed and said, *Honored Immortal, take a closer look — that fork weighs three thousand six hundred catties!* *It doesn't suit my hand,* said Wukong. *It simply doesn't suit my hand.* Now the Dragon King grew uneasy, and ordered the carp inspector-general and the bream commander-in-chief to carry out a painted-handle Square-Heaven halberd weighing seven thousand two hundred catties. When Wukong saw it he ran up, took it in his hands, struck a few stances, made a couple of flourishes, then planted it upright in the ground. *Still too light, too light!*

The old Dragon King was thoroughly alarmed by now. *Honored Immortal, this halberd is the heaviest weapon in my entire palace — there is truly nothing else.* Wukong laughed: *As the ancients say, 'Never fear that the Dragon King lacks treasures.' Go and search again. If you have anything to my liking, I will pay whatever price you name.* *Truly there is nothing more,* said the Dragon King.

## The rod that anchors the Heavenly River

As they were speaking, the Dragon Mother and the Dragon Daughter slipped out from behind a curtain and said, *Your Majesty, to judge by his appearance this sage is plainly no ordinary being. In our sea treasury there is that piece of divine iron that anchors the bed of the Heavenly River. These last few days it has glowed with brilliant rosy light and given off auspicious vapors — could it be that the thing is destined to come forth now, and has at last met its sage?*

*That,* said the Dragon King, *is the rod that the Great Yu used in taming the floods, to measure the depths of rivers and seas — a piece of divine iron. What use could it be to him?* *Never mind whether he can use it,* said the Dragon Mother. *Simply give it to him; let him do with it as he likes, so long as we send him out of the palace gate.* The old Dragon King agreed, and told Wukong the whole story. *Bring it out and let me see it,* said Wukong. The Dragon King waved his hands: *It cannot be lifted or carried — the honored Immortal must come and look at it himself.* *Where is it?* asked Wukong. *Lead me to it.*

The Dragon King duly guided him into the depths of the sea treasury, and suddenly ten thousand shafts of golden light blazed forth. The Dragon King pointed: *That glowing thing is it.* Wukong hitched up his robe and stepped forward, gave it a feel, and found it to be an iron pillar, about as thick as a peck-measure and more than twenty feet long. He grasped it with both hands and heaved with all his might. *Too thick and too long,* he said. *It should be a bit shorter and thinner to be of any use.* No sooner had he spoken than the treasure shortened by several feet and grew thinner by a full span. Wukong hefted it again. *Thinner still would be better.* And the treasure truly grew thinner by several more degrees.

Wukong was overjoyed. He carried it out of the sea treasury to examine it in full, and found that the two ends were bound with rings of gold while the middle was wrought of solid black iron. Close against one golden band a row of words was engraved, reading: *The As-You-Will Gold-Banded Cudgel — weight, thirteen thousand five hundred catties.* His heart rejoiced in secret: *No doubt this treasure does exactly as one wishes.* And as he walked along, thinking in his heart and speaking with his mouth and hefting it in his hand, he said, *Shorter and thinner still would be more wonderful yet.* By the time he carried it outside, it had shrunk to barely twelve feet long and was no thicker around than a rice bowl.

Just watch him work his magic! Making his flourishes, he came whirling through the Crystal Palace until the old Dragon King quaked with terror, the young dragon sons lost their wits, and the turtles and tortoises, the soft-shells and alligators, all drew in their necks, while the fish and shrimp, the sea-bream and crabs, all hid their heads. Wukong held the treasure in his hand, settled himself upon the dais of the Crystal Palace, and said to the Dragon King with a laugh, *Many thanks, worthy neighbor, for your generosity.* *Don't mention it, don't mention it,* said the Dragon King.

## A guest cannot trouble two hosts

*This piece of iron is good to use,* said Wukong, *but there is one thing more.* *What more has the honored Immortal to say?* asked the Dragon King. *If there had been no such iron to begin with, then so be it,* said Wukong. *But now that I hold it in my hand and have no fitting clothes upon my body, what's to be done? If you have any armor here, why not give me a suit as well? I'll thank you for everything together.* *That,* said the Dragon King, *I truly do not have.* *A guest cannot trouble two hosts,* said Wukong. *If you haven't any, I certainly won't go out this door.* *I beg the honored Immortal to try another sea,* said the Dragon King. *Perhaps there it may be found.* *Better to sit at one house than trudge round three,* said Wukong. *I implore you — find me a suit, by all means.* *Truly there is none,* said the Dragon King. *If there were, I would certainly present it.* *Truly none?* said Wukong. *Then let me try this iron out on you!*

The Dragon King panicked. *Honored Immortal, don't raise your hand, don't raise your hand! Let me see whether my brothers have any, and I'll send you a suit.* *Where are your brothers?* asked Wukong. *My brothers,* said the Dragon King, *are Aoqin, Dragon King of the Southern Sea; Aoshun, Dragon King of the Northern Sea; and Aorun, Dragon King of the Western Sea.* *Old Sun won't go running to them,* said Wukong. *As the saying goes, 'Three on credit are no match for two in hand.' I only ask that you make do, high or low, and send me a suit — and that's that.*

*There is no need for the honored Immortal to go,* said the old Dragon. *I have here an iron drum and a golden bell. Whenever some urgent matter arises, we beat the drum till it thunders and strike the bell till it rings, and my brothers come in an instant.* *Then quick — go and beat the drum and strike the bell,* said Wukong. And indeed the alligator general went to strike the bell, and the soft-shelled marshal came to beat the drum.

In a little while, roused by the sounding of bell and drum, the three other Dragon Kings arrived in a moment and gathered outside. Said Aoqin, *Elder brother, what urgent matter has come, that you beat the drum and strike the bell?* *Worthy brother,* said the old Dragon, *it's hard to say. There is a Heaven-born Sage of the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit who came this morning and claimed me for a neighbor. He demanded a weapon. I offered him a steel fork — he scorned it as too small; I presented a painted halberd — he scorned it as too light. He took up with his own hands that divine iron that anchors the bed of the Heavenly River and made a few flourishes with it. Now he sits in my palace and demands a suit of armor besides. I have none, so I sounded the bell and beat the drum to summon you, worthy brothers. Have you any armor? Give him a suit and send him out the door, and have done with it.*

When Aoqin heard this, he was furious. *Let us brothers call out our troops and seize him — wouldn't that settle it?* *Do not speak of seizing him,* said the old Dragon. *Just brush against that iron and you're dead; just graze it and you're done for; touch it and your skin splits; rub against it and your sinews are torn.* Said Aorun, Dragon King of the Western Sea, *Second brother must not come to blows with him. Let us simply put together a suit of armor and send him out the door — then we shall draw up a memorial to lay before Heaven, and Heaven itself will execute him.* *Well said,* agreed Aoshun, Dragon King of the Northern Sea. *I have here a pair of cloud-walking shoes of lotus thread.* *And I,* said Aorun of the West, *have brought a suit of chain-link golden armor.* *And I,* said Aoqin of the South, *have a phoenix-winged cap of purple gold.*

The old Dragon was delighted, and led them all into the Crystal Palace to present these gifts to Wukong. Wukong put on the golden cap, buckled on the golden armor, and stepped into the cloud-walking shoes, then set his As-You-Will cudgel swinging and fought his way back out, calling to the assembled dragons as he went, *Sorry to trouble you! Sorry to trouble you!* The four Dragon Kings of the Four Seas were greatly aggrieved, and withdrew to confer about presenting a memorial to Heaven — but of that we shall say no more for now.

## Small as a needle, tall as ten thousand fathoms

Just look at the Monkey King! He parted the waters and came straight back to the head of the iron-plank bridge, bounding up out of the river. There stood the four old monkeys leading the host, all waiting at the bridgeside. Suddenly they saw Wukong leap from the waves without a single drop of water on his body, gleaming golden as he mounted onto the bridge. The monkeys all fell to their knees in awe. *Your Majesty, what splendor! What splendor!* Wukong, his face wreathed in springtime smiles, mounted high upon his throne and stood the iron cudgel upright in the midst of them.

Those foolish monkeys, knowing no better, all came forward to lay hold of the treasure — but it was like dragonflies trying to shake an iron tree; not a hair's breadth could they stir it. One after another they bit their fingers and stuck out their tongues in amazement. *Grandfather! So heavy as this — however did you carry it back?* Wukong stepped up, reached out his hand, and snatched it up in a single grip. He said to them with a laugh, *Everything has its master. This treasure has lain hidden in the sea treasury for who knows how many thousands of years, and it just happened to shine forth in this very year. The Dragon King took it for nothing but a lump of black iron, and called it the divine iron that anchors the bed of the Heavenly River. None of those fellows could so much as lift or carry it, and in the end they had to ask me to go and fetch it myself. At that time the treasure was more than twenty feet long and as thick as a peck-measure. When I gripped it and wished it smaller, it shrank by a good deal; when I told it to be smaller still, it shrank again; and when I commanded it smaller once more, it shrank again. Holding it up to the light to read it, I found a row of words engraved upon it: 'The As-You-Will Gold-Banded Cudgel, thirteen thousand five hundred catties.' Now all of you stand aside, and watch me make it change once more.*

He hefted the treasure in his hand and cried, *Small! Small! Small!* — and at once it dwindled to the size of an embroidery needle, small enough to be tucked away inside his ear. The monkeys cried out in astonishment, *Your Majesty, bring it out again and play with it some more!* The Monkey King took it from his ear and held it on his palm, calling, *Big! Big! Big!* — and at once it grew again to a peck-measure's thickness and twenty feet in length. Carried away with delight, he leapt onto the bridge and strode out of the cave, gripped the treasure in his hand, and worked the magic of taking Heaven for his pattern and Earth for his model. He bent his waist and cried, *Grow!* — and he grew ten thousand fathoms tall, his head like Mount Tai, his waist like a towering ridge, his eyes like flashing lightning, his mouth like a basin of blood, his teeth like swords and halberds; and the cudgel in his hand reached up to the Thirty-Third Heaven above and down to the Eighteenth Hell below. The tigers and leopards, the wolves and serpents, all the monster-throngs of the mountain, and the monster-kings of the seventy-two caves were so terrified that they kowtowed and prostrated themselves in worship, trembling till their wits scattered and their souls flew away. In an instant he withdrew the magic form, turned the treasure back into an embroidery needle, hid it in his ear, and returned to his cave dwelling. The monster-kings of every cave all came rushing in great alarm to pay their respects and offer congratulations.

## Marshals, generals, and six sworn brothers

Then they flung wide the banners and struck up the drums, set the brass gongs ringing, spread out a lavish feast of rare delicacies, and filled the cups brimming with coconut wine and grape liquor. They drank and feasted with the whole host for a long while, and afterward took up their drilling as before. The Monkey King appointed the four old monkeys as his stalwart commanders: the two bare-rumped roan-apes he named Marshal Ma and Marshal Liu, and the two long-armed gibbons he named General Beng and General Ba. All matters of pitching camp and raising stockades, of rewards and punishments, he handed over to the four stalwart commanders to manage.

His mind at ease, he passed his days riding the clouds and mounting the mists, roaming the Four Seas and disporting himself among a thousand mountains. He displayed his martial arts and sought out heroes far and wide; he worked his magic and made worthy friends in plenty. At this time he became sworn brothers with six others — the Bull Demon King, the Serpent Demon King, the Roc Demon King, the Lion-Camel King, the Macaque King, and the Marmoset King — making seven in all, counting himself, the Handsome Monkey King. Day after day they discoursed on letters and debated the arts of war, passed the wine-cups and the goblets round, played the strings and sang, piped and danced; off in the morning and back at evening, there was no kind of joy they did not taste. Ten thousand miles of distance they reckoned as no more than the path across a courtyard — as the saying goes, a nod of the head and three thousand miles are crossed, a twist of the waist and eight hundred more are spanned.

One day, in his own cave, he ordered the four stalwart commanders to lay out a banquet and invited the six kings to come and drink. They slaughtered oxen and butchered horses, made sacrifice to Heaven and offerings to Earth, set the monster-throng to dancing and singing, and all drank themselves blind drunk. After seeing the six kings off, he rewarded his officers great and small. Then he reclined in the shade of the pines beside the iron-plank bridge and in a moment fell asleep. The four stalwart commanders led the host in keeping watch around him, not daring to raise their voices.

## A noose around the soul

But in his sleep the Handsome Monkey King saw two men approach carrying a warrant, on which were written the three characters *Sun Wukong*. Coming up to his side, they allowed him not a word of protest, slipped a noose about him, and led his very soul away. Stumbling and reeling, he was dragged along until they came to the wall of a great city. The Monkey King gradually felt the wine wearing off, and lifting his head he saw upon the wall an iron placard bearing three great characters: *Region of Darkness.* At once the Handsome Monkey King came to himself. *The Region of Darkness is where Yama dwells,* he said. *Why have I come here?* The two men replied, *Your span of earthly life is at an end this day; we two have received the warrant and come to summon you.*

When the Monkey King heard this, he said, *Old Sun has risen above the Three Realms and stands no longer within the Five Elements. I am no longer subject to his authority — how dare you, blindly and confusedly, come to summon me?* But the two soul-takers only kept tugging and hauling, determined to drag him inside. At this the Monkey King's temper flared. He plucked the treasure from his ear, gave it a shake until it was as thick as a rice bowl, and with one easy lift of his hand beat the two soul-takers to a pulp. He undid his own noose, shook his hands free, and wielding the cudgel came smashing his way into the city. The ox-headed demons fled and dodged east and west; the horse-faced demons ran north and south. The demon underlings rushed up to the Hall of Judgment and reported, *Your Majesties, disaster! Disaster! Outside there is some hairy-faced thunder-god come smashing his way in!*

## The Registers of Life and Death

In a fluster, the Ten Kings of the Underworld hurriedly straightened their robes and came out to look. When they saw how fierce his features were, they at once arranged themselves in their ranks and called out together in loud voices, *Honored Immortal, your name! What is your honored name?* Said the Monkey King, *Since you do not even know who I am, how is it you sent men to summon me?* The Ten Kings said, *We dare not presume — surely the men we sent were mistaken.* Said the Monkey King, *I am Sun Wukong, the Heaven-born Sage of the Water-Curtain Cave on the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit. What official ranks are yours?* The Ten Kings bowed and replied, *We are the Ten Kings of the Underworld, the sovereigns of the world of shades.* *Quick, tell me your names,* said Wukong, *and spare yourselves a beating.* Said the Ten Kings, *We are King Qinguang, King of First Rivers, King of the Song Emperor, King of Revolting Officials, King Yama, King of Equality, King of Mount Tai, King of the Capital City, King of Biancheng, and King of the Turning Wheel.*

*Since you have all attained the rank of king,* said Wukong, *and are counted among the order of bright and responsive spirits, how is it you do not know good from ill? Old Sun has perfected the way of the immortals; my life-span equals Heaven's; I have risen above the Three Realms and leapt clean out of the Five Elements. Why did you send men to lay hold of me?* *Let not the honored Immortal be wroth,* said the Ten Kings. *There are many in all the world who share the same name and surname. Perhaps the soul-taker went astray.* *Nonsense! Nonsense!* said Wukong. *As the common saying goes, 'Officials may err and clerks may err, but the man sent on the errand does not err.' Quick, fetch me the Registers of Life and Death, and let me see them!*

The Ten Kings at once invited him up into the hall to examine the records. Wukong, gripping his As-You-Will cudgel, mounted straight up onto the Hall of Judgment and sat himself down in the very center, facing south. The Ten Kings ordered the recording judge in charge of the files to bring out the documents for examination. The judge dared not delay; he went to the records office and brought out five or six bundles of documents along with the registers of the ten kinds of beings, and went through them one by one. Among the bare-skinned creatures, the hairy creatures, the feathered creatures, the insects, and the scaled and shelled kinds, his name was nowhere to be found. Then they came to the category of monkeys: now this monkey was like a man, yet was not entered among the names of men; like a bare-skinned creature, yet did not dwell within any kingdom; like a running beast, yet was not under the rule of the qilin; like a flying bird, yet was not subject to the phoenix. There was a separate register, and this Wukong examined for himself, until at the number one thousand three hundred and fifty under the character *Soul,* he found at last the name of Sun Wukong, set down as follows: *Heaven-born stone monkey; allotted life-span, three hundred and forty-two years; a good and natural death.*

*I don't trouble myself to remember how many years my span is,* said Wukong. *I'll simply strike out the name and have done.* He called for a brush. The judge hastily presented one, well-dipped in thick black ink. Wukong took up the register, and wherever there was a name among the category of monkeys he struck them all out, every one. Then he flung down the register and said, *It is settled! From now on I am no longer under your authority!* And wielding the cudgel all the way, he smashed his way back out of the Region of Darkness. The Ten Kings dared not come near him; they all made their way to the Azure Cloud Palace, joined together in worship before the Bodhisattva Kshitigarbha, and conferred about drawing up a memorial to lay before Heaven — but of that we shall say no more.

## A dream of the Southern Branch

As the Monkey King came smashing his way out of the city, he suddenly tripped over a clump of grass and stumbled headlong — and woke with a start. It had all been but a dream of the Southern Branch. Just as he was stretching himself, he heard the four stalwart commanders and the host of monkeys calling out, *Your Majesty, how much wine did you drink? You have slept the whole night through and still will not wake!* Said Wukong, *Sleeping is a small matter, but in my sleep I dreamed that two men came here to summon me and carried my soul off to the gate of the Region of Darkness. Only as I woke did I come to my senses. There I displayed my powers, shouting my way into the Hall of Judgment, and quarreled with the Ten Kings. I looked over the register of life and death, and wherever there was a name belonging to any of our kind, I struck it out — so now none of us is subject to that fellow's rule any longer!* The monkeys kowtowed and gave thanks. And from that time on, many of the mountain monkeys never grew old — because their names were no longer recorded in the Office of Shades.

When the Handsome Monkey King had finished telling all that had passed, the four stalwart commanders made it known to the monster-kings of every cave, who all came to offer congratulations. Within a few days the six sworn brothers came again to pay their respects, and when they heard the reason the names had been struck off, every one of them rejoiced; day after day they gathered for revels — but of that we shall say no more.

## Two memorials before the Jade Emperor

Now let us tell of the Most High Holy and Greatly Merciful Lord, the Jade Emperor, August Sovereign of the Mysterious Vault on high. One day he sat in court upon the precious throne of the Hall of Divine Mists in the Cloud Palace of the Golden Gate, with the immortal officers civil and martial gathered for the morning audience, when suddenly the Perfected One Qiu Hongji came forward and announced, *Your Majesty, outside the Hall of Universal Brightness, Aoguang, Dragon King of the Eastern Sea, has presented a memorial and awaits the Heavenly Lord's summons.* The Jade Emperor gave the command: *Let him be summoned in.* Aoguang was led to the foot of the Hall of Divine Mists; when he had finished making his reverences, an immortal page beside the throne took up the memorial and handed it up. The Jade Emperor read it through from the beginning. It read:

> Your subject Aoguang, lowly Dragon of the Eastern Sea of the Eastern Continent of Superior Spirits, in the watery realm of the lower world, makes report to the Great Holy Sovereign, the Most High August Emperor of the Mysterious Vault: Of late, by reason of one Sun Wukong, a monstrous immortal born on the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit and dwelling in the Water-Curtain Cave, your lowly dragon has been bullied and abused. He forced his way into the water-mansion and demanded weapons, working his magic and flaunting his might; he demanded armor, raging in his fierceness and his power. He terrorized the water-tribes and put the turtles and alligators to flight. The Dragon of the Southern Sea quakes with fear; the Dragon of the Western Sea is wretched and miserable; the Dragon of the Northern Sea has bowed his head in surrender. Your subject Aoguang prostrated himself and made obeisance, and presented the divine pearl-iron cudgel, the phoenix-winged golden cap, the chain-link armor, and the cloud-walking shoes, sending him out with all courtesy. Yet still he flourished his martial arts and displayed his powers, crying only, *Sorry to trouble you! Sorry to trouble you!* Truly he has no equal and is most difficult to control. Your subject now lays this before you, humbly awaiting Your Holiness's decision. I earnestly beg that Heavenly troops be sent to capture this monstrous creature, that the seas and mountains may be made clean and peaceful and the lower realm secure. Respectfully submitted.

When the Holy Emperor had read it through, he gave the command: *Let the Dragon Spirit return to his sea; I shall presently dispatch a general to seize him.* The old Dragon King touched his head to the ground in thanks and departed.

Then from below, the Heavenly Master Ge Xianweng came forward and announced, *Your Majesty, there is a memorial sent up from the Underworld by King Qinguang, presented on behalf of the Lord of the Region of Darkness, the Bodhisattva Kshitigarbha.* An attendant Jade Maiden took up the memorial and handed it up, and the Jade Emperor likewise read it through from the beginning. It read:

> The Region of Darkness is the shadowy office of the Earth. Heaven has its spirits and Earth has its ghosts; the bright and the dark turn in their cycles. Birds have their birth and beasts their death; male and female alternate in their courses. Life upon life, transformation upon transformation, conceiving daughters and bringing forth sons — this is the order of nature, and may not be altered. But now there is a monstrous monkey, Sun Wukong, born of Heaven in the Water-Curtain Cave on the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit, who works wickedness and commits violence and will not submit to the summons. Working his magic, he beat down the ghostly envoys of the Nine Shades; relying on his might, he terrified and harmed the merciful Kings of the Ten Courts. He made a great uproar in the Hall of Judgment and forcibly struck out the names from the registers, so that the kind of the monkeys is now beyond all restraint, and the breed of macaques live long beyond their span; stillness and the wheel of rebirth alike are undone, and each is freed from life and death. Your poor monk presents this memorial, daring to offend the Heavenly Majesty. I humbly beg that divine troops be dispatched to subdue this monster, to set the bright and the dark in order and bring lasting peace to the realm of shades. Respectfully submitted.

When the Jade Emperor had read it through, he gave the command: *Let the Lord of the Underworld return to the realm of shades; I shall presently dispatch a general to seize him.* King Qinguang likewise touched his head to the ground in thanks and departed.

## An edict of amnesty and recruitment

The Great Heavenly Lord summoned the assembled immortal officers, civil and martial, and asked, *In what year was this monstrous monkey born, and to what age did he live, that he should have attained the Way to such a degree?* Scarcely had he spoken when there stepped forth from the ranks the Thousand-Mile Eye and the Down-the-Wind Ear, who said, *This monkey is the Heaven-born stone monkey of three hundred years ago. At that time he was thought nothing of, and we do not know in what place he has these few years cultivated himself into an immortal, subduing dragons and taming tigers, and forcibly striking out the registers of death.* The Jade Emperor asked, *Which divine general shall go down to the lower world to subdue him?*

Scarcely had he spoken when there stepped forth from the ranks the Great White Planet, the Star of Long Geng, who bowed low and announced, *Most Holy One, of all beings in the Three Realms that possess the nine apertures, every one may cultivate immortality. But this monkey is a body nurtured by Heaven and Earth, a form conceived by the sun and the moon; he too treads the earth and bears up the sky; he feeds upon the dew and dines upon the rosy mists. Now that he has perfected the way of immortality and has the power to subdue dragons and tame tigers, in what is he different from a man? Your subject begs Your Majesty, in mindfulness of the merciful grace of all begetting and transformation, to send down an edict of amnesty and recruitment, summoning him up to the upper realm and granting him some office great or small, entering his name upon the rolls and so binding him here. If he accepts the Heavenly command, then afterward he may be promoted and rewarded; if he defies the Heavenly command, then he may be seized on the spot. In this way, first, the host need not be stirred nor the army wearied; and second, an immortal is gathered in by the way of the Way.* The Jade Emperor was greatly pleased and said, *We shall do as our minister proposes.* So he ordered the Star Official of Literary Songs to draft the edict, and bade the Great White Planet of Venus carry out the recruitment.

## The Planet of Venus at the cave-mouth

The Planet of Venus received the edict, went out beyond the Southern Heaven Gate, brought down his auspicious cloud, and came straight to the Water-Curtain Cave on the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit. He said to the little monkeys at the entrance, *I am an envoy of Heaven, sent by Heaven, and I have a holy edict here. I ask that your great king come up to the upper realm. Quickly, make it known.* The little monkeys outside the cave passed the word layer by layer into the depths of the cave-heaven: *Your Majesty, outside there is an old man carrying a roll of documents on his back, who says he is an envoy sent down by Heaven, and that he has a holy edict inviting you.* The Handsome Monkey King was overjoyed to hear it. *These last two days I have just been thinking I'd like to take a trip up to Heaven,* he said, *and here is a Heavenly envoy come to invite me!* He called out, *Quick, show him in!*

The Monkey King hastily put his cap and robes in order and went out beyond the gate to welcome him. The Planet of Venus came straight to the center and stood facing south. *I am the Great White Planet of Venus, of the West,* he said. *By the Jade Emperor's edict of amnesty and recruitment I have come down to the lower world to invite you up to Heaven, there to receive the immortal rolls and an appointment.* Wukong laughed and said, *Many thanks, venerable Star, for descending here.* And he ordered the little ones to lay out a banquet to entertain him. *The holy edict is upon me,* said the Planet; *I dare not linger long. I beg the great king to come along with me; once you have won your glorious advancement, there will be time enough at leisure for talk.* *It is an honor that you have called,* said Wukong. *I am sorry to send you off empty-handed — sorry to send you off empty-handed.* At once he summoned the four stalwart commanders and charged them: *Carefully train my children and grandchildren. Let me go up to Heaven and take a look at the road, and presently I'll bring you all up there to dwell together with me.* The four stalwart commanders gave their assent.

Then the Monkey King and the Planet of Venus mounted their clouds and rose into the heights of the empyrean. Truly it was a case of:

> Raised high to the rank of an immortal of the upper grade,
> His name set down in the cloud-borne ranks and the precious rolls.

But as to what office and what rank he was to receive — that you shall hear told in the next chapter.
