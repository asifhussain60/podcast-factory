# Birth of the Stone Monkey

*A stone seasoned by sun and moon since the parting of the pure and impure splits open at the summit of Flower-Fruit Mountain and gives birth to a monkey. He leads a tribe through a waterfall into a hidden cave-heaven, is crowned the Handsome Monkey King, reigns for three or four hundred years — and then, at the height of his happiness, weeps at the thought that one day King Yama will come for him. A common gibbon names the three classes of beings exempt from death — Buddhas, Immortals, and Holy Sages — and the Monkey King rafts across two oceans and walks two continents in search of them. A woodcutter on a mountain in the Western Continent points him at the cave of the Patriarch Subodhi, who, after testing him, gives him a surname and a Dharma-name: Sun Wukong — Monkey Awakened to Emptiness.*

## The reckoning of heaven and earth

Before there was a story to tell, the storyteller's tradition tells, the heavens and the earth were a single formless blur, and no eye had ever looked upon them. Then Pangu shattered through that primal murky shell, the pure rose and the impure settled, and the order of creation fell into place. All living things look up in awe to the great benevolence that brought forth every thing that is and turned every thing toward the good. If you would know how the work of making goes, and how the great world's age turns — the old chronicle says — then read the tale of *Journey to the West*, the chronicle that frees from woe.

The reckoning of heaven and earth runs in great cycles, each cycle one hundred twenty-nine thousand six hundred years long. Each cycle divides into twelve epochs, named for the twelve earthly branches: Zi, Chou, Yin, Mao, Chen, Si, Wu, Wei, Shen, You, Xu, and Hai. Each epoch endures ten thousand eight hundred years. Take the cycle in miniature, as a single day. At the hour of Zi the vital force of yang first stirs; at Chou the cocks begin to crow. At Yin the light has not yet broken through; at Mao the sun comes up. At Chen breakfast is past; at Si the household is set in order. At Wu the sun stands at heaven's center; at Wei it begins to slip westward. At Shen comes the late afternoon, and the sun sets at You. At Xu falls the yellow dusk, and at Hai the world lies down to rest.

By analogy with the great reckoning: when the epoch of Xu draws toward its close, heaven and earth grow dark and dim, and all things wither. Five thousand four hundred years onward, at the opening of the epoch of Hai, there is utter blackness, and between the two realms neither man nor thing remains. This is what the old books call chaos — the undivided murk. After another five thousand four hundred years, as the epoch of Hai nears its end, the constant turns once more toward its source. As the epoch of Zi approaches, light gradually begins to return. Master Shao Kangjie said: *At the winter solstice, the midpoint of Zi, the heart of heaven does not shift. Where the single yang first quickens — that is the moment before the myriad things are born.* At this point, heaven first has its root.

After another five thousand four hundred years, in the very midst of the epoch of Zi, the light and the pure rise upward, and there come to be sun, moon, stars, and constellations. The four together are called the Four Images. Hence the saying: *heaven opened in Zi*. Five thousand four hundred years pass, the epoch of Zi draws toward its close, the epoch of Chou approaches, and things gradually grow firm and solid. The *Book of Changes* says: *Great is the creative principle of Qian! Supreme is the receptive principle of Kun! The myriad things draw their life from it, obediently following heaven.* At this point, the earth first congeals.

Another five thousand four hundred years, and in the very midst of the epoch of Chou, the heavy and the impure settle downward; water, fire, mountains, stone, and soil come to be. These are called the Five Forms. Hence the saying: *earth was opened in Chou*. Another five thousand four hundred years, the epoch of Chou ends, the epoch of Yin begins, and the myriad things spring into being. The almanac says: *The breath of heaven descends; the breath of earth ascends. Heaven and earth join in union, and all the host of things is born.* The heaven is clear, the earth is fresh, and yin and yang are joined. After yet another five thousand four hundred years, in the very midst of the epoch of Yin, there are born human beings and beasts and birds — and so the three powers, heaven, earth, and humankind, take their fixed places. Hence the saying: *humankind was born in Yin*.

After Pangu opened the world, the Three Sovereigns ruled the age, and the Five Emperors set in order the human bonds. The world was then divided into four great continents: the Eastern Continent of Spiritual Excellence, the Western Continent of Cattle-Offering, the Southern Continent of Jambu, and the Northern Continent of Kuru. This book concerns the Eastern Continent of Spiritual Excellence alone.

## Flower-Fruit Mountain and the magic stone

Beyond its seas lay a country called Aolai. The country bordered the great ocean, and out in that ocean there rose a famous mountain called Flower-Fruit Mountain. The mountain was the ancestral vein of the Ten Continents, the source-dragon of the Three Isles. It had stood since the very parting of the pure and the impure, and had taken its shape when the primal murk was first cleft asunder. There is an old rhapsody to prove what kind of mountain it was:

> Its power overawes the boundless deep; its might brings peace to the jade-green sea.
> The tide surges up in silver hills and the fish flee to their dens; the waves roll over in drifts of snow and the sea-monsters quit their abyss.
> Where water and fire heap high at the corners, it lifts a soaring summit over the Eastern Sea.
> Cinnabar cliffs and grotesque stones, sheer escarpments and wondrous peaks.
> Upon the cinnabar cliffs, paired phoenixes call together; before the sheer escarpments, a lone unicorn lies at rest.
> On the peaks one hears in season the crowing of golden pheasants; in the stone caverns one ever watches dragons coming and going.
> In the forest dwell long-lived deer and fairy foxes; in the trees roost numinous birds and dark cranes.
> The fairy grasses and rare blossoms never fade; the green pines and emerald cypress keep eternal spring.
> The immortal peaches forever bear their fruit; the slender bamboo perpetually holds the clouds.
> A single ravine runs thick with vines and creepers, and on every side the embankments wear fresh new green.
> This is indeed the heaven-bracing pillar where the hundred rivers meet, the unmoving root of the earth that ten thousand ages cannot stir.

Now upon the very summit of that mountain there sat a magic stone. It measured three *zhang* six *chi* and five *cun* in height, and two *zhang* four *chi* around. The height of three *zhang* six *chi* and five *cun* corresponded to the three hundred sixty-five degrees of the heavens; the girth of two *zhang* four *chi* corresponded to the twenty-four solar terms of the calendar. Upon it were nine apertures and eight holes, corresponding to the Nine Palaces and the Eight Trigrams. On all four sides there were no trees to shade it, but to left and right there grew sprigs of magic fungus and orchid for company.

Ever since the world's beginning, the stone had been receiving the true essence of heaven, the fine flower of earth, the seed of the sun, and the splendor of the moon. Quickened through long ages, at last it conceived a numinous purpose and nurtured within itself a magic womb. One day it burst open and gave birth to a stone egg, round and large as a ball. Touched by the wind, the egg transformed into a stone monkey, complete in all five senses and whole in all four limbs. At once it learned to crawl and to walk, and it bowed toward the four quarters. From its eyes shot forth two beams of golden light that pierced upward to the Palace of the Dipper.

The light startled the August One on high — the supremely compassionate and benevolent Jade Emperor, the Most High Lord of the Mysterious Vault — who sat enthroned upon his Spirit-Mist Treasure Hall within the Cloud Palace of the Golden Gate. He had gathered his immortal ministers about him. Seeing the blazing golden light, he commanded Thousand-Mile Eye and Down-the-Wind Ear to open the Southern Gate of Heaven and look out. The two generals went out beyond the gate, looked clearly and listened plainly, and after a moment returned to report.

*Your servants, obeying the command,* they said, *observed and listened at the source of the golden light. It comes from the border of the little country of Aolai, east across the sea of the Eastern Continent of Spiritual Excellence. There is a mountain there called Flower-Fruit Mountain, and on the mountain a magic stone, and the stone has given birth to an egg. Touched by the wind, it became a stone monkey, which now bows toward the four quarters; from its eyes shot the golden light that pierced the Palace of the Dipper. But now that it has begun to take food and drink, the golden light is fading away.*

The Jade Emperor, in his gracious mercy, answered: *These creatures of the world below are born of the very essence of heaven and earth. It is nothing to wonder at.*

## The leap into the waterfall

The monkey in the mountains learned at once to walk and leap and run about. He ate the grasses and the trees, drank from the brook and the spring, gathered the mountain flowers and sought out the fruits of the trees. He made companions of the wolves and the insects, ran in packs with the tigers and leopards, made friends of the river-deer and the stags, and counted the gibbons and apes as his kin. By night he slept beneath the stone cliffs; by day he roamed among the peaks and caves. As the saying goes: *In the mountains there is no calendar; when the cold is gone, one does not know the year.*

One day the weather turned scorching hot, and the monkey, together with the whole troop, took refuge from the heat beneath the shade of the pines. Just look at every one of them:

> Leaping the trees and clambering the branches, plucking the blossoms and seeking the fruit;
> tossing pellets and playing with marbles; racing through the sand-pits, building little pagodas;
> chasing dragonflies, pouncing on lizards; bowing to old Heaven, worshipping the Bodhisattva;
> pulling at the kudzu vines, plaiting the grasses; catching their fleas, biting and pinching them;
> grooming their fur, picking at their nails. The pressing pressed and the rubbing rubbed,
> the shoving shoved and the squashing squashed, the dragging dragged and the hauling hauled —
> beneath the green pine forest they romped at will, beside the clear brook they bathed as they pleased.

When the troop had played a while, they went to bathe in the mountain brook. They saw how the water came rushing down, churning like a boiling cauldron. The old saying goes: *Birds have the speech of birds, and beasts the words of beasts.* All the monkeys said: *We do not know where this water comes from. Today, having nothing better to do, let us follow the brook up to its head and trace it to its source — that will be sport!* With a shout they all came running, tugging the males and leading the females, calling to younger brother and elder brother, scrambling up the mountain along the brook until they reached the very source: a leaping waterfall and a flying spring.

> A sheet of white rainbow rises; a thousand fathoms of snowy waves take flight.
> The sea-wind cannot break it; the river-moon still shines upon it.
> Its cold breath divides the blue cliffs; its overflow waters the emerald slopes.
> Murmuring, it is named a waterfall — truly like a hanging curtain of pearls.

The monkeys clapped their hands and cried in praise: *Fine water! Fine water! So it reaches far down beneath the mountain's foot and joins straight to the waves of the great ocean.* And then they said: *If any one of us has the ability to bore inside, find the source-head, and come out again unhurt, we will honor him as our king.* Three times they raised the cry, and suddenly out from the throng leaped the stone monkey, answering with a loud shout: *I'll go in! I'll go in!*

What a monkey, the old verse says — for him it was:

> Today his fair name is made plain;
> his hour has come, his great fortune flows.
> By destiny he dwells in this place;
> his king will send him into the immortal palace.

He shut his eyes, crouched low, and with one bound sprang straight into the waterfall. Then he opened his eyes, lifted his head, and looked — and there inside was neither water nor wave, but a bright and shining bridge of wrought iron. The water beneath the bridge poured through the gaps among the rocks and gushed outward, hanging downward to screen the bridge's mouth. He stepped up onto the bridgehead and walked forward, gazing about him, and saw that the place seemed a very dwelling where folk might live. He saw there:

> Green mosses heaped like blue, white clouds floating like jade, the light shimmering in shred upon shred of rosy mist.
> Empty windows and quiet chambers; smooth benches where flowers seem to bloom upon the boards.
> Stalactites hang like dragon-pearls; rare blossoms wind in profusion over all the ground.
> Beside the cliff a stove still bears the trace of fire; against the table the wine-jars show the dregs of a feast.
> Stone seats and stone beds, truly lovable; stone basins and stone bowls, more to be praised still.
> And there too one or two stalks of slender bamboo, three or five sprays of plum blossom.
> A few green pines that ever wear the rain — altogether, just like a human household.

After he had looked a good while, he leaped to the middle of the bridge and gazed left and right. There in the very center stood a stone tablet, and upon the tablet a line of large regular characters was carved: *The Blessed Land of Flower-Fruit Mountain, the Cave-Heaven of the Water-Curtain Cave.*

The stone monkey was beside himself with joy. He turned and made his way back out, shut his eyes, crouched low, and leaped clear of the water. With two great chuckles he cried: *Great fortune! Great fortune!* The monkeys crowded round him and asked: *How is it inside? How deep is the water?*

*There's no water!* the stone monkey said. *No water at all! It turns out there's a bridge of wrought iron, and beyond the bridge a household ready-made by heaven and earth.* *How can it be a household?* the others said. The stone monkey laughed and said: *This water pours through beneath the stone bridge and hangs down to screen the gateway. Beside the bridge there are flowers and trees, and there is a stone house. Within the house are a stone hearth, a stone stove, stone bowls, stone basins, a stone bed, and stone benches. In the center, on a stone tablet, are carved the words: 'The Blessed Land of Flower-Fruit Mountain, the Cave-Heaven of the Water-Curtain Cave.' Truly it is a place for us to settle. Inside it is broad and wide, with room for a thousand of us or more, old and young alike. Let us all go in and dwell there, and so spare ourselves the buffets of old Heaven. In here:*

> When the wind blows there's a place to hide; when the rain falls a good shelter for the body.
> Frost and snow we need not fear at all; the thunder's voice is never heard.
> Mist and rosy clouds forever glow; auspicious vapors ever steam and warm.
> The pines and bamboo year by year stay fair; the rare flowers grow fresh day after day.

When the monkeys heard this, every one rejoiced. *You go first and lead us in!* they cried. *In, in!* Again the stone monkey shut his eyes, crouched low, and leaped inside, calling: *Follow me in, all of you! In, in!* Those with bold hearts leaped at once; the timid ones craned their necks and drew them back, scratched their ears and rubbed their cheeks, shrieked aloud, and after fussing a good while, in they went too. Once across the bridgehead they began snatching basins and seizing bowls, claiming the stove and fighting over the bed, hauling things this way and shifting them that way — for monkeys by nature are restless and unruly, and not a one would keep still until they had worn themselves out and their spirits flagged. Only then did they stop.

## The Handsome Monkey King

The stone monkey took his seat at the head of them and said: *Gentlemen — 'a man without good faith, one does not know what he can be good for.' You yourselves just now said that whoever had the ability to get in and out again unhurt should be honored as king. Now I have gone in and come out, come out and gone in, and have found you this cave-heaven where you may sleep soundly and rest at ease, and where each may enjoy the blessing of a household ready-made. Why then do you not honor me as king?*

The monkeys folded their hands and bowed in submission without a single dissent. One by one they arranged themselves in order of age, drew up in ranks, and bowed in homage, hailing him *Great King of a Thousand Years*. From that time the stone monkey ascended to the throne. He set aside the word *stone* and styled himself the Handsome Monkey King. There is a poem to prove it:

> The three yang join in harmony to bring forth the living host;
> the magic stone, its womb enwrapping the essence of sun and moon.
> Borrowing the egg to become a monkey, he fulfills the Great Way;
> taking on a name and a surname, he matches the cinnabar's completion.
> Looked at within, there is no knowing — for it is without form;
> joined with the outer world, one clearly sees it takes on shape.
> Through all the ages every man belongs to this same kind:
> calling himself king, calling himself sage, free to roam as he will.

The Handsome Monkey King led his troop of gibbons, macaques, and horse-monkeys, and assigned to each the rank of minister, lord, aide, or officer. By day they roamed Flower-Fruit Mountain; by night they lodged in the Water-Curtain Cave. United in fellowship and of one mind, they joined neither the flocks of flying birds nor the ranks of running beasts. He was king all by himself, and his delight was boundless.

> In spring they gathered the hundred flowers for food and drink;
> in summer they sought all manner of fruits to make their living.
> In autumn they harvested chestnuts and taro to mark the season;
> in winter they hunted out the yellow-essence herb to pass the year.

## The one small but far-reaching worry

The Handsome Monkey King enjoyed his innocent pleasures, and so it went — who could have foreseen it? — for three or four hundred years.

One day, in the midst of a merry feast with the troop, he suddenly grew downcast and troubled, and let fall tears. The monkeys hurriedly gathered around him, bowing, and asked: *Great King, what is the cause of your grief?* The Monkey King said: *Though I am now in my time of joy, I have one small but far-reaching worry, and that is why I grieve.*

The monkeys laughed. *Great King, how easily you are dissatisfied! Day after day we hold merry gatherings in fairy mountains and blessed lands, in ancient caves and spirit continents. We are not subject to the rule of the unicorn, nor governed by the phoenix, nor bound by any earthly king's authority. We are free and at ease, and ours is a blessing beyond measure. Why then this far-reaching worry that makes you grieve?*

The Monkey King said: *Today, though we are not bound by the laws of any human king, nor in dread of the majesty of birds and beasts, yet in years to come, when we grow old and our blood runs thin, there is old King Yama working in secret who has us in his charge. Once the body perishes, shall we not have lived in this world in vain, unable to abide forever among the ranks of the gods?*

When the monkeys heard these words, every one of them covered his face and wept, all troubled by the thought of impermanence. Just then, out from the ranks leaped a Common-Backed Gibbon, who cried in a loud, stern voice: *If the Great King has thoughts so far-reaching as these, it is truly what is called the awakening of the heart that seeks the Way! Now among all the Five Kinds of living creatures, there are just three sorts that are not subject to old King Yama.*

The Monkey King said: *Do you know which three sorts?*

The gibbon said: *They are the Buddhas, the Immortals, and the Holy Sages — these three. They escape the wheel of rebirth; they are neither born nor extinguished, and they live as long as heaven and earth, the mountains and the rivers.*

*Where do these three dwell?* the Monkey King asked.

*They dwell only within the world of Jambudvipa, in ancient caves and on fairy mountains*, said the gibbon.

When the Monkey King heard this, his heart was filled with joy, and he said: *Tomorrow I shall take leave of you all and go down from the mountain. I shall roam the corners of the sea on cloud, journey far to the ends of heaven, and seek out these three without fail, that I may learn the secret of deathless long life and forever escape the woe of King Yama.*

That one saying caused him to leap clear of the net of the wheel of rebirth, and led at last to the making of the Great Sage Equal to Heaven.

## The farewell banquet

The monkeys clapped and cheered in praise. *Excellent! Excellent!* they cried. *Tomorrow we will cross the ridges and climb the heights and gather a wide store of fruits, and set out a great banquet to see the Great King off.*

The next day the monkeys went out to pluck the immortal peaches and pick the rare fruits, to dig up the mountain yams and grub out the yellow-essence herb. Magic fungus and fragrant orchid, fairy grasses and rare blossoms — every sort and every kind, neatly arranged in good order, they laid out on the stone benches and stone tables, and set forth the immortal wine and immortal viands. You should have seen them:

> Golden pellets and pearly bullets, ones split red and ones grown yellow-ripe.
> The winter cherries, their color true and their taste sweet; the ripened plums, their flavor fragrant and tart.
> Fresh dragon-eyes, sweet of flesh and thin of skin; fire-lychees, small of stone and red of pulp.
> Crab-apples in jade-green fruit, offered still on the branch; loquats in pale-yellow husk, held up with the leaves.
> Hare-headed pears and chicken-heart dates, that slake the thirst, dispel vexation, and sober a drunken head.
> Fragrant peaches and softened apricots, sweet as the jade-broth and jasper nectar of the gods;
> crisp plums and bayberries, tart and cool as cream and curds and clarified butter.
> Ripe watermelons with red rind and black seeds; great persimmons of four lobes and yellow skin.
> Pomegranates split open, their cinnabar grains glowing like fiery crystal beads;
> chestnuts cracked apart, their hard kernels round as golden agate.
> Walnuts and ginkgo nuts to go with the tea; coconuts and grapes that can be made into wine.
> Hazelnuts, pine-nuts, torreya and quince heaped full on the platters; oranges, sugarcane, tangerines and bitter-oranges piled high upon the boards.
> Mountain yams roasted soft, yellow-essence herb stewed tender;
> poria and Job's-tears pounded fine, simmered to a broth over a low fire in the stone pot.
> Though the world of men may have its rarest delicacies, how can they match the calm contentment of the mountain monkeys?

The troop set the Handsome Monkey King in the seat of honor, and each ranged himself below according to his age. One by one they came up in turn to offer wine, to offer flowers, to offer fruit, and they drank deep all that day.

## The voyage across the Eastern Sea

The next day the Handsome Monkey King rose early and gave the order: *Little ones, break me some dry pine boughs and weave them into a raft, cut a bamboo pole for punting, gather some fruits and the like — I am going now.* He boarded the raft alone, pushed off with all his strength, and went drifting and tossing straight out into the waves of the great ocean. With a heaven-sent wind behind him, he made his crossing toward the borders of the Southern Continent of Jambu. The voyage was truly this:

> The heaven-born fairy monkey, deep in his cultivation of the Way,
> leaves the mountain, rides the raft, and takes the wind of heaven.
> Across the ocean, over the sea, he seeks the immortal Way;
> firm in will, with hidden heart, to build a great achievement.
> Having his share and his destiny, he sets aside worldly desires;
> free of sorrow, free of care, he meets the Origin-Dragon.
> Surely he must encounter one who knows the music of his heart,
> who will lay bare the source and stream, and all the myriad ways flow clear.

From the day he boarded his wooden raft, the southeast wind blew strong for days on end and carried him to the northwest shore — the border of the Southern Continent of Jambu. He tested the depth with his pole, chanced upon a shallow, abandoned the raft, and leaped up onto the bank.

Along the shore there were people fishing, catching wild geese, digging clams, and panning for salt. He went up close, played a trick, and made himself out to be a tiger to frighten them — so that those folk threw down their baskets and abandoned their nets and scattered in all directions, fleeing. The one who could not run he seized, stripped off his clothes, and learned, like a man, to put them on his own body. Swaggering and strutting, he passed through districts and crossed prefectures, and in the marketplaces learned the manners of men and the speech of men. By morning he ate and by night he lodged, his whole heart set on inquiring after the Way of the Buddhas, the Immortals, and the Holy Sages, and on finding some recipe for deathless long life.

But he saw that the people of the world were all men bent on fame and gain, and not one among them cared for his own life and destiny. It was indeed:

> Striving for fame and snatching at gain — when will it ever cease?
> Up early and to bed late, never their own masters.
> Riding the donkey and the mule, they dream of fine steeds;
> holding the office of prime minister, they hope for the rank of king.
> They worry only over food and clothing and steep themselves in toil,
> never fearing that King Yama will come to hook them away.
> Sons after them and grandsons in their shade, they scheme for wealth and rank,
> and not a single one is willing to turn his head and look back.

The Monkey King sought everywhere after the Way of the immortals, but found no destined meeting. Through the Southern Continent of Jambu he wandered, threading the great cities and roaming the little counties, until before he knew it eight or nine years had passed.

Then suddenly he came to the great Western Ocean, and he thought to himself that beyond the sea there must surely be immortals. As before, he built himself a raft alone and drifted across the Western Sea until he reached the borders of the Western Continent of Cattle-Offering. He went ashore and searched about for a long while, until suddenly he saw a tall mountain, fair and lovely, its forests and foothills deep and secluded. Fearing neither wolves nor insects, neither tigers nor leopards, he climbed to the very summit to look about.

> A thousand peaks ranged like halberds, ten thousand fathoms spread like a screen.
> The sun lights the mountain mist and lightly locks the green; the rain clears and the dark hue coldly holds the blue.
> Withered vines twine the old trees; an ancient ford marks off the secret path.
> Rare flowers and auspicious grasses, slender bamboo and towering pine.
> For ten thousand years ever green, putting the blessed land to shame;
> never fading through the four seasons, outdoing fairy Penglai.
> The secret birds call near at hand; the spring at the source murmurs clear.
> Fold upon fold of valleys, ringed about with fungus and orchid; everywhere the steep cliffs grow with moss and lichen.
> The rolling crests are dragon-veins of good fortune — surely here some lofty man hides his name and dwells.

## The song of the woodcutter

As he gazed about, he suddenly heard from the depths of the forest the sound of someone speaking. He quickened his step, pressed into the wood, cocked his ear, and listened — and found that it was the sound of singing. The song went:

> Watching the chess, the axe-handle rots; chopping wood, the strokes ring out — *ding, ding*.
> By the cloud's edge, at the valley's mouth, I stroll at ease.
> Selling firewood to buy my wine, I laugh wild and delight in myself.
> The green path, the high autumn — facing the moon, I pillow my head on a pine-root,
> and sleep a sleep till the sky grows light.
> I know the old wood, climb the cliffs and cross the ridges,
> and with my axe cut the withered vines.
> When I have gathered a full load, I go singing along the market street
> and trade it for three measures of rice.
> No haggling, no contention — the price of the day is fair and even.
> I know no cunning schemes or clever reckonings;
> without glory or disgrace, in calm and plainness I lengthen out my life.
> Those I meet upon the way are immortals, or else followers of the Way,
> who sit in stillness and expound the *Yellow Court*.

When the Handsome Monkey King heard these words, his heart was full of joy. *So the immortals are hidden away in here!* he said. At once he sprang inside the wood, and looking carefully about, he saw it was only a woodcutter, raising his axe to chop firewood. The man was strangely dressed:

> On his head a broad bamboo hat, made of the sheath that a fresh bamboo-shoot has just cast off.
> On his body a cloth gown, made of the yarn that cotton has been twisted into.
> Round his waist a knotted girdle, made of the silk that the old silkworm's mouth has spun.
> On his feet straw sandals, woven of the stripped and dried sedge.
> In his hand a polished steel axe, and a hempen rope coiled at his side.
> For prying loose the pines and splitting the dead trees — what could be better than such a woodcutter?

The Monkey King drew near and called out: *Old immortal, your disciple makes his bow!*

The woodcutter hastily threw down his axe and turned to return the courtesy. *Not fitting, not fitting!* he said. *I am a poor, clumsy fellow who lacks even enough to eat and wear — how should I dare bear the two words 'immortal'?*

*If you are no immortal*, said the Monkey King, *how is it you speak the words of immortals?*

*What words of immortals did I speak?* the woodcutter said.

*Just now*, said the Monkey King, *as I came to the forest's edge, I heard you singing: 'Those I meet upon the way are immortals, or else followers of the Way, who sit in stillness and expound the* Yellow Court.' *The* Yellow Court *is a true scripture of the Way and its Power — if that is not an immortal, what is it?*

The woodcutter laughed. *I will tell you truly and hide nothing. This song is called* Fragrance Fills the Courtyard, *and it was an immortal who taught it to me. That immortal lives next door to my poor cottage. Seeing how I am worn down by household toil and daily vexations, he taught me that whenever I meet with troubles I should chant this song to myself — first to ease my mind, and second to dispel my weariness. Just now I had some hardships weighing on my thoughts, and so I was chanting it, never expecting that you would hear me.*

*Since your house is next door to an immortal's*, the Monkey King said, *why do you not follow him in his cultivation and learn some recipe for not growing old? Would that not be well?*

*My whole life has been bitter and hard*, said the woodcutter. *From my infancy I was raised and reared by my parents, and by the time I was eight or nine and had come to know the ways of men, my father, alas, died, and my mother lived on as a widow. I have neither brothers nor sisters — only myself, and no help for it but to wait upon her morning and evening. Now my mother is old, and all the more I dare not leave her side. Besides, my fields and garden lie fallow, and we lack enough food and clothing. So I can do nothing but cut a couple of bundles of firewood, carry them to the marketplace, sell them for a few coins, buy a few measures of rice, and cook it myself to set out some plain food and tea and provide for my old mother. That is why I cannot cultivate the Way.*

*By what you say*, the Monkey King replied, *you are a gentleman who practices filial piety, and in time to come good things will surely come of it. But I beg you to point me to that immortal's dwelling, that I may go and pay him a visit.*

*It is not far at all*, said the woodcutter. *This mountain is called the Mountain of the Spirit-Terrace and the Square-Inch; within the mountain there is a cave called the Cave of the Slanting Moon and Three Stars. In that cave there dwells an immortal called the Patriarch Subodhi. Of his disciples who have gone forth there is no counting the number, and even now some thirty or forty are cultivating the Way under him. You have only to follow that little path southward for seven or eight* li, *and you will come to his house.*

The Monkey King took hold of the woodcutter by the arm. *Elder brother*, he said, *come along with me. If I gain some benefit from it, I will surely not forget your kindness in showing me the way.*

*What a thick-headed fellow you are!* the woodcutter said. *I have just now told you everything, and still you do not understand. If I were to go with you, would I not be neglecting my livelihood? And who then would provide for my old mother? I have my firewood to cut — you go on by yourself, go on!*

Hearing this, the Monkey King had no choice but to take his leave.

## The Cave of the Slanting Moon and Three Stars

He came out of the deep forest, found the path, passed over a hillside, and after some seven or eight *li* caught sight of a cave-dwelling. He stood up straight and gazed at it — truly a splendid place:

> Mists and rosy clouds scatter their colors; sun and moon shake their light.
> A thousand ancient cypresses, ten thousand joints of slender bamboo.
> The cypresses, bearing the rain, blue-green and soft against the empty sky;
> the bamboo, wrapped in mist, filling a whole ravine with their somber hue.
> Outside the gate, rare flowers spread like brocade; beside the bridge, fairy grasses breathe out their fragrance.
> The stone cliffs jut up, their green moss moist; the hanging walls stretch high, their emerald lichen long.
> Now and again one hears the cry of fairy cranes; ever one sees the soaring of phoenixes.
> When the fairy crane cries, its voice shakes the ninefold marsh and reaches the far heavens;
> when the phoenix soars up, its plumage of five colors glows like a rainbow cloud.
> Dark gibbons and white deer come and go half-hidden; golden lions and jade elephants roam or hide at will.
> Look closely on this blessed spirit-land — truly it rivals heaven itself.

The cave-gate was tightly shut, all hushed and still, with no trace of anyone. Suddenly turning his head, he spotted at the edge of the cliff a stone tablet standing some three *zhang* and more in height and eight *chi* and more in breadth. A single line of ten great characters was carved upon it: *The Mountain of the Spirit-Terrace and the Square-Inch; the Cave of the Slanting Moon and Three Stars.*

The Handsome Monkey King was greatly delighted. *The people here are honest folk indeed*, he said. *There truly is such a mountain and such a cave.* He gazed at it for a long while but did not dare to knock at the gate. Instead he leaped up to the tips of the pine boughs, plucked pine-seeds, and ate them as he played.

After a little while, he heard the cave-gate creak open, and out from within came a fairy lad, truly of fair and noble bearing, of pure and uncommon looks, quite unlike the common run of worldly folk:

> His twin tufts of hair bound with cords of silk; his wide robe with the wind filling both its sleeves.
> His features and his form set him apart; his heart and his appearance both were void.
> A long-lived guest beyond the things of this world, an ever-youthful lad of the mountains.
> No speck of dust could stain him at all; let the years and calendars wheel as they would.

The lad came out the gate and called aloud: *Who is it making a disturbance here?*

The Monkey King leaped down from the tree with a flop, came forward, and bowed low. *Fairy lad*, he said, *I am a disciple come to seek the Way and to learn to be an immortal. I would never dare make a disturbance here.*

The fairy lad laughed. *You are one seeking the Way?*

*I am*, said the Monkey King.

*My master has just now risen from his couch and mounted the dais to expound the Way*, the lad said. *Before he had even named his reason, he sent me out to open the gate, saying: 'There is one cultivating the Way come outside — go and receive him.' It must surely be you?*

The Monkey King laughed. *It is I, it is I.*

*Come in with me, then*, the lad said.

## Before the Patriarch Subodhi

The Monkey King straightened his clothes, composed himself with reverence, and followed the lad straight into the deep recesses of the cave-heaven. Tier upon tier of deep towers and jasper pavilions, court within court of pearl palaces and conch-shell gateways — there was no telling the number of those quiet chambers and secluded dwellings. They came at last to the foot of a jasper terrace, where he saw the Patriarch Subodhi seated upright upon the dais, with thirty lesser immortals standing in attendance on either side below. Truly he was:

> The Great Enlightened Golden Immortal, of a form without stain, the Patriarch Bodhi of the wondrous likeness of the West.
> Unborn, undying, in the threefold-threefold practice; whole in vital force, whole in spirit, in his myriad-fold compassion.
> Empty and still, of himself he follows transformation; in true suchness, his original nature acts as it will.
> Of a majestic body that lives as long as heaven; a great Dharma-master whose heart shines clear through aeon upon aeon.

The moment the Handsome Monkey King saw him, he flung himself down and kowtowed without ceasing, his mouth saying only: *Master, master! Your disciple pays homage with his whole heart, pays homage with his whole heart!*

The Patriarch said: *From what region do you come? First state your native place and your name clearly, and then bow again.*

The Monkey King said: *Your disciple is a native of the Water-Curtain Cave on Flower-Fruit Mountain, in the country of Aolai, in the Eastern Continent of Spiritual Excellence.*

The Patriarch shouted his command: *Drive him out! He is nothing but a fellow who deals in lies and deceit — how should such a one cultivate any fruit of the Way?*

The Monkey King knocked his head against the ground without ceasing. *Your disciple's words are honest*, he cried. *There is surely no falsehood or deceit.*

*If you are honest*, the Patriarch said, *how is it you speak of the Eastern Continent of Spiritual Excellence? Between that place and here lie two great oceans and the whole Southern Continent of Jambu — how could you ever have come here?*

The Monkey King kowtowed and said: *Your disciple drifted across the oceans and over the seas, made landfall and roamed about for some ten years and more, before at last I found my way to this place.*

*Well*, said the Patriarch, *if you came gradually, step by step, that may pass. What is your surname?*

The Monkey King said: *I have no temper. If men curse me, I am not vexed; if they strike me, I am not angered. I merely make my apology and let it go. All my life I have had no temper.*

*I do not mean that kind of temper*, the Patriarch said. *I mean — what was your father's and mother's surname?*

*I have no father or mother either*, the Monkey King said.

*If you have no father or mother*, said the Patriarch, *then you must have grown on a tree.*

*Though I did not grow on a tree*, the Monkey King said, *I grew out of a stone. I remember only that on Flower-Fruit Mountain there was a magic stone, and that in that year the stone burst open, and so I was born.*

When the Patriarch heard this, he was secretly pleased. *Then you are a being formed by heaven and earth*, he said. *Get up and walk about, and let me see you.*

The Monkey King sprang up and went hobbling and rolling about twice. The Patriarch laughed. *Though your body is coarse and mean, you are like nothing so much as a pine-nut-eating macaque. I will take a surname for you from your own body. My thought is to give you the surname* Hu. *But if you take the beast-radical from the character* hu, *what remains is* gu *and* yue *— 'ancient' and 'moon.' Yet 'ancient' means old, and 'moon' belongs to the yin; and what is old and yin cannot transform and bring forth life. Better that I give you the surname* Sun. *If you take the beast-radical from the character* sun, *what remains is* zi *and* xi *— 'child' and 'small.' 'Child' means a young boy, and 'small' means an infant — which fits exactly the root meaning of the newborn babe. Let your surname be* Sun.

When the Monkey King heard this, his heart was filled with joy, and he kowtowed up toward the dais. *Splendid! Splendid! Splendid!* he said. *Today at last I know I have a surname! I beg the master in his mercy — since now I have a surname, grant me also a personal name, so that it will be convenient to call me.*

*Within my school there are twelve characters by which names are assigned in order*, the Patriarch said, *and you fall in the tenth generation of little disciples.*

*Which twelve characters?* the Monkey King asked.

*They are* Guang, Da, Zhi, Hui, Zhen, Ru, Xing, Hai, Ying, Wu, Yuan, Jue *— twelve characters. Counting down to you, it falls upon the character* Wu, *'Awakened.' I will give you the Dharma-name Sun Wukong — 'Monkey Awakened to Emptiness.' How is that?*

The Monkey King laughed. *Splendid! Splendid! Splendid! From this day on I am called Sun Wukong!*

It was indeed:

> When the primal murk was first cleft, there was no surname at all;
> to shatter stubborn emptiness, one must awaken to emptiness.

What fruit of the Way Sun Wukong was to cultivate from this point on — there is no knowing yet, and the next chapter will tell.
