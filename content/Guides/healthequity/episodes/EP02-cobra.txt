## Audience

Adults who have recently lost employer-sponsored health coverage — through job loss, reduced hours, divorce, or a dependent aging off a parent's plan — and need to understand their options clearly and quickly. Treat the listener as someone making a real financial decision under stress, not as a reader curious about how the law works in the abstract.

## Angle

Personal application — the walkthrough is actionable first. Walk the listener through the same sequence of decisions they face: is COBRA right for me, when do I have to decide, what does it cost, and how do I compare it to the alternatives? The law is the background, not the subject of the conversation.

---

## Central tensions

1. COBRA is expensive — often 600 to 1,500 dollars a month or more — because the listener now pays the full premium the employer used to split. The shock is real. But for someone mid-treatment, pregnant, or with a child on an ongoing prescription, the continuity it provides may be worth more than the cost.

2. The 60-day decision window is the most critical deadline in this process, and most people do not know it exists until it is almost closed. Missing it means losing the right to coverage entirely — not temporarily, permanently.

3. Marketplace plans are often cheaper than COBRA for healthy people, but switching mid-treatment can disrupt care. The right choice depends on what is actually happening in the listener's healthcare life right now.

---

## Background

COBRA — the Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act, passed in 1985 — gives employees and covered family members the right to continue employer-sponsored health coverage for a set period after a life change ends it. Employers with 20 or more employees that maintain group health plans are required to offer it. For employee job loss or reduced hours: coverage continues for up to 18 months. For a spouse or dependent whose coverage ends due to death, divorce, or a child aging off the plan: up to 36 months. The participant pays the full premium plus a 2 percent administrative fee. HealthEquity administers COBRA on behalf of employers, managing notices, enrollment, billing, and compliance.

## Beat 1 — The moment and the clock (15 to 18 minutes)

Host B just found out her coverage is ending. Host A explains what COBRA is in one sentence: you can keep the exact health plan you had — same network, same doctors, same prescription coverage — but you pay the full monthly cost yourself.

State the central thesis verbatim (first time).

Then: the 60-day decision window. From the date the election notice arrives, the listener has 60 days to choose COBRA or lose the option permanently. This deadline is the most important fact in the episode. Make it land with weight.

Walk through what the election notice is: a letter from HealthEquity explaining the options, the costs, and how to enroll. It arrives after the employer notifies HealthEquity that coverage has ended. The listener's job is to read it the moment it arrives and note the deadline date.

Address the medical urgency angle directly: if anything medical is happening right now — ongoing treatment, a pregnancy, an upcoming procedure — the guidance is clear. Elect COBRA first. Evaluate cost second. The retroactive protection (explained in Beat 3) means there is time to make the financial comparison, but the election window does not wait.

Cover who is eligible: employees who lose coverage due to job loss or hour reduction, and their covered spouses and dependents. Cover which plans it applies to: employers with 20 or more employees offering a group health plan. Medical, dental, and vision are covered. Life insurance and disability are not.

## Beat 2 — The cost reality and the comparison (18 to 20 minutes)

Walk the listener through the math. When employed, the employer paid 70 to 80 percent of the monthly premium. The employee paid only the remainder — and it felt manageable because it was deducted before they saw the money. With COBRA, the listener pays all of it plus a 2 percent administrative fee.

Use the worked example from the source: if the employer's family plan cost $1,800 a month total and the employee paid $400 a month, COBRA costs $1,836 a month — $1,800 plus 2 percent. That shock is real and common.

For a single person, COBRA commonly runs $600 to $1,500 a month depending on the plan. For a family, it frequently exceeds $1,500 a month.

Then walk through the genuine comparison with marketplace alternatives. Losing job coverage is a qualifying life event that opens a 60-day special enrollment window on the marketplace — no need to wait for open enrollment. Marketplace plans can be substantially cheaper, sometimes $200 to $400 a month for a healthy individual, and income-based subsidies may apply.

The two-question test: Is anything medical happening right now — ongoing treatment, a pregnancy, a scheduled surgery, a child mid-orthodontia, an ongoing specialist relationship? If yes, COBRA continuity is likely worth the cost. The disruption of switching plans mid-treatment can exceed the monthly premium difference. If nothing is happening medically, the marketplace may offer significantly better value.

State the central thesis verbatim (second time).

Walk through the step-by-step marketplace comparison: go to healthcare.gov, enter household income and location, compare plans, look at the network — does it include current doctors? — and compare the monthly premium plus deductible. The comparison takes about 30 minutes and is worth doing before the 60-day window closes.

---

## Beat 3 — The retroactive protection and what to do now (12 to 15 minutes)

One feature most people miss: COBRA coverage is retroactive to the date coverage ended. If something medical happens during the 60-day window before the listener has decided, they can still elect COBRA, pay the back premiums, and have that event covered.

Use the timeline example: coverage ends June 1. On July 10 the listener goes to urgent care. On July 25 they elect COBRA and pay premiums for June 1 through July 25. The July 10 urgent care visit is covered. This retroactive clock is a genuine safety net — not a reason to delay, but a reason to understand the full window.

Address common mistakes: missing the election notice (watch the mail carefully in the weeks after job loss), assuming COBRA is automatic (it requires an active election), not setting up automatic payment (a missed payment ends coverage with no reinstatement), and failing to do the marketplace comparison before deciding.

Close with the action sequence: find the last day of employer coverage, watch for the election notice from HealthEquity, compare COBRA cost against marketplace options, and decide before the 60-day window closes.

State the central thesis verbatim (third time).

## Tone constraints

Three governing analogies — use these and no others:

1. **The bridge** — COBRA is not new insurance; it is a bridge that lets the listener stay on the same road — same plan, same network — while they figure out where they are going next.
2. **The retroactive clock** — the 60-day window works backward: electing on day 59 covers everything since day one of coverage loss, as long as the back premiums are paid.
3. **The two-question test** — the only two questions that determine whether COBRA is worth the cost: Is anything medical happening right now? Can I find equivalent coverage on the marketplace for less?

Do not introduce new analogies mid-episode.
