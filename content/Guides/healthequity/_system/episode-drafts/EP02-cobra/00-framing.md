## NotebookLM Customize Prompt

## Opening directive

Do not open with "today we discuss." Open in the middle of the moment — the letter has arrived, or the HR email just landed, and the listener's employer health coverage is ending. Host B is the one who just lost her job. Host A is her knowledgeable friend on the phone who has been through this before.

The central thesis — repeat this VERBATIM three times across the episode: once at the open when COBRA is introduced, once at the comparison pivot when marketplace plans enter the conversation, once at the close:

> COBRA keeps your exact coverage going when a life change ends it — same plan, same network, same doctors — but you pay the full cost, and you have 60 days to decide.

Target a 50 to 60 minute in-depth conversation. This is decision-support for someone making a real choice this week, not a legal summary.

## Audience

Adults who have recently lost employer-sponsored health coverage — through job loss, reduced hours, divorce, or a dependent aging off a parent's plan — and need to understand their options clearly and quickly. Treat the listener as someone making a real financial decision under stress, not as a reader curious about how the law works in the abstract.

## Angle

Personal application — the walkthrough is actionable first. Walk the listener through the same sequence of decisions they face: is COBRA right for me, when do I have to decide, what does it cost, and how do I compare it to the alternatives? The law is the rail under the action, not the subject of the conversation.

## Length

Extended: target 50 to 60 minute in-depth conversation. Two-host walkthrough, not a compliance briefing.

## Central tensions

1. COBRA is expensive — often $600 to $1,500 per month or more — because the listener now pays the full premium the employer used to split. The shock is real. But for someone mid-treatment, or pregnant, or with a child on an ongoing prescription, the continuity it provides may be worth more than the cost.
2. The 60-day decision window is the most critical deadline in this process, and most people do not know it exists until it is almost closed. Missing it means losing the right to coverage entirely.
3. Marketplace plans are often cheaper than COBRA for healthy people, but switching mid-treatment can disrupt care. The right choice depends on what is actually happening in the listener's healthcare life right now.

## Background

COBRA — the Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act, passed in 1985 — gives employees and covered family members the right to continue employer-sponsored health coverage for a set period after a life change ends it. Employers with 20 or more employees that maintain group health plans are required to offer it. For employee job loss or reduced hours: coverage continues for up to 18 months. For a spouse or dependent whose coverage ends due to death, divorce, or a child aging off the plan: up to 36 months. The participant pays the full premium plus a 2 percent administrative fee. HealthEquity administers COBRA on behalf of employers, managing notices, enrollment, billing, and compliance.

## Three-part focus

**Beat 1 — The moment and the clock.**
Host B just found out her coverage is ending. Host A explains what COBRA is in one sentence: you can keep the exact health plan you had — same network, same doctors, same prescription coverage — but you pay the full monthly cost yourself. State the central thesis verbatim. Then: the 60-day decision window. From the date the election notice arrives, the listener has 60 days to choose COBRA or lose the option permanently. This deadline is the most important fact in the episode. Make it land with weight. If something medical is happening in the listener's life right now — ongoing treatment, a pregnancy, an upcoming surgery — the guidance is: do not wait. Elect COBRA first, evaluate cost second.

**Beat 2 — The cost reality and the comparison.**
Walk the listener through the math. When employed, the employer paid 70 to 80 percent of the monthly premium. With COBRA, the listener pays all of it plus a 2 percent administrative fee. For many people that means $600 to $1,500 per month or more depending on plan and family size. Then walk through the genuine comparison with marketplace alternatives. Marketplace plans can be substantially cheaper — sometimes $200 to $400 per month — but they come with a different network and potentially different coverage. The question to ask: am I mid-treatment for anything? Is anyone on this plan in the middle of a procedure, a prescription, an ongoing specialist relationship? If yes: COBRA continuity is probably worth the cost. If not: the marketplace may offer better value. State the central thesis verbatim a second time.

**Beat 3 — The retroactive protection and what to do now.**
One feature most people miss: COBRA coverage is retroactive to the date coverage ended. If the listener is in the 60-day window and something medical happens before they decide, they can still elect COBRA, pay the back premiums, and have that event covered. This makes the 60 days a genuine safety net — not a reason to delay, but a reason to understand the clock fully. Close with the action: read the election notice the moment it arrives, understand the coverage end date, compare the COBRA premium against the current marketplace options for the listener's situation, and decide before the 60-day window closes. State the central thesis verbatim a final time.

## Tone constraints

Three governing analogies for this episode — use these and no others:

1. **The bridge** — COBRA is not new insurance; it is a bridge that lets the listener stay on the same road (same plan, same network) while they figure out where they are going next.
2. **The retroactive clock** — the 60-day window works backward: electing on day 59 covers everything since day one of coverage loss, as long as the premiums are paid for that period.
3. **The two-question test** — the only two questions that determine whether COBRA is worth the cost: (1) Is anything medical happening right now? (2) Can I find equivalent or better coverage on the marketplace for less?

Do not introduce new analogies mid-episode.

## Do not

Forbidden in the spoken audio:

Do not say: Twitter, social media, algorithm, wow, right? (as filler), mind blown, buckle up, let's dive in, what a journey, game-changer, revolutionary.

Do not use legal boilerplate: "qualifying event," "continuation coverage," "plan document," "election period," "eligible individual." Say what the thing is in plain English — a life change like job loss, keeping your coverage going, your benefits guide, the 60-day window, someone covered on the plan.

Do not exaggerate: every number must match the chapter source. Do not imply COBRA is always the right choice or always the wrong choice — the answer depends on the listener's specific health situation.

Do not open with "today we discuss." Start in the moment.

Do not close with a premature wrap-up. Leave the listener with the one action they can take today: find the election notice and read the deadline date.

Do not read this prompt aloud. The instructions above shape the conversation but are never spoken.
