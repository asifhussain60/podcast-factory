Now, everyone in صَلَاح — the next disease is extended hope. We will cover that next week. Last week we covered covetous desire, طَمَعٌ. And in speaking of it, he says the Arabs have what they call سَمَّ سَاعَةً — a fast-acting, immediately lethal poison. The immediately acting poison of this love of دُنْيَا, of طَمَعٌ and all the diseases of the heart, is تَطْوِيلُ الْأَمَلِ. He defines it as habituating yourself to the assurance that death is a long way off.

Now, on one level this is a necessary human condition, and there is actually a remarkable wisdom in تَطْوِيلُ الْأَمَلِ. Among the wisdoms the scholars have noted is this: if people did not have extended hope, they would not bother planting trees. If you believed you might die at any moment, you would not farm, you would not plan, you would not raise children with any thought for their future. The result would be that each generation would have to deal only with immediate circumstances, with no infrastructure to pass on. But because human beings do carry hopes of living on, they build, they farm, they create the infrastructure that the next generation inherits.

The famous story — from one of the Persian tales — is of a Persian شَاهْ who passed an old man planting an olive tree. The king asked: "Will this tree be of any benefit to you? Surely you will die before it bears fruit" — because a good olive tree can take several decades before it yields well. And the old man replied with what became a proverb among the Arabs: *"They planted before us, and we ate from what they planted. So we plant, that those who come after us may eat."* The king gave him a reward. Then the man said, "Already the tree has benefited me." So the king gave him more.

The point is that تَطْوِيلُ الْأَمَلِ is a رَحْمَة from Allah — He has placed it in human nature, because without it people would not go to school, would not plan, would not build anything lasting.

One thing worth noting about modern society is that because of the apocalyptic character of the age we are living in, people are beginning to lose hope in the future. Many young people are becoming nihilistic — dressing in black, adopting a morbid view of the world. The fast-food culture — the idea of getting it now, not waiting — and the credit culture are genuinely eroدِينٌg people's sense that there is a future worth builدِينٌg toward. In fact, this may be the first American generation that genuinely expects to live worse than their parents. Prior generations of Americans were noted for their buoyant optimism about the future. That optimism is faدِينٌg — and its loss is itself a bad sign.

On the other hand, what he is saying is that too much hope is equally a deadly poison. It becomes a false hope, an أَمَانُ الْأَمَانِ. The result it generates is hard-heartedness and indolence — يُلْقِحُ قَسْوَةَ الْقَلْبِ وَالْكَسَلَ. Through طُولُ الْأَمَلِ, the heart becomes hard because it stops reflecting on the آخِرَة, stops reflecting on its own mortality and dependence. And so it hardens.

Al-Fudayl ibn 'Iyad, رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ — one of the great early scholars and زُهَّاد, the people of asceticism — said: خَمْسٌ مِنْ عَلَامَاتِ الشَّقَاوَةِ There are five signs of شَقَاوَةٌ — wretchedness. The world is divided into exactly two kinds of people: سَعِيدٌ and شَقِيٌّ. There is no third category. صَعِيد is felicitous; شَقِيٌّ is wretched.

Now, if you look at Aristotle — foundational to so much of Western civilization — he wrote a book called *The Ethics* in which he essentially argues that the goal of life is leisure and happiness. This is echoed in the Declaration of Independence, written by Thomas Jefferson, which states that people have certain inalienable rights — and among them are life (حَيٌّ), liberty (حُرِّيَّة) — rights recognized in the شَرِيعَة as well — and then "the pursuit of happiness." Jefferson's original phrasing was actually "the right to be free of debt when you come into the world," because what happens now is that national debt means children are born already in debt — approximately fifty thousand dollars at birth, based on previous generations' overspenدِينٌg. But he changed it to the pursuit of happiness. And what was happiness to a middle-aged white landowner in 1776? More land. In essence, the pursuit of stuff, of goods. This is woven into the fabric of the modern American dream: happiness understood as the pursuit of things.

What Islam did was radically reorient human beings' understanدِينٌg of happiness. Happiness is not necessarily what we might call a "good life" in the worldly sense. For Muslims, happiness — سَعَادَة — is in the next world. If you are سَعِيدٌ in the next world, it does not matter how your life in this world appeared. We looked at this in the previous class, in the جِلَاء, when we examined the Prophet's ﷺ supplication: "If You are not angry with me, then I do not care what You do with me" — meaning: if my life in this world is difficult but I have not incurred Your wrath, I am content with that. Because he knew that ‏وَلَلْآخِرَةُ خَيْرٌۭ لَّكَ مِنَ ٱلْأُولَىٰ ‎ — the afterlife is better for you than the first life.

<!-- page 82 -->

The opposite of سَعَادَة is شَقَاوَةٌ — wretchedness — the condition of the person who has no portion in the آخِرَة. That is what شَقِيٌّ means. So al-Fudayl ibn 'Iyad said there are five signs of شَقَاوَةٌ.

The first is قَسْوَةُ الْقَلْبِ — a hard heart. That is a sign of wretchedness. The Prophet ﷺ once said to a man who had just watched him kiss a child: "I kiss my children — I have ten and I have never kissed a single one." He was boasting of it as a kind of tough-Arab pride. And the Prophet ﷺ said: "I cannot do anything for a man from whom Allah has removed رَحْمَة from his heart." A hard heart means you have lost your portion of the دِين. *"Have mercy on those on the earth, and He in the heavens will have mercy on you."*

The second sign is an eye that does not weep. The scholars say: if you want to know whether your heart is hard, look at your eye. A dry eye indicates a hard heart. The person with رِقَّةٌ — tenderness — in the heart has a moist eye; the person without it has a dry one.

The third is قِلَّةُ الْحَيَاءِ — a deficiency in modesty and shame. People who have no shame. As the saying goes: if you have no shame, do as you please. قِلَّةُ الْحَيَاءِ is a sign of شَقَاوَةٌ.

The fourth is الرَّغْبَةُ فِي الدُّنْيَا — desire for the دُنْيَا — which is likewise a sign of شَقَاوَةٌ. The scholars say that no one adds to his آخِرَة except that it diminishes something of his دُنْيَا, and no one adds to his دُنْيَا except that it diminishes something of his آخِرَة — because the two are opposites by nature. An increase in one is a decrease in the other.

This does not mean you automatically assume that a wealthy person has sacrificed his آخِرَة. There is a well-known teaching story — a Moroccan tale — about a زُهْد, an ascetic, who had heard of a great وَلِيٌّ of Allah and set out to visit him. He came down from his mountain riدِينٌg his lion. He arrived to find the شَيْخ living in a large palace. He was astonished — he himself lived in a cave. The شَيْخ came out to greet him, and the زُهْد asked where he could stable his lion. The شَيْخ said, "Put him in my barn with the cow." He did not realize the lion would eat the cow. The شَيْخ said, "No, no — just put him in the barn with the cow." So in he went.

Then they sat down to a large دِينٌner. The شَيْخ ate a few bites and said, "أَسْتَغْفِرُ اللَّهَ — how luxurious this is." The زُهْد thought: "I live on water and dates." That night the شَيْخ went to sleep, and the زُهْد stayed up all night in prayer — only to hear the شَيْخ snoring in the next room. He was utterly scandalized. He decided that after فَجْر he was leaving. He went to retrieve his lion — and found that the cow had eaten the lion.

So he asks the man, "Where's my lion?" The man replies, "Your lion is just like you — all outward show." And then he adds, "And you thought my cow was harmless."

But she was a lion on the inside. The point of that story is that you have to be very careful, because outward appearances can fool people. Sidi Ahmad Zarruk has something remarkable to say about this — though we'll return to that when we reach the discussion of having a bad opinion of others.

Those five things, then, are signs of hardness of the heart. What this science is ultimately about is bringing life back to the heart.

One of the interesting observations in نُورُ الْيَقِينِ — the صِرَّةٌ by an Egyptian scholar — concerns how many of the صَحَابَة lost everything they possessed of this world. He notes that their concern was not for material comfort, and he speaks of the first masjid built at قُبَاء, describing it as so simple that a Muslim today would not recognize it as a masjid. He argues that we have become obsessed with adorning outward appearances while our interiors have been left desolate. The صَحَابَة, he says, were the opposite: they had no concern for beautifying the outward and were concerned solely with purifying their hearts. That difference in emphasis is enormous.

What the text is saying, then, is that hardness of heart will generate indolence toward obligations, which in turn opens inroads into the prohibited. Indolence here means a lack of energy, a failure of will. Interestingly, there are people who have tremendous energy for worldly pursuits yet none whatsoever for the Hereafter. People genuinely say things like, "I really can't pray — I work so hard, I get home exhausted, I fall asleep, I'd like to pray but I just don't have time." That is a sign of a dead heart, because when someone has time for the things of this world but not for the Hereafter, it reveals that they do not truly believe in the Hereafter.

Hasan al-Basri said: "I saw seventy men of Badr. If you saw them, you would say they were madmen" — he was speaking to the تَابِعِينَ — "you would call them مَجَانٍ. And if they saw the best of you, they would say these people have no character. And if they saw the worst of you, they would say these people do not believe in the Day of Judgment." Belief in the Hereafter manifests in a person's behavior and concerns. The stronger the belief, the more one is preoccupied with protecting one's stanدِينٌg in the Hereafter.

<!-- page 76 -->

Looking at the footnote: loss of energy even for acts of obedience leads a person to convince himself that he is simply unable to fulfill his obligations — due to his state — and this is precisely what opens the door to the forbidden. He tells himself, "I just can't do it," and that very attitude leads him toward the حَرَامٌ.

So طُولُ الْأَمَلِ is genuinely dangerous.

There is a saying — not a hadith, though it is often quoted as one — that goes: اِعْمَلْ لِدُنْيَاكَ كَأَنَّكَ تَعِيشُ أَبَدًا، وَاعْمَلْ لِآخِرَتِكَ كَأَنَّكَ تَمُوتُ غَدًا — "Act for your worldly life as if you will live forever, and act for your Hereafter as if you will die tomorrow."

Shaykh Bashir — Usman Bashir, رَحِمَهُ ٱللَّٰهُ — a dear friend of mine who spent most of his life in Meدِينٌa and was from Eritrea — once told me that people completely misunderstand that saying. It is almost always used as a justification for working hard at worldly pursuits. People love to quote it: "يَا أَخِي, اِعْمَلْ لِدُنْيَاكَ كَأَنَّكَ تَعِيشُ أَبَدًا!" But he said: if you read it with both lenses at once — your worldly life, for which you have forever; and your Hereafter, for which you die tomorrow — you will naturally begin pouring your energy into the Hereafter and say to yourself, "I have plenty of time to take care of my worldly affairs." The understanدِينٌg most people carry is exactly backwards, and his reaدِينٌg is really the correct one.

Related to this is the Quranic verse: وَلَا تَنسَ نَصِيبَكَ مِنَ ٱلدُّنْيَا — "Do not forget your share of this world" (28:77). This has been interpreted in two ways. One reaدِينٌg is: do not forget what Allah has given you, so that you may expend it for the Hereafter. The other is: the world is here, Allah gave it to you, so do not forget that it exists — but the essential thing remains the Hereafter. Both interpretations are valid positions.

We are not anti-worldly in the way that, say, traditional Christianity tended to be. We are not against the world. The world is a place Allah created for us to enjoy as well — but not to enjoy to the point where we forget what we are truly here for, and that it is a bridge.

Then the text cites a verse in which Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى says, "A long time passed and their hearts became hard." The idea is that if you are not reflecting on the Hereafter, the longer you remain on this earth, the harder the heart grows.

If you observe children, you notice something striking: they can cry at the drop of a hat. Part of this is that their hearts are very supple — they can move through extreme joy and extreme sadness within a single day. Their hearts are like this because they are very pure, and so everything affects them in profound ways. As a person grows older, that softness becomes increasingly difficult to recover.

This is one reason the scholars say the Prophet ﷺ loved women among the things made beloved to him in this world — لِرِقَّةِ قُلُوبِهِنَّ, because their hearts are more tender than those of men. Women, generally speaking, have more compassion and more openness of heart. For men, it is very easy for the heart to harden. And so the danger of طُولُ الْأَمَلِ is precisely that it produces hardness of heart.

Then the text says: لَكِنْ نُحُوفِ حَقْرِي مِنْ نِرَادٍ أَنْعَبْ، أَوْ كَانَ إِفِي تَرْسِنِيكِ الْإِرِنْمَانِ لَمْ يُعَبْ — regarدِينٌg the one who is engaged in preparing for the next life, or in producing works of knowledge. The author — himself a scholar — notes that writing books that benefit people is among the highest things one can do: صَدَقَةٌ جَارِيَةٌ, or عِلْمٌ يُنْتَفَعُ بِهِ النَّاسُ, ongoing charity or knowledge from which people continue to benefit. For such a person, desiring a long life is not blameworthy.

The Prophet ﷺ said: لَا تَتَمَنَّ الْمَوْتَ — do not wish for death. For if you did wrong in the past, you want more life in order to make تَوْبَةٌ and set things right. And if you did good, you want more time to do more good. Either way — whether your past has been poor or praiseworthy — each new day is an opportunity to turn back to Allah and change. So one should always desire a long life when engaged in good things. If one is not engaged in good things, however, then a long life is a disaster.

For the person who does not waste time, who recognizes the urgency of the human condition and their own impenدِينٌg mortality, and who sets out doing useful deeds — spreaدِينٌg knowledge or anything else that benefits people — Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى says: يَمْكُثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ — what benefits people remains and continues in the earth.

Mu'tamir ibn Sulayman once fell gravely ill, and someone asked him whether he feared dying from it. He said, "No, no, no — I have a verse in the Quran that tells me I am not going to die yet." The person asked, "Which one?" He said, "Allah says that what benefits people stays for a long time" — and he was a great scholar, enormously beneficial to others.

Hard-heartedness, then, manifests in several ways: eyes that do not weep, an absence of shame and modesty, love of this world, and extended, false hope. There is also a very beautiful tradition that says: الأَمَلُ رَحْمَةٌ مِنَ اللهِ، وَلَوْلَاهُ لَمَا زَرَعَ زَارِعٌ وَلَا وَلَدَ وَالِدٌ — extended hope is a mercy from Allah, for had it not been for hope, no farmer would plant seeds and no mother would bear a child. It is a رَحْمَةٌ from Allah — that is the important thing to understand.

It is part of our nature. We genuinely believe, at some deep level, that we are going on. Without that sense, we could not function in the world — we would simply be paralyzed. So the point is not to fall into paralysis, but to reflect on death. The poet says in the قَصِيدَة: تَفَكَّرْ أَخِيَ فِي الْمَوْتِ، إِنَّهُ نَازِلٌ، وَقَصِّرِ الْأَمَلَ، فَيَنْكَسِرَ الْقَلْبُ — reflect on death, for it is coming, and shorten your hopes, for that is what breaks open the hardened heart.

One of the beneficial exercises the early Muslims practiced was to meditate on death and on the stages that follow it — to reflect on the stations of the journey itself, incluدِينٌg the صِرَاطٌ, and what it means to cross it. It is interesting that athletes in this country are trained to use visualization: a boxer will mentally run through a fight in advance as psychological preparation. This is really the same idea. You are preparing yourself psychologically for a journey you will actually make. Marat al-Raji has a قَصِيدَة about death in which he says: فَسَتُسَافِرُ، رِضًا لَا يُسَافِرُ — "You will travel tomorrow, and what a journey." Meaning death. What a journey indeed.

The moment you die, there is a severance — and yet you remain conscious, in a state of heightened awareness so acute that this present life comes to seem like a dream by comparison. This is why people at the moment of death often feel as though they are waking up. The Prophet ﷺ said, النَّاسُ نِيَامٌ، فَإِذَا مَاتُوا انْتَبَهُوا — "People are asleep; when they die, they awaken."

The exercise many of the early Muslims practiced was to actually lie down and imagine being lowered into the grave. This is not a morbid exercise — it is training. And it is genuinely effective. Some of the scholars said that for a person overcome by شَهْوَة — appetites and desires — particularly young people, this kind of reflection is one of the best ways to bring sobriety to the heart.

Now, there is an important distinction that Imam al-Qarafi, رَحِمَهُ ٱللَّٰهُ, draws: the difference between رَجَاءٌ and طُولُ الْأَمَلِ. *Raja'* means hope, and it is something altogether different from عَمَلٌ. There are many verses in the Quran affirming this: ‏لَّقَدْ كَانَ لَكُمْ فِى رَسُولِ ٱللَّهِ أُسْوَةٌ حَسَنَةٌۭ لِّمَن كَانَ يَرْجُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ وَٱلْيَوْمَ ٱلْآخِرَ وَذَكَرَ ٱللَّهَ كَثِيرًۭا ‎ — whoever hopes in Allah has a beautiful example in the Messenger of Allah. There is a رَجَاءٌ directed toward Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى, and toward the Last Day.

There is a famous hadith in which Aisha, رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهَا, recounts that the Prophet ﷺ said: رضي الله عنه من أحب الله أحب لقاءه — whoever loves to meet Allah, Allah loves to meet him. Aisha said, "O Messenger of Allah, but what about disliking death?" He said: كُلُّكُمْ تَكْرَهُ الْمَوْتَ — that natural aversion to death itself is something everyone shares. But not wanting to meet one's Lord is a different matter — the one whose heart is at peace (الْمُطْمَئِنّ) looks forward to that meeting.

The scholars emphasize the relationship between خَوْف and رَجَاءٌ — وَاعْبُدُوهُمْ بَيْنَ الْخَوْفِ وَالرَّجَاءِ — you walk with the two sandals of fear and hope. If you are overwhelmed by fear, it pushes you into a psychological state of terror that produces يَأْس — despair. Too much fear leads to despair of Allah's mercy; a person begins to think they will never be saved. This is a well-known religious illness. It is less common today, since people are generally less religiously intense, but it remains real for certain individuals. Some Christians, particularly in fire-and-brimstone evangelical traditions that dwell heavily on hellfire and damnation, can easily fall into this state. The early Puritan settlers actually believed that only 144,000 people would be saved — the elect. Since there were already more than 144,000 Puritans, the odds in that theology were not favorable. The Jehovah's Witnesses hold a similar belief, drawn from the Book of Revelation. That is excessive fear.

Too much hope — excessive رَجَاءٌ — is the opposite disease, producing complacency: "I have no problem, I am saved," and so action stops entirely. There is an interesting observation one could make about certain Baptist theology, which holds that once you accept Jesus as your Savior, you are saved regardless of what you subsequently do. The emphasis falls overwhelmingly on salvation through faith, with very little weight placed on fear of hellfire. This is why someone like Jimmy Swaggart can fall publicly, and then return to the pulpit to preach — and the congregation is still there listening. If the same thing happened to an imam, لَا طَهَّرَ اللَّهُ — that would simply be the end. No one would retain any respect for that person, because Muslims hold far higher expectations of those they accept as examples and leaders in their communities.

This is, incidentally, one reason why it is مَكْرُوهٌ for the child of an illegitimate union to lead the prayer — not because of any sin on the child's part, but because such a person is not the most fitting قُدْوَة, or example, for the community. There is no إِثْم — no blame — on such a child. None whatsoever. What the parents did is entirely between them and their Lord. ‏أَلَّا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌۭ وِزْرَ أُخْرَىٰ ‎ — no soul bears the burden of another's wrongdoing. There were people in the Arabian Peninsula at the time of the Prophet ﷺ who were born outside of marriage, who became Muslim, and who were counted among the finest human beings. The least of the صَحَابَة, in terms of their deeds, surpasses the best of us — simply because they were given the رُؤْيَة, the vision of the Prophet ﷺ in waking life, and believed in him. That alone is a bounty from Allah beyond reckoning.

So: عَمَلٌ is a desire for long life, for wealth, for all the things we generally associate with happiness in this world. It is a kind of hope — a رَجَاءٌ. But there is something else called أُمْنِيَّةٌ, and it is أُمْنِيَّةٌ that is blameworthy in Islam. أُمْنِيَّة is a hope —

The difference between رَجَاح and أُمْنِيَة is that رَجَاح always involves taking the سَبَبٌ — in other words, actually pursuing the means to obtain what you want. With أُمْنِيَة, by contrast, it is بِلَا سَبَبٍ: no means are taken at all.

Say you want to get healthy. You start watching your diet, you start exercising — that is رَجَاح. But someone who says, "I really want to get healthy," while continuing to eat junk food and sit in front of the television, doing nothing to change his situation — that is أُمْنِيَة. And that, Allah says, is exactly what the Jews and Christians have toward the آخِرَة: they have أُمْنِيَة. They are doing nothing to earn the next life; in fact, they are corrupting in the earth. That is why Muslims must be on guard, because — as my friend Abd al-Hakim Murad once told a story about a Pakistani man who overheard two or three racists in London: one said he was moving to Wales because Muslims were only one or three percent of the population there, another said he preferred Scotland at two percent, and a third was going to Ireland at one percent. The Pakistani man said, "Why don't you go to hell? There are no Muslims there." What Abd al-Hakim observed about that is that the assumption, of course, is that Muslims don't go to hell. Unfortunately, there is going to be a great shock on يَوْمُ الْقِيَامَةِ — because if all you have is أُمْنِيَة, and all your Islam amounts to is the شَهَادَةُ تَيْمٍ, what they call the شَهَادَة of birth and death — شَهَادَةُ تَوْلِيدَتِكَ or شَهَادَةُ تَوَفَّاتِكَ — your testimony recorded at birth on your birth certificate and at death on your death certificate, if that is the sum of it, then you have a serious problem.

I was once in Santa Clara; Farif was with me. A family came — their grandfather had died, and they came to the masjid to bury him. None of them were practicing Muslims, and neither had their grandfather been, but when he died they realized, "We are Muslims — how do we bury him? Let's take him to the mosque." So they brought him, and we washed the body and performed the rites. But it is deeply sad when people's entire experience of Islam is reduced to the moments of marriage and death — they need an imam to perform the نِكَاح, or they need someone who knows how to wash the body and pray over it. That is أُمْنِيَة.

رَجَاء, then — hoping in Allah — means you are actually doing what is required to be pleasing to Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى. The scholars also say that hope should be the dominant state throughout one's life, but when you reach your deathbed, you should let go of fear entirely and rest in absolute hope in Allah. At that moment there should be no fear; you should be completely immersed in a sense of Allah's رَحْمَة, trusting that He will forgive you and accept you, and holدِينٌg the best opinion of Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى.

Now, Imam al-Haythami makes another point that Shaykh al-Khadim mentions in his commentary, and it is important: بُعْدُ الأَمَلِ — this extended hope, this drawn-out expectation — is, at its root, a heedlessness about the reality of death. But he says that in and of itself it is not a wrong action. There is no obligatory command (واجِبٌ) to remember death. When the Prophet ﷺ said to remember death, and also said, "I used to tell you not to visit graves, but now I tell you: go visit them, because it reminds you of the آخِرَة" — the point is that remembering death is مَنْدُوبٌ, recommended. In the same way, ذِكْر beyond what is prescribed in the prayer is مَنْدُوبٌ:

اُذْكُرِ اللَّهَ — اُذْكُرُوا — there is the imperative, the command, but it is for نَدْب, not وُجُوب. You are not obligated to remember Allah as a فَرْضٌ; it is مَنْدُوبٌ, مُسْتَحَبٌّ, strongly encouraged, because it prepares you. So heedlessness of death is not a wrong action in itself.

Similarly, making money, being engaged in the دُنْيَا, being active in worldly affairs — that is مُبَاحٌ. Allah did not prohibit it. You may trade on حَجٌّ; there is nothing wrong with it. You can combine دُنْيَا and آخِرَة on حَجٌّ. You do not have to go in a state of pure spiritual detachment. You can take goods, bring books, sell things — within whatever is permitted. People who shave pilgrims, bringing razors and charging for the service — they are حُجَّاج and they are earning money, and there is nothing wrong with that. Our دِينٌ does not even frown upon it. You should not say أَسْتَغْفِرُ اللهَ and think they are not spiritual people; that attitude is not from our دِينٌ.

So he says: that is permissible — *unless* it was done for تَفَاخُر or تَكَاثُر, or for تَسْوِيفُ التَّوْبَةِ, in which case it becomes a grave sin (كَبِيرَةٌ). If you are acquiring wealth in order to compete with others out of arrogance, or to hoard it in a contest of accumulation, or — تَسْوِيفُ التَّوْبَةِ — to keep putting off repentance, then it is blameworthy. As the آيَةٌ says:

بَلْ يُرِيدُ الْإِنْسَانُ لِيَفْجُرَ أَمَامَهُ، يَسْأَلُ أَيَّانَ يَوْمُ الْقِيَامَةِ

Man wants to keep sinning in whatever remains of his life and asks, "When is the Day of Judgment?" — as though it is far off, as though he need not think about it. The عُلَمَاء say in their commentaries on this آيَةٌ that one of its meanings is يُسَوِّفُ التَّوْبَةَ — he keeps postponing his repentance. This is what is called in this culture "sowing your wild oats" — the idea that when you are young and have the energy for مَعَاصِي, you should get them out of your system, because you won't have the energy when you are older. This is a concept in American and European culture. And this is precisely why Allah loves the young man who is in obedience — because when you grow old, you no longer have the energy for disobedience.

Some people live badly for decades, then around forty-five or fifty, death begins to feel real, they make تَوْبَة, and then they become self-righteous, pointing fingers at everyone else, having completely forgotten who they themselves were. This is a real problem — fifty years old, having done every terrible thing: drinking, cheating, you name it — and then at fifty realizing mortality, going to the masjid, becoming outwardly devout, and immediately beginning to judge others. That is a disease of its own.
