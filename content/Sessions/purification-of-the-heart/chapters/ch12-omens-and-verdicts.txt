Now, the next disease is called *طِيَرَة*, and it is a somewhat nuanced concept because it has a related term in the *حَدِيث* vocabulary, *طِيَرَة*. The Arabs, before Islam, when they wanted to make a decision, would go to a flock of birds and startle them — a form of divination. If the flock flew to the left, they took it as a bad omen; if it flew to the right, they took it as a positive omen. The right-flying birds they called *يُمْنٌ* — from which *Yemen*, being to the right when one faces the rising sun — and the left-flying birds they called *شُؤْم*, which is why the Levant (*الشَّامُ*) carries that sense of the left. In the Arabian Peninsula, facing the sun in the morning, Yemen is on the right (*اَلْيَمِينُ*) and الشَّامُ is to the left. You also get *شَمَلَ* — north, the left side — and *جُنُب*, south, from *جَنْب*, meaning side or flank, because you recline on your right side; so the south is on the right and the north is on the left.

This is also reflected in traditional Muslim cartography. Muslims always oriented their maps with the south at the top and the north at the bottom. I can show you maps from the classical books of *تَوْقِيتٌ* — the science of timekeeping, the *عِلْمُ الْفَلَكِ* of the Muslims — and all of them show the south at the top. This is why I have long believed that Muslims should make their own maps.

I first came to this realization years ago in New Mexico, when I was at a flea market with Hakim Archuleta. I found an old antique compass, and it was cheap. The vendor said, "There's only one problem — instead of pointing north, the arrow points south." I said, "That's fantastic." As we walked away I asked my friend Hakim, who knows a great deal about science, why it would make any difference. He said it's simply a matter of polarization — the paint is facing south because that is the *قُطْب*, the pole. That started me thinking about the question of south and north, and why we have made north dominant.

Then I came across a map by al-Idrisi — the first great cartographer of the world, a Moroccan who drew a world map with the south at the top and the north at the bottom. The European mapmakers drew directly from his work, but they recognized the significance of putting the south at the top: it placed Africa above Europe. So they flipped it, and from that point on their maps had north at the top. Marshall Hodgson makes exactly this observation in *The Venture of Islam* — he says it is unfortunate that Muslims do not recognize that they produced the first world map and oriented it with the south at the top, and that they should return to that orientation, because the north-dominant map reflects a purely Eurocentric worldview that has no historical foundation. A south-oriented map would put Africa at the top and Europe at the bottom; we would say, "I'm going down to Europe and up to Africa."

We would also have to say "up to Los Angeles and down to San Francisco." And in Arabic, this is in fact how it works — the south is up. Southern Egypt is called *صَعِيد*, Upper Egypt, while the north is Lower Egypt. In Libya as well, people say, "We are going up south" and "down to the sea," which is in the north. That is traditional cosmology, and do not think it is insignificant — the effect of spending a lifetime looking at maps that place Africa at the bottom is real. On a south-oriented map, moreover, nothing looms over Arabia. Look at the map the Australians produced when they decided they were tired of being "down under" — they simply turned it right-side up. I have a map from New Zealand, sent to me by a friend after I told her about this. It is a genuinely revealing perspective: you see how close Ireland is to Morocco, how close Germany is to England, and you begin to understand the depth of the Germanic-English connection in a way the north-dominant map never quite conveys. But that is a digression from the diseases of the heart — a worthwhile one, I think, but a digression nonetheless.

So *طِيَرَة* — the birds flying right or left — is at its root superstition, but it is also more than that, because in Arabic the word for a pessimist derives from the same concept: the pessimist is the one who always sees the worst in any situation, who will say about any plan, "It will never work." The root of *طِيَرَة* is the ignorance that all affairs belong to اللَّه alone. For the Muslim, this means having a *حُسْنُ الظَّنِّ* — a good opinion — of اللَّه. The non-Muslim has a bad opinion of اللَّه; the Muslim has a good one. That is the essential difference. So you should always cultivate this good opinion of اللَّه.

He now moves toward *سُوءُ الظَّنِّ* — bad opinion — but before doing so I want to say one more thing about *طِيَرَة*. There are really two types. The first — valid for people of sound intellect and normative experience — is when *الْحُكْمُ الْعَادِي*, empirical probability, establishes that a thing consistently causes harm. If you put your hand near a cobra, it bites you and you die. In that case, you must use your intellect; avoiding it is not *طِيَرَة*, it is rational. If something is harmful by established norm, you must avoid it, and the scholars say that if you do not, you are not considered a person of sound reason — you are acting foolishly. There are, of course, exceptional cases: one of the *أَوْلِيَاء* was walking with a student, they came upon a lion on the path, the student was terrified, the shaykh told him to take the high road and continued walking, and the lion did not touch him. But that is not a station available to everyone.

<!-- page 88 -->

Imam al-Qarafi, a great Maliki scholar, says that *طِيَرَة* is a bad opinion and *طِيَرَة* as an *action* — *عَمَلٌ* — is what results from holding that bad opinion, as long as the normative experience in which harm results does not apply. Walking under a ladder, for instance. In this culture: step on a crack, you'll break your mother's back. Children grow up avoiding the cracks in the pavement. A black cat crossing your path — thirteen years of bad luck. Breaking a mirror. Knocking on wood: "I've been healthy for years" — then you knock wood. These are all *طِيَرَة* and are in fact *حَرَامٌ* to act upon. If the Shari'a gives us specific *أَذْكَار* and practices, those are different — they come from revelation, not superstition. But knocking on wood goes back to pagan tree-worship; those who venerated sacred trees would knock on them. That is the origin. Among the Arabs in the *جَاهِلِيَّة*, things like walking between two sheep were considered bad luck. Seeing an owl was an ill omen. In some cultures, a shooting star was a bad omen. All of these are forms of *طِيَرَة*.

The Prophet ﷺ also established that no one will ever be entirely free of *طِيَرَة* — it is part of human nature. There is a book by the skeptic Michael Shermer called *Why People Believe Weird Things*, and much of it addresses exactly this: how deeply embedded superstition is in human psychology. The *حَدِيث* points to the same reality. People check their horoscope before leaving the house. People will not buy or sell stocks without first consulting their astrologer — a completely absurd premise, yet they believe in it and it gives them a sense of control. People consult the I Ching before making any significant decision.

There's people that do *رَمَلٌ* in the Sahara Desert, and there are those who flip coins or toss them into wishing wells. All of these things are really part of human nature.

So when the Prophet ﷺ mentioned that one of three things will inevitably come to you in life, one of them was ill omens — *طِيَرَة*. And he said the way to cure it is this: if you feel that bad omen rising in you, just keep going. So if, in this culture, you're about to walk under a ladder and something stirs in your heart, you just keep walking — that's how you break it. And if a plant falls on your head — no, I'm just kidding. But that really is the way you break it: you ignore it and keep moving.

A friend of mine who has strong إِيمَان told me about someone who claimed to be a وَلِيٌّ — a claim my friend didn't think was warranted — and he said something about it. The followers of this man turned to my friend and said, "Don't you know? If he makes دُعَاء against you, he can destroy you." And my friend replied, "Go ahead — make دُعَاء against me right now. I want you to." What he was trying to show them was that this is not how Muslims behave. Real Muslims don't make دُعَاء against one another, and a true وَلِيٌّ certainly doesn't. And if he truly is a وَلِيٌّ, اللَّه can bring you difficulty without him even intending it.

There is a Sheikh I know in Mauritania, Muhammad Zayn — whom some of you have met — who is from the family of the Prophet ﷺ. He is a deeply righteous man who would never willingly harm anyone. But it is well known that people who speak ill of him or bear bad attitudes toward him tend to find trouble. So you don't want to be careless with the أَوْلِيَاء. At the same time, as the Moroccans say: don't belittle any Muslim, because he might be a وَلِيٌّ of اللَّه. In other words, be generally mindful of harming any Muslim — it is simply not a good thing to do.

So that is the matter of طِيَرَة. Now the next disease is having a bad opinion of others — what is called ظَنٌّ.

ظَنٌّ is suspicion, conjecture, opinion formed without any supporting facts. The worst form of bad opinion is harboring it toward someone whose outward appearance and conduct are plainly righteous. Outward appearance was something the early Muslims actually considered significant. While they also cautioned against dismissing those who may look unimpressive — because sometimes اللَّه veils His friends behind an ordinary exterior — they recognized what the Arabs call وَسْمَة: the quality of being وَسِيمٌ, someone whose face carries a kind of nobility. When an Arab delegate came to the Prophet ﷺ and then departed, the companions asked what he thought of him. He said, "His face is not the face of a liar." This is what is called فِرَاسَةٌ — the capacity to discern goodness or corruption in a person's countenance. So forming a bad opinion of someone who possesses this وَسْمَة, who outwardly presents as a righteous person, is a particularly serious form of ظَنٌّ.

اللَّه says that some forms of ظَنٌّ are wrong action. This is important because ordinarily we are not held accountable for what merely passes through the heart — but there is a هِبَة of the heart, a form of backbiting that occurs entirely within the chest: entertaining a bad and unfounded opinion of someone, and it is written against you. This is called هِبَةُ الْقَلْبِ. It is unlike the passing thoughts that cross the mind without being invited — you are not accountable for those. What does incur accountability is what the scholars call عَزِيمَة: the thought that settles and takes root. We will go into these levels in more detail when we reach the section on قَوَاطِرُ, because there are degrees — حَدِيثُ النَّفْسِ, عَزِيمَة — and عَزِيمَة is the highest degree, the one written against you. When it runs deep in your heart such that it changes how you treat someone, it becomes هِبَةُ الْقَلْبِ — a form of backbiting in which no second person is even present. It is entirely between you and your own heart. And that is not permissible. It is written against you.

So the condition for blameworthy ظَنٌّ is that your heart has reached a verdict — عَزِيمَة — judging a person based purely on suspicion, without a دَلِيل, without any evidence that actually warrants that judgment. If someone asks you, "Why don't you like that person?" and you say, "I just feel it in my heart — something is wrong," that is a different matter we will come to shortly. But consider the حَدِيث: إِيَّاكُمْ وَالظَّنَّ، فَإِنَّ الظَّنَّ أَكْذَبُ الْحَدِيثِ — "Beware of ظَنٌّ, for ظَنٌّ is the most deceptive of speech." If you tell someone, "So-and-so is a really bad person," and they ask, "What's your proof?" and you say, "I just know" — that is أَكْذَبُ الْحَدِيثِ. That is a lie, because you have no دَلِيل. Our شَرِيعَة is built on the principle: أُمِرْتُ أَنْ أَحْكُمَ بِالظَّوَاهِرِ — "I was commanded to judge by outward matters." So it is essential to look at behavior, at what is manifest.

Then he says: لَا إِثْمَ فِي الشَّكِّ وَعَدَمِ الِاسْتِنَادِ لِسَبَبٍ فَعَدَمٌ يَكُونُ مُجَرَّدًا — there is nothing wrong with having doubts about someone, or even a bad opinion of him, if that opinion is grounded in sound reason and not merely baseless. This is a critical distinction.

The difference between شَكٌّ and ظَنٌّ is this: ظَنٌّ is when your internal scales have tipped — you have made a judgment. Shakk is when both pans are level. Is he good? Unknown. Is he bad? Unknown. That is شَكٌّ: "I simply don't know, and I must be cautious." That is not حَرَامٌ. ظَنٌّ is when you come to a conclusion — without proof — that the person is bad. Everyone understand the difference? This distinction will genuinely serve you in life.

So how do we deal with people about whom we feel genuine doubt? First, if the person is a فَاسِق — and a فَاسِق is someone who commits major sins openly, or who is habitually careless about minor ones, consistently and without remorse — then اللَّه says: if a فَاسِق comes to you with a report, verify it before you act on it. You cannot take what such a person says at face value.

Now, if what you have is mere شَكٌّ — not certainty, just unresolved doubt — what سِيدِي أَحْمَد زَرُّوق advises is excellent counsel, and though it comes from the ninth century it is even more pertinent today: do not entrust your دِين, your family, or your wealth to anyone until you have tested them a thousand thousand times. That is of course مُبَالَغَةٌ — hyperbole — but his point is plain: test people. Observe whether they are genuine and sincere. If you don't yet know, don't trust.

One of the poets said: "A good opinion in these days is foolishness — think the worst of people and stay on guard." And truly, this is what the عُلَمَاء say: when the times are full of corruption, the sound position is to be cautious and distrustful of people — not from ظَنٌّ, not from having made a judgment, but simply from prudence. You don't extend trust until it has been earned, because so many people lack تَقْوَى. They will smile at your face, speak beautifully of their own faith and virtue, and then cheat you. It happens all the time.

That is also why the عُلَمَاء say that in corrupt times, it becomes obligatory for those distributing زَكَاةٌ to investigate the circumstances of claإِيمَانts. They cannot simply hand it over. When people are generally righteous, you take them at their word. But when the times are corrupt and someone comes saying, "Brother, I have five children and no money," you bear a responsibility to verify the claim. The claإِيمَانts cannot be upset or indignant about this — they must accept it. It is not about distrusting them personally. It is, as they say in this culture, that the bad ones have ruined it for the good ones. The response has to be: "Unfortunately, because this is an أَمَانَة from اللَّه, we have to verify. It isn't that we doubt you — this is a blanket policy, for that reason. And we are sorry that the reality has come to this."

So that is the matter of bad opinion of others. Having doubts about someone — شَكٌّ — is fine. Having a bad opinion of someone when there is ample evidence he is truly a bad person is simply common sense. On the other hand: when people make تَوْبَة, do not hold their past over them. One of the worst things you can do to a person is remind them of the evil they have left behind. Let people move forward. Do not cling to their past, because it is deeply unfair.

And there is another dimension here. A good opinion of اللَّه and a good opinion of the servants of اللَّه — no مُؤْمِن was ever given anything better than these two. Generally, it is praiseworthy to have a good opinion: certainly of اللَّه, and generally of His servants. ابْنُ عَمَّارٍ said that no servant was ever harmed by a good opinion, whereas a bad opinion can injure you. And in our شَرِيعَة the principle is البَرَاءَةُ الأَصْلِيَّةُ — people are presumed innocent until there is real cause to conclude otherwise.

The other point of supreme importance is the opinion one holds of اللَّه. There is a حَدِيث reported by al-Tabarani: أَنَا عِنْدَ ظَنِّ عَبْدِي بِي، فَلْيَظُنَّ عَبْدِي بِي مَا شَاءَ — "I am as My servant thinks of Me, so let My servant think of Me whatever he will." Another حَدِيث clarifies it further: فَمَنْ ظَنَّ بِي خَيْرًا وَجَدَ خَيْرًا، وَمَنْ ظَنَّ بِي شَرًّا وَجَدَ شَرًّا — "I am as My servant thinks of Me. Whoever thinks well of Me finds good; whoever thinks ill of Me finds ill." This حَدِيث is the key to understanding another, found in the Forty Hadith of Imam al-Nawawi, in which it is stated that every servant receives what he deserves, and then: فَمَنْ وَجَدَ خَيْرًا فَلْيَحْمَدِ اللهَ — "If he finds good, let him thank اللَّه" — وَمَنْ وَجَدَ غَيْرَ ذَٰلِكَ فَلَا يَلُومَنَّ إِلَّا نَفْسَهُ — "and if he finds otherwise, let him blame no one but his own soul."

The underlying idea is that if you are a true مُؤْمِن, everything ultimately works in your favor. The affair of the مُؤْمِن is all good. Even the bad that befalls you — the true مُؤْمِن believes it carries a reason, and that there is good within it. This is a fundamentally different worldview from that of the مُتَأَيِّس, the pessimist, who falls into what might be called سَخْطَةُ الْقَدَرِ — railing against destiny, saying, "Why is this happening to me? I don't deserve this." The Prophet ﷺ himself said something to this effect: "If You are not angry with me, I do not care" — and he acknowledged that ease would naturally be more comfortable, but that the difficulty of a hard moment is itself a good thing for the مُؤْمِن. That is what it means to have a good opinion of اللَّه and a good opinion of others.
