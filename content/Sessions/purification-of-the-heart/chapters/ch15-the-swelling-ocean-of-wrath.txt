The next section covers غَضَبٌ, which means anger. He opens it by describing anger as the swelling ocean of all these diseases of the heart. Just coming to its shore — not even venturing into it — you will see things that astonish you: waves overflowing, an expanse beyond reckoning. Say of it what you will, without constraint, because it is a truly remarkable thing.

There is a hadith — one with some weakness in its chain, though its meaning is important — in which a man came to the Prophet ﷺ from the front and asked, "O Messenger of Allah, tell me: what is the best thing for drawing near to Allah?" The Prophet ﷺ replied, "حُسْنُ الْخُلُقِ" — good character. The man then came from the right side and asked the same question, and received the same answer. He came from the left and asked again, and again the Prophet ﷺ said, "حُسْنُ الْخُلُقِ." Finally, the man came from behind him and asked once more. This time the Prophet ﷺ said, "أَوَلَمْ تَفْقَهْ؟ لَا تَغْضَبْ" — "Have you not understood? Do not get angry."

In another hadith, a man asked the Prophet ﷺ what the worst thing was that could occur with respect to Allah. The Prophet ﷺ said, "His anger." The man asked how they could avoid it, and the Prophet ﷺ replied: do not get angry yourself. If you do not want Allah to be angry with you, do not give in to anger. "كَيْثَمَا تَدِينُ تُدَانُ" — as you conduct yourself, so will it be shown to you.

Then there is the famous hadith in which a man said, "يَا رَسُولَ اللهِ, give me some advice." The Prophet ﷺ replied, "لَا تَغْضَبْ" — do not get angry. The man said he wanted more advice. The Prophet ﷺ again said, "لَا تَغْضَبْ." The man asked a third time, and three times the Prophet ﷺ repeated, "لَا تَغْضَبْ." This hadith, the scholars say, is the controlling hadith — the key to good character.

Now, when the Prophet ﷺ said "لَا تَغْضَبْ," the scholars of Allah — our عُلَمَاء, including Imam النَّوَوِيُّ — explained that he did not mean: do not feel anger. He did not mean: suppress the emotion entirely. Because the Messenger of Allah ﷺ himself said, "إِنَّمَا أَنَا بَشَرٌ أَغْضَبُ مِثْلَكُمْ" — I am a human being, and I get angry just as you do. His anger was visible in his face; a vein in his forehead would appear when he was angry.

So the Prophet ﷺ was not instructing us to eliminate the feeling of anger, because anger is not inherently negative. It is, in fact, essential to the human character. Many of the world's injustices would never be opposed if people lacked the capacity for anger. What the Prophet ﷺ meant, according to the scholars, was: do not let your anger take control of you. Remain in command of your anger.

Some of the عُلَمَاء have likened anger to a hunting dog: if you do not train it, it will not bring you what you need. It must be disciplined. Without anger, injustice would go unchallenged — people could act with impunity and no one would object. That is not what Allah wants. He wants us to become angry, but not for our own sake. He wants our anger to be for His sake. This is the same transformation that applies to the other diseases of the heart: you redirect them so they work for you rather than against you.

The reason the author calls anger the ocean is that it drowns many people. They sink into the sea of wrath, and it carries them toward destruction and despair.

Now, before moving into the detailed discussion, I want to make a detour — though it is not really a detour, because it is directly related to all of this.

Imam الْغَزَالِيّ identified four essential qualities of the human soul. This framework also appears in earlier traditions and is not unique to Islam, but Muslim scholars recognized its truth and adopted it. فَخْرُ الدِّينِ الرَّازِيُّ adopted it. Imam رَاغِب الأَصْفَهَانِي adopted it in his book on أَخْلَاق. Imam الْغَزَالِيّ adopted it as well.

The model examines the human soul and recognizes four fundamental powers. The first is called قُوَّةُ الْعَقْلِ — what the Western tradition calls the rational soul. It is the power within the human being that is related to what a person knows and understands. The second is قُوَّةُ الْغَضَبِ — the irascible soul — the element that relates to emotions, whose foundation is anger. Imam Jalal al-Din al-Rumi once observed that the wisdom behind the prohibition of wine is that most people carry anger at their core, and when they drink, that root is exposed. People become belligerent; their inherent anger surfaces. He said it was as though Allah, in His mercy, veiled people from exposing what lies at their core. And indeed, for most people, قُوَّةُ الْغَضَبِ sits at the very root of the soul.

The third is قُوَّةُ الشَّهْوَةِ — the appetitive soul, the soul related to desire and appetite, to one's شَهْوَانِيٌّ nature. The fourth is قُوَّةُ الْعَدْلِ, which, according to Imam الْغَزَالِيّ, is the power that harmonizes the other three so that the human being functions in a healthy and بَلَاءٌnced way — none of them thrown out of equilibrium.

These four powers correspond to what both the Western and Islamic traditions call the cardinal virtues. The Muslims call them أُمَّهَاتُ الْفَضَائِلِ: wisdom, courage, temperance, and justice — or in Arabic, حِكْمَةٌ، شَجَاعَةٌ، عِفَّةٌ، وَعَدْلٌ.

When the rational soul is in بَلَاءٌnce, the result is حِكْمَةٌ. Allah ﷻ says: وَمَن يُؤْتَ ٱلْحِكْمَةَ فَقَدْ أُوتِىَ خَيْرًۭا كَثِيرًۭا — "Whoever is given wisdom has been given much good." Imam الْغَزَالِيّ says that حِكْمَةٌ is the mean between بَلَدَة — stupidity — and خِدَاعٌ — cunning. If the rational soul is deficient, the result is foolishness: people do stupid things. If it is excessive, the result is manipulation — the intellect is turned toward trickery and exploitation of others. This excess, incidentally, tends to be an essential qualification for becoming a CEO: that surplus of قُوَّةُ الْعِلْمِ directed toward manipulation.

شَجَاعَة arises when the irascible soul — one's غَضَبٌ — is under proper control. It, too, is a mean between two extremes: تَهَوُّر on one side, which is impetuousness, irrational behavior, total loss of self-control in which a person acts in rage and then regrets it; and جُبْن on the other side, which is cowardice — insufficient غَضَبٌ, too much fear to act. One extreme is deficiency (نَقْص), the other excess (زِيَادَة). The بَلَاءٌnced state between them is شَجَاعَة. This connects to the hadith: "خَيْرُ الْأُمُورِ أَوْسَطُهَا," the best of matters is the middle course, and to the Quranic description: "وَكَذَلِكَ جَعَلْنَكُمْ أُمَّةًۭ وَسَطًۭا" — We made you a middle community, a people of حِكْمَةٌ, a people of شَجَاعَة, possessing these virtues in their proper بَلَاءٌnce.

عِفَّة is the middle way of قُوَّةُ الشَّهْوَةِ. It lies between جُمُودٌ — a kind of rigidity or indifference, where a person has no appetite, no energy, no interest in the opposite sex, no engagement with their شَهْوَة — and فِسْق, where a person plunges entirely into indulging their appetites and becomes enslaved to the appetitive soul. جُمُودٌ is itself unhealthy in the شَرِيعَة; the Prophet ﷺ disapproved of it. The excess, فِسْق, is the opposite danger. The بَلَاءٌnce between them is عِفَّة, and عِفَّة is explicitly affirmed in the شَرِيعَة. Allah ﷻ says that ignorant people, observing poor Muslims who carry themselves with this quality, assume they are wealthy — "يَحْسَبُهُمُ ٱلْجَاهِلُ أَغْنِيَآءَ مِنَ ٱلتَّعَفُّفِ" — because their dignity and self-restraint make them appear self-sufficient. They are not out scrambling after money through trickery and deception, because their inner state is بَلَاءٌnced.

As for عَدْل — justice — it does not occupy a mean between two things. It has an opposite: ظُلْم. Justice is simply the opposite of oppression. When the other three powers are in بَلَاءٌnce, you are just. When they are out of بَلَاءٌnce, you become a ظَالِم — oppressing both yourself and others. If you completely indulge the appetitive soul, you have become ظَالِمٌ لِنَفْسِهِ, oppressing your own soul by subjecting it to what it was not created to do. Allah says: كُلُواْ وَٱشۡرَبُواْ وَلَا تُسۡرِفُوٓاْ — "Eat and drink, but do not be excessive." The middle. Eat what you need. It is now, in fact, a science: based on your body size, you can calculate almost to the gram how many calories you require each day. Eating significantly more or less than that is a form of ظُلْم.

And Allah, in His mercy, has placed a natural indicator within the human being: when you are in a state of فِطْرَة and health, you will feel satiated at the right point. To go beyond that point is ظُلْم against your own soul, because your stomach has a right — a حَقٌّ. The Prophet ﷺ said: "خَيْرُ وِعَاءٍ مَلَأَهُ ابْنُ آدَمَ، بَعْدَهُ" — the worst vessel the son of Adam fills is his stomach. The حَقٌّ of the stomach is that a person leave one-third for food, one-third for water, and one-third for air. That is the عَدَالَة of eating. To exceed that is to go beyond its proper limit.

The same principle applies to the sexual appetite. If it is expressed naturally and within its proper limits, the human being remains healthy. If he transgresses those limits and goes beyond them — and this is part of what might be called the Viagra culture of our time — the result is excess. As a person grows older, that dimension of the self naturally diminishes, and the soul should be turning toward the آخِرَة. But for someone who does not hold the آخِرَة as a concern, he continues pursuing شَهَوَات that he should be moving away from. What was once a physical شَهْوَة has now become a psychological شَهْوَة — the body no longer produces it, but the mind insists on it. That is the imبَلَاءٌnce.

These, then, are the elements of a بَلَاءٌnced human being.

By the grace of Allah, Imam الْغَزَالِيّ says, the only human being who ever perfected the بَلَاءٌnce of all four was the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. He alone achieved complete equilibrium in all three of the foundational powers, which resulted in his being the most just of all men. His character is essentially عَدَالَة. His very nature is عَدْل. This is why the more you emulate him in his behavior, the closer you come to being a truly just human being.

For this reason, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ did not fast every day — that would have been a deficiency in عِفَّة, a withholding from himself of what was due. Nor did he go to the other extreme. He said: "بَعْضَ أَيَّامٍ أَصُومُ وَبَعْضَهَا أَفْطُرُ" — some days I fast and some days I eat. That is the بَلَاءٌnce.

The Prophet ﷺ did not become angry when it was inappropriate. He became angry when it was appropriate, and that was his شَجَاعَة. He became angry when a situation demanded a مَوْقِف, a stand. Imam الْغَزَالِيّ says that the عَدْل of شَجَاعَة is to be angry at the right time, in the right place, toward the right person, and with the right measure. How does one learn to do that? By studying the Messenger of Allah ﷺ — because he never let his anger get the better of him to the point of praying against people. He never allowed it to lead him to strike anyone unjustly. He controlled it. And that is شَجَاعَة.

The Prophet ﷺ also possessed عِلْم — knowledge — beyond any human being, and it was perfectly بَلَاءٌnced knowledge. There is nothing foolish in his conduct, nothing imprudent, nothing that suggests any deficiency of wisdom. Nor did he deploy his intellectual gifts for cleverness, trickery, or accumulation of دُنْيَا. He used them for the sake of Allah, where and as it was appropriate.

Now, one of the things Imam al-Suyuti says in one of his books — and he notes, "I have not seen anyone make this observation before me" — is that, reflecting on what had been said about Abu Bakr being the most courageous of the أُمَّة after the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, he began to think about that quality. This reflection led him to give a talk he called "Abu Bakr and Courage," exploring this virtue. But then something else struck him: each of the الْخُلَفَاءُ الرَّاشِدُونَ embodies one of these four virtues as his dominant characteristic.

حِكْمَةٌ is most clearly سَيِّدِنَا 'Ali, because he is بَابُ الْعِلْمِ — the gate of knowledge. شَجَاعَة is Abu Bakr. And everyone knows عُمَر as the embodiment of عَدْل. The only one that might give one pause is سَيِّدِنَا 'Uthman — but when you look up the word عِفَّة in al-Fayruzabadi's قَامُوس, immediately after its definition he writes: "وَالْعَفَافُ هُوَ الْعَفِيفُ مُغَالَاةً" — and الْعَفَنُ is the one who is intensely عَفِيف. And his name is عُثْمَانُ بْنُ عَفَّانَ. That was what he was known for. Consider what he was doing on the day he was killed: fasting. His entire state was عِفَّة — mastery over the نَفْس. Even the angels were shy in his presence, because of his profound عِفَّة.

So each one of them embodies one of these four virtues as his most prominent quality — while possessing all four. 'Ali had شَجَاعَة; 'Ali had عَدْل; 'Ali had حِكْمَةٌ; 'Ali had عِفَّة. But his dominant characteristic was حِكْمَةٌ — that was his gift. Abu Bakr had عِلْم; he had شُجَّاعَة; he had عِفَّة; he had عَدْل. But what stands out in his mawaqif is شَجَاعَة, because courage is defined precisely as ثَبَتَ — remaining steadfast when hearts around you waver. Look across the events of the Seerah, and it was Abu Bakr who remained ثَبَتَ when everyone else wavered. That is why the Prophet ﷺ said: "I presented Islam to everyone, and the only one who did not waver was Abu Bakr." His heart was شَجَاعَة. It was his nature.

Now there is another remarkable observation about all of this. The صِرَاطُ الْمُسْتَقِيمِ — the straight path — literally means the upright path. اِسْتِقَامَةٌ is اِعْتِدَال. In the prayer, your prayer is only valid when you are مُعْتَدِل — and مُعْتَدِل in Arabic means both upright and perfectly بَلَاءٌnced. So the صِرَاطُ الْمُسْتَقِيمِ is the perfectly بَلَاءٌnced path. And there are two ways to deviate from it.

You either deviate through deficiency or excess — those are the only two ways to stray from the path. You are either falling short of what is obligatory, or you are going too far.

These two deviations correspond to the Jews and the Christians. The Christians went to excess. They went beyond what they were commanded.

<!-- page 122 -->

رَهْبَانِيَّةٌ — monastic renunciation — they invented it; We did not prescribe it for them. They sought it as a means of drawing closer to Allah's pleasure, yet they could not even fulfill what they themselves had initiated. That is the very nature of excess: it cannot be sustained. By its own definition, it is unsustainable. That is why excess is so dangerous. The Prophet ﷺ warned: إِيَّاكُمْ وَالْغُلُوَّ — beware of extremism — for Allah does not grow weary until you grow weary. When you push beyond the بَلَاءٌnce, you exhaust yourself. It is not sustainable.

So the Christian, archetypally speaking, pursues an extreme of عِفَّة — continence — that tips over into something other than its opposite, but something distorted: an excess that has lost its equilibrium. And what always follows an extreme? The pendulum swings to the other side. Drive something far enough in one direction and it snaps back to its polar opposite. The Christian ascetic tradition pushed all the way to one extreme of renunciation, and then flipped entirely — landing in modern American society, where the culture is all شَهْوَة, all appetite and indulgence. They arrived on these shores as Puritans. That was the original impulse of the Pilgrims: to escape a European society they saw as given over to شَهْوَة, to build a pure religious community. And they ended up producing the opposite — a civilization defined by extreme appetite. This is precisely what happens with these polarities.

You can see the same dynamic with شُجَاعٌ — courage. The Christian ideal says: turn the other cheek. Yet, historically, Christians have killed more human beings than any other religious or secular civilization. Now they possess weapons of mass destruction. The technologies of industrial warfare — rockets, tanks, missiles — emerged largely from this same civilization. This is the fruit of going to extremes: the ideal inverts into its opposite.

The same pattern holds with justice. Push justice to an extreme and it collapses into manipulation. The law becomes a vehicle for trickery — lawyers finding every loophole so that those with resources get whatever they want. Justice becomes its opposite: oppression. Knowledge, which was originally claimed as the servant of human flourishing — read the original Enlightenment thinkers; Bacon wrote explicitly about how Christian morality would keep technology in check and make it a force for good — that knowledge becomes a tool for getting what one wants at others' expense. This is the Roman disease: justice for us, oppression for everyone else.

<!-- page 123 -->

That is one form of imبَلَاءٌnce. The other is the way of the Jews — the way of deficiency. This is the very complaint Moses, peace be upon him, raised with the Prophet ﷺ on the night of the إِسْرَاء: his people could not even manage two prayers a day. That was all they were asked, and they could not do it. They said to Moses: *You and your Lord go and fight — we will sit here.* So نَقْص — deficiency — runs through that entire mode.

Now, these are archetypes. I want to be clear: do not take these as absolute, rigid categories. They are lenses for understanding patterns in the human soul. Within the Jewish fold you will find the Christian phenomenon, and within the Christian fold you will find the Jewish phenomenon. These archetypes can appear in a Hindu, in a Buddhist, in anyone. They are ways of reading the human condition.

This is why the one described in ٱلْمَغْضُوبِ عَلَيْهِمْ — those who have incurred wrath — is the one who possesses knowledge but goes astray, becomes ضَالّ. And it is worth noting that the Arabic root بَالٌ also carries the meaning of falling in love — بَلَاءٌ means he fell in love — because it is precisely this excessive, consuming love that leads people astray, the love of Qays, of Majnun.

The Muslim is meant to stand in the middle between these two poles. We can think of those poles as هَيْبَة — awe, majesty, the quality of justice — which is the root of the Mosaic tradition. Moses, peace be upon him, is the lawgiver. His tradition is built on عَدْل, on justice. And on the other side is رَحْمَةٌ — mercy — which is the root impulse of the Christian tradition. The Christian understanding is that everyone is forgiven through God's mercy regardless of what they do, because all they perceive is mercy; they have lost sight of هَيْبَة. Archetypally, you will find Christians who become fixated on wrath — but the dominant Christian emphasis, if you look at what they teach, is رَحْمَةٌ.

هَيْبَةٌ and رَحْمَةٌ must be held in بَلَاءٌnce. You need both to walk the صِرَاطُ الْمُسْتَقِيمِ. You need شَرِيعَةٌ — the law — and you need حَقِيقَةٌ — the spirit of the law. The law is rules; the spirit of the law is رَحْمَةٌ. Allah sent the شَرِيعَةٌ as a mercy. مَا أَرْسَلْنَاكَ إِلَّا رَحْمَةً لِلْعَالَمِينَ — We sent you as nothing other than a mercy to all the worlds. What the Prophet ﷺ brought was رَحْمَةٌ. When inward and outward are بَلَاءٌnced — inwardly إِيمَان, outwardly إِسْلَام — they produce the مُحْسِن, the person of إِحْسَان, someone whose inner and outer states have become one integrated whole.

This framework is a genuine model for understanding what the straight path actually means. Muslims, according to Ibn Taymiyyah, رَحِمَهُ اللَّهُ — and this ultimately goes back to a hadith — deviate in one of two ways: they deviate like Jews or like Christians, toward هَيْبَة or toward رَحْمَةٌ. They either go in the direction of the hair-splitting, rabbinical, tyrannical scholar — the type the Prophet ﷺ warned against: إِيَّاكُمْ وَجَبَرَاتِ الْعُلَمَاءِ, beware of tyrannical scholars — or they go in the direction of the ignorant worshipper, embodied in the Christian model, or in the extreme of تَصَوُّف: a state where everything is one, everyone is going to heaven, nothing needs to be worried about, pure peace and harmony. When you hear certain people who claim to represent تَصَوُّف and they sound more like a New Age seminar than anything connected to Islam, that is this other extreme.

The بَلَاءٌnce is to be صُوفِيٌّ فَقِيهِيٌّ — inwardly animated by the spirit of تَصَوُّف, outwardly disciplined by فِقْه and شَرِيعَةٌ. This is why Imam الشَّافِعِيُّ said: do not be a فَقِيهٌ without being a Sufi, and do not be a Sufi without being a فَقِيهٌ, because one without the other either hardens the heart or leads one astray. The بَلَاءٌnce is the joining of رَحْمَةٌ — the spirit of تَصَوُّف — and عَدْل — the spirit of شَرِيعَةٌ.

Now, our tradition is actually closer to the Christian tradition in one respect: the Christians emphasize رَحْمَةٌ, and when Muslims deal with one another, رَحْمَةٌ should predominate over هَيْبَة. You should be more inclined to forgive your brother than to condemn him. Forgiveness is the higher station. وَأَن تَعْفُوٓا۟ أَقْرَبُ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ — that you pardon is closer to تَقْوَى. That is a loftier station in Allah's دِينٌ than demanding justice. This is why it has been said that where love truly exists, justice becomes unnecessary — because when the love between people is so profound, no one wants retribution; no one wants to call the other to account.

The first فِتْنَةٌ among the Muslims arose precisely from this departure: عَدْل overrode رَحْمَةٌ. سَيِّدِنَا مُعَاوِيَة, رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ, demanded justice. سَيِّدِنَا 'Ali, رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ, possessed حِكْمَةٌ — he counseled forgiveness, recognizing that insisting on strict justice at that moment would tear the أُمَّة apart. The Shia position aside, we, as أَهْلُ السُّنَّةِ, hold a good opinion of مُعَاوِيَة. We believe, as with عَائِشَة and زُبَيْر, that his intention was genuinely to seek justice. But سَيِّدِنَا 'Ali was reaching for the higher expression of Islam — رَحْمَةٌ — and that is why we consider him to have been given تَوْفِيق in his اِجْتِهَاد, while سَيِّدِنَا مُعَاوِيَة was not given تَوْفِيق in his. That is why his group is called الْفِئَةُ الْبَاغِيَةُ — the transgressing party. The danger of عَدْل taken to its extreme is precisely that it becomes بَغْي, oppression. They were unjust toward سَيِّدِنَا 'Ali, رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ. We must be careful when speaking of all of this — we are speaking of the صَحَابَة, رَّضِىَ ٱللَّهُ عَنْهُمْ, and we love them all, سَيِّدِنَا مُعَاوِيَة included. He was a scribe of revelation; he served Islam greatly. But he made a mistake. He was a human being. He was not infallible.

Now — any questions about that?

The question is about the distinction between wisdom and cleverness, between someone who appears wise and someone who genuinely is. For instance, there are people who are clever but whose cleverness does not lead them to recognize the truth. In that case, by the standard of شَرِيعَةٌ, they are considered جَاهِل — ignorant. They only appear wise.

Take the example we mentioned earlier: عُتْبَةُ بْنُ رَبِيعَةَ, who was renowned for his sound judgment, or أَبُو الْحَكَمِ — "the man of considered opinion" — as he was called before Islam. These were men recognized for their discernment. Even أَبُو حُذَيْفَة — when his father was killed at بَدْر and thrown into the pit, the Prophet ﷺ noticed him weeping and asked whether he was troubled by what had been done with his father. He said: *No. I am thinking of how intelligent my father was, how sound his counsel was when I was young, how I always hoped he would embrace Islam — and now I am looking at where he has ended up.* That is the example.

And I think those of us who came to Islam from outside — whose parents are not Muslim — can recognize this. My own parents have given me good counsel, and I can see in them a kind of wisdom. I can see that quality. For someone born into a Muslim family, with Muslim parents, that recognition may not come as naturally. But there are non-Muslims who genuinely possess forms of wisdom and insight. The difference is that the fully realized wisdom — بَلَاءٌnced and complete — sees everything true as true and everything false as false. The only person who achieved that absolutely was the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. But the closer one draws to him, the closer one comes to the kind of wisdom that allows one to see things as they truly are.

Does that make sense? Any other questions?

Right — and others have used these qualities too. The Hellenistic tradition used them. And even modern frameworks reflect them. الْحِكْمَةُ ضَالَّةُ الْمُؤْمِنِ — wisdom is the lost property of the believer; wherever he finds it, he takes it. And you can see these qualities throughout the دِينٌ. They are called cardinal virtues because all other virtues flow from them. Every virtue can be traced back to one of these four.

Let me give an example. Take generosity. What is generosity, really? What is it that a generous person can do that others cannot? Why can't other people simply give? Because they are afraid. They fear losing what they have. So the truly generous person is, at root, a courageous person. It is شَجَاعَة — courage — that enables giving, where others are held back by fear. That is why أَبُو بَكْرٍ was the most generous of all the Companions: when the Prophet ﷺ asked what people had brought, أَبُو بَكْرٍ brought everything he had. عُمَر brought half. أَبُو بَكْرٍ's generosity flowed from his شَجَاعَة, his magnanimity.

Take another virtue. These four are called the أُمَّهَات — the matrices — of virtue. All virtues emerge from these four. Take mercy. What is mercy? Where does it really reside among the four? Mercy ultimately belongs to حِكْمَةٌ, wisdom. Because genuine compassion — like أَبُو بَكْرٍ's counsel to the Prophet ﷺ to show mercy to the people — is grounded in the wisdom that Allah gives through mercy what He does not give through wrath. There is a حِكْمَةٌ in showing mercy, a knowledge that mercy is itself the more effective and more elevated path. Work through any virtue and you will find it rooted in one of these four.

Imam al-Ghazālī goes into extraordinary depth in the إِحْيَاء demonstrating how all the virtues derive from these four matrices. The greatest minds in human history have exhausted themselves on this question — and he truly exhausted it.

<!-- page 127 -->

So how does all of this relate to غَضَب — anger? غَضَب, because it is one of these core قُوَّاتٌ — faculties — is rooted in something essential to human nature. If you look at the brain as it has been studied, what they call the triune brain — and this is not purely a modern observation; Imam al-Ghazālī touches on something similar — there are three dominant centers. The lowest, sometimes called the R-complex or reptilian brain in evolutionary frameworks, is the appetitive center: the seat of drives and urges — hunger, sexuality, the basic impulses. This would correspond to the appetitive faculty. The midbrain is where emotions are centered, and in this model the root emotion is غَضَب — anger. This is why the first emotions children display are forms of غَضَب: they get angry, they cry, they throw tantrums as they grow older. They learn *no* before they learn *yes*. You can see غَضَب rooted right there in the earliest stages of human development.

Then there is the higher brain — the cortex — which is where reason resides.

There is a beautiful hadith in Imam al-Tirmidhī in which the Prophet ﷺ said: love the one you love up to a measure, for one day he may become someone you dislike. The wisdom here is this: if you have shared every secret with someone and that person later turns against you — a very human experience, and one that devastates people — you are left completely exposed.

And also don't hate people to extremes, because one day those you hate might become people you love. So be بَلَاءٌnced in both love and hate. This, again, points to the importance of emotional equilibrium.

When emotions fall out of بَلَاءٌnce, problems follow. One of the Arabs put it memorably: don't be so sweet that people eat you alive, and don't be so bitter that no one can stand you. Be بَلَاءٌnced — that is the idea.

<!-- page 135 -->

Finally, what researchers call the new brain is the neocortex — the seat of rational thought. When all three systems function properly together, the human being is truly Adam, truly human. And this is part of the human condition: some people are excessively cerebral, living entirely in their heads, where everything is theoretical and nothing practical. Others live at the opposite extreme, like reptiles — purely reactive, with no trace of higher humanity. I once explained this in a prison, and some of the Muslim inmates responded to it powerfully. One man came up to me afterward and said, "You know that thing about the reptilian brain? There are a lot of reptiles here. These guys are like snakes, just waiting to strike." It is a striking image — people locked in a purely reactionary mode, with no humanity present. And then, of course, there are those who are overwhelmed by emotion, most commonly by anger.

I brought all of that in to put what follows in perspective. Anger is one of the great excesses of the human condition, which is why the author calls it the ocean — it really is the big one. And there are essentially three reasons people get angry.

The first relates to primal needs: food, shelter, survival, and the like. If a person does not get angry when these are threatened, he is considered أَحْمَق — foolish — because this is a matter of survival. If someone threatens your life, you must respond. If someone threatens your family, you must respond. That is a courage Allah loves. Allah loves شَجَاعَة — بَلْ قَالُوا هُوَ شَجَاعٌ — and the Prophet ﷺ said, مَنْ مَاتَ دُونَ مَالِهِ مَاتَ شَهِيدًا: whoever dies defending his wealth dies a martyr. That courage, that غَيْبَة, reflects the principle of الْأَمْرُ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنِ الْمُنْكَرِ at its most fundamental level, and if you die doing it, you died in jihad — because الْأَمْرُ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنِ الْمُنْكَرِ falls within the chapter of jihad. It is a type of jihad.

The second type is also common to human beings, but it relates to position — to what the scholars call رُؤْيَةُ النَّفْسِ, seeing oneself. This is the anger of dignity: *I am a human being; you cannot treat me this way.* It arises from a sense of being belittled, being treated with disdain, being made to feel lesser than one deserves. Sometimes that sense is justified; sometimes it is not. Some people harbor a complete delusion about their own standing — a grandiosity of soul — and their anger flows from that distortion.

The third type is specific to certain individuals and their particular attachments. A scholar, for instance, will become angry if he sees a book mistreated. An illiterate farmer will not — he doesn't grasp what the fuss is about. But that same farmer will become furious if you throw his pitchfork carelessly, because it is his tool. So this third category of anger is tied to what a person values, what is particular to them.

Imam الْغَزَالِيّ says the first type is healthy, unless taken to an extreme where a person begins to oppress others — taking their food, seizing their shelter. That becomes wrongdoing. The second type can be healthy up to a point, but the higher path is to cease seeing oneself so centrally and not to let these things provoke anger at all. The danger lies in the opposite extreme, which is called ذَكَاة — a disease he will address separately — where excessive self-effacement tips into humiliation. The Prophet ﷺ said, الْمُؤْمِنُ لَا يُذِلُّ نَفْسَهُ: the believer does not humiliate himself. And Allah says of the believers that they possess عِزَّة before the disbelievers — وَلِلَّهِ ٱلْعِزَّةُ وَلِرَسُولِهِۦ وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ — and رُحَمَآءُ بَيْنَهُمْ. You do not present yourself before a non-Muslim in a posture of meek, Uriah Heep–style obsequiousness. You carry yourself with عِزَّة. It is with fellow believers that you practice softness and yielding.

And according to Sidi Ahmad Zarruq, if a non-Muslim says something to you that does not touch your دِين — not mocking your faith or your practice — you simply let it pass. وَإِذَا خَاطَبَهُمُ ٱلْجَهِلُونَ قَالُوا۟ سَلَمًۭا: when the ignorant address them, they say "peace." Don't get angry over things that bring no benefit. All they are doing is pushing buttons. This is why Allah says: فَاصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَاهْجُرْهُمْ هَجْرًا جَمِيلًا — be patient with what they say and leave them gracefully. Turn away. Don't let it consume you. Let it go. There is no benefit in it — none at all.

The fourth type involves غَيْرَهُ. When the Prophet ﷺ spoke about this, Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ declared his own jealous protectiveness, and the Prophet ﷺ responded that he himself has more غَيْرَهُ than Sa'd, and that Allah has more غَيْرَهُ than he does. In other words, غَيْرَهُ — often translated as jealousy, though it is better understood as a protective ardor for what is one's own — is a good sign. It is the impulse that moves a Muslim when he sees his religion being threatened: he becomes غَيُور for the دِين; he wants to defend it. That is a positive anger. And Allah has made غَيْرَهُ part of human nature in order to protect lineage — النَّسَبُ. Without it, men would lose their protective care for their families, and society would unravel through adultery and confusion of bloodlines. غَيْرَة in a man toward his wife is healthy and natural, as long as it does not tip into oppression or groundless suspicion. And the غَيْرَهُ a woman feels is equally natural — Allah has made it so, and indeed it is permitted. Very few women are entirely free from it, so it is not considered a fault.

Now the author says this disease has two cures. The first prevents anger from arising in the first place. The second suppresses it once it has come. Allah praises in the Quran وَٱلْكَظِمِينَ ٱلْغَيْظَ وَٱلْعَافِينَ عَنِ ٱلنَّاسِ — those who restrain their anger and pardon people. The famous story of Ja'far al-Tayyar illustrates this: when a servant accidentally spilled water on him and he became angry, the servant immediately recited that verse, and Ja'far said, "I have suppressed it." The servant continued with the next part — وَٱلْعَافِينَ عَنِ ٱلنَّاسِ — and Ja'far said, "I forgive you." Then the servant recited, وَٱللَّهُ يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ, and Ja'far said, "You are free, for the sake of Allah." That is what a believer should be: someone who, on hearing the Quran, responds to it immediately.

Similarly, if someone is engaged in backbiting and another reminds him with آيَةٌ, he should stop at once and not resent the reminder. I was once told a story about Sheikh Ibn al-Habib, a Moroccan sheikh, and one of his most remarkable qualities: he never spoke ill of anyone. This was reported by people who had lived with him for over seventy years, seeing him every single day — and not once in all that time did they hear him say anything bad about a person. Ever.

Not long ago, someone wrote me a letter — a man I believe to have genuine تَقْوَى — saying he had mentioned me in a letter to someone else and feared there may have been some غِيبَة in it. So he sent me the original letter and asked me to read it and tell him if I found any غِيبَة, so that he could seek forgiveness. He was questioning something he'd written. As it happened, it was simply a disagreement between his understanding and mine, which caused me no difficulty at all. But that level of scrupulousness — even when it is not strictly required — reflects something called اِسْتِهْلَال. Strictly speaking in the شَرِيعَة, you don't have to go back to the person you spoke about and confess it; in fact it is often better not to, because many people will simply become angry at you. Others, though, won't mind — it depends entirely on the person.

The Prophet ﷺ gave us another way. He said, أَيَعْجِزُ أَحَدُكُمْ أَنْ يَكُونَ مِثْلَ أَبِي ضَمْضَمٍ — is any of you unable to be like Abu Damdam? Every morning Abu Damdam would give away his honor as صَدَقَةٌ for people: "يَا اللَّه, if anyone has spoken ill of me, I give back to them whatever good deeds of theirs may have come to me as a result." So the Prophet said: just do that. Who cares, in the end? If you truly contemplate the grave, the questioning in the grave, the Day of Judgment — if you really sit with those realities — none of this will matter. Once you die, you could not care less that someone once remarked you get angry too easily, or said something that stung you one afternoon. It will all be completely irrelevant.

So the author presents two remedies. Then he says: فَاذْكُرْ لِتَزْدَادَنَّ بِهَا عَلَى رَاهِرِ كَثْرَةَ مَدْحِ الْحِلْمِ وَالتَّوَاضُعِ وَالصَّبْرِ — remember, in order to adorn yourself with the quality that removes anger, reflect on how greatly حِلْم and تَوَاضُعٌ are praised. Because a truly humble person does not get angry for himself. He may become angry for Allah's sake, but not for his own sake — because the only reason people get angry is that they are seeing themselves. If the self is not there to be seen, what is there to get angry over?

Consider the Prophet ﷺ: when the Quraysh called him مُذَمَّمٌ — the opposite of his name, the condemned one — he simply said, "It is remarkable how Allah turned the tongues of Quraysh, who intended to condemn me, against some man named مُذَمَّمٌ. But I am مُحَمَّدٌ." He did not get angry. He just observed: they are not talking about me; they are talking about مُذَمَّمٌ. That is the art of letting go. When there is no self to defend, there is no anger. That is تَوَاضُعٌ.

What the author is saying is this: to acquire this quality, meditate on how universally humility and forbearance are praised. Every culture has honored these virtues in its traditions. This one largely no longer does — though it once did. There are still people who value them, but a certain cultural shift introduced what might be called the ethos of chutzpah — an overweening pride, a "if you've got it, flaunt it" sensibility — which is in fact alien to traditional American culture. Historically, Americans did appreciate humility. And humility is praised by all the sages, across every religious tradition.

The Prophet ﷺ said: whoever humbles himself for Allah's sake, Allah raises him up; and whoever raises himself up, Allah brings him low. And the Prophet's camel, القَصْوَاء, used to outrun every camel among the Arabs, and the Companions took great pride in this. But one day she lost a race to a Bedouin, and they were all dismayed. The Prophet ﷺ laughed and said, "Don't you know that nothing is ever elevated but that Allah must bring it back down?" That is the سُنَّة of Allah in His creation. If you understand the سُنَّة, you can laugh at things like that rather than be troubled by them.

So the author says: if you truly reflect on how praiseworthy حِلْم, تَوَاضُعٌ, and forbearance are — and how the Prophet ﷺ was the most forbearing of all people, forgiving those who wronged him, never given to anger, patient through everything — and if you consider that this praise is found in the شَرِيعَة, in شِعَار, in the sayings of all the sages, and that every prophet without exception was characterized by حِلْم and تَوَاضُعٌ — none of them were known for arrogance or quick temper — then you are on your way.

And then he said the second remedy — suppressing anger when it comes — lies in recognizing that nothing happens except by Allah's will. When something arises that provokes you, take a breath and say لَآ إِلَهَ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ. This is a بَلَاءٌ from Allah, a trial, to see أَيُّكُمْ أَحْسَنُ عَمَلًۭا — which of you is finest in deed. The Prophet ﷺ said: لَيْسَ الشَّدِيدُ الَّذِي يَصْرَعُ، وَإِنَّمَا الشَّدِيدُ مَنْ يَغْلِبُ نَفْسَهُ عِنْدَ الْغَضَبِ — the strong man is not the one who can wrestle others to the ground; the truly strong man is the one who overcomes himself when anger comes. That is the شَدِيد. That is real strength.

Imam الْغَزَالِيّ also observes that some people, in their foolishness, equate explosive anger with virility and courage. They see macho posturing as a virtue of character. You will even find a man who brags about his criminal exploits — how he robbed someone, fought them, dominated them — as though this were bravery. It is not. There is nothing virtuous in it whatsoever.

As for the practical means of suppressing anger, the hadith gives a sequence: if you are standing, sit down; if you are sitting, recline; if neither helps, go and make وُضُوءٌ; and if that is still not enough, begin to pray. Performing وُضُوءٌ actually dissipates the heat of anger — for anger is fire, and anger comes from شَيْطَان. It is the quality of شَيْطَان. One day the Prophet ﷺ observed a man whose face had gone red and whose veins were bulging with rage, and he said, "سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ — do you not see how, when one of you becomes angry, his face begins to resemble شَيْطَان?" And he said that had the man uttered the remembrance of Allah, it would have been lifted from him entirely.

And I was once in a conflict-resolution situation, and it was really interesting — things started getting heated and both parties stood up at the same time. They had been sitting on couches, and I had to make them sit back down. But that is the nature of anger: you start rising, and the way to overcome it is to sit down.

If you are in a room and you begin to feel anger, leave the room. If someone is making you angry, just leave. Go to another place, relax, sit down, and let your anger subside — then you can return. This is a practice that, if you actually do it, works. If you do not, you will either end up in a physical confrontation — and if the other person is bigger than you, he beats you, and if he is smaller, everyone sees you as a bully. So the best thing is simply to avoid it. There is no benefit in it, none whatsoever. It is empty and meaningless, and it is simply best to walk away.

That is the سُنَّة: cold water, silence, sitting down, and وُضُوء. All of these came in the سُنَّة. Imam al-Razi mentioned that one man with a fierce temper actually hired someone to insult him and paid him to do so, just so he could practice listening without reacting until he could master his self-control. That may sound extreme, but the point is that people took these matters very seriously, because anger is a terrible quality and no one wants to be possessed by it.

There is a hadith in which the Prophet ﷺ said that anger will even afflict the best of this أُمَّة. No one illustrates this better than سَيِّدِنَا عُمَر. He was a man of fierce anger — yet if you trace his growth as a human being and as a Muslim, by the end of his life عُمَر was not someone whose anger ever got the better of him. In fact, it was quite the opposite: he became known for his forgiveness. You can see this clearly in his later years. Even when he was mortally stabbed, the first thing he asked was whether his assailant was a Muslim. When he was told no, he praised Allah that no Muslim had done this to him — he simply did not want that on a Muslim's account. He said الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ, and that was his nature. For all his ferocity — he was truly a lion of Allah — his anger never consumed him.

On one occasion the Prophet ﷺ was sitting with some women of the Quraysh in Madinah — this was before the verse of hijab was revealed — and when عُمَر entered, they all immediately fled into another room and hid behind a curtain. The Prophet ﷺ was laughing when عُمَر came in. عُمَر asked, مَا أَضْحَكَكَ يَا رَسُولَ اللهِ — "What makes you laugh, O Messenger of Allah?" The Prophet ﷺ said he was laughing at these Qurayshi women, who had fled the moment they saw him. عُمَر went over to the curtain and called them back. He asked them: "How is it that you flee from me, yet you are not shy before the Messenger of Allah, who is far more worthy of your awe?" They replied, "Because you are harsher and sterner than he is — he does not get angry." At this the Prophet ﷺ laughed and said, يَا عُمَرُ، لَوْ سَرَكْتَ فِي جَادٍّ لَسَرَخَ الشَّيْطَانُ فِي جَادٍّ آخَرَ — "O عُمَر, if you went down one path, شَيْطَان would take another path entirely."

That example shows what it means to master the quality of anger. He truly mastered it. Yet at the same time, his anger was rooted in غَيْرَهُ and in عَدْل — in righteous indignation and the demand for justice. He became angry at wrongdoing because he wanted to right it. Even his coming to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and accepting Islam was driven by that impulse — he felt something was wrong and he wanted to correct it. Everything in him was rooted in his convictions. He had an extraordinarily powerful sense of justice, and the Khattab family were traditionally known for that quality, even in pre-Islamic Arab culture.

Now let me address the question that came in. As for children under seven, one must be very careful about expressing anger toward them. For children over seven, however, discipline is necessary — they have to be disciplined. And here it is worth noting something relevant to the three souls and the question about a teacher needing to establish discipline. The classical scholars described three stages corresponding to the first three sets of seven years. The first seven years are governed by the appetitive soul — which is why young children are constantly eating, constantly wanting things; it is all desire and the fulfillment of appetite. The second seven years are governed by غَضَبٌ, and the third by the rational faculty, by عِلْم. This is precisely why the hadith says لَاعِبُوهُمْ سَبْعًا — play with them for seven years — because play is the fulfillment of the appetitive soul. The Prophet ﷺ noted that there is no لَعِبٌ — no play — in Islam except in certain contexts, one of which is with one's spouse. That is a wholesome and praiseworthy kind of play: مُدَاعَبَة, the playful affection between husband and wife, and the Prophet ﷺ himself engaged in this with his wives. So the hadith instructs us: play with your children for seven years — during this period of the appetitive soul, allow them to explore and indulge their nature.

It is part of what children are meant to do. They are discovering the world, and that discovery is entirely sensory — they have come from a spiritual realm into the realm of sensation, and so they must inhabit their bodies and experience them. This is why things that are quite traumatic often happen to children in those first seven years: they get burnt, they fall from high places. All of this is the process of learning to live inside a body and discovering that the body has limits. It is a حِكْمَةٌ of Allah that these things happen to children, because they literally teach the lesson that the body is bounded — unlike the رُوحٌ, which is a more limitless kind of entity. The body is limited, and the Sharia is a protection for the body: touch fire and you learn to stay away from it; stay away from heights; do not endanger yourself. So you allow children to discover the world. Those first seven years really are a time of discovery, and that is why the word هُدًى, whose root means to lust — رَلُمَ, to lust — captures it well. It is a lusty experience: children are very sensuous, they love to be touched, and that is part of how they develop and discover themselves.

Then comes the second stage. He said: أَدِّبُوهُمْ لِسَبْعٍ — discipline them for seven years, because now their حَبَبٌ needs to be disciplined. They are moving into a new stage and require firm guidance. Discipline here means that when you see them becoming angry, you do not allow it. You do not let children rage at their parents — that is a bad sign. You have to teach them to control their anger. And if you yourself cannot control your anger, it will be a disaster: you cannot give what you do not have. If children see you losing your temper constantly, they will simply imitate what they see, and then you will wonder where they got that temper. It came from you, because you are their model. So the أَدَب of those second seven years is precisely this.

Then he said: أُصَاحِبُهُمْ لِسَبْعٍ — befriend them for the next seven years, because this last stage is the stage of عِلْم. They are now moving into a phase where they need your guidance, your counsel — your شَوَرَ — because you will help direct their education, their vocation, and the shape of their lives. That is the traditional model.

Any questions? — It is worth noting that the word for عِلْم: what is its root in Arabic? You know the answer, but what about the word أَخْلَمَ? When they said صَفَّاهُ أَخْلَمَانَا — the intellect — forbearance is actually a sign of intellect. Why? Because you have your قُوَّةُ الْغَضَبِيَّةِ under control; it is directed by the rational soul. Your عَقْل is working, because the عَقْل is an عِكَالٌ — يَقِيلُهُ — it is what puts the irascible and the concupiscent soul in check. عَقْل means to prevent, to restrain, and that is precisely what the rational mind is supposed to do: keep the غَضَبٌ and the شَهْوَة in check. If it fails to do that, you are not عَاقِل, you are not حَلِيمٌ — you are, as they say, a حِمَارٌ. If someone becomes truly enraged, people say he is like a donkey: he is acting like a beast, not like a human being. And usually he is a حِمَارٌ because his anger has taken over.

But the crucial distinction is تَحَلُّم — and this is a very good point that was just raised, and I am genuinely glad it came up because I had forgotten to mention it. There is a hadith that is important here, because some of us will say: "My God, I cannot do any of this — it is all too hard. I am a mess. I get angry. I overindulge." The hadith says: إِنَّمَا الْعِلْمُ بِالتَّعَلُّمِ — knowledge is acquired; you are not born an عَلِيمٌ, you become one. How? Through effort, through مُجَاهَدَة. The عَلِيمٌ is the one who struggles to learn: he rises at night, he studies, he reads, he attends lessons, he listens, he takes notes, he reviews. That is مُجَاهَدَة. You were not born with that knowledge; it is something you acquire. حِلْم is exactly the same. The hadith says: وَإِنَّمَا الْحِلْمُ بِالتَّحَلُّمِ — forbearance too is acquired. And what happens when someone becomes a true عَلِيمٌ? When you encounter such a person, you do not see the student he once was. It seems as though he always had that knowledge — you did not witness all those years of study and struggle. The same is true of the حَلِيمٌ: you do not see when he was wrestling with himself; you assume he was simply born that way. But it is something acquired, something that took real effort. تَأَلُّم comes from تَفَرُّد, which is تَكَلُّدٌ — you have to work for it, and it is hard.

Now, here is a very important point. Imam الْغَزَالِيّ uses the metaphor of a calligrapher. He asks: what do you do when your handwriting is poor? And this is the metaphor for dealing with anger. You go to someone with an excellent hand, you watch him, you ask him to form the letters for you, you go home and you practice those letters again and again. At first they will be poor — but after a month or two, you begin to notice improvement. It is not mastery, it is far from it, but it is noticeably better. And someone who has never started will look at your work and say, "You have beautiful handwriting," while the master will look at the same page and say, "This is terrible" — because حَسَنَاتُ الْأَبْرَارِ سَيِّئَاتُ الْمُقَرَّبِينَ: you are advancing through ranks, and the standard rises with every rank. When you reach mastery, what happens? I once watched مُحَمَّدٌ Zakariyya — I took a calligraphy lesson from him, a single lesson — and I watched him work, and I thought: "I can do that. That looks easy." Why did I think that? Because he is a master. He is an American master of calligraphy who wins awards in Turkey. But I did not see the years of nights he had put into it. Now he just flows across the paper as if he were born writing perfect calligraphy. Then I picked up the pen and started, and it was like watching something jump up and down — it was very hard. He simply flowed. That is mastery.

So what do you do when you want good character? Three things. First, you learn what it is. You have to learn what bad character is and what good character is — and that is precisely what we are doing here in these sessions. The first stage is learning. Second: وَٱلَّذِينَ جَهَدُوا۟ فِينَا لَنَهْدِيَنَّهُمْ سُبُلَنَا — those who struggle for Our sake, We will guide them to Our paths. You have to find people of good character and spend time with them. There is also a benefit in being around people who get angry easily, because it allows you to see clearly how unpleasant it is. This is why Imam al-Shafi'i, when someone asked him how he had developed such excellent character, said: "I listened to what my detractors said about me, and then I looked into my own soul to see whether it was true. When it was, I struggled with myself until I removed it." Sayyidna 'Isa was once asked the same question — how did you develop such excellent character? — and what the tradition records is this: "I looked at the bad character of people and I avoided it." So part of it is simply مُجَاهَدَة. The third stage is to be with someone who has good character — and although that is genuinely difficult to find, we should never despair. At least we should be trying.

I will tell you this: it really works. I am serious. I am nowhere near where I would like to be, but it helped me enormously to learn these things and to struggle with myself as much as I could. You do change. You do improve. Anger — خَطَّاب — was a serious difficulty for me for a very long time, and it is much less so now. Part of that, I will admit, is simply aging. Imam Sayyidna 'Ali said that youth is a kind of madness, and the cure is to grow old. So there is that.

On anger toward parents — the principle is that you should be angry at the act, not at the actor. What disturbs you should be the action itself, but if it is your father or your mother, you must be especially careful. If you are in a position to act, then الْأَمْرُ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنِ الْمُنْكَرِ applies, provided it will not lead to a worse فِتْنَة. But with parents you must be very careful. A man came to the Prophet ﷺ and said: "My father takes my wealth and it really angers me." The Prophet ﷺ said: "You and your wealth belong to your father" — meaning that he was the instrument by which you were given existence, and that carries an immense weight. Parents hold a very high مَقَام in Islam. The best approach is to recognize that they are human beings with their limitations. I had a teacher once — not a Muslim, but a very wise person — who told me that he thanked his father because his father had taught him how not to be a father. His father had been so harsh with him that he did the precise opposite with his own children. In the end he genuinely felt that it was a gift. He was not being sarcastic — he meant it. Obviously it would be better if your father showed you how to be a father rather than how not to be one, but the idea is taking the worst situation and making the best of it. وَمَن يُؤْتَ ٱلْحِكْمَةَ فَقَدْ أُوتِىَ خَيْرًۭا كَثِيرًۭا — that is حِكْمَة. And that is the beauty of Islam: in any situation, it can be turned to benefit.

So, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, we are actually coming — believe it or not — to the end. There are only four sections remaining after today, and I think we will finish all the diseases of the heart next week and then move on. He has a few excellent sections ahead: one is called the cure for all the diseases of the heart together — a single, overarching remedy. The Gordian knot, you might say. Everyone knows about the Gordian knot: a massive knot in Turkey that no one could untie, and the legend was that whoever untied it would rule the land. When Alexander the Great arrived, they showed him the knot — and he drew his sword and cut straight through it. The diseases of the heart are like that, and sometimes the sword is the best answer. Then we will do the section on هَوَاجِسُ — thoughts — because one of the most obvious manifestations of the diseases of the heart is diseased thinking: impure thoughts that afflict the mind, but also thoughts that a person might believe are genuinely good yet are in fact from شَيْطَان. شَيْطَان is described in the hadith as being like a wolf among a flock: if he finds the shepherd watching from one side, he comes from another. One of the key differences between شَيْطَان's وَسْوَاس and the هَوًى of the نَفْس is that شَيْطَان's whisperings are compulsive, while the نَفْس has its own habitual pull. We know from Ramadan that the شَيَاطِين are locked up — صُفِّدَتِ الشَّيَاطِينُ — and yet people still struggle. That is where you begin to distinguish between شَيْطَان, the نَفْس, هَوًى, and دُنْيَا, because those are the four enemies of the human being: شَيْطَان, نَفْس, هَوًى, and دُنْيَا.
