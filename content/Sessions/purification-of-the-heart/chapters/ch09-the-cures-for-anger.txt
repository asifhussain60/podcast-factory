The first relates to primal needs: food, shelter, survival, and the like. If a person does not get angry when these are threatened, he is considered أَحْمَق — foolish — because this is a matter of survival. If someone threatens your life, you must respond. If someone threatens your family, you must respond. That is a courage Allah loves. Allah loves شَجَاعَة — بَلْ قَالُوا هُوَ شَجَاعٌ — and the Prophet ﷺ said, *مَنْ مَاتَ دُونَ مَالِهِ مَاتَ شَهِيدًا*: whoever dies defending his wealth dies a martyr. That courage, that غَيْبَة, reflects the principle of الأَمْرُ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنِ الْمُنْكَرِ at its most fundamental level, and if you die doing it, you died in جِهَادٌ — because الأَمْرُ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنِ الْمُنْكَرِ falls within the chapter of جِهَادٌ. It is a type of جِهَادٌ.

<!-- page 136 -->

The second type is also common to human beings, but it relates to position — to what the scholars call رُؤْيَةُ النَّفْسِ, seeing oneself. This is the anger of dignity: *I am a human being; you cannot treat me this way.* It arises from a sense of being belittled, being treated with disdain, being made to feel lesser than one deserves. Sometimes that sense is justified; sometimes it is not. Some people harbor a complete delusion about their own standing — a grandiosity of soul — and their anger flows from that distortion.

The third type is specific to certain individuals and their particular attachments. A scholar, for instance, will become angry if he sees a book mistreated. An illiterate farmer will not — he doesn't grasp what the fuss is about. But that same farmer will become furious if you throw his pitchfork carelessly, because it is his tool. So this third category of anger is tied to what a person values, what is particular to them.

Imam al-Ghazali says the first type is healthy, unless taken to an extreme where a person begins to oppress others — taking their food, seizing their shelter. That becomes wrongdoing. The second type can be healthy up to a point, but the higher path is to cease seeing oneself so centrally and not to let these things provoke anger at all. The danger lies in the opposite extreme, which is called ذَاءَهْ — a disease he will address separately — where excessive self-effacement tips into humiliation. The Prophet ﷺ said, *الْمُؤْمِنُ لَا يُذِلُّ نَفْسَهُ*: the believer does not humiliate himself. And Allah says of the believers that they possess عِزَّة before the disbelievers — وَلِلَّهِ ٱلْعِزَّةُ وَلِرَسُولِهِۦ وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ — and رُحَمَآءُ بَيْنَهُمْ. You do not present yourself before a non-Muslim in a posture of meek, Uriah Heep–style obsequiousness. You carry yourself with عِزَّة. It is with fellow believers that you practice softness and yielding.

<!-- page 137 -->

And according to Sidi Ahmad Zarruq, if a non-Muslim says something to you that does not touch your دِين — not mocking your faith or your practice — you simply let it pass. وَإِذَا خَاطَبَهُمُ ٱلْجَهِلُونَ قَالُوا۟ سَلَمًۭا: when the ignorant address them, they say "peace." Don't get angry over things that bring no benefit. All they are doing is pushing buttons. This is why Allah says: فَاصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَاهْجُرْهُمْ هَجْرًا جَمِيلًا — be patient with what they say and leave them gracefully. Turn away. Don't let it consume you. Let it go. There is no benefit in it — none at all.

The fourth type involves غَيْرَة. When the Prophet ﷺ spoke about this, Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ declared his own jealous protectiveness, and the Prophet ﷺ responded that he himself has more غَيْرَة than Sa'd, and that Allah has more غَيْرَة than he does. In other words, غَيْرَة — often translated as jealousy, though it is better understood as a protective ardor for what is one's own — is a good sign. It is the impulse that moves a Muslim when he sees his religion being threatened: he becomes غَيُور for the دِين; he wants to defend it. That is a positive anger. And Allah has made غَيْرَة part of human nature in order to protect lineage — النَّسَبُ. Without it, men would lose their protective care for their families, and society would unravel through adultery and confusion of bloodlines. غَيْرَة in a man toward his wife is healthy and natural, as long as it does not tip into oppression or groundless suspicion. And the غَيْرَة a woman feels is equally natural — Allah has made it so, and indeed it is permitted. Very few women are entirely free from it, so it is not considered a fault.

<!-- page 138 -->

Now the author says this disease has two cures. The first prevents anger from arising in the first place. The second suppresses it once it has come. Allah praises in the Quran وَٱلْكَظِمِينَ ٱلْغَيْظَ وَٱلْعَافِينَ عَنِ ٱلنَّاسِ — those who restrain their anger and pardon people. The famous story of Ja'far al-Tayyar illustrates this: when a servant accidentally spilled water on him and he became angry, the servant immediately recited that verse, and Ja'far said, "I have suppressed it." The servant continued with the next part — *وَٱلْعَافِينَ عَنِ ٱلنَّاسِ* — and Ja'far said, "I forgive you." Then the servant recited, وَٱللَّهُ يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ, and Ja'far said, "You are free, for the sake of Allah." That is what a believer should be: someone who, on hearing the Quran, responds to it immediately.

Similarly, if someone is engaged in backbiting and another reminds him with آيَةٌ, he should stop at once and not resent the reminder. I was once told a story about Sheikh Ibn al-Habib, a Moroccan sheikh, and one of his most remarkable qualities: he never spoke ill of anyone. This was reported by people who had lived with him for over seventy years, seeing him every single day — and not once in all that time did they hear him say anything bad about a person. Ever.

<!-- page 139 -->

Not long ago, someone wrote me a letter — a man I believe to have genuine تَقْوَى — saying he had mentioned me in a letter to someone else and feared there may have been some غِيبَة in it. So he sent me the original letter and asked me to read it and tell him if I found any غِيبَة, so that he could seek forgiveness. He was questioning something he'd written. As it happened, it was simply a disagreement between his understanding and mine, which caused me no difficulty at all. But that level of scrupulousness — even when it is not strictly required — reflects something called اِسْتِحْلَال. Strictly speaking in the شَرِيعَة, you don't have to go back to the person you spoke about and confess it; in fact it is often better not to, because many people will simply become angry at you. Others, though, won't mind — it depends entirely on the person.

The Prophet ﷺ gave us another way. He said, *أَيَعْجِزُ أَحَدُكُمْ أَنْ يَكُونَ مِثْلَ أَبِي ضَمْضَمٍ* — is any of you unable to be like Abu Damdam? Every morning Abu Damdam would give away his honor as صَدَقَةٌ for people: "Ya Allah, if anyone has spoken ill of me, I give back to them whatever good deeds of theirs may have come to me as a result." So the Prophet said: just do that. Who cares, in the end? If you truly contemplate the grave, the questioning in the grave, the Day of Judgment — if you really sit with those realities — none of this will matter. Once you die, you could not care less that someone once remarked you get angry too easily, or said something that stung you one afternoon. It will all be completely irrelevant.

<!-- page 140 -->

So the author presents two remedies. Then he says: *فَاذْكُرْ لِتَزَادَنَا بِهَا عَلَى رَاهِرِي كَثْرَةَ مَدْحِ الْحِلْمِ وَالتَّوَاضُعِ وَالصَّبْرِ* — remember, in order to adorn yourself with the quality that removes anger, reflect on how greatly حِلْم and تَوَاضُعٌ are praised. Because a truly humble person does not get angry for himself. He may become angry for Allah's sake, but not for his own sake — because the only reason people get angry is that they are seeing themselves. If the self is not there to be seen, what is there to get angry over?

Consider the Prophet ﷺ: when the قُرَيْشٌ called him *مُذَمَّمٌ* — the opposite of his name, the condemned one — he simply said, "It is remarkable how Allah turned the tongues of قُرَيْشٌ, who intended to condemn me, against some man named مُذَمَّمٌ. But I am Muhammad." He did not get angry. He just observed: they are not talking about me; they are talking about مُذَمَّمٌ. That is the art of letting go. When there is no self to defend, there is no anger. That is تَوَاضُعٌ.

What the author is saying is this: to acquire this quality, meditate on how universally humility and forbearance are praised. Every culture has honored these virtues in its traditions. This one largely no longer does — though it once did. There are still people who value them, but a certain cultural shift introduced what might be called the ethos of chutzpah — an overweening pride, a "if you've got it, flaunt it" sensibility — which is in fact alien to traditional American culture. Historically, Americans did appreciate humility. And humility is praised by all the sages, across every religious tradition.

The Prophet ﷺ said: whoever humbles himself for Allah's sake, Allah raises him up; and whoever raises himself up, Allah brings him low. And the Prophet's camel, القَصْوَاء, used to outrun every camel among the Arabs, and the Companions took great pride in this. But one day she lost a race to a Bedouin, and they were all dismayed. The Prophet ﷺ laughed and said, "Don't you know that nothing is ever elevated but that Allah must bring it back down?" That is the سُنَّة of Allah in His creation. If you understand the سُنَّة, you can laugh at things like that rather than be troubled by them.

So the author says: if you truly reflect on how praiseworthy حِلْم, تَوَاضُعٌ, and forbearance are — and how the Prophet ﷺ was the most forbearing of all people, forgiving those who wronged him, never given to anger, patient through everything — and if you consider that this praise is found in the شَرِيعَة, in شِعَار, in the sayings of all the sages, and that every prophet without exception was characterized by حِلْم and تَوَاضُعٌ — none of them were known for arrogance or quick temper — then you are on your way.

And then he said the second remedy — suppressing anger when it comes — lies in recognizing that nothing happens except by Allah's will. When something arises that provokes you, take a breath and say *لَآ إِلَهَ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ*. This is a بَلَا from Allah, a trial, to see *أَيُّكُمْ أَحْسَنُ عَمَلًۭا* — which of you is finest in deed. The Prophet ﷺ said: *لَيْسَ الشَّدِيدُ الَّذِي يَصْرَعُ، وَإِنَّمَا الشَّدِيدُ مَنْ يَغْلِبُ نَفْسَهُ عِنْدَ الْغَضَبِ* — the strong man is not the one who can wrestle others to the ground; the truly strong man is the one who overcomes himself when anger comes. That is the شَدِيد. That is real strength.

Imam al-Ghazali also observes that some people, in their foolishness, equate explosive anger with virility and courage. They see macho posturing as a virtue of character. You will even find a man who brags about his criminal exploits — how he robbed someone, fought them, dominated them — as though this were bravery. It is not. There is nothing virtuous in it whatsoever.

As for the practical means of suppressing anger, the حَدِيث gives a sequence: if you are standing, sit down; if you are sitting, recline; if neither helps, go and make وُضُوءٌ; and if that is still not enough, begin to pray. Performing وُضُوءٌ actually dissipates the heat of anger — for anger is fire, and anger comes from شَيْطَان. It is the quality of شَيْطَان. One day the Prophet ﷺ observed a man whose face had gone red and whose veins were bulging with rage, and he said, "سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ — do you not see how, when one of you becomes angry, his face begins to resemble شَيْطَان?" And he said that had the man uttered the remembrance of Allah, it would have been lifted from him entirely.

And I was once in a conflict-resolution situation, and it was really interesting — things started getting heated and both parties stood up at the same time. They had been sitting on couches, and I had to make them sit back down. But that is the nature of anger: you start rising, and the way to overcome it is to sit down.

If you are in a room and you begin to feel anger, leave the room. If someone is making you angry, just leave. Go to another place, relax, sit down, and let your anger subside — then you can return. This is a practice that, if you actually do it, works. If you do not, you will either end up in a physical confrontation — and if the other person is bigger than you, he beats you, and if he is smaller, everyone sees you as a bully. So the best thing is simply to avoid it. There is no benefit in it, none whatsoever. It is empty and meaningless, and it is simply best to walk away.

That is the سُنَّة: cold water, silence, sitting down, and وُضُوء. All of these came in the سُنَّة. Imam al-Razi mentioned that one man with a fierce temper actually hired someone to insult him and paid him to do so, just so he could practice listening without reacting until he could master his self-control. That may sound extreme, but the point is that people took these matters very seriously, because anger is a terrible quality and no one wants to be possessed by it.

<!-- page 106 -->

There is a حَدِيث in which the Prophet ﷺ said that anger will even afflict the best of this أُمَّةٌ. No one illustrates this better than سَيِّدِنَا عُمَرَ. He was a man of fierce anger — yet if you trace his growth as a human being and as a Muslim, by the end of his life 'Umar was not someone whose anger ever got the better of him. In fact, it was quite the opposite: he became known for his forgiveness. You can see this clearly in his later years. Even when he was mortally stabbed, the first thing he asked was whether his assailant was a Muslim. When he was told no, he praised Allah that no Muslim had done this to him — he simply did not want that on a Muslim's account. He said *الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ*, and that was his nature. For all his ferocity — he was truly a lion of Allah — his anger never consumed him.

On one occasion the Prophet ﷺ was sitting with some women of the قُرَيْشٌ in Madinah — this was before the verse of حِجَابٌ was revealed — and when 'Umar entered, they all immediately fled into another room and hid behind a curtain. The Prophet ﷺ was laughing when 'Umar came in. 'Umar asked, *مَا أَضْحَكَكَ يَا رَسُولَ اللَّهِ* — "What makes you laugh, O Messenger of Allah?" The Prophet ﷺ said he was laughing at these قُرَيْشٌi women, who had fled the moment they saw him. 'Umar went over to the curtain and called them back. He asked them: "How is it that you flee from me, yet you are not shy before the Messenger of Allah, who is far more worthy of your awe?" They replied, "Because you are harsher and sterner than he is — he does not get angry." At this the Prophet ﷺ laughed and said, *يَا عُمَرُ، لَوْ سَرَكْتَ فِي جَادٍّ لَسَرَخَ الشَّيْطَانُ فِي جَادٍّ آخَرَ* — "O 'Umar, if you went down one path, شَيْطَان would take another path entirely."

That example shows what it means to master the quality of anger. He truly mastered it. Yet at the same time, his anger was rooted in *غَيْرَة* and in *عَدْل* — in righteous indignation and the demand for justice. He became angry at wrongdoing because he wanted to right it. Even his coming to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and accepting Islam was driven by that impulse — he felt something was wrong and he wanted to correct it. Everything in him was rooted in his convictions. He had an extraordinarily powerful sense of justice, and the Khattab family were traditionally known for that quality, even in pre-Islamic Arab culture.

<!-- page 107 -->

Now let me address the question that came in. As for children under seven, one must be very careful about expressing anger toward them. For children over seven, however, discipline is necessary — they have to be disciplined. And here it is worth noting something relevant to the three souls and the question about a teacher needing to establish discipline. The classical scholars described three stages corresponding to the first three sets of seven years. The first seven years are governed by the appetitive soul — which is why young children are constantly eating, constantly wanting things; it is all desire and the fulfillment of appetite. The second seven years are governed by *غَضَب*, and the third by the rational faculty, by *عِلْمٌ*. This is precisely why the حَدِيث says *لَاعِبُوهُمْ سَبْعًا* — play with them for seven years — because play is the fulfillment of the appetitive soul. The Prophet ﷺ noted that there is no *لَعِبٌ* — no play — in Islam except in certain contexts, one of which is with one's spouse. That is a wholesome and praiseworthy kind of play: *مُدَاعَبَة*, the playful affection between husband and wife, and the Prophet ﷺ himself engaged in this with his wives. So the حَدِيث instructs us: play with your children for seven years — during this period of the appetitive soul, allow them to explore and indulge their nature.

It is part of what children are meant to do. They are discovering the world, and that discovery is entirely sensory — they have come from a spiritual realm into the realm of sensation, and so they must inhabit their bodies and experience them. This is why things that are quite traumatic often happen to children in those first seven years: they get burnt, they fall from high places. All of this is the process of learning to live inside a body and discovering that the body has limits. It is a حِكْمَةٌ of Allah that these things happen to children, because they literally teach the lesson that the body is bounded — unlike the رُوحٌ, which is a more limitless kind of entity. The body is limited, and the شَرِيعَة is a protection for the body: touch fire and you learn to stay away from it; stay away from heights; do not endanger yourself. So you allow children to discover the world. Those first seven years really are a time of discovery, and that is why the word هُدًى, whose root means to lust — رَلُمَا, to lust — captures it well. It is a lusty experience: children are very sensuous, they love to be touched, and that is part of how they develop and discover themselves.

Then comes the second stage. He said: *أَدِّبُوهُمْ لِسَبْعٍ* — discipline them for seven years, because now their حَبَب needs to be disciplined. They are moving into a new stage and require firm guidance. Discipline here means that when you see them becoming angry, you do not allow it. You do not let children rage at their parents — that is a bad sign. You have to teach them to control their anger. And if you yourself cannot control your anger, it will be a disaster: you cannot give what you do not have. If children see you losing your temper constantly, they will simply imitate what they see, and then you will wonder where they got that temper. It came from you, because you are their model. So the أَدَب of those second seven years is precisely this.

Then he said: *أُصَاحِبُهُمْ لِسَبْعٍ* — befriend them for the next seven years, because this last stage is the stage of عِلْمٌ. They are now moving into a phase where they need your guidance, your coأُنْسel — your شَوَرَ — because you will help direct their education, their vocation, and the shape of their lives. That is the traditional model.

<!-- page 139 -->

Any questions? — It is worth noting that the word for عِلْمٌ: what is its root in Arabic? You know the answer, but what about the word أَخْلَمَ? When they said *صَفَّاهُ أَخْلَمَانَا* — the intellect — forbearance is actually a sign of intellect. Why? Because you have your قُوَّةُ الْغَضَبِيَّةِ under control; it is directed by the rational soul. Your عَقْل is working, because the عَقْل is an عِكَالٌ — *يَقِيلُهُ* — it is what puts the irascible and the concupiscent soul in check. عَقْل means to prevent, to restrain, and that is precisely what the rational mind is supposed to do: keep the غَضَب and the شَهْوَة in check. If it fails to do that, you are not عَاقِل, you are not حَلِيم — you are, as they say, a حِمَارٌ. If someone becomes truly enraged, people say he is like a donkey: he is acting like a beast, not like a human being. And usually he is a حِمَارٌ because his anger has taken over.

But the crucial distinction is تَحَلُّم — and this is a very good point that was just raised, and I am genuinely glad it came up because I had forgotten to mention it. There is a حَدِيث that is important here, because some of us will say: "My God, I cannot do any of this — it is all too hard. I am a mess. I get angry. I overindulge." The حَدِيث says: *إِنَّمَا الْعِلْمُ بِالتَّعَلُّمِ* — knowledge is acquired; you are not born an عَالِمٌ, you become one. How? Through effort, through مُجَاهَدَة. The عَالِمٌ is the one who struggles to learn: he rises at night, he studies, he reads, he attends lessons, he listens, he takes notes, he reviews. That is مُجَاهَدَة. You were not born with that knowledge; it is something you acquire. حِلْم is exactly the same. The حَدِيث says: *وَإِنَّمَا الْحِلْمُ بِالتَّحَلُّمِ* — forbearance too is acquired. And what happens when someone becomes a true عَالِمٌ? When you encounter such a person, you do not see the student he once was. It seems as though he always had that knowledge — you did not witness all those years of study and struggle. The same is true of the حَلِيم: you do not see when he was wrestling with himself; you assume he was simply born that way. But it is something acquired, something that took real effort. تَأَلُّم comes from تَفَرُّدٌ, which is تَكَلُّدٌ — you have to work for it, and it is hard.

Now, here is a very important point. Imam al-Ghazali uses the metaphor of a calligrapher. He asks: what do you do when your handwriting is poor? And this is the metaphor for dealing with anger. You go to someone with an excellent hand, you watch him, you ask him to form the letters for you, you go home and you practice those letters again and again. At first they will be poor — but after a month or two, you begin to notice improvement. It is not mastery, it is far from it, but it is noticeably better. And someone who has never started will look at your work and say, "You have beautiful handwriting," while the master will look at the same page and say, "This is terrible" — because *حَسَنَاتُ الْأَبْرَارِ سَيِّئَاتُ الْمُقَرَّبِينَ*: you are advancing through ranks, and the standard rises with every rank. When you reach mastery, what happens? I once watched Muhammad Zakariyya — I took a calligraphy lesson from him, a single lesson — and I watched him work, and I thought: "I can do that. That looks easy." Why did I think that? Because he is a master. He is an American master of calligraphy who wins awards in Turkey. But I did not see the years of nights he had put into it. Now he just flows across the paper as if he were born writing perfect calligraphy. Then I picked up the pen and started, and it was like watching something jump up and down — it was very hard. He simply flowed. That is mastery.

So what do you do when you want good character? Three things. First, you learn what it is. You have to learn what bad character is and what good character is — and that is precisely what we are doing here in these sessions. The first stage is learning. Second: *وَٱلَّذِينَ جَهَدُوا۟ فِينَا لَنَهْدِيَنَّهُمْ سُبُلَنَا* — those who struggle for Our sake, We will guide them to Our paths. You have to find people of good character and spend time with them. There is also a benefit in being around people who get angry easily, because it allows you to see clearly how unpleasant it is. This is why Imam al-Shafi'i, when someone asked him how he had developed such excellent character, said: "I listened to what my detractors said about me, and then I looked into my own soul to see whether it was true. When it was, I struggled with myself until I removed it." Sayyidna 'Isa was once asked the same question — how did you develop such excellent character? — and what the tradition records is this: "I looked at the bad character of people and I avoided it." So part of it is simply مُجَاهَدَة. The third stage is to be with someone who has good character — and although that is genuinely difficult to find, we should never despair. At least we should be trying.

I will tell you this: it really works. I am serious. I am nowhere near where I would like to be, but it helped me enormously to learn these things and to struggle with myself as much as I could. You do change. You do improve. Anger — خَطَّاب — was a serious difficulty for me for a very long time, and it is much less so now. Part of that, I will admit, is simply aging. Imam Sayyidna 'Ali said that youth is a kind of madness, and the cure is to grow old. So there is that.

On anger toward parents — the principle is that you should be angry at the act, not at the actor. What disturbs you should be the action itself, but if it is your father or your mother, you must be especially careful. If you are in a position to act, then *الْأَمْرُ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَالنَّهْيُ عَنِ الْمُنْكَرِ* applies, provided it will not lead to a worse فِتْنَة. But with parents you must be very careful. A man came to the Prophet ﷺ and said: "My father takes my wealth and it really angers me." The Prophet ﷺ said: "You and your wealth belong to your father" — meaning that he was the instrument by which you were given existence, and that carries an immense weight. Parents hold a very high مَقَامٌ in Islam. The best approach is to recognize that they are human beings with their limitations. I had a teacher once — not a Muslim, but a very wise person — who told me that he thanked his father because his father had taught him how not to be a father. His father had been so harsh with him that he did the precise opposite with his own children. In the end he genuinely felt that it was a gift. He was not being sarcastic — he meant it. Obviously it would be better if your father showed you how to be a father rather than how not to be one, but the idea is taking the worst situation and making the best of it. *وَمَن يُؤْتَ ٱلْحِكْمَةَ فَقَدْ أُوتِىَ خَيْرًۭا كَثِيرًۭا* — that is حِكْمَة. And that is the beauty of Islam: in any situation, it can be turned to benefit.

So, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, we are actually coming — believe it or not — to the end. There are only four sections remaining after today, and I think we will finish all the diseases of the heart next week and then move on. He has a few excellent sections ahead: one is called the cure for all the diseases of the heart together — a single, overarching remedy. The Gordian knot, you might say. Everyone knows about the Gordian knot: a massive knot in Turkey that no one could untie, and the legend was that whoever untied it would rule the land. When Alexander the Great arrived, they showed him the knot — and he drew his sword and cut straight through it. The diseases of the heart are like that, and sometimes the sword is the best answer.

Then we will do the section on هَوَاجِس — thoughts — because one of the most obvious manifestations of the diseases of the heart is diseased thinking: impure thoughts that afflict the mind, but also thoughts that a person might believe are genuinely good yet are in fact from شَيْطَان. شَيْطَان is described in the حَدِيث as being like a wolf among a flock: if he finds the shepherd watching from one side, he comes from another. One of the key differences between شَيْطَان's وَسْوَاس and the هَوًى of the نَفْس is that شَيْطَان's whisperings are compulsive, while the نَفْس has its own habitual pull. We know from Ramadan that the شَيَاطِين are locked up — *صُفِّدَتِ الشَّيَاطِينُ* — and yet people still struggle. That is where you begin to distinguish between شَيْطَان, the نَفْس, هَوًى, and دُنْيَا, because those are the four enemies of the human being: شَيْطَان, نَفْس, هَوًى, and دُنْيَا.

The next section is on غَفْلَة — *وَغَفْلَةٌ عَنْ رُوحِ الدُّنْيَا نَهْيًا وَنَهًى وَحُكْمٍ وَهَيْئَتِهَا* — and this is a truly important concept. Imam الْجُنَيْد said that غَفْلَة is the root disease. And even though we have heard different candidates for the root disease proposed — عِبَادٌ, and then الْكِبْرُ, and then تَسْوِيف, which is procrastination — procrastination is itself a form of غَفْلَة, so I think those scholars would likely agree. The reason you procrastinate is that you are not truly attending to the matter at hand; if you genuinely saw the urgency of it, you would act. So غَفْلَة is a serious illness of the heart.

He calls it غَفْلَة: inattentiveness, heedlessness, unawareness. The Arabic word for a simpleton — someone you can easily fool — is مُغَفَّل, a passive form: one who has been caught unaware. And so غَفْلَة, in his definition, is صَحْو. As you know, صَحْو is a vital concept in the prayer itself — the سُجُودُ السَّهْوِ. The very fact that سُجُودُ السَّهْوِ exists, and is an essential element of knowing how to pray, tells us something: the nature of the human being is forgetfulness. Ghafla is a kind of forgetting.

The word in Arabic for the human being — إِنْسَان — is revealing. Some say it derives from أُنْس, meaning intimacy, because the human being is a creature that needs companionship, that needs أُنْس; he is not وَشِي, not a creature that can exist in isolation. Others say it comes from نِسْيَانٌ, which means to forget. If you examine the word إِنْسَان — and إِنْسَان applies to both male and female, though some linguists note that إِنْسَانَةٌ is a valid form, إِنْسَانَةٌ فِي تَنَاتُنْ, appearing in a line of جَاهِلِيٌّ poetry, though rare — the human being is إِنْسَان, male and female alike:

*الْإِنْسَانُ الْإِنْسَانُ الذَّكَرُ وَالْأُنْثَى*
*‏هَلْ أَتَىٰ عَلَى ٱلْإِنسَنِ حِينٌۭ مِّنَ ٱلدَّهْرِ لَمْ يَكُن شَيْـًۭٔا مَّذْكُورًا ‎*

So إِنْسَان is the human being. And in the Quran, when إِنْسَان is used, it sometimes refers to Adam, عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ, and the first slip of our father Adam was نِسْيَانٌ — he was caught unaware by شَيْطَان. That is why the human being must be vigilant; he must have عَاصِم, a guard. And so the disease of غَفْلَة is صَحْو — a forgetting — and *يَحْتَرِيَاتُ الْإِنْسَانِ مِنْ قِلَّةِ التَّحَفُّظِ وَالتَّيَقُّظِ*: it befalls the human being as a result of an absence of تَحَفُّظ — vigilance — and تَيَقُّظ — wakefulness.
