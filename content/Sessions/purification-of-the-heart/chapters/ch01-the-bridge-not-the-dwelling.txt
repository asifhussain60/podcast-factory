In other words, it does not include loving the world in order to spare others from dependence on you, or to free yourself from dependence on Allah's servants. This is why the Islamic tradition teaches that the world is a bridge: cross it toward the next life, and do not try to build on it as though you intend to stay forever.

He also points out that blameworthy love of this world is not a single, undifferentiated thing — in fact, love of the world falls under the five categories of legal rulings, and its judgment depends entirely on what it leads one to achieve. So love of worldly things that help one attain the next life — love of the Kaaba, love of the Prophet ﷺ, love of the people of Allah, love of books of knowledge, love of the means that draw one closer to God, love of fellow Muslims who support one in one's religion — all of that is obligatory, وَاجِب. There is also love that is recommended, مَنْدُوبٌ; love that is permissible, مُبَاح, such as love of one's children or one's spouse; love that is disliked, مَكْرُوهٌ; and love that is outright forbidden, حَرَامٌ.

He states: to love anything that assists one in committing something forbidden is itself prohibited. And censuring the world applies only to those aspects of it that do not assist toward salvation. Its reproach is restricted to love of it for those kinds of reasons. Indeed, the best of creation ﷺ forbade cursing the world. There is a hadith in which the Prophet said, لَا تَسُبُّوا الدُّنْيَا — do not curse the world — because Allah created it, and it is a means of attaining knowledge of Allah, Glorified and Exalted.

The word دُنْيَا itself is worth reflecting on. Scholars differ: some say it derives from دَنَاءَ, others from دَنُو. Either way, it is distinct from أَلَمْ, which refers to the world in the sense of the cosmos, nature, all of existence. دُنْيَا refers specifically to حَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا — the ephemeral dimension of life, the hustle and preoccupation that people get swept up in. That is what Allah warns us about: being deceived by this transient thing. The أَلَمْ that Allah created, by contrast, derives from a root meaning عَلَامَة — a sign. The cosmos is the greatest sign of Allah, which is precisely why we do not despise or look down on it.

This sets Islam apart from traditional Christian doctrine — though Christianity has been moving away from this position over the past century. The traditional Christian view condemned the world as evil. Islam does not say that. Allah says He created everything in the world for human beings: some things to reflect upon, as signs leading to Allah; others to benefit from as stewards and custodians of the earth. All of that is affirmed.

Things are praised or blamed according to what they produce — like a remedy or a poison. If something leads to disease, it is blameworthy; if it leads to a cure, it is praiseworthy. That is the lens through which one should view worldly things. Whatever of the material necessities of earthly life one obtains — food, housing, shelter — these are not considered دُنْيَا in the blameworthy sense. Even one's first wife, according to the scholars of Islamic law, is not considered دُنْيَا. And if a man marries to care for an orphan or a woman in need, that too falls outside the blameworthy category. If he takes a second, third, or fourth wife for purely worldly reasons, it falls under a different ruling — though it remains permissible, مُبَاح, it does not carry the same merit.

Similarly with wealth and reputation — جَهْ, meaning standing or position. Scholars have said that having a good name can be valuable insofar as it enables one to help others, not as an end in itself. The scholars have generally regarded the accumulation of excessive wealth as disliked, مَكْرُوهٌ, because the more wealth one acquires, the greater the probability of falling into something displeasing to Allah. Becoming absorbed in amassing and competing for wealth is a dangerous occupation. People become addicted to the stock market, to the chase of accumulation — it becomes a compulsion.

As for earning wealth in order to show off — to say, look at me, look how clever I am, look how much I have — the scholars counted this among the people who have acquired enormities. مُكْتَسِبُ الْكَبَائِرِ: it is a major sin, حَرَامٌ, to do this.

He then says: love of praise is another disease. Love of being praised for what one has not done or accomplished is caused by craving something other than the Exalted. Those who love to be praised for things Allah has not praised them for — this is considered forbidden, a disease of the heart. One should only desire to be praised for what one has genuinely done, though some scholars go further and say one should not really desire praise except from Allah, Glorified and Exalted — while stopping short of prohibiting it entirely. It is not necessarily a bad thing when someone says, أَحْسَنْتَ, you did well. The Prophet ﷺ did say this to people.

There is a distinction between مَدْحٌ that is forbidden and مَدْحٌ that is permitted. The forbidden kind is flattery. The Prophet ﷺ said: throw dirt in the faces of flatterers — meaning those who tell you how wonderful you are when it is not true, or when they do not mean it. The poets who would compose elaborate panegyrics for the caliphs exemplify this: al-Mutanabbi wrote poem after poem for Sayf al-Dawla, all of it مَدْحٌ, telling him how magnificent he was. That is the blameworthy kind. Whereas sincerely telling someone أَحْسَنْتَ, or that they did good work, is not prohibited — especially when there is no risk that it will make them conceited. It functions as encouragement, as a form of gratitude.

He goes on to say that طَمَعْ — covetousness, greediness of expectation — is the very reason people flatter others and also why they love to be praised themselves. They want something in return. This, too, is a disease. To enjoy being told you did a great job for something you did not do, while knowing full well you did not do it, is a deeply contemptible thing. You smile and say, thank you so much, I'm glad you appreciate the effort — when you put in none. This happens in universities, where graduate students do all the work and the professor takes the credit. It happens in corporations, where a manager assigns tasks, the team does the work, and the manager claims the recognition. It is a very common human tendency — which is precisely why scholars recognized it centuries ago, and why the Quran addresses it. Allah says that there are those who love to be praised for what they have not done. It is, in that sense, a Quranic reality.

He then turns to envy — and here I will pause, because envy is a lengthy topic and a major illness. We will cover envy and رِيَاء next week, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ. We are continuing with this text now in our third meeting, and we should reach the diseases of fear and desire of other than Allah, though whether we get that far depends on how much ground remains.
