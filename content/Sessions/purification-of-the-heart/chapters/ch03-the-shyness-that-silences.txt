He then addresses الْحَيَاءُ الْمَذْمُومُ — blameworthy modesty. Modesty itself is a virtue; the Prophet ﷺ said, الْحَيَاءُ مِنَ الْإِيمَانِ — حَيَاء is from faith. But blameworthy modesty is a different thing entirely: it is the shyness that prevents you from speaking against wrongdoing when you witness it. You see a مُنْكَرٌ and you should say something, but you hold back out of embarrassment. A grown child, for instance, may feel that the modesty owed to parents prevents them from addressing a wrong the parents are committing — but a مُنْكَرٌ is a مُنْكَرٌ regardless of who is committing it.

A few important conditions attach to the obligation of إِنْكَارُ الْمُنْكَرِ. First, the مُنْكَرٌ must be مُجْمَعٌ عَلَيْهِ — agreed upon by scholars — before you can speak against it. You cannot make إِنْكَار of something that is مُخْتَلَفٌ فِيهِ, a matter of scholarly disagreement. Wherever difference of opinion exists, you must proceed with great caution, because what one scholar considers a مُنْكَرٌ another may consider permissible. It is said of Imam الْمَازِرِيُّ that his knowledge was so vast he rarely made إِنْكَار of anyone, because he could almost always find a valid proof for their action somewhere in the tradition. This is something Muslims desperately need to hear today, given how freely they condemn one another.

Blameworthy modesty also includes the shyness that prevents a person from asking questions about the religion. This is why — as 'Ā'isha رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهَا remarked — the best women were those of the أَمْصَار, the great cities, because modesty did not stop them from seeking knowledge of the deen. She was praising them: despite the sensitivity of certain topics, it did not deter them. The Prophet ﷺ himself once had a woman come to him asking about menstruation; she kept pressing with questions until eventually he asked 'Ā'isha to show her what to do, because the subject had become difficult for him to address directly.

And 'Ā'isha could answer the question because she was a فَقِيهًا. But the point is that the woman wanted to know. Some women would even send, in a small box, a cloth from their menstrual period to ask 'Ā'isha about its ruling — the حُكْم — regarding color, to make sure they understood when the حَيْض began and ended.

Obviously, most women would not feel comfortable doing that at all, given their modesty. But those women were so firmly committed to doing what Allah commanded that their modesty did not prevent them. This tendency runs stronger in women than in men, but it applies to both — there are men who are so modest that they refrain from asking questions they ought to ask.

Then he said — and this is from the line of poetry — أَمَّا حَيَاءُ أُخْرَى مِنْ كَمَا جَرَى، لِرَمْسِ صَفَا إِذْ زَيْنَبًا تَكَمْ مَرَى — as for the حَيَاءٌ of generosity, an example of it is what occurred with مُصْطَفَى ﷺ when he married Zaynab. تَكَمْ مَرَا refers to spending the night — that is, the first night of their marriage.

What happened was that the Prophet ﷺ had married Zaynab رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهَا, and he invited people over for a وَلِيمَة, a wedding celebration. The guests came, ate, and then some of them simply did not leave. Three men stayed behind, and the Prophet ﷺ — generous as he was — sat with them. But he had just married, and it was his wedding night. He was waiting for them to leave, while they, for their part, were savoring his company and did not want to go.

So he got up and left the room, hoping to signal to them that it was time to leave. He returned, and they were still there. He sat down again, then eventually left once more. It was at that point that the آيَةٌ was revealed — instructing believers to leave when they had finished their business, not to linger, to move on. The verse essentially said: when you finish eating, go.

That kind of حَيَاءٌ is not blameworthy, because there was no شَرْعِيٌّ obligation being neglected — no حُكْمٌ شَرْعِيٌّ at stake. It was purely out of his كَرَمٌ, his noble generosity, that he did not simply say, "It is my wedding night; please go now." Some people would have had no trouble saying that at all. But it was not in his nature to do so, ﷺ, and so the point is that this is not a blameworthy form of modesty.

وَأَشْوَاقُ الْقَلَمِ الْوَلِيمَةِ — he had fed them. وَخَرَجُوا عَنِ الْحِسِّ وَثَرَثَتِهِ — and they left, except for three. لَا بُوَّ وَلَا مُؤْتَمَرَ بِالْبَلَاخِ، فَهُوَ مِنْ مَحَاسِنِ الْأَخْلَاقِ — they stayed, and he did not tell them to leave, and that is from good character. It is not blameworthy.

Then he said — referring to a line of poetry — كَانَ رَجُلًا صَرِيحًا, and it echoes a hadith: لَوْ كَانَ الْحَيَاءُ رَجُلًا، لَكَانَ رَجُلًا صَالِحًا — if modesty were a man, he would be a righteous man. The Prophet ﷺ used this expression to convey that modesty is among the highest of qualities.
