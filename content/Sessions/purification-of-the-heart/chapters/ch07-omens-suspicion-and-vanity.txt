Imam الْـQarafi, a great Maliki scholar, says that *طِيَرَة* is a bad opinion and *طِيَرَة* as an *action* — *عَمَل* — is what results from holding that bad opinion, as long as the normative experience in which harm results does not apply. Walking under a ladder, for instance. In this culture: step on a crack, you'll break your mother's back. Children grow up avoiding the cracks in the pavement. A black cat crossing your path — thirteen years of bad luck. Breaking a mirror. Knocking on wood: "I've been healthy for years" — then you knock wood. These are all *طِيَرَة* and are in fact *حَرَامٌ* to act upon. If the شَرِيعَة gives us specific *أَذْكَار* and practices, those are different — they come from revelation, not superstition. But knocking on wood goes back to pagan tree-worship; those who venerated sacred trees would knock on them. That is the origin. Among the Arabs in the *جَاهِلِيَّة*, things like walking between two sheep were considered bad luck. Seeing an owl was an ill omen. In some cultures, a shooting star was a bad omen. All of these are forms of *طِيَرَة*.

The Prophet ﷺ also established that no one will ever be entirely free of *طِيَرَة* — it is part of human nature. There is a book by the skeptic Michael Shermer called *Why People Believe Weird Things*, and much of it addresses exactly this: how deeply embedded superstition is in human psychology. The *حَدِيث* points to the same reality. People check their horoscope before leaving the house. People will not buy or sell stocks without first consulting their astrologer — a completely absurd premise, yet they believe in it and it gives them a sense of control. People consult the I Ching before making any significant decision.

There's people that do رَمَلْ in the Sahara desert, and there are those who flip coins or toss them into wishing wells. All of these things are really part of human nature.

So when the Prophet ﷺ mentioned that one of three things will inevitably come to you in life, one of them was ill omens — طِيَرَة. And he said the way to cure it is this: if you feel that bad omen rising in you, just keep going. So if, in this culture, you're about to walk under a ladder and something stirs in your heart, you just keep walking — that's how you break it. And if a plant falls on your head — no, I'm just kidding. But that really is the way you break it: you ignore it and keep moving.

A friend of mine who has strong إِيمَان told me about someone who claimed to be a وَلِيٌّ — a claim my friend didn't think was warranted — and he said something about it. The followers of this man turned to my friend and said, "Don't you know? If he makes دُعَاء against you, he can destroy you." And my friend replied, "Go ahead — make دُعَاء against me right now. I want you to." What he was trying to show them was that this is not how Muslims behave. Real Muslims don't make دُعَاء against one another, and a true وَلِيٌّ certainly doesn't. And if he truly is a وَلِيٌّ, اللهُ can bring you difficulty without him even intending it.

There is a Sheikh I know in Mauritania, Muhammad Zayn — whom some of you have met — who is from the family of the Prophet ﷺ. He is a deeply righteous man who would never willingly harm anyone. But it is well known that people who speak ill of him or bear bad attitudes toward him tend to find trouble. So you don't want to be careless with the أَوْلِيَاء. At the same time, as the Moroccans say: don't belittle any Muslim, because he might be a وَلِيٌّ of اللهُ. In other words, be generally mindful of harming any Muslim — it is simply not a good thing to do.

So that is the matter of طِيَرَة. Now the next disease is having a bad opinion of others — what is called ظَنٌّ.

ظَنّ is suspicion, conjecture, opinion formed without any supporting facts. The worst form of bad opinion is harboring it toward someone whose outward appearance and conduct are plainly righteous. Outward appearance was something the early Muslims actually considered significant. While they also cautioned against dismissing those who may look unimpressive — because sometimes اللهُ veils His friends behind an ordinary exterior — they recognized what the Arabs call وَسْمَة: the quality of being وَسِيمٌ, someone whose face carries a kind of nobility. When an Arab delegate came to the Prophet ﷺ and then departed, the companions asked what he thought of him. He said, "His face is not the face of a liar." This is what is called فِرَاسَة — the capacity to discern goodness or corruption in a person's countenance. So forming a bad opinion of someone who possesses this وَسْمَة, who outwardly presents as a righteous person, is a particularly serious form of ظَنٌّ.

اللهُ says that some forms of ظَنٌّ are wrong action. This is important because ordinarily we are not held accountable for what merely passes through the heart — but there is a هِبَة of the heart, a form of backbiting that occurs entirely within the chest: entertaining a bad and unfounded opinion of someone, and it is written against you. This is called هِبَةً طَلْقَ الْقَلْبِ. It is unlike the passing thoughts that cross the mind without being invited — you are not accountable for those. What does incur accountability is what the scholars call عَزِيمَة: the thought that settles and takes root. We will go into these levels in more detail when we reach the section on قَوَاطِر, because there are degrees — حَدِيثُ النَّفْسِ, عَزِيمَة — and عَزِيمَة is the highest degree, the one written against you. When it runs deep in your heart such that it changes how you treat someone, it becomes هِبَةً طَلْقَ الْقَلْبِ — a form of backbiting in which no second person is even present. It is entirely between you and your own heart. And that is not permissible. It is written against you.

So the condition for blameworthy ظَنٌّ is that your heart has reached a verdict — عَزِيمَة — judging a person based purely on suspicion, without a دَلِيل, without any evidence that actually warrants that judgment. If someone asks you, "Why don't you like that person?" and you say, "I just feel it in my heart — something is wrong," that is a different matter we will come to shortly. But consider the حَدِيث: إِيَّاكُمْ وَالظَّنَّ، فَإِنَّ الظَّنَّ أَكْذَبُ الْحَدِيثِ — "Beware of ظَنٌّ, for ظَنٌّ is the most deceptive of speech." If you tell someone, "So-and-so is a really bad person," and they ask, "What's your proof?" and you say, "I just know" — that is أَكْذَبُ الْحَدِيثِ. That is a lie, because you have no دَلِيل. Our شَرِيعَة is built on the principle: أُمِرْتُ أَنْ أَحْكُمَ بِالظَّوَاهِرِ — "I was commanded to judge by outward matters." So it is essential to look at behavior, at what is manifest.

Then he says: لَا إِثْمَ فِي الشَّكِّ وَعَدَمُ الِاسْتِنَادِ لِسَبَبٍ فَعَدَمٌ يَكُونُ مُجَرَّدًا — there is nothing wrong with having doubts about someone, or even a bad opinion of him, if that opinion is grounded in sound reason and not merely baseless. This is a critical distinction.

The difference between شَكٌّ and ظَنٌّ is this: ظَنٌّ is when your internal scales have tipped — you have made a judgment. Shakk is when both pans are level. Is he good? Unknown. Is he bad? Unknown. That is شَكٌّ: "I simply don't know, and I must be cautious." That is not حَرَامٌ. ظَنّ is when you come to a conclusion — without proof — that the person is bad. Everyone understand the difference? This distinction will genuinely serve you in life.

So how do we deal with people about whom we feel genuine doubt? First, if the person is a فَاسِق — and a فَاسِق is someone who commits major sins openly, or who is habitually careless about minor ones, consistently and without remorse — then اللهُ says: if a فَاسِق comes to you with a report, verify it before you act on it. You cannot take what such a person says at face value.

Now, if what you have is mere شَكٌّ — not certainty, just unresolved doubt — what Sidi Ahmad Zarruq advises is excellent counsel, and though it comes from the ninth century it is even more pertinent today: do not entrust your دِين, your family, or your wealth to anyone until you have tested them a thousand thousand times. That is of course مُبَالَغَة — hyperbole — but his point is plain: test people. Observe whether they are genuine and sincere. If you don't yet know, don't trust.

One of the poets said: "A good opinion in these days is foolishness — think the worst of people and stay on guard." And truly, this is what the عُلَمَاء say: when the times are full of corruption, the sound position is to be cautious and distrustful of people — not from ظَنٌّ, not from having made a judgment, but simply from prudence. You don't extend trust until it has been earned, because so many people lack تَقْوَى. They will smile at your face, speak beautifully of their own faith and virtue, and then cheat you. It happens all the time.

That is also why the عُلَمَاء say that in corrupt times, it becomes obligatory for those distributing زَكَاةٌ to investigate the circumstances of claإِيمَانts. They cannot simply hand it over. When people are generally righteous, you take them at their word. But when the times are corrupt and someone comes saying, "Brother, I have five children and no money," you bear a responsibility to verify the claim. The claإِيمَانts cannot be upset or indignant about this — they must accept it. It is not about distrusting them personally. It is, as they say in this culture, that the bad ones have ruined it for the good ones. The response has to be: "Unfortunately, because this is an أَمَانَة from اللهُ, we have to verify. It isn't that we doubt you — this is a blanket policy, for that reason. And we are sorry that the reality has come to this."

So that is the matter of bad opinion of others. Having doubts about someone — شَكٌّ — is fine. Having a bad opinion of someone when there is ample evidence he is truly a bad person is simply common sense. On the other hand: when people make تَوْبَة, do not hold their past over them. One of the worst things you can do to a person is remind them of the evil they have left behind. Let people move forward. Do not cling to their past, because it is deeply unfair.

And there is another dimension here. A good opinion of اللهُ and a good opinion of the servants of اللهُ — no مُؤْمِن was ever given anything better than these two. Generally, it is praiseworthy to have a good opinion: certainly of اللهُ, and generally of His servants. ابْنُ عَمَّارٍ said that no servant was ever harmed by a good opinion, whereas a bad opinion can injure you. And in our شَرِيعَة the principle is بَرَاءَةُ الْأَصْلِيَّة — people are presumed innocent until there is real cause to conclude otherwise.

The other point of supreme importance is the opinion one holds of اللهُ. There is a حَدِيث reported by الطَّبَرَانِي: أَنَا عِنْدَ ظَنِّ عَبْدِي بِي، فَلْيَظُنَّ عَبْدِي بِي مَا شَاءَ — "I am as My servant thinks of Me, so let My servant think of Me whatever he will." Another حَدِيث clarifies it further: فَمَنْ ظَنَّ بِي خَيْرًا، وَجَدَ خَيْرًا؛ وَمَنْ ظَنَّ بِي شَرًّا، وَجَدَ شَرًّا — "I am as My servant thinks of Me. Whoever thinks well of Me finds good; whoever thinks ill of Me finds ill." This حَدِيث is the key to understanding another, found in the Forty Hadith of Imam النَّوَوِيُّ, in which it is stated that every servant receives what he deserves, and then: فَمَنْ وَجَدَ خَيْرًا فَلْيَحْمَدِ اللَّهَ — "If he finds good, let him thank اللهُ" — وَمَنْ وَجَدَ غَيْرَ ذَلِكَ فَلَا يَلُومَنَّ إِلَّا نَفْسَهُ — "and if he finds otherwise, let him blame no one but his own soul."

The underlying idea is that if you are a true مُؤْمِن, everything ultimately works in your favor. The affair of the مُؤْمِن is all good. Even the bad that befalls you — the true مُؤْمِن believes it carries a reason, and that there is good within it. This is a fundamentally different worldview from that of the مُتَأَيِّس, the pessimist, who falls into what might be called سَخْطَةُ الْقَدَرِ — railing against destiny, saying, "Why is this happening to me? I don't deserve this." The Prophet ﷺ himself said something to this effect: "If You are not angry with me, I do not care" — and he acknowledged that ease would naturally be more comfortable, but that the difficulty of a hard moment is itself a good thing for the مُؤْمِن. That is what it means to have a good opinion of اللهُ and a good opinion of others.

The next disease is called عُجْب.

Imam الْغَزَالِيّ says the distinction between عُجْب and كِبْرٌ is that كِبْرٌ requires two people. Kibr is arrogance — it needs the مُتَكَبِّر, the one who is arrogant, and the one toward whom the arrogance is directed. If زَيْدٌ comes to أَمْرٌ and says, "You insignificant fool — why didn't you do what I told you?" زَيْدٌ is the مُتَكَبِّر and أَمْرٌ is the one being looked down upon. Ujb, by contrast, is like كِبْرٌ, except that it requires no second person. The person afflicted with عُجْب is simply impressed with himself. If he has a mirror, he is content — he looks and is genuinely dazzled by how wonderful and magnificent he is.

He defines عُجْب as follows: you magnify and exalt the نِعْمَ — the blessing — while forgetting the one who gave it. In this culture, if someone is told, "You're so beautiful," and they respond, "Thank you," as though they had done something to bring it about — that is a strange thing, if you think about it carefully. Perhaps they spent a great deal on reconstructive surgery, but even then they should be thanking the surgeon, not congratulating themselves. The point is: if اللهُ made someone beautiful, they should be grateful to اللهُ. The مُنْعِم — the one who bestowed the gift — is اللهُ, not the person themselves. They did nothing to bring it about.

And even when real human effort is involved — when someone has a genuine talent that took years to develop — that talent, in its raw form, was still a gift from اللهُ. The labor it took to develop it was itself made possible by تَوْفِيق from اللهُ. You see this clearly within families.

<!-- page 95 -->

Consider beautiful calligraphy. It looks effortless to the observer. I once sat with Sidi Muhammad Zakariyya — the well-known American calligrapher — and watched him work. He made it look so natural that I thought, "I could do that." They say the mark of a master is precisely this: he makes it look so easy that you believe you could do it yourself — but you couldn't. Imam الْـJurjani said much the same of the master of rhetoric: when he speaks, he speaks with such ease and fluency that the listeners think, "I could do that." They could not.

What you have to bear in mind when you see Sidi Muhammad Zakariyya execute his calligraphy is the years of patient, painstaking repetition that preceded it — practicing waws and ya's and ba's, writing out passages again and again. You see the finished work and say, "سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ." And that is a gift from اللهُ. Now if someone given such a gift were to say, "Look at all my accomplishments — I did this myself, I pulled myself up by my own effort" — that is عُجْب of the worst kind. And there are countless people in the world who think exactly this: that whatever they have achieved, they achieved alone. "I built myself. I earned this." That is عُجْبُ النَّفْسِ.

The مُؤْمِن, by contrast, understands: if اللهُ gave you the تَوْفِيق to be disciplined, that too was from اللهُ.

One person grows up with discipline; another, in the same household with the same environmental factors, does not. The disciplined one goes on to master their studies, graدُعَاءtes with honors — and all of it is from اللهُ. As Imam الْـRazi says, اللهُ is the maker of every maker and every made thing. اللهُ created the one who built the airplane and the airplane itself. اللهُ created the one who designed the computer and the computer itself. Once you truly grasp that, the urge to be impressed with yourself dissolves.

And then there is this: above everyone who knows something, there is always someone who knows more. This is the lesson مُوسَى, عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ, had to learn. When he was asked whether anyone on earth was more knowledgeable than he was, he said he did not think so. So اللهُ sent him to الْخَضِر — who was not a prophet — and made him a student. Even then, he did not prove to be as patient a student as he could have been. The Prophet, صلى الله عليه وسلم, said that had مُوسَى not asked those questions, he would have learned far more from الْخَضِر. He did not absorb everything he might have, yet he was still taught. And the greatest lesson he took away was this: there is always someone who knows more than you, so never forget that.

We must choose our words carefully when speaking of prophets. I am not saying مُوسَى was a poor student — he was, I am sure, extraordinary. But he had a lesson to learn, and اللهُ taught it to him through الْخَضِر, عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ.

Now, regarding vanity — the reason I translated *عُجْب* as vanity is that, in the dictionary, vanity is defined as excessive pride in one's appearance or accomplishments, which maps closely onto the Arabic. The root *عَجِيبَة* means to wonder or marvel at something; *عُجْب* is to marvel at oneself or one's own achievements. The word *vanity* itself derives from the Latin *vanus*, meaning empty — and so the very source of our vanity is empty and will one day vanish.

The Romans understood this. When a general like Caesar held a victory parade through Rome, riding through the city on a chariot while crowds cheered, a slave would walk alongside him holding a laurel wreath over his head and repeating: *all is vanity*. That slave was there to remind the triumphant general — it is all vanity. This is an ancient wisdom, a *حِكْمَة*, a reminder that all these accomplishments will eventually be forgotten. Charles Lindbergh is a case in point: he received an enormous ticker-tape parade in New York City, and within ten years the American government considered him a criminal for his Nazi sympathies. The hero had fallen.

There is a story — I believe it was written by James Thurber, who wrote *The Secret Life of Walter Mitty* — about a man who saves a child, and the reporters rush in to make him a hero. It turns out he is actually a disreputable person who simply wants to profit from the incident. As he stands at the window watching the adoring crowds below, one of the reporters pushes him out. The point is that there is always another side. No matter how much good a person does, there will always be detractors — or the glory itself will simply prove hollow.

Think of the Yankees parading through New York, celebrated as the greatest team in the world, while the city itself is in moral, social, and ethical disarray. That is *عُجْب* at a collective scale — *الْمُعْجَبَةُ بِالنَّفْسِ* — and it is a deeply troubling condition.

He then notes: knowing that you are incapable, that you can neither produce any benefit nor ward off any harm on your own, should be enough to strip away all vanity. Vanity originates from nothing other than ignorance of two truths: that اللهُ alone is the fashioner and giver of blessings, and that you are incapable of anything by yourself. Forgetting those two things is how *عُجْب* takes hold.

This is a disease one must always be on guard against — especially when one does something well. And it is particularly relevant for those whom اللهُ has blessed with outward, physical beauty. Becoming vain about one's appearance is a dangerous trap, one that both men and women can fall into, though it is an especially acute danger for women.

The next disease he addresses is *غِشٌّ* — fraud. He defines it as concealing some fault or harm, whether religious or worldly, even when the other party is a member of a protected minority or someone who has a treaty with the Muslims. Others have interpreted it as adorning something that has no real benefit, or making an evil appear to be good.

There is a حَدِيث in which the Prophet, صلى الله عليه وسلم, said: *whoever tricks us is not from us.* A Muslim is not someone who engages in fraud. And fraud, he notes, can occur in worldly affairs as well as religious ones. In Islamic law, it is prohibited to sell something while concealing a known defect. If you sell a car and genuinely do not know that the engine is in poor condition, that is one matter — but if you know it, you are obligated to disclose it. Failing to do so is a disease of the heart; that is precisely where it originates.

I have noted in the commentary that this disease is conspicuous in modern advertising, which to my mind suggests that only a diseased heart could produce that kind of work comfortably. It also has a historical precedent in the sophists of ancient Greece. The sophists worshipped rhetoric and were, in effect, the first historical relativists. Relativism holds that there is no right and wrong — might makes right, and the most persuasive argument wins, regardless of its truth. That is a thoroughly modern disease as well, but the sophists were its original practitioners. They mastered rhetoric and believed the only thing that truly mattered was being convincing. Truth or falsehood was beside the point; what mattered was getting what you wanted through the art of persuasion.

For the Muslim intellectual tradition, by contrast, rhetoric is the art of presenting truth in the most compelling way — not of dressing up falsehood. It is a fundamentally different worldview. The sophists, by their own admission, set out to make the worse cause appear to be the better one. They believed that men are either wolves or sheep: smart wolves eat the dumb sheep. This is, unfortunately, a widely held view in modern corporate culture — that competition justifies whatever means are available to you. The word *sophisticated* itself traces back to the Greek root that gave us *sophist* — people who thought themselves clever and wise.

Next week, he is going to address what I think is one of the most consequential diseases in the entire book — *الْعُجْبُ الْكَبِيرُ*, or rather *الرِّيَاءُ* — and he spends nearly as much time on it as he does on some of the other major diseases. We will take that up next session.

Are there any questions?

*On الْخَضِر and مُوسَى:* Yes, مُوسَى met الْخَضِر before he was sent to the بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ. Al-Khadir was a prophet — that is a separate matter.

*On bad opinion versus hatred:* Having a bad opinion of someone means thinking ill of them without evidence. Hatred is something different — it is an actual emotion, a feeling. We will get into that in more detail when we reach that chapter. It becomes truly dangerous when you act on it. The Arabic terms are *سُوءُ الظَّنِّ* and *بُغْضٌ*.

*On fraud toward non-Muslims:* What he is saying is that *غِشٌّ* — fraud — applies even in dealings with a *ذِمِّي*, a non-Muslim living under the protection of the Muslim state. You cannot cheat a non-Muslim simply because they are not Muslim. You are obliged to be upright in your transactions with everyone.

*On reminding people of past wrongs:* Generally speaking, if something happened in the past and a person has moved on from it, continually reminding them of it is not fair, because it closes the door to change. If they are repeating the behavior, that is a different matter — but for past mistakes they have left behind, it is better to let it go.

*On the word* يُسْرَى *and the grammatical point about* عُسْر: There is a tradition of saying that one difficulty will not overcome two eases, and the grammatical basis for this is in the Quranic ayah. اللهُ says: *‏إِنَّ مَعَ ٱلْعُسْرِ يُسْرًۭا ‎* — and then repeats it. The word *الْعُسْر* carries the definite article *الْـ*, which in the second instance is what grammarians call *عَهْدٌ* — it refers back to the same *عُسْر* mentioned before. So it is the same singular difficulty appearing both times. But *يُسْرَى*, the ease, is indefinite — *نَقِّرَا* — in both instances. Because it is a new indefinite noun each time, it counts as two separate eases. The lesson is that with any one difficulty come two eases.

*On energy and vibrations between people:* I think there is something real to the experience of feeling uplifted or dragged down by the people around you. We would express it in our tradition as someone having a good or bad soul, or being a good or bad person. The Arabic *مُنْسَجَم* literally means to be in the flow, in harmony — and you can see the analogy in music: a cacophonous sound creates a vibration that hurts the ear, while something melodious has a positive effect. So the sense that certain people carry a certain energy is not entirely foreign to Islamic thought, even if we frame it differently.

All of us have had the experience of being around someone who is deeply depressed and finding it difficult not to start feeling depressed ourselves. People do seem to have fields — auras, if you want to use that word — that are either uplifting, draining, or simply neutral.

I would also say this: feeling uncomfortable around someone is not the same as judging them to be a bad person. One of my teachers, Sheikh Hamid, told me a story from his student days. There was a fellow student who liked him very much and always wanted to be near him, but he simply did not feel a connection with that person. He went to his teacher feeling guilty about it, and his teacher said: souls are arranged in ranks. Some souls are simply not harmonious with one another. It does not mean either party is bad — it is just that some people you do not feel a natural affinity with, while with others you feel an immediate and powerful connection.

Some of the scholars say this has to do with our pre-worldly existence — the gathering of the *أَرْوَاح*, the spirits, before they entered this world. The closer your soul was to another soul on that day, the more affinity you will feel toward that person in this life. The further away, the more distant you will feel. This would explain why you can meet someone for the first time and feel as though you have known them all your life. I felt that immediately with Sheikh Abdallah الْـQadi — the moment I met him, it was as though I had always known him. Other connections develop slowly, over time. There is wisdom in both kinds.

*On distrust as distinct from bad opinion:* Having a bad opinion of a specific person without evidence is one thing. Being generally cautious and guarded as a disposition is something else. The scholars say that in difficult times, a degree of wariness is actually wisdom — it is being on your guard, not maligning anyone in particular.

Don't become paranoid — that's a psychological condition to be avoided. Seriously. You don't want to be the person who says, "Do you hear what he said? Did you see how he looked at me?" That kind of thinking is an affliction.
