The next disease is antipathy towards death: كَرَاهِيَةُ الْمَوْتِ. He says about it: كَرَاهَةُ الْمَوْتِ بِحَيْثُ يَنْفِرُ مِنْهُ وَيَأْنَفُ إِذَا مَا يُذْكَرْ، حَتَّى كَأَنَّهُ بِذَوْقِ كُلِّ نَفْسٍ لَهُ الَّذِي أَتَى ذُو الْجَهْلِ — antipathy towards death, this dislike of it, is when one flees from it and becomes annoyed whenever it is mentioned, as if he is entirely ignorant of Allah's words: كُلُّ نَفْسٍۢ ذَآئِقَةُ ٱلْمَوْتِ — every soul shall taste death.

Death is our inevitable end in this world. Each of us will taste it. This dislike of death he is describing is the kind where one runs from it.

<!-- page 255 -->
‏قُلْ إِنَّ ٱلْمَوْتَ ٱلَّذِى تَفِرُّونَ مِنْهُ فَإِنَّهُۥ مُلَقِيكُمْ ۖ ثُمَّ تُرَدُّونَ إِلَىٰ عَلِمِ ٱلْغَيْبِ وَٱلشَّهَدَةِ فَيُنَبِّئُكُم بِمَا كُنتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ ‎ — say: this death you are fleeing from will certainly meet you, and then you will be returned to the Knower of the seen and the unseen, who will inform you of what you used to do.

Death is inevitable, and fleeing from it is futile. Some people observe that this culture is a death-denial culture. Muslims are less afflicted by this than non-Muslims — non-Muslims actively avoid mentioning death, find it uncomfortable in conversation, consider it almost bad manners to bring it up. I was once in a federal building getting a passport, riding up in an elevator with two men who looked like bureaucrats. One said to the other: I'm just waiting for that guy to die — he's about to drop dead any minute, and I'm really looking forward to it. The other asked why. He said: it'll just be nice to be in a world without him. Then the second one said: maybe we'll die before him. And the first said: no, no, he's almost gone.

That exchange is a revealing example of death denial in action — death is only something that happens to sick people, to old people, to the man on his deathbed. The healthy, the young — they operate as though death simply does not apply to them.

You know, what if we're in Los Angeles and an earthquake strikes? There is a deep غَفْلَة that people fall into regarding death — a profound heedlessness. They don't truly believe they are going to die.

<!-- page N -->

And if they do acknowledge it, it's mere lip service: *Oh, that's a long way off.* So when someone hears death mentioned and it genuinely disturbs them — *I don't want to talk about that. Let's change the subject. This is depressing. This is morbid* — that attitude is foreign to our tradition.

The Prophet ﷺ once passed by a group of people laughing in a gathering and said, "Mix your gatherings with remembrance of the destroyer of pleasures." In other words, the remembrance of death brings a kind of sobriety and gravity to a gathering that has become too frivolous — not that people cannot enjoy themselves, but gatherings can drift into pure levity with no substance and no تَطْهِير. That is precisely what the Prophet ﷺ was warning against: sitting in مَجْلِسُ الْغَفْلَةِ.

The Prophet ﷺ also said that there is no hour in this life you will regret in the آخِرَة except one spent without remembering Allah. And in another narration, he said that anyone who sits in a gathering and then rises from it without having remembered Allah is like one who rises from a dead carcass — جِفَاءً, carrion. We were created to worship Allah, and we are naturally drawn to intimacy and companionship, so our gatherings should not be purely for entertainment. They should carry some purpose: discussing matters of importance, making دُعَاء for one another, and so forth.

Then the author states that this — hating death — is counted among the diseases of the heart, and he says: be content with what Allah the Exalted has decreed for you. If you are in a state of genuine acceptance of death, it will not disturb you — and accepting death is accepting the decree of Allah. One shaykh once told me in a gathering: "We are all on death row. We are simply waiting for the executioner." اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ. We have all been condemned to death; we are just waiting. But once you realize that the One who condemned you to death is the very One who gave you life in the first place, you can be content — because He has done it for a wisdom.

From another perspective, death is a celebration for the believer. Shaykh Ibn al-Habib said that in death, for the مُؤْمِن, there is a thousand-fold repose. He said that as long as you are in this world, there is not a single cell in your body that cannot suffer pain or disease. Once you leave this world, all of that ends. There is a رَهَا in death. He also said on another occasion that death for the مُؤْمِن is like being moved from the third-class compartment to first class — the conductor comes and says, "No, no, you shouldn't be here. Come up to first class." And do you say, "No, no, I want to stay in third class"? Of course not. It is like being bumped up to first class on an airplane. You're not upset. الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ. For the مُؤْمِن, death is not a bad thing.

That is also why our mourning period is only three days. I had a professor who taught a course called Death and Dying, and he said that one of the worst things about this country is the mourning industry — that it does people a disservice by prolonging and commercializing grief. Many people go into mourning for years and spend years in therapy trying to recover. All of that is love of the دُنْيَا and attachment to it. The people of Allah, when they accept Allah's decree — if Allah takes a beloved person, a child, anyone — خَلَاصٌ. It is painful; the Prophet felt pain and wept. But it was Allah who did it, and we must be pleased with what Allah does. That is why the مُؤْمِن is able to pass through such trials.

And there is something remarkable about Muslims in this regard. No people — as far as I know, certainly not the Europeans — can face death the way Muslims can. When calamities befall Muslims, you see their true character. When the same calamities befall non-Muslims, many fall apart, some take their own lives. Muslims, because of their strength with Allah and their divine connection, are able to do what others cannot — including face death.

Maurice Bucaille, the French physician, said that one of the things that first drew him toward Islam was watching how Algerians died in France compared to the French. He was profoundly impressed by the way North Africans faced death. Having worked on hospital wards myself where people were dying, I can say: you see people in this culture who simply do not know how to die — they have no idea. But watch a devout Muslim, and you will see someone who has been preparing for death his entire life. When it comes, he is ready.

Shaykh Uthman Bashir Uthman, رَحِمَ اللهُ, was a close friend of mine. He was in his early eighties, had grown up in Eritrea, and eventually came to the United States. I told him about Darul Islam and he said we had to go there. We went together — I came along because he couldn't speak Arabic — and afterward he returned to Medina, where he died shortly after. He told me that he had always known he would die in Medina, because when he was a young man in Eritrea, his shaykh had told him: "There will be great فِتْنَةٌ in this country. The communists will take over and you will have to flee. But Allah, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, will open a place for you in Medina. You will live there, die there, and be buried in بَقِيعٌ." I heard that from his own lips.

This is what they call كَشْف — something that happens for certain people of Allah. It is not revelation; rather, Allah grants them a kind of opening through which they perceive things and can sometimes convey them to others. At the time, he had always assumed his shaykh was simply saying kind words. But sure enough, the communists took over. He was a wealthy businessman and had to flee. A place was opened for him in Medina; he received citizenship there and lived his remaining years traveling in and out. He told me, "I truly feel, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, that I will die in Medina."

When the time came, they had him in a hospital in Jeddah. He had cancer. He was very weak, hooked up to equipment, barely able to move. Then one day he told his son, "We have to go to Medina today." The doctor said he absolutely could not leave. He said, "I am leaving today. My son will drive me." The doctor was adamant — he was too ill, he needed to stay. He said no. They took him that night, drove all the way to Medina. He arrived, spent the entire night saying لَآ إِلَهَ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ. Just before فَجْر, he told his son a few things to convey to certain people, then said the شَهَادَة and died. He was buried in بَقِيعٌ, in the old section next to Imam Malik and Nafi', in a spot where people are no longer usually buried — but when they arrived, they found a grave already dug, and they placed him in it. His جَنَازَة prayer was held on جُمُعَة at the Prophet's Mosque ﷺ, and he was the only person that day with a جَنَازَة there.

I tell that story because it is an example of how Muslims who know how to die, die.

There is also a story — and Allah knows its full truth — told about Farid al-Din al-Attar, who was a perfumer. The account of how he made his تَوْبَة goes like this: a man once came begging for alms, and al-Attar told him to go get a job. The man asked what he did for a living. Al-Attar said he sold perfumes. The man replied, "The most important thing you will ever do is die. And I know how to do that." Then he said, أَشْهَدُ أَنْ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا اللهُ، أَشْهَدُ أَنَّ مُحَمَّدًا رَسُولُ اللهِ — and died. Whether or not the story is literally true, there is a truth within it. There really are people who know how to die. When the moment comes, they know exactly what to do. And there are others who do not — when the time comes, they fall into complete confusion.

This is one of the blessings of remembering death often. Our scholars, following the Prophet ﷺ based on his حَدِيث on the remembrance of death, have also recommended passing through the states of the آخِرَة in one's mind: visualizing the moment of dying, being washed, being wrapped, being placed in the grave, having the earth cover you, being questioned by the angels, the resurrection, the مِيزَان. To reflect on those stages as a spiritual practice. It is a deeply sobering exercise — one that can rouse you from the sleep we are all walking through in this life.

The author then adds: أَمَّا إِذَا قَلَاهُ لَا لِذَاتِهِ وَلَا لَلْإِنْسَرَامِ عَنْ لَذَّاتِهِ بَالْخَوْفَ قَطْعِهِ عَنْ إِسْتِعْدَادِهِ بِطَاعَةِ اللَّهِ إِلَىٰ مَعَادِهِ — if someone dislikes death not merely because it is death, and not because it will sever him from the pleasures of the دُنْيَا, but out of fear that he has not had enough time to prepare for what lies ahead, then that is a legitimate reason. The Prophet ﷺ encouraged people to desire a long life on that basis: if you are a person who has done wrong, you have time to make amends; if you are a person doing good, you have more time to increase your good deeds and strengthen your صَحِيفَة — your scroll — for the يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ. He mentioned that طُولُ الْأَمَلِ, desiring a long life, is a good thing when one is engaged in righteous deeds.

The Prophet ﷺ also said to Aisha رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهَا: رضي الله عنه من أحب الله أحب لقاءه — whoever loves Allah loves to meet Allah. She responded, يا رسول الله الموت نكره الموت — O Messenger of Allah, we dislike death. The Prophet ﷺ indicated that this was natural — but said that what truly mattered was the dislike of the آخِرَة itself, of meeting Allah.

The point is that disliking death is obviously natural. Allah has placed it in our bodies at a cellular level. That is why, when life is threatened, you enter a heightened state of functioning and do things not consciously chosen in order to protect yourself or others. Allah has placed that in us and it is a good thing. That is not the dislike being addressed here. The Quran describes Ibrahim ﷺ: when he offered the angels food and they did not eat, فَأَوْجَسَ مِنْهُمْ خِيفَةًۭ — he felt a twinge of fear, a natural human response. That kind of instinctive fear is not what is being criticized.

Then the author says: أَوْ فَوَّضَ الْأَمْرَ إِلَى مَوْلَاهُ فَمَا يَشَاءُ أَرْضَهُ أَوَبْقَاهُ — or, if he fully entrusts his affair to his Master, and whatever Allah wills — whether to take him or to grant him more time — contents him. In other words, you are simply at peace with whatever Allah chooses to do with you. Both of these stances are good stances.

That is why the author says: فَذَانِ مَمْدُوحَانِ مَحْمُودَانِ وَالْكُرْهُ لَا يُبَعِّضُ مِنْ كَدَّانِ — both attitudes toward death are commendable and praiseworthy. A dislike of death in no way distances you from its proximity. Even if you dislike it, that dislike changes nothing — it will not push death any further away. It is far better, for the sake of your own inner state, to accept it.

Then the author says: ذَاكِرُهُ يُكْرَمُ بِالْقَنَاعَةٌِ وَبِنَشَاطِ قَلْبِهِ لِلطَّاعَةِ — the one who remembers death often is honored with certain qualities, among them قَنَاعَةٌ. Because when you genuinely believe you could die at any moment, there is a natural inclination to be present with what you have rather than perpetually deferring enjoyment to some imagined future when you'll "finally get it together" — which, as they say, never actually happens.

Contentment is vital. The Prophet ﷺ said: القَنَاعَةٌُ كَنزٌ لَا يَفْنَى — contentment is a treasure that is never exhausted. And he prayed: اللَّهُمَّ ارْزُقْ آلَ مُحَمَّدٍ الْقَنَاعَةٌَ وَالْكَفَافَ — O Allah, grant the family of Muhammad contentment and sufficiency. Just give them enough to live on and make them content with it.

The قُنُوعٌ — the person in a state of قَنَاعَةٌ — is the only truly wealthy person. A poor man who is content is in reality a rich man. A rich man who is discontented is in reality a poor man. Because فَقْرٌ is want — and قَنَاعَةٌ is the absence of want. If you have no want, you are wealthy, because wealth is defined by the absence of need. If you have no need, you are rich. That is why the Prophet ﷺ said: الْغِنَى غِنَى النَّفْسِ — real wealth is the wealth of the soul. لَيْسَ الْغِنَى عَنْ كَثْرَةِ الْعَرَضِ — it is not having an abundance of things. وَإِنَّمَا الْغِنَى غِنَى النَّفْسِ — real wealth is the contentment of the soul.

And so when you remember death, Allah grants you قَنَاعَ — contentment — as one of its blessings. This is all the more significant in an age when so many false needs and wants are being constantly planted in people's hearts. That is precisely what media does: يَغْرِسُ — it sows the seeds of طَمَعٌ, of desire, of شَهْوَةٌ, embedding all of these things deep within.

He also says that remembering death gives you energy — it directs the heart's activity toward obedience. Thinking about death makes you want to be more obedient to Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى, producing a kind of نَشَاط, an energized readiness to do good deeds. وَبِبِدَارِي تَوْبَةٌٍ وَيُبْتَرَى.

Beyond that, remembering death moves you to hurry toward تَوْبَةٌ. If a slip occurs and you do something wrong, the awareness of death prompts you to make تَوْبَةٌ quickly — to rectify the wrong. تَوْبَةٌ corrects bad actions, and that is itself a gift that comes from remembering death. When you do something wrong and then reflect, سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ, I am accountable for this — أَسْتَغْفِرُ اللَّهَ — that awareness drives you to seek Allah's forgiveness سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى.

The opposite holds equally true. The one who forgets death receives the three opposites of these gifts. He will always want more and will never attain قَنَاعَةٌ. He will يُرْسِلُ جَوَارِحَهُ فِي المَعْصِيَة. — give free rein to his limbs in disobedience — because death and the يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ and the حِسَابٌ are not on his mind. And when he does wrong, he feels no remorse, because he is not concerned with what awaits him in the Akhirah.
