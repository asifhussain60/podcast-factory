The next section is on غَفْلَة — *وَغَفْلَةٌ عَنْ رُوحِ الدُّنْيَا نَهْيًا وَنَهًى وَحُكْمٍ وَهَيْئَتِهَا* — and this is a truly important concept. Imam al-Junayd said that غَفْلَة is the root disease. And even though we have heard different candidates for the root disease proposed — عِبَادٌ, and then الْكِبْرُ, and then تَصْوِيفٌ, which is procrastination — procrastination is itself a form of غَفْلَة, so I think those scholars would likely agree. The reason you procrastinate is that you are not truly attending to the matter at hand; if you genuinely saw the urgency of it, you would act. So غَفْلَة is a serious illness of the heart.

He calls it غَفْلَة: inattentiveness, heedlessness, unawareness. The Arabic word for a simpleton — someone you can easily fool — is مُغَفَّل, a passive form: one who has been caught unaware. And so غَفْلَة, in his definition, is صَحْو. As you know, صَحْو is a vital concept in the prayer itself — the سُجُودُ السَّهْوِ. The very fact that سُجُودُ السَّهْوِ exists, and is an essential element of knowing how to pray, tells us something: the nature of the human being is forgetfulness. غَفْلَة is a kind of forgetting.

The word in Arabic for the human being — إِنْسَان — is revealing. Some say it derives from أُنْس, meaning intimacy, because the human being is a creature that needs companionship, that needs أُنْس; he is not وَشْيٌ, not a creature that can exist in isolation. Others say it comes from نِسْيَانٌ, which means to forget. If you examine the word إِنْسَان — and إِنْسَان applies to both male and female, though some linguists note that إِنْسَانَةٌ is a valid form, إِنْسَانَةٌ فِي تَنَاتُنْ, appearing in a line of Jahili poetry, though rare — the human being is إِنْسَان, male and female alike:

*الْإِنْسَانُ الْإِنْسَانُ الذَّكَرُ وَالْأُنْثَى*
*‏هَلْ أَتَىٰ عَلَى ٱلْإِنسَنِ حِينٌۭ مِّنَ ٱلدَّهْرِ لَمْ يَكُن شَيْـًۭٔا مَّذْكُورًا ‎*

So إِنْسَان is the human being. And in the Quran, when إِنْسَان is used, it sometimes refers to Adam, عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ, and the first slip of our father Adam was نِسْيَانٌ — he was caught unaware by شَيْطَان. That is why the human being must be vigilant; he must have عَاصِم, a guard. And so the disease of غَفْلَة is صَحْو — a forgetting — and *يَحْتَرِيَاتُ الْإِنْسَانِ مِنْ قِلَّةِ التَّحَفُّظِ وَالتَّيَقُّظِ*: it befalls the human being as a result of an absence of تَحَفُّظ — vigilance — and تَيَقُّظٌ — wakefulness.

When Sayyidna 'Ali, رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ, said: *النَّاسُ نِيَامٌ، فَإِذَا مَاتُوا انْتَبَهُوا* — "people are asleep, and when they die they wake up" — what he meant is that people are غَافِلٌ, sunk in heedlessness about their destiny: where they are going, and what they will be called to account for when they get there. Because if they truly knew, with real wakefulness — يَقَظَة — they could not do the things they do. That is why Imam al-Junayd said this is the core disease: if the human being truly knew what was coming, there is no way he could act as he does.

This idea is not entirely unlike the Socratic understanding, which belongs to a parallel tradition in the West — the idea that people do wrong because they are genuinely unaware; if they really knew what they were doing, they would not do it. The metaphor given in that tradition is the metaphor of the cave: human beings are like people sitting inside a cave with a lamp behind them, unable to turn around, watching shadows on the wall and taking those shadows for reality. Then one of them escapes, goes out into the real world, sees the sun and the stars and the trees, returns to the cave, and tells the others: "This is all an illusion — what we thought was real is not real; the real thing is somewhere else." And they kill him. That is the role of the prophets: they come and tell people, "You are all asleep — wake up, do you not realize?" So the concept of غَفْلَة is profound, and that is why Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى uses the word in many Quranic verses.

Another word the Quran uses is صَمَدٌ — and صَمَدٌ is similar to غَفْلَة, but there is a greater degree of awareness in it; the صَمَدٌ knows, in some sense, but the غَافِل does not. Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى says in سُورَةُ الْقَمَرِ: *‏لَّقَدْ كُنتَ فِى غَفْلَةٍۢ مِّنْ هَذَا فَكَشَفْنَا عَنكَ غِطَآءَكَ فَبَصَرُكَ ٱلْيَوْمَ حَدِيدٌۭ ‎* — "You were in heedlessness about this; We have removed the veil from you, and your sight today is sharp." There is a veil of غَفْلَة over the human being, screening him from reality. And Allah says in the Quran that there is a رِشَا — *وَعَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ أَكِنَّةٌ* — and this رِشْوَة prevents them from seeing things as they truly are. So the غَافِل is not seeing things as they truly are, while the يَقِظٌ — the one who is truly awake — sees things as they truly are.

The Prophet ﷺ prayed: *اللَّهُمَّ أَرِنِي الْأَشْيَاءَ كَمَا هِيَ* — "O Allah, show me things as they really are." And in another hadith: *اللَّهُمَّ أَرِنِي الْحَقَّ حَقًّا وَارْزُقْنِي اتِّبَاعَهُ، وَأَرِنِي الْبَاطِلَ بَاطِلًا وَارْزُقْنِي اجْتِنَابَهُ* — "O Allah, show me truth as truth and grant me the ability to follow it; and show me falsehood as falsehood and grant me the ability to avoid it." The one who is not غَافِل has precisely that state: they see truth as truth and falsehood as falsehood, they follow the truth and avoid the falsehood. And that is the مُتَّقٍ — that is him, the person of تَقْوَى: he does what Allah commanded and avoids what Allah prohibited.

So he is saying that غَفْلَة is *غَفْلُ الْمَرْءِ عَمَّا أَمَرَهُ اللَّهُ بِهِ وَنَهَاهُ عَنْهُ* — heedlessness about what Allah has commanded and what He has forbidden. That is why people do wrong: not out of pure malice in most cases, but out of heedlessness about what they have been commanded and what they have been prohibited from doing. And this is why, if you see a believer doing something wrong and you remind him, and he responds with *أَسْتَغْفِرُ اللَّهَ* — that is a good person, a مُتَّقٍ. You have brought him out of his غَفْلَة. The مُتَكَبِّر, by contrast — because كِبْرٌ is what follows after all that we have been discussing — when reminded of his غَفْلَة, will not accept the reminder. *أَخَذَتْهُ ٱلْعِزَّةُ بِٱلْإِثْمِ*: he takes refuge in his own pride and refuses. You say to him gently, and he gets angry at you: "Who do you think you are?" That is why the cure for غَفْلَة in a believer is good company — صُحْبَة. Be with people of صِدْق, of sincerity; be with people of تَقْوَى; be with truthful, sincere people who will tell you plainly when you are doing something wrong, and encourage you when you are doing something right. Those are the people who will pull you out of your غَفْلَة.

He then says: *وَهِيَ لَدَيْهِمْ أَصْلُ كُلِّ ذَنْبٍ* — those of this science consider غَفْلَة the source of all wrong actions, as Imam al-Junayd al-Baghdadi held. *وَدَوَاؤُهَا بِأَرْبَعِ دَوَاءٍ* — and the cure consists of four things that carry rectifying qualities: *فَاسْتَغْفِرِ اللهَ وَزُرْ وَصَلِّ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ وَاكْتُبْ وَاذْكُرْ*. So he gives the four things.

The first is اِسْتِغْفَار, because once you realize you have been غَافِل, you begin to see what you have done wrong as a consequence of that heedlessness, and you want to ask forgiveness. Many of the later scholars said that اِسْتِغْفَار and صَلَاةً عَلَى النَّبِيِّ are the two ذِكْرٌ of the final era — the two pillars of devotion for this last period of time. Istighfar is asking forgiveness of Allah, and it is a form of تَوْبَة. The Prophet ﷺ said that you should ask اِسْتِغْفَار at least seventy times, up to a hundred times a day — *أَسْتَغْفِرُ اللَّهَ* — so it is always a good practice. A complementary practice that most of the early Muslims observed was مُحَاسَبَة. The idea comes from what merchants do at the end of the trading day: they count the money, see what was gained and what was lost, and balance the scales. مُحَاسَبَة for the soul is the same: at the end of the day, you look at your day and ask — was there loss with Allah, or gain? If there was loss, you ask اِسْتِغْفَار. If there was gain, you say *الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ* and are grateful. *لَىِٕن شَكَرْتُمْ لَأَزِيدَنَّكُمْ* — if you are grateful, Allah increases you. So that is one way. He goes into شُكْر as well, since that is one of the مَقَامَاتٌ we will reach إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ.

The second remedy is زِيَارَة — visiting righteous people. The صَالِحِينَ occupy the lowest of four ranks: the أَنْبِيَاء and رُسُل are first, then the صِدِّيقٌ, then the شُهَدَاء, then the صَالِحِين. And yet they are all, in a sense, صَالِحٌ — a شَاهِد must be a صَلِيح, a صِدِّيق must be a صَلِيح, and the أَنْبِيَاء are all صَالِحِينَ by their very nature. صَلَاحٌ in Arabic means to be sound, and this is related to سَلَامَة — soundness, wholeness, the person who possesses a قَلْبٌ سَلِيمٌ. The definition of a صَلِيح is: *يُؤَدِّي حَقَّ اللهِ وَيُؤَدِّي حَقَّ الْعِبَادِ* — he gives what is due to Allah and he gives what is due to the servants of Allah. حَقُّ اللَّهِ is تَقْوَى: doing what He commanded and avoiding what He prohibited. حَقُّ الْعِبَادِ are all the responsibilities you carry in the world toward other people. So the صَلِيح cannot cheat — that is فِسْقٌ, the very opposite of صَلَاح. He does not lie, because the حَقٌّ of people upon you is that you be truthful to them. The صَلِيح is a person worth visiting.

Most scholars not only encourage visiting the صَالِحِينَ who are alive, but also those who have died. If you are in Medina, you go to بَقِيعْ and visit the صَالِحِينَ there — you give them سَلَامٌ, you say *رَحِمَهُمُ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ*. The Prophet ﷺ used to visit Sayyidna Hamza, and he visited the graveyard and said: *كُنْتُ نَهَيْتُكُمْ عَنْ زِيَارَةِ الْقُبُورِ فَزُورُوهَا* — "I used to forbid you from visiting graves," because of what the pre-Islamic Arabs used to do at them, "but now that they have been purified of that جَاهِلِيَّة, visit them." There is a benefit in visiting graves — it is a reminder of the آخِرَة — and there is a greater benefit in visiting the graves of the people of صَلَاح.

On the question of women and graves: there is a hadith — *لَعَنَ اللَّهُ زَوَّارَاتِ الْقُبُورِ*, and in one narration *زَائِرَاتُ الْقُبُورِ* — and زَوَّرَ is an intensive form, referring to someone who visits graves excessively and repeatedly. There is a hadith that is considered مُتَوَاتِر in which the Prophet ﷺ passed by the graveyard in Medina and found a woman there weeping. He said it would be better to be patient. She replied: *مَا أَصَابَكَ مَا أَصَابَنِي* — "You have not been struck by the calamity I have been struck by" — and she did not recognize him. He left her, and then someone told her that had been the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. She immediately went and knocked at his door and said: "I did not know it was you." And the Prophet ﷺ said: *الصَّبْرُ عِنْدَ الصَّدْمَةِ الْأُولَى* — "Patience is with the first blow." Patience is when the affliction first strikes — that is when Allah expects it from you. After a year, if the grief has passed and you have moved forward, that is not صَبْر; that is simply time doing its work. The صَبْر is at the moment the مُصِيبَة occurs, and that is where Allah wants to see ثَبَاتٌ — steadfastness — and not a shaking that would cause one to do something displeasing to Him. But the point of this hadith for our discussion is that the Prophet ﷺ did not tell her to leave the graveyard — and scholars take from that the permissibility of women visiting graves, though there is indeed a خِلَافٌ on the matter.

As for the أَدَب of visiting graves: what happens at some graves nowadays has nothing to do with what is being discussed here. At some shrines — and this applies in many parts of the Muslim world — people tie knots, women who cannot conceive go to ask the وَلِيٌّ to give them a son, people make vows to the buried saint. All of that has nothing to do with a proper زِيَارَة. He is talking about visiting with أَدَب, for the blessing of the visit itself — a زِيَارَة. That is something quite different. And this confusion is part of what has given تَصَوُّف a bad name: much of what is done under its label is بِدْعَة — in fact, it really is بِدْعَة. Women sitting around in a مَوْلِد without حِجَابٌ, chatting and gossiping all day — those are all problems. In such cases, the scholars' strategy is sometimes simply to prohibit the practice altogether, because the corruption has become so pervasive. But the best, without question, is to visit the living صَالِحِينَ.

Unfortunately, in our time, this is quite rare. To find a truly righteous person, you often have to travel far and go to difficult places, because many of them have simply retreated to places that are hard to reach. Someone like Murabbit al-Hajj — رَحِمَهُ اللهُ — whom we visited in Shinqit, the father of Shaykh Abdallah — I consider him, in my estimation, from the صَالِحِينَ. When Allah says *يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَكُونُوا مَعَ الصَّادِقِينَ* — "be with the truthful ones" — He would not command us to do something if there were no such people and no way to recognize them. It would be foolish to think we cannot recognize people of صِدْق and صَلَاح. There are obviously degrees and levels, but the basic criterion is the شَرِيعَة: if someone is outwardly upright in their observance of the شَرِيعَة, that is our standard. We judge by the outward, and we judge by truth.

One of the dangers I have noticed in modern تَصَوُّف — in many Muslim countries — is that there are people who go by the name of بِرّ or شَيْخٌ or whatever, and many of them inherited the title from their father, who inherited it from his father, and many of them have very little knowledge, sometimes almost none. And they sometimes use their positions for material gain — extracting money from followers, arranging advantageous marriages for their daughters, and so on. There are a great many charlatans. And nothing is worse than a religious charlatan — nothing. There is a man in Fez, Sidi Ma'ib, whom some here know, and he once said to me: "Allah has made the world vast, and He has made the means of earning حَلَالٌ provision without number — there are untold ways of earning حَلَالٌ. So whenever you see someone using the religion to gain دُنْيَا, know that it is a bad thing." If they are using it to accumulate wealth and exploit their position, that is a serious problem — and unfortunately there are many who claim to be شُيُوخٌ or صُوفِي شُيُوخٌ who do exactly that.

That said, having a livelihood is a different matter entirely. *خَيْرُ مَا أَكَلَ الرَّجُلُ مِنْ كَسْبِ يَدِهِ* — the best thing a man can earn from is the work of his own hands. The عُلَمَاء have taken from the hadith on teaching the Quran that it is permissible to earn from teaching. And even though the early سَلَفٌ were generally opposed to taking payment for teaching, the later scholars recognized that people must have a livelihood and that the world has become more complicated. That is not the issue. The issue is amassing and accumulating wealth — using religion as a vehicle for that. Imam Malik, رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ, was given enormous amounts of wealth because of who he was, but what he did was spend it all out. Murabit al-Hajj is a fine example of this: money comes from all over, and it all goes straight back out. You can see it — a few cows, a tent, an adobe house, a few robes, some books, and constant giving. People give to righteous people because they trust them, and practicing Muslims are generous, so they want to give. The question is not receiving — the question is hoarding, accumulating beyond one's needs, refusing to let it flow out. When your needs are taken care of and you begin to hoard, that is where the problem lies.

You have to be very careful, and it has been a problem for a long time. The scholars complained about it centuries ago. One scholar — and this man himself was a genuine شَيْخٌ, a مُرَبٍّ, a person of real تَرْبِيَة and outward knowledge both — said that in his time, the ninth century, most people who called themselves صُوفِي شُيُوخٌ were complete charlatans. So it is an old problem. And that is a condition, by the way: anyone who does تَرْبِيَة must have outward knowledge. They cannot be without it.

Unfortunately, in the Muslim world today there is a profound split between تَصَوُّف and exoteric Islam — between the inward and outward dimensions of the tradition — and they have become two camps pitched against each other. Nowhere is this more visible than on the Arabian Peninsula, where you have the Najdis and the Hijazis: most of the Najdis are staunchly anti-صُوفِي, and many of the Hijazis are enthusiastically pro-مَوْلِد and pro-تَصَوُّف, and they are at loggerheads, with real hostility between them. We are out of balance as an أُمَّة, and the truth lies somewhere between the two positions. Make دُعَاء for them, and ask to benefit from their respective knowledges.

Ahmad Baba al-Timbukti — the great scholar from Timbuktu who was taken as a slave to Morocco — when he arrived in Marrakech, he was so much more learned than any of the scholars there that they quickly realized they had made a grave mistake. He mentions in his book — Dibaj al-Mudhahhab, his خَشْيَة on Ibn Farhun's طَبَقَات — visiting several of the graves of the righteous in Marrakech and asking Allah at one of them to grant him understanding of two books he was working on. He says that by Allah, he returned to those books and could read them without any difficulty. He was a great scholar, and that was the traditional practice: to ask for the blessing of their knowledges, since most of those buried in such places were scholars, and to ask *رَحِمَهُ اللهُ* — that Allah give them light in their graves and bless them.

Qadi Abu Bakr ibn al-'Arabi — whose grave is just outside of Fez — gave a fatwa permitting the visiting of the graves of the صَالِحِينَ, as did Ibn al-Hajj. For those who say it is بِدْعَة: that is simply not consistent with the position of the vast majority of our scholars, who have not only permitted it but recommended it. And these are outward scholars, jurists — well known in the tradition. Qadi Abu Bakr ibn al-'Arabi's grave is well known outside of Fez; he was poisoned by his enemies and died a martyr there, having also fought jihad multiple times. He wrote two commentaries on the Qur'an. He brought the إِحْيَاءُ عُلُومِ الدِّينِ from Imam al-Ghazali back to Morocco and Andalusia — he had studied it with al-Ghazali directly and transmitted it west. He is a great scholar, and anyone who has visited that place knows there is a great deal of بَرَكَةٌ there, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ.

But the living — if you have the opportunity to visit them, do so. And you should have high هِمَّة: desire to be one of them, so that our أُمَّة has people worth visiting. We need these people. Make the intention to be one yourself, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, and ask Allah to give you تَوْفِيق — and give all of us تَوْفِيق for that. One of the poets said: *إِذَا الْمَرْءُ لَمْ يَرْتَدِ الْعُلَا* — if a man does not reach the heights, at least he asبِرّes to them. *وَمَنْ قَنِعَ بِالدُّونِ كَانَ دَنِيًّا* — whoever is content with less than heights is himself less than heights. The greatest asبِرّation is to be close to Allah. Have high هِمَّة. And Sidi Ibn 'Ajiba said: if you do not believe that Allah can take you in this very moment and make you one of His أَوْلِيَاء, then you know nothing of His قُدْرَة. Never despair. Asبِرّe to be from the people of وِلَايَة, the people who are close to Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى.

So زِيَارَة is important. When you visit these people — those I have visited from the people of this path — you will notice that they do not engage in idle chatter. You do not go for a social call. You go for the benefit of learning something, and for the benefit of their دُعَاء. Ask them for their دُعَاء, because it may well be accepted, and if it is, you receive the blessing of it. Visit them, ask for their دُعَاء, and show humility in their presence.

I will share a story about Mulla Ramadan, who was from the صَالِحِينَ and was the father of Shaykh Sa'id Ramadan al-Buti. Shaykh Sa'id wrote a book called *هٰذَا وَالِدِي* — "This Is My Father" — describing what a great man his father was. I once asked Dr. Bukar — a Libyan scholar, I believe he is in New Jersey now — about Mulla Ramadan, because it is always strengthening to the إِيمَان to hear stories of these people. He told me — سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ — that the most remarkable thing about visiting that man was this: he said he had always found the practice of kissing hands rather unnecessary, something he had never been comfortable with. He said he went in, Mulla Ramadan extended his hand, and he felt a kind of pressure on his head — and found himself going down and kissing his hand. When he looked back, there was nothing there. The fourth practice is تِلَاوَةُ الْقُرْآنِ — recitation of the Qur'an, preferably with تَدَبُّر, which means reflection and pondering the meanings rather than merely reading the words. Reciting with تَدَبُّر awakens the heart; it is one of the things that genuinely rouses it from slumber. Even reciting without deep reflection carries its own benefit. You should have a daily portion of Qur'an — ideally at least a حِزْبٌ or a جَزْء each day. If that is not possible, then at minimum recite the surahs the Messenger of Allah ﷺ recommended: يَاسِين, generally after فَجْر; and the surahs after مَغْرِب and الْمُلْك after إِحْسَاء.

For those who have not yet learned Arabic, reading the meanings in English is something, but it is limited — and this is the honest truth — because part of what the Qur'an does operates through the actual sounds of the language. Think of it like medicine. From an energetic perspective, every substance in the world resonates at a specific wavelength. Diseases resonate, and a medicine that cures a disease resonates in a way that counteracts it. The recitation of the Qur'an works through the sounds themselves, the sounds that emanate from you as you speak. We now know from material science that sound waves resonate through every cell in the body. When you recite the Qur'an, you are producing divine words that Allah revealed — and those words carry a sound.

I was once with a man at a gas station. He was asking about Islam. A Muslim mechanic was there, and I was present wearing a robe, so he began asking me about it. He mentioned that he had read the Qur'an. I said, "You have never read the Qur'an unless you read it in Arabic." He said he couldn't believe that. I said it was a fact. He asked why, and I said, "Just listen." Then I recited the opening of Surat al-Rahman to him — and وَاللَّهِ, by the One besides whom there is no god — when I finished, he simply said, "I understand now." He was not joking. Non-Arab people, عَجَمِي people, are often stunned when they hear the Qur'an, even if they are not Muslim. They are amazed by the sounds.

You have to recognize that the Qur'an is not only its meanings — it is also the sounds, which transcend and participate in the meaning in a realm we do not fully comprehend, because everything in reality is meaning. Allah has taken meaning all the way down to the phonemes of the Qur'an. In modern linguistics, phonemes are considered meaningless in isolation; they acquire meaning only when joined into polysyllabic forms. Someone once wrote a nasty critique of Dr. Hamid Algar, complaining that his speech was filled with polysyllabic sentences. Dr. Algar pointed out in his rebuttal that a sentence that is not polysyllabic is, by definition, meaningless. And yet even individual phonemes carry meaning in Arabic. We may not know what that meaning is, but the meaning goes all the way down. Those are the four practices he identifies.

Now, Allah warns the Prophet ﷺ about sitting with those whose hearts He has placed in a state of heedlessness. Allah clarifies elsewhere in the Qur'an that when people deviated, He caused their deviation to increase. Those who turn away from the truth, Allah places in a state of غَفْلَة. غَفْلَة, then, is itself a punishment. A Muslim who is in غَفْلَة is experiencing a consequence of his own disobedience toward Allah ﷻ. It is a serious condition — you do not want to be among the غَافِلِينَ.

And yet, in all honesty, all of us will move in and out of غَفْلَة. That is simply the nature of existence in this world. غَفْلَة ends the moment you die. What matters is that you spend more of your life in a state of wakefulness than in a state of heedlessness. You want to be someone who is not in غَفْلَة.

This is why سَيِّدِنَا Umar said that the best remembrance of Allah is when you reach a limit He has set. In other words, when you are on the verge of committing a sin — none of you drink wine, but imagine you were about to — and in that moment you remember Allah and think, "I am about to put fire into my stomach," and that remembrance becomes a زَجْرٌ, a مَنِيع, a deterrent that prevents you from the حَرَامٌ — that is the best ذِكْرٌ. When the desire for disobedience rises and the remembrance of Allah holds you back: that is what he means. So there are degrees of غَفْلَة, and what he is saying is that the disease is the غَفْلَة that prevents you from fulfilling what Allah has commanded and avoiding what He has prohibited. Anything beyond that, remembering Allah is مُسْتَحَبّ.

Allah has prescribed remembrance at certain fixed times of day. He did not make remembrance throughout the entire day an obligation, because not everyone is capable of that. He said: remember Me at appointed times, أَوْقَاتٌ مُعَيَّنَةٌ. But He also encourages us to make much remembrance of Allah — and that is نَفْل, recommended, not obligatory. The more you remember Allah, the less likely you are to fall into the غَفْلَة you do not want. And again, the disease of the heart is specifically the غَفْلَة that prevents you from fulfilling your obligations or refraining from what has been prohibited.

As for the opposite — those in whose hearts Allah has written إِيمَان — one of the meanings here relates to writing itself: to inscribe without diacritical marks or voweling signs. That is one dimension of the meaning. The إِيمَان that Allah writes into the heart is of that nature.
