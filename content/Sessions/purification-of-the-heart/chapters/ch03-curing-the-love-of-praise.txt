He now lists what must be removed from the heart in order to treat and purify it from رِيَاءٌ. There are four things: love of praise, fear of blame, desire for worldly benefit from people, and fear of harm from people.

The first means of accomplishing this is having يَقِين — certainty — that only اللَّه can benefit or harm you. This begins at the level of عَقِيدَة, because it is a premise built into the belief system we are commanded to hold. In the Islamic understanدِينٌg of reality, there is no harm and no benefit except from اللَّه — النَّافِعُ and الضَّرُّ are two of His names. Once a person genuinely believes this, he recognizes that no one else can truly benefit or harm him. It is an illusion. And once that illusion is seen for what it is, the craving for praise and the dread of blame begin to lose their grip. What you ultimately want is for اللَّه to be pleased with you, not people, because the approval of people — divorced from the approval of اللَّه — is simply a waste of time. If the whole world is praising you and اللَّه is not pleased with you, that is a disaster, and the disaster becomes fully visible at the moment of death, which is when the reality of this life finally comes into focus.

Do not be deluded by praise and blame. Muhammad Ali, to take one example, was once the most praised man in the world. And then the time came when he was no longer the man who could defeat others in the ring, and the calls stopped coming. That is the nature of دُنْيَا. Praise and blame follow worldly position; when you lose the position, the praise evaporates. Some of the عُلَمَاء noted that one of the clearest proofs of this is how quickly people find it difficult to maintain friendships when they have nothing, and how suddenly everyone wants to be your friend the moment things start going well. That is simply the way of the world.

This is part of why, in the traditional Muslim world — unlike today's wealth-oriented culture — knowledge was what people sought to be associated with, not wealth. Scholars achieved extraorدِينٌary stanدِينٌg simply on account of their learning. That is largely no longer the case, but it was genuinely true.

I was reaدِينٌg Helen Keller's autobiography recently. It is very short and remarkably powerful — I would genuinely recommend it. She was deaf, blind, and unable to speak, and the book traces the awakening of her consciousness. One thing she wrote struck me as particularly beautiful. She became deaf, blind, and mute after a bout of scarlet fever in childhood — she was not born that way. She lost her sight and hearing at nineteen months old, yet she said that even that brief glimpse of the world — having once seen a tree, just once, before the age of two — was enough. When she later felt a tree with her hands, the memory of having seen one was still there, and she said that single blessing was sufficient for her. The power of the human eye is just extraorدِينٌary.

She also wrote something that has stayed with me: there is no slave in this world who does not have a king somewhere in his ancestry, and there is no king who does not have a slave somewhere in his. The vicissitudes of life. Everyone's lineage, somewhere down the line, probably passes through both a throne and a servant's quarters. And that is دُنْيَا — nothing in it is permanent.

When you read the history of the Arabs, the rise and fall of rulers is astonishing. Take al-Mansur of Andalusia. He was not a caliph but a minister — a mediocre student at the مَدْرَسَة, by some accounts, but extraorدِينٌarily ambitious. He worked his way into the palace, cultivated the favor of one of the powerful women there — a mother of the caliph — and through her patronage was elevated to positions of authority. There was one minister before him who was the most feared man in the court, one of the wealthiest people in Cordoba. Al-Mansur eventually had him imprisoned, and that man ended his life as a pauper, dying in rags in his cell. Imagine the arc of that life. That is the nature of دُنْيَا.

<!-- page 50 -->

So: firm certainty that no harm and no benefit comes except from اللَّه, and that if the entire creation wished to harm you, they could not do so without His will. If someone believes that with genuine conviction, رِيَاءٌ ceases to arise in them — because رِيَاءٌ exists precisely to obtain some worldly benefit, and when you know that worldly benefit flows only from اللَّه, the performance for others loses its purpose.

In the traditional Muslim world, because religious learning and piety were so honored, there was a particular danger of showing off in order to gain stanدِينٌg among people. That danger has not disappeared — Muslims still respect religious comportment, so the temptation remains.

He now gives his definition: رِيَاءٌ means performing an act of devotion for other than the Creator's sake, in reality seeking worldly benefit, or praise from creation, or protection from its opposite — harm. Some people do it purely to hear how wonderful they are, and the praise itself is what they are after.

He then goes on to discuss رِيَاسٌ various forms. The worst of them is displaying religiosity in order to be entrusted with an orphan's wealth — and then misusing that trust. This is religious hypocrisy deployed as an instrument of outright fraud, using the appearance of piety to steal what belongs to the most vulnerable. That is a particularly grave form of it.

I once met a Moroccan man who had a beautiful voice and could have been paid to sing praises of the Prophet ﷺ at wedدِينٌgs and gatherings — something that does happen in Morocco. But he refused to do it for money. He said: اللَّه has made countless ways for a person to earn provision in this دُنْيَا. Do not seek it through something that was created to draw you near to Him.

<!-- page 51 -->

There are indeed countless ways to earn a living, especially in a place like America — my father used to say he realized this early on, in the early 1960s, when he sent in a dollar for a barbecue starter kit and received back a match and a small piece of flint. People find every conceivable means. There are the psychic hotlines, for instance — I have never called one, thank God, but people do. The caller gets on the line and hears: "You're looking for a better job." Well, of course — everyone is looking for a better job. "You're looking for a better relationship." They also know that anyone calling them is someone whose life is not working out, so they know precisely what to play on. It is all a performance.

The next category he discusses, after the other-worldly misuse just described, is the worldly misuse: a religious endowment — a وَقْف, or funds collected for the sake of اللَّه — that is then redirected for worldly purposes. The money was gathered in اللَّه's name, and the people entrusted with it use it for themselves. That is another of the graver forms of رِيَاءٌ.

That is the worst type of رِيَاء — to display religiosity and make people think you are trustworthy, so they put you on the masjid board, make you treasurer, and then you misuse the funds. You read about people who deceive the elderly and the vulnerable, and it is stunning what human beings will do. There are degrees of despicable behavior — not all wrongdoing is equally despicable — and this ranks among the worst.

He then says that the least form of رِيَاء is what is done out of fear of others' scorn — you act a certain way simply because you do not want people to think badly of you. That is the mildest form of it.

<!-- page 46 -->

The cure, he says, lies in knowing that if all of creation joined forces to oppose you or support you, they could not do so without اللَّه's permission. اللَّه alone holds the reward for your actions in both abodes — this world and the next. The disease is also cured by maintaining awareness of the harm رِيَاء causes, which leads to a genuine hatred of it and allows it to pass. That is the intellectual treatment.

The practical treatment is accomplished by veiling one's actions from the eyes of others and by reciting سُورَةُ الْإِخْلَاصِ frequently — along with the دُعَاء of اِسْتِخَارَة. This is a vitally important treatment, because this disease is serious and we all want to be free of it.

Veiling your actions does not mean you should never act in front of people. It means that out of fear of رِيَاء, when you are able to act privately, that is preferable. If you are going to donate money, it is better not to do it publicly — though the عُلَمَاء say that if your genuine intention is to encourage others, it is permissible. The key is to look into your own heart and determine your real motive. If you want to be seen as the one giving the large donation, then give secretly. If you truly feel no such pull and genuinely wish to inspire others, then it is allowed. You must watch and guard your own heart. This is a very subtle matter.

Avoiدِينٌg public displays extends to night prayers, recitation of القُرْآن, and similar acts of worship — doing them where no one will see you. The عُلَمَاء do note, however, that for scholars it is sometimes better to perform such acts visibly, to encourage others. But people of knowledge are equally susceptible to this disease — often more so, given the nature of their position.

<!-- page 47 -->

The other practice is سُورَةُ الْإِخْلَاصِ. The word إِخْلَاص comes from خَلُصَ, meaning to be pure. It relates to the image of pure honey — خَلَاصٌ — or pure milk, fresh from the udder, uncontaminated by anything. That is what الدِّينُ الْخَالِصُ لِلَّهِ means: worship of اللَّه untainted by رِيَاء, by hidden شِرْك, by any motive other than اللَّه. The مُخْلِصِينَ are the people who attain this.

In the القُرْآن, two related words appear: مُخْلِص and مُخْلَص. The first is the active participle — one who makes himself sincere. The second is the passive — one who has been made sincere by اللَّه. The passive form points to something essential: تَوْفِيق from اللَّه is required. Your own sincerity is not sufficient on its own; اللَّه's enablement must accompany it. That is the secret of success. This is why Abu al-Hasan al-Shadhili said: "اللَّهُمَّ اجْعَلْ سَيِّئَاتِي سَيِّئَاتِ الْمَحْبُوبِ إِلَيْكَ، وَلَا تَجْعَلْ حَسَنَاتِي حَسَنَاتِ الْمَرْدُودِ عَلَيْكَ" — O اللَّه, make my bad actions the bad actions of those You love, and do not make my good actions the good actions of those You reject. In other words, تَوْفِيق from اللَّه must be present.

اللَّه promises this تَوْفِيق when you fulfill certain conditions, so the two go together. This is why being مُخْلَص but being a مُفْتَدِيكَ means you are not truly مُخْلَص. A Christian can be absolutely sincere — genuinely so. A Buddhist, a Jew, a person in any tradition can possess sincerity. But that person is not مُخْلَص. Why? Because the action is not in accordance with what اللَّه has prescribed. The مُخْلَص who is also مُخْلِص is one whose action is sincerely for اللَّه alone and simultaneously aligned with what اللَّه loves — not what اللَّه has prohibited. That distinction is critical.

<!-- page 48 -->

اللَّه has commanded us and given us a شَرِيعَة to follow. If we are not in accordance with it, تَوْفِيق does not come — no matter how sincere we are. And I understand this is a difficult point for many Christians, whose tradition tends to say the law is secondary and the spirit is what matters. We say both are essential. The تَوْفِيق comes from following both the letter and the spirit of the law together.

You can be following the law outwardly — praying five times a day as commanded, paying زَكَاةٌ, performing حَجٌّ — and yet be doing all of it for other than اللَّه. That is رِيَاء, and there is no تَوْفِيق in it. Conversely, you can be inwardly sincere, acting for God, doing charitable works in secret — yet not be acting in accordance with what اللَّه has prescribed. So you must have both: the spirit of the law and the letter of the law. Our دِينٌ is the middle way. The tendency of one tradition has been to focus on the letter and neglect the spirit; another has tended toward the spirit and neglected the letter. Both are incomplete. The active form, مُخْلِص, is what comes from you. The passive form, مُخْلَص, is what اللَّه does in you.

Reciting سُورَةُ الْإِخْلَاصِ is therefore one of the core practices for purifying from رِيَاء. The word إِخْلَاص also derives from إِخْلَاص or إِخْلَاصًا, meaning to render something pure. The صُرَّةٌ is literally the صُرَّةٌ that makes you pure — it purifies through its very recitation. مُرَابِطُ الْحَاجِّ, may اللَّه preserve him, prescribes سُورَةُ الْإِخْلَاصِ to most people who come to him with diseases of the heart, incluدِينٌg وَسْوَسَة — obsessive intrusive thought. Do not underestimate this prescription. It is a real cure.

You should recite it at minimum three times in the morning and three times in the evening — that is the least. Since it equals one-third of the القُرْآن in weight and virtue, it is a tremendous صُرَّةٌ with a special stanدِينٌg.

<!-- page 49 -->

The second practice for treating رِيَاء is the دُعَاء of سَيِّدُ الِاسْتِغْفَارِ. It comes from a حَدِيث narrated on the authority of Buraydah, may اللَّه be pleased with him, recorded by Imam Ahmad, Abu Dawud, Ibn Mahdi, Ibn Khuzayma, and al-Hakim — its appearance in so many collections signals its importance. The Prophet ﷺ said that whoever recites it in the morning and in the evening, and then dies that day or that evening, will enter جَنَّة. You should recite it three times in the morning and three times in the evening, and ideally after every prayer — even in سَجْدَة — as a regular دُعَاء:

*اللَّهُمَّ أَنْتَ رَبِّي، لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا أَنْتَ، خَلَقْتَنِي وَأَنَا عَبْدُكَ، وَأَنَا عَلَى عَهْدِكَ وَوَعْدِكَ مَا اسْتَطَعْتُ*

This is a genuine cure. Try it on yourself — this is a science, not conjecture. Do what is being prescribed and you will see results. Recite the دُعَاء every evening. Recite سُورَةُ الْإِخْلَاصِ. Do it with the intention of إِخْلَاص — يَا اللهُ, grant me إِخْلَاص. Say it to اللَّه alone in the night. If you do it with sincerity, He will respond. But if you move through it casually — ticking boxes and moving on to the next disease — the effect will not come. This is serious. This is our lives.

<!-- page 50 -->

I read Imam al-Ghazali's signs of the insincere preacher versus the sincere preacher, and I recognized far more of myself in the insincere column. It is unnerving. When you read this material, you start to see yourself in it — and you should be unsettled by that, because we will be held to account for all of it. This is what life is about. Either we grasp the point or we do not. But the guidance has been given to us; we are being told.

There is another important دُعَاء not mentioned in the text here, but cited in the نَصِيحَة. It is from the Prophet ﷺ, so it must be great. Recite it three times in the morning and three times in the evening:

*اللَّهُمَّ إِنِّي أَعُوذُ بِكَ أَنْ أُشْرِكَ بِكَ شَيْئًا وَأَنَا أَعْلَمُ، وَأَسْتَغْفِرُكَ لِمَا لَا أَعْلَمُ*

This is a دُعَاء of اِسْتِعَاذَةٌ مِنَ الرِّيَاءِ — seeking refuge from hidden شِرْك — and the Prophet ﷺ himself recited it. The القُرْآن is a شِفَاءٌ for what is in the hearts. It is a healing, as اللَّه states: it cures the hearts of the believers. And what the Prophet gave you, take it — so this دُعَاء is a sound daily practice.

He then adds: as for the chronically diseased heart that has long been given to showing off through good works, even it will find a cure in this — and what a cure. He is telling you this is a serious and effective treatment. Believe it, practice it, and إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ you will see the results.

<!-- page 51 -->

He then turns to the question of hypocrisy. Here, رِيَاء is closer to a type of hypocrisy that involves veiling one's wrong actions — concealing one's faults. This kind of concealment is, accorدِينٌg to ابْنُ زُكْرِي, actually وَاجِب. If someone asks you whether you ever drank alcohol, and you have made sincere تَوْبَة, you say no. If someone asks whether you ever stole, and you have repented, you say no. That is not رِيَاء; it is an obligation.

My note here is that concealment does become رِيَاء when the person is hiدِينٌg the misdeed because they want others to think better of them than they really are — that motive makes it رِيَاء. But the obligation to conceal one's wrongdoing exists for a different reason entirely: so that misdeeds are not taken lightly by the community. If I ask you whether you have ever drunk alcohol and you freely say yes, I unconsciously think: well, I am not so bad then. The admission softens the enormity. The word كَبِيرَة means enormity — and enormities should remain enormous in the communal consciousness. You do not want society to become comfortable with them.

I would add that when people freely admit their transgressions, it leads to complacency and the erosion of shame. Although there is an apparent hypocrisy in concealment, it is in reality a kind of homage that vice pays to virtue — and that homage is what allows virtue to function as a deterrent in the social arena. In a healthy society, virtue is the norm — the مَعْرُوف — and vice is the مُنْكَرٌ. In an unhealthy society, the reverse is true. When virtue is still the norm, vice must pay tribute to it by hiدِينٌg. That is not hypocrisy; it is a form of respect for virtue, and the شَرِيعَة honors it.

<!-- page 52 -->

Freud argued the opposite: that all of this is mere hypocrisy, that we are all seething with suppressed desires, and that letting it all out is the path to health. The consequences of that idea are now visible. There is no longer shame — none. Vice no longer hides itself or pays homage to virtue. And the argument has even been made that the real problem is not the act itself but the lie about it — as if concealment of wrongdoing were the greater offense. That inversion is a sign of how far things have gone.

It was not always this way. In the 1960s, in most American cities, if an unmarried young woman became pregnant, she quietly disappeared from the community. Several months later she would reappear, and people would say she had gone to stay with relatives. What actually happened was that her family sent her away to give birth, the baby was placed for adoption, and she returned with the community none the wiser — because there was shame. That is how recently this was true. In 1968, a woman was expelled from Radcliffe for living in the same apartment as a man. Radcliffe was considered a relatively progressive institution at the time, and even there, that boundary held. Now there are coeducational dormitories and no one thinks twice about it.

So how did we get from there to a shrug — a collective "who cares?" Islam says no: it is actually better to maintain that sense of shame, because what it really represents is an honoring of virtue. That is the essential point.

The hypocrisy itself is seen as less harmful than what happens when everything is treated as acceptable, when nothing is a big deal. That permissiveness is the true danger, not the hypocrisy. And this, again, reflects the principle in شَرِيعَة of choosing the lesser of two evils. Both options involve something wrong — no one wants to be a hypocrite, no one wants to deny something they actually did. But the شَرِيعَة holds that the greater evil lies in open admission, because it diminishes the weight of the action in the eyes of others. People begin to say, "Oh, he does it too," or "Well, maybe it's not that serious." Rather than that, the instinct is to recoil — "Of course not, I would never do that" — because the act genuinely is serious. This is how Muslims have understood the world. There was shame. The person who denies isn't trying to look virtuous for their own sake; they are refusing to let others reduce their fear and revulsion toward the wrong action. That is what matters.

Even now, Imam Junaid — and I find this a genuinely healthy view — said: if you commit a wrong action and then make تَوْبَة, let it go. Do not dwell on it. Maintain a fear of returning to it, yes, but do not dwell on it, because dwelling produces guilt, a kind of despair, and self-loathing. None of those are healthy responses. If you say "أَسْتَغْفِرُ اللَّهَ," make a sincere تَوْبَة, resolve not to return, and then leave it behind as though it never happened — that is the right approach.

Now, to address the broader picture: if someone is caught up in a major sin — رِبَا, for instance — that is not كُفْر. It is a grave enormity, and it must not be taken lightly. But it does not remove a person from the fold of Islam. If you have gotten yourself into that situation, and it is not easy to get out, you must make تَوْبَة and take whatever steps are necessary to extricate yourself. Begin that process, make the تَوْبَة, and work your way out. Once you are out, you perform what is required. And تَوْبَة must be sincere — the signs of sincerity are genuine remorse, immediate cessation of the act, and a firm intention never to return. If you do that, then that is a valid تَوْبَة.

And when you make تَوْبَة, you should have a good opinion of اللَّه. To say "I am so horrible, I am so wretched, اللَّه will never forgive me" — no. اللَّه tells us not to think that way. That kind of despair is, frankly, a Christian disease. Many Christians fall into it. Though to be fair, Christianity is complex on this point, because alongside that tendency toward despair there exists the opposite extreme — particularly in Baptist Christianity, where a person can do the most outrageous things and be entirely convinced they are safe. That's why the phenomenon of the fallen Baptist minister — the Jimmy Swaggart syndrome — doesn't actually shock Baptist congregations. The attitude is: the devil got you that time, you'll get him the next. That's not تَوْبَة.

In Islam, تَوْبَة is real. And when a non-Muslim accepts Islam, that is itself the تَوْبَة of a non-Muslim from his كُفْر — and by the consensus of the أُمَّهْ, that تَوْبَة is accepted. When a person enters Islam, all their previous wrong actions are forgiven, by إِجْمَاعٌ. It is as though they were just born. Whereas the تَوْبَة of a Muslim is a matter of خِلَافٌ: if اللَّه wills, He accepts it; if He does not, He may decline. But you should hope that He accepts it, because we have رَجَاء — hope. Do not be absolutely certain either way; the uncertainty keeps the fear alive and guards against returning to the sin.

Someone asked: "I have heard people say that when you take شَهَادَة, all your bad deeds become good deeds — is that correct?"

No, but your good deeds do come with you into Islam. Your bad deeds are wiped out; your good deeds remain. So you lose nothing and only gain. That is a really important point.

On the question of اللَّه and evil: we do not say that اللَّه brings evil. We say that within the decree of اللَّه, good and evil both exist — اللَّه has measured them out, and they exist in the world. But the Prophet ﷺ said in his دُعَاء: "All good comes from You, and evil does not return to You." So while اللَّه has created a world in which evil is permitted to exist, we do not attribute evil to اللَّه, even though we believe it is اللَّه who has measured it out.

Evil, interestingly, can only exist where there is moral agency — and that means human beings. It cannot exist in the animal kingdom. If a lion kills a baby, that is not an evil act; the lion has no moral obligation, no compunction. You cannot call the lion evil. You cannot call a cobra evil for biting someone — it is a snake, doing what it was created to do. But if a human being takes a gun, robs someone of their hard-earned money, and then shoots them — the robbery is evil, and the shooting on top of it is a heinous evil.

What we say is that اللَّه created the human being, gave him the capacity to choose, and then measured out what he would choose. اللَّه طَهَارَةُ الْقَلْبِ has decreed that at that moment, that person would make that choice. The person is fully responsible for it. اللَّه permitted it — empowered the person to carry it out through His قُدْرَة — and it was اللَّه's مَشِيئَة, His will and providence, that allowed that person to will what he willed.

وَمَا تَشَآءُونَ إِلَّآ أَن يَشَآءَ ٱللَّهُ

You do not will anything except that اللَّه wills it at the same time. If I intend to go and harm someone and begin walking toward them, my مَشِيئَة has started. But if اللَّه does not will harm to come to that person — say the person suffers a heart attack and dies before I arrive — then I willed something that اللَّه did not will. So anything you actually carry out can only be carried out if اللَّه wills it as well.

And this is the distinction: when what you will is what اللَّه loves, that is تَوْفِيق. When what you will is what اللَّه hates, that is خِذْلَانٌ. اللَّه is the مُوَفِّق and the خَاذِل. But you are ultimately the one performing the evil. We do not attribute the evil to اللَّه, yet we acknowledge that اللَّه has measured it out in the world. It is part of His قَدَرٌ. اللَّه has permitted evil to exist — it is part of His creation. And we are fully responsible for the consequences of our actions, whether good or evil.

Ultimately, had اللَّه not willed it, no evil could exist. But it does not return to Him — we do not attribute it to Him.

Someone asked: "How do you know, in the moment before an action, whether what you will aligns with اللَّه's will?"

If it accords with شَرِيعَة, there is تَوْفِيق. If it does not accord with شَرِيعَة, there is no تَوْفِيق. If what you are doing is done with إِخْلَاص — sincerity — and in accordance with اللَّه's laws, then there is تَوْفِيق. That is what مُوَفَّق means: a person whose will is aligned with what اللَّه loves for them to do.

You should never engage in anything without first knowing the حُكْم of اللَّه regarدِينٌg it. It is impermissible to act without knowing what اللَّه has decreed about that action. And, thankfully, the vast majority of things are permissible; only a small category is حَرَامٌ, and those can be learned. It is not that complex.

اللَّه did not make the دِينٌ difficult. The دِينٌ is not hard. The problem is that human beings simply resist certain things. تَقْوَى is straightforward — it has four elements: doing what اللَّه commanded, inwardly and outwardly; inwardly with إِخْلَاص, outwardly in accordance with His laws. And not doing what He prohibited, inwardly and outwardly — refraining not out of fear that someone might see you walking into a bar, but out of fear of اللَّه. That is the inward dimension. And outwardly, not doing it whether you are alone or in company. That is all تَقْوَى is.

And if you have تَقْوَى, اللَّه promises you every good in this world: He promises you مَخْرَج — a way out. He promises to rectify your children. He promises to forgive your wrong actions. He promises to open the gates of heaven and send rain and bless your agriculture. He promises you a good life — حَيَاةً طَيِّبَةً. Those are the worldly promises. And then the otherworldly ones: He promises to forgive you, to enter you into paradise, to place you in the company of the نَبِيِّينَ — the prophets — the صِدِّيقِينَ, the righteous, the شُهَدَاء, the martyrs, and the صَالِحِينَ. What a deal. What a bargain.

On the matter of إِخْرَاجٌ — publicly exposing someone's wrong actions — the Arabs use that word to describe putting someone in an embarrassing position, particularly regarدِينٌg sins. It is not a good thing to do. Again, this is a matter of مُرُوءَة, of personal dignity and honor. And the Prophet ﷺ said: "From the beauty of a person's Islam is that he leaves what does not concern him." What happens between another person and اللَّه is no concern of yours. And it is better not to know, because knowing often leads to thinking ill of others.

Someone asked about disclosing one's age — the Prophet ﷺ was reportedly asked about his age and responded: "Is it any concern of yours?" He explained that if you tell someone your age, either they say, "Oh, I thought you were older" — and they diminish you — or they say, "I thought you were younger" — and you still can't win.

But that ruling is different when there is a legitimate concern, such as marriage. Age is a valid consideration in marriage. It is entirely reasonable for a man to be concerned about a woman's age, and vice versa, because age bears directly on marriage and its circumstances. That is why it is impermissible, for instance, to dye gray hairs and present yourself to a prospective spouse as a young man, and then have her discover the truth once married. That is a serious problem — a form of deception.

In traditional cultures, people generally knew good families from bad families — it was common knowledge. A good family meant people who were not engaged in wrongdoing. Unfortunately, that is no longer reliably true. Good families, bad families — the lines have blurred. There are many people who have committed زِينَة, consumed alcohol, taken drugs. And the stakes are especially high now because of diseases and other serious consequences tied to these behaviors.

I think, therefore, there is a valid concern. In the context of marriage and زِينَة specifically — people do have a right to know what they are entering into. Now, the Quranic verse stating that a fornicator can only marry a fornicator or a polytheist applies to the person's condition before تَوْبَة. The بَرِيَّة was a woman who sold herself openly in the marketplace; it was prohibited to marry someone in that active condition. But if they made تَوْبَة, there is no prohibition. Still, given the situation we are in today, I think people have a legitimate right to know. Does that make sense?
