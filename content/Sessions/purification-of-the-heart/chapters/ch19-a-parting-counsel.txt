And understand that an عَمِّي — a common person — in Arabic does not mean someone who is ignorant of the sacred sciences. There are scholars who consider themselves above the عَوَامّ because they are learned, yet they are still عَوَامّ. The عَمِّي can be the street sweeper who knows only a few سُوَرٌ but whose heart is entirely with اللهُ. That is why the saying goes: how many people are not fasting yet with اللهُ they are fasting, and how many people are fasting yet with اللهُ they are not fasting? There are people throughout the year who guard their eyes, their ears, their tongues, their feet, their genitals, and their stomachs — in reality they are living the purpose of fasting, because fasting is an exercise designed to teach us how to do that properly. Others fast physically but with اللهُ they are not fasting. That is the point. So when he speaks of the خُشُوعٌ, do not imagine that means someone who has mastered Imam Sibawayhi's Al-Kitab or memorized all the تَفَاسِير. These people can be nearly illiterate — if the heart is with اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى, that is what counts.

Consider Imam Sahnun — Muhammad ibn Sahnun, son of the Sahnun who wrote the مُدَوَّنَة. He had a مَجْلِس in الْقَيْرَوَان. Every day during his teaching sessions, a man would come in, walk over people's shoulders to reach the sheikh who was seated in a chair, and whisper in his ear for a while — then leave. This happened every time the sheikh taught. The sheikh would actually ask people to move aside so the man would not have to step over them. One day the man stopped coming. Imam Muhammad ibn Sahnun asked his students about him, sent someone to find him, and the man came. The sheikh asked: what happened, you stopped visiting me? The man said: سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ — I have to explain myself. People who envy you came to me — I am a very poor man with many daughters I need to marry off. They said: go and disturb him every day in his مَجْلِس, and if you can make him angry and humiliate him in front of his students, we will give you money to marry your daughters. So every day I would come and I would curse you in your ear and say the most horrible things. And I realized it was having no effect on you whatsoever. So I gave up. Muhammad ibn Sahnun said: why didn't you simply come and ask me for money? I would have given it to you.

That is the صَبْرٌ you want to learn. That is the صَبْرٌ of the people of the Messenger of اللهُ, the صَبْرٌ of his family. And Salman is from our family — Salman الْفَارِسِي. He was patient from the very beginning of his search. He was a Zoroastrian, and he used to see the sacred fire go out and be quietly relit, while the worshippers were told it never goes out. His father was in charge of tending it, so Salman knew it was a lie. He set out to find the truth. He went to the Christians, served a monk for years, and witnessed that monk stealing the people's charity and burying it in his backyard. He then went to another monk who was genuinely devout, and that monk told him: there is a prophet who will appear in the land of Arabia in your time.

In terms of this month, here is my recommendation to myself first and foremost, and to all of you. Don't do a lot of unnecessary talking. Utilize the time. Every Muslim should be conscious that this month is unlike any other — don't waste it. The سَلَفْ used to spend six months before رَمَضَان begging اللهُ to let them reach this month. This is not an ordinary time. And none of us knows whether this is our last رَمَضَان; none of us even knows whether we will make it to the end of this رَمَضَان. So seize the moment. Utilize this time. Don't let the month slip by and find yourself in the last few days full of regret about what you didn't do. We will all feel that to some degree regardless — human beings are such that we never do anything without realizing afterward we could have done it better. But hopefully the regret will be mild because we did a great deal, not because we did very little.

Utilize the night. The خَتْم of the Quran in the تَرَاوِيح is the tradition, but even praying one صُرَّةٌ is effective. Even if you prayed your تَرَاوِيح with قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ in every رَكْعَة — if that is all you know — and you completed your رَكَعَاتٌ, that counts as قِيَامٌ. So do قِيَامٌ every night, even if it is twenty رَكَعَاتٌ with قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ. Do قِيَامٌ at night. In fact, one scholar said that toward the end of time it is good to pray the night prayer with the shorter سُوَرٌ that carry great reward, simply because there is so little time and most people no longer have the هِمَّة for long recitation. So utilize the time. Try to pray the ضُحَى prayer. Try to rise before فَجْرٌ, do some قِيَامٌ before فَجْرٌ, and then utilize the time after فَجْرٌ — try not to sleep during that period, which is مَكْرُوه in any case, and in رَمَضَان that time is even more precious than at other times. Then try to do the ضُحَى. The Prophet, صلى الله عليه وسلم, instructed أَبُو هُرَيْرَةَ never to abandon the ضُحَى, so you should try to pray it — it is مَنْدُوب and has a very strong basis in practice, since the Prophet did it consistently: two, four, six, or eight رَكَعَاتٌ before the زَوَالٌ, all the way up to the زَوَالٌ. For the Malikis and the Hanafis, there is the period during which the نَافِلَةٌ cannot be prayed, but otherwise pray نَافِلَةٌ freely.

And then: prayer on time. Prayer on time. We heard earlier that the Messenger of اللهُ, صلى الله عليه وسلم, on his deathbed — his final وَصِيَّة to his أُمَّهْ — his last words were: الصَّلَاةَ، الصَّلَاةَ. Prayer is above everything. Guard your prayer. Use this month to discipline yourself to pray on time. If you were not praying on time before رَمَضَان — and you should have been — then use this time to build that discipline. Pray on time.

If you're working, just stop — your prayer matters more than your job. I guarantee it. Prayer is more important than your job.

Pray first. And pay particular attention to the time between أَسْر and مَغْرِب, which is a powerful time throughout the year — a time of ذِكْرٌ. During أَسْر especially, it carries a particular weight. It is, as the Quran tells us, a time for the people who believe — a time of love for one another.

<!-- page 302 -->

And that is why in this culture you see people at the end of the day completely worn out, needing to go out and drink. They call it happy hour. No, seriously — that is what they call it. They need oblivion so they don't have to think about how empty their lives are. For us, that time is about preparing for sunset, which is a metaphor for death. أَسْر to مَغْرِب represents the later years of a person's life. That entire period carries symbolic weight. And breaking the fast is like what the Messenger described — there are two joys in it. The analogy being made is that breaking the fast resembles meeting your Lord: when the day is over, when death is over, you return to اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى.

After you break your fast, the best thing is to break it on رُطَبٌ, or تَمْر, or a few sips of water. If you have none of that, you can break it on whatever is available. If there is no food at all, then go ahead and pray. Delaying the إِفْطَارٌ is only مَكْرُوه when the delay comes from believing it is somehow better to delay — at that point it becomes a بِدْعَة. But if there is a valid reason to delay, it is perfectly fine. Just do not eat before praying مَغْرِب.

When you visit Muslim households, they will sometimes ask: do you want to eat first or pray? Tell them — let's pray first. Do not be shy about that. Because if people sit down to eat, the مَغْرِب prayer can slip out of its time. مَغْرِب has a very narrow window, and you do not want to delay it. The preferred time is right at the beginning. After the أَذًى comes in, make your دُعَاء, find a clean place, and pray. During the fast you are given a small window — break the fast with something to drink, make the دُعَاء, and then when you finish, move to prayer. These are good habits to build: learning the دُعَاءٌ, practicing them, carrying them with you.

As for the Isha prayer — if you have a strong resolve, it is better to pray your night prayers at home. But if you know yourself well enough to know that your resolve is not strong, or if there is a مُقْرِئ doing a خَتْم of the Quran in the masjid and you want to be part of that خَتْم, then going to the masjid for it is also a good thing. It is مَنْدُوب.

<!-- page 303 -->

What you should avoid — and unfortunately many of our brothers have fallen into this — is the idea that تَرَاوِيح is only eight رَكَعَاتٌ, and so once the eight are done, they say, "الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ, we're following the سُنَّة," and then spend the next hour standing outside the masjid talking. Just be honest with yourself: it is fine to say you find twenty رَكَعَاتٌ long. But do not dress your tiredness up as following the سُنَّة. Because the سُنَّة is certainly not to loiter at the door of the masjid and make idle conversation — I guarantee you that. The Prophet, صلى الله عليه وسلم, did not waste his time. And empty talk at the door of the masjid is — that is سُوق-level behavior. That is what the marketplaces are for. We do not want to be part of that.

And do not belittle people, do not look down on them. I am not trying to be judgmental — everyone is where they are for their own reasons. The point is simply this: do not see yourself as better than someone else. Instead, thank اللهُ that He has not afflicted you with that affliction — because it is an affliction. When the Prophet, صلى الله عليه وسلم, saw fifty people doing such things, he said what he said. Recognizing a نِعْمَةٌ that اللهُ has given you is not arrogance. Arrogance is thinking you deserve it more than someone else does. It is simply a favor اللهُ has extended to you, for reasons known only to Him — and He could take it from you tomorrow, give your state to that person, and leave you standing at the door of the masjid talking.

There is a story about one man who accused Mu'adh ibn Jabal of doing something حَرَامٌ, and Mu'adh — who was known as مُسْتَجَابُ الدُّعَاءِ, someone whose supplications were answered — made a دُعَاء against him. In his old age, that man was seen on the streets of Medina, pinching young women as he walked, because his eyelids had literally fallen over his eyes. He could not see. That was the ابْتِلَاءٌ اللهُ gave him in his old age, for falsely condemning Mu'adh ibn Jabal.

So I am not asking anyone to be self-righteous — myself included. But the point stands: do not go down that road. If people are gathered and talking, greet them. It is a joyous time and there is a place for that. But keep it in its proper place.

<!-- page 304 -->

And remember our brothers and sisters who are in great tribulation right now — the people of Palestine, the people of Iraq, the people of Algeria, the people of Bosnia, the people of India. I could go on and on, and I would rather not. Those people are genuinely suffering. So temper your joy with some gravity as well.

One thing I learned a long time ago about spiritual light is that sometimes, when a great deal of it descends on people, their reaction is to become giddy — to laugh, to get frivolous. You can see it happen. And it is a way of spilling the light, of wasting it. رَمَضَان can do that to people. Because there is so much light pouring in, and they have not learned how to contain it, they end up laughing through the whole month, socializing, having a good time — and by the end they have spilled it all out. So try to hold on to what you are receiving. Try to contain the benefits of this month.

What is that? I am glad you mentioned that. The other thing: Imam Malik, رَحِمَهُ اللهُ, during رَمَضَان did not like storytelling, did not like idle talk. He said: this is a time of قِيَامٌ and قِرَاءَة. So the best ذِكْرٌ during رَمَضَان is Quran. You should still be doing your أَوْرَاد and وِرْدٌ — those are important in رَمَضَان as at any other time. But you need to have your reading of Quran.

Imam Abu Hanifa, رَحِمَهُ اللهُ, said that if you read the Quran at least twice a year, you are not considered to have abandoned it — meaning you will not fall under the آيَة in which the Messenger of اللهُ complained that his people had left the Quran. رَمَضَان is the time to spend with the Book of اللهُ. Ideally, if you can complete more than one خَتَمَ, that would be excellent. I know some of you personally — and one person I know completed twenty-six خَتْمٌ in رَمَضَان. But that was essentially all he was doing. For people who work, that is clearly difficult.

If you are not proficient in recitation, there is also the option of listening to a مُقْرِئ — if you have a recording. When you are driving and you do not have much memorized, it is better to recite what you do know from memory, but if you do not have much to draw from, then simply listen to recitation. And also do some تَدَبُّر of the Quran during رَمَضَان — not only the تَعَبُّد of it, but genuine reflection on its meaning.

If you want a structured approach, there is the seven-day وِرْدٌ of the Quran. The pattern goes: three, five, seven, nine, eleven, thirteen, and then the مُفَصَّل. On the first day, you read the first three سُوَرٌ. On the second day, the next five. Then seven, then nine, then eleven, then thirteen, and then the مُفَصَّل — which runs from سُورَةُ ق to the end of the Quran, or according to some, from سُورَةُ الْحُجُرَاتِ. In seven days you can complete the Quran. This is how many of the سَلَفْ approached it: breaking it into those odd-numbered portions — three, five, seven, nine, eleven, thirteen, and the مُفَصَّل.

Question. If you owe فَرْدٌ prayers, I would not pray تَرَاوِيح. Use رَمَضَان as the time to make up what you owe. Do the فَرْدٌ. Instead of قِيَامٌ, use that time to make up your missed فَرْدٌ prayers. The only نَفْل prayers you should prioritize if you owe فَرْدٌ prayers are the وِتْر and the رَغِبَ — the pre-dawn prayer. Everything else should go toward making up what is owed.

According to the reckoning of the scholars: if you owe prayers and you are performing fewer than five make-up prayers at each prayer time throughout the year, you are not doing enough. If you owe a great deal — years' worth — then do the minimal valid form of the prayer, just the أَرْكَان and the مُؤَكَّدُ سُنَنٍ. That is the واجِبٌ you owe. Do that consistently, and إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, اللهُ will accept it.

<!-- page 306 -->

As for estimating, the scholars say: do your best to figure out when you stopped praying. Most people remember roughly when they went seriously astray in their lives — that tends to be a recognizable turning point. Start from there.

By minimal prayer, I mean: perform the أَرْكَان — the sixteen pillars — and the eight مُؤَكَّدُ سُنَنٍ. That is it. There are sixteen أَرْكَان and eight مُؤَكَّدُ سُنَنٍ. That is the form you focus on when making up a large number of prayers.

If you are still learning and you have doubts about whether you are making mistakes in the prayers you are currently practicing — I think you just have to move forward. There are scholars who would say you must make those up, but my view is: if you are learning فِقْه now and your errors were from ignorance, then make تَوْبَةٌ and ask اللهُ. If there was an obligation you were not even aware of — because your parents never taught you, you genuinely did not know — then in a real sense, that responsibility traces back further. A Muslim child is an أَمَانَةٌ, and one of the obligations of that أَمَانَةٌ is to teach them the فَرَائِض. Many Muslims were simply never told any of this. They thought they were doing right.

For those people — إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ — I would say: make تَوْبَةٌ, ask اللهُ to excuse you for your ignorance, and ask Him to accept those prayers. I would not burden them beyond that. I think that is the appropriate position.

Question. Can a رَغِبَ include the two رَكَعَاتٌ after مَغْرِب? No — the رَغِبَ is not that. Some scholars say you should pray at least two رَكَعَاتٌ of نَفْل after مَغْرِب, and there is a خِلَافٌ about that. But the وِتْر and the رَغِبَ should definitely be maintained even when one owes فَرْدٌ prayers.

<!-- page 307 -->

Question. If you owe فَرْدٌ prayers, what about Quran recitation in the meantime? If you have time that you would use to make up prayers — that time should go to the فَرْدٌ. The فَرْدٌ takes precedence. As the hadith states: "My servant does not draw near to Me with anything more beloved to Me than the فَرْدٌ." So the فَرْدٌ is above everything. Truly. If you are performing نَفْل and not the فَرْدٌ, you are wasting your time.

If the number owed is fewer than five, do them in order. If more than five, you do not need to do them in order. And some scholars say: if you owe a large number, simply commit to a set amount at each prayer time — for example, five prayers at ظُهْر time, five at أَسْر time, five at مَغْرِب time, and so on. You do not have to go in sequence.

بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ

By the blessing of اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى, we were able to go through this text and complete it. We ask اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى to grant us, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, the ability to implement what we have learned.

In closing this text, I would like to share some counsel written by one of our great scholars, سِيدِي Ahmad Zarruq, who died in 1492. He was a great scholar from Morocco who died in Misratah in Libya. He said that the fragrance of his teaching would spread throughout the world long after his bones were laid to rest — and this has proven to be one of his كَرَامَات, his saintly miracles, because his teaching is still reaching us and shaping us today.

He said — رَحِمَهُ اللهُ:

Know — may اللهُ grant you and us success, rectify our worldly and otherworldly lives, and grant us adherence to the way of truth in our journeys and in our sojourns — that repentance is a key, and تَقْوَى is vast, and uprightness is the source of all rectification. A servant is never free of blunders, or shortcomings, or lassitude. Therefore, never be neglectful of تَوْبَةٌ. Never turn away from returning to اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى. Never neglect the acts that bring you closer to Him. Every time one of these three occurs — repent and return. Every time you make a mistake — listen and obey. Every time you display shortcomings or lack of enthusiasm — do not give up. Let your primary concern be to remove from your outward state anything that is displeasing to اللهُ, and then to maintain that outward state through continuous self-counsel. Keep doing this until you find that you naturally flee from anything outwardly displeasing, and that your avoidance of the boundaries of the prohibited has become like a protective net placed before you.

At this point, the time has come to turn inward — toward your heart's presence and its reality — through both reflection and remembrance. Do not hasten the end result before you have completed the beginning, but likewise, do not begin without keeping the end in view. The one who seeks what belongs to the outset at the end loses providential care, and the one who seeks what belongs to the end at the outset loses providential guidance.

Act in accordance with principles and the appropriate legal rulings, not in accordance with stories and fantasies. Do not consider accounts of how things went with others except as a tonic to strengthen your resolve — certainly not as a reference to imitate based on their outward forms or what they appear to be saying.

In all of this, rely on a clear path you can refer to and a foundation you can depend upon in every state. The best of these is the path of Ibn Ata'illah, for it contains a clear direction toward اللهُ. Do not take from others' words unless what they say accords with your own path, but submit to their implications if you desire realization.

Avoid all forms of vain and foul speech to your absolute utmost. Set aside anything whose benefit you cannot discern immediately. Beware of being excessively hard on yourself before you have attained mastery over it, but equally beware of being too lax in anything that concerns sacred rulings.

This is because the self is constantly fleeing from moderation in everything, inclining toward extremism in both deviance and guidance alike. To pause and reflect on this is extremely important, because what the Shaykh is telling us is that the nature of the self is extremism — and he is warning us to be aware of this in both good deeds and foul ones, in both obedience to اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى and disobedience to Him.

So he is telling us: beware of being excessively hard on yourself before you have attained mastery over it, but also beware of being too lax in anything that concerns sacred rulings. He continues: seek out a companion to help you in your affairs and take his counsel in matters that arise from both your inward states and your outward affairs. If you do take his companionship, then treat him in a manner commensurate with his state, and offer your counsel according to his limitations and capacities, for the perfected friend is no longer to be found.

Indeed, in these times even a suitable, agreeable companion rarely lasts. Beware of the majority of people in matters concerning your religious and worldly affairs, unless you have established that a person has a sound relationship with his Lord — grounded in knowledge free of caprice or love of leadership, and in a sound intellect free of hidden agendas. Do not be heedless of the machinations of others or their hidden states.

Consider people from the vantage point of both their origins and their actions. A person of character and distinguished lineage will rarely affect you with anything other than good, whereas a person of low origins typically lets those roots lead him to disregard you when times grow difficult. Be extremely vigilant of the dominant qualities of the people in any given land, do not be heedless of the divine wisdom in creation, and take notice of both gatheredness and separation.

Some of this we have already covered in our book الْقَرَائِب, so refer to it there. Engage with any matter only in a manner appropriate to what that moment specifically calls for, using gentleness and toleration, and be very wary of both harshness and laxity. Too much laxity in permissible matters pulls the heart backward in its journey until even a person of firm resolve ends up resembling a foolish boy.

Work for this world as if you will live forever, but work for the next life as if you will die tomorrow. Do not neglect the externals of your worldly needs, and in the meantime do not be heedless of your end and final resting place.

Be extremely vigilant about avoiding positions of leadership, but should you be tried with such responsibilities, at least know your own limitations. Be utterly sincere — with the sincerity of one who knows full well who is placing demands upon him. Surrender completely to His decree with the submission of one who knows he can never overcome Him.

Have a firm foundation for all your affairs and you will be safe from their pitfalls. Organize your devotional practices and you will find your time extended through the بَرَكَة — the blessings — within it. Never be fanatical about anything, whether it is truth or falsehood, and your heart will remain sound toward others.

Never claim anything you are entitled to, let alone what you are not entitled to, and you will be safe from tricks and treachery. Anyone who claims a rank above his own will fall in humiliation; those who claim a rank they do not warrant will have it stripped from them; while those who claim a station less than their true rank will be elevated to levels even higher than they actually deserve. Never give your companion anything of your inner state beyond what his own state warrants. If you descend to his level, he will show you contempt, whereas if you attempt to raise him to your level, he will abandon you.

Never demand a right from anyone, whether an intimate or a stranger. A stranger in reality owes you nothing, and someone close to you is too precious to be the target of your blame. Never assume that anyone in this world can truly understand your circumstances except through the lens of their own. In reality, everyone sees things in accordance with their own frame of reference and personal path. Yet when aims, purposes, and aspirations are similar, people tend to work together toward a common goal.

Never belittle any talk that involves people in their absence, even if it seems harmless, given the possibility that harm may enter into it. Guard your secrets even when you feel safe with someone, for the person you confide in is not a safer vessel than your own heart from which the secret first arose. Never abandon even an atom's weight of your regular devotional practice. Never be lenient with yourself, whether in times of laxity or times of high resolve. Should you miss some of your practice at a given time, make it up at another. If you are unable to perform your usual practice, at least occupy yourself with something comparable.

Never obey your ego, even for a moment. Never believe any of its claims, no matter what it tells you. Be vigilant about your resolve in all your affairs to your utmost. Should you resolve to do something, do it immediately before the resolve wanes. Examine your soul constantly regarding what you are obliged or need to do. Anything you have no need to do, leave it — even if it is recommended. That means not involving yourself in anything beyond what is absolutely necessary and what constitutes a real, discernible need.

Treat others exactly as you would wish to be treated, and fulfill what is due to them. All of this is beautifully captured in the words of the poet:

*If you desire to live such that your religion is safe, your portion full, and your honor sound — guard your tongue, and never mention another's faults, remembering that you yourself have faults and others have tongues. Watch your eye; should it ever reveal to you the faults of others, say to it: O my eye, other people have eyes too. Live treating others well, and avoid aggression, and should others aggress against you, leave them — but in the best way.*

The source of these words is nothing other than the traditions of the Prophet, peace and blessings of اللهُ be upon him, when he said: *Be vigilant of اللهُ wherever you are, and follow a misdeed with a good deed — it will remove it — and treat others with the most excellent of character.* In another narration he said, peace and blessings of اللهُ be upon him: *Every child of Adam makes mistakes, and the best of those who make mistakes are those who seek to redress them.* And again: *The Holy Spirit inspired my heart's core that no soul will die until it fulfills its decreed portion of this world and its appointed time here. So be conscious of اللهُ and make your requests with dignity.*

In summation, repentance, awareness of اللهُ, and uprightness are the foundations of all that is beneficial. The truth is clear, and its details are weighty and significant. The affair belongs only to اللهُ; success is in His hands. Peace be upon you.
