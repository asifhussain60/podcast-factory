The next disease is called عُجْب.

Imam al-Ghazali says the distinction between عُجْب and كِبْرٌ is that كِبْرٌ requires two people. Kibr is arrogance — it needs the مُتَكَبِّر, the one who is arrogant, and the one toward whom the arrogance is directed. If Zayd comes to Amr and says, "You insignificant fool — why didn't you do what I told you?" Zayd is the مُتَكَبِّر and Amr is the one being looked down upon. Ujb, by contrast, is like كِبْرٌ, except that it requires no second person. The person afflicted with عُجْب is simply impressed with himself. If he has a mirror, he is content — he looks and is genuinely dazzled by how wonderful and magnificent he is.

He defines عُجْب as follows: you magnify and exalt the نِعْمَ — the blessing — while forgetting the one who gave it. In this culture, if someone is told, "You're so beautiful," and they respond, "Thank you," as though they had done something to bring it about — that is a strange thing, if you think about it carefully. Perhaps they spent a great deal on reconstructive surgery, but even then they should be thanking the surgeon, not congratulating themselves. The point is: if Allah made someone beautiful, they should be grateful to Allah. The مُنْعِمٌ — the one who bestowed the gift — is Allah, not the person themselves. They did nothing to bring it about.

And even when real human effort is involved — when someone has a genuine talent that took years to develop — that talent, in its raw form, was still a gift from Allah. The labor it took to develop it was itself made possible by تَوْفِيق from Allah. You see this clearly within families.

Consider beautiful calligraphy. It looks effortless to the observer. I once sat with Sidi Muhammad Zakariyya — the well-known American calligrapher — and watched him work. He made it look so natural that I thought, "I could do that." They say the mark of a master is precisely this: he makes it look so easy that you believe you could do it yourself — but you couldn't. Imam al-Jurjani said much the same of the master of rhetoric: when he speaks, he speaks with such ease and fluency that the listeners think, "I could do that." They could not.

What you have to bear in mind when you see Sidi Muhammad Zakariyya execute his calligraphy is the years of patient, painstaking repetition that preceded it — practicing waws and ya's and ba's, writing out passages again and again. You see the finished work and say, "سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ." And that is a gift from Allah. Now if someone given such a gift were to say, "Look at all my accomplishments — I did this myself, I pulled myself up by my own effort" — that is عُجْب of the worst kind. And there are countless people in the world who think exactly this: that whatever they have achieved, they achieved alone. "I built myself. I earned this." That is عُجْبُ النَّفْسِ.

The مُؤْمِن, by contrast, understands: if Allah gave you the تَوْفِيق to be disciplined, that too was from Allah.

One person grows up with discipline; another, in the same household with the same environmental factors, does not. The disciplined one goes on to master their studies, graduates with honors — and all of it is from Allah. As Imam al-Razi says, Allah is the maker of every maker and every made thing. Allah created the one who built the airplane and the airplane itself. Allah created the one who designed the computer and the computer itself. Once you truly grasp that, the urge to be impressed with yourself dissolves.

And then there is this: above everyone who knows something, there is always someone who knows more. This is the lesson Musa, عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ, had to learn. When he was asked whether anyone on earth was more knowledgeable than he was, he said he did not think so. So Allah sent him to الْخَضِر — who was not a prophet — and made him a student. Even then, he did not prove to be as patient a student as he could have been. The Prophet, صلى الله عليه وسلم, said that had Musa not asked those questions, he would have learned far more from الْخَضِر. He did not absorb everything he might have, yet he was still taught. And the greatest lesson he took away was this: there is always someone who knows more than you, so never forget that.

We must choose our words carefully when speaking of prophets. I am not saying Musa was a poor student — he was, I am sure, extraordinary. But he had a lesson to learn, and Allah taught it to him through الْخَضِر, عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ.

Now, regarding vanity — the reason I translated *عُجْب* as vanity is that, in the dictionary, vanity is defined as excessive pride in one's appearance or accomplishments, which maps closely onto the Arabic. The root *عَجِيبَة* means to wonder or marvel at something; *عُجْب* is to marvel at oneself or one's own achievements. The word *vanity* itself derives from the Latin *vanus*, meaning empty — and so the very source of our vanity is empty and will one day vanish.

The Romans understood this. When a general like Caesar held a victory parade through Rome, riding through the city on a chariot while crowds cheered, a slave would walk alongside him holding a laurel wreath over his head and repeating: *all is vanity*. That slave was there to remind the triumphant general — it is all vanity. This is an ancient wisdom, a *حِكْمَة*, a reminder that all these accomplishments will eventually be forgotten. Charles Lindbergh is a case in point: he received an enormous ticker-tape parade in New York City, and within ten years the American government considered him a criminal for his Nazi sympathies. The hero had fallen.

There is a story — I believe it was written by James Thurber, who wrote *The Secret Life of Walter Mitty* — about a man who saves a child, and the reporters rush in to make him a hero. It turns out he is actually a disreputable person who simply wants to profit from the incident. As he stands at the window watching the adoring crowds below, one of the reporters pushes him out. The point is that there is always another side. No matter how much good a person does, there will always be detractors — or the glory itself will simply prove hollow.

Think of the Yankees parading through New York, celebrated as the greatest team in the world, while the city itself is in moral, social, and ethical disarray. That is *عُجْب* at a collective scale — *الْمُعْجَبَةُ بِالنَّفْسِ* — and it is a deeply troubling condition.

He then notes: knowing that you are incapable, that you can neither produce any benefit nor ward off any harm on your own, should be enough to strip away all vanity. Vanity originates from nothing other than ignorance of two truths: that Allah alone is the fashioner and giver of blessings, and that you are incapable of anything by yourself. Forgetting those two things is how *عُجْب* takes hold.

This is a disease one must always be on guard against — especially when one does something well. And it is particularly relevant for those whom Allah has blessed with outward, physical beauty. Becoming vain about one's appearance is a dangerous trap, one that both men and women can fall into, though it is an especially acute danger for women.
