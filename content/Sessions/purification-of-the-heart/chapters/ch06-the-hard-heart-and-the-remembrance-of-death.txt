One of the interesting observations in *نُورُ الْيَقِينِ* — the *سِيرَةٌ* by an Egyptian scholar — concerns how many of the صَحَابَة lost everything they possessed of this world. He notes that their concern was not for material comfort, and he speaks of the first masjid built at قُبَا, describing it as so simple that a Muslim today would not recognize it as a masjid. He argues that we have become obsessed with adorning outward appearances while our interiors have been left desolate. The صَحَابَة, he says, were the opposite: they had no concern for beautifying the outward and were concerned solely with purifying their hearts. That difference in emphasis is enormous.

What the text is saying, then, is that hardness of heart will generate indolence toward obligations, which in turn opens inroads into the prohibited. Indolence here means a lack of energy, a failure of will. Interestingly, there are people who have tremendous energy for worldly pursuits yet none whatsoever for the Hereafter. People genuinely say things like, "I really can't pray — I work so hard, I get home exhausted, I fall asleep, I'd like to pray but I just don't have time." That is a sign of a dead heart, because when someone has time for the things of this world but not for the Hereafter, it reveals that they do not truly believe in the Hereafter.

Hasan al-Basri said: "I saw seventy men of Badr. If you saw them, you would say they were madmen" — he was speaking to the تَابِعِينَ — "you would call them *مَجَانِينُ*. And if they saw the best of you, they would say these people have no character. And if they saw the worst of you, they would say these people do not believe in the Day of Judgment." Belief in the Hereafter manifests in a person's behavior and concerns. The stronger the belief, the more one is preoccupied with protecting one's stanدِينٌg in the Hereafter.

Looking at the footnote: loss of energy even for acts of obedience leads a person to convince himself that he is simply unable to fulfill his obligations — due to his state — and this is precisely what opens the door to the forbidden. He tells himself, "I just can't do it," and that very attitude leads him toward the حَرَامٌ.

So *طُولُ الْأَمَلِ* is genuinely dangerous.

There is a saying — not a حَدِيث, though it is often quoted as one — that goes: *اِعْمَلْ لِدُنْيَاكَ كَأَنَّكَ تَعِيشُ أَبَدًا، وَاعْمَلْ لِآخِرَتِكَ كَأَنَّكَ تَمُوتُ غَدًا* — "Act for your worldly life as if you will live forever, and act for your Hereafter as if you will die tomorrow."

Shaykh Bashir — Usman Bashir, رَحِمَهُ اللهُ — a dear friend of mine who spent most of his life in Meدِينٌa and was from Eritrea — once told me that people completely misunderstand that saying. It is almost always used as a justification for working hard at worldly pursuits. People love to quote it: "يَا أَخِي, *اِعْمَلْ لِدُنْيَاكَ كَأَنَّكَ تَعِيشُ أَبَدًا*!" But he said: if you read it with both lenses at once — your worldly life, for which you have forever; and your Hereafter, for which you die tomorrow — you will naturally begin pouring your energy into the Hereafter and say to yourself, "I have plenty of time to take care of my worldly affairs." The understanدِينٌg most people carry is exactly backwards, and his reaدِينٌg is really the correct one.

Related to this is the Quranic verse: *وَلَا تَنسَ نَصِيبَكَ مِنَ ٱلدُّنْيَا* — "Do not forget your share of this world" (28:77). This has been interpreted in two ways. One reaدِينٌg is: do not forget what اللهُ has given you, so that you may expend it for the Hereafter. The other is: the world is here, اللهُ gave it to you, so do not forget that it exists — but the essential thing remains the Hereafter. Both interpretations are valid positions.

We are not anti-worldly in the way that, say, traditional Christianity tended to be. We are not against the world. The world is a place اللهُ created for us to enjoy as well — but not to enjoy to the point where we forget what we are truly here for, and that it is a bridge.

Then the text cites a verse in which اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى says, "A long time passed and their hearts became hard." The idea is that if you are not reflecting on the Hereafter, the longer you remain on this earth, the harder the heart grows.

If you observe children, you notice something striking: they can cry at the drop of a hat. Part of this is that their hearts are very supple — they can move through extreme joy and extreme sadness within a single day. Their hearts are like this because they are very pure, and so everything affects them in profound ways. As a person grows older, that softness becomes increasingly difficult to recover.

This is one reason the scholars say the Prophet ﷺ loved women among the things made beloved to him in this world — *لِرِقَّةِ قُلُوبِهِنَّ*, because their hearts are more tender than those of men. Women, generally speaking, have more compassion and more openness of heart. For men, it is very easy for the heart to harden. And so the danger of *طُولُ الْأَمَلِ* is precisely that it produces hardness of heart.

Then the text says: *لَكِنْ نُحُفِهَا قَرِيٌّ مِنْ نِرَادٍ أَنْعَبُ، أَوْ كَانَ إِفِي تَرْسِنِيكِ الْإِنْمَانُ لَمْ يُعَبْ* — regarدِينٌg the one who is engaged in preparing for the next life, or in producing works of knowledge. The author — himself a scholar — notes that writing books that benefit people is among the highest things one can do: *صَدَقَةٌ جَارِيَةٌ*, or *عِلْمٌ يُنْتَفَعُ بِهِ النَّاسُ*, ongoing charity or knowledge from which people continue to benefit. For such a person, desiring a long life is not blameworthy.

The Prophet ﷺ said: *لَا تَتَمَنَّ الْمَوْتَ* — do not wish for death. For if you did wrong in the past, you want more life in order to make توبةٌ and set things right. And if you did good, you want more time to do more good. Either way — whether your past has been poor or praiseworthy — each new day is an opportunity to turn back to اللهُ and change. So one should always desire a long life when engaged in good things. If one is not engaged in good things, however, then a long life is a disaster.

For the person who does not waste time, who recognizes the urgency of the human condition and their own impenدِينٌg mortality, and who sets out doing useful deeds — spreaدِينٌg knowledge or anything else that benefits people — اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى says: *يَمْكُثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ* — what benefits people remains and continues in the earth.

Mu'tamir ibn Sulayman once fell gravely ill, and someone asked him whether he feared dying from it. He said, "No, no, no — I have a verse in the Quran that tells me I am not going to die yet." The person asked, "Which one?" He said, "اللهُ says that what benefits people stays for a long time" — and he was a great scholar, enormously beneficial to others.

Hard-heartedness, then, manifests in several ways: eyes that do not weep, an absence of shame and modesty, love of this world, and extended, false hope. There is also a very beautiful tradition that says: *الأَمَلُ رَحْمَةٌ مِنَ اللهِ، وَلَوْلَاهُ لَمَا زَرَعَ زَارِعٌ وَلَا وَلَدَ وَلَدٌ* — extended hope is a mercy from اللهُ, for had it not been for hope, no farmer would plant seeds and no mother would bear a child. It is a *رَحْمَة* from اللهُ — that is the important thing to understand.

It is part of our nature. We genuinely believe, at some deep level, that we are going on. Without that sense, we could not function in the world — we would simply be paralyzed. So the point is not to fall into paralysis, but to reflect on death. The poet says in the *قَصِيدَة*: *تَفَكَّرْ أَخِي فِي الْمَوْتِ، إِنَّهُ نَاظِرٌ، وَقَصِّرِ الْأَمَلَ، فَيَكْسُرَ الْقَلْبَ* — reflect on death, for it is coming, and shorten your hopes, for that is what breaks open the hardened heart.

One of the beneficial exercises the early Muslims practiced was to meditate on death and on the stages that follow it — to reflect on the stations of the journey itself, incluدِينٌg the صِرَاطٌ, and what it means to cross it. It is interesting that athletes in this country are trained to use visualization: a boxer will mentally run through a fight in advance as psychological preparation. This is really the same idea. You are preparing yourself psychologically for a journey you will actually make. Marat al-Raji has a *قَصِيدَة* about death in which he says: *فَسَتُسَافِرُ، رِضًا الْيَسَافِرُ* — "You will travel tomorrow, and what a journey." Meaning death. What a journey indeed.

The moment you die, there is a severance — and yet you remain conscious, in a state of heightened awareness so acute that this present life comes to seem like a dream by comparison. This is why people at the moment of death often feel as though they are waking up. The Prophet ﷺ said, *النَّاسُ نِيَامٌ، فَإِذَا مَاتُوا انْتَبَهُوا* — "People are asleep; when they die, they awaken."

The exercise many of the early Muslims practiced was to actually lie down and imagine being lowered into the grave. This is not a morbid exercise — it is training. And it is genuinely effective. Some of the scholars said that for a person overcome by *شَهْوَة* — appetites and desires — particularly young people, this kind of reflection is one of the best ways to bring sobriety to the heart.

Now, there is an important distinction that Imam al-Qarafi, رَحِمَهُ اللهُ, draws: the difference between رَجَاء and *طُولُ الْأَمَلِ*. *Raja'* means hope, and it is something altogether different from عَمَلٌ. There are many verses in the Quran affirming this: *‏لَّقَدْ كَانَ لَكُمْ فِى رَسُولِ ٱللَّهِ أُسْوَةٌ حَسَنَةٌۭ لِّمَن كَانَ يَرْجُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ وَٱلْيَوْمَ ٱلْآخِرَ وَذَكَرَ ٱللَّهَ كَثِيرًۭا ‎* — whoever hopes in اللهُ has a beautiful example in the Messenger of اللهُ. There is a رَجَاء directed toward اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى, and toward the Last Day.

There is a famous حَدِيث in which Aisha, رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهَا, recounts that the Prophet ﷺ said: *رضي الله عنه من أحب الله أحب لقاءه* — whoever loves to meet اللهُ, اللهُ loves to meet him. Aisha said, "O Messenger of اللهُ, but what about disliking death?" He said: *كُلُّكُمْ تَكْرَهُ الْمَوْتَ* — that natural aversion to death itself is something everyone shares. But not wanting to meet one's Lord is a different matter — the one whose heart is at peace (*الْمُطْمَئِنّ*) looks forward to that meeting.

The scholars emphasize the relationship between *خَوْف* and رَجَاء — *واعْبُدْهُمْ بَيْنَ الْخَوْفِ وَالرَّجَاءِ* — you walk with the two sandals of fear and hope. If you are overwhelmed by fear, it pushes you into a psychological state of terror that produces *يَأْس* — despair. Too much fear leads to despair of اللهُ's mercy; a person begins to think they will never be saved. This is a well-known religious illness. It is less common today, since people are generally less religiously intense, but it remains real for certain individuals. Some Christians, particularly in fire-and-brimstone evangelical traditions that dwell heavily on hellfire and damnation, can easily fall into this state. The early Puritan settlers actually believed that only 144,000 people would be saved — the elect. Since there were already more than 144,000 Puritans, the odds in that theology were not favorable. The Jehovah's Witnesses hold a similar belief, drawn from the Book of Revelation. That is excessive fear.

Too much hope — excessive رَجَاء — is the opposite disease, producing complacency: "I have no problem, I am saved," and so action stops entirely. There is an interesting observation one could make about certain Baptist theology, which holds that once you accept Jesus as your Savior, you are saved regardless of what you subsequently do. The emphasis falls overwhelmingly on salvation through faith, with very little weight placed on fear of hellfire. This is why someone like Jimmy Swaggart can fall publicly, and then return to the pulpit to preach — and the congregation is still there listening. If the same thing happened to an imam, *لَا طَهَّرَ اللَّهُ* — that would simply be the end. No one would retain any respect for that person, because Muslims hold far higher expectations of those they accept as examples and leaders in their communities.

This is, incidentally, one reason why it is *مَكْرُوه* for the child of an illegitimate union to lead the prayer — not because of any sin on the child's part, but because such a person is not the most fitting *قُدْوَة*, or example, for the community. There is no *إِثْم* — no blame — on such a child. None whatsoever. What the parents did is entirely between them and their Lord. *‏أَلَّا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌۭ وِزْرَ أُخْرَىٰ ‎* — no soul bears the burden of another's wrongdoing. There were people in the Arabian Peninsula at the time of the Prophet ﷺ who were born outside of marriage, who became Muslim, and who were counted among the finest human beings. The least of the صَحَابَة, in terms of their deeds, surpasses the best of us — simply because they were given the *رُؤْيَة*, the vision of the Prophet ﷺ in waking life and believed in him. That alone is a bounty from اللهُ beyond reckoning.

So: عَمَلٌ is a desire for long life, for wealth, for all the things we generally associate with happiness in this world. It is a kind of hope — a رَجَاء. But there is something else called أُمْنِيَّة, and it is أُمْنِيَّة that is blameworthy in Islam. أُمْنِيَّة is a hope —

The difference between رَجَاح and أُمْنِيَة is that رَجَاح always involves taking the سَبَبٌ — in other words, actually pursuing the means to obtain what you want. With أُمْنِيَة, by contrast, it is بِلَا سَبَبٍ: no means are taken at all.

Say you want to get healthy. You start watching your diet, you start exercising — that is رَجَاح. But someone who says, "I really want to get healthy," while continuing to eat junk food and sit in front of the television, doing nothing to change his situation — that is أُمْنِيَة. And that, اللهُ says, is exactly what the Jews and Christians have toward the آخِرَة: they have أُمْنِيَة. They are doing nothing to earn the next life; in fact, they are corrupting in the earth.

That is why Muslims must be on guard, because — as my friend Abd al-Hakim Murad once told a story about a Pakistani man who overheard two or three racists in London: one said he was moving to Wales because Muslims were only one or three percent of the population there, another said he preferred Scotland at two percent, and a third was going to Ireland at one percent. The Pakistani man said, "Why don't you go to hell? There are no Muslims there." What Abd al-Hakim observed about that is that the assumption, of course, is that Muslims don't go to hell. Unfortunately, there is going to be a great shock on يَوْمُ الْقِيَامَةِ — because if all you have is أُمْنِيَة, and all your Islam amounts to is the شَهَادَةٌ تَامَّةٌ, what they call the شَهَادَة of birth and death — شَهَادَةُ تَوْلِيدَتِكَ or شَهَادَةَ تَوَفَّاتِكَ — your testimony recorded at birth on your birth certificate and at death on your death certificate, if that is the sum of it, then you have a serious problem.

<!-- page 84 -->

I was once in Santa Clara; Farif was with me. A family came — their grandfather had died, and they came to the masjid to bury him. None of them were practicing Muslims, and neither had their grandfather been, but when he died they realized, "We are Muslims — how do we bury him? Let's take him to the mosque." So they brought him, and we washed the body and performed the rites. But it is deeply sad when people's entire experience of Islam is reduced to the moments of marriage and death — they need an imam to perform the نِكَاح, or they need someone who knows how to wash the body and pray over it. That is أُمْنِيَة.

رَجَاءٌ, then — hoping in اللهُ — means you are actually doing what is required to be pleasing to اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى. The scholars also say that hope should be the dominant state throughout one's life, but when you reach your deathbed, you should let go of fear entirely and rest in absolute hope in اللهُ. At that moment there should be no fear; you should be completely immersed in a sense of اللهُ's رَحْمَة, trusting that He will forgive you and accept you, and holدِينٌg the best opinion of اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى.

Now, Imam al-Haythami makes another point that Shaykh al-Khadim mentions in his commentary, and it is important: بُعْدُ الْأَمَلِ — this extended hope, this drawn-out expectation — is, at its root, a heedlessness about the reality of death. But he says that in and of itself it is not a wrong action. There is no obligatory command (واجِبٌ) to remember death. When the Prophet ﷺ said to remember death, and also said, "I used to tell you not to visit graves, but now I tell you: go visit them, because it reminds you of the أَخِيرًا" — the point is that remembering death is مَنْدُوب, recommended. In the same way, ذِكْرٌ beyond what is prescribed in the prayer is مَنْدُوب:

*اُذْكُرِ اللَّهَ* — *أُذْكُرُوا* — there is the imperative, the command, but it is for *نَدْب*, not *وُجُوب*. You are not obligated to remember اللهُ as a *فَرْضٌ*; it is مَنْدُوب, *مُسْتَحَبٌّ*, strongly encouraged, because it prepares you. So heedlessness of death is not a wrong action in itself.

<!-- page 85 -->

Similarly, making money, being engaged in the *دُنْيَا*, being active in worldly affairs — that is *مُبَاح*. اللهُ did not prohibit it. You may trade on *حَجٌّ*; there is nothing wrong with it. You can combine *دُنْيَا* and أَخِيرًا on *حَجٌّ*. You do not have to go in a state of pure spiritual detachment. You can take goods, bring books, sell things — within whatever is permitted. People who shave pilgrims, bringing razors and charging for the service — they are *حُجَّاج* and they are earning money, and there is nothing wrong with that. Our *دِينٌ* does not even frown upon it. You should not say *أَسْتَغْفِرُ اللهَ* and think they are not spiritual people; that attitude is not from our *دِينٌ*.

So he says: that is permissible — *unless* it was done for *تَفَاخُرٌ* or *تَكَاثُر*, or for *تَسْوِيفُ التَّوْبَةِ*, in which case it becomes a grave sin (*كَبِيرَةٌ*). If you are acquiring wealth in order to compete with others out of arrogance, or to hoard it in a contest of accumulation, or — *تَسْوِيفُ التَّوْبَةِ* — to keep putting off repentance, then it is blameworthy. As the *آيَةٌ* says:

*بَلْ يُرِيدُ الْإِنْسَانُ لِيَفْجُرَ أَمَامَهُ، يَسْأَلُ أَيَّانَ يَوْمُ الْقِيَامَةِ*

Man wants to keep sinning in whatever remains of his life and asks, "When is the Day of Judgment?" — as though it is far off, as though he need not think about it. The *عُلَمَاء* say in their commentaries on this *آيَةٌ* that one of its meanings is *يُسَوِّفُ التَّوْبَةَ* — he keeps postponing his repentance. This is what is called in this culture "sowing your wild oats" — the idea that when you are young and have the energy for *مَعَاصِي*, you should get them out of your system, because you won't have the energy when you are older. This is a concept in American and European culture. And this is precisely why اللهُ loves the young man who is in obedience — because when you grow old, you no longer have the energy for disobedience.

Some people live badly for decades, then around forty-five or fifty, death begins to feel real, they make *تَوْبَة*, and then they become self-righteous, pointing fingers at everyone else, having completely forgotten who they themselves were. This is a real problem — fifty years old, having done every terrible thing: drinking, cheating, you name it — and then at fifty realizing mortality, going to the masjid, becoming outwardly devout, and immediately beginning to judge others. That is a disease of its own.

Now, the next disease is called *طِيَرَة*, and it is a somewhat nuanced concept because it has a related term in the *حَدِيث* vocabulary, *طِيَرَة*. The Arabs, before Islam, when they wanted to make a decision, would go to a flock of birds and startle them — a form of divination. If the flock flew to the left, they took it as a bad omen; if it flew to the right, they took it as a positive omen. The right-flying birds they called *يُمْنٌ* — from which *Yemen*, being to the right when one faces the rising sun — and the left-flying birds they called *شُؤْم*, which is why the Levant (*الشَّامُ*) carries that sense of the left. In the Arabian Peninsula, facing the sun in the morning, Yemen is on the right (*اَلْيَمِينُ*) and الشَّامُ is to the left. You also get *شَمَلَ* — north, the left side — and *جَنُوب*, south, from *جَنْب*, meaning side or flank, because you recline on your right side; so the south is on the right and the north is on the left.

This is also reflected in traditional Muslim cartography. Muslims always oriented their maps with the south at the top and the north at the bottom. I can show you maps from the classical books of *تَوْقِيتٌ* — the science of timekeeping, the *عِلْمُ الْفَلَكِ* of the Muslims — and all of them show the south at the top. This is why I have long believed that Muslims should make their own maps.

I first came to this realization years ago in New Mexico, when I was at a flea market with Hakim Archuleta. I found an old antique compass, and it was cheap. The vendor said, "There's only one problem — instead of pointing north, the arrow points south." I said, "That's fantastic." As we walked away I asked my friend Hakim, who knows a great deal about science, why it would make any difference. He said it's simply a matter of polarization — the paint is facing south because that is the *قُطْب*, the pole. That started me thinking about the question of south and north, and why we have made north dominant.

Then I came across a map by al-Idrisi — the first great cartographer of the world, a Moroccan who drew a world map with the south at the top and the north at the bottom. The European mapmakers drew directly from his work, but they recognized the significance of putting the south at the top: it placed Africa above Europe. So they flipped it, and from that point on their maps had north at the top. Marshall Hodgson makes exactly this observation in *The Venture of Islam* — he says it is unfortunate that Muslims do not recognize that they produced the first world map and oriented it with the south at the top, and that they should return to that orientation, because the north-dominant map reflects a purely Eurocentric worldview that has no historical foundation. A south-oriented map would put Africa at the top and Europe at the bottom; we would say, "I'm going down to Europe and up to Africa."

<!-- page 87 -->

We would also have to say "up to Los Angeles and down to San Francisco." And in Arabic, this is in fact how it works — the south is up. Southern Egypt is called *صَعِيد*, Upper Egypt, while the north is Lower Egypt. In Libya as well, people say, "We are going up south" and "down to the sea," which is in the north. That is traditional cosmology, and do not think it is insignificant — the effect of spenدِينٌg a lifetime looking at maps that place Africa at the bottom is real. On a south-oriented map, moreover, nothing looms over Arabia. Look at the map the Australians produced when they decided they were tired of being "down under" — they simply turned it right-side up. I have a map from New Zealand, sent to me by a friend after I told her about this. It is a genuinely revealing perspective: you see how close Ireland is to Morocco, how close Germany is to England, and you begin to understand the depth of the Germanic-English connection in a way the north-dominant map never quite conveys. But that is a digression from the diseases of the heart — a worthwhile one, I think, but a digression nonetheless.

So *طِيَرَة* — the birds flying right or left — is at its root superstition, but it is also more than that, because in Arabic the word for a pessimist derives from the same concept: the pessimist is the one who always sees the worst in any situation, who will say about any plan, "It will never work." The root of *طِيَرَة* is the ignorance that all affairs belong to اللهُ alone. For the Muslim, this means having a *حُسْنُ الظَّنِّ* — a good opinion — of اللهُ. The non-Muslim has a bad opinion of اللهُ; the Muslim has a good one. That is the essential difference. So you should always cultivate this good opinion of اللهُ.

He now moves toward *سُوءُ الظَّنِّ* — bad opinion — but before doing so I want to say one more thing about *طِيَرَة*. There are really two types. The first — valid for people of sound intellect and normative experience — is when *الْحُكْمُ الْعَادِي*, empirical probability, establishes that a thing consistently causes harm. If you put your hand near a cobra, it bites you and you die. In that case, you must use your intellect; avoiدِينٌg it is not *طِيَرَة*, it is rational. If something is harmful by established norm, you must avoid it, and the scholars say that if you do not, you are not considered a person of sound reason — you are acting foolishly. There are, of course, exceptional cases: one of the *أَوْلِيَاء* was walking with a student, they came upon a lion on the path, the student was terrified, the shaykh told him to take the high road and continued walking, and the lion did not touch him. But that is not a station available to everyone.
