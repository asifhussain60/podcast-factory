And know that the love of the world that is blameworthy is love motivated solely by the benefit of the نَفْس — not the kind of love that keeps you safe, or keeps others safe from you. In other words, it does not include loving the world in order to spare others from dependence on you, or to free yourself from dependence on Allah's servants. This is why the Islamic tradition teaches that the world is a bridge: cross it toward the next life, and do not try to build on it as though you intend to stay forever.

He also points out that blameworthy love of this world is not a single, undifferentiated thing — in fact, love of the world falls under the five categories of legal rulings, and its judgment depends entirely on what it leads one to achieve. So love of worldly things that help one attain the next life — love of the Kaaba, love of the Prophet ﷺ, love of the people of Allah, love of books of knowledge, love of the means that draw one closer to God, love of fellow Muslims who support one in one's religion — all of that is obligatory, وَاجِب. There is also love that is recommended, مَنْدُوبٌ; love that is permissible, مُبَاحٌ, such as love of one's children or one's spouse; love that is disliked, مَكْرُوهٌ; and love that is outright forbidden, حَرَامٌ.

He states: to love anything that assists one in committing something forbidden is itself prohibited. And censuring the world applies only to those aspects of it that do not assist toward salvation. Its reproach is restricted to love of it for those kinds of reasons. Indeed, the best of creation ﷺ forbade cursing the world. There is a hadith in which the Prophet said, *لَا تَسُبُّوا الدُّنْيَا* — do not curse the world — because Allah created it, and it is a means of attaining knowledge of Allah, Glorified and Exalted.

The word *دُنْيَا* itself is worth reflecting on. Scholars differ: some say it derives from *دَنَاءَة*, others from *دَنَا*. Either way, it is distinct from *أَلَمْ*, which refers to the world in the sense of the cosmos, nature, all of existence. *دُنْيَا* refers specifically to *حَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا* — the ephemeral dimension of life, the hustle and preoccupation that people get swept up in. That is what Allah warns us about: being deceived by this transient thing. The *أَلَمْ* that Allah created, by contrast, derives from a root meaning *عَلَامَة* — a sign. The cosmos is the greatest sign of Allah, which is precisely why we do not despise or look down on it.

This sets Islam apart from traditional Christian doctrine — though Christianity has been moving away from this position over the past century. The traditional Christian view condemned the world as evil. Islam does not say that. Allah says He created everything in the world for هُمَاn beings: some things to reflect upon, as signs leading to Allah; others to benefit from as stewards and custodians of the earth. All of that is affirmed.

Things are praised or blamed according to what they produce — like a remedy or a poison. If something leads to disease, it is blameworthy; if it leads to a cure, it is praiseworthy. That is the lens through which one should view worldly things. Whatever of the material necessities of earthly life one obtains — food, housing, shelter — these are not considered *دُنْيَا* in the blameworthy sense. Even one's first wife, according to the scholars of Islamic law, is not considered *دُنْيَا*. And if a man marries to care for an orphan or a woman in need, that too falls outside the blameworthy category. If he takes a second, third, or fourth wife for purely worldly reasons, it falls under a different ruling — though it remains permissible, مُبَاحٌ, it does not carry the same merit.

Similarly with wealth and reputation — *جَهْ*, meaning standing or position. Scholars have said that having a good name can be valuable insofar as it enables one to help others, not as an end in itself. The scholars have generally regarded the accumulation of excessive wealth as disliked, مَكْرُوهٌ, because the more wealth one acquires, the greater the probability of falling into something displeasing to Allah. Becoming absorbed in amassing and competing for wealth is a dangerous occupation. People become addicted to the stock market, to the chase of accumulation — it becomes a compulsion.

As for earning wealth in order to show off — to say, look at me, look how clever I am, look how much I have — the scholars counted this among the people who have acquired enormities. *مُكْتَسِبُ الْكَبَائِرِ*: it is a major sin, حَرَامٌ, to do this.

He then says: love of praise is another disease. Love of being praised for what one has not done or accomplished is caused by craving something other than the Exalted. Those who love to be praised for things Allah has not praised them for — this is considered forbidden, a disease of the heart. One should only desire to be praised for what one has genuinely done, though some scholars go further and say one should not really desire praise except from Allah, Glorified and Exalted — while stopping short of prohibiting it entirely. It is not necessarily a bad thing when someone says, *أَحْسَنْتَ*, you did well. The Prophet ﷺ did say this to people.

There is a distinction between *مَدْح* that is forbidden and *مَدْح* that is permitted. The forbidden kind is flattery. The Prophet ﷺ said: throw dirt in the faces of flatterers — meaning those who tell you how wonderful you are when it is not true, or when they do not mean it. The poets who would compose elaborate panegyrics for the caliphs exemplify this: الْمُتَنَبِّي wrote poem after poem for سَيْفُ الدَّوْلَةِ, all of it *مَدْح*, telling him how magnificent he was. That is the blameworthy kind. Whereas sincerely telling someone *أَحْسَنْتَ*, or that they did good work, is not prohibited — especially when there is no risk that it will make them conceited. It functions as encouragement, as a form of gratitude.

He goes on to say that *طَمَعٌ* — covetousness, greediness of expectation — is the very reason people flatter others and also why they love to be praised themselves. They want something in return. This, too, is a disease. To enjoy being told you did a great job for something you did not do, while knowing full well you did not do it, is a deeply contemptible thing. You smile and say, thank you so much, I'm glad you appreciate the effort — when you put in none. This happens in universities, where graduate students do all the work and the professor takes the credit. It happens in corporations, where a manager assigns tasks, the team does the work, and the manager claims the recognition. It is a very common هُمَاn tendency — which is precisely why scholars recognized it centuries ago, and why the قُرْآن addresses it. Allah says that there are those who love to be praised for what they have not done. It is, in that sense, a قُرْآنic reality.

He then turns to envy — and here I will pause, because envy is a lengthy topic and a major illness. We will cover envy and *رِيَاءٌ* next week, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ. We are continuing with this text now in our third meeting, and we should reach the diseases of fear and desire of other than Allah, though whether we get that far depends on how much ground remains.

Now, حَسَد. We ended on حَسَد last time. Hasad is probably one of the most serious diseases. There is a difference of opinion among scholars about which disease is the root of all others: some say حَسَد is the root disease. I would argue it always traces back to *هُمَا* — desire — because within حَسَد there is always an underlying desire. So ultimately it goes back to هُمَا. Or to *حُبُّ الْجَاهِ*, as ابْنُ عَاشِرٍ suggests — though even ابْنُ عَاشِرٍ admits that the source of all wrong is *حُبُّ الْعَاجِلَةِ*, love of the immediate world, which again connects to desire.

According to the scholars, حَسَد is the first disease, the first manifestation of disobedience in the heavens. The first act of disobedience toward Allah in the heavenly realm was حَسَد — because nothing prevented إِبْلِيس from prostrating except حَسَد. The particular form is called *تَزَكٍّ*: he could not bear that Adam, peace be upon him, would hold a higher station than him. He could not endure it. It is also connected to *تَكَبُّر* — he was filled with arrogance and pride. The حَسَد and the arrogance were intertwined.

There is a hadith in which the Prophet ﷺ said that حَسَد devours good deeds the way fire devours dry wood. If you have ever watched fire consume dry wood, you should let that image tell you what happens to your *حَسَنَاتٌ*. The person who harbors envy is called *الْحَاسِد*, and the object of his envy is called *الْمَحْسُودُ*. In the verse of the قُرْآن that people should commit to memory: *‏وَمِن شَرِّ حَاسِدٍ إِذَا حَسَدَ ‎* — the *حَسَدٌ* referred to is shaytan, *الْحَاسِد*. To harbor envy is to embody one of the attributes of shaytan.

There is also a hadith in which the Prophet ﷺ said: *كُلُّ ذِي نِعْمَةٍ مَحْسُودٌ* — every possessor of a blessing, any blessing at all, is envied. There will always be someone who envies you for whatever you have. Consider two street sweepers: one has only his brooms to carry; the other has a donkey pulling his cart. To you, this is entirely trivial. But the one without the donkey looks at the one with the donkey and envies him. Whatever the level, however small the difference, envy operates. It is a strange and pervasive force that runs through هُمَاn culture and society.

It is also understood that envy carries real harm — *مَضَرَّةٌ*. But what the scholars say is that the envy of the *حَسَدٌ* harms him before it harms the object of his envy. He is damaged by it faster than he can damage the one he envies.

The evil eye is generally connected to envy, though it does not require envy. Some people simply have a powerful gaze — a kind of psychic force. It does not necessarily mean they envy; it can operate independently, though it is still something to take seriously. Across many Muslim cultures, traditionally, a firstborn male child would have his ears pierced and be dressed as a girl for the first several years of life, precisely out of fear of the evil eye. This was widespread practice. I knew an older man who had holes in his ears, and when I asked him, that was the explanation.

In contemporary Western culture, much of this is dismissed as superstition. But it is striking that every culture in the world has some concept of the evil eye. Every one. The Latin root of the English word *invidious* — meaning to envy — literally means to look with evil. The evil eye is an evil glance. The Chinese have a concept of it as well and employ various ritual protections against it; you will often see mirrors on Chinese houses, placed there to deflect the evil eye. This is a universal هُمَاn belief. And when a belief is universal, it usually has some foundation — it is not simply a shared fantasy.

The anthropologist might say: things happen to people, and when they cannot explain why, they reach for explanations like the evil eye. It becomes a convenient way of accounting for the inexplicable. Someone falls ill for no discernible reason, no one can figure it out, and people say it might be the eye. So that dynamic exists too.

But the hadith *الْعَيْنُ حَقٌّ* — the evil eye is real — is a sound, صَحِيح hadith. And it is worth considering its source. The Prophet ﷺ was someone who actively worked to dismantle superstition. When the moon eclipsed on the day his son Ibrahim died, the Arabs believed a lunar eclipse meant a great person had died. It was a perfect moment for a charlatan to exploit — and in fact, people became Muslim that day precisely because they thought the moon had eclipsed for his son. He went out and announced to the people: the sun and the moon are signs of Allah; they do not eclipse for anyone's death or life. He dispelled that superstition on the spot.

So when a man of that character said *الْعَيْنُ حَقٌّ* in a sound hadith, he was telling us that there is something genuinely real in the world called the evil eye, and it deserves to be taken seriously. In English, people say, "if looks could kill" — there is a folk intuition that a gaze carries force, that something passes between people through the eyes.

So: the hadith establishes that حَسَد devours good deeds the way fire devours dry wood. And the reason for that becomes clear when he explains it: if you desire that someone lose a blessing they possess, you are envious of them. It can be anything — a house, a car, a job. Someone received a promotion over you and you wish they would fail, get fired, so that you might take their position. Someone has a spouse you envy, and you think they do not deserve that person and you wish the marriage would fall apart. The variations are endless, but at the core of every one of them is the desire for the نِعْمَ — the blessing — to be lost. And نِعْمَ, from the Arabic root نِعِمَّا, relates to softness, ease — it is the opposite of hardship.

So نِعْمَ are things that ease life — that is what a نِعْمَ is. Now, obviously, a blessing can be hidden inside what looks like a hardship, and a hardship can be hidden inside what looks like a blessing. In English we say "a blessing in disguise" or "every cloud has a silver lining" — expressions that remind us to be careful about judging what is and is not a blessing. A نِعْمَ is something Allah gives, and Allah's name is الْمُنْعِم: the Giver of blessings. If Allah gives a person a blessing and you wish that person would lose it, you are essentially saying Allah was wrong to place that blessing with them. You are passing judgment on Allah's wisdom.

One of my favorite stories — and some people have heard this — concerns الأَصْمَعِيُّ, who used to travel the desert encampments collecting words and lore. He was once at a Bedouin's tent, and, as is part of Bedouin culture then and now, the women came in, served, and sat. This man had a remarkably beautiful wife, and he himself was remarkably ugly. When the man stepped out to slaughter a sheep in الأَصْمَعِيُّ's honor, الأَصْمَعِيُّ could not resist asking the woman: "How did you end up with him? How did a woman as beautiful as you come to be with a man who looks like that?" The woman said — and الأَصْمَعِيُّ is the one relating this, which tells you something about the kind of man he was — "اِتَّقِ اللَّهَ," fear Allah. Then she said: "لَعَلَّهُ أَحْسَنَ إِلَى رَبِّهِ وَأَنَا جَزَاؤُهُ" — perhaps he did good things with his Lord, and I am his reward. Or perhaps I did some bad things, and he is my punishment.

The point الأَصْمَعِيُّ was making is that Allah has a حِكْمَةٌ, and you do not know what it is. So you must be very careful before asking, "Why is that person with that person?" or "What is that doing there?" Allah is الْمُنْعِم, the Giver of blessings, and when you question the نِعْمَ, you are questioning the One who gave it. That is why Allah hates حَسَد — and it is precisely why شَيْطَان fell into disobedience.

The difference between شَيْطَان and Adam, عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ, is that when Adam made his mistake, he made تَوْبَة. The حَاسِدٌ does not make تَوْبَة — cannot make تَوْبَة — because part of his problem is كِبْرٌ. He believes he is more worthy and that the other person does not deserve what they have. That is تَكَبُّر: "I am better than him; he does not deserve what he has." A person in that state finds it nearly impossible to admit he is wrong, so he never turns away from his حَسَد. He simply does not turn away from it.

So when the author says that the true meaning of حَسَد is that if you could, through some scheme, eliminate that blessing yourself, you would — that is the شَرِيعَة definition he is pointing toward. There is a حَدِيث: "لَا يَسْلَمُ الْمُؤْمِنُ مِنْ ثَلَاثَةٍ" — the believer will never be entirely free of three things — and حَسَد is one of them. The other two are طِيَرَة and ظَنْ, which he will address later. Since no believer is ever fully safe from حَسَد, the cure prescribed is: لَا تَبْرِي — do not act on it. The حَدِيث says: if you feel envy, do not harm the person, do not oppress them. As long as you do not actually do something to cause that person to lose their blessing, your حَسَد does not fall into the category that destroys your حَسَنَاتٌ. The حَسَد that devours your good deeds is the حَسَد where, if you were able, you would actually cause the person to lose that blessing — "وَرَأَى الْإِبْلَا" — and then act on it.

Then the author says: if your fear of Allah is what holds you back from harming the person, then that is not the kind of حَسَد that will damage you. But if you would act on it given the chance — that is dangerous. He then attributes this distinction to إِمَامُ الْغَزَالِيِّ, رَحِمَهُ اللَّهُ, and that is the basis of his judgment here.

Then he says that إِمَامُ الْغَزَالِيِّ also said: if you hate the envy you feel — if you despise that part of yourself that is generating it — then that is a sign that you are not truly an envious person. Hating the experience of envy within yourself is evidence that you are not an envious person at your core. This again points to the recognition that you have a نَفْس and a رُوح — which are, in a real sense, the same thing, except that the نَفْس is the رُوح that still carries the tarnish of this world; it has not been purified. Recognizing that these stirrings in the heart are part of that taint — part of the رَانَ that accumulates over the heart through wrong actions and through being immersed in this world — and then detesting those stirrings: that is a sign that the رُوح, the pure aspect of yourself, is the stronger force within you.

Then he turns to the دَوَاءٌ — the treatment — and as always he gives us two. The rectification of the heart requires two things: knowledge and action. He has already given the knowledge base: you must understand that the path out of حَسَد is to refuse to harm the person, to have تَقْوَى of Allah, and to recognize just how destructive حَسَد is. That is your foundation of knowledge.

Now he gives the عَمَلٌ — the practical work. The first thing is not to follow your هَوًى. الْهَوَى is caprice, whim, passion. The word comes from a root meaning "to fall," and it is related to the word for wind — هَوَاء. The nature of هَوًى is exactly like the nature of wind: it sweeps in, shakes everything, does its damage, and then dies down and moves on. Sometimes it rises in the self; sometimes it subsides. If you follow the هَوًى, you are following something unstable, something with no fixed foundation — unlike the عَقْل, which is ثَبِيتٌ, fixed and firm. حَقٌّ is ثَبِيتٌ. بَاطِل is something that يَزُولُ — something that passes away. Look at history and you can see it clearly: falsehoods rise and fall, rise and fall, rise and fall. حَقٌّ, by contrast, is always recognized for what it is by the people of حَقٌّ, no matter where or when. بَاطِل shifts from people to people; it has no foundation. So he is saying: do not follow your هَوًى. And it is worth noting that هَاوِيَة — one of the names of جَهَنَّمُ — is from the same root, because your هَوًى is what pulls you down into it. "أُمُّهُ هَاوِيَةٌ" — its mother is the abyss. So when he says to do the opposite of what your هَوًى demands, he then gives you a concrete example.

<!-- page 23 -->

"كَنَظِيرِهِ إِنْ كَانَ ضَرَّاً" — like benefiting the person when your هَوًى is urging you to harm him. When your نَفْس wants to cause harm, that is exactly the moment for مُجَاهَدَة: you do the opposite and actively benefit the person you feel envy toward. This is a crucial point. If you feel envy toward someone and you want to harm them, you go out and give them a gift. You do the very opposite of what the نَفْس is screaming at you to do. That goes against the نَفْس, earns reward with Allah, and simultaneously begins to dissolve the disease.

Or — another thing he mentions — "عَلَيْهِ حَيْثُ لَكَانَ ذَمًّا زَيَّنًا": praise him when your نَفْس is itching to say something bad about him. If someone you feel حَسَد toward is mentioned in a gathering and you feel the urge to say, "Oh, you know what he did?" — that is the moment to say something good about him instead. And there is always something good you can say about someone. Even about the worst of people, you can find something. So you choose. When you want to say something bad, say something good instead.

One effect of doing this is that it tends to soften that person's heart toward you. As their heart inclines toward you — and now obviously this is not guaranteed, especially if they carry their own حَسَد toward you and have not done this work on themselves — but generally, good people are inclined to love those who treat them well. If you do good toward that person, they will tend to respond in kind. And when they do, it changes something in your own heart as well. What سِيدِي أَحْمَد زَرُّوق is saying is: if you actually apply this, you will witness a transformation — in their heart and in yours.

Then he adds another dimension of knowledge: you should know that حَسَد harms the one who envies, not the one who is envied. In this world, the حَاسِدٌ is perpetually anxious and agitated. Why did he get that promotion? He didn't deserve it. He goes home and spends two hours complaining to his wife about how so-and-so got the position he should have had. He becomes consumed by the object of his envy. It is a disease of the heart that produces nothing but غَمٌّ — a persistent sadness — and it is completely pointless. There is absolutely no meaning in it. Envying another person is, at its core, an irrational act. The Arabs have a powerful saying: "الْحَاسِدُ لَا يَسُودُ" — the envious person never attains leadership. The very fact of the resentment prevents the حَاسِدٌ from ever accomplishing anything. A person free of envy, when he sees someone else doing something impressive, may be motivated to go out and do it himself. He does not sit there stewing over why that is happening for someone else and not for him. He simply acts. The one who envies, by contrast, will never achieve سِيَادَة.

One of the striking things about the Muslim world today is how much envy saturates it. When many Muslims look at Europeans and Americans — even those who dress it up in Islamic rhetoric about the evils of the West — the underlying reality for a great many of them is simply: they have the دُنْيَا and we do not. That is the root of the حَسَد. Or consider the case of those who got the oil wealth: you have people across the Arab world and beyond perpetually denouncing the Gulf Arabs — they are wasting the Muslims' money, it all belongs to the أُمَّة, and so on. But the honest question to put to those critics is: if oil had been discovered in their own country, would they have been distributing it as عَطَاءٌ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ across the Muslim world? That is the question they need to sit with. It is easy to condemn the wealthy when you have nothing. The real test is what you do when the دُنْيَا suddenly lands in your lap.

And in fairness, there is a strong case that the Gulf Arabs give far more than most other nations would in their position, because generosity is deeply woven into traditional desert Arab culture, and there are genuinely many extraordinarily generous people there. So the question worth asking yourself is: is your anger coming from resentment and حَسَد, or is it genuinely driven by an altruistic concern for wealth to be used properly? That is a real question, and it deserves an honest answer.

You can also look at the communist revolution. Communism is essentially institutionalized حَسَد. Marx's writing is saturated with resentment — it is entirely about taking back what rightfully belongs to "us" from the rich. Now, the wealthy love to hear this framing because it lets them dismiss any critique as mere envy. But from the perspective of the شَرِيعَة, both sides carry an obligation. The poor have an obligation not to envy the rich. The rich have an obligation not to belittle the poor. It goes both ways. The fact that one side is not fulfilling its obligation does not justify becoming a communist and trying to overturn the entire order. And nowhere in Islam does it say you must remain impoverished. You can go out and earn. But wealth does tend, historically, to concentrate in families — and that pattern persists even today. America is held up as the land of opportunity, where you can come, work eighty-hour weeks, open your grocery store or whatever it may be, grind hard enough, and acquire the دُنْيَا. We already covered in an earlier section why that path has its own pitfalls — because often the moment you finally acquire it all, you drop dead. But even so, the concentration of wealth is remarkable: in this country, well over ninety percent of the wealth sits in the hands of less than three percent of the population. What most people think of as wealthy — a millionaire with two, three, four million dollars — is not real wealth by that standard. And even Bill Gates, for all the attention he receives, was at one point worth only about thirty percent of what Rockefeller was worth in real terms. He was, in that sense, an upstart.

The point is that money is a دَوْلَة — it circulates among people — "بَيْنَ ٱلْأَغْنِيَآءِ" — and one of the most historically radical things Allah did in the قُرْآن was to legislate inheritance laws specifically designed to break monopolies. The شَرِيعَة inheritance system is built to disperse wealth. In traditional cultures, the firstborn son — the progenitor — inherited everything. In English culture, women could literally lose their homes if they lacked a male protector. That is one of Jane Austen's recurring themes: women watching everything slip away the moment their father died, because some uncle or distant male cousin inherited it all under a system that gave women nothing. Austen saw it as an injustice — even though she was primarily a comic writer, she recognized this as a real wrong done to women. In Islam, Allah broke that pattern entirely. A woman suddenly had her own inheritance. And what did that mean? When a woman married outside the family, wealth moved outside the family — which is precisely why wealthy families so often preferred to marry within the family, trying to keep the money consolidated.

But one can see that Islam genuinely attempts to break down the monopoly of wealth. Once that monopoly takes hold, you get what the قُرْآن calls a دَوْلَة — wealth circulating only among the rich — and that inevitably breeds resentment. In classical Islamic civilization, there was traditionally a very strong middle class. Where a strong middle class exists, social pressure is far less severe than in a society where, say, ten percent of the population holds all the wealth while ninety percent lives in poverty.

<!-- page 22 -->

That kind of extreme gap creates massive tension. When forty to sixty percent of people occupy a middle ground, it remains possible for those at the bottom to move upward, because the distance is not insurmountable. But when the gap is enormous, it breeds resentment and حَسَد.

So poor people begin to envy the rich, and at that point a society becomes ripe for something like the Communist Manifesto — and we know how many millions died in communist revolutions, all of it fueled by resentment. What is also striking about Marxism is that nowhere does Marx criticize industrialization or mass production itself. He never questions the technological society and the direction it was heading. Baudrillard wrote a fascinating book addressing precisely this blind spot in communist critique: the factories themselves are never challenged. Marx's answer was simply to hand the factories over to the workers who run them — but whether factory life is something we genuinely want for هُمَاn beings is a question he never asked.

That is an important thing to reflect on. Much of what people call righteous indignation is, in reality, nothing more than حَسَد — envy and resentment dressed up in moral language. This has played out visibly in the Muslim world: many communists of the 1970s and 80s became Islamists, simply swapping the vocabulary. The language of communist social justice was replaced with the language of Islamic social justice, but the underlying impulse was the same. Islam was being used as a vehicle for anger rooted not in sincerity toward Allah, but in dissatisfaction with one's worldly position. People must be genuinely wary of that — it is a subtle and treacherous thing.

He then makes an additional point: envy does not benefit you in the slightest, nor does it harm the one you envy. The person you resent is not going to lose his blessing because you feel envious of it. Allah gave it to him, and Allah will take it away — not you. So envy is, in the end, a complete waste of time and energy.

He then moves into the أَسْبَاب — the causes of حَسَد — which is particularly important, because if you understand the causes, you can recognize whether you are afflicted and know how to rid yourself of it.

The first cause he mentions is عَدَاوَة — enmity. When someone is your enemy, their good fortune infuriates you. Consider how many people who are loudly anti-American are simultaneously obsessed with American culture — the would-be militant wearing Nike shoes, a Mickey Mouse T-shirt, ordering hamburgers and milkshakes. American culture has spread globally with remarkable speed. People in Beijing line up at McDonald's. One has to ask honestly: is the anti-American sentiment rooted in a genuine perception of injustice and oppression, or is it simply envy of the fact that America happens to be the most powerful country in the world by worldly measures? People must be very honest with themselves about where their motivations actually lie. عَدَاوَة, then, is a cause of حَسَد. You may dislike a colleague at work, and so when good things happen to him, anger rises in you — that is حَسَد born of enmity.

The next cause is *تَخَايُل* — a kind of rivalrous competition. There is almost a darkly comic dimension to it, though it can turn vicious. *تَخَايُل* involves two or more people competing for the same object of desire. The clearest example is siblings competing for a parent's love and attention. A mother has five children but only so many hours in a day, so the children vie for her notice. There is a book — I believe by Frank Sulloway — called *Born to Rebel*, which is an extensive statistical study of birth order. Sulloway argues that personality is shaped significantly by this competition for parental attention. With sweeping theories one must always be cautious, but there is clearly something to it. First-borns tend to be less rebellious; later-borns, seeing the first-born enjoying all the advantages, are more inclined to challenge the status quo. Most of the historically rebellious figures in social history, he argues, were later-borns. It is an interesting study.

That is *تَخَايُل*. Another manifestation is students competing for a teacher's favour — the classic teacher's-pet dynamic. Another is what is called *دَاءُ الدَّرَا* — the disease of second wives. In a polygamous household, the husband's time is finite, and so the wives compete. This is precisely why Islamic law is so insistent on fairness between wives: whatever you give one, you must give the other, and the same applies to how one divides one's nights. Significantly, the Arabic word for a second wife, *دَرَا*, literally means harm. Whether or not it was the first wife who coined the term, the Arabs clearly understood something about the psychology of plural marriage — that the second wife introduces a kind of *دَرَا* into the life of the first. That is *تَخَايُل*.

The next cause is *تَكَبُّر* — arrogance. When someone arrogant sees another person getting ahead, he thinks: *he doesn't deserve that; I'm better than him.* This was precisely the attitude of figures like al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra and Abū Jahl toward the Prophet ﷺ. They called him *يَتِيمُ بَنِي هَاشِمٍ* — the orphan of بَنِي هَاشِمٍ — or *ابْنُ أَبِي كَبْشَةَ*, referring to his foster father's lineage. Their position was: he doesn't deserve the blessing of revelation from the heavens. *We* are more deserving. That is *تَكَبُّر*, which is fairly straightforward.

*تَعَزُّزٌ* is subtler. *تَعَزُّزٌ* is not about wanting the other person to fall — it is about a person being comfortable as long as things are equal. As long as we are on the same level, it is fine. But then you receive a promotion and I do not, and I begin to think: now he is going to look down on me. The equilibrium has shifted. So *تَعَزُّز* is an attempt to preserve one's sense of *عِزَّة*, one's dignity and self-worth. It is, at root, a manifestation of poor self-esteem. The person feels that their worth has been diminished by another's gain, and envy arises as an attempt to restore balance.

You can see this in the words of those early opponents of Islam who said, لَوْلَا نُزِّلَ هَذَا ٱلْقُرْآنُ عَلَىٰ رَجُلٍۢ مِّنَ ٱلْقَرْيَتَيْنِ عَظِيمٍ — "Why was this قُرْآن not revealed to a great man from one of the two cities?" In other words, had it been revealed to al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra, they could have accepted it — bowing to him would not have disturbed them because he was already their superior. But this is the orphan of بَنِي هَاشِمٍ. How can he be above us? They wanted the equilibrium preserved. And similarly those who said, هَٰذَا بَشَرٌ مِّثْلُنَا — "This is just a man like us." How can he be a prophet while we are not? If it were a king, they could accept it — a king is already above them. But their next-door neighbour? He should have nothing that they do not have. And Pharaoh's contempt for Moses carries a similar logic — how can a prophet come from a people who are our slaves?

*تَعَجُّب* is different. It is not *تَكَبُّر* and not *تَعَزُّز* — it is more a kind of stunned disbelief. *I cannot believe this is happening.* This person won the jackpot? I have known him my whole life. It does not make sense. The حَسَد here is rooted in a kind of incredulity — an inability to accept that this particular person could have received such a blessing.

Then there is *حُبُّ الرِّيَاسَةِ* — love of leadership and the desire to maintain one's position of authority. A person may not want another to receive a blessing precisely because it would disturb the hierarchy, shifting the balance of authority between them. This is similar to *تَكَبُّر* but distinct: the person either already holds a position of authority over the other or desires to, and another's gain threatens that arrangement.

And finally, *شُحُّ النَّفْسِ* — covetousness. This too is a cause of envy. You see that someone has something and a craving seizes you — you simply want it. Allah speaks to this when He says, وَمَن يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِۦ فَأُو۟لَٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْمُفْلِحُونَ — those who are saved from the *شُحٌّ* of their own souls, those are the ones who truly succeed.

Now, one of the concepts Imam al-Ghazālī returns to repeatedly is that these diseases are woven into هُمَاn nature, and so the task is not simply to eliminate them but to transform them — to redirect them into something beneficial. He draws on a parallel: certain Native American tribes would give their children names associated with physical disability, like Lame Deer. The reasoning was that animals with disabilities tend to overcompensate, becoming more alert and more resourceful than their peers. The disability becomes an advantage. In worldly terms, this is what genuinely successful people do with their weaknesses. And what Imam al-Ghazālī is saying is that this is precisely what those seeking the next life must do with the darker aspects of their character — turn liabilities into assets.

The Prophet ﷺ said, لَا حَسَدَ إِلَّا فِي اثْنَتَيْنِ — "There is no envy except in two cases": رَجُلٍ آتَاهُ اللَّهُ مَالًا — a man whom Allah has given wealth — فَسَلَّطَهُ عَلَى هَلَكَتِهِ فِي الْحَقِّ — and he deploys it entirely in the service of what is right. One narration adds that he gives it out by night and by day. That is a worthy object of envy — not envy that you want him to lose his blessing, but *غِيبَة*, the positive longing to be like him, to have wealth so that you too can give. This is called *غِيبَة* in Arabic, and it is distinct from حَسَد. In *غِيبَة* you want the other person to keep what they have; you simply wish you had the same. The second case is رَجُلٍ آتَاهُ اللَّهُ حِكْمَةً — a man given wisdom — فَهُوَ يَقْضِي بِهَا وَيُعَلِّمُهَا — and he judges by it and teaches it to others. The message is clear: do not envy people over trivial worldly things — it is not worth it. Reserve your envy for what is truly high, what belongs to the next life. And when you do, that envy becomes a spur to action, driving you to achieve what they have achieved.

He then turns to the question of حَسَد directed at a disbeliever or a sinful Muslim — a *فَاجِر*, someone openly engaged in grave sins. In that case, he says, a form of حَسَد resembling *دَاءُ الدَّرَا* is actually permissible. The crucial distinction, however, is the reason. Earlier the point was made that envying non-Muslims simply because they enjoy worldly prosperity you do not is not a sound basis for حَسَد. What makes it permissible here is that they possess worldly power or resources they are actively using for harm and oppression. When that is the case, the feeling is justified.

He then addresses *الْحَيَاءُ الْمَذْمُومُ* — blameworthy modesty. Modesty itself is a virtue; the Prophet ﷺ said, الْحَيَاءُ مِنَ الْإِيمَانِ — حَيَاءٌ is from faith. But blameworthy modesty is a different thing entirely: it is the shyness that prevents you from speaking against wrongdoing when you witness it. You see a *مُنْكَرٌ* and you should say something, but you hold back out of embarrassment. A grown child, for instance, may feel that the modesty owed to parents prevents them from addressing a wrong the parents are committing — but a *مُنْكَرٌ* is a *مُنْكَرٌ* regardless of who is committing it.

A few important conditions attach to the obligation of *إِنْكَارُ الْمُنْكَرِ*. First, the *مُنْكَرٌ* must be *مُجْمَعٌ عَلَيْهِ* — agreed upon by scholars — before you can speak against it. You cannot make *إِنْكَار* of something that is *مُخْتَلَفٌ فِيهِ*, a matter of scholarly disagreement. Wherever difference of opinion exists, you must proceed with great caution, because what one scholar considers a *مُنْكَرٌ* another may consider permissible. It is said of Imam al-Māzirī that his knowledge was so vast he rarely made *إِنْكَار* of anyone, because he could almost always find a valid proof for their action somewhere in the tradition. This is something Muslims desperately need to hear today, given how freely they condemn one another.

Blameworthy modesty also includes the shyness that prevents a person from asking questions about the religion. This is why — as 'Ā'isha رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهَا remarked — the best women were those of the *أَمْصَار*, the great cities, because modesty did not stop them from seeking knowledge of the دِينٌ. She was praising them: despite the sensitivity of certain topics, it did not deter them. The Prophet ﷺ himself once had a woman come to him asking about menstruation; she kept pressing with questions until eventually he asked 'Ā'isha to show her what to do, because the subject had become difficult for him to address directly.

And 'Ā'isha could answer the question because she was a فَقِيهًا. But the point is that the woman wanted to know. Some women would even send, in a small box, a cloth from their menstrual period to ask 'Ā'isha about its ruling — the حُكْم — regarding color, to make sure they understood when the حَيْض began and ended.

Obviously, most women would not feel comfortable doing that at all, given their modesty. But those women were so firmly committed to doing what Allah commanded that their modesty did not prevent them. This tendency runs stronger in women than in men, but it applies to both — there are men who are so modest that they refrain from asking questions they ought to ask.

Then he said — and this is from the line of poetry — *أَمَّا حَيَاءٌ أُخْرَى مِنْ كَمَا جَرَى، لِرْمُسْ صَفَا إِسْ زَيْنَبًا تَكَمْ مَرَى* — as for the حَيَاءٌ of generosity, an example of it is what occurred with مُصْطَفَى ﷺ when he married Zaynab. *تَكَمْ مَرَا* refers to spending the night — that is, the first night of their marriage.

What happened was that the Prophet ﷺ had married Zaynab رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهَا, and he invited people over for a وَلِيمَة, a wedding celebration. The guests came, ate, and then some of them simply did not leave. Three men stayed behind, and the Prophet ﷺ — generous as he was — sat with them. But he had just married, and it was his wedding night. He was waiting for them to leave, while they, for their part, were savoring his company and did not want to go.

So he got up and left the room, hoping to signal to them that it was time to leave. He returned, and they were still there. He sat down again, then eventually left once more. It was at that point that the آيَةٌ was revealed — instructing believers to leave when they had finished their business, not to linger, to move on. The verse essentially said: when you finish eating, go.

That kind of حَيَاءٌ is not blameworthy, because there was no شَرْعِيّ obligation being neglected — no حُكْمٌ شَرْعِيٌّ at stake. It was purely out of his كَرَمٌ, his noble generosity, that he did not simply say, "It is my wedding night; please go now." Some people would have had no trouble saying that at all. But it was not in his nature to do so, ﷺ, and so the point is that this is not a blameworthy form of modesty.

*وَأَشْوَىٰ الْقَلَمُ الْوَلِيمَةِ* — he had fed them. *وَخَرَجُوا عَنِ الْحِسِّ وَالثَّرَثَةِ* — and they left, except for three. *لَا بُوَّ وَلَا مُؤْتَمَرَ بِالْبَلَاخِ، فَهُوَ مِنْ مَحَاسِنِ الْأَخْلَاقِ* — they stayed, and he did not tell them to leave, and that is from good character. It is not blameworthy.

Then he said — referring to a line of poetry — *كَانَ رَجُلًا صَرِيحًا*, and it echoes a hadith: *لَوْ كَانَ الْحَيَاءُ رَجُلًا، لَكَانَ رَجُلًا صَالِحًا* — if modesty were a man, he would be a righteous man. The Prophet ﷺ used this expression to convey that modesty is among the highest of qualities.

Now the text turns to the heart's engagement in matters that do not concern it — the subject of الْخَلْدُ.

*خَلْدُ الْقَلْبِ* is the movement or stirring of the heart. He will go into considerable detail later about the various types of thoughts, and we will get to that إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ. What he establishes here is that if the heart moves and dwells upon something prohibited, then that dwelling itself is prohibited. This is what is called *تَخَيُّل* — not *تَفَكُّر*. *تَفَكُّر* is reflection on intelligible, rational matters, while *تَخَيُّل* is mental imaging of sensory things, things perceived through the senses.

He says: *الْخَلْدُ، حَيْثُ مَا كَانَ، إِنَّمَا يَحْرُمُ وَحَيْثُ كَانَ فِيمَا حَرُمَ* — wherever the heart's movement lands, it becomes prohibited when it lands on what is حَرَامٌ. He gives an example: *كَالْفِكْرِ فِي مَحَاسِنِ الْأَجَانِبِ* — reflecting on or fantasizing about the attractive qualities of those to whom one is not married, whether male or female. Allowing the mind to dwell on what it should not is itself considered prohibited.

Also included in this is dwelling on the weaknesses and faults of Muslims, whether they are absent or present. Simply sitting and mentally cataloguing the faults of other Muslims is something one should not do. There is a saying: there is a tree in paradise for the one who is so preoccupied with his own faults that he forgets the faults of others. His own self-examination keeps him too busy to scrutinize everyone else.

The older you get, the more seriously you should take that. Life is simply too short. Everyone has faults. If you spend your time thinking about other people's faults, you are a fool — the whole point is to rid yourself of your own.

Also worth noting — because it appears in the commentary — is that reflecting on the nature of Allah's Essence, the *ذَاتٌ* of Allah, is حَرَامٌ. The *ذَاتٌ* of Allah is beyond the reach of هُمَاn reflection, and one cannot think about it in any meaningful or permissible sense.

I will go until 2:30 إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, and then we can pray.

The next topic: *وَأَصْلُ الْخَوْفِ مِنَ الْفَقْرِ سُوءُ الظَّنِّ بِهِ تَعَالَى وَدَوَافِرِ حُسْنِهِ وَأَنْعُمِهِ أَنَّمَا دَرَّهُ يَعْقِلُ وَأَنَّمَا تُرْسَكَهُ لَكَ يَصُولُ* — the root of the fear of poverty is a bad opinion of Allah.

Fear of poverty is one of the diseases of the heart. In the قُرْآن, Allah tells us that شَيْطَان promises you poverty: *يَعِدُكُمُ ٱلْفَقْرَ* — he promises you that you will lose your wealth. That is one of شَيْطَان tools.

This fear is particularly alive right now. We are living through a time of real *خَوْفُ الْفَقْرِ*, with uncertainty spreading across the world — collapses in various places, Americans growing anxious about markets, portfolios shrinking, and so on. But the truth is, the people worrying about this have little idea what real poverty looks like. If you want to see poverty, go to parts of India, West Africa, or places like Kosovo — there you will see it plainly. Most people speaking of poverty have never encountered it.

Fear of poverty is, at its root, a bad opinion of Allah — because Allah has promised to provide for you. In Arabic, this is a *جُمْلَةٌ شَرْطِيَّةٌ*, a conditional sentence: if you have تَقْوَى, I will provide for you. That is a direct promise from Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى. If you have تَقْوَى, He will provide, and He will do it from sources you would never expect — not necessarily your job or your monthly salary, but from wherever He wills. The condition, however, is تَقْوَى.

Without تَقْوَى, Allah is not obligated to care for you. If you are not fulfilling what He has asked of you as a condition of walking this earth with dignity, you will walk it in humiliation. This is especially true for Muslims, who should always remember that the دُنْيَا is the paradise of the non-believer. Whatever good they have is all they are getting. Let them enjoy it — seriously, reflect on that. This is all they are getting.

There is a story whose meaning is sound, even if scholars differ on exactly who it is attributed to. There was a Jewish man in Cairo who sold oil — he also heated oil and sold it hot. He was covered in oil, his clothes were dirty, and he was poor. One day a famous and wealthy عَالِمٌ rode out on a white horse, well-dressed and impressive. This Jewish man approached the horse and said: do you narrate the hadith that says this world is the paradise of the disbeliever and the prison of the believer? The scholar said yes. The man said: then I have a question — what kind of paradise am I in, and what kind of prison are you in?

The scholar looked up, then looked down. Then he said to him: when I contemplate what awaits you in the next world because of your disbelief, I can only conclude that you are in paradise right now. And when I contemplate what Allah has prepared for me in the next world because of my belief, I can only see this life as a prison. According to the story, the man then said, *أَشْهَدُ أَنْ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ*. But the point, regardless, is that everything is relative.

Poverty is a relative condition. And the fear of poverty is also a foolish thing, because what benefit does it bring? The American humorist Mark Twain said that most of the problems he worried about never actually came to pass. You build up all these concerns in your mind, and they never materialize — they exist only in your head. That is part of what makes fear of poverty so corrosive. And if you are already impoverished, what are you afraid of? That is simply your state. But if you have money and live in constant fear of losing it, what is the point of having it? The anxiety it generates may be worse than having nothing.

There is a hadith: *الهَمُّ نِصْفُ الهَرَمِ* — anxiety is half of aging. And another: *الْبِرُّ يَزِيدُ الْعُمْرَ* — righteousness lengthens your life. One interpretation of this that I find particularly striking is that righteous people do not carry anxiety. People who are irreligious live in a constant inner turmoil; their hearts are never at ease, so they age faster. They wear out sooner than others. Their lives dissipate more quickly.

So fear of poverty is a waste of time and energy.

The cure for it is to have a good opinion of Allah. Allah says — and this is the meaning — I do not want them to feed Me or provide for Me. Allah says that. He expects nothing of that kind from you. He then describes Himself as *ٱلرَّزَّاقُ ذُو ٱلْقُوَّةِ ٱلْمَتِينُ* — the Provider, the Possessor of enduring strength. *رَزَّاق* is a form of intensive exaggeration: always providing, endlessly providing. So Allah is the Provider — have a good opinion of Him.

Then the text says: know that giving does not diminish Allah's dominion. His *مُلْك* is not reduced by what He distributes. If He makes someone wealthy, that does not take anything from His kingdom. This is also why you should not harbor حَسَد toward someone who has been given wealth — just as Allah made that person wealthy, He can make you wealthy. But the foundation should be contentment. That is why the Prophet ﷺ said: *القَنَاعَةُ كَنزٌ لَا يَفْنَى* — contentment is a treasure that is never exhausted.

Know also that what has been allotted for you will reach you. There is a difference of opinion among the scholars about the nature of *رِزْق*. Some hold that the *رِزْق* written for you — which was decreed in your mother's womb — consists only of what you will actually consume and use in this life. Others say it is everything you possess. The dominant opinion, however, is that it is only what you use. If you die leaving behind a million dollars you never touched, that wealth was not your *رِزْق* — it was the *رِزْق* of those who inherited it. If you owned a house you never once visited, it was not your *رِزْق*, even though you held legal title to it. You derived no benefit from it, so it was not decreed for you.

I came across a book recently titled along the lines of *How to Live Well* — or more bluntly, the idea that you should live wealthy and die broke. Just spend it all. That is, of course, a problematic notion from an Islamic standpoint, because Islam encourages thinking about your children and those who come after you. It is praiseworthy to leave something behind. The Prophet ﷺ himself did not leave behind worldly goods — but that is because prophets are not permitted to leave the دُنْيَا as inheritance. That is their particular station.

The next disease is *مُدَاهَنَة*. This is an important one.

A person who uses his religion as a means of securing worldly benefit is a sycophantic hypocrite — a *مُدَاهِنٌ*. In his transaction, he has been cheated in the end. *مُدَاهَنَة* is when you leverage your دِينٌ to fix your دُنْيَا.

Ibn Khaldūn put it plainly: we try to stitch our دُنْيَا together by tearing our دِينٌ apart. The result, he said, is that we end up with neither — our دِينٌ is gone, and our دُنْيَا was never actually repaired. Because Allah will not rectify your دُنْيَا through the destruction of your دِينٌ. He will not grant *تَوْفِيق* in that transaction. You lose both.

*مُدَاهَنَة*, practically speaking, is when you fail to speak against something wrong because it involves your business, your boss, your livelihood — some worldly stake. You perform a kind of religious hypocrisy out of fear of losing a worldly benefit. That is the essence of it. Now, if something is genuinely none of your business, or it is not *وَاجِب* upon you to act — and in many cases it is not — then you have a choice, and staying silent may be perfectly acceptable. But *مُدَاهَنَة* is when you actually sacrifice your دِينٌ because of your love of the دُنْيَا. That is where the line is crossed.

There is a related concept worth distinguishing: *مُدَارَاةٌ*. The root of *مُدَاهَنَة* is *دُهْن* — oil, like the oil used in cosmetics — something that covers and conceals. It implies a cosmetic covering of something rotten, and scholars have described it as a form of spiritual deception.

Shaykh ʿAlī al-ʿAdawī says in *الْخَاتِمَة*: *مُدَاهَنَة* is when a man surrenders his religion in order to protect his wealth or his life — sitting with people of disobedience, keeping their company, praising their actions, displaying approval of their condition, and offering no condemnation, all for some worldly gain. That is حَرَامٌ.

<!-- page 28 -->

*مُدَارَاةٌ*, by contrast, is expending something of your worldly life — your دُنْيَا — for the sake of your آخِرَة, or for some legitimate worldly benefit, or for both. *أُدَارِي* them, keep the peace, as long as you do not compromise your دِينٌ. The saying goes: *دَارِهِمْ مَا دُمْتَ فِي دَارِهِمْ، وَحَيِّهِمْ مَا دُمْتَ فِي حَيِّهِمْ، وَرَاعِهِمْ مَا دُمْتَ فِي أَرْضِهِمْ* — greet them and get along with them as long as you are among them, and do not make unnecessary enemies. That is permissible. It is not obligatory, but it is permissible.

If it costs your دِينٌ, it is *مُدَاهَنَة*. If it does not cost your دِينٌ, it is *مُدَارَاةٌ*, and there is nothing wrong with it.

A small example: my brother-in-law had just arrived from Morocco and drove forty-five minutes to reach an office, only to be told he needed an appointment he did not have. He asked if anything could be done, and the clerk said no. He said something along the lines of, "That's America, I suppose," and I told him: do not say that. The way people will hear that is "go back where you came from," and most Americans will take it exactly that way. *مُدَارَاةٌ* would have been to stay calm, be gracious, and make an appointment — because you might very well need to return.

Now she remembers that you said that, so she doesn't write it down in the appointment book. When you show up, there's no record of your visit. *مُدَارَاةٌ*, then, covers situations like that — where it is permissible, even though you would rather say something else, to say something pleasant as a means of securing a worldly benefit, or of avoiding a worldly harm, or both.

It can also apply to someone who wants to help another person, and it is not considered *نِفَاق*. This is not hypocrisy. Islam recognizes that there are things in the world one simply has to navigate, and this is a *رُخْصَة* — a dispensation — for people.

Another example is using whatever influence you have on someone else's behalf. If you are close to a minister and someone has been wronged, and you are in a position to get in and help that person, that is a *مُدَارَاةٌ*. Your relationship with the minister becomes a means of obtaining justice for someone else.

All of these are praiseworthy in Islam. By contrast, the kind of culture that prevails in some circles today — the "I'm okay, you're okay" attitude — is مُدَاهَنَة. People do wrong, and everyone says it is fine, there is nothing wrong with it.

A conspiracy of silence takes hold. In Islam, by contrast, a believer is the mirror of his brother or sister — he wants to see them improve, and so he acts accordingly.

The sheikh now turns to the root causes of the next disease. He says its origin is طَمَعٌ — base desire — and doing works of worship in order to earn a place in the hearts of others. That is called رِيَاءٌ.

رِيَاءٌ is performing an action that is a قُرْبَى — something that draws one near to Allah — but doing it for other than Allah's sake. Whoever does that is called a مُرَائِي. The Prophet ﷺ called it a شِرْك, specifically الشِّرْكُ الْأَصْغَرُ — the lesser polytheism.

In a hadith, the Prophet ﷺ said that he does not fear for his community that they will worship the sun, the stars, or the moon, but that he fears for them that they will worship other than Allah through رِيَاءٌ. And in another hadith: "The thing I fear most for my أُمَّة is doing things for other than the sake of Allah." That is رِيَاءٌ.

Now, if someone is a skilled archer, for instance, and people are watching, and someone else arrives and that person suddenly performs with extra effort to impress them — that is مَذْمُومٌ مُرُوءَةً، لَا شَرْعًا. In other words, it is blameworthy in terms of هُمَاn dignity and comportment, but we must distinguish between شَرِيعَة and مُرُوءَة. مُرُوءَةٌ refers to the هُمَاn dignity a person is expected to preserve. Showing off in a non-religious context is blameworthy, but it is not the شِرْك with Allah that religious رِيَاءٌ constitutes.

So the source of رِيَاءٌ is desire — طَمَعٌ. You want people to think well of you; you want a place in their hearts. The sheikh says his cure for طَمَعٌ is the same as his cure for رِيَاءٌ. "Roll up your sleeves," he says — get to work and set out on the cure for the root of all these diseases.

طَمَعٌ and رِيَاءٌ go together, because رِيَاءٌ is itself a form of طَمَعٌ: you are desiring something from people rather than from Allah.

He says that what he means by this — what he calls the head of the matter, the عَقْدُ الأَوْعِيَةِ — is showing off in good works, رِيَاءٌ. The Arabs use the expression عَقْدُ الأَوْعِيَةِ for the one who begins something, the one who raises the banner at the outset.

Now comes something important. He identifies three signs of رِيَاءٌ.

The first is laziness and a diminishment of action when one is alone with Allah. You have no energy to read the قُرْآن in private, but when you are in the مَسْجِد surrounded by people, the energy appears from nowhere. The second is the opposite — enthusiastic effort and abundant action when people are present. The third is increasing one's acts of worship when praised and diminishing them when praise is absent. A person wants to hear others say, "سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ, that man is always praying — it is incredible." And inside, he thinks: *What about my fasting? Do they know about that too?*

There is something worth noting here. Even though a heightened sense of energy in the company of others can be a sign of رِيَاءٌ, it is not always so. There is something to be said for the effect of good company — seeing another person worship can stir you to worship as well, and that impulse may not come from رِيَاءٌ but simply from a kind of esprit de corps, a collective energy that lifts everyone. So that is not necessarily negative. The danger is when the driving force is not Allah but the audience.

On the matter of praise: behavioral psychology has built entire systems on exactly this — rewards and incentives to motivate people. In the شَرِيعَة, there is certainly room for encouragement. The Prophet ﷺ, when he saw people do good, would affirm them. And there is a hadith that says when a believer hears someone praising him, it increases him in إِيمَان — it makes him want to do more for Allah. So praise produces the opposite effect in a sound heart: it redirects the person toward Allah rather than away from Him.

The distinction the sheikh is drawing is this: the dangerous thing is to perform acts of worship *for* the praise. That is what he is warning against.

There is also a شَرِيعَة distinction between مَدْح — flattery, saying what is not true — and ثَنَاءٌ — genuine acknowledgment of someone's good. Affirming people for what they actually do is not prohibited; in fact, it is encouraged. The danger lies inside the heart of the one who *craves* the praise. That is what a person must work on, and a cure is coming.
