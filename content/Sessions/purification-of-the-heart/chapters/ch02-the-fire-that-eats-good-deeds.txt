Now, *حَسَدٌ*. We ended on حَسَدٌ last time. Hasad is probably one of the most serious diseases. There is a difference of opinion among scholars about which disease is the root of all others: some say حَسَدٌ is the root disease. I would argue it always traces back to *هُمَا* — desire — because within حَسَدٌ there is always an underlying desire. So ultimately it goes back to هُمَا. Or to *حُبُّ الْجَاهِ*, as Ibn 'Ashraf suggests — though even Ibn 'Ashraf admits that the source of all wrong is *حُبُّ الْعَاجِلَةِ*, love of the immediate world, which again connects to desire.

According to the scholars, حَسَدٌ is the first disease, the first manifestation of disobedience in the heavens. The first act of disobedience toward Allah in the heavenly realm was حَسَدٌ — because nothing prevented Iblis from prostrating except حَسَدٌ. The particular form is called *تَزُزّ*: he could not bear that Adam, peace be upon him, would hold a higher station than him. He could not endure it. It is also connected to *تَكَبُّر* — he was filled with arrogance and pride. The حَسَدٌ and the arrogance were intertwined.

There is a hadith in which the Prophet ﷺ said that حَسَدٌ devours good deeds the way fire devours dry wood. If you have ever watched fire consume dry wood, you should let that image tell you what happens to your *حَسَنَاتٌ*. The person who harbors envy is called *الْحَسِدُ*, and the object of his envy is called *المَحْسُودُ*. In the verse of the Quran that people should commit to memory: *‏وَمِن شَرِّ حَاسِدٍ إِذَا حَسَدَ ‎* — the *حَاسِد* referred to is shaytan, *الْحَسِدُ*. To harbor envy is to embody one of the attributes of shaytan.

There is also a hadith in which the Prophet ﷺ said: *كُلُّ ذِي نِعْمَةٍ مَحْسُودٌ* — every possessor of a blessing, any blessing at all, is envied. There will always be someone who envies you for whatever you have. Consider two street sweepers: one has only his brooms to carry; the other has a donkey pulling his cart. To you, this is entirely trivial. But the one without the donkey looks at the one with the donkey and envies him. Whatever the level, however small the difference, envy operates. It is a strange and pervasive force that runs through هُمَاn culture and society.

It is also understood that envy carries real harm — *مَضَرَّة*. But what the scholars say is that the envy of the *حَاسِد* harms him before it harms the object of his envy. He is damaged by it faster than he can damage the one he envies.

The evil eye is generally connected to envy, though it does not require envy. Some people simply have a powerful gaze — a kind of psychic force. It does not necessarily mean they envy; it can operate independently, though it is still something to take seriously. Across many Muslim cultures, traditionally, a firstborn male child would have his ears pierced and be dressed as a girl for the first several years of life, precisely out of fear of the evil eye. This was widespread practice. I knew an older man who had holes in his ears, and when I asked him, that was the explanation.

In contemporary Western culture, much of this is dismissed as superstition. But it is striking that every culture in the world has some concept of the evil eye. Every one. The Latin root of the English word *invidious* — meaning to envy — literally means to look with evil. The evil eye is an evil glance. The Chinese have a concept of it as well and employ various ritual protections against it; you will often see mirrors on Chinese houses, placed there to deflect the evil eye. This is a universal هُمَاn belief. And when a belief is universal, it usually has some foundation — it is not simply a shared fantasy.

The anthropologist might say: things happen to people, and when they cannot explain why, they reach for explanations like the evil eye. It becomes a convenient way of accounting for the inexplicable. Someone falls ill for no discernible reason, no one can figure it out, and people say it might be the eye. So that dynamic exists too.

But the hadith *الْعَيْنُ حَقٌّ* — the evil eye is real — is a sound, صَحِيحٌ hadith. And it is worth considering its source. The Prophet ﷺ was someone who actively worked to dismantle superstition. When the moon eclipsed on the day his son Ibrahim died, the Arabs believed a lunar eclipse meant a great person had died. It was a perfect moment for a charlatan to exploit — and in fact, people became Muslim that day precisely because they thought the moon had eclipsed for his son. He went out and announced to the people: the sun and the moon are signs of Allah; they do not eclipse for anyone's death or life. He dispelled that superstition on the spot.

So when a man of that character said *الْعَيْنُ حَقٌّ* in a sound hadith, he was telling us that there is something genuinely real in the world called the evil eye, and it deserves to be taken seriously. In English, people say, "if looks could kill" — there is a folk intuition that a gaze carries force, that something passes between people through the eyes. So: the hadith establishes that حَسَدٌ devours good deeds the way fire devours dry wood. And the reason for that becomes clear when he explains it: if you desire that someone lose a blessing they possess, you are envious of them. It can be anything — a house, a car, a job. Someone received a promotion over you and you wish they would fail, get fired, so that you might take their position. Someone has a spouse you envy, and you think they do not deserve that person and you wish the marriage would fall apart. The variations are endless, but at the core of every one of them is the desire for the *نِعْمَ* — the blessing — to be lost. And *نِعْمَ*, from the Arabic root *نِعْمَ*, relates to softness, ease — it is the opposite of hardship.

So نِعْمَ are things that ease life — that is what a نِعْمَ is. Now, obviously, a blessing can be hidden inside what looks like a hardship, and a hardship can be hidden inside what looks like a blessing. In English we say "a blessing in disguise" or "every cloud has a silver lining" — expressions that remind us to be careful about judging what is and is not a blessing. A نِعْمَ is something Allah gives, and Allah's name is الْمُنْعِم: the Giver of blessings. If Allah gives a person a blessing and you wish that person would lose it, you are essentially saying Allah was wrong to place that blessing with them. You are passing judgment on Allah's wisdom.

One of my favorite stories — and some people have heard this — concerns الأَصْمَعِيُّ, who used to travel the desert encampments collecting words and lore. He was once at a Bedouin's tent, and, as is part of Bedouin culture then and now, the women came in, served, and sat. This man had a remarkably beautiful wife, and he himself was remarkably ugly. When the man stepped out to slaughter a sheep in الأَصْمَعِيُّ's honor, الأَصْمَعِيُّ could not resist asking the woman: "How did you end up with him? How did a woman as beautiful as you come to be with a man who looks like that?" The woman said — and الأَصْمَعِيُّ is the one relating this, which tells you something about the kind of man he was — "اِتَّقِ اللَّهَ," fear Allah. Then she said: "لَعَلَّهُ أَحْسَنَ إِلَى رَبِّهِ وَأَنَا جَزَاؤُهُ" — perhaps he did good things with his Lord, and I am his reward. Or perhaps I did some bad things, and he is my punishment.

The point الأَصْمَعِيُّ was making is that Allah has a حِكْمَة, and you do not know what it is. So you must be very careful before asking, "Why is that person with that person?" or "What is that doing there?" Allah is الْمُنْعِم, the Giver of blessings, and when you question the نِعْمَ, you are questioning the One who gave it. That is why Allah hates حَسَد — and it is precisely why شَيْطَانٌ fell into disobedience.

The difference between شَيْطَانٌ and Adam, عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ, is that when Adam made his mistake, he made تَوْبَة. The حَاسِدٌ does not make تَوْبَة — cannot make تَوْبَة — because part of his problem is كِبْرٌ. He believes he is more worthy and that the other person does not deserve what they have. That is تَكَبُّر: "I am better than him; he does not deserve what he has." A person in that state finds it nearly impossible to admit he is wrong, so he never turns away from his حَسَد. He simply does not turn away from it.

So when the author says that the true meaning of حَسَد is that if you could, through some scheme, eliminate that blessing yourself, you would — that is the شَرِيعَة definition he is pointing toward. There is a حَدِيث: "لَا يَسْلَمُ الْمُؤْمِنُ مِنْ ثَلَاثٍ" — the believer will never be entirely free of three things — and حَسَد is one of them. The other two are طِيَرَة and ظَنٌّ, which he will address later. Since no believer is ever fully safe from حَسَد, the cure prescribed is: لَا تَبْرِي — do not act on it. The حَدِيث says: if you feel envy, do not harm the person, do not oppress them. As long as you do not actually do something to cause that person to lose their blessing, your حَسَد does not fall into the category that destroys your حَسَنَاتٌ. The حَسَد that devours your good deeds is the حَسَد where, if you were able, you would actually cause the person to lose that blessing — "وَرَأَى الِابْتِلَاءَ" — and then act on it.

Then the author says: if your fear of Allah is what holds you back from harming the person, then that is not the kind of حَسَد that will damage you. But if you would act on it given the chance — that is dangerous. He then attributes this distinction to إِمَامُ الْغَزَالِيِّ, رَحِمَهُ اللّٰهُ, and that is the basis of his judgment here.

Then he says that إِمَامُ الْغَزَالِيِّ also said: if you hate the envy you feel — if you despise that part of yourself that is generating it — then that is a sign that you are not truly an envious person. Hating the experience of envy within yourself is evidence that you are not an envious person at your core. This again points to the recognition that you have a نَفْس and a رُوح — which are, in a real sense, the same thing, except that the نَفْس is the رُوح that still carries the tarnish of this world; it has not been purified. Recognizing that these stirrings in the heart are part of that taint — part of the رَانَ that accumulates over the heart through wrong actions and through being immersed in this world — and then detesting those stirrings: that is a sign that the رُوح, the pure aspect of yourself, is the stronger force within you.

Then he turns to the دَوَاءٌ — the treatment — and as always he gives us two. The rectification of the heart requires two things: knowledge and action. He has already given the knowledge base: you must understand that the path out of حَسَد is to refuse to harm the person, to have تَقْوَى of Allah, and to recognize just how destructive حَسَد is. That is your foundation of knowledge.

Now he gives the عَمَل — the practical work. The first thing is not to follow your هَوًى. الْهَوَى is caprice, whim, passion. The word comes from a root meaning "to fall," and it is related to the word for wind — هَوَاء. The nature of هَوًى is exactly like the nature of wind: it sweeps in, shakes everything, does its damage, and then dies down and moves on. Sometimes it rises in the self; sometimes it subsides. If you follow the هَوًى, you are following something unstable, something with no fixed foundation — unlike the عَقْل, which is تَثْبِيت, fixed and firm. حَقٌّ is تَثْبِيت. بَاطِل is something that يَزُولُ — something that passes away. Look at history and you can see it clearly: falsehoods rise and fall, rise and fall, rise and fall. حَقٌّ, by contrast, is always recognized for what it is by the people of حَقٌّ, no matter where or when. بَاطِل shifts from people to people; it has no foundation. So he is saying: do not follow your هَوًى. And it is worth noting that هَاوِيَة — one of the names of جَهَنَّم — is from the same root, because your هَوًى is what pulls you down into it. "أُمُّهُ هَاوِيَةٌ" — its mother is the abyss. So when he says to do the opposite of what your هَوًى demands, he then gives you a concrete example.

"كَنَظِيرِهِ إِنْ كَانَ ضَرَّاً" — like benefiting the person when your هَوًى is urging you to harm him. When your نَفْس wants to cause harm, that is exactly the moment for مُجَاهَدَة: you do the opposite and actively benefit the person you feel envy toward. This is a crucial point. If you feel envy toward someone and you want to harm them, you go out and give them a gift. You do the very opposite of what the نَفْس is screaming at you to do. That goes against the نَفْس, earns reward with Allah, and simultaneously begins to dissolve the disease.

Or — another thing he mentions — "عَلَيْهِ حَيْثُ لَكَانَ ذَمًّا زَيَّنًا": praise him when your نَفْس is itching to say something bad about him. If someone you feel حَسَد toward is mentioned in a gathering and you feel the urge to say, "Oh, you know what he did?" — that is the moment to say something good about him instead. And there is always something good you can say about someone. Even about the worst of people, you can find something. So you choose. When you want to say something bad, say something good instead.

One effect of doing this is that it tends to soften that person's heart toward you. As their heart inclines toward you — and now obviously this is not guaranteed, especially if they carry their own حَسَد toward you and have not done this work on themselves — but generally, good people are inclined to love those who treat them well. If you do good toward that person, they will tend to respond in kind. And when they do, it changes something in your own heart as well. What سِيدِي أَحْمَد زَرُّوق is saying is: if you actually apply this, you will witness a transformation — in their heart and in yours.

Then he adds another dimension of knowledge: you should know that حَسَد harms the one who envies, not the one who is envied. In this world, the حَاسِدٌ is perpetually anxious and agitated. Why did he get that promotion? He didn't deserve it. He goes home and spends two hours complaining to his wife about how so-and-so got the position he should have had. He becomes consumed by the object of his envy. It is a disease of the heart that produces nothing but غَمٌّ — a persistent sadness — and it is completely pointless. There is absolutely no meaning in it. Envying another person is, at its core, an irrational act. The Arabs have a powerful saying: "الْحَاسِدُ لَا يَسُودُ" — the envious person never attains leadership. The very fact of the resentment prevents the حَاسِدٌ from ever accomplishing anything. A person free of envy, when he sees someone else doing something impressive, may be motivated to go out and do it himself. He does not sit there stewing over why that is happening for someone else and not for him. He simply acts. The one who envies, by contrast, will never achieve سِيَادَة.

One of the striking things about the Muslim world today is how much envy saturates it. When many Muslims look at Europeans and Americans — even those who dress it up in Islamic rhetoric about the evils of the West — the underlying reality for a great many of them is simply: they have the دُنْيَا and we do not. That is the root of the حَسَد. Or consider the case of those who got the oil wealth: you have people across the Arab world and beyond perpetually denouncing the Gulf Arabs — they are wasting the Muslims' money, it all belongs to the أُمَّة, and so on. But the honest question to put to those critics is: if oil had been discovered in their own country, would they have been distributing it as عَطَاءٌ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ across the Muslim world? That is the question they need to sit with. It is easy to condemn the wealthy when you have nothing. The real test is what you do when the دُنْيَا suddenly lands in your lap.

And in fairness, there is a strong case that the Gulf Arabs give far more than most other nations would in their position, because generosity is deeply woven into traditional desert Arab culture, and there are genuinely many extraordinarily generous people there. So the question worth asking yourself is: is your anger coming from resentment and حَسَد, or is it genuinely driven by an altruistic concern for wealth to be used properly? That is a real question, and it deserves an honest answer.

You can also look at the communist revolution. Communism is essentially institutionalized حَسَد. Marx's writing is saturated with resentment — it is entirely about taking back what rightfully belongs to "us" from the rich. Now, the wealthy love to hear this framing because it lets them dismiss any critique as mere envy. But from the perspective of the شَرِيعَة, both sides carry an obligation. The poor have an obligation not to envy the rich. The rich have an obligation not to belittle the poor. It goes both ways. The fact that one side is not fulfilling its obligation does not justify becoming a communist and trying to overturn the entire order. And nowhere in Islam does it say you must remain impoverished. You can go out and earn. But wealth does tend, historically, to concentrate in families — and that pattern persists even today. America is held up as the land of opportunity, where you can come, work eighty-hour weeks, open your grocery store or whatever it may be, grind hard enough, and acquire the دُنْيَا. We already covered in an earlier section why that path has its own pitfalls — because often the moment you finally acquire it all, you drop dead. But even so, the concentration of wealth is remarkable: in this country, well over ninety percent of the wealth sits in the hands of less than three percent of the population. What most people think of as wealthy — a millionaire with two, three, four million dollars — is not real wealth by that standard. And even Bill Gates, for all the attention he receives, was at one point worth only about thirty percent of what Rockefeller was worth in real terms. He was, in that sense, an upstart.

The point is that money is a دَوَاءٌ — it circulates among people — "بَيْنَ ٱلْأَغْنِيَآءِ" — and one of the most historically radical things Allah did in the قُرْآن was to legislate inheritance laws specifically designed to break monopolies. The شَرِيعَة inheritance system is built to disperse wealth. In traditional cultures, the firstborn son — the progenitor — inherited everything. In English culture, women could literally lose their homes if they lacked a male protector. That is one of Jane Austen's recurring themes: women watching everything slip away the moment their father died, because some uncle or distant male cousin inherited it all under a system that gave women nothing. Austen saw it as an injustice — even though she was primarily a comic writer, she recognized this as a real wrong done to women. In Islam, Allah broke that pattern entirely. A woman suddenly had her own inheritance. And what did that mean? When a woman married outside the family, wealth moved outside the family — which is precisely why wealthy families so often preferred to marry within the family, trying to keep the money consolidated.

But one can see that Islam genuinely attempts to break down the monopoly of wealth. Once that monopoly takes hold, you get what the قُرْآن calls a *دَوَاءٌ* — wealth circulating only among the rich — and that inevitably breeds resentment. In classical Islamic civilization, there was traditionally a very strong middle class. Where a strong middle class exists, social pressure is far less severe than in a society where, say, ten percent of the population holds all the wealth while ninety percent lives in poverty.

That kind of extreme gap creates massive tension. When forty to sixty percent of people occupy a middle ground, it remains possible for those at the bottom to move upward, because the distance is not insurmountable. But when the gap is enormous, it breeds resentment and *حَسَدٌ*.

So poor people begin to envy the rich, and at that point a society becomes ripe for something like the Communist Manifesto — and we know how many millions died in communist revolutions, all of it fueled by resentment. What is also striking about Marxism is that nowhere does Marx criticize industrialization or mass production itself. He never questions the technological society and the direction it was heading. Baudrillard wrote a fascinating book addressing precisely this blind spot in communist critique: the factories themselves are never challenged. Marx's answer was simply to hand the factories over to the workers who run them — but whether factory life is something we genuinely want for هُمَاn beings is a question he never asked.

That is an important thing to reflect on. Much of what people call righteous indignation is, in reality, nothing more than *حَسَدٌ* — envy and resentment dressed up in moral language. This has played out visibly in the Muslim world: many communists of the 1970s and 80s became Islamists, simply swapping the vocabulary. The language of communist social justice was replaced with the language of Islamic social justice, but the underlying impulse was the same. Islam was being used as a vehicle for anger rooted not in sincerity toward Allah, but in dissatisfaction with one's worldly position. People must be genuinely wary of that — it is a subtle and treacherous thing.

He then makes an additional point: envy does not benefit you in the slightest, nor does it harm the one you envy. The person you resent is not going to lose his blessing because you feel envious of it. Allah gave it to him, and Allah will take it away — not you. So envy is, in the end, a complete waste of time and energy.

He then moves into the *أَسْبَاب* — the causes of *حَسَدٌ* — which is particularly important, because if you understand the causes, you can recognize whether you are afflicted and know how to rid yourself of it.

The first cause he mentions is *عَدَاوَة* — enmity. When someone is your enemy, their good fortune infuriates you. Consider how many people who are loudly anti-American are simultaneously obsessed with American culture — the would-be militant wearing Nike shoes, a Mickey Mouse T-shirt, ordering hamburgers and milkshakes. American culture has spread globally with remarkable speed. People in Beijing line up at McDonald's. One has to ask honestly: is the anti-American sentiment rooted in a genuine perception of injustice and oppression, or is it simply envy of the fact that America happens to be the most powerful country in the world by worldly measures? People must be very honest with themselves about where their motivations actually lie. *عَدَاوَة*, then, is a cause of *حَسَدٌ*. You may dislike a colleague at work, and so when good things happen to him, anger rises in you — that is *حَسَدٌ* born of enmity.

The next cause is *تَخَايُل* — a kind of rivalrous competition. There is almost a darkly comic dimension to it, though it can turn vicious. *تَخَايُل* involves two or more people competing for the same object of desire. The clearest example is siblings competing for a parent's love and attention. A mother has five children but only so many hours in a day, so the children vie for her notice. There is a book — I believe by Frank Sulloway — called *Born to Rebel*, which is an extensive statistical study of birth order. Sulloway argues that personality is shaped significantly by this competition for parental attention. With sweeping theories one must always be cautious, but there is clearly something to it. First-borns tend to be less rebellious; later-borns, seeing the first-born enjoying all the advantages, are more inclined to challenge the status quo. Most of the historically rebellious figures in social history, he argues, were later-borns. It is an interesting study.

That is *تَخَايُل*. Another manifestation is students competing for a teacher's favour — the classic teacher's-pet dynamic. Another is what is called *دَاءُ الدَّرَا* — the disease of second wives. In a polygamous household, the husband's time is finite, and so the wives compete. This is precisely why Islamic law is so insistent on fairness between wives: whatever you give one, you must give the other, and the same applies to how one divides one's nights. Significantly, the Arabic word for a second wife, *داراء*, literally means harm. Whether or not it was the first wife who coined the term, the Arabs clearly understood something about the psychology of plural marriage — that the second wife introduces a kind of *داراء* into the life of the first. That is *تَخَايُل*.

The next cause is *تَكَبُّر* — arrogance. When someone arrogant sees another person getting ahead, he thinks: *he doesn't deserve that; I'm better than him.* This was precisely the attitude of figures like الْوَلِيدُ بْنُ الْمُغِيرَةِ and أَبُو جَهْل toward the Prophet ﷺ. They called him *يَتِيمُ بَنِي هَاشِمٍ* — the orphan of بَنِي هَاشِمٍ — or *ابْنُ أَبِي كَبْشَةَ*, referring to his foster father's lineage. Their position was: he doesn't deserve the blessing of revelation from the heavens. *We* are more deserving. That is *تَكَبُّر*, which is fairly straightforward.

*تَعَزُّز* is subtler. *تَعَزُّز* is not about wanting the other person to fall — it is about a person being comfortable as long as things are equal. As long as we are on the same level, it is fine. But then you receive a promotion and I do not, and I begin to think: now he is going to look down on me. The equilibrium has shifted. So *تَعَزُّز* is an attempt to preserve one's sense of *عِزَّة*, one's dignity and self-worth. It is, at root, a manifestation of poor self-esteem. The person feels that their worth has been diminished by another's gain, and envy arises as an attempt to restore balance.

You can see this in the words of those early opponents of Islam who said, لَوْلَا نُزِّلَ هَذَا ٱلْقُرْآنُ عَلَىٰ رَجُلٍۢ مِّنَ ٱلْقَرْيَتَيْنِ عَظِيمٍ — "Why was this Quran not revealed to a great man from one of the two cities?" In other words, had it been revealed to al-Walīd ibn Mughīra, they could have accepted it — bowing to him would not have disturbed them because he was already their superior. But this is the orphan of بَنِي هَاشِمٍ. How can he be above us? They wanted the equilibrium preserved. And similarly those who said, هَٰذَا بَشَرٌ مِّثْلُنَا — "This is just a man like us." How can he be a prophet while we are not? If it were a king, they could accept it — a king is already above them. But their next-door neighbour? He should have nothing that they do not have. And Pharaoh's contempt for Moses carries a similar logic — how can a prophet come from a people who are our slaves?

*تَعَجُّب* is different. It is not *تَكَبُّر* and not *تَعَزُّز* — it is more a kind of stunned disbelief. *I cannot believe this is happening.* This person won the jackpot? I have known him my whole life. It does not make sense. The *حَسَدٌ* here is rooted in a kind of incredulity — an inability to accept that this particular person could have received such a blessing.

Then there is *حُبُّ الرِّيَاسَةِ* — love of leadership and the desire to maintain one's position of authority. A person may not want another to receive a blessing precisely because it would disturb the hierarchy, shifting the balance of authority between them. This is similar to *تَكَبُّر* but distinct: the person either already holds a position of authority over the other or desires to, and another's gain threatens that arrangement.

And finally, *شُحُّ النَّفْسِ* — covetousness. This too is a cause of envy. You see that someone has something and a craving seizes you — you simply want it. Allah speaks to this when He says, وَمَن يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِۦ فَأُو۟لَٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْمُفْلِحُونَ — those who are saved from the *شُحٌّ* of their own souls, those are the ones who truly succeed.

Now, one of the concepts Imam al-Ghazālī returns to repeatedly is that these diseases are woven into هُمَاn nature, and so the task is not simply to eliminate them but to transform them — to redirect them into something beneficial. He draws on a parallel: certain Native American tribes would give their children names associated with physical disability, like Lame Deer. The reasoning was that animals with disabilities tend to overcompensate, becoming more alert and more resourceful than their peers. The disability becomes an advantage. In worldly terms, this is what genuinely successful people do with their weaknesses. And what Imam al-Ghazālī is saying is that this is precisely what those seeking the next life must do with the darker aspects of their character — turn liabilities into assets.

The Prophet ﷺ said, لَا حَسَدَ إِلَّا فِي اثْنَتَيْنِ — "There is no envy except in two cases": رَجُلٍ آتَاهُ اللَّهُ مَالًا — a man whom Allah has given wealth — فَسَلَّطَهُ عَلَى هَلَكَتِهِ فِي الْحَقِّ — and he deploys it entirely in the service of what is right. One narration adds that he gives it out by night and by day. That is a worthy object of envy — not envy that you want him to lose his blessing, but *غِيبَة*, the positive longing to be like him, to have wealth so that you too can give. This is called *غِيبَة* in Arabic, and it is distinct from *حَسَدٌ*. In *غِيبَة* you want the other person to keep what they have; you simply wish you had the same. The second case is رَجُلٍ آتَاهُ اللَّهُ حِكْمَةً — a man given wisdom — فَهُوَ يَقْضِي بِهَا وَيُعَلِّمُهَا — and he judges by it and teaches it to others. The message is clear: do not envy people over trivial worldly things — it is not worth it. Reserve your envy for what is truly high, what belongs to the next life. And when you do, that envy becomes a spur to action, driving you to achieve what they have achieved.

He then turns to the question of *حَسَدٌ* directed at a disbeliever or a sinful Muslim — a *فَاجِر*, someone openly engaged in grave sins. In that case, he says, a form of *حَسَدٌ* resembling *دَاءُ الدَّرَا* is actually permissible. The crucial distinction, however, is the reason.

Earlier the point was made that envying non-Muslims simply because they enjoy worldly prosperity you do not have is not a sound basis for *حَسَدٌ*. What makes it permissible here is that they possess worldly power or resources they are actively using for harm and oppression. When that is the case, the feeling is justified.
