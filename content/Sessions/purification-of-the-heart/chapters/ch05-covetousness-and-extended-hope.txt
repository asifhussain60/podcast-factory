He says: وَسُمْعَةُ إِكْبَارِ الطَّاعَةِ بَعْدَ الْخُلُوصِ مِنْهُ مِنَ الْآفَاتِ — you performed the action for اللهُ, and then you went and told people about it. And so: أَخْبَتَ الْعَمَلَ — you ruined it. لِبَاسُ الْأَعْرَاضِ الرِّيَاءِيُّ وَالْعَمَلُ تُفْسِدُهُ، وَلَكِنْ إِنْ تُبْتُمْ ضَمَادٌ — رِيَاءٌ taints the action and ruins it, but if you make تَوْبَةٌ, it can be rectified. So تَوْبَةٌ is available to someone who fell into سُمْعَة. And he also addresses the one who performs a deed intending from the outset to tell others about it.

So he didn't act with إِخْلَاص. He actually did it in the first place intending to tell other people about it — and that, too, is سُمْعَة.

The note here clarifies that the difference between seeking reputation by telling others of one's deeds and showing off is that the former operates through hearing and the latter through sight. In other words, they are one and the same disease, but they travel different routes to affect the hearts of others. Ibn Abd al-Salam says that if the reason for disclosure is a desire to encourage others or to acknowledge اللهُ's blessings upon you, then it is a different matter.

The حَدِيث states that whoever informs others of his good deeds out of a desire for their benefit — and that is the explanation, not the literal text — اللهُ will inform them of his foul deeds on the Day of Judgment. And whoever displays his good deeds to others, اللهُ will display his bad deeds to them on يَوْمُ الْقِيَامَةِ. It is related by Ahmad and Imam Muslim.

It is a well-established practice among the مُحَدِّثِينَ to cite Imam Ahmad before Imam Muslim, even though صَحِيح مُسْلِم is the more rigorously authenticated collection. The reason is the جَلَاءً — the exalted standing — of Imam Ahmad's مَقَام in the أُمَّهْ. You will see this ordering frequently in classical texts.

Now he moves on to discuss what lies ahead, and this is a fascinating passage. There was a brigand from Bani Ababa, and the Arabs used him as a proverb — أَقْتَمُ مِنْ شِلَابٍ, "more of a brigand than Shilab" — meaning if you want to describe someone as a truly ruthless robber, you invoke his name. The author uses this image deliberately: just as a highway robber severs the road and robs travelers of what they carry, the disease of طَمَعٌ — covetousness — severs the road to اللهُ and robs the seeker of his rewards. طَمَعٌ is that brigand lying in wait on the path, and so we return once again to the root disease of disordered desire.

Abu al-Abbas al-Mursi relates that he was once in the marketplace intending to buy something when he spotted someone he recognized — a man well known for his scholarship and spiritual standing. The thought crossed his mind that if he approached this person, the man might offer him a favorable price out of deference to a fellow man of religion. At that moment, he says, he heard a call — نُودِيَ لَهُ, a هَاتِف, an inner voice — telling him: "سَلَامَةُ الدِّينِ is to have no desire toward created things." That is the meaning of a sound دِين: to strip the heart of طَمَعٌ toward other people, entirely and without exception.

This is precisely the warning being sounded, because if you look back over the previous diseases discussed, they all share a common thread — the illusion that something can be gained from them. You imagine you are going to get something out of them. And the root cause of طَمَعٌ, according to Sidi Ahmad Zarruq, رَحِمَهُ اللَّهُ, is غَفْلَة — heedlessness. You forget that اللهُ alone is الرَّزَّاقُ and الضَّارِي. You forget that human beings have nothing to give you that was not first given to them.

This غَفْلَة that descends on the heart is something اللهُ warns against repeatedly in the Quran. One verse reminds us: إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ هُوَ ٱلرَّزَّاقُ — اللهُ, He is the رَزَّاق, the sole Provider. Another verse states: وَإِن يَمْسَسْكَ ٱللَّهُ بِضُرٍّۢ فَلَا كَاشِفَ لَهُۥٓ إِلَّا هُوَ — "If اللهُ causes you harm, there is none who can remove it except Him." And in the famous حَدِيث, when the Prophet ﷺ addressed Ibn Abbas, he said, "O young man, I will teach you some words." Among what he taught him was this: if all of creation were to gather together to harm you, and اللهُ had not decreed it for you, they could not harm you in the slightest. And if all of creation were to gather to prevent some harm from reaching you, they could not prevent what اللهُ had decreed. The pens have been lifted and the ink is dry. It is settled.

Everything that will happen to you is in the hands of اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى. So your desire should be directed toward اللهُ — not toward what other people possess.

And what about people who wish you ill — who pray against you? Well, if you are standing upright with اللهُ, their prayers will gain them nothing. If you are oppressing them, then you have genuine cause for fear, because the Prophet ﷺ said: "Fear the prayer of the oppressed." But if you are not oppressing anyone, you have nothing to fear. A prayer made against someone without just cause does not reach its target — it returns to the one who made it. That is their affair, not yours.

اللهُ did not command us to make prayers against people except in the case of oppressors: لَا عُدْوَانَ إِلَّا عَلَى الظَّالِمِينَ — "There is no transgression except against the wrongdoers." And it is said that دُعَاء is the weapon of the مُؤْمِن. In many situations, the believers have nothing else at their disposal — no army, no political power — only دُعَاء. And to say "only دُعَاء" is to understate it, because دُعَاء is immense. اللهُ answers the prayers of those who call on Him sincerely.

So there is no need to worry about people's prayers against you unless they have a justifiable grievance, in which case the appropriate response is to seek their forgiveness.

غَفْلَة is heedlessness. And what Sidi Ahmad Zarruq said is that the cause of طَمَعٌ is غَفْلَة — you have become heedless of how creation actually works. If you genuinely believe that another person can benefit you independently of اللهُ, that is غَفْلَة. It is like someone who recovers from an illness and says, "I'm doing great — I took this remarkable medicine and it really worked," attributing the cure to the medicine itself and forgetting اللهُ entirely. That is غَفْلَة. A believer says: "اَلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ, اللهُ cured me. I took some medicine, and may اللهُ have placed healing in it — He cured me through it." When you remind someone of this, they usually say, "سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ, of course, of course" — and they mean it. But the point is, human beings slip into this forgetfulness constantly. They forget how things truly work. And among the most consequential forms of this forgetfulness is the belief that people can harm or benefit you independently of اللهُ's will. They cannot. Not in any way, unless اللهُ wills it.

Now, in matters of armies and military strategy, one must obviously remain in the realm of أَسْبَاب and deal with things realistically. But when it comes to رِزْق, to عِبَادَة, to رِيَاءٌ, to طَمَعٌ, to سُمْعَة — to all these spiritual diseases — the belief that people hold power over your outcomes is pure وَهْم, an illusion with no foundation.

غَفْلَة is a disease of the heart, and a serious one. Some scholars went so far as to say it is the only disease. Imam al-Junayd reportedly said he never witnessed a single wrong action that was not ultimately a consequence of غَفْلَة. If the heart is genuinely awake and aware of اللهُ, wrongdoing becomes impossible. He considered it the source disease, the root of all others.

The word غَافِل — heedless — is related to مُغَفَّل, which means a simpleton, someone easy to deceive. غَفْلَة, then, is a state of being fooled, but the غَافِل is fooling himself — he has simply stopped seeing things as they are. It is a kind of careless stupidity, though not a permanent one. It can be cured.

Then the text turns to ask: why would you speak ill of someone? What is the point? There is some طَمَعٌ lurking there — some desire. Why would you want to speak badly about a person to someone else unless you wanted that listener to think poorly of them? That is طَمَعٌ. You want something out of it. There is no other reason to do it. Lying, too — بَنَاتُ الْغُرُورِ, the deceits of the ego — falls into the same category. People lie because they want something. طَمَعٌ will also occupy and distract the heart during prayer. And it produces insincere flattery of others — saying things you do not believe, like the salesman who tells every customer they look wonderful in what they are trying on, because he wants to make the sale. He may privately think the item looks terrible. But he will not say so. He says, "That looks really good on you" — because there is طَمَعٌ in his heart.

There is a well-known حَدِيث that the truthful, honest merchant will be with the martyrs on يَوْمُ الْقِيَامَةِ. Consider what that means: to be a merchant without طَمَعٌ in the heart. How is that possible? It is possible when the merchant sees everything as coming from اللهُ. If a customer arrives, اللهُ has brought his provision to him. If the customer leaves without buying, اللهُ did not decree it. The merchant can simply be a human being. He does not need to lie. He does not need to manufacture enthusiasm he does not feel.

<!-- page 76 -->

In traditional Muslim societies, the marketplace was organized in a way that defies modern marketing logic entirely. All the merchants selling the same goods would be clustered together — the سُكُّ الْعَطَّارِينَ, the سُكُّ الْإِسْكَافِيِّينَ, each trade in its own row. In Damascus you still know exactly where to find the clothes merchants and where to find the cobblers. They all sat side by side. Not only that, but traditionally they often prayed together, belonged to the same guilds, trained the same apprentices, attended the same circles of knowledge, and shared the same شَيْخٌs. They were, genuinely, close friends.

What would happen — and this is not an idealized fiction — is that if a merchant felt he had sold enough for the day, he would close his stall, go home or to the mosque, and let his fellow merchants serve the remaining customers. There are still a few people like this alive today, though they tend to be quite old, and their sons — back from business school with their MBAs — often think they are being foolish.

Compare that with a culture whose business literature is full of titles like *How to Swim with the Sharks*, and whose advertisements picture a young man in a hotel mirror declaring he is going to "eat the competition for breakfast" — followed by a line about taking his children to an amusement park afterward. That image captures something true about the dominant culture: competitive capitalism in its purest form. Eliminate the competition. Whoever stands in your way, consume them. This mentality scales — from the individual merchant to the geopolitical stage — and the same logic that drives one businessman to undercut a neighbor drives nations to bomb other nations into ruin. It is, at its root, the same disease, just magnified to catastrophic proportions.

<!-- page 77 -->

This is precisely what the Prophet ﷺ warned of when he said that what he feared most for this أُمَّهْ was التَّنَابُسُ — the frenzied competition over دُنْيَا. He described it as the disease of civilizations, the thing that ultimately destroys them. Because if your philosophy is "us against them," the problem is that everyone else's philosophy is also "us against them" — and you are their "them," just as they are your "them." In the end, the logic of mutual destruction runs its course.

Some scholars observed that the vessels of طَمَعٌ are all مُجَوَّفَة — hollow, open-ended, impossible to fill. You always want more. The desire is never satisfied. The Greeks had a word for this kind of longing: Eros — not in its later reduced sense, but as an insatiable yearning, always reaching toward something just beyond grasp. The Prophet ﷺ captured this perfectly when he said that nothing will fill the mouth of the son of Adam except the soil of his grave. If he had one mountain of gold, he would desire a second. Nothing suffices.

He also said that two types of people will never be quenched: the seeker of knowledge and the seeker of دُنْيَا. So you choose. Those are the two insatiable drives in this world — and you are either one or the other. You are either seeking knowledge or seeking دُنْيَا.

Now, it must be said: if طَمَعٌ is directed toward اللهُ, it becomes something praiseworthy. Desire itself is a human faculty — it is built into us. The disease is when desire is directed toward other than اللهُ, when you imagine that benefit lies in created things. The same principle applies to حَسَد: envy in its blameworthy form is resenting someone for what they have. But if you see someone surpassing you and it moves you to emulate them, to do what they are doing so that you might reach their level — that is a constructive use of the same impulse. These faculties can be turned toward your benefit or your destruction. It depends entirely on where they are pointed.

Now, listen to what comes next — it is a truly remarkable passage.

He says: if you ask desire what its trade is, it will answer, "Earning humiliation." That is its *حِرْفَة*. If you ask about its father, it will say, "Doubt concerning the divine measuring out — what is not coming to me." And if you ask about its goal, it will say, "The deprivation of the very thing it desires."

Now, another matter.

In the *خَاتِمَةٌ* — and this is genuinely important, so I want everyone to listen closely — what he is saying is that your desiring something relates directly to what has been allotted to you in this life, which is called *رِزْق*. There are some vital concepts here. One is that اللهُ rarely pairs two names with the same attribute. You find it occasionally — *رَفِيعُ الدَّرَجَاتِ*, things like that — but He is both *الرَّزَّاقُ* and *الرَّازِقُ*, and we are called *مَرْزُوق*: the recipients, the passive form. What we receive is the *رِزْق*, and what gives it is اللهُ.

I mentioned last week that scholars differ on the definition of *رِزْق*: some say it is only what you actually benefit from; others say it is everything you possess. The dominant opinion is that it is what you benefit from. There is nothing on earth except that its *رِزْق* comes from اللهُ — He has given every creature its provision. For اللهُ has divided the *أَرْزَاق* of people, their provisions, and there are two kinds: *رِزْقُ بُوبَانِي* — inward provision — and *رِزْقٌ ظَاهِرِيٌّ* — outward provision. The outward *رِزْق* is your food, your health, the things we can observe. The inward *رِزْق* is your company, your knowledge, what you genuinely learn, the character you develop — all of that.

Now, one of the striking things about life is that the same people keep appearing in it. It is a remarkable phenomenon. There are five billion people on this planet, yet when you think carefully about the people who truly matter in your life, the number is actually very small — and they keep returning, again and again, because they are part of your *رِزْق*. They are written into it. So your company will largely consist of the same people cycling back through your life. And within that *رِزْق* there is both trial and blessing, hardship and good. Some people hold a pessimistic view of this; others hold an optimistic one — especially once you understand that all of it comes from اللهُ, and اللهُ is the one bringing it about.

Here is what is truly important to grasp. اللهُ has given the human being wealth as a *نِعْمَةٌ* and placed it within him, and in His divine decree He has distributed the *أَرْزَاق* among people. But some of one person's *رِزْق* He has placed with another person, and there is a *صَبَبٌ* — a means — through which you must go and retrieve it. So here is this person, and he holds your *رِزْق*. It is not his *رِزْق*; it is yours. But اللهُ has placed it in his possession. How you receive it depends on the circumstances. If it is food he has that was written for you, he will invite you to dinner — he may not even particularly like you, but if it was written for you, you will end up at that table. Perhaps you were not even invited; you arrived with someone else. That is *رِزْق* — it is there, it is *مَقْصٌ* for you, and he cannot consume it, because it is not for him.

That is why the Prophet, ﷺ, loved welcoming guests more than almost anything. The guest arrives with his own provision. You lose nothing by feeding a guest — that provision was not written for you; it was written for him. And he departs carrying the wrong actions of the household with him: when you are generous and courteous to a guest, اللهُ removes wrong action as a *كَفَّارَةٌ*. The guest, then, is a tremendous blessing from اللهُ.

Now, because of the *أَسْبَاب* — these means are so mysterious — people begin to think, "My *رِزْق* comes from him." That is the *غَفْلَة*, the heedlessness. Because اللهُ has arranged things so that your *رِزْق* travels through someone else, people begin to reason: "He benefits those people; perhaps I can get something from him too." And so the *طَمَعٌ* enters — you see the *صَبَبٌ*, the means, and lose sight of the *مُسَبِّب*, the true originator, the one who causes those means to exist. This is precisely where *طَمَعٌ* becomes dangerous, and the clearest sign of it is when you grow angry at not receiving what you want. That is the surest indicator. When an expectation goes unfulfilled and you become angry, that is the sign.

This is obviously not about legal rights — *حُقُوق*. If someone owes you something, has it, and is withholding it, that is injustice, and your anger at injustice is legitimate. What we are speaking about here is *رِزْق*: what is coming to you, what is yours by divine decree.

This is why the Prophet ﷺ said in the حَدِيث — conveying what Jibril told him — that no soul will die until it has completed every portion of the *رِزْق* allotted to it. It must complete it.

I was once on a plane in Mauritania, flying from Néma to Nouakchott. A man boarded at the front in a wheelchair. The moment we landed in Nouakchott — just as we touched down — the seats went back and the crew began performing CPR. He had died. And then, in the way that is not so extraordinary in Mauritania, the man who had come to meet us at the plane turned out to be a close friend of the deceased. So we learned the man's whole story. He had been a minister, well respected, around forty-four years old. He had fallen ill and had gone out into the desert to recuperate. Someone administered an injection; he went into a coma. They brought him anyway — they were transporting him to Nouakchott. The point is that he had an appointment with the Angel of Death in that place. He had been out in the middle of the Mauritanian desert in a tent, but he was destined to die in Nouakchott. So all the *أَسْبَاب* were arranged so that he would reach the place where his death had been decreed, because there was no other place he could die — it was *مَقْصٌ* for him to be in that place at that time.

And so it is: if اللهُ intends for someone to reach a certain place, He will furnish the reasons to go there. The same holds true for *رِزْق*. This is why the Prophet ﷺ said — Jibril conveying it to us — that all your *رِزْق* is written for you. Then he said: have *تَقْوَى* of اللهُ. Do not do anything *حَرَامٌ* to obtain your *رِزْق*, because it is coming to you regardless. Do not go about begging or soliciting from people. In fact, among the pledges the Companions took with the Messenger of اللهُ ﷺ — one item of the *بَيْعَة* — was that they would not ask people for money. That was explicitly part of the covenant. *إِيمَان* should free a person from all such anxieties.

<!-- page 80 -->

And then he said something further: do not let the delay of your *رِزْق* make you disobedient to اللهُ. If it is not arriving as quickly as you wish, do not let that delay become the cause of your falling into disobedience. That is a vital principle.

Now, everyone in *صَلَاحٌ* — the next disease is extended hope. We will cover that next week. Last week we covered covetous desire, *طَمَعٌ*. And in speaking of it, he says the Arabs have what they call *سَمُّ سَاعَةٍ* — a fast-acting, immediately lethal poison. The immediately acting poison of this love of *دُنْيَا*, of *طَمَعٌ* and all the diseases of the heart, is *تَطْوِيلُ الْأَمَلِ*. He defines it as habituating yourself to the assurance that death is a long way off.

Now, on one level this is a necessary human condition, and there is actually a remarkable wisdom in *تَطْوِيلُ الْأَمَلِ*. Among the wisdoms the scholars have noted is this: if people did not have extended hope, they would not bother planting trees. If you believed you might die at any moment, you would not farm, you would not plan, you would not raise children with any thought for their future. The result would be that each generation would have to deal only with immediate circumstances, with no infrastructure to pass on. But because human beings do carry hopes of living on, they build, they farm, they create the infrastructure that the next generation inherits.

The famous story — from one of the Persian tales — is of a Persian *شَاهْ* who passed an old man planting an olive tree. The king asked: "Will this tree be of any benefit to you? Surely you will die before it bears fruit" — because a good olive tree can take several decades before it yields well. And the old man replied with what became a proverb among the Arabs: *"They planted before us, and we ate from what they planted. So we plant, that those who come after us may eat."* The king gave him a reward. Then the man said, "Already the tree has benefited me." So the king gave him more.

The point is that *تَطْوِيلُ الْأَمَلِ* is a *رَحْمَة* from اللهُ — He has placed it in human nature, because without it people would not go to school, would not plan, would not build anything lasting.

<!-- page 81 -->

One thing worth noting about modern society is that because of the apocalyptic character of the age we are living in, people are beginning to lose hope in the future. Many young people are becoming nihilistic — dressing in black, adopting a morbid view of the world. The fast-food culture — the idea of getting it now, not waiting — and the credit culture are genuinely eroding people's sense that there is a future worth building toward. In fact, this may be the first American generation that genuinely expects to live worse than their parents. Prior generations of Americans were noted for their buoyant optimism about the future. That optimism is fading — and its loss is itself a bad sign.

On the other hand, what he is saying is that too much hope is equally a deadly poison. It becomes a false hope, an *أَمَانُ الْأَمَانِ*. The result it generates is hard-heartedness and indolence — *يُلْقِيهِ قَسْوَةَ الْقَلْبِ وَالْكَسَلَ*. Through *طُولُ الْأَمَلِ*, the heart becomes hard because it stops reflecting on the *آخِرَة*, stops reflecting on its own mortality and dependence. And so it hardens.

Al-Fudayl ibn 'Iyad, *رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ* — one of the great early scholars and *زُهَّاد*, the people of asceticism — said: *خَمْسٌ مِنْ عَلَامَاتِ الشَّقَاوَةِ* There are five signs of *شَقَاوَة* — wretchedness. The world is divided into exactly two kinds of people: *صَعِيدٌ* and *شَقِيٌّ*. There is no third category. *سَعِيد* is felicitous; *شَقِيٌّ* is wretched.

Now, if you look at Aristotle — foundational to so much of Western civilization — he wrote a book called *The Ethics* in which he essentially argues that the goal of life is leisure and happiness. This is echoed in the Declaration of Independence, written by Thomas Jefferson, which states that people have certain inalienable rights — and among them are life (*حَيٌّ*), liberty (*حُرِّيَّة*) — rights recognized in the *شَرِيعَة* as well — and then "the pursuit of happiness." Jefferson's original phrasing was actually "the right to be free of debt when you come into the world," because what happens now is that national debt means children are born already in debt — approximately fifty thousand dollars at birth, based on previous generations' overspending. But he changed it to the pursuit of happiness. And what was happiness to a middle-aged white landowner in 1776? More land. In essence, the pursuit of stuff, of goods. This is woven into the fabric of the modern American dream: happiness understood as the pursuit of things.

What Islam did was radically reorient human beings' understanding of happiness. Happiness is not necessarily what we might call a "good life" in the worldly sense. For Muslims, happiness — *سَعَادَة* — is in the next world. If you are *صَعِيدٌ* in the next world, it does not matter how your life in this world appeared. We looked at this in the previous class, in the *سِرَا*, when we examined the Prophet's ﷺ supplication: "If You are not angry with me, then I do not care what You do with me" — meaning: if my life in this world is difficult but I have not incurred Your wrath, I am content with that. Because he knew that *‏وَلَلْآخِرَةُ خَيْرٌۭ لَّكَ مِنَ ٱلْأُولَىٰ ‎* — the afterlife is better for you than the first life.

<!-- page 82 -->

The opposite of *سَعَادَة* is *شَقَاوَة* — wretchedness — the condition of the person who has no portion in the *آخِرَة*. That is what *شَقِيٌّ* means. So al-Fudayl ibn 'Iyad said there are five signs of *شَقَاوَة*.

The first is *قَسْوَةُ الْقَلْبِ* — a hard heart. That is a sign of wretchedness. The Prophet ﷺ once said to a man who had just watched him kiss a child: "I kiss my children" — "I have ten and I have never kissed a single one." He was boasting of it as a kind of tough-Arab pride. And the Prophet ﷺ said: "I cannot do anything for a man from whom اللهُ has removed *رَحْمَة* from his heart." A hard heart means you have lost your portion of the *دِين*. *"Have mercy on those on the earth, and He in the heavens will have mercy on you."*

The second sign is an eye that does not weep. The scholars say: if you want to know whether your heart is hard, look at your eye. A dry eye indicates a hard heart. The person with *رِقَّةٌ* — tenderness — in the heart has a moist eye; the person without it has a dry one.

The third is *قِلَّةُ الْحَيَاءِ* — a deficiency in modesty and shame. People who have no shame. As the saying goes: if you have no shame, do as you please. *Qillat al-haya'* is a sign of *شَقَاوَة*.

The fourth is *الرَّغْبَةُ فِي الدُّنْيَا* — desire for the *دُنْيَا* — which is likewise a sign of *شَقَاوَة*. The scholars say that no one adds to his *آخِرَة* except that it diminishes something of his *دُنْيَا*, and no one adds to his *دُنْيَا* except that it diminishes something of his *آخِرَة* — because the two are opposites by nature. An increase in one is a decrease in the other.

This does not mean you automatically assume that a wealthy person has sacrificed his *آخِرَة*. There is a well-known teaching story — a Moroccan tale — about a *زَاهِد*, an ascetic, who had heard of a great *وَلِيٌّ* of اللهُ and set out to visit him. He came down from his mountain riding his lion. He arrived to find the *شَيْخٌ* living in a large palace. He was astonished — he himself lived in a cave. The *شَيْخٌ* came out to greet him, and the *زَاهِد* asked where he could stable his lion. The *شَيْخٌ* said, "Put him in my barn with the cow." He did not realize the lion would eat the cow. The *شَيْخٌ* said, "No, no — just put him in the barn with the cow." So in he went.

Then they sat down to a large dinner. The *شَيْخٌ* ate a few bites and said, "أَسْتَغْفِرُ اللهَ — how luxurious this is." The *زَاهِد* thought: "I live on water and dates." That night the *شَيْخٌ* went to sleep, and the *زَاهِد* stayed up all night in prayer — only to hear the *شَيْخٌ* snoring in the next room. He was utterly scandalized. He decided that after *فَجْر* he was leaving. He went to retrieve his lion — and found that the cow had eaten the lion.

So he asks the man, "Where's my lion?" The man replies, "Your lion is just like you — all outward show." And then he adds, "And you thought my cow was harmless."

But she was a lion on the inside. The point of that story is that you have to be very careful, because outward appearances can fool people. Sidi Ahmad Zarruq has something remarkable to say about this — though we'll return to that when we reach the discussion of having a bad opinion of others.

Those five things, then, are signs of hardness of the heart. What this science is ultimately about is bringing life back to the heart.
