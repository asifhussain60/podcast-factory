When Sayyidna 'Ali, رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ, said: *النَّاسُ نِيَامٌ، فَإِذَا مَاتُوا انْتَبَهُوا* — "people are asleep, and when they die they wake up" — what he meant is that people are غَافِلٌ, sunk in heedlessness about their destiny: where they are going, and what they will be called to account for when they get there. Because if they truly knew, with real wakefulness — يَقَظَةٌ — they could not do the things they do. That is why Imam al-Junayd said this is the core disease: if the human being truly knew what was coming, there is no way he could act as he does.

This idea is not entirely unlike the Socratic understanding, which belongs to a parallel tradition in the West — the idea that people do wrong because they are genuinely unaware; if they really knew what they were doing, they would not do it. The metaphor given in that tradition is the metaphor of the cave: human beings are like people sitting inside a cave with a lamp behind them, unable to turn around, watching shadows on the wall and taking those shadows for reality. Then one of them escapes, goes out into the real world, sees the sun and the stars and the trees, returns to the cave, and tells the others: "This is all an illusion — what we thought was real is not real; the real thing is somewhere else." And they kill him. That is the role of the prophets: they come and tell people, "You are all asleep — wake up, do you not realize?" So the concept of غَفْلَة is profound, and that is why اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى uses the word in many Quranic verses.

Another word the Quran uses is صَمَدٌ — and صَمَدٌ is similar to غَفْلَة, but there is a greater degree of awareness in it; the صَمَدٌ knows, in some sense, but the غَافِل does not. اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى says in سُورَةُ الْقَمَرِ: *‏لَّقَدْ كُنتَ فِى غَفْلَةٍۢ مِّنْ هَذَا فَكَشَفْنَا عَنكَ غِطَآءَكَ فَبَصَرُكَ ٱلْيَوْمَ حَدِيدٌۭ ‎* — "You were in heedlessness about this; We have removed the veil from you, and your sight today is sharp." There is a veil of غَفْلَة over the human being, screening him from reality. And اللهُ says in the Quran that there is a رِيشَا — *وَعَلَى قُلُوبِهِمْ أَكِنَّةٌ* — and this رِشْوَةٌ prevents them from seeing things as they truly are. So the غَافِل is not seeing things as they truly are, while the يَقِظ — the one who is truly awake — sees things as they truly are.

The Prophet ﷺ prayed: *اللَّهُمَّ أَرِنِي الْأَشْيَاءَ كَمَا هِيَ* — "O اللهُ, show me things as they really are." And in another hadith: *اللَّهُمَّ أَرِنِي الْحَقَّ حَقًّا وَارْزُقْنِي اتِّبَاعَهُ، وَأَرِنِي الْبَاطِلَ بَاطِلًا وَارْزُقْنِي اجْتِنَابَهُ* — "O اللهُ, show me truth as truth and grant me the ability to follow it; and show me falsehood as falsehood and grant me the ability to avoid it." The one who is not غَافِل has precisely that state: they see truth as truth and falsehood as falsehood, they follow the truth and avoid the falsehood. And that is the مُتَّقٍ — that is him, the person of تَقْوَى: he does what اللهُ commanded and avoids what اللهُ prohibited.

So he is saying that غَفْلَة is *غَفْلُ الْمَرْءِ عَمَّا أَمَرَهُ اللهُ بِهِ وَنَهَاهُ عَنْهُ* — heedlessness about what اللهُ has commanded and what He has forbidden. That is why people do wrong: not out of pure malice in most cases, but out of heedlessness about what they have been commanded and what they have been prohibited from doing. And this is why, if you see a believer doing something wrong and you remind him, and he responds with *أَسْتَغْفِرُ اللَّهَ* — that is a good person, a مُتَّقٍ. You have brought him out of his غَفْلَة. The مُتَكَبِّر, by contrast — because كِبْرٌ is what follows after all that we have been discussing — when reminded of his غَفْلَة, will not accept the reminder. *أَخَذَتْهُ ٱلْعِزَّةُ بِٱلْإِثْمِ*: he takes refuge in his own pride and refuses. You say to him gently, and he gets angry at you: "Who do you think you are?" That is why the cure for غَفْلَة in a believer is good company — صُحْبَة. Be with people of صِدْقٌ, of sincerity; be with people of تَقْوَى; be with truthful, sincere people who will tell you plainly when you are doing something wrong, and encourage you when you are doing something right. Those are the people who will pull you out of your غَفْلَة.

He then says: *وَهِيَ لَدَيْهِمْ أَصْلُ كُلِّ ذَنْبٍ* — those of this science consider غَفْلَة the source of all wrong actions, as Imam al-Junayd al-Baghdadi held. *وَدَوَاؤُهَا بِأَرْبَعِ دَوَاءٍ* — and the cure consists of four things that carry rectifying qualities: *فَاسْتَغْفِرِ اللهَ وَزُرْ وَصَلِّ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ وَاكْتُبْ وَاذْكُرْ*. So he gives the four things.

The first is اِسْتِغْفَار, because once you realize you have been غَافِل, you begin to see what you have done wrong as a consequence of that heedlessness, and you want to ask forgiveness. Many of the later scholars said that اِسْتِغْفَار and صَلَاةٌ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ are the two ذِكْرٌ of the final era — the two pillars of devotion for this last period of time. Istighfar is asking forgiveness of اللهُ, and it is a form of تَوْبَة. The Prophet ﷺ said that you should ask اِسْتِغْفَار at least seventy times, up to a hundred times a day — *أَسْتَغْفِرُ اللَّهَ* — so it is always a good practice. A complementary practice that most of the early Muslims observed was مُحَاسَبَة. The idea comes from what merchants do at the end of the trading day: they count the money, see what was gained and what was lost, and balance the scales. مُحَاسَبَة for the soul is the same: at the end of the day, you look at your day and ask — was there loss with اللهُ, or gain? If there was loss, you ask اِسْتِغْفَار. If there was gain, you say *الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ* and are grateful. *لَىِٕن شَكَرْتُمْ لَأَزِيدَنَّكُمْ* — if you are grateful, اللهُ increases you. So that is one way. He goes into شُكْر as well, since that is one of the مَقَامَاتٌ we will reach إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ.

The second remedy is زِيَارَة — visiting righteous people. The صَالِحِينَ occupy the lowest of four ranks: the أَنْبِيَاء and رُسُل are first, then the صِدِّيقٌ, then the شُهَدَاء, then the صَالِحِين. And yet they are all, in a sense, صَالِحٌ — a شَاهِد must be a صَلِّحْ, a صِدِّيق must be a صَلِّحْ, and the أَنْبِيَاء are all صَالِحِينَ by their very nature. صَلَاحٌ in Arabic means to be sound, and this is related to سَلَامَةٌ — soundness, wholeness, the person who possesses a قَلْبٌ سَلِيمٌ. The definition of a صَلِّحْ is: *يُؤَدِّي حَقَّ اللَّهِ وَيُؤَدِّي حَقَّ الْعِبَادِ* — he gives what is due to اللهُ and he gives what is due to the servants of اللهُ. حَقُّ اللهِ is تَقْوَى: doing what He commanded and avoiding what He prohibited. حَقُّ الْعِبَادِ are all the responsibilities you carry in the world toward other people. So the صَلِّحْ cannot cheat — that is فِسْقٌ, the very opposite of صَلَاح. He does not lie, because the حَقٌّ of people upon you is that you be truthful to them. The صَلِّحْ is a person worth visiting.

Most scholars not only encourage visiting the صَالِحِينَ who are alive, but also those who have died. If you are in Medina, you go to بَقِيعٌ and visit the صَالِحِينَ there — you give them سَلَامٌ, you say *رَحِمَهُ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ*. The Prophet ﷺ used to visit سَيِّدُنَا حَمْزَة, and he visited the graveyard and said: *كُنْتُ نَهَيْتُكُمْ عَنْ زِيَارَةِ الْقُبُورِ فَزُورُوهَا* — "I used to forbid you from visiting graves," because of what the pre-Islamic Arabs used to do at them, "but now that they have been purified of that جَاهِلِيَّة, visit them." There is a benefit in visiting graves — it is a reminder of the آخِرَة — and there is a greater benefit in visiting the graves of the people of صَلَاح.

On the question of women and graves: there is a hadith — *لَعَنَ اللَّهُ زَوَّارَاتِ الْقُبُورِ*, and in one narration *زَائِرَاتُ الْقُبُورِ* — and زَوَّرَ is an intensive form, referring to someone who visits graves excessively and repeatedly. There is a hadith that is considered مُتَوَاتِر in which the Prophet ﷺ passed by the graveyard in Medina and found a woman there weeping. He said it would be better to be patient. She replied: *مَا أَصَابَكَ مَا أَصَابَنِي* — "You have not been struck by the calamity I have been struck by" — and she did not recognize him. He left her, and then someone told her that had been the Messenger of اللهُ ﷺ. She immediately went and knocked at his door and said: "I did not know it was you." And the Prophet ﷺ said: *الصَّبْرُ عِنْدَ الصَّدْمَةِ الْأُولَى* — "Patience is with the first blow." Patience is when the affliction first strikes — that is when اللهُ expects it from you. After a year, if the grief has passed and you have moved forward, that is not صَبْرٌ; that is simply time doing its work. The صَبْرٌ is at the moment the مُصِيبَة occurs, and that is where اللهُ wants to see ثَبَاتٌ — steadfastness — and not a shaking that would cause one to do something displeasing to Him. But the point of this hadith for our discussion is that the Prophet ﷺ did not tell her to leave the graveyard — and scholars take from that the permissibility of women visiting graves, though there is indeed a خِلَافٌ on the matter.

As for the أَدَب of visiting graves: what happens at some graves nowadays has nothing to do with what is being discussed here. At some shrines — and this applies in many parts of the Muslim world — people tie knots, women who cannot conceive go to ask the وَلِيٌّ to give them a son, people make vows to the buried saint. All of that has nothing to do with a proper زِيَارَة. He is talking about visiting with أَدَب, for the blessing of the visit itself — a زِيَارَة. That is something quite different. And this confusion is part of what has given تَصَوُّف a bad name: much of what is done under its label is بِدْعَة — in fact, it really is بِدْعَة. Women sitting around in a مَوْلِد without حِجَاب, chatting and gossiping all day — those are all problems. In such cases, the scholars' strategy is sometimes simply to prohibit the practice altogether, because the corruption has become so pervasive. But the best, without question, is to visit the living صَالِحِينَ.

Unfortunately, in our time, this is quite rare. To find a truly righteous person, you often have to travel far and go to difficult places, because many of them have simply retreated to places that are hard to reach. Someone like Murabbit al-Hajj — رَحِمَهُ اللَّهُ — whom we visited in Shinqit, the father of Shaykh Abdallah — I consider him, in my estimation, from the صَالِحِينَ. When اللهُ says *يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَكُونُوا مَعَ الصَّادِقِينَ* — "be with the truthful ones" — He would not command us to do something if there were no such people and no way to recognize them. It would be foolish to think we cannot recognize people of صِدْقٌ and صَلَاح. There are obviously degrees and levels, but the basic criterion is the شَرِيعَة: if someone is outwardly upright in their observance of the شَرِيعَة, that is our standard. We judge by the outward, and we judge by truth.

One of the dangers I have noticed in modern تَصَوُّف — in many Muslim countries — is that there are people who go by the name of بِرٌّ or شَيْخٌ or whatever, and many of them inherited the title from their father, who inherited it from his father, and many of them have very little knowledge, sometimes almost none. And they sometimes use their positions for material gain — extracting money from followers, arranging advantageous marriages for their daughters, and so on. There are a great many charlatans. And nothing is worse than a religious charlatan — nothing. There is a man in Fez, Sidi Ma'ib, whom some here know, and he once said to me: "اللهُ has made the world vast, and He has made the means of earning حَلَالٌ provision without number — there are untold ways of earning حَلَالٌ. So whenever you see someone using the religion to gain دُنْيَا, know that it is a bad thing." If they are using it to accumulate wealth and exploit their position, that is a serious problem — and unfortunately there are many who claim to be شَيْخٌs or Sufi شَيْخٌs who do exactly that.

That said, having a livelihood is a different matter entirely. *خَيْرُ مَا أَكَلَ الرَّجُلُ مِنْ كَسْبِ يَدِهِ* — the best thing a man can earn from is the work of his own hands. The عُلَمَاء have taken from the hadith on teaching the Quran that it is permissible to earn from teaching. And even though the early سَلَفٌ were generally opposed to taking payment for teaching, the later scholars recognized that people must have livelihood and that the world has become more complicated. That is not the issue. The issue is amassing and accumulating wealth — using religion as a vehicle for that. Imam Malik, رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ, was given enormous amounts of wealth because of who he was, but what he did was spend it all out. Murabbit al-Hajj is a fine example of this: money comes from all over, and it all goes straight back out. You can see it — a few cows, a tent, an adobe house, a few robes, some books, and constant giving. People give to righteous people because they trust them, and practicing Muslims are generous, so they want to give. The question is not receiving — the question is hoarding, accumulating beyond one's needs, refusing to let it flow out. When your needs are taken care of and you begin to hoard, that is where the problem lies.

You have to be very careful, and it has been a problem for a long time. The scholars complained about it centuries ago. One scholar — and this man himself was a genuine شَيْخٌ, a مُرَبِّي, a person of real تَرْبِيَة and outward knowledge both — said that in his time, the ninth century, most people who called themselves Sufi شَيْخٌs were complete charlatans. So it is an old problem. And that is a condition, by the way: anyone who does تَرْبِيَة must have outward knowledge. They cannot be without it.

Unfortunately, in the Muslim world today there is a profound split between تَصَوُّف and exoteric Islam — between the inward and outward dimensions of the tradition — and they have become two camps pitched against each other. Nowhere is this more visible than on the Arabian Peninsula, where you have the Najdis and the Hijazis: most of the Najdis are staunchly anti-Sufi, and many of the Hijazis are enthusiastically pro-مَوْلِد and pro-تَصَوُّف, and they are at loggerheads, with real hostility between them. We are out of balance as an أُمَّة, and the truth lies somewhere between the two positions. Make دُعَاء for them, and ask to benefit from their respective knowledges.

Ahmad Baba al-Timbukti — the great scholar from Timbuktu who was taken as a slave to Morocco — when he arrived in Marrakech, he was so much more learned than any of the scholars there that they quickly realized they had made a grave mistake. He mentions in his book — الدِّيبَاجُ الْمُذْهَبُ, his حَاشِيَة on Ibn Farhun's طَبَقَاتٌ — visiting several of the graves of the righteous in Marrakech and asking اللهُ at one of them to grant him understanding of two books he was working on. He says that by اللهُ, he returned to those books and could read them without any difficulty. He was a great scholar, and that was the traditional practice: to ask for the blessing of their knowledges, since most of those buried in such places were scholars, and to ask *رَحِمَهُ اللَّهُ* — that اللهُ give them light in their graves and bless them.

Qadi Abu Bakr ibn al-'Arabi — whose grave is just outside of Fez — gave a فَتْوَى permitting the visiting of the graves of the صَالِحِينَ, as did Ibn al-Hajj. For those who say it is بِدْعَة: that is simply not consistent with the position of the vast majority of our scholars, who have not only permitted it but recommended it. And these are outward scholars, jurists — well known in the tradition. Qadi Abu Bakr ibn al-'Arabi's grave is well known outside of Fez; he was poisoned by his enemies and died a martyr there, having also fought جِهَادٌ multiple times. He wrote two commentaries on the Quran. He brought the إِحْيَاءُ عُلُومِ الدِّينِ from Imam al-Ghazali back to Morocco and Andalusia — he had studied it with al-Ghazali directly and transmitted it west. He is a great scholar, and anyone who has visited that place knows there is a great deal of بَرَكَة there, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ.

But the living — if you have the opportunity to visit them, do so. And you should have high هِمَّة: desire to be one of them, so that our أُمَّة has people worth visiting. We need these people. Make the intention to be one yourself, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, and ask اللهُ to give you تَوْفِيق — and give all of us تَوْفِيق for that. One of the poets said: *إِذَا الْمَرْءُ لَمْ يَرْتَدِ الْعُلَا* — if a man does not reach the heights, at least he asبِرٌّes to them. *وَمَنْ قَنِعَ بِالدُّونِ كَانَ دَنِيًّا* — whoever is content with less than heights is himself less than heights. The greatest asبِرٌّation is to be close to اللهُ. Have high هِمَّة. And Sidi Ibn 'Ajiba said: if you do not believe that اللهُ can take you in this very moment and make you one of His أَوْلِيَاءُ, then you know nothing of His قُدْرَة. Never despair. Asبِرٌّe to be from the people of وِلَايَة, the people who are close to اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى.

So زِيَارَة is important. When you visit these people — those I have visited from the people of this path — you will notice that they do not engage in idle chatter. You do not go for a social call. You go for the benefit of learning something, and for the benefit of their دُعَاء. Ask them for their دُعَاء, because it may well be accepted, and if it is, you receive the blessing of it. Visit them, ask for their دُعَاء, and show humility in their presence.

I will share a story about Mulla Ramadan, who was from the صَالِحِينَ and was the father of Shaykh Sa'id Ramadan al-Buti. Shaykh Sa'id wrote a book called *هَذَا وَالِدِي* — "This Is My Father" — describing what a great man his father was. I once asked Dr. Bukar — a Libyan scholar, I believe he is in New Jersey now — about Mulla Ramadan, because it is always strengthening to the إِيمَان to hear stories of these people. He told me — سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ — that the most remarkable thing about visiting that man was this: he said he had always found the practice of kissing hands rather unnecessary, something he had never been comfortable with. He said he went in, Mulla Ramadan extended his hand, and he felt a kind of pressure on his head — and found himself going down and kissing his hand. When he looked back, there was nothing there. They would pray on the Prophet ﷺ. Imam Malik, may اللهُ be pleased with him, said he never spent a single night without seeing the Prophet ﷺ in a dream. There is a great blessing in it, and people bear witness to that.

The fourth practice is تِلَاوَةُ الْقُرْآنِ — recitation of the Qur'an, preferably with تَدَبُّر, which means reflection and pondering the meanings rather than merely reading the words. Reciting with تَدَبُّر awakens the heart; it is one of the things that genuinely rouses it from slumber. Even reciting without deep reflection carries its own benefit. You should have a daily portion of Qur'an — ideally at least a حِزْبٌ or a جُزْء each day. If that is not possible, then at minimum recite the surahs the Messenger of اللهُ ﷺ recommended: يَاسِين, generally after فَجْر; and the surahs after مَغْرِب and الْمُلْك after إِصْحَاء.

For those who have not yet learned Arabic, reading the meanings in English is something, but it is limited — and this is the honest truth — because part of what the Qur'an does operates through the actual sounds of the language. Think of it like medicine. From an energetic perspective, every substance in the world resonates at a specific wavelength. Diseases resonate, and a medicine that cures a disease resonates in a way that counteracts it. The recitation of the Qur'an works through the sounds themselves, the sounds that emanate from you as you speak. We now know from material science that sound waves resonate through every cell in the body. When you recite the Qur'an, you are producing divine words that اللهُ revealed — and those words carry a sound.

I was once with a man at a gas station. He was asking about Islam. A Muslim mechanic was there, and I was present wearing a robe, so he began asking me about it. He mentioned that he had read the Qur'an. I said, "You have never read the Qur'an unless you read it in Arabic." He said he couldn't believe that. I said it was a fact. He asked why, and I said, "Just listen." Then I recited the opening of سُورَةُ الرَّحْمَٰنِ to him — and وَاللَّهِ, by the One besides whom there is no god — when I finished, he simply said, "I understand now." He was not joking. Non-Arab people, عَجَمِيّ people, are often stunned when they hear the Qur'an, even if they are not Muslim. They are amazed by the sounds.

You have to recognize that the Qur'an is not only its meanings — it is also the sounds, which transcend and participate in the meaning in a realm we do not fully comprehend, because everything in reality is meaning. اللهُ has taken meaning all the way down to the phonemes of the Qur'an. In modern linguistics, phonemes are considered meaningless in isolation; they acquire meaning only when joined into polysyllabic forms. Someone once wrote a nasty critique of Dr. Hamid Algar, complaining that his speech was filled with polysyllabic sentences. Dr. Algar pointed out in his rebuttal that a sentence that is not polysyllabic is, by definition, meaningless. And yet even indiviدُعَاءl phonemes carry meaning in Arabic. We may not know what that meaning is, but the meaning goes all the way down. Those are the four practices he identifies.

Now, اللهُ warns the Prophet ﷺ about sitting with those whose hearts He has placed in a state of heedlessness. اللهُ clarifies elsewhere in the Qur'an that when people deviated, He caused their deviation to increase. Those who turn away from the truth, اللهُ places in a state of غَفْلَة. غَفْلَة, then, is itself a punishment. A Muslim who is in غَفْلَة is experiencing a consequence of his own disobedience toward اللهُ ﷻ. It is a serious condition — you do not want to be among the غَافِلِينَ.

And yet, in all honesty, all of us will move in and out of غَفْلَة. That is simply the nature of existence in this world. غَفْلَة ends the moment you die. What matters is that you spend more of your life in a state of wakefulness than in a state of heedlessness. You want to be someone who is not in غَفْلَة.

This is why Sayyidina Umar said that the best remembrance of اللهُ is when you reach a limit He has set. In other words, when you are on the verge of committing a sin — none of you drink wine, but imagine you were about to — and in that moment you remember اللهُ and think, "I am about to put fire into my stomach," and that remembrance becomes a زَجْرٌ, a مَنِيع, a deterrent that prevents you from the حَرَامٌ — that is the best ذِكْرٌ. When the desire for disobedience rises and the remembrance of اللهُ holds you back: that is what he means. So there are degrees of غَفْلَة, and what he is saying is that the disease is the غَفْلَة that prevents you from fulfilling what اللهُ has commanded and avoiding what He has prohibited. Anything beyond that, remembering اللهُ is مُسْتَحَبٌّ.

اللهُ has prescribed remembrance at certain fixed times of day. He did not make remembrance throughout the entire day an obligation, because not everyone is capable of that. He said: remember Me at appointed times, أَوْقَاتٌ مُعَيَّنَةٌ. But He also encourages us to make much remembrance of اللهُ — and that is نَفْل, recommended, not obligatory. The more you remember اللهُ, the less likely you are to fall into the غَفْلَة you do not want. And again, the disease of the heart is specifically the غَفْلَة that prevents you from fulfilling your obligations or refraining from what has been prohibited.

As for the opposite — those in whose hearts اللهُ has written إِيمَان — one of the meanings here relates to writing itself: to inscribe without diacritical marks or voweling signs. That is one dimension of the meaning. The إِيمَان that اللهُ writes into the heart is of that nature.

The next disease is غِلٌّ. غِلٌّ is a disease of the heart related to rancor — resentful malice. The root word is a ثُلَاثِي, a three-letter root that can go three ways: غِلٌّ, غِلّ, and غِلٌّ. The غِلٌّ or the غِلٌّ is a قَيْد, a shackle. The people of the Fire have a غِلٌّ that binds them. The Prophet ﷺ was sent to relieve humanity of their burdens and the things that were binding them — that is what the غِلٌّ is. And غِلٌّ is when the heart becomes bound, as though shackled, to treachery, malice, or the desire to betray or harm someone. There is a lock holding it in place, just as a handcuff has the mechanism that holds it closed.

What holds the غِلٌّ together is resentful malice — something that results from being angry at a person and keeping that anger inside the heart until it festers and eventually becomes a desire to harm that person. This is why among the things اللهُ mentions in the Qur'an is the supplication: do not place in our hearts غِلٌّ toward the believers. You do not want غِلٌّ in your heart toward any believer, because it will kill your heart. Literally kill it.

One thing اللهُ does in جَنَّة is remove غِلٌّ entirely. مُؤْمِنٌ may die still carrying غِلٌّ, but you do not want to bring it with you. اللهُ says in the Qur'an: وَنَزَعۡنَا مَا فِی صُدُورِهِم مِّنۡ غِلٍّ — "And We will remove whatever غِلٌّ is in their hearts," and He makes them brothers, reclining together on couches.

In the دُنْيَا, سَيِّدِنَا عَلِيٍّ had an experience of this. A man who had fought against him — it may have been at the Battle of جَمَال — came to him and apologized. And that is the verse سَيِّدِنَا عَلِيٍّ recited: We removed from their hearts. He was saying, الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ, اللهُ will take that غِلٌّ out of the heart. You can carry rancor toward a person and اللهُ can remove it. So ask اللهُ to remove the غِلٌّ from you.

He then tells us how to do it: أَحْسِنْ إِلَيْهِ — do good to that person. That is what the man did when he went to سَيِّدِنَا عَلِيٍّ. He humbled himself and apologized, and that was his إِحْسَان. If you have غِلٌّ toward a مُؤْمِن, do good to him. The nature of إِحْسَان is captured in a hadith: جُبِلَتِ الْقُلُوبُ عَلَى حُبِّ مَنْ أَحْسَنَ إِلَيْهَا — by their nature, hearts are inclined to love those who do good to them. اللهُ has built this inclination into the heart regardless of a person's will. If you do good to someone, اللهُ has made his heart naturally drawn toward you.

<!-- page 139 -->

Now some of the Arabs noted — and an Arab poet said — that when you do good to a noble person you win him over, but if you do good to a lowly person it can make him more rebellious, more agitated. So there are people like that. But you have done your part. If you do good to someone in order to cure your own heart, that is what اللهُ is asking of you. You do your best. If the other person does not respond, you have done what was in your power. You are then left to make دُعَاء for them and to remember that اللهُ can forgive them.

He also says this will cause your enemies — meaning شَيْطَان and his minions who love to sow غِلٌّ among the believers — to despair. شَيْطَان loves to do this. He loves to set the believers at each other's throats. I once asked a شَيْخٌ why it is that so many disbelievers seem to live peacefully together without much conflict, while even sincere Muslims often fight one another. He said: because شَيْطَان is not occupied with the disbelievers anymore. They are already in كُفْر — he can leave them alone. But he is obsessed with the people of إِيمَان. He works on them constantly, trying to make them stray. That is why, as has been transmitted, شَيْطَان has despaired of being worshipped on the Arabian Peninsula, but he has not despaired of causing فِتْنَة and dissension among the people. We have to be vigilant against him.

<!-- page 140 -->

And تُقْنِتُهُ — the way you cause شَيْطَان to despair when غِلٌّ begins to arise in the heart is to reject it and to do good to that person at precisely the moment you want to harm them.

Then remember what came in the authentic hadith about Mondays and Thursdays. According to that hadith, the Prophet ﷺ said that اللهُ forgives people on those two days, and when the angels bring the records, they say: there are two people here who are in conflict, both believers. And اللهُ says: leave them — leave them until they reconcile with one another. What this means is that if you carry غِلٌّ toward a fellow believer, اللهُ is not forgiving you. If you are at war with another Muslim, that forgiveness is withheld. All you have to do is extend the greeting with sincerity. That is all. Do not turn your back. If you say السَّلَامُ عَلَيْكُمْ and they do not reply, you have fulfilled your obligation. It is then in their hands. Do what you can — that is all you are responsible for. But this is a frightening hadith. You do not want animosity toward another believer, because it will prevent you from receiving اللهُ's forgiveness.

Now, to — أَعُوذُ بِاللهِ — this is the significant one: فَخْر and كِبْرٌ. كِبْرٌ. فَخْر.

<!-- page 141 -->

فَخْر is, at its core, boasting. Boasting is a disease of the heart. فَخْر differs from مُبَاحَة and similar things in that فَخْر, as understood here, is boasting about something you have not yourself earned. It is boasting about lineage — saying, for instance, "I am a شَرِيف," or trading on what your father or ancestors did.

One of the most striking moments illustrating this is from the Battle of Uhud. The Arabs would shout out, "أَنَا ابْنُ فُلَانٍ" — "I am the son of so-and-so" — as a form of war cry, a display of lineage. The Prophet ﷺ would respond: "أَنَا ابْنُ الْعَوَاتِكِ" — "I am the son of the women named Atika." The Arabs did not boast through their mothers, so he was doing the opposite of what they intended. Many of his grandmothers bore the name Atika, and that was his response to their boasting of fathers.

<!-- page 142 -->

On the matter of boasting about parents, الْخُدَّام mentions — and the saying goes — لَئِنْ فَخَرْتُ بِآبَاءٍ ذَوِي شَرَفٍ، لَقَدْ صَدَقْتَ وَلَكِنْ بِئْسَ مَا وَلَدُوا: if you boast about ancestors who truly had honor, what you say may even be true — if they were from بَنِي هَاشِمٍ, for instance, then yes, it is factually true — but what a wretched child they left behind. If you are boasting about your parents and have yourself accomplished nothing, then as the Arabs say: وَلَيْسَ الْفَتَى مَنْ قَالَ كَانَ أَبِي، إِنَّمَا الْفَتَى مَنْ قَالَ هَا أَنَا — the man is not the one who says "my father was." The man is the one who says "here I am." You do something yourself.

Jalal al-Din al-Rumi has a poem in which he names all the great figures who came before, and then says: but do not be content with the stories of those who went before you — go out and create your own story. Be one of those people that those who come after you will speak about. This is part of our teaching: the idea of being genuinely significant — not for the sake of دُنْيَا, but for the sake of being someone who actually does something.

This is boasting. Saying "my father did this" — it is what small children do. Children say, "My dad is a fireman." "Well, my dad is an astronaut." It is childish. Mentioning a parent's إِحْسَان, something good they did for you — that is a different matter entirely, and there is nothing wrong with it. But boasting is something we all recognize.
