And finally, he speaks of taking refuge — of returning to the One to whom all affairs return, since Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى says that to Allah all affairs return. Seeking refuge in Allah is, above all else, the truest remedy.

This is why a scholar of the path says that the only real cure for all these diseases of the heart is to go before Allah in a state of complete neediness — please, I need to change, and I need You to help me change — with a genuine sense of urgency. If that is the state a person is in, then إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, Allah will help them change.

For my own part, I truly believe that my coming to Islam was the result of a state I entered before I was Muslim — a state of complete افْتِقَار. I called on Allah by the name I knew, which was God, and I simply asked: give me guidance, show it to me. I was in a state of utter neediness. Most of the people I have met who became Muslim will confirm something similar: that there was a moment of absolute — just show me what to do. And then the guidance opened up, and it became very clear. خَلَاص. After that it becomes easy, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ.

I believe Allah is merciful, and that where there is sincerity, even within a person's limited and imperfect understanding, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ that is enough. There is an apocryphal story — and because it is apocryphal, no عَقِيدَة is based on it, I want to be clear about that — but it carries a wisdom that has been told: about the Prophet Musa, عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ, encountering a man who is speaking to Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى about wanting to wash His feet, give Him a massage, and tend to Him — a man who is an anthropomorphist without knowing it.

And Musa, moved by righteousness, rebuked the man and explained the true meaning of تَوْحِيد. Yet Allah reminded Musa that he had been harsh, for the man had spoken those words out of love — he was simply ignorant. The right response to ignorance is to teach. So the point is that everyone is at the level where they are. Even the one bowing before an idol — that is his understanding; that is where he stands. This is why Allah says:

وَلَا تَجَعَلُوا لِلَّهِ أَنْدَادًا

Do not set up rivals to Allah —

وَأَنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

— while you know it is wrong. Once knowledge reaches a person, that is when شِرْك destroys him. If someone has no knowledge, and Allah's messenger has not yet reached him, Allah will not punish him:

وَمَا كُنَّا مُعَذِّبِينَ حَتَّىٰ نَبْعَثَ رَسُولًۭا

So if someone does not know Islam and is genuinely seeking guidance, that guidance comes, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ.

وَاللَّهُ تَعَالَى عَنْهُمْ

Then the author says:

بِأَنْ يَكُونَ كَغَرِيقًا أَوْ كَمَنْ ضَلَّ بِتِيهِنْ لَا يَرَى الْغِيَاثَةِ مِنْ سِوَى الْمُهَيْمَنَ الْعَظِيمِ الْقَدْرِ وَهُوَ الْمُجِيبُ دَعْوَةٍ مُقَرِّي

He is saying that you must be like someone who is drowning, or like someone lost in a barren desert who sees no source of help except the Almighty God. This is precisely what happened to Yusuf Islam. I heard him describe it in a lecture: he was caught in the undertow at Malibu, being dragged out to sea — genuinely terrifying — and in that moment he called on God completely and promised: give me guidance and I will follow it, whatever you ask of me. That is the غَرِيقٌ — the drowning man — with nowhere left to turn.

And then there is Ikrimah ibn Abi Jahl, whose guidance came on a boat carrying Abyssinian Christians. When the waves began to overwhelm the vessel and everyone feared they would drown, Ikrimah started calling on Hubal. The Christians on board laughed at him. They said: you are calling on an idol made of wood, sitting in Mecca, to save you out here? And in that moment it struck him — how absurd it was. He realized he could only call on Allah. And تَوْحِيد entered his heart right there. That is the state of the drowning man. And the author says this is in fact the most beneficial state — the one that will truly help us. It must reach the point where you are like a man going under.

So that is that.

<!-- page 193 -->

Now, on the actions that purify the heart, he says:

وَمَابِهِ لَلْقَلْبِ صَفْوً مِنْ عَمَلٍ أَنْفَعَهُ هُوَ الْمُدَامُ وَلَوْ يَقِلْ

As for the action most beneficial for purifying the heart, none surpasses the one that is consistent — even if it is small. This comes from the hadith:

خَيْرُ الْأَعْمَالِ أَدْوَمُهَا وَإِنْ قَلَّ

The best actions are the ones maintained continuously, even if they are slight.

Think of it this way: if you take a coin and polish it hard with a few vigorous strokes, then set it down, versus taking the same coin and polishing it gently but without stopping — the second coin becomes bright and shiny in a way the first never will. One burst of effort cannot do what quiet consistency achieves over time. The heart works the same way. What encrusts it through accumulated wrong actions is not undone by one intense push. A little effort, maintained steadily, is what graدُعَاءlly breaks that crust away. That is why small deeds done consistently far outweigh large deeds done once.

The proof is in how the Companions conducted themselves. They were always consistent in their practices. The man who asked the Prophet about fasting every other day — the fast of Dawud — came to regret it in his old age. But the scholars took that very regret as evidence: the Companions held even voluntary practices so seriously that they would not abandon what they had taken upon themselves. It was not that the practice was obligatory — it was that they had committed to it.

And if you read the ahadith, you find that the Prophet used to fast so much that the Companions thought he never broke his fast — and then there were times when they thought he never fasted at all. He used to spend so much of the night in prayer that they thought he never slept — and then there were times when they thought he barely did any قِيَامٌ.

<!-- page 194 -->

There is something very important to understand about the Messenger of Allah. He is the universal prophet — his being encompasses the possibilities of every good believer. So in him, every good believer has a model. And different believers are opened to different things. There are people for whom قِيَامٌ is not the door Allah opens for them. There are people for whom recitation of the Quran is not their particular opening — their opening may be in hadith. Others are opened through صَدَقَةٌ, not fasting. Others through fasting, not صَدَقَةٌ. Some through wisdom. It is very rare for anyone to be opened through all of them simultaneously.

This is why, when the Prophet described the different gates of paradise — the gate for the fasters, the gate for the mujahideen — Abu Bakr asked: يَا رَسُولَ اللَّهِ، أَيَدْخُلُ أَحَدٌ هَذِهِ الْأَبْوَابَ كُلَّهَا؟ Can anyone enter through all of them? And the Prophet said: وَأَرْجُو أَنْ تَكُونَ مِنْهُمْ — yes, and I hope you are one of them. As they say, I hope he was. The point is: keep working. That applies to all of us.

So some people are given broad and wide-ranging تَوْفِيق. Imam Malik, for instance, wore fine clothes, used the best scents, burned oud — and to an outsider, especially among the ascetics, this looked extravagant. One of the زُهْدs wrote him a letter saying: I cannot believe your state. You eat the finest flour, you wear expensive clothes, the perfume alone must cost a fortune — you would do better giving all of that up and spending less time with people. He was, after all, teaching extensively in the masjid.

So the زُهْد was essentially telling Imam Malik: compared to me, you are in a bad state. This is what is called مَرْجَبٌ — essentially a kind of spiritual boasting. And Imam Malik wrote back an extraordinary letter. He said: يَا عَبْدَ اللَّهِ — servant of Allah — Allah has opened for His servants many doors of goodness.

<!-- page 195 -->

He then says: include action done in the absence of witnesses, and action done out of love for Allah or in awe of Him. So one of the things that benefits the heart is performing acts that no one else can see. Giving charity privately. Praying in a way that no one observes. This protects you from رِيَاءٌ and عُجْب and everything that flows from them.

There is a story — it was actually Fudayl ibn Ayyad, not Bishr al-Hafi — who was once walking down a street and overheard someone say: there goes a man who spends the entire night in prayer. When Fudayl heard it, he burst into tears and said: I cannot remember ever spending a single complete night in prayer. And then he said: how great is the honor Allah has bestowed on me by veiling me and making people think I am better than I am. That idea — of concealing one's state, of hiding — is the better path.

And then action done out of love: that is the highest. The scholars say there are two principal motivations. One is خَوْف — fear — which, when it reaches the level of خَوْفُ مَقَامِهِ, becomes جَلَاء: awe of Allah's greatness, a profound sense of هَيْبَة in the divine presence. The other is مَحَبَّة — love — born of Allah's mercy, yearning for Allah, doing things out of that longing. That is the highest motive, and the author tells you that the final مَقَامٌ is مَحَبَّة.

He also says that the purest actions are those of the زُهْد — the one who is free of worldly wants, who does what he does seeking nothing in return, no thanks, no gratitude, not even reward in the آخِرَة. He does it solely for Allah's sake. That is the highest and best action for the heart. زُهْد is not an outward condition — it is the absence of attachment within the heart. The زُهْد is not like the Christian ascetic who wears rough clothing and practices flagellation. In Islam, the ascetic is the one for whom the world has no grip on the heart. That is why Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf was a زُهْد, even though he was one of the wealthiest men in Mecca.

<!-- page 196 -->

Then the author says that the actions of those who strive out of hope are higher and more exalted than those driven by fear alone. If fear is your only motivation, that is still valid — Allah tells us to have خَوْف — but hope lifts the action higher. And the Prophet, صلى الله عليه وسلم, is the حَبِيبُ اللهِ — the beloved of Allah — and Allah loved him. That is the pinnacle: mutual love between the servant and his Lord.

He then says: particularly, action whose benefits extend to others. An action that purifies the heart is made greater when it reaches beyond the self. Charity does not only benefit your own soul — it benefits another human being. And in every creature that possesses a moist liver — that is, every living thing — there is reward. Water an animal and there is reward. Plant a tree and every creature that eats from it, for as long as that tree stands, earns you reward. All of these things are elevated precisely because they extend outward.

Then he speaks of fasting in relation to one's character. If you are someone who has poor control over his appetite — if gluttony is your weakness — then fasting is particularly powerful for you. It strikes at the root of that disorder. But for someone who already has reasonable control over his relationship with food, fasting is still good, yet it will not work the same transformation as it does on the one who genuinely needs to discipline that aspect of himself.

Similarly with the miser: for a generous person, giving in charity is natural. For someone who is stingy, giving out is a far greater struggle — and for that very reason, it carries more weight. The hadith on this is direct: the best صَدَقَةٌ is what you give when you are healthy, when you want to hold on to it, and when you fear poverty. That is when giving out really costs you something. That is the صَدَقَةٌ that truly rubs شَيْطَان's nose in it.

<!-- page 197 -->

Then he says: you earn reward in proportion to your avoidance of wrong. And this brings up a related point. There are people in the أُمَّة who spent the better part of their lives in disobedience, then in old age made تَوْبَة and began lecturing the young. But that person does not carry the same مَقَامٌ as the young person who had every opportunity and every impulse toward disobedience — and chose otherwise. The older person no longer has the energy for most of those wrongs anyway. تَوْبَة comes more easily when the temptation is largely gone.

There is also a real danger in the opposite direction: the heart that has been in disobedience for decades sometimes becomes so encrusted that a genuine return becomes very difficult. And yet الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ, many Muslims do make تَوْبَة as they grow older. They reach forty, forty-five, and something shifts. In Tunisia, they say the year of responsibility is forty — because that is when the وَحْيٌ came to the Prophet. Which has given some people the mistaken impression that you can do whatever you like before forty. This is ignorance, pure and simple. And yet it has become a kind of cultural artifact in parts of the Muslim world — people who say: when I go to حَجٌّ, I will grow my beard and become a proper Muslim. Some of them do come back changed. But these attitudes are a marker of how far we have drifted from our tradition.

What Allah loves is the young person who is raised up in obedience. شَبَابٌ — youth — the Arabs generally put between fifteen and thirty-five, some say up to forty. After forty you are no longer a صَحْب; you are a رَجُلٌ, a man.

<!-- page 198 -->

Then he says: and also the contribution of a miser — giving out of the best of his wealth, while he is in sound health, seeking the pleasure of Allah — all of these things purify the heart. Moreover, anything done with discretion and concealment is also purifying. And the best of endeavors is the one that purifies the heart. So the best thing you can do with your life is what is genuinely good for your heart.

Likewise — and this is the other side — the worst things are those that harden the heart, especially when a person persists in them or takes pleasure in them. The نَفْس that enjoyed them: those are the things most destructive to the heart.

One thing that hardens the heart is speaking at length without mentioning Allah. This is recorded in the مُوَطَّأ of Imam Malik. سَيِّدِنَا عِيسَى is reported to have said: do not sit in gatherings without remembering Allah, for a gathering in which Allah is not mentioned will harden the heart — and you will not even notice it happening. The hardening comes graدُعَاءlly, spiritually and even, one might say, physically — and you are unaware. But the more the tongue is occupied with remembrance of Allah, the softer the heart becomes. That is simply the reality. The more ذِكْرٌ someone does, the more compassion flows from them, and the more they benefit.

<!-- page 199 -->

Now this brings us to a truly important point. Allah says that a wrong action that produces in its wake a sense of shame and brokenness before Allah is better than an act of obedience that produces pride and arrogance in the one who performs it. And this is the secret of wrong actions — the wisdom concealed within them. Allah permitted us to stumble so that through the stumbling we might arrive at this state. The Prophet, صلى الله عليه وسلم, said: if you did not commit wrong actions, I would fear for you something worse than wrong actions — namely, being pleased with yourself, admiring yourself, vanity, the feeling that you are something wonderful. That is worse.

Now, Sidi Ahmad Zarruq clarifies this so there is no confusion. He says the good of طَهَارَة is in the very essence of طَهَارَة, and the evil he is describing is what obedience results in when it goes wrong. So do not misread this as saying that there is such a thing as good مَعْصِيَةٌ or bad طَهَارَة. طَاءٌ is good. مَعَاصِيَه is bad. Full stop. What is being said is about the secondary results. If the secondary result of an act of obedience was arrogance — and the secondary result of a disobedience was تَوْبَة — then in terms of what was ultimately produced, the تَوْبَة was the better outcome. The obedience generated poison; the disobedience generated a return to Allah.

Think of people in America who commit terrible crimes, go to prison, and find their way to Allah there. That happens often. The disobedience was genuinely wrong. But it became part of the causal chain through which Allah guided them. It was not a good thing — but it resulted in a good thing. And that is what he is saying: a wrong action that results in humility is preferable to an obedience that results in arrogance, in terms of what they produce in the heart.

Then he says:

وَذَرَّةٌ مِنْ عَمِلَ الْقَلْبِ الْعَلِي مِثْرُ الرِّضَى وَالزُهْدِ وَالتَوَكْرِ أَغْضَرُوا عَنْدَ اللَّهِ مِنْ جِبَالِ شَمَخْنَ مِنْ ظَوَاهِرَ الْعَمَالِ

An atom's weight of a lofty inward action — such as contentment, زُهْد, or trust in Allah — is greater with Allah than mountains of outward deeds performed without any heart. And this is the same principle as:

فَإِنَّ رَكَعَتَيْنِ مِنْ مُحِبٍ خَيْرَ مِنْ أَلْثٍ مِنْ غَيْرِ حُبِّ

Two raka'ats prayed with love are better than a thousand prayed without it. Immeasurably better.

<!-- page 200 -->

Then he mentions what Imam Sahnun, may Allah be pleased with him, said:

وَتَرْكُوا دِرْهَمٍ لِكَوْنِهِ حَظَرَ أَظْدَرُ مِنْ تَصَدُقَاتٍ وَعُمَرَ

Leaving a single دَانِق of what Allah has made حَرَامٌ is better than performing seventy thousand حَجٌّ and عُمْرَة. A دَانِق is the smallest unit of currency in the Arabic tradition — a farthing, a penny. And they also relate that Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq said there are four things whose very best still carries a kind of diminishment:

أَرْبَعَةٌ عِزُّهَا وَظُلُّ

الدَّيْنُ وَلَوْ دَانِقٌ

A debt, even if it is only a دَانِق.

أَبِنْتْ وَلَوْ مَرْيَمْ

A daughter, even if she is Maryam — not because daughters are lesser than sons in worth, but because in that social context she lacks the physical strength and social protection a man possesses. And the word عَزِيز in Arabic, let us not forget, also means قَوِيّ — physically strong, or strong in position. عَزِيزُ النَّصْرِ.

عَزِيزُ النَّصْرِ.

وَسُؤَادْ وَلَوْ كَيفَ الطَّرِيق

Asking a question, even if it is only: which way do I go? There is a humility inherent in asking that is itself a small diminishment of the self — not a wrong thing, just an honest reality.

وَالْوَحْشَةَ وَلَوْ لَيْلًا

And being alone — even for a single night.

Now we come to the root causes of the diseases. And what I will do is cover the treatment of diseases through ذِكْرٌ next week — إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, Shaykh Khatri will be here, and I will ask him to teach that class. He knows this book deeply. He loves it. He has spent a great deal of time with it. I have read sections of it with him — and I read much of it with Shaykh Abdullah as well. We actually completed a خَتْم of it on حَجٌّ, on the day of Arafat. We went through every disease, named them all, and made دُعَاء asking Allah to purify us of all of them. إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, for all of us.

Now he says: the comprehensive root — in the view of both Imam al-Hilali and Imam Ibn Askar — of all these diseases is love of the temporal world, حُبُّ الدُّنْيَا. There is some difference of opinion on this, as we saw: Ibn Abbad considered the root to be طَمَعٌ — desire. But you can see how طَمَعٌ flows from حُبُّ الدُّنْيَا. And when Ibn Askar says the root is حُبُّ الرِّيَاسَةِ — love of status and power — even that is حُبُّ الدُّنْيَا, because why do you want status? You want power, you want the world. So in the end it really does come back to حُبُّ الدُّنْيَا. There is a hadith on this — though it is classified as weak — رَأْسُ كُلِّ خَطِيئَةٍ حُبُّ الْعَاجِلَةِ: the love of this world is at the root of every wrong action.

Ibn Ata'illah, on the other hand, considered the root to be a man's self-satisfaction with himself. That is a distinct and fascinating take. He was a Maliki jurist who died in 709 AH, corresponding to 1309 CE, and he is considered one of the greatest masters of this science. He wrote the حِكَم. Imam al-Banna said that had it been permissible to recite something other than the Quran in prayer, it would have been permissible to recite the حِكَم — a clear hyperbole, but an indication of the profound esteem in which the work was held.

<!-- page 202 -->

In the thirty-fifth aphorism, Ibn Ata'illah says that the source of every disobedience, every indifference, and every passion is self-satisfaction with the self. And the source of every obedience, every vigilance, and every virtue is dissatisfaction with the self. He says: it is better for you to keep company with an ignorant man who is dissatisfied with himself than with a learned man who is satisfied with himself. And then: what knowledge is there in a self-satisfied scholar? And what ignorance is there in an unlearned man who is dissatisfied with himself — and who therefore keeps asking, keeps wanting to learn?

This connects directly to something strange about modern culture. Think of the whole industry of self-help books aimed at getting rid of shame, building confidence, feeling good about yourself. But why is the person standing in that self-help aisle in the first place? Because he is dissatisfied with himself. And if he were completely self-satisfied, he would never pick up a book. The author of the book, incidentally, has no real interest in your becoming self-satisfied — because then you would stop buying books. Most of that industry is built on keeping you just dissatisfied enough to keep purchasing. But the point stands: dissatisfaction with the self is what drives a person to want to change and improve.

If anyone here were truly satisfied with themselves, you would not be in this class studying the diseases of the heart. The very fact that people have these struggles is what moves them to seek help. If you feel you lack guidance, you seek guidance. The one who submits to Allah begins seeking because he knows he needs Allah's direction. But if you are walking through life perfectly content — I am already guided, I have no need — you will never reach out.

<!-- page 203 -->

Now, Sidi Ahmad Zarruq identifies three signs of contentment with the soul.

The first is seeing that the حَقٌّ belongs to oneself. This is interesting to consider in the modern context: we have a bill of rights but no bill of responsibilities. Islam is far more focused on your responsibilities than on your rights. The Quran, in a real sense, is a bill of Allah's rights over us. And the concept of human rights, as understood in the secular modern framework, carries a very different set of assumptions than the Islamic one. Human beings do have rights — but in the Islamic understanding, those rights originate with Allah. They are Allah's rights embedded in His servants. If someone is protected from oppression, it is because Allah has a حَقٌّ that His creation not be oppressed. If someone takes a life in accordance with شَرِيعَة, it is because Allah demands His right be fulfilled. The human being has a right — but it was given by Allah, and the other person fulfills it because he fears Allah.

This is why تَقْوَى — fear of Allah — is the very foundation of a Muslim society. The first sign of self-satisfaction, then, is always seeing the rights as belonging to the self: they wronged me, it is all their fault, I am always the injured party. This is an extremely common psychological pattern.

There is a Moroccan story about a shaykh and a self-satisfied scholar who disliked him. This scholar came to visit one day and said: the least of my students is better than you. The shaykh said: show me. Come to my مَدْرَسَة tomorrow. The next day, the shaykh secretly instructed all his students: when the visiting scholar enters, do not return his سَلَامٌ. And when my student comes in — who had been set up to visit — do not say سَلَامٌ عَلَيْكُمْ to him either.

<!-- page 204 -->

The self-satisfied scholar walked in, no one returned his greeting, and his reaction was immediate outrage: are these your students? Have they not heard what Allah commands? Have they not heard His words — return a greeting with what is better, or at least return it in kind?

Then the shaykh's own student came in. No one returned his سَلَامٌ. He said: سَلَامٌ عَلَيْكُمْ — again. Still nothing. Then he said: O brothers, please forgive me. I must have done something wrong. What did I do? Why have you not responded? Whatever I have done, please excuse me.

Those two responses reveal everything. The self-satisfied man arrived saying: I have a right, and you have violated it. The student arrived thinking: what right of yours did I fail to fulfill? One looked outward in demand; the other looked inward in concern. That is why, even as the least of the shaykh's students, the young man showed something the scholar — for all his learning — did not possess. This is the حِكْمَةٌ behind Ibn Ata'illah's words: what knowledge does a self-satisfied scholar truly have? And what real ignorance remains in an unlearned man who is genuinely dissatisfied with himself and wants to be taught?

The second sign of contentment with the self is ignoring one's own faults by interpreting them away — convincing yourself that you are already in a state of purity, even if you have to construct a justification to get there. For instance, a person gets angry and tells himself: well, the Prophet said anger can be from the best of the أُمَّة, and I am from the best of the أُمَّة, so this is fine. He sees the fault but reframes it so that it no longer registers as a fault. This is what the poet meant when he said: the eye of contentment does not see faults. Just as the eye of one in love cannot see the beloved's flaws — and one of the signs of falling out of love is that you begin noticing them all: I never noticed before that one ear is larger than the other.

<!-- page 205 -->

The third sign of self-satisfaction is excessive self-pity — poor me, I am having such a hard time — chronic complaining and self-indulgent lamentation.

Those are the three signs of a person who is satisfied with himself. The three signs of the opposite — of a person genuinely dissatisfied with himself — are these:

The first is constant self-scrutiny, a persistent suspicion of one's own intentions. And this is modeled even by سَيِّدina Yusuf, peace be upon him, who is a prophet — مَعْصُوم. There is a scholarly disagreement about the precise meaning of his statement, which سَيِّد Ahmad Zarruq and others discuss. But سَيِّدina Yusuf said: I will not declare my نَفْس innocent — for by its nature, the نَفْس commands to evil. So always examine your intentions. Ask yourself: am I doing this for رِيَاءٌ? حَضْرَة Abu Bakr said: the only one who feels safe from نِفَاق is the hypocrite. The true believer is always watchful over himself, always alert to the blemishes of the soul. This is reflected in the دُعَاء the Prophet made: do not leave me to my own soul even for the blink of an eye, or less than that.

The second sign is forcing the self to do difficult things — things it dislikes. Eating less. Giving out money. Going out in the path of Allah. These are مَكْرُوه to the نَفْس — the self naturally recoils from them. جِهَادٌ is genuinely hard. Charity is genuinely hard. The نَفْس wants to hold on to what it has. Compelling it to do otherwise is one of the marks of a person who has not surrendered to self-satisfaction.

<!-- page 206 -->

Abu Uthman said: whoever sees anything good about himself has not truly seen the faults of his soul.

Now there is an obvious question here — and it is an important one, because the whole Western discourse around self-help, psychology, and modern wellness is built on the opposite premise. Is this not just self-loathing? Is this not low self-esteem? Is this not exactly what the self-esteem movement told us is harmful?

And the answer is: look at the actual people shaped by this tradition. Look at the real شُيُوخ. They are not miserable. They are not broken. They carry genuine humility alongside genuine dignity. Consider Aisha, may Allah be pleased with her. She was the wife of the best of creation. She was physically beautiful. She was among the most brilliant minds in Islamic history — and in human history. She came from a noble family, the aristocracy of قُرَيْش. And yet, when she yearned for Allah to declare her innocence in the incident of the slander, she said:

وَكُنتُ أَرَى نَفْسِي حَقِيرًۭا

I considered myself so insignificant that I would never have imagined the Quran would be revealed on my account.

Despite all of that — beauty, lineage, brilliance, position — she did not regard herself as someone for whom Quran would descend. And yet Aisha was not a woman without self-confidence. She was a woman of enormous strength. She was one of the very few people who questioned the Prophet directly — she challenged him on a point of Quran when he said that a person given an easy reckoning would enter paradise:

يَدْخُلُ الْجَنَّةِ بِلَا حِسَابٍ وَلَمْ يَخُلُ اللَّهُ فَسَوْفْ يُحَاسِبُ حِسَابٍ يَسِيرًۭا

She pressed him: but how can you say that, given what Allah says? Most of the Companions would not have done that. It takes a person of extraordinary inner confidence to question the Prophet of Allah on a matter of Quranic interpretation.

<!-- page 207 -->

She also led an army out. She later regretted it, but in the moment she said: he is wrong and we are right — let us go resolve this. She rode out in her hawdaj to the Battle of the Camel. And when one of the men addressed her as يَا أُمَّ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ, she replied:

لَا أُمِّنُكَ

Which in the Arabic of the time carried the force of: do not call me your mother — you are no son of mine. That is a sharp, strong response. She was a strong woman.

And yet, with all of that, her sense of her own standing before Allah was one of profound smallness. That is what this teaching is about. It is not about collapsing into self-loathing. It is not about erasing confidence. It is about never becoming content with where you are — always knowing there is more, always wanting to improve. Be confident, yes. But do not be complacent.

No matter how accomplished you become, there is always someone better. No matter how much Quran you have mastered, there is always more mastery to pursue. The master calligrapher looks at his work and sees room to improve. If he stops seeing it, he stops improving. And every one of these sciences — every field of knowledge, every spiritual state, every skill — is endless. True mastery belongs not to the one who has arrived, but to the one who never stops. The one committed to constant improvement is the real master, because they are always moving forward, always refining their technique. But the moment a person is self-satisfied, the improvement stops — and in a very real sense, so does the life of the heart.

<!-- page 208 -->

This is what he means when he says that self-dissatisfaction acts as an impetus to seek virtuous character and to remain vigilant about what is unbecoming. So you remain watchful, الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ.
