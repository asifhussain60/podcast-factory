The next disease is كَرَاهَةُ الذَّمِّ — dislike of blame. ذِمّ means to blame someone. By human nature, we do not like to be blamed; it is something inherent in us. We teach this to our children early on: we praise them for certain things and blame them for others, and they quickly learn that praise is pleasant — it comes with smiles, gifts, warmth, hugs — while blame is painful, accompanied by frowns, anger, raised voices, and sometimes physical punishment. So from very early on, children learn to want praise and to dislike blame. The problem is that this natural aversion becomes a disease: كَرَاهِيَةُ ذَمٍّ.

He says: كَرَاهَةُ ذَمِّ بَنَانٍ مَأْلُوفٍ — it is a well-known disease. وَالنَّظَرُ إِلَى الْعِبَادِ وَالْوُقُوفُ مَعَهُمْ حِجَابٌ عَنْ مَقَامِ الْإِحْسَانِ What he is saying is that displeasure with blame is a well-known disease of the heart. Preoccupation with what people think of you — craving their praise and resenting their criticism — becomes a barrier between you and the station of إِحْسَان, because your actions begin to be driven by the desire to be praised or the fear of being blamed.

<!-- page 248 -->
One example from the seerah class: Sa'd ibn Mu'adh رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ. When the Prophet ﷺ asked the Aus to accept his judgment regarding Bani Quraydah — since Bani Quraydah were confederates of the Aus, and the Khazraj had secured the release of Bani Nadir through the intercession of Abdullah ibn Ubayy, the Aus wanted the same outcome for their confederates. The Prophet ﷺ put the matter to Sa'd. Now, what Bani Quraydah had done was actually far graver than what Bani Nadir had done — they had joined the enemy army during the siege. Bani Nadir had attempted to assassinate the Prophet ﷺ, which is a more serious crime in the larger picture, but the treachery of Bani Quraydah during battle was of a different order. In any case, when Sa'd came forward, people were urging him: please intercede with the Prophet, get them off just as Bani Nadir were spared. And he said — and this is the important point — that he had reached a place in his مَوْقِف where he had no fear of the blame of any blamer. He was near death. He said: الْآنَ وَجَدْتُ نَفْسِي فِي مَقَامٍ لَا أَخَافُ فِيهِ لَوْمَةَ لَائِمٍ — I have found myself in a position where I am not concerned with what people will say.

What the Aus were worried about was public perception: our confederates were all killed while the Khazraj got theirs off. Sa'd ibn Mu'adh said: I am about to meet my Lord. I am not concerned with what people will say.

<!-- page 249 -->
The point is that, in reality, you are in that very moment at every instant of your life. Death is simply veiled from you — that veil is طُولُ الْأَمَلِ, which is another disease in itself. It makes you think you will be around. But that is a complete illusion; you have no guarantees whatsoever. So what the example of Sa'd illustrates is مَقَامُ الْإِحْسَانِ — all he could see was Allah. Creation did not enter into it.

That is the whole point of كَرَاهِيَةُ الذَّمِّ: when you start worrying about what others will think, it often prevents you from doing the right thing — because very often, the right thing is precisely what everyone else will oppose. That is the nature of this world. Taking a courageous stance usually means taking a stance you will be blamed for. And courageous positions are often only recognized much later.

Geronimo is a good example. When he was fighting the US cavalry, he was portrayed as an evil, horrific barbarian. Now he is seen as a hero by both whites and Native Americans alike — a great freedom fighter defending his land. At the time, even his own people turned against him; most had already surrendered, and he kept fighting until he was finally betrayed by those around him. Malcolm X is another example. Once enough time passes and a person is no longer perceived as a threat, the very forces that opposed him will co-opt his legacy — that is what they do. But at the time, he was taking positions that virtually the whole of society was against. Had he been worried about their opinion, he could never have spoken the truth. That is what happens when people are consumed by concern for what others think: they lose the ability to take the courageous positions that are unpopular precisely because they are true, and that attract attack for that very reason.

Then he says that overcoming this barrier comes through the realization that there is no benefit or harm except from Allah. This is, as you will notice, a recurring cure for many of these diseases — genuinely recognizing two names of Allah: النَّافِعُ وَالضَّارُّ. النَّافِع is the One who benefits, and الضَّرُّ is the One who harms. These are attributes of Allah alone; no human being possesses them. Only Allah can truly benefit, and only Allah can truly harm. So if concern about what people think is preventing you from doing what you should do for the sake of Allah, you are not seeing reality. You are in a delusional state — one that is not only harmful to your iman but is simply out of accord with how things actually are.

This is why the Prophet ﷺ said to Ibn Abbas رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ, giving him this counsel: إذا استعنت فاستعن بالله — when you seek help, seek it only from Allah. وإذا سألت فاسأل الله — and when you ask, ask only from Allah. And then he said: know that even if all of creation were to gather together to harm you, they could not harm you except with what Allah has decreed for you. And if they gathered to benefit you, they could not benefit you except with what Allah has decreed. Creation can do nothing to you — they can neither harm you nor benefit you.

Now, this obviously does not mean ignoring the أَسْبَاب — the Prophet ﷺ wore two coats of chain armor at the Battle of Uhud. Those are أَسْبَاب. He knew better than anyone that no harm and no benefit come except from Allah, yet that did not negate using one's intellect and engaging with the world of أَسْبَاب. It also does not negate diplomacy, because as the poet said: لَقَدْ خُطَّهَا حَقٌّ وَلَٰكِنْ مَا كُلُّ حَقٍّ يُقَالُ — I said the truth, but not every truth needs to be said. There are times when discretion is the wiser course.

I know of a Libyan man — an Azhari graduate — who was part of an official delegation in Iraq at a large gathering. Someone was making a toast to the Ba'ath Party, and this man stood up and said: لَعَنَ اللَّهُ حِزْبَكَ وَمَنْ دَعَا لَهُ — may Allah curse your party and those who pray for it — and he went on cursing. The rest of the delegation were horrified and tried to cover for him, telling the hosts he was not quite right in the mind and not to pay him any attention. In the end, nothing happened to them. But the point is: he probably should not have been there supporting those people in the first place, and the outburst, however sincere, was not the most sagacious move.

كَلِمَةُ حَقٍّ أَمَامَ سُلْطَانٍ جَائِرٍ — speaking the truth in the face of an oppressive tyrant — the Prophet ﷺ called that the greatest jihad, and if you die for it, you are a shaheed without question. But that does not mean every moment calls for it. If there is long-term benefit to be weighed, if this is a situation where الْحَرْبُ خُدْعَةٌ — war is strategy — then you have to measure things carefully.

Then he says: يَسْتَمِدُّ الْحَرَامُ مِنْهُمَا جَرَّاءَ إِرَاءٍ مُحَرَّمٍ كَمَا الْغَزَّالِيُّ فَصَّلَ الْغَزَّالُ — the impermissible dimension of this disease is what leads in turn to the prohibited, as Imam al-Ghazali has clarified. In other words, while not liking to be blamed is natural, what becomes haram is whatever that aversion leads you to do that is itself haram. For instance: if you are obliged to enjoin the right and forbid the wrong, but you hold back out of worry about what people will say — out of fear of لَوْمَةُ لَائِمٍ, the blame of the blamer — and that fear causes you to abandon an obligation, that is where the disease becomes sinful. Or, say you are at work and it is time to pray, but you start thinking about what your co-workers will think if you ask to step out — because praying at work is not exactly popular in America. If that concern stops you from praying, that is clearly كَرَاهِيَةُ الذَّمِّ in a form that is not acceptable. Being concerned about people's opinion when doing something genuinely wrong is natural and appropriate. The disease — the haram — is when that concern prevents you from fulfilling your obligations.

Another important point about this disease: real iman does not become established in the heart until, as the tradition of Ibn Mas'ud states, لَا يُؤْمِنُ أَحَدُكُمْ حَتَّى يَسْتَوِيَ عِنْدَهُ الذَّمُّ وَالْمَدْحُ — blame and praise become equal to him. That is, you are acting for Allah, and you genuinely do not care what people think. If someone praises you — fine. If someone blames you — fine. You did it for the sake of Allah, and Allah knows that. That is what you are concerned with: not the eyes of others. That is the perfection of sincerity.

On a related note: one of the things I observed in West Africa that struck me as genuinely wise was that in the desert tradition, they never have young children perform displays of their Quran memorization — no "come on, let's hear you recite this" followed by everyone applauding. They do not put children on display like that. I think that practice, however well-intentioned it may seem, is actually harmful, because it plants the early roots of رِيَاءٌ — the desire for praise, for showing off. Parents naturally want to show their children off, and that desire is understandable.

Even Sayyidina Umar رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ — when Ibn Umar was in a gathering and the Prophet ﷺ asked which tree resembles the believer, standing firm in the desert, and everyone started guessing — the Qalha, the Zainab, all sorts of trees — Abdullah ibn Umar, who was a young boy sitting among the great صَحَابَة, immediately thought: it is the palm tree. But he was too shy to speak up in front of Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, and the senior companions. So he stayed quiet. When no one got the answer, the Prophet ﷺ told them it was the palm tree, and afterward Ibn Umar told his father that he had known. Umar said: I would have loved for you to have said it — he would have been proud for his son to have spoken up in that gathering.
