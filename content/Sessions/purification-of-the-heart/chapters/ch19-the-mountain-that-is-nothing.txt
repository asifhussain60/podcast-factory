Now, to — أَعُوذُ بِاللَّهِ — this is the significant one: فَخْر and كِبْرٌ. كِبْرٌ. فَخْر.

<!-- page 141 -->

فَخْر is, at its core, boasting. Boasting is a disease of the heart. فَخْر differs from مُبَاحَة and similar things in that فَخْر, as understood here, is boasting about something you have not yourself earned. It is boasting about lineage — saying, for instance, "I am a شَرِيف," or trading on what your father or ancestors did.

One of the most striking moments illustrating this is from the Battle of Uhud. The Arabs would shout out, "أَنَا ابْنُ فُلَانٍ" — "I am the son of so-and-so" — as a form of war cry, a display of lineage. The Prophet ﷺ would respond: "أَنَا ابْنُ الْعَوَاتِكِ" — "I am the son of the women named عَاتِكَة." The Arabs did not boast through their mothers, so he was doing the opposite of what they intended. Many of his grandmothers bore the name عَاتِكَة, and that was his response to their boasting of fathers.

<!-- page 142 -->

On the matter of boasting about parents, الْخُدَّام mentions — and the saying goes — لَئِنْ فَخَرْتُ بِآبَاءٍ ذَوِي شَرَفٍ، لَقَدْ صَدَقْتَ وَلَكِنْ بِئْسَ مَا وَلَدُوا: if you boast about ancestors who truly had honor, what you say may even be true — if they were from بَنِي هَاشِمٍ, for instance, then yes, it is factually true — but what a wretched child they left behind. If you are boasting about your parents and have yourself accomplished nothing, then as the Arabs say: وَلَيْسَ الْفَتَى مَنْ قَالَ كَانَ أَبِي، إِنَّمَا الْفَتَى مَنْ قَالَ هَا أَنَا — the man is not the one who says "my father was." The man is the one who says "here I am." You do something yourself.

Jalal al-Din al-Rumi has a poem in which he names all the great figures who came before, and then says: but do not be content with the stories of those who went before you — go out and create your own story. Be one of those people that those who come after you will speak about. This is part of our teaching: the idea of being genuinely significant — not for the sake of دُنْيَا, but for the sake of being someone who actually does something.

This is boasting. Saying "my father did this" — it is what small children do. Children say, "My dad is a fireman." "Well, my dad is an astronaut." It is childish. Mentioning a parent's إِحْسَان, something good they did for you — that is a different matter entirely, and there is nothing wrong with it. But boasting is something we all recognize.

<!-- page 143 -->

Now he says — the mountain, فَوْدُهَا شَامِخٌ, this high mountain — deem that mountain insignificant if you desire to bring it down to the ground. كِبْرٌ: arrogance. In Arabic, كِبْرٌ with a فَتْحًا gives you كِبَرٌ, meaning old age. كِبْرٌ with a سُكُونٌ over the بَا gives you arrogance. The root is كَبُرَ or كَبِيرَة — two related roots. كَبُرَ means to grow, to be great; كَبِيرَة means to grow old. كَبِيرٌ means great — Ibrahim, peace be upon him, said: "بَلْ فَعَلَهُۥ كَبِيرُهُمْ" — "the great one did it." So كَبِير is greatness. كِبْرٌ is something that lives in the heart. When it manifests in outward behavior, you call it تَكَبُّر. كِبْرٌ and تَكَبُّر are the same disease — تَكَبُّر is simply the action of it. تَكَبَّرَ عَلَيَّ: he was arrogant toward me, meaning he did something that made it visible.

<!-- page 144 -->

There are many signs of كِبْرٌ. And I will tell you honestly — of all these diseases, this is the one I find most frightening. Because you have some degree of it. All of us do. It exists on a continuum: it can be severe, it can be mild, and you will rarely encounter someone who has none of it at all. When you do meet such a person, you feel it — their absence of كِبْرٌ is palpable. But nearly everyone carries some. And the most frightening thing about it is the حَدِيث in which the Prophet ﷺ said:

لَا يَدۡخُلُ الۡجَنَّةَ مَنۡ كَانَ فِي قَلۡبِهِ مِثۡقَالُ ذَرَّةٍ مِنۡ كِبۡرٍ

No one will enter Paradise — or, as the حَدِيث puts it, كَمَا قَدْ — no one will enter Paradise who carries even the weight of an atom of كِبْرٌ, of arrogance, in his heart. Arrogance, then, is literally a بَاrrier to Paradise.

This is grounded in the Quranic verse: سَأَصْرِفُ عَنْ آيَتِىَ ٱلَّذِينَ يَتَكَبَّرُونَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ بِغَيْرِ ٱلْحَقِّ — "I will divert My signs from those who show arrogance on the earth without right." The scholars note that the phrase بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ implies that there are situations in which تَكَبُّر is actually appropriate. This was precisely the case with Abu Dujanah's famous تَبَخْتُرٌ — his swaggering gait on the بَاttlefield — which carried a حَقٌّ, a legitimate justification. In that context, what would ordinarily be reprehensible became praiseworthy.

So Allah says He will divert His signs from those who are arrogant on the earth without justification, without any legitimate right. And He also says: كَذَلِكَ يَطْبَعُ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ قَلْبِ مُتَكَبِّرٍۢ جَبَّارٍۢ — Allah will seal the heart of every arrogant tyrant. And: إِنَّهُۥ لَا يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُسْتَكْبِرِينَ — He does not love those who harbor اِسْتِكْبَار. اِسْتِكْبَار is essentially the same as كِبْرٌ, but with an additional nuance: the person actively considers himself great. يَسْتَكْبِرُ — he thinks he is great. And at its root lies عُجْب, that self-admiration he discussed earlier — مُعْجَبٌ بِنَفْسِهِ, thoroughly impressed with himself.

There is also the حَدِيث in which Allah says, as reported by the Prophet ﷺ: يَقُولَ اللَّهُ تَعَالَى الْكِبْرِيَاءُ رِدَائِ وَالْعَظَمَةُ إِذَارِ فَمَن نَازَعْنِي فِيهِمَا قَصَمْتُهُ كِبْرِيَاءُ — Greatness is My cloak, and majesty is My lower garment. Whoever tries to compete with Me over either of them, I will break him. These are two attributes Allah has reserved exclusively for Himself, and He does not permit any created being to lay claim to them.

This brings to mind something relevant to the subcontinent: there are certain names commonly given there that would proبَاbly be better avoided. Ibn al-Hajj observed that Shaytan entered this أُمَّة through two different doors. With the eastern Muslims, he entered through names of grandiosity. With the western Muslims, he entered by causing them to mispronounce good names — dropping the عَبْد — so that, for example, in parts of West Africa, عَبْدُ الْكَبِيرِ becomes simply الكَبِيرُ, or عَبْدُ الْقَادِرِ becomes الْقَادِرُ. And in Sudan, the pronunciation shifts similarly — الْقَادِرُ, which sounds as though it derives from غَدَرَ, meaning the treacherous one, when of course Allah's name is الْقَادِرُ. Ibn al-Hajj identified this as one of Shaytan's stratagems.

As for the eastern Arabs, Shaytan led them toward names such as فَخْرُ الدِّين شَمْسُ الدِّين جَمَالُ الدِّين. This is why Imam Yahya al-Nawawi, whose father named him مُحْيِي الدِّينِ, flatly refused that name and insisted on being called Yahya. He would not accept it at all. Names in this category — أَكْبَرُ، عَظِيمٌ، عُزْمَةٌ and the like — carry that same problem. The issue is that such names constitute a تَزْكِيَة, a self-endorsement. The Prophet ﷺ had a wife named بِرَّ and renamed her زَيْنَب, and said: لَا تُزَكُّوا أَنْفُسَكُمْ — do not declare yourselves pure. He also said: أَصْدَقُ الْأَسْمَاءِ حَارِثٌ وَهَمَّامٌ — the truest names are Harith and Humam, because every person يَحْرِثُ — he toils — and every person carries concern. Those, then, are the most honest names. And the best names are those that include عَبْدَ اللَّهِ or عَبْدَ الرَّحْمَنِ, or names like أَحْمَد. If your name happens to be one of the others, that is simply what your parents gave you — so live up to it as best you can. As was pointed out with some humor, the trouble is when someone bears a lofty name yet turns out to be مِلْعَقَةُ الدِّينِ — a spoon of the religion, just eating all the time.

Now there is a حَدِيث — and it needs verification, though the transmission appears to be there — that is simply astonishing: يَحْشَرَ الْمُتَكَبِّرٌُونَ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ فِي مِثْرِ صُورَ الْذَرَّ تَطَّأُهُمَ النَّاسُ ذَرًّا فِي مِثْرِ صُورَ الرِّجَالِ يَعْلُوهُمْ كُلُّ شَيْءٍ مِنَ الصَّغَارِ ثُمَّ يُسَاقُونَ إِلَى السِّسْنِ فِي جَهَنَمٍ يُقَالُوا لَهُ بَوْلَسٍ. It is reported in al-Tirmidhi: the people of arrogance will be raised on the Day of Judgment transformed into atoms, and people will walk over them. Then they will be driven to a prison in Jahannam called بَلَاصْ. SubhanAllah. The word بَلَاصْ is strikingly close to what Arabic speakers call the police — خُذُوا إِلَى الْبَوْلَسِ، جَبَّارُونَ خُذُوا إِلَى الْبَوْلِسِ. Whether that detail is precisely established in al-Tirmidhi needs to be confirmed, but if it is, it is extraordinary. The word ذَرّ can mean either an atom or an ant — both readings exist.

Moving on to the nature of اِسْتِكْبَار itself: there are two types. The worst form of تَكَبُّر is refusing to accept the truth and refusing to submit to the worship of Allah — أَعْظَم التَكَبُّر امتنع قبول الحق وإذعان للعبادة.. Everything else falls beneath that in degrees of severity.

A companion once came to the Prophet ﷺ and said: يا رسول الله إني امرأ أحب الجمال — O Messenger of Allah, I am a man who loves beautiful things. I wear fine shoes and a nice garment. He then asked: أَهَذَا مِنَ الْكِبْرِ — Is this from كِبْرٌ? The Prophet ﷺ replied: لا إن الله جميل يحب الجمال وإنما الكبر بطر الحق وغمص الناس — No. Allah is beautiful and loves beauty. كِبْرٌ is, rather, rejecting the truth and looking down on people.

This is an important حَدِيث, because the man first explained his motivation: he loved beautiful things. The scholars clarify that the problem arises when a person dresses or adorns himself in order to تَكَبَّرَ عَلَى النَّاسِ — to demonstrate superiority over others. This is painfully familiar in contemporary Muslim culture. Go to some Muslim weddings and you will see people consumed with outdoing one another — "عَبْدُ اللهِ and Khadija are going to show everyone what a real wedding looks like." That competitive one-upmanship is a form of كِبْرٌ, because the underlying drive is تَتَكَبَّرُ عَلَيْهِمْ — to assert superiority. It springs from كِبْرٌ.

So اِسْتِكْبَار has two faces. On one side, a person wants to achieve greatness and be great. Imam Raghib al-Isfahani says that if this drive is oriented toward something genuinely great, exercised at the right time, and for the right reasons, then it is praiseworthy. اِسْتِكْبَار in this sense is not inherently negative — and this returns to the broader principle of transforming these diseases into virtues. A desire to be great for the sake of Allah is a good thing. A desire to accomplish great things is a good thing. The صَحَابَة had towering هِمَّةٌ — tremendous aspiration. The Prophet ﷺ's هِمَّةٌ was the highest of all, and he lifted them all toward it. They set their sights on al-Thuraya, the Pleiades. They reached for the heights in everything they did, and Allah made them great people. That is praiseworthy هِمَّةٌ.

The blameworthy kind, by contrast, is يظهر من نفسه أنه أفضل من غيره. — when a person tries to project the image that he is better than others. That is what is censured. The one exception is demonstrating to non-Muslims that Muslims are better — because Allah actually commands this. Allah commands us to show them. Someone raised this very point the previous night regarding the Christian response to human nature. The Christian answer, in simplified terms, is that human beings are irredeemably corrupt by original sin — there is nothing to be done about it. Allah's answer is: show them this is not true. كَذَٰرِكَ جَعَلْنَاكُمْ أُمَّةً وَسَطًى — We made you a just, بَاlanced community — لِّتَكُونُوا۟ شُهَدَآءَ عَلَى ٱلنَّاسِ — so that you may be witnesses to mankind, demonstrating that all other ways fall short. وَيَكُونَ ٱلرَّسُولُ عَلَيْكُمْ شَهِيدًۭا — and the Messenger is a witness over you.

The Prophet ﷺ was a witness to us — he proved it was true. His Companions were witnesses to us — they proved it was true. Their followers were witnesses in turn. But each generation becomes more diluted, until eventually there are no more شُهَدَاء, no more living witnesses. This is one reason why so many people in the end of times fall into disbelief: they no longer see the truth embodied anywhere, so they lose it.

One of the signs of the end of times, according to Imam al-Biqa'i, is that Allah will hand the دُنْيَا over to the disbelievers, and Muslims will aبَاndon Islam in pursuit of what the disbelievers have. He read this in the verses of Surah al-Zukhruf: had Allah not wished to prevent everyone from becoming disbelievers, He would have made their houses of silver and given them staircases to climb. Look around today — that is almost precisely what you see. People in Mali and Niger watch television and see those gleaming houses and want them. It is a real and serious problem.

Can the sealed heart be penetrated? Yes. Allah says that even rock can be split and water can flow from it — seals can be broken. The scholars say they are broken by the power of the وَارِد, the spiritual influx. A prophet, through the sheer spiritual force he carries — by the permission of Allah — can penetrate even the sealed heart. Sidi Ahmad Zarruq says that when someone with a sealed heart hears the truth, several factors determine whether it gets through: the stature of the one delivering it, the intrinsic strength of the truth itself, and the force with which it reaches the heart. If these converge with sufficient power, it can shake the heart and crack the seal. But if nothing follows — no تَوْبَة, no action taken — the crack can close over again. This is what happens when someone attends a خُطْبَة, feels something stir in the heart, then steps outside and returns to the same life: it seals بَاck up.

Now, before concluding, here is a brief survey of the different types of كِبْرٌ and their distinguishing qualities.

The first type: كِبْرٌ that is مُسْتَقَرٌّ — fixed and settled in the heart. The person genuinely believes he is better than others. There is a حَدِيث in which a man was being praised in the Prophet's ﷺ presence as a person of virtue. When the man entered the gathering, the Companions said: يا رسول الله هذا الذي ذكرناه لك — O Messenger of Allah, this is the man we mentioned to you. The Prophet ﷺ said: إني أرى في وجهه صفعة من الشيطان. — I see in his face a trace of Shaytan. The man gave سَلَامٌ and sat down. Then the Prophet ﷺ turned to him and said: أسألك بالله حدثتك نفسك أليس في القوم أفضل منك. — I ask you by Allah: did your نَفْس tell you, when you walked into this gathering, that no one here is better than you? The man said: Yes. So outwardly he appeared to be a righteous man, but the Prophet ﷺ saw the كِبْرٌ embedded in his heart — and wanted the Companions to understand how subtle and hidden كِبْرٌ can be.

Imam al-Ghazali says that among all people, scholars are in the greatest danger of كِبْرٌ. And that includes students of sacred knowledge — people in exactly the situation under discussion. This is one of the gravest spiritual hazards of pursuing religious learning. If a person fights it, however — if he sees the كِبْرٌ within but forces himself to resist it — he is at least preventing the bitter fruits of that tree from ripening. The tree is there, the roots are there, but the fruit does not emerge.

The second type is where كِبْرٌ moves from the interior to the exterior: the person begins to manifest it in behavior, looking down on others, turning his face away, frowning in people's faces. Frowning at someone is a sign of كِبْرٌ. Refusing to give someone the time of day is a sign of كِبْرٌ. The Prophet ﷺ never did this. Adi ibn Hatim said he knew the Prophet ﷺ was not a king precisely because an old woman stopped him on the street and he simply stood there and listened to her — that was how he was. This second type includes the عَابِد, the devout worshipper, who looks down on others because of his worship — he becomes مُتَكَبِّرٌ through his religiosity.

The third type is تَكَبُّر on account of حَسَبٌ and نَسَبٌ — one's personal qualities and one's lineage. These two traditionally went together. Western culture has the concept of *noblesse oblige*: those born into nobility are obliged to behave nobly. Most traditional cultures hold something similar. Among the Arabs, if you were a شَرِيف of the Ta'i clan, you were expected to be generous — generosity was the hallmark of Ta'i. If you were Qurayshi, you were expected to carry dignity, to be a زَعِيمٌ. If you were Yemeni, you upheld the qualities associated with Yemen. Each group had its defining virtues, and نَسَبٌ carried an obligation to embody them.

But نَسَبٌ also becomes a source of arrogance. People with noble lineage begin to see themselves as superior to others. Some Arabs, simply because they are Arab, look down on Indians — this is common. الْحَقُّ يُقَالُ — to tell the truth — there are many Arabs who think this way. One Arab once said plainly: I never thought a non-Arab could truly be a real Muslim. He said that directly. Similarly, in Pakistan, Indians who did not make هِجْرَة may be seen as lesser. If you are Hyderaبَاdi, certain Hyderaبَاdis consider themselves automatically superior to all other Indians by virtue of being from Hyderaبَاd. Americans carry their own version — "we're number one." In Hausaland, many Hausa people are convinced of their superiority over the Yoruبَا and Igbo, and proبَاbly over the Fulani — though the Fulani, for their part, see themselves as superior to the Hausa. In Mauritania, the Kunta see themselves as better than the Massouma. In Greater Syria, the Damascenes consider themselves superior to the people of Aleppo — they even guard the name شَامِي jealously, reluctant to let anyone else claim it. This is a human disease, widespread and indiscriminate.

This is a disease of the heart — thinking yourself better simply because of some quality you happen to possess, like being from Damascus. Or being a Nejdi. The Nejdis deem themselves superior to the Hijazis; they call them أَخْلَافُ الْحُجَّاجِ. You find this kind of arrogance embedded in the language, in the terminology of every culture. It is all تَكَبُّر.

Allah says, إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ أَتْقَىٰكُمْ — the most honored of you before Allah are those with تَقْوَى. Yet this disease deludes a person into assuming that someone else couldn't possibly have تَقْوَى — how could he? He's a Hindi. He's a Nejdi. He's whatever.

This is not a modern phenomenon. It goes بَاck a long way. Even among the صَدَأ — those who trace their lineage to the family of the Prophet ﷺ — you find تَكَبُّر. And of all things to feel superior about in terms of lineage, that would have to rank first. I'd put it at number one in نَسَبٌ. And that is precisely the danger, because sometimes these distinctions do carry genuine weight.

There is something real and honorable about descending from the family of the Prophet ﷺ — it is a tremendous distinction. But there are حَدِيث that should put fear in your heart. One حَدِيث says: إِذَا أَبْطَأَكُمُ الْعَمَلُ لَمْ يُسْرِعْ بِكُمُ النَّسَبُ — if your deeds hold you بَاck, your lineage will not carry you forward. In other words, if you have the نَسَبٌ but no deeds to go with it, it will get you nowhere. A fine car with no fuel. If you're a شَرِيف, it's a Mercedes — but deeds are still the petrol. You are not going anywhere without them. There are many حَدِيث warning of this danger.

<!-- page 144 -->

Now, as for the cure — one of them is to reflect on where you actually came from. Allah says that you are from مَاءٍ مَهِينٍ, a vile fluid. Your very origin is a vile fluid. One of the سَلَفٌ, speaking of someone who was being arrogant, said: سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ يَحْمِلُ بَيْنَ جَنْبَيْهِ الْعَذِرَةِ — glory be to God, he carries between his two sides nothing but filth. Where does the تَكَبُّر come from? He is a بَاg of blood like everyone else. Every culture has its own crude ways of making this point, which I won't go into — but people everywhere understand it. Nobody likes تَكَبُّر.

The problem is that the مُتَكَبِّرٌ never sees his own arrogance. That is why, when someone is arrogant with him, he loathes it — even though he himself is guilty of the same thing. This is the meaning of the saying: التَّكَبُّرُ فِي وَجْهِ الْمُتَكَبِّرٌِ عِبَادَةٌ — to show arrogance in the face of an arrogant person is itself a form of worship. It is throwing it بَاck at them. You give them a taste of their own medicine — but it should be understood as a دَوَاءٌ, a remedy, just enough for them to feel what it is like.

That is كِبْرٌ. It is a serious problem. And if you feel that you are a righteous person — that itself is كِبْرٌ, right from the start. That is why, when you look at the early generations, they were people who genuinely saw themselves differently. One of the companions of Abu Hanifa was sitting with him when a man moved his foot aside out of deference. The companion asked why he had done so. The man said, out of respect for someone like you. He replied: وَاللَّهِ لَا أَرَى أَحَدًا هُنَا أَشَرَّ مِنِّي — by God, I see no one in this gathering worse than me.

That is how those people were. It was not performance. They genuinely saw themselves that way.

Look at Sayyidina Ali. There is a sound حَدِيث in which a man asked him: who is better, you or Abu Bakr? He said, Abu Bakr. The man asked, then what about you and Omar? He said, Omar. Then the man asked about himself, and Ali said: ما أنا إلا رجل من المسلمين. — I am just a man among the Muslims. No claim, no station.

For us, looking at this from the outside, it reads as humility. But for the Imam himself, he was not calculating: *I should say this in order to appear humble.* No — that way of thinking is itself a disease. Ibn Ata'illah says: if you are aware of your humility, you are مُتَكَبِّر.

This is why the character of Uriah Heep in Dickens' *David Copperfield* is so remarkable. He is a complete hypocrite who is forever saying, "In my very 'umble opinion, sir" — always performing lowliness, always deferring — while simultaneously stealing money and scheming behind everyone's بَاck. The point is: if you think of yourself as humble, you are مُتَكَبِّر, because you are observing your own lowliness from a position of height. Humility means to be low. If you can see how low you are, you must be looking down from somewhere above. That is كِبْرٌ by definition.

We are diseased. We simply have to accept that and work with it. Our scholars have said: if you cannot yet be like the true people of virtue, at least imitate them — because there is benefit even in the imitation. The Imam himself acknowledges this: pretending at humility is better than being an outright مُتَكَبِّر.

One of the things Imam al-Ghazali, رَحِمَهُ اللهُ, says is this: if you want to master calligraphy, you go to the master calligrapher and you repeat what he does, over and over again. It feels completely unnatural at first, but eventually you become a master calligrapher. Interestingly, a neurosurgeon at Stanford said something that maps onto this perfectly: the brain learns through repetition. Whatever you do most consistently becomes embedded in the brain — like driving. Most people drive in a nearly subconscious state; they are not aware of their own driving. That is the ideal of humility — it becomes so woven into your nature that the brain is no longer conscious of it.

When you first start learning to drive, you are actually more careful. Those poor learners with the dual-control car on top, checking the mirror, checking the blinker, anxious and alert — they are often safer than the experienced driver who is singing along to music, talking on the phone, and thinking about other things. The experienced driver is the one who gets into the accident, precisely because it has all become unconscious. And what the neurologist pointed out was striking: stroke patients and those with dementia often retain the things they did most repetitiously. They may forget almost everything else, but they can still drive — because those neural pathways are so deeply fixed that they remain.

This is the idea behind repetition in spiritual practice: إِنَّمَا الْعِلْمُ بِالتَّعَلُّمِ — you acquire it through learning, through تَكْرَار, doing it over and over again. You must force yourself to act with humility until it genuinely becomes your nature. Most of us do not have these qualities naturally — if we did, we would not need to study any of this. That is why we are studying it: we all recognize these diseases in ourselves.

Now the fourth type is التَّفَاخُرُ بِالْجَمَالِ — arrogance on account of physical beauty. One of the cures for this is the old adage: beauty is only skin deep. Beauty is an illusion. First, no one retains their beauty forever — everyone loses it in the end. Second, Allah gave it to you; it is not something you earned. So taking arrogant pride in beauty is a particularly shallow and foolish form of كِبْرٌ.

What I find genuinely fascinating is a study that examined what makes people beautiful. Researchers looked at large numbers of faces and found that what people consistently identified as beautiful was, in fact, average — statistically. An average nose, average eyes, average mouth — all placed together on one face. Beautiful people are, in a real sense, mean-looking. They represent the median. I thought that was remarkable: beauty is the average. And there is a kind of truth in it — the median holds something. It is a foolish thing to take pride in.

There is also a deeply sobering حَدِيث involving Aisha, رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهَا, in which a woman entered and the Prophet ﷺ asked who it was. She indicated the short one. The Prophet ﷺ said: اغْتَبْتِهَا — you have بَاckbitten her. The scholars explain that the reason it constituted بَاckbiting was that it arose from تَكَبُّر: she had, in that moment, a sense of her own greater height, an إِعْجَابٌ with her own stature — مُعْجَبَةٌ بِقَامَتِهَا — and looking at the other woman from that place of self-regard made the remark an act of تَكَبُّر. The Prophet ﷺ rebuked her for it.

The fifth type is كِبْرٌ بِالْمَالِ — pride in wealth. This afflicts kings with their treasuries, merchants with their goods, and people who own vast tracts of land, fine clothes, horses, or — in our time — expensive cars. They look down on the poor, and even on other wealthy people who are less wealthy than themselves. Think of Leona Helmsley, who famously said that only the little people pay taxes. I once read a remarkable interview — I cannot recall exactly who it was — one of those figures this culture calls "beautiful people." They were being airlifted by private helicopter to a ski slope while a lodge for less-wealthy skiers sat below, and someone looked down and said, "Look at all the little people down there." At that very moment, the helicopter tipped and they crashed. The point is: she got the message. Some people would not have seen it that way. So be very careful about how you regard others, because we are all little people. The homeless man on the street looks up at the CEO and sees a little person; the CEO looks down at the homeless man and sees a little person. It is all perspective. That is why the مُتَكَبِّرٌٌ are transformed into ants on the Day of Judgment — because they looked at everyone else as ants to be crushed. It is a very dangerous thing.

<!-- page 194 -->

The sixth is كِبْرٌ بِالْقُوَّةِ — pride in physical strength. An example is Rukana al-Muttalibi, who challenged the Prophet ﷺ to a wrestling match. The Prophet threw him twice, and still he did not accept Islam. There is a narration suggesting he did eventually embrace Islam — إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ he did, though I cannot say with certainty. The point is that he had never been thrown by anyone, and that fed his arrogance. Such a person will inevitably grow old. The strength will leave him. He will no longer be able to do what he once could.

The seventh is تَكَبُّرٌ بِالْأَتْبَاعِ — pride derived from one's followers, supporters, students, and servants. A teacher who has many students may look down on a teacher with fewer. Someone with many powerful friends, influential relatives, or a connection to royalty — even if he is not the king himself — may feel superior because of that association. This is a very dangerous form of كِبْرٌ rooted in social standing and prestige.

And then there is تَكَبُّر through knowledge, which the imam identifies as among the most insidious forms of all. The reason is precisely that knowledge is so honored in the tradition. People naturally revere and defer to the learned. So when someone genuinely possesses knowledge — especially a significant amount of it — people show him respect, and that deference becomes dangerous for the heart. He begins to think, "I am somebody. I matter." This is what Shaytan does to him.

Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى says in the Quran: how has man been destroyed — what makes him such an ingrate? And then Allah says: let man think about what We created him from. He came from a fluid. He did not even exist before that fluid. He was not even a thought on anyone's tongue fifty years ago. He was nothing, nonexistent. Then he came into existence, and when he first arrived in this world, he needed his diaper changed seven or eight times a day — all he did was defecate, urinate, sleep, cry, and nurse. That is the human being. Then he grows, reaches the peak of his strength, and becomes arrogant. And then it reverses — because the trajectory is curvilinear. If you live long enough, you end up where you started. People reach that forty-year peak and think everything is settled, everything is fine. Then Allah says:

مِن نُّطْفَةٍ خَلَقَهُۥ فَقَدَّرَهُۥ

He created him from a نُطْفَة — an ejaculated fluid — and then measured out everything for him. Man did not arrange any of that himself. And then Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى says:

<!-- page 195 -->

أَوَلَمْ يَرَى الْإِنسَانِ أَنَّا خَلَقْنَاهُ مِنْ نُطْفَةٍ فَإِذَا هُوَ خَصِيمٌ مُبِينٌ

Has man not considered that We created him from a نُطْفَة, and now he has become an open adversary against Us? It is astonishing. Allah created the human being, and then that human being rises up in rebellion against his Creator — and he is nothing. Allah can stir up a hurricane — like Hurricane Mitch — and it is just a small wind in one corner of one planet in one solar system within a vast universe. It is nothing. And yet for those struck by it, entire homes, wealth, everything — gone. From a wind that Allah sent. And Allah says in the Quran that when people see such a wind approaching, they think it is a good wind bringing rain — and it turns out to be a torment.

The point of all of this is that we are simply foolish to be in rebellion against Allah. He gave us a tongue, two eyes, the capacity to think — all of these gifts. He guided us and showed us two roads, telling us plainly: at the end of this road is a painful punishment, and at the end of that road is a reward. Choose. And yet people choose the road of ruin, and at the root of that choice is تَكَبُّر.

Look at the Sira — I guarantee you, every single person who refused to submit, the reason was تَكَبُّر. It is unmistakable. The reason people cannot submit is تَكَبُّر.

Consider the prayer. There is a kind of servitude embedded in it that some people find deeply offensive. Many Western people struggle profoundly with the concept of being a slave of God — عَبْدُ اللهِ. They recoil from it: "I am free." And yet that same person is a slave to his desires, a slave to his appetite, a slave to his petty ambitions — completely unable to control them. He is enslaved to all of these things. But a slave to Allah? No. He will gladly be a slave to his own passions but not to Allah. It is a thoroughly delusional state.

<!-- page 196 -->

So the imam is saying that the way to uproot this disease is: first, to know your Lord, and second, to know yourself. That is, admittedly, an enormous undertaking. Some scholars say the only way to know your Lord is to know yourself — and Allah points us in that direction: *in yourselves, do you not see?* If you reflect deeply on the self, what you come to realize is that you are completely bound. Even عِيسَى ٱبْنَ مَرْيَمَ was not too proud to acknowledge that he was عَبْدُ اللهِ — he accepted it. And the Prophet ﷺ said, أَنَا سَيِّدُ وَلَدِ آدَمَ وَلَا فَخْرَ — I am the best of the children of Adam, and I say this without boasting. The ulama explain that he said this not out of pride, but because he was commanded by Allah to inform the Companions of his station — and precisely because the جَاهِلِي Arabs used boasting as an act of arrogance, he was careful to add وَلَا فَخْرَ, making clear this was not that. The ulama draw a further إِشَارَة from this: not only was there no فَخْر in it, but his true source of honor was his عُبُودِيَّة to Allah — his being the most perfect slave of Allah — not his being the master of creation. That is where his glory lay. This is why there is a حَدِيث — some scholars question its chain, but مُلَّا عَلِي الْقَارِي affirmed that its meaning is sound — that his empowerment before Allah was his glory.

So: know yourself, know your Lord — and whoever truly grasps both is humbled, feels insignificant, يَتَوَضَّأُ وَيَهُونُ. مَنْ تَوَاضَعَ لِلَّهِ رَفَعَهُ اللَّهُ — whoever humbles himself for the sake of Allah, Allah raises him up.

He also makes another point: مَقَامُهُ يَنْفِي مَقَامَ الشُّكْرِ — the station of كِبْرٌ makes genuine gratitude to Allah impossible. The way you show gratitude to Allah is through عُبُودِيَّة, and if you are مُتَكَبِّرٌ, you are not an عَبْد — because an عَبْد by his very nature cannot be مُتَكَبِّرٌ. That is what makes him an عَبْد. And if you are not a true عَبْد, you cannot be a شَكُورٌ. It is categorically impossible. Conversely, the nature of humility is that it leads you to gratitude, because when you are genuinely humbled before Allah, you see everything in its true proportion — you see how generous Allah has been, how much He has given you that you do not deserve. And that sense of being undeserving is precisely what generates gratitude. If you feel you deserve something, gratitude does not arise. As the Moroccans say: لَا شُكْرَ عَلَى الْوَاجِبِ — there is no thanks for what is owed. If you feel entitled, you simply think: لَا، أَعْطَانِي مَا هُوَ حَقِّي — he only gave me what was mine. But once you recognize you are undeserving, everything changes.

That is why he says: حَقِّرْهُ — see this towering mountain of كِبْرٌ for what it actually is: insignificant and meaningless. When you see it that way, you will bring it down. This is also why we say اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ — and traditionally, whenever Muslims saw something impressive in themselves or in others, they would refer it بَاck to its source: اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ. It is a constant reminder. اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ مِنِّي، اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ مِنْكُمْ، اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ مِنَ الدُّنْيَا وَمَا فِيهَا — Allah is greater than me, greater than you, greater than this world and all it contains. He is الْكَبِيرُ الْمُتَعَالِي. And it is to the مُتَعَالٍ that He says: ‏ذُقْ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْكَرِيمُ ‎ — now taste it; you are the عَزِيزٌ now. So taste the punishment. This is the تَحَكُّم of the Quran.

So the last topic remaining is ذُلّ and دَاءٌ — but we will need to prepare for that, so we will take it up next week, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ.

اللَّهُمَّ صَلِّ عَلَى سَيِّدِنَا مُحَمَّدٍ وَعَلَى آلِ سَيِّدِنَا مُحَمَّدٍ كَمَا صَلَّيْتَ عَلَى سَيِّدِنَا إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَعَلَى آلِ سَيِّدِنَا إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَبَارِكْ عَلَى سَيِّدِنَا مُحَمَّدٍ وَعَلَى آلِ سَيِّدِنَا مُحَمَّدٍ كَمَا بَارَكْتَ عَلَى سَيِّدِنَا إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَعَلَى آلِ سَيِّدِنَا إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَفِي الْعَالَمِينَ إِنَّكَ حَمِيدٌ مَجِيدٌ. اللَّهُمَّ لَا عِلْمَ إِلَّا مَا عَلَّمْتَنَا إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْحَكِيمُ الْعَلِيمُ.

اللَّهُمَّ عَلِّمْنَا مَا يَنْفَعُنَا وَانْفَعْنَا بِمَا عَلَّمْتَنَا وَزِدْنَا عِلْمًا. اللَّهُمَّ انْشُرْ عَلَيْنَا رَحْمَتَكَ وَانْشُرْ عَلَيْنَا حِكْمَتَكَ يَا ذَا الْجَلَالِ وَالْإِكْرَامِ. وَصَلَّى اللَّهُمَّ عَلَى سَيِّدِنَا مُحَمَّدٍ وَعَلَى آلِهِ وَصَحْبِهِ وَسَلَّمَ

Now, this section just covered brings us to the end of that class. We are nearly done with the first half of the book. The second half — which I mentioned to some of you last night — deals with the opposite: after treating the diseases of the heart, it moves to adorning the heart with the states and stations of certainty. We will إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ begin that in February.

We have been looking at فَخْر and كِبْرٌ — serious illnesses of the heart. One of the things worth noting here is the concept of the mean, what is called الْوَسَطِيَّة in Arabic. Virtues are means between two extremes — with one important exception: justice, which is simply the opposite of injustice. It has an opposite, but not two flanking extremes in the same way.

Now, the next disease the imam addresses is one that sits at the opposite extreme from كِبْرٌ. The virtue is humility. An excess of it — زِيَادَةٌ — slides into arrogance. A deficiency — نَقْص — slides into aبَاsement and self-humiliation, which is not a virtue in Islam. One might argue it is regarded as a virtue in certain strands of Christian thought, but in Islam it is not. The imam calls this ذُلّ.

In the Quran, Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى mentions that ذُلّ was among the afflictions visited upon Bani Israel as a consequence of their disobedience. So aبَاsement is often the result of disobedience — Allah afflicts people with it as a response to their arrogance. The sun never sets on the British Empire — and then it sets. The British lion becomes the tail of America. For Anglo-Saxons who always viewed the Americans as colonials — as the lesser people — those lesser people now dictate British foreign policy. That is what Allah does with peoples. And it will come to the Americans as well, just as it comes to everyone. Their turn is coming; there are already many signs of it.

So ذُلّ can be a social phenomenon afflicting an entire people. Many Muslim countries find themselves in this condition today. You will find Muslims who will do unbelievable things — lie, cheat, scheme — just to obtain a visa to a European country or to America. Inside their own countries, they conduct themselves in ways that are simply shocking in terms of بَاsic human dignity. All of it traces بَاck to this inner lowliness, this humiliation. And yet — despite that — you can also find a people crushed by great tribulations who nevertheless maintain their human dignity entirely. That is something else altogether.

اَلْمُؤْمِنُ لَا يُذِلُّ نَفْسَهُ — the believer never humiliates himself, never aبَاses himself. This is a حَدِيث of the Prophet ﷺ. So when we speak of humility, we do not mean humiliation. You humble yourself; you do not humiliate yourself.

He then mentions: أَذَلُّ وَبَاعَ — to be lowly, to be brought low. And the Prophet ﷺ said: nothing raises itself except that Allah will aبَاse it. That is the sunna of Allah in His creation. If you elevate yourself, Allah will aبَاse you. These are wisdoms to be treasured.

Before Allah, you should have ذُلّ. Before Allah you should be ذَلِيل — you should carry a sense of ذُلِّ وَالْهَوَانِ وَبَاءَ. That is the proper orientation before Allah سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى. This is why Sidi Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani said that he looked upon all the doors leading to Allah and found them crowded — except one: the door of ذُلٌّ وَتَوَاضُعٌ, humility and lowliness. And so he called out to people: هَلُمَّ ارْعَ رَبَّكُمْ — come, you want to reach your Lord? This is the door. No one is there. It is hard on the نَفْس, which is precisely why the path is empty.

He then warns against the opposite failure: do not aبَاse yourself before creation. In fact, he says: وَاكْبُرْ عَلَى الْغَنِيِّ الْمُسْتَكْبِرِ — hold yourself with dignity before the rich and arrogant. It has been said that among the noble things of this world is a غَنِيٌّ مُتَوَاضِعٌ — a wealthy man who is humble. That is genuine nobility. And شَرِيفٌ سُنِّيٌّ — a شَرِيف who follows the Sunna. These are among the things mentioned as truly distinguished. But the غَنِيُّ الْمُسْتَكْبِرِ — the arrogant rich man — is unfortunately the norm.

إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَنَ لَيَطْغَىٰٓ — أَن رَّآهُ ٱسْتَغْنَىٰٓ — Haven't you seen? Man transgresses when he deems himself self-sufficient. When he becomes wealthy, he imagines he has no need of anyone — and that imagined independence causes him to overstep the boundaries of Allah.

<!-- page 246 -->
And that is why, like Nimrod — because Allah gave him dominion over the earth, he thought he was God. He argued with Ibrahim. And this is what wealthy people often do: they think that because they have wealth, it gives them the right to hold opinions on everything, to be an expert on everything. It is a serious matter.

So he said: التَّكَبُّرُ فِي وَجْهِ الْمُتَكَبِّرٌِ عِبَادَةٌ — it is a حَدِيث, and in one رِوَايَة the word عِبَادَة appears. That is the second person.
