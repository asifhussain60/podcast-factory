Now we move to the final and comprehensive section: a treatment plan for the diseases of the heart as a whole. The author addresses the root causes of those diseases, the source of good and bad character, and treatment through the ذِكْرٌ of اللَّهُ. That will be the last section, and as I mentioned, we have essentially covered the full ground of the preceding material.

One more thing before we close — and I am glad I remembered it. Does anyone know the seven deadly sins? Any former Catholics in the room?

Pride. Anger. Envy. Sloth. Greed. Gluttony. Lust.

Now, of all those seven, which ones did the author cover? Pride — yes. Anger — yes. Envy — yes. Sloth — essentially, in its spiritual dimension. Greed — yes. That is five. But gluttony and lust — did he cover those?

He mentions شَهْوَة in relation to causes and contributing factors, but he does not treat it as a standalone disease. And that is deliberate. Both gluttony and lust are فِطْرَة inclinations — built into human nature by design. This is why even in the Catholic tradition they were placed in a different category; the other five were considered graver spiritual failings than these two.

Imam al-Ghazali addresses gluttony and lust in their own dedicated book — كَسْرُ الشَّهْوَتَيْنِ, "Breaking the Two Desires." Part of that work has been translated beautifully by Abd al-Hakim Winter — T.J. Winter — with excellent notes, and I would strongly recommend it as a companion to what we have been studying here.

The connection between the two is important: gluttony tends to fuel lust. Feed the appetite excessively, and the other desire grows alongside it. The link is not absolute — people who fast rigorously can still struggle with lust — but the general pattern holds. This is why, when the Prophet ﷺ spoke to young men who could not afford to marry, he said: "فَعَلَيْكُمْ بِالصِّيَامِ" — "Then you must fast" — "فَإِنَّهُ لَكُمْ وِجَاءٌ" — "for it will act as a shield." The وِجَاءٌ was a small pouch placed on a goat to prevent it from impregnating others — a form of restraint. Fasting functions the same way: it dampens and subdues that desire.

Both hunger and sexual desire are part of human nature. They are not meant to be eliminated — you do not want to eradicate شَهْوَة any more than you want to eradicate شَهِيَّة. They share the same Arabic root: desire for food and desire for intimacy. The goal is not suppression but mastery — balance, not obliteration. And the book is worth reading for anyone who wants to work through these two more deeply.

I mention all this because, looking at the seven deadly sins, the author covers five of them in considerable depth. Those two he leaves largely untouched here, though they are not absent from his broader work. In any case — إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ — we are approaching closure on the diseases of the heart. What remains now is the comprehensive treatment plan: the general remedies, the root causes of corrupt character, the sources of virtue and vice, and the central role of ذِكْرٌ in healing the heart.

So he says, وَقَبُّوا أَمْرَاضِ الْقُلُوبِ الْجَامِعُ فَهُوَ نَهْيُ النَّفْسِ عَمَّا تَتَّبِعُ — that the comprehensive and general treatment plan for the diseases of the heart is to prohibit the self from its own desires, coupled with — and then he gives us: وَالصَّغَبُ وَالسَّهْرُ اللَّيَالِ وَالصَّمْتُ وَالفِكْرَةُ وَهُوَ خَالِي — hunger, nighttime vigil, silence, and meditation in private.

He says, رَحِمَهُ اللَّهُ, that in order to treat the self — the heart itself — you must prohibit the self from what it desires. There is a حَدِيث in which the Prophet ﷺ said: لا يؤمن أحدكم حتى يكون هواه تبعاً لما يأتوا به — that none of you truly believes until his هَوًى, his caprice or desire, conforms to what I have brought.

The way you purify the heart, then, is this: all those diseases he just enumerated are forbidden — they run contrary to the guidance اللَّهُ and His Messenger have given us. So the Messenger of اللَّهُ ﷺ tells us that faith is incomplete, that the heart is not sound, until your هَوًى accords with what he brought — which is guidance. And the way to achieve that is by withholding the self from precisely those things that conflict with guidance, until you no longer desire them.

This is the meaning of the verse: وَنَفْسُ كَطِفْرِي إِن تَهْمِلْهُ شَبَّعَ لَحُبَّ الرَّضَاعِ وَإِن تَفْتَمْهُ يَنْفَطِمِي — the نَفْس is like an infant: if you neglect it, it grows up craving to suckle; but if you wean it, the craving fades. Think about it — there are people in this culture, like followers of La Leche League, who breastfeed until the child is four or five years old. The child just keeps wanting it, and they keep going. But if you wean a child at two years, or one year, or six months — whatever age you choose — the desire passes. The Sharia actually prefers weaning at two years, and there is wisdom in that.

There was a study done in Israel — this is a digression — that performed a statistical analysis comparing breastfed children with children who were not breastfed. Correlations in studies like that are always the tricky part, but the finding was an increase in IQ of three points for every month of breastfeeding. There are obviously many variables in a study of that kind, but it is interesting, because the Prophet ﷺ said in a حَدِيث: إِيَّاكُمْ وَالْحَمْقَىٰ فَإِنَّهَا تُغَذِّي — beware of the wet nurse who lacks intelligence, for she is nourishing the brain. Traditionally, people sought out women of sound mind and good character to serve as wet nurses. And Halima — Halima Sa'diyah, رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ — is a remarkable example of this. Read her حَدِيث. If you do not know Arabic, read it in translation. In every حَدِيث collection that includes it, the scholars have to annotate nearly every word because her Arabic is so elevated. She was extraordinarily eloquent. عَلَيْهَا السَّلَامُ — she is the nursing mother of the Messenger of اللَّهُ ﷺ.

This is what he is pointing us toward. And it is the same teaching found in the Quran: وَأَمَّا مَنْ خَافَ مَقَامَ رَبِّهِ وَنَهَى النَّفْسَ عَنِ الْهَوَى فَإِنَّ الْجَنَّةَ هِيَ الْمَأْوَى — the one who fears standing before his Lord, and then actually restrains the self from what it desires — for such a person, Paradise is the abode.

So this is the work of مُجَهَّدُ قَمْعِ النَّفْسِ — literally suppressing the desires of the نَفْس. It is self-discipline. مَنْ دَانَ نَفْسَهُ — you must discipline the soul. Discipline the soul and you will find happiness. Fail to discipline it and you will be miserable. Look at all the self-help literature in this culture — it is overwhelmingly about people who cannot control themselves.

This is precisely why Islam gives us prayer: to train us in discipline over our time and our days. It gives us ritual washing: to discipline us in cleanliness. It gives us fasting — اَللَّهُمَّا صِرِعَتَ سَيْنِ مُحَمَّدٍ — which is entirely about learning discipline: disciplining the tongue, the stomach, the genitals. Everything Islam brings relates, at its core, to learning how to discipline yourself.

Because if you have no discipline over time, you will not pray on time. Especially in this culture, where there is no أَذًى ringing out and no mosques on every corner, you truly have to stay vigilant. Whenever you travel to a Muslim-majority country from here, it is striking how effortless prayer becomes. The difference is enormous. Here, maintaining that discipline requires active effort — and that effort itself is part of the training.

So you discipline the soul, discipline the tongue. You learn to catch yourself when you feel the urge to backbite — stopping and saying inwardly, I don't want to do this. You have to learn to do that. It is a process. But if you do it enough, it reaches a point where you simply are not doing it anymore. And that is where the هَوًى finally comes into alignment with what the Messenger brought. That is what he is telling us.

Now he gives us the means. What are the core things the soul craves? صَقْلٌ — hunger. The stomach. كَسْرُ الشَّهْوَتَيْنِ — control the stomach and you gain control over the genitals as well. The stomach is the primary driving impulse. If you can learn to master your eating, that is why one of the early masters, as quoted by Imam al-Qushayri, said: for me to lift my hand from the plate while I am still hungry is better than spending an entire night in prayer. Because if a person can discipline that one thing, everything else becomes easier.

People get very agitated when food is delayed. They blame it on hypoglycemia — and there are people who genuinely have that condition, I do not want to dismiss that — but there are also people who simply become angry if food is not on time. You see it in restaurants; waitstaff deal with it constantly. Where is my food? And that is America: fast food, drive-throughs, get it now. The infantile impulse — because the very first thing a newborn does is cry for food. The mother hears the cry and her milk flows. The baby gets the breast and immediately relaxes into a state of complete contentment. That is the infantile pattern, and it is what we must all work to discipline within ourselves: the ability to do without food, to not become grumpy over it, to recognize that missing a single meal will not kill you. People say, "I'm starving." You are not starving. There are people in the world who are genuinely starving — truly dropping from hunger — but missing a meal is not that.

This is why the early shuyukh focused on hunger above almost everything else in their teaching. This does not mean becoming anorexic. The point is simply: learn discipline. Two meals a day is enough — I can say that with confidence. Morning and late afternoon. If you want a third, make it something light in the evening. But two genuine meals are sufficient.

There is a man mentioned — he was from Yemen, from the tribe of Bani Hanifa, actually of the Hanafi persuasion, and I cannot recall his name at this moment — who came to the Prophet ﷺ, and the story is in al-Bukhari. The Prophet ﷺ gave him a bowl of milk. He drank it. The Prophet asked if he wanted more. He said yes. The Prophet kept giving, bowl after bowl, until the man had drunk seven bowls. The next day he embraced Islam, and that evening when he returned, he could only manage one bowl. The Companions who had watched him drink seven the night before were astonished. They asked the Prophet ﷺ about it. He said: وَالْمُؤْمِنُ يَأْكُلُ فِي مِعًى وَاحِدٍ وَالْكَافِرُ يَأْكُلُ فِي سَبْعَةِ أَمْعَاءٍ — the disbeliever eats from seven intestines, وَالْمُؤْمِنُ يَأْكُرُوا۟ مِنْ مَعِينٍ وَاحِدٍ — but the believer eats from one. So إِيمَان actually reduces the appetite.

Now, people today eat far more than people have eaten at virtually any other point in human history. There is a massive surplus of food, driven by agribusiness. Meat consumption in particular has risen dramatically. Ask your grandparents — if they are still alive — how often they ate meat. If they were not from a wealthy family, the answer in most Muslim-majority countries was probably once a week, if they lived in a city; far less if they lived in a village. Many Yemenis, for instance, ate meat only on Eid. This is within living memory.

And in this country too, people who are older remember a time when you did not have snacks available around the clock. Children did not have constant access to food between meals. You ate when it was mealtime, and that was that. There were no convenience stores on every block selling cold drinks and packaged food at any hour. All of this is part of a modern culture that in this regard is making people behave more like animals — اللَّهُ mentions in the Quran that such people are like cattle, simply grazing. Some of those co-op-style bulk food stores actually post signs near the open bins that say "No Grazing." That sign says something. Sociologists call it "food contact," and research suggests the average American has around twenty food contacts per day — twenty separate occasions during the day when food is consumed or handled. Some nutritionists even recommend this pattern of small, frequent eating as healthier, but if you look at traditional cultures worldwide, people ate at set times and did not eat between meals. The absence of refrigeration alone enforced much of that. A Bedouin from Mauritania can go days on very little food, because that kind of discipline has been built into his life.

As for the American military: they now have self-heating field rations — you pour in water, a chemical reaction activates, and the food is hot. They have Indian curries for Sikh soldiers, and various options for different ethnic groups, with special requests accommodated. All of this erodes fortitude. And for every one soldier in the field, seventeen support personnel are required. The plans at the time called for every soldier to eventually carry a personal PC. It is difficult to imagine an army of that kind maintaining its edge — but that is a whole other discussion.

The point is that fasting instills discipline. Muslims, from the practice of Ramadan alone, have the experience of going extended periods without food — they are used to it. The tragedy is that Ramadan itself has been compromised: Muslims now often eat more during Ramadan than in any other month. I have seen it — the night is not given to worship but to feasting, massive spreads lasting until dawn. Walimas and dinner invitations have become extravagant productions, to the point where a person feels unable to invite anyone over without preparing something elaborate. The Prophets of اللَّهُ, peace be upon them, warned against all of this. The Prophet ﷺ would sometimes host guests with dates and water. You do not have to go that far, but when you are a guest, be content with whatever is served and do not take it as an insult if the spread is modest. If someone keeps things simple, assume the best — perhaps they are following the سُنَّة. حُسْنُ الظَّنِّ, a good opinion of people, is always the wiser path. You never lose by assuming the best.

So, regarding hunger — there is much more that could be said, but practically speaking: if food is something you struggle to discipline yourself around, begin by reducing your portions. Try to eat with others rather than alone — eating alone leads to eating more, and there is less بَرَكَةٌ in solitary meals. The more people sharing a plate, the more بَرَكَةٌ descends, and the less food each person needs. Sheikh Khadir, when he returned to Mauritania, was once asked what had struck him most about life there. Among the things that shocked him most, he said, was that everyone ate from separate plates. When he described this to people back home, they were genuinely baffled — they simply could not imagine it. He said he embarrassed himself repeatedly, because a large communal plate would be set down and he would start eating from it, not realizing that people were waiting to serve themselves small portions onto their own dishes.

So eat together. There is بَرَكَةٌ in it. Aim for two meals a day if you can manage it. If you cannot, at the very least avoid heavy meals — eat what is enough, and stop. And keep in mind: the higher the quality of the food, the less of it you need. Part of the reason people here eat such enormous quantities is that the food is nutritionally poor. I am confident that in a typical American restaurant, the single serving placed in front of one person would feed ten people in West Africa. Ten people. And most diners here clean their plates.

So, you know, if the food is good, you'll need less of it. If the food is poor — empty calories, fast food, that sort of thing — you'll eat more and still feel unsatisfied. By good food and bad food I mean genuinely nutritious food: wholesome grains, vegetables, things like that. I wouldn't set foot in a fast food restaurant myself. Even if it's a McFish burger or whatever it is, I'd rather skip it. I might even say the cardboard box it came in is more nutritious. Throw the hamburger out — at least cardboard has roughage.

اللَّهُمَّ وَفِّقْنَا. If you want to bring the heart to life, give some time to the night. They say even two رَكَعَاتٌ is enough. Sheikh Muhammad Ali ibn Malik said: never abandon قِيَام, even if it is only two رَكَعَاتٌ.

Sheikh Abdul Khadim, حَفِظَهُ اللهُ, adds that in this age, when so many people find these practices difficult, it is far worse to do it sporadically than to do it modestly but consistently. One night you get up and pray ten رَكَعَاتٌ with long سُوَرٌ and spend هَلْf an hour or more — and then the next night nothing, and the night after that nothing. It is far better to get up just five minutes before فَجْر and pray two رَكَعَاتٌ, every single night without fail. Make it a habit. Give some prayer to the night, do it consistently, and you will see results. A little here and a little there produces nothing. Ahmad Zarruq compared it to drilling a hole — a little bit here, a little bit there, and you will never strike water.

So give some portion to prayer. A few things about the prayer itself.

When the Prophet ﷺ arrived in Medina, 'Abdullah ibn Salam — may اللَّهُ be pleased with him, the rabbi who embraced Islam — narrated that the Prophet ﷺ gave a khutbah after descending upon Quba. He said: O people, spread peace — greet one another with peace. Feed people. وَصَلُّوا بِاللَّيْلِ وَالنَّاسُ النِّيَّامِ And pray in the night when others are sleeping. تَدْخُلَ الْجَنَّةَ بِالسَّلَامِ You will enter جَنَّة without any difficulty.

Just look at how straightforward that is. أَفْشُوا السَّلَامَ — spread peace. Do not be a cause of division. Be someone who unifies, who brings people together. أَطْعِمُوا الطَّعَامَ — feed people. Be generous. When you feed someone, you are showing you care about them. So: spread peace, be concerned about others, وَصَلُّوا بِاللَّيْلِ وَالنَّاسُ النِّيَّامِ — and then tend to your own soul. Correct yourself. If you do those things, تَدْخُلُواا الْجَنَّةَ بِالسَّلَامِ — and that تَدْخُلُوا is a جَوَابُ شَرْطٍ: if you do those things, that is the condition. خَلَاصٌ. That is all. If every one of us took that single حَدِيث and made it our practice, that is a guarantee from the Messenger of اللَّهُ ﷺ that we will enter Paradise.

They said of one man who did some questionable things during the day but prayed at night: سَتَنْهَاهُ — it will eventually stop him, if he keeps at it. On the other hand, there is another حَدِيث, quite frightening, about a woman who prayed at night and gave صَدَقَةٌ but was cruel to her neighbors. كَانَتْ تُؤْذِي جِيرَانَهَا — she used to treat her neighbors badly. And the Prophet ﷺ said: هِيَ فِي النَّارِ. She is in the Fire. And it is a صَحِيحٌ حَدِيث. Then of another person, they said: he does not pray at night, he does not give صَدَقَةٌ, he does not do these other things — وَيُحْسِنُوا إِلَى جِيرَانِ — but he is good to his neighbors. سَيَدْخُلَ الْجَنَّةَ. He will enter جَنَّة.

So you can pray all night if you like — but without good character, it counts for very little. Keep things in perspective. Tahajjud is immensely important, and اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى has encouraged it for all believers. He says: وَمَنِ اللَّيْلِ فَتَهَجَّدْ بِهِ نَافِرَةً لَكَ عَسَىٰ أَن يَبَعَثَكَ رَبُّكَ مَقَامًا مَحْمُودًا — And from the night, spend a portion of it in تَهَجُّد as a نَافِلَة, a voluntary act. Perhaps your Lord will raise you to a praiseworthy station. So this practice helps purify the heart.

And اللَّهُ says of the believers: مُؤْمِنُون تَتَجَافَتْ جُنُوبُهُمْ عَنِ الْمَضَاجِعِ — they lift their sides from their beds at night when they would far rather sleep. Sleep is something the نَفْس loves. We all love sleep. And that is precisely why restraining the نَفْس from sleep — in moderation — is spiritually powerful. Not all night. You do not want to push yourself into a sleepless psychotic state. You do not deprive yourself entirely. Keep it within reasonable bounds.

In the same way, when they speak of hunger as a discipline, they do not mean the emaciated ascetic whose spine is visible — the image people call the starving Buddha, that famous statue. That is not our tradition. The point is to learn to govern the food-driven نَفْس. The same applies to night prayers: you do not spend the entire night in prayer. There was a ta'ifa among the صَحَابَة who spent a great deal of the night in prayer — not all of it, but a great deal. According to al-Raghib al-Isfahani, a ta'ifa is fewer than a thousand, so it was a number of the صَحَابَة who sustained this, though not all of them.

Consider the well-known حَدِيث about the man the Prophet ﷺ said was a man of Paradise. One of the صَحَابَة followed him for several days and observed nothing remarkable. So he asked him directly: what are you doing? The man replied: all I know is that every night before I sleep, I make sure there is no rancor in my heart toward any Muslim. He was not praying at night, was not fasting, was not doing anything outwardly exceptional. Not all the صَحَابَة prayed through the night.

But اللَّهُ says of a group among them: كانوا قليلا من الليل ما يهجعون — in one reading, they would not sleep at night; in another reading, كانوا قليلا من الليل ما يهجعون — they slept only a small portion of the night. Both readings are valid. The point is that for some among them, this was their particular practice.

For us, I think what is rational and reasonable is a steady, consistent practice — something regular. Shaykh Abdullah al-Haddad advises that given the nature of this age, it is good to recite the shorter سُوَرٌ that carry great reward. I mentioned last night the line: إذا العشرون من شعبانا ولت فواصل شرب ليلك بالنهار — when your life is drawing toward its end, start doing as much as you can. He actually recommends even just reciting قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ ten times in short رَكَعَاتٌ to accumulate great reward.

So even short رَكَعَاتٌ count. If your practice is to pray some portion of the night and you happen to oversleep until just before فَجْر, it is still permissible and acceptable to pray those رَكَعَاتٌ as تَهَجُّد before your فَجْر prayer — as long as you will not miss فَجْر itself and as long as there is still some darkness before the إِسْفَار sets in. You can easily pray ten رَكَعَاتٌ with قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ and proper طُمَأْنِينَةٌ in seven or eight minutes — they will not be rushed, they will be sound رَكَعَاتٌ, and you can complete them in ten minutes or so. A minute per rakaat is quite manageable.

For women who cannot pray at certain times, it is perfectly acceptable to get up in the night and read something beneficial, or to meditate, or to make دُعَاء. You can recite قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ in every rakaat. The سُوَرٌ that carry particular benefit — إِذَا زُلْزِلَتِ ٱلْأَرْضُ زِلْزَالَهَا، قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ، الْمُعَوِّذَتَيْن، قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ الْفَلَقِ، قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ النَّاسِ، لِإِيلَافِ قُرَيْشٍ، أَلْهَاكُمُ التَّكَاثُرُ — those are the سُوَرٌ the Prophet ﷺ used frequently. وَٱلتِّينِ وَٱلزَّيْتُونِ is another. He recited إِذَا زُلْزِلَتِ ٱلْأَرْضُ زِلْزَالَهَا often. وَالضُّحَى وَاللَّيْلِ إِذَا سَجَى as well. And it is not a بِدْعَةٌ to recite the same surah in every rakaat of a voluntary prayer.

You shouldn't make it a regular practice, and there is some خِلَافٌ about this. The Shafi'is, I believe, would permit it, while the Malikis would consider it a بِدْعَةٌ. So be careful — for some imams it may be a بِدْعَةٌ, for others it may be acceptable. For Imam Malik, it is a بِدْعَةٌ to recite قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ at every Maghrib prayer, whereas Shaykh Abdullah al-Haddad, in one of his treatises, mentioned that it was a good thing to do, and there is a حَدِيث indicating that one of the صَحَابَة did exactly that. So you have to be a little careful with these matters.

There is also the حَدِيث about Sayyidina Abdullah ibn Umar, may اللَّهُ be pleased with him. The Prophet ﷺ once walked past him and said, نعم الرجل لو كان يقوم الليل — "What an excellent man — if only he would spend some time at night in prayer." The scholars have clarified that this is not simply a compliment; the statement contains the word لَيْتَ, a particle of wishing, as in لَيْتَ لَيْتُهُ كَانَ يَقُومَ اللَّيْل. What the scholars took from this is that a person can still be a good person even without praying at night — like Abdullah ibn Umar — but how much better would he be if he did? The believer who hears that desires perfection. And so, when Abdullah ibn Umar heard those words — and he himself is the narrator of the حَدِيث, which he related to his son — he never abandoned قِيَامُ اللَّيْلِ again after that.

The scholars also drew from this حَدِيث that it is permissible to praise someone who will not be made conceited by that praise. The Prophet ﷺ knew that Abdullah ibn Umar would not hear those words and think, "I'm already excellent, so I don't need to pray at night." Rather, it spurred him on to do more — which is exactly what happened.

Then there is the well-known حَدِيث of Aisha, which is a صَحِيح حَدِيث, in which she describes how the Prophet ﷺ would pray so long at night that his feet would crack and become slightly swollen. What the physicians call venous stasis — when you stand for extended periods, blood can pool in the feet and cause mild edema. This is why people who are on their feet all day, such as nurses, often wear compression stockings to help the blood return upward. It happens to many people.

So Aisha said, "يَا رَسُولَ اللهِ, why do you do this when your Lord has forgiven you everything — past and future?" And he replied, "Should I not be a grateful servant?" In other words, that very fact should make me want to show even more gratitude. Worship, then, is a way of expressing gratitude to اللَّهُ. The scholars say this حَدِيث shows that one of the greatest ways to give thanks for the نِعْمَةٌ of اللَّهُ is to do some night work — سَهَرُ اللَّيَالِي. And they say: من قرب المعالي سهر الليالي — whoever seeks high things stays awake at night.

For students especially, the night is the best time to study. If فَجْر falls at a particular hour, divide the night from Maghrib onward into six equal parts. Each part, in a twelve-hour night, would be roughly two hours. The last sixth of the night — the final two hours before فَجْر — is the best time for قِيَامُ اللَّيْلِ. If we take, for simplicity, Maghrib at six p.m. and فَجْر at six a.m., then four a.m. to six a.m. would be that optimal period. That said, any prayer after Isha counts as قِيَامُ اللَّيْلِ, and some scholars say even after Maghrib.

For people who find it genuinely difficult to rise in the early morning — those with what the tradition calls a "heavy liver," meaning a slow constitution — it is perfectly valid to pray some رَقَائِق before going to bed. You should do something in the night hours. Interestingly, many people find it easier to wake up around three or four in the morning than right before فَجْر, because the sleep immediately before فَجْر is recognizably the heaviest sleep of the night. This is a حِكْمَةٌ from اللَّهُ, and it also corresponds to what science tells us: cortisol levels in the brain are at their peak at that time. Ibn Ata'illah said the wisdom in this is that اللَّهُ wants you to be able to pull yourself out of sleep precisely when it is most difficult to do so.

As for the number of رَقَائِق: eight and ten are both established by صَحِيح حَدِيث, and I was told ten is the best. You pray two by two — two, four, six, eight, ten — and it is better done after Isha, unless you are someone who by nature rises every night without fail, in which case you may wait. Sayyidina Abu Bakr, who was very strong in his spiritual practice and certainly prayed the night prayer, used to pray before he went to bed.

What if you are staying up late — do you have to sleep first and then get up? No, you don't have to. Obviously it is better to sleep and then rise, because the Prophet ﷺ said that was the best way — the way of Dawud, peace be upon him, who would sleep for a portion, rise for a portion, sleep again, and rise again. That is harder on the self than simply staying up, and he maintained that practice just as he maintained his fast of every other day. It is true مُجَاهَدَة — real mastery over the نَفْس — because it is genuinely difficult to fall asleep and then get up again.

And never forget — أَعُوذُ بِاللَّهِ — that شَيْطَان is always nearby. شَيْطَانٌ يَعْقِدُ عُقَدًا ثَلَاثَةً — he ties three knots on your head while you sleep, in the unseen world, and whispers, أَمَامَكَ لَيْلٌ طَوِيلٌ — "You have a long night ahead of you, just relax." In winter especially, this is all too convincing. Those three knots are heavy. The Prophet ﷺ said that if you wake up and recite the دُعَاء of waking — الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ الَّذِي أَحْيَانِي بَعْدَ مَا أَمَاتَنِي وَإِلَيْهِ النُّشُورُ — the first knot is loosened. If you get up and make وُضُوء, the second knot is loosened. And if you then pray, the third knot is loosened and you are freed entirely.

The Prophet ﷺ was also asked about a man who slept straight through until morning without praying — and the fact that the صَحَابَة asked about it shows this was not their norm. He said, ذَاكَ رَجُلٌ بَالَ الشَّيْطَانُ فِي أُذُنِهِ — "That is a man in whose ears شَيْطَان urinated." الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ. Anyone who does not pray the night prayer has شَيْطَان using his ear as a toilet. We should not want that. The way out is simply to do something — get up in the night, pray even a few رَقَائِق for اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ.

<!-- page 243 -->

Some of the scholars said this should be understood حَقِيقَةً — literally — that شَيْطَان literally urinates in the ear. And the principle in Islamic jurisprudence is that الحقيقة لا تُصرف إلى المجاز إلا لضرورة — the literal meaning is not set aside for a metaphorical one unless there is a necessity to do so. Some scholars found no such necessity and took it literally. Others interpreted it metaphorically, saying it means شَيْطَان deprives that person of all the good that comes from spending part of the night in worship. وَاللَّهُ تَعَالَى أَعْلَمُ.

Can you remind us of the دُعَاء? الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ الَّذِي أَحْيَانِي بَعْدَ مَا أَمَاتَنِي وَإِلَيْهِ النُّشُورُ — Praise be to the One who brought me back to life after He caused me to die, and to Him is my return.

<!-- page 244 -->

There is also the practice of reciting the closing verses of سُورَةُ آلِ عِمْرَانَ upon waking. I spent many nights in مُرَبِّي حَجٌّ's tent, and every single night he would get up — usually four, sometimes five hours before فَجْر — and he would wake you with those verses: إِنَّ فِي خَلْقِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَاخْتِلَافِ اللَّيْلِ وَالنَّهَارِ لَآيَاتٍ لِأُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ الَّذِينَ يَذْكُرُونَ اللَّهَ قِيَامًا وَقُعُودًا وَعَلَى جُنُوبِهِمْ وَيَتَفَكَّرُونَ فِي خَلْقِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ رَبَّنَا مَا خَلَقْتَ هَذَا بَاطِلًا — and I can still hear his voice reciting them. إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, اللَّهُ give us تَوْفِيق. It is all تَوْفِيق from اللَّهُ — the ability to do these things is itself a gift from Him.

What prevents us from قِيَامُ اللَّيْلِ — what blocks it — is غِيبَة, eating what is حَرَامٌ, and similar violations. These are what close the door. This is a gift from اللَّهُ, and those who have experienced it and then lost it know that when they had it, it truly felt like a gift, and when it was taken away, it felt like a punishment. So make تَوْبَةٌ, ask اللَّهُ to restore it, and إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ He will.

<!-- page 245 -->

The people of the قَوْم used to hold themselves to such rigorous مُحَاسَبَة that whenever something afflicted them they could trace the cause. One scholar said, "I once said something about someone that I should not have said, and I was deprived of تَهَجُّد for forty days because of it." They could see the direct connection between cause and consequence. Another scholar said that near the end of his life he went bankrupt, and he realized it was the result of something that had happened forty years earlier — he had once said to a man, "يَا مُفْلِسُ," meaning "you bankrupt one," and he understood that اللَّهُ had given him exactly what he had called that other man. As the saying goes, the good actions of the righteous are the bad actions of those who are near to اللَّهُ — meaning what is an ordinary slip for most people carries far greater weight for those whose hearts are refined.

<!-- page 246 -->

On the question of وِتْرٌ: do not pray the وِتْرٌ twice. Witr should be the last prayer of the night — that is the correct practice. It is perfectly valid to pray additional prayers after وِتْرٌ, but وِتْرٌ should come last. So if you go to bed early, it is better to pray your وِتْرٌ before you sleep, since وِتْرٌ should be completed before فَجْر. If you wake up and forgot to pray it, you can still make it up after فَجْر, but it is better done before. In the الْمَذْهَبُ الْحَنَفِيُّ, وِتْرٌ is وَاجِب; in the other مَذَاهِب, it is a سُنَّةٌ مُؤَكَّدَةٌ — a strongly emphasized سُنَّة.

The شِفَاءٌ and وِتْرٌ should be prayed before you go to bed. Then if you rise during the night, do not repeat the وِتْرٌ; simply pray تَهَجُّد in pairs — two by two, مَتْنًا مَتْنًا, as the حَدِيث says. You do not pray four together; you pray two, then two.

If you are asking whether you can do تَهَجُّد after وِتْرٌ — yes, you can. The preferable practice is to delay the وِتْرٌ so that it is the last prayer of the night. That is the ideal. But put it this way: if you are as certain as one can reasonably be that you will wake up before فَجْر, then it is better to delay your وِتْرٌ. If your consistent, reliable practice is to wake up هَلْf an hour or an hour before فَجْر and pray — and you are confident in that pattern — then it is better to delay. If, on the other hand, you are not consistent in waking up, then it is better to pray your وِتْرٌ before you go to sleep.

<!-- page 247 -->

And if you do wake up during the night before فَجْر, you pray your تَهَجُّد without ending with وِتْرٌ, since you have already prayed it and there is only one وِتْرٌ in the night.

— Ingrizi nahi malum hai. That's all the Urdu I know. There is an Afghani joke along these lines. An American traveled to Afghanistan having heard that the country was home to great spiritual teachers and saints. He wanted to find the greatest of them. So he approached an Afghani man and asked, the way Americans do — assuming everyone speaks English — "Who is the greatest spiritual teacher in Afghanistan?" The man replied, "Nami fahman." So the American went around asking after Nami Fahman, treating it as a name.

At some point a funeral procession passed by, and he saw a crowd watching solemnly. He went up to one of the Afghans and asked, "Who just died?" The man replied, "Nami fahman." Nami fahman, of course, means "I don't understand" in Persian.

<!-- page 248 -->

On another point — about taking things easy and not driving yourself into the ground — Shaykh Habib said in الْقَصِيدَة that you should travel the path with ease toward yourself. You need yourself to complete the journey. Your horse has to carry you to where you are going, so you treat your horse well. Do not wear it out to the point where it cannot carry you any further. He is not saying be lazy — he is saying do not exhaust yourself so completely that you lose the capacity to continue.

The goal is to move along the path with yearning. If you are not too hard on yourself, your self will actually begin to do these things. If you try to stay up the whole night, it is too much for the self. If you try to stay up هَلْf the night, it is still too much. The self loses energy and gives up entirely. But if you start small, something shifts — you begin to taste the sweetness of it, you feel the benefit, and you start to want it. The self begins to look forward to it. That is the point he is making.

And then he says: two رَقَائِق prayed by a lover are better than a thousand prayed without love. If you pray two رَقَائِق with شَوْق — with genuine longing, with a real desire for اللَّهُ's pleasure — اللَّهُ will love it. But if you are going through the motions thinking, "I have to do this, it is important, I must," with no love behind it — there is no love there. The action is driven by an idea in your head, not by your heart. The whole point is that all of this should arise from a yearning for اللَّهُ, as a مَحَبَّة from اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى.

<!-- page 249 -->

And then he says: وَالصَّمْتُ — silence. One of the diseases he addresses is lust for speech — اَلْكَلَامُ — which is, for most people, a deeply ingrained drive. The Prophet ﷺ said, مَنْ أُوتِيَ صَمْتًا فَقَدْ أُوتِيَ الْحِكْمَةَ — whoever is given silence as part of his nature has been given wisdom. There is wisdom in silence itself, simply in being silent. Even an ignorant person, if he is given the gift of silence, possesses a kind of wisdom — because at the very least, if you are ignorant, silence keeps you from making a fool of yourself.

If you have something to say, speech is better than silence. If you have nothing to say, it is permissible — مُبَاحٌ — but there is no benefit in it. And if that permissible speech amounts to casual pleasantries — how are you, where have you been — that is fine.

But if it turns to "did you hear about so-and-so," it slides toward either مَكْرُوهٌ or حَرَامٌ. Learning to govern the tongue is a serious discipline.

Imam al-Shafi'i said that whenever he was in a gathering and felt moved to speak, he would first look inward and ask himself whether he was speaking out of a desire of the نَفْس — to prove a point or display his knowledge. He would check whether he was content for someone else to say the very same thing. If he could honestly say yes, he would speak. If not, he would stay silent, as a تَرْبِيَة for his نَفْس. He also said that whenever he entered a debate, he would make دُعَاء that اللَّهُ cause the truth to appear on his opponent's tongue, so that he himself could submit to it. He had no wish to win — he would have preferred to lose, because losing would make him humble. That is a رَجُلٌ, that is a man.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, was the most reserved of people. When he spoke, he spoke the truth. He was not what is called فَرْفَدْ. He said, صلى الله عليه وسلم, إِنَّ أَبْعَدَكُمْ مِنِّي وَأَبْعَدَكُمْ إِلَيَّ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ، الْفَرْفَارِينَ، الْمُتَفَيْهِقِينَ — the most distant of you from me, and the most distant to me on the Day of Judgment, are the فَرْفَرٍ and the المُتَفَيْهِقِينَ. The companions recognized the other terms, but did not know المُتَفَيْهِقِينَ, and he explained it as مُتَكَدِّر. فَرْفَدْ is someone who speaks incessantly — فَرْفَرَ، يُفَرْفِرُ — they simply never stop talking. He disliked the chatterbox. If you are teaching or saying something of use, that is another matter entirely. But speech for speech's sake is a waste.

<!-- page 243 -->

Imam Malik, رَحِمَهُ اللَّهُ, said of one of his students: نِعْمَ الرَّجُلُ، إِلَّا أَنَّهُ يَتَكَلَّمُ كَلَامَ الشَّهْرِ فِي يَوْمٍ وَاحِدٍ — he is a fine man, except that he speaks a whole month's worth of words in a single day.

Then there is الْفِكْرَةُ وَهُوَ خَالٍ — reflection in solitude. خَالِي means to be in a place with no one around, engaged in reflection. In English we call this meditation, and it is a genuine practice — not yogic, but simply sitting and meditating. Within the Islamic tradition there are various forms. One is التَّفَكُّرُ فِي الْمَوْتِ, or contemplating the states of the hereafter: you sit and walk through the raising from the grave and the scenes of the Day of Judgment. Another is تَفَكُّر in the divine names: you move through them — يَا اللهُ، يَا رَحْمَٰنُ، يَا رَحِيمُ — and reflect on each attribute. So فِكْرَة in solitude benefits the heart.

Then there is صُحْبَةُ الْأَخْيَارِ — keeping company with the righteous. This practice is rooted in a Quranic injunction: يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَكُونُوا مَعَ الصَّادِقِينَ — O you who believe, have تَقْوَى of اللَّهُ and be with the truthful ones. This is what is called صُحْبَة in the Persian tradition: keeping good company with righteous people in order to purify the heart.

Imam Ibn 'Ata'illah says in the حِكَم: لَا تَصْحَبْ مَن لَا يُنْهِضُكَ حَالُهُ وَلَا يَدُلُّكَ عَلَى اللَّهِ مَقَالُهُ — Do not take as a companion someone whose state does not lift you, and whose speech does not direct you toward اللَّهُ.

Sidi Ahmad Zarruq notes in his commentary that this is not referring to spiritual masters — it is referring to the friends you spend ordinary time with. You should seek people who will elevate your state, not drag it down. And when they elevate yours, their own state rises too.

What this means in practice is that such people are in a good state with اللَّهُ — a state of gratitude. When you are around people who are genuinely grateful, it becomes difficult to complain. They are saying, اَلْحَمْدُ لِلَّٰهِ, سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ, I feel truly grateful, اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ, اللَّهُ has given us so much — and you find yourself sitting there unable to say, sorry, I can't agree, life is hard. They are in a different هَلْ from you. So if you are the one in شُكْر, do not keep company with the one in شَكْوَى. And if you are the one in شَكْوَى, seek out the one in شُكْر, so that you may become like him. Do not drag him down — let him lift you up. That is the teaching.

And as for their speech directing you to اللَّهُ: there are people who, if you did complain to them, would gently say, سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ — وَإِن تَعُدُّوا۟ نِعْمَةَ ٱللَّهِ لَا تُحْصُوهَآ — if you try to enumerate the blessings of اللَّهُ, you cannot count them; that applies to everyone. So perhaps instead of cataloguing your troubles, you might try counting his blessings. They help you shift your focus. And it is remarkable how a person's entire state can be transformed simply by sitting with someone who offers sincere and good counsel. A heart that was utterly dark and negative can be turned around entirely, because the effect was real and valuable — the person's perspective changes, and that is guidance.

That is what you want. And on this, Sidi Abu al-Hasan said: مَن دَلَّكَ عَلَى الدُّنْيَا فَقَدْ غَشَّكَ — whoever directs you toward this world has cheated you. If someone's entire counsel to you is about financial planning, acquiring property, securing your future in this life — that is a sign that he has cheated you, because you could die at any moment.

The example I like to use is this: imagine two salesmen come to you. The first says, "I have a house for sale. The only drawback is that it could disappear at any time — I cannot tell you exactly when, maybe fifty years from now, but it will be gone within seventy at the most, and it could vanish at any point before that." The second says, "I have another house. It will last forever and is guaranteed — but it is more expensive." Which one do you buy? Seriously — which one? Any person of sound intellect is going to buy the second one.

That is the آخِرَة. And you purchase it with your soul. The other house, you also purchase with your soul — but you buy it by destroying the soul, not by preserving it.

So: keeping good company. Then Sidi Abu al-Hasan also said that the one who tells you to rush and pile up deeds has merely exhausted you — if you believe you will reach جَنَّة through your deeds alone, you will wear yourself out. وَمَن دَلَّكَ عَلَى اللَّهِ فَقَدْ نَصَحَكَ — but the one who shows you the path to اللَّهُ has given you sincere counsel. That path is the contentment of اللَّهُ. If you are content with اللَّهُ, a small amount of action will be enough. If you are not, no amount of action will ever suffice.

And finally, he speaks of taking refuge — of returning to the One to whom all affairs return, since اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى says that to اللَّهُ all affairs return. Seeking refuge in اللَّهُ is, above all else, the truest remedy.

This is why a scholar of the path says that the only real cure for all these diseases of the heart is to go before اللَّهُ in a state of complete neediness — please, I need to change, and I need You to help me change — with a genuine sense of urgency. If that is the state a person is in, then إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ, اللَّهُ will help them change.

For my own part, I truly believe that my coming to Islam was the result of a state I entered before I was Muslim — a state of complete اِفْتِقَارٌ. I called on اللَّهُ by the name I knew, which was God, and I simply asked: give me guidance, show it to me. I was in a state of utter neediness. Most of the people I have met who became Muslim will confirm something similar: that there was a moment of absolute — just show me what to do. And then the guidance opened up, and it became very clear. خَلَاصٌ. After that it becomes easy, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ.

I believe اللَّهُ is merciful, and that where there is sincerity, even within a person's limited and imperfect understanding, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ that is enough. There is an apocrypهَلْ story — and because it is apocrypهَلْ, no عَقِيدَة is based on it, I want to be clear about that — but it carries a wisdom that has been told: about the Prophet Musa, عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ, encountering a man who is speaking to اللهُ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى about wanting to wash His feet, give Him a massage, and tend to Him — a man who is an anthropomorphist without knowing it.

And Musa, moved by righteousness, rebuked the man and explained the true meaning of تَوْحِيد. Yet اللَّهُ reminded Musa that he had been harsh, for the man had spoken those words out of love — he was simply ignorant. The right response to ignorance is to teach. So the point is that everyone is at the level where they are. Even the one bowing before an idol — that is his understanding; that is where he stands. This is why اللَّهُ says:

وَلَا تَجَعَلُوا لِلَّهِ أَنْدَادًا

Do not set up rivals to اللَّهُ —

وَأَنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

— while you know it is wrong. Once knowledge reaches a person, that is when شِرْك destroys him. If someone has no knowledge, and اللَّهُ's messenger has not yet reached him, اللَّهُ will not punish him:

وَمَا كُنَّا مُعَذِّبِينَ حَتَّىٰ نَبْعَثَ رَسُولًۭا

So if someone does not know Islam and is genuinely seeking guidance, that guidance comes, إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ.

وَاللَّهُ تَعَالَى عَنْهُمْ

Then the author says:

بِأَنْ يَكُونَ كَغَرِيقًا أَوْ كَمَنْ ضَلَّ بِتِيهِنْ لَا يَرَى الْغِيَاثَةِ مِنْ سِوَى الْمُهَيْمَنَ الْعَظِيمِ الْقَدْرِ وَهُوَ الْمُجِيبُ دَعْوَةٍ مُقَرِّي

He is saying that you must be like someone who is drowning, or like someone lost in a barren desert who sees no source of help except the Almighty God. This is precisely what happened to Yusuf Islam. I heard him describe it in a lecture: he was caught in the undertow at Malibu, being dragged out to sea — genuinely terrifying — and in that moment he called on God completely and promised: give me guidance and I will follow it, whatever you ask of me. That is the غَرِيقٌ — the drowning man — with nowhere left to turn.

And then there is Ikrimah ibn Abi Jahl, whose guidance came on a boat carrying Abyssinian Christians. When the waves began to overwhelm the vessel and everyone feared they would drown, Ikrimah started calling on Hubal. The Christians on board laughed at him. They said: you are calling on an idol made of wood, sitting in Mecca, to save you out here? And in that moment it struck him — how absurd it was. He realized he could only call on اللَّهُ. And تَوْحِيد entered his heart right there. That is the state of the drowning man. And the author says this is in fact the most beneficial state — the one that will truly help us. It must reach the point where you are like a man going under.

So that is that.
