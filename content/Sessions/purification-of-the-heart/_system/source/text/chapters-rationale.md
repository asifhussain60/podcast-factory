## Source chapter 1 — Love of the World, Praise, and Envy

`ch01a-the-world-and-the-envious-heart.txt` — Source chapter 1 runs 10,621 words, roughly 1.8x the upper bound of the extended tier, so it is cut once rather than twice, at the seam the lecture itself makes when it stops describing envy and starts accounting for it ("he then moves into the causes of hasad"). Everything before that seam is this episode: the definition of blameworthy worldly love and the five legal categories it distributes across; wealth, reputation and the prohibition on earning in order to display; the appetite for praise, the line between flattery and honest encouragement, and covetousness as the motive underneath both ends of that exchange; then envy itself — the first disobedience in the heavens, the fire-and-dry-wood image, the universality of the evil eye and the sound hadith behind it, the meaning of ni'ma and the argument that wishing it away is a verdict on the Giver; then the cure in its two parts, knowledge and action; and finally the same disease scaled up to a civilisation, in the material on resentment of the West, the Gulf oil wealth, communism as institutionalised envy, and the inheritance laws legislated to break the monopoly of wealth. Three concept sections carry it, which required folding love of praise into the section on worldly attachment rather than giving it its own heading — a defensible grouping because the source itself moves from reputation to the appetite for reputation without a break, treating praise as the world's least material currency, and because the source table of contents assigns only three topics to this half of the chapter. The written episode is 5,999 words, inside the 5,500–6,000 band.

`ch02b-where-envy-comes-from.txt` — This episode takes the lecture from the causes of envy through to the end of the source chapter. Its first concept is the causal taxonomy and what the author does with it: seven named causes (enmity, rivalry over a shared object, arrogance, defence of one's standing, incredulity, love of authority, covetousness), then Imam al-Ghazali's claim that these diseases are to be converted rather than eliminated, then the hadith of the two permitted envies and the distinction of ghibta from hasad, then the narrow permission to envy an oppressor. The second concept pairs two ailments the source itself pairs, both of which concern restraint applied in the wrong place: blameworthy modesty (with the conditions fencing the duty to object, the women who would not let modesty stop them asking, and the wedding-night restraint that is explicitly *not* blameworthy) and the stirring of the heart into matters that do not concern it. The third concept carries the remaining diseases of the slice — the fear of poverty and its cure, mudahana against its permissible near-twin mudarat, and showing off with its three diagnostic signs. Grouping three distinct diseases under one heading is the one place this design presses against the concept ceiling; it follows the grouping the source table of contents already made for this stretch, and the alternative — a fourth concept section — would have broken the ceiling outright. The cure for showing off is deliberately withheld, because the source withholds it: it opens the next source chapter, which is episode three. The written episode is 5,860 words, inside the band.
