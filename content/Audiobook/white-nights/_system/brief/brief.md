The narrator of this story has no name, and that is fitting, for he has almost no life to attach a name to. He is twenty-six years old and has lived in Petersburg for eight years without making a single friend. Having no people to be intimate with, he has grown intimate with things and with strangers: he forms attachments to the houses along the streets, greeting them as acquaintances, and invents whole histories for the passers-by he will never address. He calls himself, without much apology and without much pride, a dreamer. His real existence being empty, he lives instead upon invented romances, and the rare moments in which something actually happens to him he carries home and relives in fantasy until they are worn thin. The immediate cause of his sadness at the opening is small and precise: everyone in Petersburg is departing for the summer villas, and he is left behind, with nowhere to go and no one to invite him. It is out of this particular loneliness — the city emptying, the dreamer remaining — that everything else proceeds.

After a joyful day spent walking in the countryside outside the city, he returns at ten at night along a deserted canal embankment, and there he sees a young woman leaning upon the railing and crying. He does not dare speak to her. She notices him and hurries away; and here chance intervenes, for a drunken man begins to follow her, and the narrator, who would never have found the courage on his own account, drives the man off with his walking stick. That act of protection is what gives him the right to walk beside her, and thus the whole acquaintance rests upon an accident.

He does not court her. He confesses. Immediately and completely, without being asked, he tells her that he is shy, that he has never in his life had a relationship with a woman, that he falls in love only with imagined ideals, and that he does not know how to speak to a living one. What is remarkable, and what turns the encounter from an incident into a story, is that she does not mock him. She answers with warmth. She tells him that she too has no one in whom to confide, and that she feels as though she has known him twenty years already. She agrees to meet him at the same spot at ten the following night — but she insists that she must be there in any case, for a private reason of her own, which she will not disclose. And she sets one condition upon their friendship: he must not fall in love with her.

He accepts the condition, which is the only way he could have been given the friendship at all. He cannot go home. He walks the whole night through, declaring to himself that in two minutes she has made him happy forever, and has perhaps reconciled him with himself. In that declaration the tension of the whole story is already set: the happiness of a dreamer built upon two minutes of borrowed reality, and the question, not yet asked aloud, of what will be left of him when the loan is called in.

On the second night she returns and insists that they proceed sensibly. Before anything else, she demands that he tell her his whole story, for she does not yet know him. He answers that he has no story, because he has lived utterly alone: he has seen people all his life and has never truly spoken to any of them. Since he cannot give her events, he gives her a description of himself instead, and he gives it as though he were describing a species rather than a man. He is a type, he says: a dreamer, a creature who hides in a dim Petersburg corner as a snail hides in its shell, and who drives away his acquaintances one by one until there is no one left to drive away. He tries to make her understand the peculiar wealth of that condition — that the dreamer's joy is real, an inner richness fed by fantasy, so intense and so obedient to his wishes that ordinary life afterwards seems dull, lifeless and slow by comparison. But he does not present this as a good bargain. He warns that a day arrives when the dreamer would give away every dream he has ever had for one moment of real, imperfect life. And he confesses that for him the day has come: his fancy has worn itself out, he has begun to celebrate the anniversaries of feelings that never occurred and of events that never took place, and he is afraid of growing old with nothing real behind him, not even anything worth regretting.

He expects laughter. What he receives is tears. Nastenka — she gives him her name — weeps, and tells him that she recognizes herself in what he has said, because she felt precisely the same thing during the two years in which she sat pinned to her grandmother. She reveals that she lives with a blind grandmother who, two years earlier, pinned Nastenka's dress to her own so that she could not run off. That single detail explains her: a girl kept literally fastened to an old woman's side, whose only escape has been the same one he has taken.

He tells her that she has made him stop despising himself, and that these two evenings have given him the only real happiness he has known — and that he dreads, even while saying so, the moment when it will end. She will not hear of an ending. She vows that they will never part, and asks him to be to her like an elder brother, one who gives her warm advice. They clasp hands upon it, as though sealing a pact, and then, the pact being sealed, Nastenka begins to tell her own story.

She is seventeen and an orphan, raised by the blind grandmother who pinned her dress to her own to keep her from misbehaving. The house has a top floor which is let to lodgers, and at a certain point a new lodger arrived: a young man, pleasant to look at, about whom the grandmother at once warned Nastenka to get no ideas. The bond between them began not with a declaration but with an act of tact. The lodger came upon Nastenka pinned to the old woman, saw her humiliation, said nothing at all, bowed silently and withdrew. He afterwards lent her books — French novels, Walter Scott, Pushkin — and these replaced her childish daydreams with a fuller imaginative life, which is to say that he made her, in a smaller way, what the narrator already was.

Then he invited her to the theatre. She refused, because she would not deceive her grandmother; whereupon he took a box at The Barber of Seville for all three of them, so that the old woman might come too. Nastenka understood only later that the invitation had been a test of her character, and that she had passed it. After the opera season, however, he withdrew from them entirely. Nastenka grew restless and began to waste away, and concluded that what she had taken for love had been nothing but pity.

Then she learned that he was to leave for Moscow for a year. That knowledge broke her. She made up a bundle of her things and went to his room at night, and offered, in so many words, to go away with him. He did not take her. He refused because he was poor and had no right to bind her to himself; but he swore that if in a year's time she still loved him, he would come back and marry her. They kept the promise secret from the grandmother, and Nastenka waited.

The year is now up. He has been back in Petersburg three days, and he has not come to the bench where they agreed to meet at ten o'clock. That is the private reason she would not disclose; that is why she was standing at the railing weeping when the narrator first saw her; and that is why she has been at the same place each night since.

The narrator, who has by this time begun to love her, does the thing that seals his own fate. He advises her to write. He dictates a letter for her — a letter which asserts her right to write without reproach and without pleading, neither accusing the man nor begging him — and Nastenka, laughing, produces the letter already written, sealed and carried about with her all the while. She entrusts it to the narrator to deliver, and asks him to bring any answer to the bench at ten the next night. So he becomes the agent of his rival's return, and does it willingly. They laugh together over Rosina, part with "Till tomorrow," and he is left standing in the street elated and wholly in love.

It is on the third night that the truth of his position becomes clear to him. He understands that all the warmth Nastenka has shown him — the vow never to part, the brotherhood, the tears — has been the overflow of a joy that was meant for another man, spilling onto whoever stood nearest. It was never love for him. She arrives radiant and early, certain that her lover will answer that night, and she tells the narrator, with perfect frankness and no cruelty at all, that she likes him precisely because he has not fallen in love with her, and that once she is married they will be friends closer than brother and sister.

The lover does not come. Footsteps approach along the embankment, raise their hopes and then carry someone else past; and Nastenka's gaiety collapses into anxiety. The narrator tries, obliquely, to say what he feels, reaching for the image of a forgotten melody suddenly reawakening in a man who thought he had lost the capacity for it; but Nastenka guesses his meaning before he can arrive at it and hushes him. So he swallows it, and turns instead to comforting her: he invents reasons why the letter may have been delayed, and promises to go and ask for a reply at first light. Weeping, she compares the two men aloud. The narrator, she says, is the better man, kinder and more thoughtful — and yet she loves the other more. She feels, too, that he is sacrificing something for her sake, and thanks him for it, telling him that he is no longer the isolated dreamer he described himself as being. They part with her promise to come the day after tomorrow whatever happens, and with her question: they will always be together, will they not?

The fourth day is rainy, and she does not come. The narrator wanders to their bench, and then as far as her door, and turns back in shame at the last moment, and the day ends in the old loneliness. No reply to the letter has arrived either, and he concludes bitterly that the lovers must already be together.

But on the following night she is at the canal at nine, and he must tell her that there is no letter and that her lover has not come. It destroys her last hope. She collapses into sobbing, calling three days of complete silence cruel and inhuman, and tormenting herself above all with the shame of it — that she was the one who went to him first, that she carried her bundle to his room, that she begged to be loved. She refuses the narrator's offer to go to the man in her name, or to write again for her; she declares that she no longer knows him, no longer loves him, and will forget him. Then, in her grief, she asks the narrator whether he could ever have abandoned a girl who had bared her whole soul to him — and that question breaks his restraint.

He confesses that he loves her. He apologizes in advance for the absurdity of it and for the wretchedness of the moment he has chosen. And he insists that his love would never be a burden to her: he would love her so completely that even if she went on loving the other man, she would feel nothing from him but warmth, and the knowledge that there was someone alive whose whole occupation was her happiness.

She accepts him. She says she will get over the other, because that man mocked her while the narrator wept beside her; that she loves the narrator now because he is the nobler of the two. She reframes the whole year of waiting as possibly a childish delusion, born of a life shut up with her grandmother, and concludes that the man was never worthy of her at all. She gives him her hand and promises that her gratitude will grow into a love worthy of his.

What follows is the happiest passage of the narrator's life and the shortest. They spend the night in a delirium of practical planning. He will move the very next day into the empty room on the top floor and become Granny's lodger; they will bring Granny, and Matrona, and Fekla; Nastenka will give lessons; they will go to the theatre. For an hour or two the dreamer has a future made of ordinary furniture and ordinary people, which is exactly what he told her, on the second night, he would exchange every dream to obtain.

Then, as he is walking her home, a figure appears in the street and calls her name. It is the lover. Nastenka tears herself out of the narrator's arms and runs into his. Before she goes, she comes running back once more, to throw her arms round the narrator and kiss him warmly, in gratitude; and then she takes her lover's hand and the two of them disappear into the night, leaving him standing where he stands, frozen and hollowed out.

In the morning the servant Matrona brings a letter, and he knows the handwriting at once. In it Nastenka begs his forgiveness. She admits that she deceived him and deceived herself equally, and that what passed between them was a dream. She tells him that she loves him still, and that she wishes she could love them both at once — echoing what she had said before, that she wished he had been the lodger. She explains why her heart went back so instantly: because it had never truly left, because it had always belonged to that man, and there was nothing in it for her to bring back to him. She announces that she is to be married next week, and she asks the narrator to remain her friend and her brother, and to come and meet them both.

Alone in his room, he has a vision of the years: Matrona grown old, the room and the house across the street darkened and decayed, the wallpaper faded — and himself, fifteen years on, unchanged and just as lonely as he is now. The dream of the top-floor room, of Granny and Fekla and the lessons and the theatre, is closed off, and what remains is precisely the fate he had described to Nastenka on the second night, when he confessed that he feared arriving at old age with nothing real behind him.

And yet the ending does not fall where bitterness would put it. He refuses to blame her. He will not darken her happiness with reproach, or lay any weight of guilt upon her, or let a single bitter thought cross her sky; he wishes her clear skies and an untroubled joy, and he wishes it sincerely, without irony and without the concealed hope that she may one day regret. The story closes on the question he puts to himself instead: whether one whole moment of pure happiness is not enough for the entire life of a man. He does not argue the point. He simply leaves it standing, and the shape of his answer is plain — that it is enough, that it must be made enough, because for him it is all there is.

What the story is built to carry is contained in that last exchange between wish and fact. The dreamer's disease is that he has substituted imagination for living, and has grown rich inside and destitute outside; his cure, when it arrives, arrives in the person of a girl who is herself half a dreamer, made so by being pinned to an old woman's dress. For four nights he is given the thing he had told her he would trade every fantasy to possess: a real person, imperfect, in real time, who needs him. But the reality is not his. Every warmth he receives is overflow, every service he performs — the dictated letter, the delivered envelope, the promise to go at daybreak for an answer — hastens the return of the man who will take her away. He is, throughout, the instrument of his own dispossession, and he acts as that instrument knowingly, out of kindness, which is the only thing in him that is not fantasy.

Nastenka does not betray him in any ordinary sense. She says nothing untrue at the moment she says it. She sets her condition honestly at the start, that he must not fall in love with her; she warns him fairly that she likes him because he has not; and when she accepts him it is in good faith, and she is undone by her own heart rather than by any calculation. That is what makes the ending land as it does. There is no villain to blame, and therefore no consolation of blame. There is only the fact that a heart that has always belonged elsewhere will return there the instant it is called, and a man left standing in a street at night with the taste of a grateful kiss and a future dissolved.

He had said, after two minutes of her company on the first night, that she had made him happy forever and had perhaps reconciled him with himself. The last page tests that sentence and, in its way, upholds it. He is not reconciled to his life, which he foresees will decay around him unchanged. But he is reconciled to her, and to the fact of having once been alive for a few nights, and he declines to spoil that fact by resenting it. A whole moment of happiness, offered against a lifetime — the dreamer, who has spent his years counting anniversaries of things that never happened, has finally acquired one that did.
