# White Nights

## Opening Credits

This is White Nights, a poignant tale of love, loneliness, and dreams, written by Fyodor Dostoevsky, translated by Tim Zengerink, narrated by Zeke Ring.

## Introduction

The Poetic Heart of a Lonely Dreamer White Nights is one of Fyodor Dostoevsky's most lyrical and tender works, a short story that captures the fragile beauty of human connection and the aching poignancy of solitude. First published in 1848, early in Dostoevsky's career, it belongs to a period before his more mature philosophical and psychological novels such as Crime and Punishment or The Brothers Karamazov. Yet, despite its brevity and relative simplicity, White Nights contains emotional and thematic depths that have made it one of his most beloved and

enduring stories. Set in the magical twilight of a St. Petersburg summer, when the sun never fully sets and the nights are awash in a surreal silvery light, the story follows a solitary, unnamed narrator, a dreamer who wanders the empty streets at night. He lives more in his imagination than in the real world, finding comfort in fantasies and silent buildings, in poetry and ideas. But his quiet, repetitive life is interrupted when he meets Nastenka, a young woman weeping on

a bench. Over the course of four nights and one morning, they share a brief, intense intimacy that changes them both. The story is not just a romantic interlude. It is an exploration of the human need to be seen, to be heard, to be loved. It is about the yearning for connection in a world that often feels cold and distant. Dostoevsky, who himself was prone to melancholy and isolation, gives voice here to a character who

longs not for glory or wealth, but simply for a moment of understanding. Through the dreamer's confession, the reader is drawn into a soul that feels deeply and asks for very little, but finds that even a little can be everything. Unlike many of Dostoevsky's later characters, who are torn by guilt, ideology, and inner chaos, the narrator of White Nights is gentle, kind, and almost painfully innocent. He is a man untouched by cruelty, yet marked by the pain of being invisible.

His love for Nastenka is pure and unselfish, rooted not in desire but in a deep emotional bond. And that is what makes the story so moving. It is not about possession or fulfillment. It is about giving without expecting, loving without demanding, hoping even in the face of certain loss. Light, Shadows, and the Ephemeral Nature of Love The setting of White Nights is more than just a backdrop.

It is a vital part of the story's emotional atmosphere. The White Nights of St. Petersburg. Petersburg, those luminous summer evenings where night never fully arrives, mirror the dreamer's emotional state, a space between darkness and light, between sleep and waking, between fantasy and reality. It is a time when ordinary life is suspended and something otherworldly seems possible. This luminous unreality is what makes the dreamer's encounter with Nastenka feel so enchanted. They meet not in daylight, where the world

imposes its rules and expectations, but in this in-between space where hearts speak more freely. For four nights, they walk and talk, sharing their hopes and sorrows. The dreamer opens up in ways he never has before. He is not trying to impress or manipulate. He simply wants to be close to someone. Nastenka, too, is lonely and vulnerable. She tells him of her strict grandmother, her confined life, and the man she loves who promised to return but has not.

Their connection is real, but fragile. They understand one another, yet they are not meant to last. The dreamer falls in love with Nastenka, but she is still in love with someone else. When her former lover returns, she goes to him, and the dreamer is left alone once more. But Dostoyevsky does not present this as a tragedy. There is sadness, yes, but also beauty. The dreamer has had his moment.

He was seen. He was needed. He was loved, even if briefly. And for someone who has lived in the shadows, that is no small thing. His final words are not bitter, but full of gratitude. A whole moment of happiness. Is that too little for the whole of a man's life? This line captures the spirit of White Nights. It is a story about fleeting joy, not

because it is destined to vanish, but because its very ephemerality gives it meaning. The dreamer is not destroyed by disappointment. He returns to his solitude, but something in him has changed. He has touched another soul. He has known the light. This emotional nuance is part of what makes White Nights resonate so powerfully. In a world where love is often portrayed as dramatic, possessive, or transactional, Dostoyevsky offers a vision of love that is quiet, selfless, and

tender. A love that expects nothing and gives everything. A love that, though brief, illuminates a life. THE DREAMER, THE OUTSIDER, AND THE SEEDS OF DOSTOYEVSKY'S THEMES White Nights is not just a love story. It is a portrait of the dreamer as an outsider. The narrator lives on the margins of life, observing others, constructing imaginary relationships, and yearning for a reality he cannot enter. He is not mentally ill, nor is he

a social rebel. He is simply alone, painfully aware of his isolation, and yet he maintains a hopeful, even childlike heart. This figure of the dreamer would evolve in Dostoyevsky's later works into more complex, conflicted characters. Raskolnikov, the underground man, Prince Mishkin, Ivan Karamazov, all share with the dreamer a sense of dislocation, a struggle between inner vision and outer reality. But in White Nights, the dreamer is still innocent.

He has not yet been hardened by suffering or drawn into philosophical rebellion. He still believes in the possibility of love, kindness, and simple human connection. What makes this story so valuable, especially within Dostoyevsky's larger body of work, is its emotional purity. It shows the author in a more tender mode, unburdened by religious or political weight. There is no murder, no confession, no courtroom, no philosophical duel, just two people walking through twilight, trying to make sense of their hearts.

And yet, the themes that would come to define Dostoyevsky's writing are already present. The search for meaning, the pain of being unseen, the dignity of suffering, the paradox of joy and sadness coexisting. Even the idea of redemption, not through ideology or punishment, but through human connection, is here in its earliest, gentlest form. The form of the story, first-person narration, framed as a confession or letter to the reader, also anticipates Dostoyevsky's later narrative techniques. The reader becomes a witness, not just to

events, but to emotion. We are not told what to think or feel. We are invited to listen, to empathize, to imagine ourselves in the dreamer's shoes. White Nights also has a universal quality that transcends time and culture. Anyone who has ever felt invisible, who has longed for connection, who has experienced a brief, unforgettable love, will find themselves in these pages. It is a story about moments, about how the smallest encounter can echo through a lifetime,

about how the heart, even when broken, can remain soft. As you begin reading White Nights, let yourself be drawn into its quiet world. Feel the stillness of the city, the glow of the sky, the hesitations and confessions of the characters. Let the story move slowly. It is not meant to shock or thrill. It is meant to make you feel gently, honestly, deeply. Welcome to White Nights, a tale for the

lonely, the hopeful, the dreamers. A soft whisper in a sleepless night, a reminder that even the briefest connection can light up the soul.

## First Night

It was a beautiful night, the kind of night that only feels possible when we are young, dear reader. The sky was full of stars, so bright and endless that, as I looked at it, I couldn't help but wonder how grumpy or moody people could live under such a sky. That's a very youthful thought too, dear reader, so youthful. But may God grant that such thoughts enter your heart more often. Speaking of grumpy and moody people, I can't

help but think about how I felt all day. From the moment I woke up a strange sadness weighed on me. I couldn't shake the feeling that I was completely alone, that everyone was leaving me behind. Of course, you might ask, who is this everyone you're talking about? After all, I had lived in Petersburg for almost eight years and barely knew anyone. But what did I need acquaintances for? I already felt like I knew all of

Petersburg. That's why, when everyone in Petersburg seemed to pack their things and move to their summer homes, it felt like they were all leaving me. The thought of being left behind scared me, and for three whole days I wandered the city feeling down, unsure of what to do with myself. Whether I walked along Nevsky Prospekt, visited the gardens, or strolled by the river bank, I didn't see a single familiar face.

Those people didn't know me, of course, but I knew them. I knew them so well that it felt like I had studied their faces. Their happiness made me happy, and their gloom brought me down. I even felt as though I had formed a sort of bond with one old man I saw every single day at the same time on Fontanka. He had a serious, thoughtful expression, and he always mumbled to himself while waving his left

hand. In his right hand he carried a long, knobby stick with a gold handle. He even seemed to notice me and care about me in his own way. If I wasn't at my usual spot on Fontanka, I was sure he felt disappointed. Over time we almost started nodding to each other in greeting, especially when we were both in good spirits. Just the other day, after missing each other for two days, we nearly tipped our hats

as we passed, but we caught ourselves just in time and simply exchanged curious glances. I know the buildings in Petersburg too. As I walk down the streets, it feels like the buildings lean forward, peeking out their windows to greet me. Some of them seem to say, Good morning. How are you? I'm doing well, thank you, and I'll be getting a new floor added in May. Others might say, I'm being repainted tomorrow, or I narrowly escaped a fire and was so

frightened. I even have favorites among the buildings. Some of them feel like old friends. One building plans to undergo some renovations by the architect this summer, and I'll make a point of visiting daily to ensure the work goes well. Heaven forbid anything goes wrong. But I'll never forget what happened with a small, charming house that used to be a lovely shade of pink. It was such a delightful little brick house.

It stood proudly among its plain neighbors, and seeing it always lifted my spirits. Then one day last week I passed by, and it seemed to cry out to me, They're painting me yellow! I was horrified. Those barbarians! They didn't spare a single detail, not the columns, not the cornices. My poor little friend was as yellow as a canary. It made me feel sick.

Even now I can't bring myself to visit that house, now ruined by its awful new color. So now you understand, dear reader, how I feel connected to all of Petersburg. I already mentioned that I had been feeling uneasy for three whole days before I finally realized what was bothering me. I felt restless on the streets. This person was gone. That one was gone. And what had happened to the other?

Even at home I didn't feel like myself. For two nights I racked my brain trying to figure out what was wrong in my little corner. Why was it so uncomfortable all of a sudden? In my confusion I carefully inspected the dingy green walls, the ceiling covered in cobwebs that Matrona had allowed to flourish, and all my furniture. I checked every chair, wondering if the issue was with them, because if even one chair

isn't in the same spot as the day before, I feel unsettled. I even studied the window, but none of it helped. Nothing felt better. I thought about calling Matrona to talk about the cobwebs and her general carelessness, but when I finally did, she just stared at me in silence, clearly baffled, and left without saying a word. The cobwebs, of course, are still hanging there, undisturbed.

It wasn't until this morning that I finally figured it out. Ah, everyone is leaving for their summer homes. Excuse the simplicity of my words, but I'm not in the mood for fancy language right now. Everything and everyone in Petersburg had either left or was preparing to leave for the holidays. Every respectable-looking gentleman I saw taking a cab seemed to transform in my mind into a devoted head of the household on his way to join his family at a summer

villa. Every passerby seemed to carry an air of transition, as if to say to the world, I'm only here for a little while longer, and soon I'll be off to the summer villa. If a window opened and a pair of delicate snow-white fingers tapped on the glass, before a pretty young woman leaned out to call to a flower-seller, I immediately imagined the flowers weren't for her stuffy apartment, but for the countryside, where she would soon be

moving. I became so caught up in my new obsession that I began to feel I could tell, just by looking at people, which summer villa they were heading to. Those going to Kameny or Aptokarsky Islands or the Peterhof Road stood out with their polished manners, stylish summer outfits, and elegant carriages. Those bound for Pargolovo or more distant places had a calm and dignified air. Meanwhile, people heading to Krestovsky Island were unmistakable with their carefree, lively energy.

If I happened to see a long line of carts carrying heaps of furniture—tables, chairs, sofas, and all sorts of household items—often with an old cook perched on top of it all, fiercely guarding her employer's belongings, or boats loaded with goods slowly gliding along the Neva or Fontanka on their way to the Black River or the Islands, it felt as though these scenes were everywhere. In my mind I multiplied these wagons and boats tenfold, a hundredfold. It seemed like the whole city was moving

in caravans to summer villas. It felt as if Petersburg was on the verge of becoming a ghost town. I started to feel embarrassed, frustrated, and deeply sad that I had no place to go for the holidays and no reason to leave the city. I wanted to join every wagon, ride off with every dignified gentleman in a cab, but no one, not a single person, invited me. It was as if they had all forgotten about me entirely, as if I didn't belong

to their world at all. I went on long walks, often so lost in thought that I forgot where I was until I suddenly found myself at the city gates. As soon as I realized where I was, I felt a wave of relief. I crossed the boundary and walked through fields and meadows. I didn't feel tired at all, only as though a heavy weight had been lifted from my heart.

Everyone I passed looked at me with such kind expressions that it felt like they were silently greeting me. They all seemed so cheerful, as if something wonderful had just happened, and nearly all of them were smoking cigars. Their happiness made me feel a joy I hadn't known before. It felt almost as if I had been transported to Italy. So powerful was the effect of nature on someone like me, a half-sick city dweller,

suffocating within the walls of the town. There is something incredibly moving about the nature around Petersburg. Especially in early spring, when it seems to use every ounce of strength it has. It bursts into bloom, covers itself in green leaves, and sprinkles itself with flowers, as if putting on its finest attire. It reminds me of a delicate, sickly girl, the kind you might look at with pity or tenderness, or perhaps not notice at all. Then, out of nowhere, she becomes breathtakingly beautiful,

almost as if by accident. Her sad, thoughtful eyes suddenly glow with fire, her pale cheeks flush with color, her features soften, and her face fills with passion and life. Her smile becomes radiant, her laughter sparkles, and her beauty overwhelms you. It's impossible not to wonder what caused this transformation. What gave her such vitality, such life? What brought that glow to her face and made her movements so full of grace?

But then the moment vanishes. The next day you might see her again, but she's back to the way she was before, quiet, pale, and distant, with a look of sorrow in her eyes. You can even sense regret or anguish in her expression, as if she's mourning the brief, fleeting moment of beauty she allowed herself. And you feel sad too, heartbroken that her beauty disappeared so quickly, that it came and went like a passing dream. You grieve because it felt like you didn't

even have enough time to fully appreciate it, to truly love her. And yet, as beautiful as that day was, the night that followed was even better. Here's what happened. I returned to the city very late, and it was already ten o'clock by the time I was heading back to my lodging. My path took me along the canal embankment, a place where, at that hour, you never see a single person. To be fair, I live in a very

isolated part of the city. I walked along, humming a song. When I'm happy, I always hum to myself, like anyone who's joyful but has no one to share that happiness with. Then, out of nowhere, something completely unexpected happened. Leaning against the canal railing stood a woman, her elbows resting on the rail, as she stared intently at the murky water below. She wore a lovely yellow hat and a stylish black shawl. She's young, and I bet she has dark

hair, I thought. She didn't seem to notice the sound of my footsteps or react in any way as I walked past her, my heart pounding loudly in my chest. How odd, I thought. She must be lost in her thoughts. Then, suddenly, I stopped in my tracks, frozen. I heard a soft, muffled sob. Yes, there was no mistaking it. The woman was crying. A moment later, the quiet sobs became louder,

one after another. My heart sank. Though I was usually nervous around women, this was different. It felt like an extraordinary moment. I turned back, took a step toward her, and was just about to say, Miss, but stopped myself, realizing how cliché it sounded, like something out of every Russian novel I'd ever read. That thought alone made me hesitate. As I fumbled for the right words, she

seemed to collect herself. She turned her head slightly, noticed me, started, and quickly looked down. Without a word, she moved past me and began walking along the embankment. Instinctively, I followed her. She must have realized it because she veered off the embankment, crossed the street, and continued along the pavement. I didn't dare cross after her, my heart beating wildly like a trapped bird. Then fate seemed to step in and offer

me a chance. On the same side of the pavement where she was walking, a man suddenly appeared. He was wearing evening clothes and looked to be middle-aged, though his unsteady gait and the way he leaned against the wall made him seem less than dignified. The woman quickened her pace, walking with the nervous urgency you see in women who want to avoid being approached at night. It seemed likely the staggering man wouldn't follow her, except, unfortunately, he did.

Without any warning, the man sprang forward and began chasing her at full speed. She ran like the wind, but he was catching up, getting closer and closer. She let out a sharp scream, and in that instant I silently thanked the sturdy walking stick I happened to be carrying in my right hand. In a heartbeat I crossed the street, and in another the drunken man seemed to understand the situation. He noticed my stick, recognized the unspoken thread

it carried, and immediately backed off without saying a word. Only when we were far down the road did I hear him muttering curses in our direction, but his voice was too faint to make out. Take my arm, I said to the woman. He won't bother us any more. She hesitated for only a moment before linking her trembling arm with mine. She was still shaking, overwhelmed by fear and excitement.

Oh, drunken stranger, how I silently thanked you in that moment! I glanced at her out of the corner of my eye and saw that she was even more beautiful than I had guessed, dark-haired, just as I'd imagined. Her long, dark lashes were still wet with tears, though I couldn't tell if they were from the terror she had just experienced or from whatever had made her cry earlier. Yet a faint smile was beginning to form on her lips.

She, too, stole a quick glance at me, her cheeks turning a soft pink before she lowered her gaze again. You see, I said gently, why did you push me away earlier? If I'd been there, this never would have happened. But I didn't know you, she replied quietly. I thought you might have been like him. And now? Do you think you know me better? Maybe a little, she said, still looking down.

But why are you shaking? Oh, you've guessed it right away, I replied, thrilled that this girl was so clever. Intelligence always goes so well with beauty. Yes, from the very first look you figured me out completely. You're right, I'm shy around women. I get nervous just like you did a moment ago when that man scared you. To be honest, I'm a little nervous now. This feels like a dream. And I never imagined, even in my wildest

dreams, that I'd ever have a real conversation with a woman. What? Really? Yes, really. If my arm is trembling, it's because it has never been held by a small, lovely hand like yours before. I've never had much to do with women. You see, I'm always alone. I don't even know how to talk to them properly.

In fact, I'm not sure if I've already said something foolish to you. Be honest with me. Please tell me if I have. I promise I won't be offended. No, no, not at all. Quite the opposite. And if you really want me to be honest, I'll tell you that women actually like that kind of nervousness. And to be even more honest, I like it too.

I won't send you away until I've made it home safely. You're going to make me lose all my shyness, I said, almost breathless with happiness. And once that happens, all my chances will be gone. Chances? What chances? That's not a very nice thing to say. I'm sorry it just slipped out, but how can anyone not want to make a good impression at a moment like this?

You mean to be liked, right? Yes, exactly. But please be kind to me. Think about who I am. I'm twenty-six years old, and I've never known anyone like you. How can I possibly say the right things or be smooth and confident? Maybe you'll understand better if I just tell you everything. I don't know how to keep quiet when my heart is speaking, but never mind all

that. Believe me, I've never been with a woman, not even once. No friendships, no relationships, nothing. Every day I just dream about finally meeting someone. Oh, if only you knew how many times I've fallen in love in my dreams. Really? With who? With no one in particular, with an ideal, with a person I imagine in my dreams.

I make up entire stories in my head. You don't know me at all. Of course, I've met a few women before, but what kind of women were they? They were all landladies, people like that, but I'm sure you'll laugh if I tell you this. I've even thought about speaking to some elegant woman on the street a few times. Of course, only if she was alone. I imagined myself approaching her timidly and respectfully, explaining how lonely I am, begging her not

to turn me away. I'd tell her that I don't have any opportunities to meet women, and that it's almost a moral duty for her not to reject such a desperate request. I'd ask her to say just a few kind words to me, as if she were my sister. I wouldn't ask for much, just two or three words of sympathy. She could laugh at me if she wanted to, but I'd hope she wouldn't reject me

I'm only laughing at how you're your own worst enemy. If you had tried, you might have succeeded, even if it had been on the street. Simplicity is best. No kind-hearted woman, unless she were either

I was judging by myself. I know quite a bit about other people's lives. Oh, thank you, I exclaimed. You don't know what you've just done for me? I'm glad, I'm so glad.

But tell me, how did you know I was the kind of woman who—well, someone you'd think worthy—of your attention and friendship? Not just a landlady, as you say? What made you decide to approach me? What made me? Well, you were alone. That man was being far too rude. And it was night-time. You have to admit, it felt like my duty. No, no, I mean before, when you were

## Second Night

Well, you've made it through, she said, holding both my hands tightly. I've been here for the past two hours. You have no idea what a state I've been in all day. I know, I know. But let's get down to business. Do you know why I came? Not to talk nonsense like yesterday. Here's the thing. We need to act more sensibly from now on.

I thought a lot about this last night. In what way? What do you mean by more sensibly? I'm ready to do my part, but honestly, nothing has ever seemed more sensible to me than this. What's happening right now? Really? Well, first of all, please don't squeeze my hand so hard. Secondly, I have to tell you, I spent a lot of time thinking about you today,

and I wasn't sure what to make of it. And? How did it end? How did it end? Well, I realized that we have to start fresh, because after thinking it over, I concluded that I don't know you at all.
