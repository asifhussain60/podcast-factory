## The First Emanation: Origination, the First Intellect, and the Sabiq

### The Bewilderment of the Intellect and the Three Powers of the Created Essences

Before the discourse can move forward into the unfolding of the First Emanation, it must first settle a matter concerning the very faculty by which all of this is to be apprehended. There is a saying, well known among the people who reflect upon these realities — and Iqbal too gives voice to it — that the limit of the intellect (`aql`) is bewilderment (`hayrat`). One must ask carefully what this means. Is bewilderment the limit of one particular essence among the created essences, or is it the limit of all the powers that were bestowed upon them together?

To answer this, it is necessary to recall what the created essences were given. Allah, exalted is He, created the intellects (`uquul`), and He created the essences (`dhawat`), and into each of these created essences He breathed life and granted three powers (`quwwat`): the power of life (`hayat`), the power of knowledge (`ilm`), and the power of volition or free choice (`ikhtiyar`). These three are the substances or essential jewels — the `jawahir` — that constitute the inner constitution of every created being in this luminous order. They are not three separate things stacked beside one another, but three faculties bound up together, and the intellect is the crown that gathers them. When, therefore, it is said that the limit of the intellect is bewilderment, the meaning extends to all three: the limit of the three `jawahir` taken together, the limit of life and knowledge and choice as they cohere in the intellect, is `hayrat`.

And what is this bewilderment? It is the astonishment of the intellect concerning its own God. The intellect knows with perfect certainty that Allah created it; this it cannot doubt, for its very existence testifies to its Originator. Yet, for all its knowledge and all its power, it cannot attain the gnosis (`ma'rifat`) of that God. It stands before the reality of the One who made it and is overwhelmed, unable to encompass Him, unable to comprehend His essence. This is no defect in the intellect; it is the truest mark of its perfection. The intellect that grows bewildered at the threshold of its Originator has understood the one thing it most needed to understand — that the Originator is unlike it, unreachable by the very faculties that the Originator Himself bestowed. The limit of `aql`, then, is precisely this: that it becomes astonished about its God, knowing that He created it, yet confessing that it cannot attain His gnosis. This bewilderment is where created knowing ends and adoration begins.

### The Two First Emanations and the Meaning of Munba'ith

With this foundation laid, the discourse turns to what proceeded from the noble First Intellect (`al-'aql al-sharif`). The text of the third chapter, from the second section of the First Pavilion (`Suradaq al-Awwal`), reads:

`Summa innahu abiha lima abiha hadha al-aql al-sharif lahu ithnan min tilka al-ashbah al-suwariyyah, wa huma al-mu'arrab 'anhuma bil-munba'ithayn al-awwalayn. Sabaqa ahaduhuma al-akhar. Fa-nazara bi-dhatihi ila dhatihi wa ila abna'i jinsihi kama nazara al-awwal. Fa-'alima anna lahu wa lahum mubdi'an.`

The verb `abiha` here is the key that opens the line. Its meaning is `'alima` — he knew: `abiha ya-abahu, ay 'alima bihi`. So the sense of the opening is: "Then, indeed, when this noble intellect knew." When the First Intellect — the originated First Intellect, the `aql-e mubda'-e awwal` — sought to recognize its God, two of those formal luminous phantoms (`al-ashbah al-suwariyyah`) came to the knowing of Him. Two among the luminous forms moved forward to know, and these two are the ones referred to, in the technical language of this science, as the two First Emanations: `al-munba'ithayn al-awwalayn`.

A word must be said about why they are named `munba'ith`. The root carries the original letter `alif`, and from this `alif`, when the word is unfolded in pronunciation, there emerge two further letters — `lam` and `fa`. The `alif` is written and read as one, yet when it is sounded out it yields `alif`, `lam`, `fa`. This unfolding of the one written letter into the spoken letters that emerge from it is the very image of emanation, and so these two who emerged from the noble intellect — set, as it were, opposite the originating `alif` — are called `munba'ithayn`, the two who emanated. They are further qualified as `al-awwalayn`, the first two, because they were the first to emerge, the first to move forward into the knowing of the Originator by seeing.

It is essential to grasp how their advancing differed from the advancing of the First Intellect itself. These two `munba'ith`s did not initiate; they moved forward by seeing. The First Intellect was near them, and they beheld it — they saw its noble gaze upon the Originator — and by that seeing they were carried forward, whereas others, more distant, could not see and so did not advance in this manner. This was possible because that realm is the realm of free choice (`ikhtiyar`), and in it there is no obstacle. As the text affirms, `la 'a'iqa fi dhalika al-tiraz al-'aali` — there is no impediment in that high plane. In that luminous order there was no barrier of time or of space; each could behold the gaze of the other without hindrance. So when the First Intellect first turned its gaze upon the Exalted Originator (`al-mabda' ta'ala`), the two luminous forms beheld that noble gaze of the First Intellect, whose form and state (`hay'at`) had by then already been established. And these two who beheld it were the ones defined by the name of the two First Emanations.

`Sabaqa ahaduhuma al-akhar` — one of them preceded the other. This precedence must be understood with great care, for it does not contradict the simultaneity of their emergence. The two emerged together, at the same instant; the word `al-awwalayn` itself testifies that they appeared as a pair. And yet, though they emerged together, one of them advanced ahead of the other. The precedence (`sabaqiyat`) was real, but it was a precedence in their own order, not a precedence of one emerging before the other in time. Both came forth at once; the precedence lay elsewhere — and where it lay will become the central question of all that follows.

### How the First Emanation Knew, Negated, and Glorified

The line continues: `Fa-nazara bi-dhatihi ila dhatihi wa ila abna'i jinsihi kama nazara al-awwal. Fa-'alima anna lahu wa lahum mubdi'an bi-khilafihim. Fa-nafa 'an dhatihi wa 'anhum al-ilahiyyah, wa athbataha lahu ta'ala.`

Here the First Emanation looked with his own essence (`bi-dhatihi`) toward his own essence (`ila dhatihi`) and toward the members of his kind (`abna'i jinsihi`), exactly in the manner that the First had looked. He did what the First Intellect had done, in the very same way — only by seeing. And looking thus, he knew that for himself and for all the members of his kind there is an Originator, a Creator, who is unlike them (`bi-khilafihim`). The grammar is precise here, and it matters: the verb is `'alima`, singular — one knew. Of the two `munba'ith`s, it was the First Emanation alone who attained this knowing in its fullness, who knew that for himself and for all his kind there is an Originator, a Creator, utterly unlike them. The same words that had been used of the First Intellect are now used of him.

And so, in the pattern already established by the First Intellect, the First Emanation negated divinity (`ilahiyyat`) from his own essence and from all the members of his kind — `fa-nafa 'an dhatihi wa 'anhum al-ilahiyyah` — and affirmed that divinity for Allah alone, Mighty and Majestic, for the Exalted Originator: `wa athbataha lahu ta'ala`. We should be clear about what `ilahiyyat` denotes here. It is the power to create, the divinity that belongs to the existence of the true Essence. To negate divinity from oneself is to confess that one does not possess the power to create, that one is created and not creator; to affirm it for Allah is to confess that this creative power belongs to Him alone.

The text proceeds: `Wa sabbaha al-aql al-awwal wa qaddasahu wa tanzih wa tajrid, idh kana sabiqan 'alayhi il-tawhid al-mubdi'i wa tanzih wa tajrid.` Having negated divinity from himself and affirmed it for his Originator, the First Emanation then glorified the First Intellect (`sabbaha al-aql al-awwal`) and sanctified it (`qaddasahu`) with transcendence and abstraction, because the First Intellect had preceded him in the affirmation of the unity of the Originator (`tawhid`), in declaring His incomparability (`tanzih`), and in abstracting Him from all likeness (`tajrid`). And then: `Wa i'tarafa bi-sabqihi wa tawlihi wa sharafihi 'ala abna'i jinsihi wa fadlihi` — he acknowledged the precedence of the First Intellect, its bounty, its nobility over the members of his kind, and its virtue.

This acknowledgment is the hinge upon which the whole destiny of the First Emanation turns. Because he glorified and sanctified the First Intellect, because he confessed its precedence and virtue, the aids and blessings of the First Intellect reached him: `Fa-wasalat-hu mawadduhu wa barakatuhu`. From the side of the First Intellect there flowed to him substances and aids (`mawad`), blessings (`barakat`), confirmations (`ta'yidat`), and glances (`lahazat`). And more than this: `wa-ittasala bihi 'inda dhalika nurun ilahi` — at that moment he was connected with the First Intellect by a divine light. Through this connection there came to him `al-nur al-jari wa al-jud al-sari` — the flowing light and the pervading generosity. For from the sacred formula `La ilaha illa Allah` two things flow: a divine light (`nur-e ilahi`) and a flowing knowledge (`ilm-e jari`). This is itself a link of the `kalimah`: just as the First Intellect receives from the Exalted Originator, so this Second received, by his connection, from the First Intellect.

### The Second Perfection and the Single Difference of Rank

The text completes the thought: `wa-ittasala bihi 'inda dhalika nurun ilahi wa al-'ilm al-ghayr al-mutanahi. wa balagha biha al-kamal al-thani wa sara 'aqlan azaliyyan tamman kamilan ma'suman fadilan, la farqa baynahu wa bayna al-awwal illa bi-rutbat al-sabq.` By this connection there reached him a divine light and an infinite knowledge — a knowledge that has no end. And through it he attained the Second Perfection (`al-kamal al-thani`), and he became an intellect that is eternal (`azali`), perfect (`tamm`), complete (`kamil`), infallible (`ma'sum`), and excellent (`fadil`). And between him and the First Intellect there is no difference whatsoever — `la farqa baynahu wa bayna al-awwal` — except for one thing alone: the rank of precedence, `rutbat al-sabq`.

This is a teaching of the highest consequence and must be allowed its full weight. In the Second Perfection, the First Intellect and the First Emanation are, as it were, brothers seated upon thrones facing one another. Everything between them has been leveled; every distinction has been dissolved. What remains? Only `sabaqiyat` — precedence. The one who came forth with a delay came later; that is the whole of the difference. And this precedence will remain forever; it does not pass away. The void of the Second Perfection has dissolved all else, but the rank of precedence is permanent.

From this there follows a further teaching of great import concerning the fixedness of ranks in the luminous realm. In the Second Perfection no being can surpass another, and no circle (`da'irah`) can surpass another circle. The ranks are locked; the pen has been lifted. The seventh intellect can never overtake the sixth intellect under any condition; the sixth advances and the seventh remains behind it. The seventh cannot leap to the position of the fifth, leaving the sixth behind; they are ranged one after another in fixed order, and a lock has been set upon that order. This must be contrasted sharply with our own present condition. In this world — the state of the First Perfection (`kamal-e awwal`) — there does exist a free choice (`ikhtiyar`) by which one may surpass one's benefactor or teacher (`mufid`). So long as a being is not yet in the luminous realm, in the condition of `munba'ith` and in the other cycles (`karrat`), he possesses the free choice of planning and management (`ikhtiyar-e tadbir`): if he plans rightly, he can surpass the very one from whom he benefited. But not there. In the Second Perfection there is no such choice. The First Perfection is the state in which the Exalted Originator created everyone equal, and where the contest of advancement remains open; the Second Perfection is the state in which the ranks have been finally assigned and sealed.

### Ibda' and Inbi'ath: The Witness of Sayyidna Hamiduddin

The text now names, with precision, the nature of the single difference that separates the First Intellect from the First Emanation: `La farqa baynahu wa bayna al-awwal illa bi-rutbat al-sabaqiyat, wa kawnahu inbi'athan wa al-awwal ibda'an, wa al-ibda'u la ka-al-inbi'ath.` There is no difference between him and the First except in the rank of precedence — and in this, that he is an emanation (`inbi'ath`) while the First is an origination (`ibda'`), and origination is not like emanation.

This is the doctrinal core of the whole section. The First Intellect is `ibda'`: one who advances on his own, from his own essence, without seeing another before him. The First Emanation is `inbi'ath`: one who advances by seeing another. This is the only difference, but it is an irreducible one. To advance on one's own is one state; to advance by seeing someone else is another state altogether, and the two cannot be collapsed into one. We must guard here against a misunderstanding that might arise from the doctrine of the fallen tenth. Even though the First Emanation, after his fall and recovery, will be counted among those who attain the Second Perfection, and even though the fallen one who recovers takes his place in the order — the one who is the `ashir`, the tenth, when his contemplation is complete, finds his rank fixed as the tenth — none of this dissolves the difference between origination and emanation. The First Intellect remains `ibda'`, and the First Emanation remains `inbi'ath`.

This is the express teaching of Sayyidna Hamiduddin, may Allah elevate his sanctity, in the book `Rahat al-'Aql`: `Kama qala Sayyidna Hamiduddin a'la Allahu qudsahu fi Rahat al-Aql.` His words are: `Wa law kanat kayfiyyat al-ibda'i ka-kayfiyyat al-inbi'athi, la-kana al-ibda'u inbi'athan wa al-inbi'athu ibda'an` — and if the modality of origination were like the modality of emanation, then origination would be emanation and emanation would be origination. The conditional is offered precisely to expose its impossibility. If the manner of `ibda'` were identical to the manner of `inbi'ath`, there would be no difference between the two, and the one who advanced on his own and the one who advanced by seeing would be indistinguishable. But this is an impossible matter. The one who advanced on his own — his modality cannot be matched by any other; and the one who advanced by seeing him will of necessity stand after him. The First Intellect is therefore ahead in every way, without question. It is the locus of unity (`mazhar-e wahdat`); it is the circle of the very beginning (`da'irah of ibtida'`).

### The Delusion of Equality and the Two Gazes

It is here that the discourse first sounds a theme that will return with full force in the account of the Second Emanation, but which is anticipated already in the contrast between the two First Emanations. The greatest crime that was established against the Second Intellect — against the one who is the `ashir`, the Tenth — was the delusion of equality (`wahm-e masawat`). For he said, in effect, "We two are one." Because both emanated together, he supposed that there could be no precedence between them, and from his words one might be tempted to conclude that they did not emanate together at all. But this would be an error. Nowhere does the text say that they emanated at separate moments; on the contrary, `al-munba'ithayn al-awwalayn` declares that both emerged at the same time. `Sabaqa ahaduhuma al-akhar` does not place their emergence at different instants — the precedence stands in its own place, distinct from the moment of emergence. Both came forth together.

So we must ask: if both emerged together, both recognized the First Intellect, both negated divinity from themselves and affirmed it for the Originator, and both did this side by side — wherein lies the precedence that one of them refused to acknowledge? The answer was given earlier and is now made the foundation of everything: the gaze of the one was the nobler gaze (`nazar-e ashraf`), and the gaze of the other was the lower gaze (`nazar-e adna`). Before the emanation of the two, two gazes of the First Intellect had already been established. The nobler gaze, the `nazar-e ashraf`, arose from the First Intellect's affirmation of unity, transcendence, and abstraction (`tawhid`, `tanzih`, `tajrid`), and from the luminous joy that this affirmation brought to it. The lower gaze, the `nazar-e adna`, arose differently: when the First Intellect saw that in the entire cosmos no one had advanced ahead of it — that it was the first to see, the first to gaze, the first to affirm the unity and transcendence of God — it attained a joy at its own firstness (`awwaliyyat`). This joy of being first was the lower gaze.

Now the difference between the two First Emanations is exactly this: the gaze of the First Emanation was directed toward the nobler gaze, the `nazar-e ashraf`; the gaze of the Second was directed toward the lower gaze, the `nazar-e adna`. The Second Emanation, however, reasoned thus: "Since we two advanced together, our gazes must be equal." This supposition is the delusion of equality, the `tawahhum-e masawat`. And here lies the gravity of the crime: he understood that there was a difference of gaze. He did perceive the distinction between the noble and the lower vision. And yet, having perceived it, he still claimed that they were equal. It was not ignorance of the difference that condemned him, but the refusal to honor a difference he had seen. This delusion of equality is what halted his rank.

### The Names of the First Intellect and the Specificity of Ibda'

Having established the nature of the First Intellect as `ibda'`, the text now unfolds the names that belong to it by right, and the reason each name belongs. It begins with the act from which all the names flow: `Li-anna al-aql al-awwal hajama bi-dhatihi 'ala tawhid mubdi'ihi wa tanzihhi wa tasbihihi wa taqdisihi, mubtadi'an dhalika min dhatihi li-dhatihi bi-dhatihi.` Because the First Intellect rushed, with its own essence, upon the affirmation of the unity of its Originator, upon His transcendence, His glorification, and His sanctification — initiating that from its essence, for its essence, with its essence. This threefold formula — `min dhatihi li-dhatihi bi-dhatihi`, from its essence, for its essence, with its essence — captures the wholly self-originating character of the act. The First Intellect rushed to the affirmation by its own self, with no instruction, no model, no predecessor.

The text continues: `min ghayri awwalin taqaddama 'alayhi, wa la sabiqin sabaqahu ila dhalika al-fi'l. alladhi bihi sama 'ala abna'i jinsihi wa 'ala 'alihim. wa-istahaqqa jami' al-asma' al-sharifah allati lam takun li-ghayrihi.` Without any first one who had advanced before it, and without any predecessor who had preceded it to that act — the act by which it was raised above the members of its kind and above them all — it deserved all the noble names that belong to no one other than it, `illa hu`, except for it. Because it alone performed an act that no other performed, no prior, no predecessor, it alone became deserving of all these noble names.

The first of these names is `al-ibda'` and `al-mubda' al-awwal`. The First Intellect is itself called `ibda'` — origination — and `al-mubda' al-awwal`, the first originated. Then the names cascade, each given with the same structure of the abstract and its first concrete bearer: `al-wujud wa al-mawjud al-awwal` — existence and the first existent; `al-haqiqah wa al-haqq al-awwal` — the reality and the first truth; `al-wahdah wa al-wahid al-awwal` — the unity and the first one; `al-tamam wa al-tamm al-awwal` — perfection and the first perfect; `al-kamal wa al-kamil al-awwal` — completeness and the first complete; `al-azal wa al-azali al-awwal` — eternity and the first eternal; `al-aql wa al-'aqil al-awwal` — intellect and the first intellective; `al-ilm wa al-'alim al-awwal` — knowledge and the first knower; `al-qudrah wa al-qadir al-awwal` — power and the first powerful; `al-hayat wa al-hayy al-awwal` — life and the first living; `al-sabq wa al-sabiq al-awwal` — precedence and the first predecessor; `al-'ismah wa al-mu'tasim al-awwal` — infallibility and the first infallible; and `mudahhir al-duhur wa al-dahr al-awwal` — the one who rolls out the ages, and the first age. Each name attaches to the First Intellect as the first of its kind. This is the one whom we address in our prayers.

There is, however, a crucial distinction to be drawn within this list, and the text draws it deliberately. The fourth chapter, from the second section of the First Pavilion, states: `Wa innama waqa'at 'alayhi hadhihi al-asma' al-sharifah li-ma'anin qama biha wahdahu, wa-khtassa biha duna ghayrihi siwa ism al-mubda'. Fa-qad jama'ahu dhalika huwa wa abna'u jinsihi.` These noble names fell upon the First Intellect for meanings that it established alone — meanings to which it gave subsistence by its own essence, performing the establishment with its own essence — and it was specified with these names apart from all others. Save for one exception: the name `al-mubda'`. For the name `mubda'` gathered together both the First Intellect and the members of his kind.

The reason is then given: `Wa dhalika annahu waqa'a 'alayhi ism al-mubda' bi-hal wujudihi al-awwal huwa wa abna'u jinsihi 'an mubdi'ihim ta'ala, fa-'ammahum jami'an hadha al-ism.` The name `al-mubda'` — the originated — fell upon the First Intellect in the state of his first existence, he and the members of his kind alike, from their Exalted Originator. The word `mubda'` simply means "the one who has been created," and so it embraces every created thing; the entire cosmos is gathered under it, with no distinction between the First Perfection and the Second — everyone is included. So this single name became applicable to all of them together.

But `al-ibda'` is otherwise: `wa amma ism al-ibda' fa-khtassa bihi, lamma hajama 'alayhi wa-btada'ahu min tawhid mubdi'ihi wa fi'lihi alladhi fa'alahu wa qama bihi duna abna'i jinsihi.` As for the name `ibda'`, the First Intellect was specified with it alone, because it was he who rushed upon it and initiated it — from the affirmation of the unity of his Originator, and from that act which he performed and to which he gave subsistence, apart from all the members of his kind. The distinction may be illustrated by the way certain men are described among us. A man may possess piety (`taqwa`), but to call him `muttaqi` is one thing; to say of him that he is `taqwa-e mujassam`, piety embodied, is another — it names a man so pious that piety itself has taken form in him. So too with justice: a man may be just, but the one of whom we say `adl-e mujassam`, embodied justice, is the one in whom justice has become incarnate. In just this way, the name `ibda'` rests upon the First Intellect: while `mubda'` applies to everyone, `ibda'` belongs to the First Intellect alone, for he is the one from whom the spiritual creation (`khalq-e ruhani`) came forth. From him it happened; for this reason he, and he alone, is called `ibda'`.

### Unfolding the Names: From Mawjud al-Awwal to Hayy al-Awwal

The text then takes up the names one by one, supplying for each the reason of its application. `Wa ism al-mawjud bi-hal wujudihi huwa wa abna'u jinsihi min al-'adam` — the name of the existent attaches to him and to the members of his kind in the state of their coming into existence from non-existence (`'adam`); existence in this general sense belongs to all of them. But `Wa ism al-mawjud al-awwal li-kawnihi awwal mawjudin fi kamal al-thani` — the name of the first existent is specific to him, because he was the first to enter the Second Perfection. Existence belongs to all; "first existent" belongs to him alone.

`Wa ism al-haqiqah li-anna man intaha ila ma'rifatihi fa-qad balagha haqiqat al-ashya' al-mawjudah.` The name of reality (`haqiqah`) belongs to him because whoever attains his gnosis has thereby reached the reality of all existing things; he is, as it were, total knowledge (`ilm-e kulli`), so that to know him is to reach the truth of everything that exists. `Wa ism al-'alim al-awwal li-annahu awwal man ittasala bihi al-'ilm al-jari min Allah ta'ala.` The name of the first knower belongs to him because he was the first to be connected with the flowing knowledge (`ilm-e jari`) from Allah, exalted is He — the flowing knowledge and the pervading light (`nur-e sari`).

`Wa ism al-qudrah li-anna bi-muwalatihi wa tasbihihi wa taqdisihi iqtadara talihi 'ala bulugh ma balaghahu min al-kamal al-thani.` The name of power (`qudrah`) belongs to him because, by his loyalty (`muwalat`), his glorification (`tasbih`), and his sanctification (`taqdis`), his follower was empowered to attain that very Second Perfection which the First Intellect had attained. The subject of the empowering is the follower: it is through the First Intellect's loyalty, glorification, and sanctification — that is, through the relation the First Intellect sustains — that the one who follows is enabled to reach what the First reached. This implies that the total intellect, the total power, the total knowledge, are all gathered up in the First Intellect; it is from his treasures that every other receives.

This raises a question of the deepest importance, and the discourse pauses to answer it. If everyone receives from his treasures, may the First Intellect himself be expanding still? The answer is that he must be. The secrets and bounties of the Exalted Originator are being attained by him without cease. One who has put his hand into the treasure-chest of a king of kings — the bounty that flows from such a treasure is no small or finished thing. As is intimated in the manner of Surah Yasin, the First Intellect is forever running in the gnosis (`irfan`) of the `kalimah`; in its splendor, in its greatness, there can always be an increase that we cannot estimate. This means that the First Intellect is in continuous evolution. Allah's treasures and bounties have no limit; they come one after another, and just when one might say, "So many bounties have come, surely now they are exhausted," they are not exhausted — more can come, for there is no limit to the bounties. To say that he has received everything would be to set a limit upon God's power, to bound the bounties of the Originator — which cannot be. And so for the First Intellect, falling is not an option; only rising lies ahead. Only rising. And how much he will rise belongs to the knowledge of the unseen (`'ilm al-ghayb`); it cannot be estimated, for the bounties of Allah are beyond all reckoning. What the text records is that he attained his perfection in that state, at that moment; but the splendor and the perfection only increase, forever upward, never downward.

`Wa ism al-qadir al-awwal li-annahu awwal muqtadirin 'ala fi'lihi.` The name of the first powerful one belongs to him because he was the first to have power over the act that he performed, and by that he became powerful over whatever he willed. `Wa ism al-hayat li-kawnihim hayatan li-man dunahum min al-'awalim.` The name of life belongs to him because he is life for those worlds that are below him; the entire living, knowing, powerful spiritual realm — the `alam-e hayy, qadir, ruhani` — derives its life from his bestowing. The very faculties that were given in the First Perfection — `ilm`, `hayat`, `qudrah` — are now applied to him in their first and fullest sense.

`Wa ism al-hayy al-awwal li-kawnihim awwal man ittasala bihi ruh al-hayat al-haqiqiyyah, fa-ahyahu bil-hayat al-abadiyyah al-sarmadiyyah.` The name of the first living one belongs to him because he was the first to be connected with the spirit of true life (`ruh al-hayat al-haqiqiyyah`), so that this spirit gave him life — an everlasting, perpetual life (`al-hayat al-abadiyyah al-sarmadiyyah`). And this connects directly with the divine word. In `Allahu la ilaha illa Huwa al-Hayy al-Qayyum`, the pronoun `Huwa` refers to the Originator (`al-mabda'`); `al-Hayy` is the First Intellect, the station of unity (`wahdat`); and `al-Qayyum` is the First Emanation (`munba'ith al-awwal`). And with `al-Qayyum`, the First Emanation, are gathered all the Second Perfections that are yet to be attained in the future — all of them are with him — while with `al-Hayy` is the station and rank of unity itself.

A subtle objection might be raised: if all these attributes belong to the First Intellect only because he was the first to attain the Second Perfection, and if all who follow attain it too, should not these names apply to the followers as well? The resolution is that the life the followers truly received, they received because of the First Intellect. Had the First Intellect not acted in that realm, what would have come to pass cannot even be said. The matter is like the relation of the Prophet Muhammad to the Imams of his community: many Imams, in their own right, attained whatever ranks they attained, yet because they are gathered into his community by their seeing of the Prophet, the Prophet's station remains unique and unshared. So with the First Intellect: though others attain the Second Perfection, they attain it through him, and the names of firstness remain his alone.

### Al-Sabiq, Al-Mu'tasim, and Mudahhir al-Duhur

`Wa ism al-sabq li-annahu nihayatan la yablughuha al-sabiqun wa la yantahi ilayha al-mu'ajjifun.` The name of precedence belongs to him because he is a limit that no predecessor reaches and at which no hastener arrives — not even those who run fast can attain it. `Wa ism al-sabiq al-awwal li-sabqihi abna'a jinsihi ila ma sabaqa ilayhi min tawhid mubdi'ihi wa tanzihihi.` And the name of the first predecessor belongs to him because of his precedence over the members of his kind to that which he preceded toward — the affirmation of the unity of his Originator and the declaration of His incomparability.

`Wa ism al-'ismah li-anna man iltazama bihi wa ata'ahu wa 'arafahu ya'tasimu min al-khata'i wa al-zalal.` The name of infallibility (`'ismah`) belongs to him because whoever adheres to him, obeys him, and knows him — knows the First Intellect — is made pure from error and from slipping. `Wa ism al-mu'tasim al-awwal` — and the name of the first protected one — belongs to him because he was the first to be protected from error and slipping. And by what was he protected? `bi-hablillah al-jari wa nurihi al-sari` — by the flowing rope of God (`hablillah`) and His pervading light. He was protected by the `kalimah`, by the light of `La ilaha illa Allah`; everything is contained in it.

`Wa ism mudahhir al-duhur ay mufa''il al-fa'ilin, wa muwahhid al-muwahhidin, wa mu'azzil al-mu'azzilin.` He bears the name of the one who rolls out the ages (`mudahhir al-duhur`) — that is, the one who enables the doers (`mufa''il al-fa'ilin`): the one who makes those who perform the affirmation of unity able to perform it, who becomes the cause that brings them toward `tawhid`. He is `muwahhid al-muwahhidin`, the one who teaches the affirmation of unity to those who affirm it; and `mu'azzil al-mu'azzilin`, the one who teaches eternity to those who attain eternity, granting them their endurance.

The reason for these names is given: `li-anna al-duhur hum al-hudud al-'alawiyyah, wa huwa al-mumiddu lahum bil-nur al-ilahi, wa huwa al-muwahhid wa al-mu'azzil, wa hum al-muwahhidun wa al-mu'azzilun bima yuwasilun min barakatihi wa yashmuluhum min sawari al-lahazati, fa-waqa'a 'alayhim ism al-duhur.` Because the "ages" (`duhur`) are in reality the higher boundaries — the higher `hudud` (`al-hudud al-'alawiyyah`) — and he is the one who aids them with the divine light. He is the unifier and the eternalizer, and they are the unifiers and eternalizers by virtue of what they receive of his blessings (`barakat`) and what reaches them of his flowing glances (`lahazat`) — the flowing of his bounties. So the name "ages" fell upon them; these higher `hudud` are called `duhur`, and he is the one who rolls them out, `mudahhir al-duhur`. Were he not there, none would perform the affirmation of unity or the declaration of incomparability. This is set against the physical order: `Kama waqa'a 'ala al-hudud al-jismaniyyah ism al-a'wam wa al-sinin` — just as the name of years and eras (`a'wam` and `sinin`) fell upon the bodily `hudud`. The higher `hudud` are named "ages"; the physical `hudud` are named "years and eras."

Finally: `Wa ism al-dahr al-awwal li-annahu alladhi dahara dhatuhu, ay azzalaha, wa awjadaha 'ala al-wujud al-haqiqi, wa abqaha wa ayyadaha bi-fi'lihi alladhi fa'alahu.` The name of the first age belongs to him because he is the one who made his own essence into an age — that is, he rendered it eternal (`azzalaha`), brought it into true existence (`awjadaha 'ala al-wujud al-haqiqi`), made it endure, and sustained it by the act that he performed. All that he receives, he receives by virtue of his own essence and of that light of the `kalimah`. The Exalted Originator brought him forth, but the act was his own; he exercised his own free choice, and by seeing him all the others were made. Had he not been made, none could have been made; by seeing him, all were made. And so, in that luminous realm, the praise that is offered is all praise of the First Intellect; there is no mention of `Hu`, the Originator, in that praising, for all are occupied with benefiting from him — `mashghul bi-l-istifadah`. The one by seeing whom all were made stands at the center of that whole order of praise.

### The Quality of the First Emanation: Contemplation and Imamate

The fifth chapter, from the second section of the First Pavilion, turns to the First Emanation in himself, opening: `Fa-amma al-inbi'ath al-awwal, fa-kawnuhi kayfiyyatin ghayr kayfiyat al-ibda', fa-innahu lamma nazara ila al-'aql al-awwal, wa ma sara 'alayhi min al-'azmah wa al-'ula, wa al-jalal wa al-thana.` As for the First Emanation, his state was a quality other than the quality of origination. For when he looked toward the First Intellect and beheld what had come upon it of greatness (`'azmah`) and loftiness (`'ula`), of majesty (`jalal`) and praise (`thana`) — `wa khafiya fi dhatihi anna dhalika bi-ma sabaqa ilayhi min al-fi'l, wa ikhtassa bihi min tawhid al-mubdi'i ta'ala wa tanzihuhu wa tasbihuhu wa taqdisuhu, wa inba'atha dhalika fi dhatihi` — there was hidden within his essence the realization that all of this had come to the First Intellect because of the act that it had preceded toward, and because it had been specified with the affirmation of the unity of the Exalted Originator, His transcendence, His glorification, and His sanctification; and then that very realization emanated within his own essence.

This is the precise mechanism of the First Emanation's advance, and it must be distinguished carefully from origination. The First Emanation contemplated the First Intellect; he saw the greatness, loftiness, majesty, and praise that had come to it; and there arose in his mind the understanding that the First Intellect had received all this by virtue of its own preceding act and its affirmation of the unity, transcendence, glorification, and sanctification of the Originator. Then that contemplation — that understanding — emanated within his own essence. And so: `fa-qama bihi ka-qiyam al-awwal, mukhtafiyan lahu wa musta'imman bihi.` He stood with it, with that emanated realization, just as the First had stood — following him (`mukhtafiyan lahu`) and taking him as an Imam (`musta'imman bihi`). The First Emanation made the First Intellect his Imam.

The grammatical form here is illuminating. `Musta'imman` derives from the root `amma`, "to lead as an Imam," through the form `istif'al`, which carries the sense of seeking or desiring; just as `istifham` (from `fahima`, "he understood") means to seek understanding, so `ista'amma` means to seek to make someone an Imam. The First Emanation, then, sought to take the First Intellect as his Imam; the prefix `alif-sin` in Arabic carries this sense of seeking and desiring. He did, by seeing, what the First Intellect had done from itself.

### Why He Is Called the First Emanation: Ba'th and the Resurrection of the Dead

The text gives the reason for his name: `wa qila 'alayhi al-munba'ith al-awwal, li-ma inba'atha 'indahu ma ibtada'ahu al-awwal.` He was called the First Emanation because there emanated within him that which the First had originated (`ibtada'ahu`). What the First Intellect had originated, what it had first done from its own essence, that same thing emanated within him — with the single difference that he did it after seeing. As the text continues: `wa hajama 'alayhi min dhatihi, la min ghayrin sabaqahu ilayhi.` He rushed upon it from his own essence, not from any other that preceded him to it. It was not that the First Intellect instructed him or explained it to him; the realization arose from his own self. For the First Intellect the term is `min dhatihi`, from its essence — that which originates from itself; for the First Emanation, the contemplation was `fi dhatihi`, within his essence, and his rushing was `min dhatihi` after his seeing. He saw it, and after seeing, the realization came to his own mind and rushed upon him from himself. `Wa la ista'amma bi-siwahu` — and he took no Imam other than the First Intellect.

The text then unfolds a teaching of remarkable depth concerning why the word "emanation" carries within it the doctrine of resurrection: `idh la yuqalu bu'itha shay'un illa ba'da wujudihi awwalan wa durusihi thumma wujudihi thaniyan... wa min dhalika ishtaqqa ba'th al-mawta 'inda jumhur al-khilqa fi qiyam yawm al-qiyamat al-kubra ba'd al-mawt.` For a thing is not said to be "sent forth" (`bu'itha`) except after its existence first, then its effacement (`durus`), and then its existence a second time. There must be a first existence, then an effacement of it, then a second coming into existence — and only this second existence is properly called `ba'th`, a sending forth. And from this very structure the word `ba'th al-mawta`, the resurrection of the dead, is derived, according to the majority of creation, in the rising of the great Day of Judgment after death. As the text affirms: `wa inna al-ba'tha min al-qubur haqq` — and indeed the resurrection from the graves is true.

The pattern is one and the same. A human being first exists; he is created. Then he undergoes `durus`, effacement — he is placed in the grave and his form passes away. Then from that grave he is sent forth again: this rising is the second existence, and this is `ba'th`. First existence, then `durus`, then `ba'th`. And it is from this very pattern, present in the meaning of `inbi'ath`, that the doctrine of the resurrection of the dead at the great Day of Judgment is drawn, and the truth that resurrection from the graves is real.

A question arises as to how `durus`, effacement, can be predicated of the First Emanation, who is a luminous being. The answer lies in the relation of the two perfections. During the time when the First Intellect performed the affirmation of unity and the declaration of incomparability — when its first perfection was being attained and its act accomplished — the state of the one who would become the Second Emanation was `durus`: it was as though non-existent, effaced, while the First acted. Then his existence came a second time. So the structure of existence, effacement, and second existence is fulfilled, and from it the resurrection is derived.

### Al-Tali, Al-Lawh, and Al-Nafs al-Kulliyya

The First Emanation bears further names, each with its reason. `Wa qila 'alayhi al-tali, li-ma kana taliyan li-l-'aql al-awwal, fi-ma qama bihi min al-fi'l.` He is called the follower (`al-tali`), because he was the follower of the First Intellect in that act which he undertook — the act which the First Intellect performed, he performed after it, following it.

`Wa kadhalika qawluhum al-'aql al-thani, li-makan al-thani li-l-awwal, fi-ma fi'lihi min al-tawhid wa al-tanzih wa al-tajrid.` And likewise he is called the second intellect (`al-'aql al-thani`), because he stands as the second to the First in that which he did of affirmation of unity, transcendence, and abstraction (`tawhid`, `tanzih`, `tajrid`). The First Emanation is the second intellect because he was second after the First in performing these.

`Wa qila 'alayhi al-lawh, li-makanuhu matrahan li-shu'a' al-qalam, alladhi huwa al-'aql al-awwal.` He is called the tablet (`al-lawh`), because his place became a receptacle for the rays of the pen (`al-qalam`), and that pen is the First Intellect. The First Intellect is the pen; the First Emanation is the tablet. Just as upon a tablet one writes with a pen, so upon the tablet of the First Emanation there was written, with the lights of the First Intellect, all that the First Intellect inscribed. The pen is the First Intellect; the tablet is the First Emanation.

`Wa qila 'alayhi al-nafs al-kulliyya, li-ma kana anfas 'alamahu wa ashraf mawjudat al-awwal, wa sara kullan lahum wa ilayhi intihahum.` He is called the universal soul (`al-nafs al-kulliyya`), because he was the most precious thing in his world and the noblest of the existents after the First, and he became a "whole" (`kull`) for them, and to him is their end. He became a whole for them all, the universal soul into which the particulars are gathered.

### The End of All Intellects: Jannat al-Ma'wa and Sidrat al-Muntaha

This last name opens onto a teaching of singular importance concerning the final destination of all the intellects. The end (`intiha`) of all the intellects is toward the First Emanation — not toward the First Intellect. The First Intellect abides in the circle of unity, unapproachable; but the goal toward which all others move is the First Emanation. This is given scriptural anchorage in the image of the Garden of Abode: `Jannat al-ma'wa 'inda sidrat al-muntaha` — the Garden of Abode, near the Lote-tree of the Farthest Boundary. The Garden of Abode (`Jannat al-ma'wa`), which is the paradise of everyone, is the First Emanation; its station is the station of the First Emanation. And the Lote-tree of the Farthest Boundary (`Sidrat al-Muntaha`) is the very self of the First Intellect. The Garden of Abode is located near the Lote-tree of the Farthest Boundary, just as the First Emanation is the one nearest to the First Intellect.

So the final station for all of us is the Garden of Abode of the First Emanation. None can attain the Lote-tree of the Farthest Boundary — none can reach the self of the First Intellect — except the First Intellect alone, because he affirmed from his own self, while all the others affirmed by seeing. His rank is therefore higher, set apart and unreachable. The First Emanation, to be sure, also acted from his own self, but with the difference of precedence; and the Second Emanation too acted from his own self. Yet the difference remains: only the First Intellect affirmed from himself with no predecessor whatever, and so only he attains the Lote-tree of the Farthest Boundary, while the rest find their end and their abode in the Garden that is the First Emanation.

### The Second Emanation: The Same Act, the Transgression of Equality

The third section of the First Pavilion, gathering five chapters, opens its first chapter with the Second Emanation: `thumma inna al-inbi'ath al-thani... inba'atha fi dhatihi, ma inba'atha fi dhat al-awwal.` Then indeed the Second Emanation — there emanated within his essence what had emanated within the essence of the First Emanation. In this regard there is no difference at all between them: what was in the First Emanation was likewise in the Second. `Wa nazara ka-ma nazara` — and he looked exactly as the First Emanation had looked; his vision was the same as the First Emanation's vision.

`Wa nafa 'anhu al-ilahiyya la ibda'an min jinsihi, ka-ma nafa.` And he negated divinity from himself — `'anhu`, from his own self — not claiming origination from his own kind, just as the First Emanation had negated it. He too pronounced `La ilaha illa Allah`: there is no deity worthy of worship except Allah; and so he negated divinity from his own self and from his kind, exactly as the First Emanation had negated it. `Wa athbataha li-mubdi'ihi ta'ala wa takabbara ka-ma athbata` — and he affirmed it for his Exalted Originator and magnified Him, just as the First Emanation had affirmed. The divinity, with its honor and its decline (`izzat-o-zawal`), belongs to Him alone. `Wa sabbaha al-'aql al-awwal wa qaddasahu... ka-ma sabbaha wa qaddasa` — and he glorified the First Intellect and sanctified it, just as the First Emanation had glorified and sanctified it.

Thus far the Second Emanation has done everything the First Emanation did, and done it identically. But now comes the turning, and it is here that the text records the slip: `wa takhatta'ahu wa lam ya'tarif bi-fadlihi 'alayhi wa sabqihi lahu, bal takhatta'ahu wa zanna annahu wa iyyahu fi halin mutamathila wa musawat wa mushakila. fa-kana dhalika minhu zallatan wa khati'ah.` He transgressed against the First Emanation (`takhatta'ahu`) — that is, he committed an error with respect to him. He did not glorify the First Emanation, nor did he sanctify it; he did not acknowledge its virtue over him, nor its precedence to him. Rather, he transgressed against it and supposed that he and it — he himself and the First Emanation — were in a similar, equal, and alike state (`mutamathila`, `musawat`, `mushakila`): that they were equals. And so this became, on his part, a slip (`zalla`) and a sin (`khati'a`).

The very word `zalla` invites a precise reflection, for in Arabic there are several near-homophones distinguished only by their letters, each carrying a different meaning. `Zalla` written with the letter `zal` means that he was abased. Written with `zay`, it means that he slipped. Written with `dwad` — as `dalla` — it means that he went astray. And written with `zwa`, it carries the sense that a shadow was cast. The Second Emanation's fault is the slip, the `zalla` of slipping: a stumble of vision, a sin of supposition.

### The Principle of Precedence: Vision of the Root and Vision of the Branch

From this slip the discourse extracts a universal principle, a kind of law governing all who come after. Each being must acknowledge the precedence of those who came before it — and must acknowledge the precedence of their vision: that this one's vision was higher, that one's was lower. The question naturally arises: we who come long afterward, behind whom countless beings have already passed, are we then obliged to acknowledge all of them, even those we do not know?

The answer reframes the obligation. What is required is not an impossible cataloguing of every predecessor by name, but the seeing of the reality (`haqiqat`) — the seeing of the principles, the root (`asl`). The one who sees the reality, who perceives what the principles are and acts upon them, has a noble vision (`nazar-e ashraf`); and the one who sees only the branches (`furu'`) has a lower vision (`nazar-e adna`). And being noble of vision elevates a being, while seeing only the branches lowers it. To know the reality is to know that this vision is noble and stands higher than that one. The acknowledgment of precedence, then, is fundamentally the acknowledgment of the nobility of the root-seeing vision over the branch-seeing vision.

Why, it may be asked, could the Second Emanation not see who had preceded him, when for us such knowledge is genuinely obstructed? The answer turns on the absence of obstacles in that luminous realm. Here we are hindered by space and by time; there were no such hindrances in the world of lights. There was no obstacle that would make the knowing of precedence take time; the matter was perfectly clear and immediate. Yet the Second Emanation said: since both of us came into being together, we are equal. And this is the heart of his fault — for the obstacle was not there. The difference of vision was present and plain. He understood that there was a difference of vision; and having understood it, he still declared that they were equal. He grasped that the two came into being at the same instant, that both affirmed the unity and transcendence of Allah, that both acknowledged the First Intellect, that in this they advanced together — and from this togetherness he concluded equality, while the difference of vision, which was a very high difference, he refused to accept.

We should be careful here in our naming. The Second Emanation is not to be called a denier in the manner of one who rejects the truth outright. He affirmed the Originator, he acknowledged the First Intellect, he performed the same affirmations — his fault was confined to the refusal of the difference of vision, the claim of equality with the First Emanation.

### The Cutting Off of Bounties and the Two Joys

The consequence of this claim was severe and exact: `fa-inqata'at mawaddat al-inbi'ath al-awwal 'anhu wa lahazatuhu wa ta'yidatuhu wa barakatuhu.` The bounties of the First Emanation were cut off from him — from the Second Emanation — along with its glances (`lahazat`), its confirmations (`ta'yidat`), and its blessings (`barakat`). Because the First Emanation possessed the nobler vision, the confirmations and bounties from the First Intellect were directed toward him; and because the Second Emanation did not acknowledge this, the bounties, confirmations, and blessings were not directed toward him, and he did not attain the Second Perfection.

Here lies the most delicate and instructive point of the whole account: the very same action was performed by both, and the difference lay only in the reality of vision and in intention. To illuminate this, the discourse offers homely analogies drawn from our own experience. Suppose a scholar delivers a religious gathering (`majlis`). If one listener comes and praises only the beauty of his voice, how much of the scholar's attention can that praise truly hold? But if another draws his attention to the scholarly substance, to the depth of his learning, then his attention turns naturally and fully toward that. The one who sees the real thing draws the real response. Or consider two men eating the same food at the same table: one eats for the sake of the heavenly reward (`thawab`), that Allah may reward him; the other eats merely because it is a custom, "the community is gathered, we must host them." The action is identical, but the difference in intention is enormous. The difference is in intentions.

So it was in that realm. The First Emanation understood that the First Intellect attained its greatness because it performed the affirmation of unity, transcendence, and abstraction — and that he too, by performing the same `tawhid`, `tanzih`, and `tajrid`, could attain that state, the Second Perfection. He fixed his vision upon the core reality: that the essential thing is the affirmation directed to God, by which radiance is attained, and not the mere superiority over those below. The Second Emanation, by contrast, did not see this. He saw only that the First Intellect had advanced beyond all the worlds, and he fixed his gaze upon that superiority. He performed the same action, yet the reality of his vision was absent; and because for the Second Perfection there must not be even the slightest blemish, even the faintest lowness of vision, he could not attain it.

The text then continues the account of his fall: he was darkened and made ignorant of his rank, and he became proud, and he fell from his rank, and the rank of the First Emanation became distant from the rank of the Second Emanation. `idh huwa hadd al-qarib ilayhi, al-shahid 'alayhi, al-muthab bihi, al-mu'aqab bi-sababih.` For the First Emanation is the high limit (`hadd`) of the one near to him, the Second Emanation — the witness over him (`al-shahid 'alayhi`), the one by whom reward is given (`al-muthab bihi`), the one by whose means punishment is inflicted (`al-mu'aqab bi-sababih`). And so the Second Emanation came to know that darkness had fallen upon him and that he had sinned.

This recognition, however, did not come at once. In the beginning he did not even realize that he was failing to attain the Second Perfection, that something was wrong. He acknowledged his fault only after the seven intellects had advanced past him through the `da'wah`, and countless individuals had advanced past him; then at last he understood — all these have attained this radiance; why have I not attained it? When a being does not know and slips, the darkness that comes upon him follows from a delusion he held; he did not attain the Second Perfection, for which complete purity is required, with no blemish, with the vision fixed upon the question, "What is the core reality? For whom does one truly work?"

The difference of the two emanations is finally crystallized in the image of two joys (`masarratayn`). Consider a man who comes to the mosque for the dawn prayer. The core purpose of his coming is the command of Allah — that he should obey it, and through the prayer rise. But upon arriving he notices that he is the first to have come, and he feels a joy at his own precedence. Two visions are thereby created within him, and two joys: the first joy, the higher joy (`masarrat al-'ala`), which is from the worship of God and is the core reality; and the second joy, the lower joy (`masarrat al-adna`), which is from his superiority over all the other worshippers. If a man comes for the sake of being among the first to arrive rather than for the sake of the prayer itself, this is the real flaw of the heart — he has not come for prayer at all. In that luminous realm it was the same difference of vision. The vision of the First Emanation was upon the higher joy, the core reality; the vision of the Second Emanation was upon the lower joy. And despite his vision resting upon the lower, he still claimed equality, failing to weigh properly the difference between the higher and the lower vision.

### Union, Veil, and Gate: The First Intellect Establishes the Da'wah

The second chapter of the third section turns to the relation of union between the First Intellect and the First Emanation: `thumma inna al-'aql al-awwal ittahada bi-l-inbi'ath al-awwal, wa ittakhadhahu lahu hijaban wa ila ta'atihi wa ta'at mubdi'ihi baban.` Then indeed the First Intellect united with the First Emanation, and took him as a veil (`hijab`) for himself, and as a gate (`bab`) toward his own obedience and the obedience of his Originator. The First Emanation became a veil for the First Intellect, and a gate through which one passes to the obedience of the First Intellect and, beyond it, to the obedience of the Originator.

The word "gate" here is not to be taken in its literal architectural sense, but in the sense in which we speak of the chapters of jurisprudence (`fiqh`) and the chapters of logic as "gates" or "sections" of a science. There are two modes of advancing in this order. Some advance through the call, the `da'wah`; and some advance by seeing — and these are called gates (`abwab`), like the First Emanation. The structure is mirrored in the parallel order of the visible world. The Imam stands opposite the First Intellect, and the Bab — the Gate — stands opposite the First Emanation. For in the world of intellects, the First Emanation advanced by seeing the First Intellect; so too, in the parallel structure, the Imam advances on his own, while the Bab advances by seeing the Imam, and then the apparent and hidden gates advance through the `da'wah`. As it happened there, so it happens here.

We may state the parallel precisely. The Imam, peace be upon him, stands at the station of the First Intellect: he advanced on his own — through the gnosis of the `'ayn 'azima`, the Imam of the Imam, by whose gnosis he advanced toward the affirmation of unity. The Gate of Emanation (`bab al-inbi'ath`) corresponds to the First Emanation: he advanced by seeing the Imam. And the third, the hidden gates, advance through the `da'wah`. Thus the First Intellect, having united with the First Emanation and taken him as veil and gate, established the call in that world: `wa aqama al-da'wa fi dhalika al-'alam, fa-da'ahum minhu wa khatabahum bihi.` And he established the `da'wah` in that world, so that he called them all toward himself through the First Emanation, and addressed them all through the First Emanation. The connecting particle `fa` is deliberate: because the First Intellect established the `da'wah` in that world, he called all toward himself through the First Emanation and addressed all of them through him. The `da'wah` belongs to the First Intellect, but it is conducted through the First Emanation.

### The Seven Luminous Forms and the Predestined Count

`fa-ajabuhu sab'at ashbahin suriyya, mutafawitun fi al-ijaba, wahidun ba'da wahid.` Seven luminous formal phantoms (`sab'at ashbahin suriyya`) responded to the First Intellect's call, differing in their response (`mutafawitun fi al-ijaba`), one after another (`wahidun ba'da wahid`). It is here, in their successive response, that the matter of before and after has its place. And of each one of them: `wa wahhada kull wahidin minhum mubdi'ahu wa nazzahahu wa i'tarafa bi-rububiyyat al-'aql al-awwal, wa sabqihi wa ta'ilihi wa rutbatihi, wa subbaha wa qaddasa haddahu, alladhi huwa waqifun bihi.` Each one of them affirmed the unity of his own Originator and declared His incomparability, and acknowledged the lordship (`rububiyya`) of the First Intellect, and its precedence, its being a follower, and its rank — that is, the rank of the First Emanation as well. There is here a `da'wah` of three: the Exalted Originator, the First Intellect, and the follower (`al-tali`, the First Emanation). And each one glorified and sanctified his own limit (`had`), the station at which he himself stood.

Why were they exactly seven? Because Allah placed seven disconnected letters (`muqatta'at`) in the formula `La ilaha illa Allah`. The count did not end at seven by chance — it could conceivably have been eight, nine, or ten — but Allah ordained it so. The `kalimah`, the sacred Word, stands above the First Intellect, and Allah has placed within it everything that was and that will be. The meaning of `muqatta'at` is the points where the tongue stops, where the pen stops — where, as a compass draws a circle, the circle is closed. A circle was drawn and ended; then a second, a third, a fourth, until seven circles were made. This is because Allah had decreed it all in `La ilaha illa Allah`: where the `muqatta'at` fall is where a circle ends. The Word comes first, in the knowledge of Allah, and the circles are formed afterward in accordance with it; in His knowledge, He had decreed that seven circles would be formed, and this was destiny, lodged within `La ilaha illa Allah`. For `kalimatu bayn al-mubdi' wa al-mubda' kalima` — the Word stands between the Originator and the originated.

This yields a precise teaching about the scope of free choice. Did the beings, then, lack absolute choice? In one respect they had absolute choice: each had absolute choice in advancing on his own, in the destiny that was open to his effort. But in another respect they did not: the count had to end at seven, and this was not theirs to determine. To advance on one's own was a matter of absolute choice; but where the count would end was fixed. And the turn by which the fallen one becomes the tenth (`'Ashir`) — this too was set, so that after so many souls this particular one should come and be the tenth. The choice of the creature is itself contained within the choice of the One who grants choice: the creature may exercise his choice in any way he wills, yet that this one should arrive after so many souls is a matter of the choice of Allah. This is the one who is `bi-'ashara sabiqat wa 'ashara taliyat` — with ten preceding and ten following.

### The Ascending Acknowledgment and the Flow of Divine Light

The praise of the seven became known, and their ranks rose, through an ascending sequence of acknowledgment. Each one of the seven acknowledged the limit of the one above it. The first of the seven made his acknowledgment; the second acknowledged; the third acknowledged the second; the fourth acknowledged the third — and so upward, until the seventh intellect acknowledged the prior six, and then acknowledged the First Emanation, and then the First Intellect, and then the Exalted Originator. Thus each rank confessed the rank above it, ascending step by step to the very summit.

And through this acknowledgment, the divine light flowed: `wa ittasala bihim nur ilahi wa 'ilmun mutanahi, al-jari min al-'aql al-awwal ila al-'aql al-thani, mustamirrun ilayhim, wa bihi al-bulugh ila kamal al-thani.` A divine light and an endless knowledge connected to them — flowing from the First Intellect to the second intellect, continuing onward toward them, toward those seven intellects; and through it is the attainment of the Second Perfection. What is meant by "divine light" and "endless knowledge"? It is what was stated at the beginning: the light of `La ilaha illa Allah`. The divine light is the flowing knowledge; the divine light flows, and the flowing knowledge flows, from the First Intellect to the second intellect and onward to the seven. By receiving it, the path to the Second Perfection opens.

This is precisely why the Second Emanation did not attain the Second Perfection: because the divine light and the endless knowledge did not reach him from the First Intellect. And why did it not reach him? Because he did not know the reality. He acknowledged the reality of the Exalted Originator, and he acknowledged the First Intellect, but the difference in the precedence of vision he did not acknowledge — and this is the decisive point. For this reason the light did not reach him. So the `da'wah` took place, and through it seven advanced, while the Second Emanation was left behind. As the text adds, `wa al-mahall al-'ali min al-dani` — and the high station was attained from the low — that is, the ascent to perfection proceeded from the lower toward the higher.

### The Testimony of Rahat al-'Aql and the Bearers of the Throne

This is the conclusion of the words of Sayyidna Hamiduddin, peace be upon him, in the fifth spring of the fourth secret of `Rahat al-'Aql`: `a-la inna al-wujud 'an al-'aql al-awwal wa al-munba'ith al-awwal wa 'uqul sab'a.` Lo, indeed, existence is from the First Intellect, and from the First Emanation, and from the seven intellects. The First Emanation is the very first; after him came the eighth, the ninth, and the tenth in being. And it is worth noting that the later authorities counted the bearers of the Throne (`hamalat al-'arsh`) as eight, not counting the First Intellect at all: they reckon the seven `muqatta'at` of `La ilaha illa Allah`, plus `La ilaha illa Allah` itself, which together make eight. This is why the later ones speak of eight bearers of the Throne.

The text continues: `wa inna nur kull wahidin minhum... wujud kull wahidin minhum 'an al-akhir, siwa al-munba'ith al-awwal... 'an al-akhir... kull wahidin minhum sa'idan ila al-'aql al-awwal.` And indeed the existence of each one of them is from the last (`'an al-akhir`) — except for the First Emanation — each one of them ascending toward the First Intellect, ascending toward the First Emanation. The structure is that the existence of each derives from the last individual of the preceding circle, with the First Emanation as the sole exception at the head, and the whole order ascending toward the First Intellect.

### The Making of the Circles, the Center, and the Twelve Chiefs

The text closes by describing how the circles are generated, one from another. In that world, when the souls were created, the last of the existing souls — the final individual of a completed circle — was commanded: "You perform the `da'wah`." From him the call began, and from this point the circles started to be formed. When a circle is completed, its last individual is commanded to give the call, and from him a new circle takes shape; then the last individual of that circle, in turn, forms yet another circle, and so on without end.

A question arises: if the last individual of a given circle gives the call to the next, and so seems to occupy a position of giving, is he then greater than the intellect of the following circle? The answer lies in the predestined count. When a circle reaches its appointed limit — when, for example, the twenty-eighth limit is reached and the circle of twenty-eight is completed (for the Exalted Originator had decreed in `La ilaha illa Allah` that it should be twenty-eight, just as in other reckonings it is forty, or a hundred) — then upon completion the last is commanded to give the `da'wah` and form a new circle. The limits were thus predestined in the `kalimah`; the beings had absolute choice in advancing, but not in where the count would end.

The image of the compass makes the structure vivid. In the middle of each circle there is a center, and this center is the chief — it is the seventh intellect, or the sixth, or the fifth, which serves as the center of its own circle. The compass is set at this center; its arm goes around, and when it returns to the point where it began, when the two ends of the compass meet, the circle is closed, and the last individual is commanded to perform the `da'wah`. And within each circle there are twelve chiefs (`ru'asa'`), because the letters of `La ilaha illa Allah` are twelve in number. In every circle, then, there are twelve chiefs — the chiefs of the circle.

Of all these, the text affirms: `wa inna nur kull wahidin minhum sariyan fi-ma wujida 'an al-awwal min al-hayula wa al-sura.` And indeed the light of each one of them is flowing in that which was found from the First, of prime matter (`hayula`) and form (`sura`). The light of each shines and flows through prime matter and form — through the matter that comes from the self and the form that comes from the form of the self, by which existence is constituted. `yuridu bi-dhalika ithbat al-da'wat lahum wa ijabatahum lahu wahidan ba'da wahid` — by this is intended the establishment of the `da'wah` for them and their response to the First Intellect, one after another. And this continues, `ka-ma sabaqa min al-qawl, ila an balaghu kamal al-thani, alladhi bihi al-najat wa al-'isma wa al-fawz wa al-ni'ma` — as has been stated before, until they reached the Second Perfection, through which come salvation (`najat`), infallibility (`'isma`), success (`fawz`), and bounty (`ni'ma`). And after the Second Perfection there is no decline whatsoever; there is only ascent, and joy, and bounty, and the ceaseless increase of bounty. That is the whole of it.

## The Spiritual Hierarchy: the Mumaththal, the Asas, and the Cherubim

### The Descent of the Kingly Light through the Natiq and the Asas

The discourse opens with the great act of sprinkling by which the luminous order was set in motion. `Thumma innahu rashsha ʻaqlan awwalan` — "Then He sprinkled forth a First Intellect." Upon this First Intellect there was cast the kingly luminous light, the `nūr-e shāhshānī ilāhī` — a light at once royal and divine, described as `ṭāhirah jiddan jiddan`, extremely and exceedingly pure. This is the radiance that streams from the highest origin and gives the originated existents their order. From the very moment of its outpouring, this light rendered the originated existents (the `wujūdāt-e ibdāʻī`) just — `ʻādilan` — establishing equity both `baynahumā`, between the two highest principles, and `bayna sāʼir al-ʻuqūl`, between all the remaining intellects. The light does not merely illuminate; it apportions, balancing each rank against the others so that nothing is wronged in the distribution of being.

The same kingly light then flowed downward in an unbroken transmission. From the First Intellect, which here is identified with the Natiq, the `nūr-e shāhshānī ilāhī` passed to the Asas, the Foundation — `dālika bi-anna al-Natiq`, "that is because of the Natiq." From the Natiq the luminous flow reached the Asas, and through the Asas it continued to descend to the seven intellects that follow. After the seven had received their portion, the transmission reached its full extent. What was given was given; whatever happened, happened — and there is no undoing it. In exactly the same manner, the `ḥudūd`, the ranks of the hierarchy, were rendered `ʻādilan` between the two and between all the other intellects, so that justice ran through every level of the descending order. This is the essential pattern: the kingly luminous light originates at the summit, passes through the Natiq and the Asas, and through them seeds the seven intellects, with the hudud made just between every grade. From the previous discussion the principle is plain — first the Natiq, then through it the Asas, and then the sevenfold company, each receiving the portion of light that fits its station, and the equity of God holding the whole in balance.

When the intellects beheld the unity of God and the divinity of that oneness — `wa ḥadd waḥdah, wa ilāhiyyat al-waḥdat` — they were seized with awe. They became agitated and, upon perceiving that station of oneness, they were confounded, perplexed, and amazed. In that confoundment they prostrated. They made of their own selves the means by which they bowed, and they prostrated before His greatness. This prostration of the abstract intellects before the divine oneness is the proper response of a created mind that suddenly glimpses the unconditioned majesty of its Originator: not comprehension, for the Originator is beyond comprehension, but adoration.

### The Believer in the Time of Occultation and the Threefold Witness

This luminous economy is not confined to the supernal world; it descends to the condition of the believer who lives in the age of the Imam's concealment. The realities mentioned here are drawn, the teacher notes, from the book in question — the `kitāb-e mazkūr`, which is *Rāḥat al-ʻAql* — wherein from its beginning to its end the reality is set forth in the mention of the Daʻi himself. And the spiritual difficulty the believer faces is acute. "Where shall I find the answer to this question?" — `ayna sa-aʻīduhā` — for how can a reply be sought `mimman lā yaʻrifu`, from one who does not know? The answer lies with the Daʻi who is veiled, `fī ḥijāb`: the caller of the first and the last, of the manifest (`zāhir`) and the hidden (`bāṭin`), the `dāʻī fī ḥijāb`. With that veiled caller the believer becomes familiarized; he himself becomes veiled (`maḥjūb`) and intimate (`mānūs`) with the one who guides from behind the veil.

Nothing in this dispensation came about by accident, and nothing was skipped. The knowledge of the First and the Second is obtained through intellect (`ʻaql`) — for the Awwal and the Thani are realities the mind can reach by its own ascending contemplation. But the knowledge of the Imam is of another order: it is obtained only through the knowledge of his occultation (`ghaybah`), and it is granted by means of his command, his call, and his summons. The believer who attains this knowledge is one upon whom a great blessing has been bestowed — and that blessing itself bears a name: it is `al-ghaybah`. The occultation, far from being a deprivation, is in truth a `niʻmah`, a favor. Through the very fact of the ghaybah, all the rights and graces pertaining to it were given over to the believer. He occupies himself with its remembrance (`zikr`), with the supplication for relief — `wa al-faraj ʻan awliyāʼika wa al-muʼminīna min ʻibādika`, "and relief for Your friends and the believers from among Your servants." So the season of concealment is itself a station of nearness for the one who knows how to dwell in it.

To establish the reality of witness within this order, the teacher turns to the Qurʼan. As the Exalted has declared: `shahida Allāhu annahu lā ilāha illā Huwa wa al-malāʼikatu wa ulū al-ʻilm` — "Allah witnesses that there is no deity except Him, and [so do] the angels and those of knowledge." Allah Himself has borne witness that there is no object of worship save Him; and He has issued the judgment. Then the angels have borne witness, and the people of knowledge — the `ṣāḥibān-e ʻilm`, the learned — have borne witness. Three, then, give testimony: Allah, the angels, and the people of knowledge. And the believer too is a witness, `shāhid ʻalā mā yaʼtīhi ʻani al-Imām ʻalayhi al-salām` — a witness to what reaches him from the Imam, upon whom be peace. Because Allah Himself gives witness, and because His witness is of such a kind that the one who proclaims the testimony is drawn into it, the name of Allah came to rest upon the witnessing believer — `innamā huwa ka-ismika ʻalā ismika, ism Allāh`, "it is but like Your name upon Your name, the name of Allah."

This is intimately bound to the meanings of the occultation. In the state of ghaybah this descended light is called `nūr-e shāshānī`, and the name that comes to rest upon the believer is the name of Allah itself. With respect to the rights of such a believer near the Ahl al-Bayt: when he attains this state, when he is raised to the condition of `nūr-e shāshānī`, he himself becomes Ahl al-Bayt. This is the crowning blessing of the age of concealment — that the believer who attains the rank of the kingly light in the time of the Imam's ghaybah is gathered into the very household of the Prophet. So the chapter of lights (`bāb al-anwār`) proceeds, and the matter of the seventeenth chapter is the matter of the intellect: the narration that, when the believer affirms `lā ilāha illā Huwa` with that intellect and holds it with certainty, he negates divinity from his own self and from his own kind — `min nafsika wa min jinsika` — and affirms it for the Originator alone. By his intellect, and by the divine word `lā ilāha illā Huwa shāhidan bihi`, "there is no deity but He, being a witness to it," he proves with his own statement that there is no deity except Him. He is the witness, and the very saying establishes the One to whom he bears witness.

### The Mumaththal al-Awwal, the Sabiq, and the Impossibility of Indication without Nabi and Wasi

From the theme of witness the discourse turns to the precise rank from which the call to God issues. The matter concerns indication (`ishārah`) — the pointing of the mind toward the Originator (`mubdiʼ`). The Originator lies, as it were, in a certain direction, and the indication is directed toward Him: toward the mubdiʼ, for the sake of glorification and the affirmation of His transcendence — `taʻẓīman wa tanzīhan`. The glorification (`taʻẓīm`) exalts Him; the declaration of transcendence (`tanzīh`) removes from Him every defect and likeness. But here the text states a strict limit: `idh lā yakūnu ʻalayhi ishārah` — "since there can be no indication toward Him" — except through the Nabi (the Prophet) and the Wasi (the Successor). No mind can point toward the Originator without the mediation of Prophet and Successor; and if one were to attempt indication without them, the indication would in truth be a mention raised against Allah, the Exalted, rather than toward Him.

The rank from which the call proceeds is named `al-Mumaththal al-Awwal`, the First Representative — `wa huwa al-mumaththal al-awwal wa al-sābiq al-awwal, al-mujīb li-daʻwatihi ʻinda qiyāmi daʻwatihi`, "and he is the First Representative and the first Precedent, the one who responds to his call at the establishment of his call." Here a careful distinction must be drawn, for the two ranks of Mumaththal and Sabiq are easily confused. The Sabiq, the Precedent, is the one who at the time the call is raised is the first to respond to it — `al-mujīb li-daʻwatihi`. But the Mumaththal al-Awwal is something higher: he is the one who establishes the call in the first place. The First Representative does the establishing of the call; the Sabiq does the responding. The Mumaththal al-Awwal therefore stands above the Sabiq. He has, as it were, already come before the call is sounded; the call is his, and the Sabiq is the first to answer when it is sounded.

These two are precisely the angels named in the Qurʼanic verse. The Mumaththal al-Awwal and the Sabiq are the `ulū al-ʻilm`, the people of knowledge, the `ṣāḥibān-e ʻilm`, the owners and masters of knowledge — for `ulū` means owners, and they have been made owners of knowledge. `Hum ahl al-daʻwat al-ṣaḥīḥah` — they are the people of the correct, sound call. The speech concerning them, the text promises, will come in due course — `alladhī yaʼtī ʻalayhim al-kalām baʻda al-ishārah`, "the discourse upon them will follow after the indication" — and so it does. Reading the verse once more for its full sense — `shahida Allāhu annahu lā ilāha illā Huwa wa al-malāʼikatu wa ulū al-ʻilm qāʼiman bil-qisṭ`, "Allah witnesses that there is no deity except Him, and so do the angels and those of knowledge, maintaining creation in justice" — the witnesses resolve as follows. `Shahida Allāhu`: Allah has borne witness that there is no god but He. `Wa al-malāʼikatu`, "and the angels": these are the Sabiq, the first-sent precedent (`mubaʻath awwal`). `Wa ulū al-ʻilm`, "and those of knowledge": these are the people of soundness, the Mumaththal al-Awwal and the company of the correct call. The commentator, expounding this verse, traced the entire chain from the Prophet downward — `thumma al-Imām, al-ʻĀlim wa ʻĀlimuhu`, "then the Imam, the Knower and His Knower" — preserving the whole silsilah from the Nabi through to the Mumaththal al-Awwal. The Sabiq and the hujjat are matters apart, whose detailed exposition belongs to its own place; but the verse itself, when read through the hierarchy, names Allah, the angels as the Sabiq, and the people of knowledge as the Mumaththal al-Awwal.

### True Existence as the Second Perfection, and the Names of the World of Command

The discourse now passes — following the text of *Mabdaʼ Maʻād* — to the nature of true existence and the names by which this supernal world is known. The world under discussion is named the world of pure existence within the world of existence, on account of its coming forth from non-existence, which is not existence at all. The term `bulūgh`, "attainment," signifies reaching perfection (`kamāl`); and the question arises whether the attainment intended is the first perfection (`kamāl awwal`) or the second (`kamāl thānī`). The first existence is only the first perfection of a thing — it is mere life. But the true existence, the `wujūd ḥaqīqī`, is the second perfection (`kamāl thānī`). For `wujūd` means the path of coming into being, the passage into existence from `ʻadam`, non-existence — and that non-existence was not a "time," for there was no time, nothing whatsoever; from there, all things came to be. The first existence, the bare coming-into-being from nothing, is only life (`zindagī`); but the genuine, realized existence — `al-ladhī huwa bulūghuhum al-ḥaqīqī`, "which is their true attainment" — is the second perfection. The first existence is the kamal awwal of a thing; the true existence that follows upon it is the kamal thani, and it is this latter, realized existence that the text designates as wujud haqiqi.

This world bears many names, all of them drawn from its relation to the command of God. It is called `ʻālam al-amr`, the World of Command — `wa huwa al-amr wa al-ʻilm al-muttaṣil billāhi taʻālā ittiṣālan ʻaqliyyan`, "for it is the Command and the Knowledge connected to God, the Exalted, by an intellectual connection." From this world a flow of command issues; it is the knowledge that is joined to Allah by an intellectual joining. How did the command become so connected? By the `kāf` of His word — the divine "Be." And from it there flows a stream toward what lies below it, namely the intellects (`ʻuqūl`). It is also the Spirit — `wa huwa al-rūḥ` — and for this reason this world was named the World of Spirits: `wa li-dhālika summiya hādhā al-ʻālam ʻālam al-arwāḥ`. It is called `ʻālam al-rūḥ`, the World of the Spirit, and from this the name `ʻālam al-arwāḥ`, the World of Spirits, is derived, `iḍāfatan ilā hādhā al-rūḥ`, in relation to this Spirit — the Spirit connected to Allah, which grants eternal life (`ḥayāt abadiyyah`) to those who are joined to it. It is likewise named `ʻālam al-ibdāʻ`, the World of Origination; `ʻālam al-ʻaql al-kull`, the World of the Universal Intellect; and `ʻālam al-quds`, the World of Sanctity. For the Spirit itself is this world, and every one of these titles belongs to it `li-nisbatihi ilā amrillāh`, on account of its relation to the command of God. World of Command, World of the Spirit, World of Spirits, World of Origination, World of Universal Intellect, World of Sanctity — these are not six worlds but one, named in six ways according to the facets of its single relation to the divine amr.

### The World of Subtlety, the World of Attributes, and the Eight Worlds

The exposition continues from the first emanation (`ṣadr-i awwal`), that which is `sārī fī al-ashyāʼi kullihā`, pervasive in all things. When a denier is found in its regard, the attribution divides into the Precedent (`sābiq`) and the Subsequent (`tālī`). Proceeding from the first emanation, the discourse names the world of subtlety, `ʻālam-i laṭāfat`, in which there is no physical density. And there is the world of attributes, `ʻālam-i ṣifāt`, so named on account of its `ṣafāʼ`, its purity and clarity: these are pure essences, free by reason of their purity from every impurity and density. From these the world of existence (`ʻālam-i wujūd`) was brought forth.

The teacher then enumerates the worlds, which are eight in number. They are: `ʻālam-i rūḥānī`, the spiritual world; `ʻālam-i abdān`, the world of bodies; `ʻālam-i ʻaql`, the world of intellect; `ʻālam-i nafs`, the world of soul; `ʻālam-i laṭāfat`, the world of subtlety; and `ʻālam-i ṣifāt`, the world of attributes — to which belong also the world of bodies and the world of existence already named, completing the eight. In this graded series the luminous, subtle forms stand highest, having neither density nor admixture; the souls are a later and fallen degradation of those luminous forms; and the bodies are a yet more fallen form still, the densest and lowest sediment of the descent. So the order from subtlety to density traces the whole declension: the realm of subtlety and attributes possesses no corporeal weight at all, the soul is a degradation from the luminous degree, and the body a degradation below even the soul.

### From One Command Seven Forms, and from the Seven the Innumerable

The text now turns to the multiplication of forms from the single divine command: `thumma innahu anshaʼa minhu sabʻa ṣuwarin wa ṣuwaran kathīran` — "Then indeed He produced from it seven forms, and many forms." From the first `ḥukm`, the single decree, seven forms emerged; and from those seven forms there came forth `ṣuwaran kathīran`, innumerable forms, by way of `ijābat`, response. From this it is understood that the first seven forms were established by direct command, and that after their emergence the gate of permission (`ijāzat`) was closed. Once those primary forms had come forth, it was decreed that within the emergence of each one there should arise countless further forms — but no longer by a fresh act of origination, for the gate had shut; rather, by response. And here a precise and striking principle is stated: the last form of the first circle is superior to the second circle. First the subtlety was brought forth before the company; and when the second circle was summoned, the call (`daʻwat`) was extended to the very last individual of the first circle, so that the last member of the first ranks higher than the whole of the second that comes after. Then, within each of the seven, many forms appeared `bi-ijābatihim`, through their response — the one accepted becoming established, and the chief appearing to them in his form, so that by his action he elevated what he had done before.

The relation of each such chief to those gathered under him is then defined through a threefold likeness — `wa huwa lahum kal-imām wa hum lahu kal-maʼmūm`: he is to them as the Imam, and they are to him as the followers who pray behind him. `Wa kal-ḥadd lahum wa hum lahu kal-maḥdūdīn`: he is to them as the boundary (`ḥadd`), and they are to him as the bounded (`maḥdūdīn`). `Wa kar-raʼīs lahum wa hum lahu kal-marʼūsīn`: he is to them as the chief (`raʼīs`), and they are to him as his subjects (`marʼūsīn`). For this reason it was said that he is `ghālibun bi-wujūdihī`, victorious by his very existence — the one who, by the fact of his being, prevails over and gathers his circle. This is the constant structure of every cycle of the call: at its head stands one who is at once imam, boundary, and chief, and beneath him stand those who are his followers, his bounded ones, and his subjects.

### The Composition of the Seven and the Closing of Each Circle

The discourse draws on *Kanz al-Anwār* and *Maʻād* to clarify the standing of the seven. There are no others before the first ones, and there is none beside the seven — `lā ghayr, lā ghayr` — there is nothing else. The benediction is pronounced "upon the first ones and the second" (`wa ʻalā al-awwalayn wa al-thānī`), which is to say upon the composition, the `taʼlīf`. It is for this reason that one sends `darūd` and supplication upon these individuals, these chiefs — not as a reckoning of their accountability or merit, but as the appointed benediction upon the chief of the seven and upon the first ones before them, those whose `taʼlīf` has been performed. The word `taʼlīf`, "composition," names this very `mubāḥathah`, this discursive arrangement of the ranks: `wa huwa al-qawl al-awwal wa ʻalā alladhīna ghayrahum ʻalā al-ḥukm fī hāʼulāʼi al-sabʻa`, "and this is the first statement, and upon those other than them according to the ruling concerning these seven." The benediction is then withheld from those who do not know these seven in the divine ḥukm — those who held incorrect sayings (`aqwāl`) concerning them.

This is grounded `li-qawlihi taʻālā`, in the saying of the Exalted in the verse of Sūrat al-Baqarah: `lā farqa bayna aḥadin minhum` — "we make no distinction between any of them" — `kamā bayna al-awwal wa al-thānī`, "just as between the first and the second." The meaning is that just as the chief accepted the call (`daʻwat`) in one form, so the call is built upon his form, and from his form and his action (`fiʻl`) a very great gathering came under his charge with no need for him to preach afresh. From that one act of acceptance, all of it followed. Thereafter the last individual was given the command to undertake the call in his turn; and just as the first had done, so this last performed the call, and a very great gathering accepted it — the companions who were his — and with that the circle (`dāʼirah`) was closed. This is the very form that stands before us, and between the first chief and the last there remains only the difference of "before" (`qabl`) and "after" (`baʻd`), nothing more. `ʻAlā man kāna fī ḏimnihim` — "upon those who are within their charge" — `min baʻd al-hajr min al-awwalīn`, "after the restriction, from the first ones." `Wa al-ḥaqq fī jumlat mā dhukira`, "and the truth in all that has been mentioned," is contained within and attributed to that one chief; for that single one, according to the narration, from the seven principles (`uṣūl`), each one stands `ʻalā man kāna fī ḏimnihim`, over those who fall within his charge — over the restriction (`hajr`), and over his believers (`muʼminīn`) from among the correct ones.

The circle's geometry makes the unity of each cycle vivid. They are connected to the chief `bi-ismika`, by his name; they are drawn toward him (`ilayhi`); and `wa huwa wāḥidun`, he is one. He who is the chief is a single form, and as many as fall within his circle, they and all who are within their circle stand together in one sphere (`falak`), in just one sphere. The circle, whether viewed as separate (`munfaṣilah`) or as connected (`muttaṣilah`), becomes joined in this way: this one becomes the chief, a single form; then a circle is drawn; and in the drawing, the beginning meets the end, so that however many there are, all are connected to this one. He is the center (`markaz`), and they stand under the authority of the circle. Thereafter the last individual within the circle is given the command: "Draw another circle." He calls; he extends an invitation; and having given the invitation, he himself becomes a center, and beneath him another circle is established. So `wa hādhā al-ʻālam bi-ajmaʻihi ʻālamun kabīrun ʻaẓīmun`, "and this entire world is a great and mighty world," and `lā yuḥṣīhi al-ʻadad`, "number cannot encompass it." Number cannot surround it; the multiplication of circles, each with its center and its company, runs beyond all counting, even as every circle remains anchored to a single one at its heart.

### The Seven Intellects, the Cherubim, and the Spiritual Angels

For the standing of the seven intellects in relation to the angelic order, the teacher cites *Rāḥat al-ʻAql* of Sayyiduna al-Muʼayyad al-Shīrāzī, may Allah be pleased with him. The text reads: `wa hādhihi al-sabʻa ʻuqūl bi-ḥiddatihā wa surʻatihā bi-qabūli al-qurūbiyyah` — "and these seven intellects, by their sharpness and their swiftness in receiving, [linked the heaven] with the qurūbiyyah." The key term is `ḥiddah`, sharpness, which here pertains to vision. For there may be two visions at one and the same time, but one of them possesses ḥiddah — a penetrating acuity that reaches the essential reality of a thing — while the other apprehends only the external surface. It is the sharpness of the vision that makes the difference. By this penetrating sharpness, then — `min ḥiddatihā` — and by the speed of their apprehension, these seven intellects `rabaṭū al-samāʼ bi-al-sabʻ al-qurūbiyyah`, "linked the heaven with the seven brought-near ones." The seven qurūbiyyah are the `malāʼikah qurūbiyyah`, the Angels Brought Near, and `wa alladhīna fī ḏimāmihim summū bi-al-malāʼikah al-rūḥāniyyīn`, "and those under their charge were named the spiritual angels." Those who fall under the charge of the seven, whom God the Exalted received, are named `malāʼikah rūḥāniyyīn`, the spiritual angels; while the `qurūbiyyīn` themselves are called the chiefs (`ruʼasāʼ`), and beneath them stand the spiritual angels.

### The Three Ranks of Angels and the People of the Barzakh

The hierarchy of the angels is then set out in full. At the summit are the `Malāʼikah Kurūbiyyīn`, the Cherubim, also named in the Qurʼan the `Malāʼikat al-Muqarrabūn`, the Angels Brought Near. But a careful distinction must be drawn here, for two distinct groups can wear the name "brought near," and they must not be confused. The Cherubim, the Kurūbiyyīn, are those who are first from the very beginning — seated in their station from the outset of the order, fixed there as the highest. They did not ascend to their rank; they were established in it. The Muqarrabūn in the second sense, however, are those who ascend from below: from the whole of the world of nature (`ʻālam al-ṭabīʻah`), rising past the tenth ḥadd, mounting up degree by degree. The one who attains the third rank, then the sixth, then the fifth, ascending from beneath toward the summit — these are called `Malāʼikat al-Muqarrabūn`, "the Angels Brought Near," precisely because they are coming near. A difference of seven degrees still separates them from those above; but because they are drawing nearer from below, they bear the name "brought near." So the title belongs by right to the highest as those seated near from the first, and by ascent to those who are coming near from the depths.

Set in order, the ranks are three. Highest are the `Malāʼikah Kurūbiyyīn`, the Cherubim, seated from the beginning. Beneath them, in their circle, are the `Malāʼikah Rūhāniyyīn`, the spiritual angels, likewise seated from the beginning. And after these come the `Malāʼikat al-Muqarrabūn`, the Angels Brought Near — those who are ascending from below, called Muqarrab because they are coming near. And of the tenth ḥadd it is said: `wa yuqālu li-l-ḥadd al-ʻāshir ahl al-barzakh`, "the tenth ḥadd is called the people of the barzakh." The people of the tenth ḥadd, the `ʻāshir`, are the people of the isthmus (`ahl al-barzakh`); the barzakh is the tenth, the ʻashir, standing after the Cherubim. The rank of the ʻashir is not attained at the outset; for one who is of the rank of the ʻashir, that station is not yet reached. It is reached only when he performs repentance (`tawbah`) — then he attains the rank of the ʻashir.

Of the equivalent human ranks (`ḥudūd al-insāniyyah al-musāwiyah`) it is likewise said: the whole world of nature ascends, up to the ranks of the Cherubim (`ilā ḥudūd al-Kurūbiyyīn`). Among the seven cherubic ranks, those who ascend toward them out of the entire world of nature are called the `Malāʼikat al-Muqarrabūn`. And those who are the ʻashir are the people of the barzakh, and their ranks are equivalent (`musāwiyah`). From among them — that is, from the people of the barzakh — are three ranks (`al-ḥudūd al-thalāthah`). The body of the tenth (`jism ʻāshir`) lies below the rank of the ʻashir; the rank of the ʻashir is the barzakh itself; and "from them," meaning from the people of the barzakh, are these three ranks, the `muʻāṣirūn`, those who are contemporaneous with and bear witness to every place.

### The Jadd, the Tongue of Wisdom, and the Tongue of the Law

Above the barzakh, and beyond it, the discourse reaches the Jadd. From the barzakh, passing beyond it, the seekers grasped and expounded the Jadd. The teaching is that with every Imam there abides the Jadd: it remains above him as his hujjat. It stands above the Imam — above every Imam, indeed above all the twenty-four Imams — abiding as the hujjat of each. The Jadd came down, in the earlier degree, from Asas to Asas, and in the barzakh it had come from the Asas. The Jadd is the `Lisān al-Ḥikmah`, the Tongue of Wisdom, and it is likewise the `Lisān al-Imāmah`, the Tongue of the Imamate. This is the faith of the first origins (`al-aṣālat al-awwalīn`) that does not let the seeker halt but draws him in this upward direction. Over against the Tongue of Wisdom stands the `Lisān al-Sharīʻah`, the Tongue of the Law — the tongue of the religious law by which the soul is reformed. The two tongues thus mark two registers of guidance: in the `Lisān al-Ḥikmah` is the Jadd, the Tongue of Wisdom and of the Imamate, which draws the spirit upward to its origins; and in the `Lisān al-Sharīʻah` the believer's soul is set right and reformed through the prescriptions of the sacred law. The seekers, the text adds, set a balance and recorded these realities, establishing them by other names in their own special books, with the questioner, the Daʻi, established in the barzakh.

### The Third Intellect's Error and His Fall to the Tenth Rank

The discourse now turns — opening the fourth `faṣl` of the third `bāb` from the *Mabdaʼ al-Awwal* — to the central drama of the descent: the error of the Third Intellect and his fall. The text reads: `thumma innahu shaba al-thālith al-munqaʻna ʻanhu bi-al-inbiʻāth al-thānī al-tālī li-al-inbiʻāth al-awwal lammā takhaṭṭaʼahu wa ẓanna annahu wa iyyāhu fī ḥāl al-musāwāt wa inqaṭaʻat ʻanhu maddatun azlamat dhātuhu`. The Third Intellect — the third likeness (`shibh al-thālith`), which was given the name of the Second Emanation (`al-inbiʻāth al-thānī`), the Subsequent (`al-tālī`) that followed upon the First Emanation (`al-inbiʻāth al-awwal`) — committed an error. The word `takhaṭṭaʼahu` derives from `khaṭaʼ`, error; and the pronoun "him" (`hu`) in it refers to the first-sent (`mubʻath al-awwal`), the First Emanation. `Lammā takhaṭṭaʼahu` — "when he erred concerning him" — that is, the Third Intellect committed an error regarding the First Emanation. He thought that he and the First Emanation stood in a state of equality (`musāwāt`); he imagined himself the equal of the rank above him. By reason of this presumption his supply (`maddah`) was cut off from him; and when the supply was severed, his essence became dark (`aẓlamat dhātuhu`).

`Wa buʻida ʻanhu wa ṣāra ʻāshiran fī ufuq al-ʻaql al-tāsiʻ` — "and he was distanced from him, and he became the Tenth in the horizon of the Ninth Intellect." Having been distanced from the First Emanation, he descended to become the Tenth (`ʻāshir`), set in the horizon of the Ninth Intellect — that is, below it. `Huwa wa man fī ḏimnihi baʻda an kāna thālithan` — "he and whoever was within his charge, after he had been the Third." For he did not fall alone; he and the great company within his charge descended with him, after he had stood third in the order. The mechanism of this fall is precisely traced: even in his state of heedlessness, even after the darkness had overcome him, his circle was still established. In the horizon of the Ninth Intellect his own circle was formed; after the nine forms had run their course, he became the Tenth, emerging from his own essence as the tenth form once the nine had ended. Thus the one who had been Third became Tenth, and a vast company within his charge fell with him into that darkened station.

### The Awakening, the Repentance, and the Intercession of the Seven

`Wa naẓara ilā inḥiṭāṭ rutbatihi wa buʻdihi min ʻillatihi` — "and he looked upon the lowness of his rank and his distance from his cause." The ʻashir, in the horizon of the Ninth Intellect, beheld how far his station had declined. The nine spheres that had been his companions attained the Second Perfection (`kamāl al-thānī`), but he did not; he saw the decline of his own rank while they rose, and he perceived his distance from his cause — a distance that was the consequence of his cause. `Fa-astayqaẓa ʻan al-ghaflah` — "so he awoke from heedlessness." Then he awoke from the slumber of his negligence. `Wa ʻalima annahu qad waqaʻa fī al-khaṭīʼah wa al-zillah` — "and he knew that he had fallen into error and into a slip." He came to know with certainty that he had fallen into error, that he had stumbled into a lapse. `Wa taʼammala mā al-sababu al-mūjiba li-dhālik` — "and he contemplated what was the cause that necessitated this." He reflected upon the cause: why had the nine attained the Second Perfection while he did not? `Wa mā al-muqīʻ lahu fīmā hunālik` — "and what it was that had cast him down into that condition." He pondered what had brought him into the state in which he now found himself.

`Wa aqbala ʻalā aqrab al-ʻuqūl ilayhi munājiyan` — "and he turned toward the nearest of the intellects to him, supplicating." He turned toward the nearest intellect — the Ninth, the very one against whom his error had been committed — in earnest supplication. `Wa li-raḥmatihi ṭāliban wa rājiyan` — "seeking His mercy and hoping for it." Then the nearest intellect, the Ninth, reminded him: `fa-dhakkarahu tazkiratan` — "and it reminded him with a reminder" — recalling to him that he had transgressed his proper boundary. The verb here is precise: `dhakara` means "he remembered," but `dhakkara` means "he reminded"; it was the Ninth Intellect that reminded the ʻashir of his trespass. `Wa inkāruhu ḥaddahu al-sābiq ʻalayhi kāna min istikbārihi idh jahila wa istakbara` — "and his denial of his preceding limit that was over him came from his arrogance, since he was ignorant and grew arrogant." His denial of the rank that preceded and stood above him sprang from arrogance (`istikbar`) joined to ignorance.

Then came the turning of the heart. `Fa-aʻtarafa bi-khaṭīʼatihi wa aqarra bi-zallatihi` — "so he confessed his error and acknowledged his slip." `Wa akhadha wa tāba wa istakāna wa anāba` — "and he took it upon himself, and repented (`tāba`), and became humble (`istakāna`), and turned penitently (`anāba`)." He grew humble and lowly, a supplicant brought to wretchedness before the rank he had denied. `Wa tawassala bi-jamīʻi al-sābiqīn ʻalayhi ilā rabbihi` — "and he sought intercession through all those who preceded him, to his Lord." He sought the mediation of every rank above him, directing his plea, through them, to his Lord — and his Lord here is the First Intellect (`ʻAql al-Awwal`), the ultimate term toward whom the whole ascent of supplication is directed, the one being addressed being the highest. `Wa astaghfarahu` — "and he sought forgiveness from Him." `Fa-ghufira lahu jamīʻu dhunūbihi` — "so all his sins were forgiven him." Every one of his sins was forgiven; for indeed, `inna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīm`, "indeed Allah is Forgiving, Merciful" — when the servant performs the single act of seeking forgiveness, God performs two: He forgives, and He sends mercy.

### The Restoration of the Tenth to the Second Perfection

The mercy of the abstract intellects then descended upon the fallen one. `Wa ḥannat hādhihi al-ʻuqūl al-mujarradah ʻalayhi` — "and these abstract intellects showed tenderness toward him." The verb `ḥannat` derives from `ḥanna`, which means to show kindness, compassion, and tenderness; it is in the feminine form because it agrees with `ʻuqūl`, the intellects. These abstract intellects, then, inclined toward the ʻashir in compassion. `Wa ramat bi-ashiʻʻatihā ilayhi raḥmatan lahu` — "and they cast their rays toward him as a mercy to him." The word `ashiʻʻah` is the plural of `shuʻāʻ`, a ray. The intellects flung their rays upon his person out of mercy for him — `wa raʼfatan wa miḥnānan bihi min abnāʼi jinsihā`, "and out of tenderness and affection for him, from the children of their own kind." For the ʻashir, though fallen, was of their own genus; and so, as soon as he repented, the whole of the luminous beings turned their rays toward him.

`Fa-ittaṣalat bihi al-mawāʼid al-quṣiyyah wa al-taʼyīdāt al-ʻulwiyyah` — "so the distant promises and the higher supports connected with him." The far-reaching graces and the elevated reinforcements joined themselves to him. `Fa-ashraqat dhātuhu ishrāqan kulliyyan` — "so his essence shone with a total illumination." His darkened essence, which had been dimmed when his supply was cut off, now blazed with a complete radiance. `Wa tajarrada min tilka al-ẓulmati ʻaqlan nūrāniyyan` — "and he was stripped of that darkness, becoming a luminous intellect." He was severed from the darkness and made a luminous intellect once more. `Fa-balagha rutbat al-kamāl al-thānī` — "so he reached the rank of the Second Perfection." When the supports arrived, he attained the kamāl al-thānī that he had lost — and this attainment belongs to the ʻashir. `Wa antaẓama fī silk al-ʻālam al-rūḥānī` — "and he was set in order within the string of the spiritual world," strung once more among the luminous beings. `Wa ṣāra min ahl al-ʻiṣmah wa maqāmāt al-raḥmah rājiʻan ilā nafsihi qāʼinan min jumlat ʻālam al-ṣafāʼ wa qudsihi` — "and he became of the people of infallibility (`ʻiṣmah`) and the stations of mercy, returning to his self, abiding among the world of purity and its sanctity." He attained infallibility and the stations of mercy, and returning to his self — his "self" here being the First Intellect — he took his place among the world of purity and holiness.

### The Reassured Soul: the Qurʼan of the Tenth Intellect

This restored station of the ʻashir is precisely what is symbolized in the Qurʼan, `mā rumiza bihi fī al-kitāb al-karīm min qawlihi samīʻin ʻalīm`, "what is symbolized in the Noble Book, from the saying of the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing" — and this symbol is for the ʻashir. The verse is: `yā ayyatuhā al-nafsu al-muṭmaʼinnah` — "O reassured soul." The word `muṭmaʼinnah` derives from `iṭmiʼnān`, reassurance, whose root is the three letters `ṭā-mīm-nūn`; from this come `iṭmaʼanna, yaṭmaʼinnu, muṭmaʼinnun`, all bearing the sense of tranquil assurance. The address `yā ayyatuhā` is in the feminine form — not `ayyuhā` but `ayyatuhā` — because `nafs` in Arabic is feminine. `Yā ayyatuhā al-nafsu al-muṭmaʼinnatu irjiʻī ilā rabbiki` — "O reassured soul, return to your Lord." The verb `irjiʻī`, "return," is likewise feminine — not the masculine `irjaʻ` — and it is a command addressed to a female soul, who is to return to her Lord (`rabbiki`, "your Lord," again feminine).

Who speaks this verse? It is the saying of the First Intellect (`ʻAql al-Awwal`). From the place where the command descends — the circle of oneness, from the ʻAql al-Awwal — the verse comes down, addressed to the ʻashir. Earlier the text had spoken of his "returning to his self" (`rājiʻan ilā nafsihi`), his return to his own self; but now, after his sins were forgiven, he is commanded to return to his Lord — `irjiʻī ilā rabbiki`. `Rāḍiyatan marḍiyyah` — "well-pleased and well-pleasing." The reassured soul is herself `rāḍiyah`, pleased with her Lord; and she is also `marḍiyyah`, well-pleasing — the one with whom the other, her Lord, is also pleased. She is content, and her Lord is content with her: the ʻashir is glad, and all the higher spheres, the First Emanation among them, are glad at his repentance. `Rāḍiyatan marḍiyyatan fadkhulī fī ʻibādī wa adkhulī jannatī` — "well-pleased and well-pleasing, so enter among My servants and enter My Paradise." Then she is bidden to enter among His servants and to enter His Paradise (`jannah`). And the Paradise named here is the circle of the First Emanation (`mubʻath al-awwal`): his circle is the Paradise into which the restored ʻashir is admitted.

### The Tenth as the Spiritual Adam and the Words from his Lord

`Wa huwa idhan Ādam al-rūḥānī al-mukhbaru ʻanhu bi-āyi al-kitāb baʻda tawbatihi wa iyābihi` — "and he is, then, the spiritual Adam, of whom the verses of the Book give tidings, after his repentance and his return." The ʻashir is thus the spiritual Adam (`Ādam al-rūḥānī`); the Qurʼanic story of Adam, in one of its meanings, points to the ʻashir, and the ʻashir is intended by it. He is the one of whom the verses of the Book bring tidings, after his repentance (`tawbah`) and his return (`iyāb`). And the verse follows: `fa-talaqqā Ādamu min rabbihi kalimātin fa-tāba ʻalayhi innahu huwa al-tawwābu al-raḥīm` — "Then Adam received from his Lord words, and He relented toward him; indeed He is the Accepter of repentance, the Merciful." Adam received `kalimāt`, words, from his Lord, and thereupon his repentance was accepted; for He is the `Tawwāb`, the Accepter of repentance, and the `Raḥīm`, the Merciful — and when a person repents, God both accepts the repentance and sends down mercy.

And what are these `kalimāt`? They are the abstract intellects, the seven. The words that Adam received from his Lord are the intercession of the seven intellects: the kalimāt are these seven abstract intellects whose mediation the spiritual Adam received, and through whose intercession his repentance was accepted. So the verse, read in the supernal sense, recounts how the fallen ʻashir, the spiritual Adam, was raised again through the intercession of the seven luminous beings, which are the "words" he received from his Lord.

### The First Intellect as the True Word, and his Two Worships

A crucial distinction must now be drawn, lest the seven be confused with the one who is the Word in truth. The seven are called `kalimāt` only by attribution to the First Intellect. For `wa huwa al-mawṣūf bihā dūna ʻālam al-ʻaql` — "and he is the one described by this name, to the exclusion of the world of intellect." The true Word, the `Kalimah` and the `Kalimatullāh`, is the First Intellect alone; the rest are named "words" only because they are attributed to him. `Alladhī bihi qāma al-ʻālam li-nuṭqihi bi-kalimat al-ikhlāṣ qabla kulli nāṭiq` — "by whom the world was established, on account of his uttering the word of sincerity before every speaker." The world was established through the First Intellect's utterance of the `kalimat al-ikhlāṣ`, the word of sincerity — the `kalimah lā ilāha illā Allāh` — which he was the first to pronounce, `qabla kulli nāṭiq`, before every speaker. `Wa huwa shahādatuhu li-mubdiʼihi fī ibdāʼihi` — "and that is his testimony for his Originator in His origination." This utterance is his witness borne to his Originator in the very act of his being originated.

And here the discourse makes a weighty point concerning the two worships of the First Intellect. `Wa hiya fī al-ḥaqīqah ʻibādatuhu al-ʻamaliyyah` — "and that, in reality, is his practical worship." The uttering of the kalimah, the giving of testimony, the speaking of the word of sincerity — this is his practical worship (`ʻibādah ʻamaliyyah`). `Wa ammā ʻibādatuhu al-ʻilmiyyah fa-mā wujida ʻalayhi min mubdiʼihi al-taʻālā min ilāhiyyah wa waḥdāniyyah` — "and as for his gnostic worship, it is that which came into existence for him from his Exalted Originator, of divinity and oneness." His gnostic worship (`ʻibādah ʻilmiyyah`) is the very content that came to be for him from his Originator — the knowledge of His divinity and His oneness; it is his thought, his inner contemplation. So the distinction is twofold: what the First Intellect uttered is his practical worship, and what he thought and knew of his Originator's divinity is his gnostic worship. This is an indication toward contemplation: knowledge and action. The teacher draws the parallel to our own condition, where knowledge and action are likewise distinct, though for us they are sundered: we perform the ritual prayer (`namāz`), and that is our practical worship; and the awe and humility (`khushūʻ` and `khuḍūʻ`) we bring before Allah within it is our gnostic worship. Between these two worships there is a clear order of rank: the gnostic worship is the more noble (`ashraf`), and the practical worship is the lower (`adwan`). The inner knowledge of the divine oneness is nobler than its outward utterance.

`Wa li-dhālika khuṣṣa bi-ism al-kalimah dūna ghayrihi min al-ʻuqūl al-tāliyah` — "and for this reason he was singled out by the name of the Word, to the exclusion of the following intellects." Because the First Intellect was the first to make the Word manifest, and because the gnostic and practical conditions of the Word originated from him, he alone is named `Kalimah`. `Wa summat hādhihi al-ʻuqūl al-sabʻa bi-al-kalimāt istiʻāratan minhu lahum bi-sabab iḍāfatihim ilayhi kamā usmū bi-ʻālam al-ʻaql wa ʻālam al-ibdāʻ wa ʻālam al-quds li-iḍāfatihim ilayhi` — "and these seven intellects were named 'words' by way of metaphor borrowed from him for them, on account of their attribution to him, just as they were named the World of Intellect, the World of Origination, and the World of Sanctity on account of their attribution to him." The seven are "words" only metaphorically (`istiʻāratan`), by attribution; just as the World of Intellect, the World of Origination, and the World of Sanctity all belong in truth to the First Intellect, and the seven bear those names only because they are attributed to him. The true World of Intellect is the world of the First Intellect; the World of Origination is his; the World of Sanctity is his; and all the rest are attributed to him — for were he not, there would be nothing at all. The First Intellect is not attributed to them; they are attributed to him. `Wa bi-ikhtiṣāṣihi bi-hādhihi al-asmāʼ dūna mā siwāhu li-anna al-mafḍūla yuḍāfu ilā al-fāḍil wa al-nāqiṣa yunsabu ilā al-ashhar al-kāmil` — "and by his being singled out with these names to the exclusion of all besides him, because the inferior is attributed to the superior, and the deficient is related to the more renowned, the perfect." For it is the rule that the lesser (`mafḍūl`) is referred to the greater (`fāḍil`), and the deficient (`nāqiṣ`) to the perfect and more renowned (`al-ashhar al-kāmil`).

### The Surpassing Pre-eminence of the First Intellect

`Wa hādhihi al-ʻuqūl al-mujarradah wa in kānat qad balaghat al-ghāyah` — "and these abstract intellects, even though they have reached the utmost limit" of virtue, nobility, splendor, and perfection — `fa-inna al-awwal qad fāqahā wa taʻālāhā wa sāmāhā bi-sabaqiyatihi lahā wa fiʻlihi wa ʻibādatihi wa ʻilmihi wa ʻamalihi` — "yet indeed the First has surpassed them and risen above them and towered over them, by his precedence over them and his action and his worship and his knowledge and his deed." However far the abstract intellects ascend, the First Intellect remains above them, surpassing them through five excellences: his precedence (`sabaqiyyah`), his action, his worship, his knowledge, and his deed. And the principle is absolute: however much they advance, they can never surpass the one who is above them. The treasures of Allah suffer no deficiency; therefore, as time passes without end, the joy and bounty of the First Intellect will keep increasing forever — and even as the joy and bounty of those in the lower spheres of his circle increase, his own bounties increase yet more. The pre-eminence of the First Intellect is thus not a fixed quantity but an ever-widening one, perpetually outpacing all that lies beneath it.

This pre-eminence calls forth gratitude, and the teacher recalls the words of praise: `al-ḥamdu lillāhi alladhī manna ʻalaynā bimā ḥarramahu ghayranā wa jaʻalanā min ḥamalati ʻilmihi wa khuzzāna sirrihi al-maknūn` — "all praise is for Allah, who has bestowed favor upon us with that which He withheld from others, and made us among the bearers of His knowledge and the treasurers of His hidden secret." The verb `manna`, "He bestowed favor," must be read with care; were its gemination (`tashdīd`) omitted, the unwary might read it as `min` ("from") or `man` ("who"), and the meaning would be lost. The imperative `munna ʻalaynā`, "bestow favor upon us," appears in the supplication of ʻArafah — `fa-munna ʻalā faqīrinā` — and it is from this same root. He bestowed favor upon us with what He withheld from others, and made us among the bearers of His knowledge (`ḥamalati ʻilmihi`) and the treasurers of His hidden secret (`khuzzāna sirrihi al-maknūn`). `Wa ṣallā Allāhu ʻalā nabiyyihi wa ālihi al-ṭāhirīn` — "and may Allah's blessings be upon His Prophet and his pure progeny."

### The Three Sects Distanced with the Tenth, and the Birth of Iblis

The discourse turns finally to those who fell with the ʻashir and did not rise with him. Opening the fourth `bāb` from the *Mabdaʼ al-Awwal*, which gathers five chapters (`fuṣūl`), in its first chapter the text proceeds: having reached in its speech the discourse upon the World of Origination, it now follows that with the discourse upon the World of the Abstract (`ʻālam al-ajrad`) and the beginning of its coming-to-be and the coming-to-be of what follows it. Then, after the supplication, it returns to the fall: `innahu kāna qad inʻazala fī ẓill al-mubʻath al-thānī` — "indeed he had been separated in the shadow of the Second Emanation." When the ʻashir was separated in the shadow of the Second Emanation, he became the Tenth, like a group of spirits. `Wa baʻudat bi-buʻdihi` — "and they were distanced by his distance." Those who were distanced by his distance did not attain the Second Perfection; they were of his company, but upon beholding him, they moved away and acted as he had acted.

`Wa hiya munqasimatun ʻalā thalāthat firaqin` — "and it is divided into three sects." The great company distanced with the ʻashir divided into three sects (`firaq`). These three are not independent of the ʻashir but connected to him; in his group there were three types of people, and what follows describes them. And all of this was before the ʻashir's repentance.

The first sect: `firqatun nafati al-ilāhiyyah ʻanhā wa abnāʼi jinsihā wa athbatahā li-mubdiʼihim al-taʻālā` — "a sect that negated divinity from itself and from its own kind, and affirmed it for its Exalted Originator." This sect negated divinity from itself and from its own kind and affirmed it for the Originator — just as the ʻashir had done, performing glorification (`tasbīḥ`) and sanctification (`taqdīs`) of the Originator. But `wa takhaṭṭaʼati al-mubʻath al-awwal wa jahilathu kamā takhaṭṭaʼathu` — "and they erred concerning the First Emanation and were ignorant of it, just as he had erred concerning it." Like the ʻashir, they erred regarding the First Emanation and were ignorant of it. This was not the ʻashir's private perception alone; everyone within his spheres shared in it. A very great company acted as he did, `mustamliyatan ʻanhu` — "deriving it from him," taking the matter from the ʻashir — `mushabbihatan bihi fī subūqihi`, "imitating him in his precedence," exercising their own choice just as the ʻashir had exercised his. `Idh huwa ḥadduhā wa hiya maḥdūduhu` — "since he is their limit, and they are his bounded ones." And this first sect is identified with the spouse of the spiritual Adam: `wa hiya zawjuhu wa quwwatuhu al-munfaʻilah` — "and she is his spouse and his receptive faculty." She is his pair, the wife of Adam — `Ḥawwāʼ` — who did as the ʻashir had done: she acknowledged the Originator and glorified the First Intellect, yet erred regarding the First Emanation, exactly as he had. `Al-muḥtawiyyah ʻalā mā ḥawāhu min al-taṣawwur wa al-iʻtiqād wa al-tafakkur` — "the one who contains what he contained, of conception, belief, and contemplation." She encompassed all that the ʻashir encompassed: his conception (`taṣawwur`), his belief (`iʻtiqād`), and his contemplation (`tafakkur`) were hers as well. `Wa al-muzāwajatu lahu fī dhālik` — "and being his partner in that." `Wa huwa Ādam al-rūḥānī kamā sabaqa bihi al-qawl` — "and he is the spiritual Adam, as the discourse has already established." And this is what the Noble Book symbolizes in the saying of Allah the Exalted: `wa qulnā yā Ādamu uskun anta wa zawjuka al-jannata wa kulā minhā raghadan ḥaythu shiʼtumā wa lā taqrabā hādhihi al-shajarata fa-takūnā min al-ẓālimīn` — "And We said, 'O Adam, dwell, you and your wife, in Paradise and eat therefrom in ease and abundance wherever you will, but do not approach this tree, lest you be of the wrongdoers.'" This was the first sect, the spouse of the spiritual Adam, his receptive faculty.

The second sect: `wa al-firqat al-thāniyah aqarrat bi-al-mubdaʼi al-taʻālā wa tarakati al-ʻaql al-awwal wa al-inbiʻāth al-awwal wa ẓannat annahā wa iyyāhumā fī ḥāl al-musāwāt wa al-mumāthalah` — "and the second sect affirmed the Exalted Originator, but left the First Intellect and the First Emanation, and thought that they and those two stood in a state of equality and likeness." This sect affirmed the Exalted Originator but "left" — that is, denied the precedence of — the First Intellect and the First Emanation. They held all the intellects to be equal; they saw the state of the First Perfection and denied the Second. They imagined that they themselves, together with the First Intellect and the First Emanation, were all alike in rank — equal and similar (`musāwāt wa mumāthalah`). They believe in Allah, and that Allah created them; but, denying the precedence of the highest intellects, they remain in the First Perfection (`kamāl al-awwal`) and never enter the Second.

The third sect: `wa al-firqat al-thālithah minhum man aqāmat daʻwatan fī dār al-ibdāʻ ankarat wa takabbarat wa ṣaddat wa istakbarat wa lam tuwaḥḥid mubdiʼahā wa lā iltazamat bi-aḥadin min al-ʻuqūl wa hiya ashaddu al-firaq kufran wa aʻẓamuhā surūdan wa nukran` — "and the third sect, among them are those who established a call in the abode of origination, who denied and grew arrogant and turned away and waxed proud, and did not affirm the oneness of their Originator, nor adhered to any of the intellects; and it is the most severe of the sects in disbelief, and the greatest of them in opposition and denial." This third sect established a call (`daʻwah`) in the Abode of Origination; they denied, grew arrogant, blocked the way, and acted with pride. They did not affirm the oneness of their Originator, and they adhered to none of the intellects — neither the nine nor the seven. They said that Allah does not exist, claiming, "We created ourselves" — that they had brought themselves into being from nothing. They are therefore the most severe of all the sects in disbelief (`kufr`), and the greatest in opposition and denial. And from this third sect, the last sect, the point that denied God altogether — from it Iblis was born. Each of these three new sects, in turn, has its own spheres and its own point (`nuqṭah`), and the multiplication of these darkened worlds runs beyond measure; but it is from the last and most arrogant of them, the sect of total denial, that Iblis came forth.

## The Descent of the Tenth Intellect and the First Cohort

### The Obligation of Deliverance: "He Who Breaks a Bone Must Mend It"

When repentance and turning toward God arose from the Tenth Intellect — established, as the text affirms, by his own word and confession — the consequence was not a private absolution but a public commission. The Originator did not simply forgive the penitent intellect and leave the three fallen groups to their fate; rather, by His decree the deliverance (khalas) of those groups was made obligatory upon the Tenth Intellect himself. This is the force of the line as it stands in the more reliable copies, where the verb is rendered in the active voice: `alzamahum al-mubdi'u` — "the Originator obligated them," that is, He made the rescue of these groups binding upon him. He bound the Tenth Intellect to the task of their deliverance and charged him with it, `wa kallafahu`, "and he was tasked."

The logic of this obligation is moral and exact. The Tenth Intellect was not a bystander to the error of the three groups; he had been their chief, their head, the one to whom they looked. And he was, in a real sense, a cause of the error of some of them — `wa sababan li-zalali ba'dihim`, "and a cause for the error of some of them." Their misconception did not arise in a vacuum; it took its shape, in part, from his own conception and his own belief. The text is precise here: `wa huwa al-mutalabu lahum 'ala tasawwurihi wa i'tiqadihi` — "and he is the one held responsible for them, on account of his conception and his belief." Because the deviation of the followers traced back to the misconceived station of the one who led them, justice required that the same one undertake their repair.

It is for exactly this reason that the maxim is cited: `wa li-dhalik qila: man kasara 'azman fa-'alayhi jabruhu` — "and for that reason it was said: he who breaks a bone, upon him is its mending." The image is drawn from the broken limb: the one whose act fractured the bone is the one upon whom the obligation of setting it falls. The Tenth Intellect had, by his arrogated claim and the equality-error it seeded, broken something in the spiritual order of those who followed him; the mending of that fracture, therefore, was laid upon him as a debt of justice — the principle of jabr, restitution, that he who causes a breaking owes its repair.

### Restoration to the First Intellect's Station, and the Three Groups Addressed

To equip the Tenth Intellect for this work of deliverance, the Originator restored him to a station of authority. He was established among the three fallen groups in precisely the rank that the First Intellect had held in the world of origination: `wa uqima fi ma fihim maqama man kana awwalan fi 'alam al-ibda'` — "and he was set among them in the station of the one who was first in the world of origination." Just as the First Intellect had stood at the foremost station in the world of origination, the inaugurating and presiding rank, so now the Tenth Intellect was placed at the equivalent foremost station among the very groups he was sent to rescue. His position over them became, in their fallen world, the analogue of the First Intellect's primacy in the world of pure origination. And from that station the command was issued: `fa-umira bi-khalasihim` — "so he was commanded for their deliverance."

It is worth marking who these three groups were, for their error defines the whole drama. The first group acknowledged the Originator, exalted is He, and acknowledged the First Intellect — and crucially acknowledged that the First Intellect is distinct from and dependent upon the Originator, recognizing the First Intellect's true subordinate right. That recognition is what set the first group apart. The second and third groups, by contrast, fell into error of degree. One held that the Originator and the First Intellect were equal — the equality-doctrine, the claim that the two stand on the same level with no precedence between them. And the third group committed the gravest disbelief of all, the most severe kufr: it denied Allah outright. These were the three cohorts now arrayed before the restored Tenth Intellect, and to them he was sent.

So he addressed them: `fa-khataba hadhihi al-firaq al-thalathah` — "so he addressed these three groups." He called them to the truth, summoned them to recognize the right order. But they refused. `Fa-nafaru wa zajarahum` — "but they refused, and he rebuked them." He rebuked them and sought to restrain them from the false conviction they clung to (zajara meaning to restrain or halt, inzajara to be restrained), yet they would not be halted. He admonished them further, counseled them — `wa wa'azahum` — but `fa-saddu wa istakbaru`, "they turned away and were arrogant." They met his counsel with arrogance and with a hardening rejection. And in consequence `wa izdadu zulmatan` — "and they increased in darkness." Their refusal did not leave them where they were; it thickened them, multiplying the darkness that already clouded them.

Only after admonition had been exhausted and they still would not stop did the sentence fall: `fa-istahaqqu al-hubut wa al-tasafful wa al-suqut` — "so they deserved the descent, the degradation, and the fall." Three terms name the single ruin: hubut, the descent from their station; tasafful, the degradation to a lower and baser condition; and suqut, the fall outright. The order of events matters: it was the warning and the admonition that came first, and only when these were spurned was the command of descent issued. Their fall was not arbitrary but earned, the deserved end of an obstinacy that had been given every chance to relent.

### The Three Motions and the Birth of the Dimensions

With the sentence of descent the groups began to move, and from their motion the very dimensions of the cosmos were born. As they fell they did not remain discrete; they mixed and interpenetrated, exerting influences upon one another. `Wa imtazaja ba'duhum bi-ba'din` — "and some of them mixed with others" — colliding, this one's conviction pressing against that one's, contending and commingling. `Wa tarakamu ka-tarakum al-ghuyum wa al-sahab` — "and they accumulated like the accumulation of clouds and vapors." The image is exact: as banks of cloud pile and merge into a single accumulating mass, so the fallen groups heaped and mingled together into one accumulating matter.

From this commingled mass the first motion arose. `Wa taharraku min zawatihim` — "and they moved from their own selves." The movement issued from their own essences, an intrinsic agitation, and from that first motion length became necessary: `lazima biha al-tul` — "length became necessary from it." Because they moved, extension in one direction — length — was the inevitable consequence of the motion. And this first motion had a definite course, a beginning and an end. `Wa kana ibtida' hadhihi al-harakah hararatan wa tali'an wa hamalan` — "and the beginning of this motion was heat, and the rising point, and Aries (al-hamal)." It commenced in heat, at the ascendant. `Wa muntahaha burudatan wa ghariban wa mizanan` — "and its end was coldness, and the setting point, and Libra (al-mizan)." The heat, as it travels its course, diminishes and diminishes until it turns toward coldness; the end of the first motion is coldness. Thus along with length there came into being four constellations marking the span of this motion: tali' (the rising point), hamal (Aries), gharib (the setting point), and mizan (Libra). These four are the constellations created together with the first dimension.

Then the second motion stirred. `Thumma taharrakat harakatan ukhra thaniyatan lazima biha al-'ard` — "then it moved another, second, motion, from which breadth became necessary." After length came breadth; the second motion produced extension in the second direction. `Wa kana awwalaha rutubatan wa 'ashirun wa jadyun` — "and its beginning was moisture, and Capricorn (al-jady)." This second motion began in moisture, in the sign of Capricorn. `Wa muntahaha rabi'an wa yubusatan wa sartanan` — "and its end was Cancer (al-sartan), and dryness." It ran from moisture at its outset to dryness at its terminus, ending in the sign of Cancer. So breadth, like length, was bounded by its own constellations, the second dimension carved out between Capricorn and Cancer.

And then a third motion came, but this one was different in its impulse. The first two motions had simply unfolded length and breadth upon the fallen groups; but the very imposition of these dimensions bred in the groups a kind of distress, an alienation, a longing to escape the condition that length and breadth had stretched upon them. `Thumma taharraku harakatan thalithatan talabu an yatadaraku biha zawatahum` — "then they moved a third motion, seeking to remedy their own selves with it." They sought, by this third motion, to overtake and repair themselves, to recover from the strain of their stretched-out state. But the attempt did not lift them out; it carried them deeper: `wa waqa'u fi al-'umq` — "and they fell into the depth." The third motion, sought as a remedy, produced instead the third dimension, depth, plunging them into it.

None of these three motions was random or self-willed in the groups; all three were directed. `Wa kanat hadhihi al-harakat bi-qasdin min 'inayat ilahiyyah ka-'aql al-'ashir` — "and these motions were by an intention from divine grace, like the Tenth Intellect." Allah set them in motion by His own special providence, His intentional grace ('inayat ilahiyyah). The first motion, the second, and the third alike were not the groups' own initiative but the deliberate working of divine grace operating through the Tenth Intellect, who was the one entrusted with them and charged over them — `al-muwakkal bihim` — and who therefore directed them with motion: `fa-qasadahum bi-al-harakah`, "so He intended toward them with motion." The Tenth Intellect, as the appointed steward of their deliverance, was the conduit through which this intentional grace moved them.

### The Necessity of the Da'wah and the Logic of the Test

Behind these motions lay a settled purpose in the thought of the Tenth Intellect, and the text now unfolds it. There established itself in his mind a clear conviction about how the fallen could possibly be saved: `li ma qama fi fikrihi an Allah khalaqa min da'watihi al-hadiyah ila umurihi al-marziyyah dhalika al-shay' al-mutarakib min da'watihi al-hanifiyyah wa ma sharakahum fihi al-ashbah al-suwariyyah` — "because of what stood firm in his thought, that Allah created, from his guiding call to His pleasing affairs, that composite thing fashioned from his upright (Hanafiyyah) call, and that in which the imaginal forms had a share with them."

What had become firm in his thought was this: after such a slip, such pride, such rejection and densification, there was no deliverance for the fallen except by their coming into Hanafiyyah human forms — forms made to resemble the imaginal forms (al-ashbah al-suwariyyah). The human personalities of the Hanafiyyah dispensation were to be made the counterparts of those imaginal forms. The relation is exact and one-directional: it is not that the imaginal forms were modeled on these human personalities, but that the human being now to be created was fashioned in the likeness of the imaginal forms. And the reason the human form was chosen as the vessel of deliverance is given plainly: `li-kawni shakliha afdal al-ashkal wa ashrafaha` — "because its form is the best of forms and the noblest of them." The human form is the highest and most honorable of all forms, and only in the noblest form could the fallen find their way back.

But such forms alone would not suffice; deliverance required an instrument, a summoning. `Wa la sabila ila dhalika illa bi-qa'im da'watin mumathilatin li-al-da'wat al-qa'imah fi al-ibda'` — "and there is no way to that except through the establisher of a call (da'wah) resembling the call established in the world of origination." There had to be a da'wah, a summons to truth, raised in this fallen world to mirror the da'wah that the First Intellect had established in the world of origination. Just as the call that summoned all the forms had been established in origination from the First Intellect, so a corresponding call had to be established here.

Yet the two da'wahs are not identical, and the difference is the whole point. `Wa lan yakuna dhalika illa bi-makanin wa zamanin wa shuja'atin 'azimatin wa imtihanin` — "and that will not be except with place, and time, and a great trial, and a test." The original da'wah in the world of origination had been without place and without time, a summons in pure unconditioned being. This new da'wah, the da'wah of deliverance, is bound to place and to time, and it carries with it a great ordeal and a testing (imtihan) that the original never had. Place, time, severe trial, and examination are the new conditions, and they exist for a reason: to sort the fallen.

`Wa hina'idhin yasdu ma taraffa'a wa safa, wa yartakisu ma dama alazza wa jafa` — "and at that time, that which has risen and become pure is separated off, and that which has remained base and harsh falls back." This is the function of the test. Through the trial, what has elevated itself and grown pure (taraffa'a wa safa) is sifted out and lifted up, while what has stayed base, clinging to its hardness and coarseness (dama alazza wa jafa), sinks back down (yartakisu). The test is the mechanism of separation, dividing the purified ascent from the obstinate residue.

It is precisely upon this logic that the maxim of the sages rests: `wa li-dhalik qalat al-hukama': al-insan awwal al-fikr wa akhir al-'amal` — "and for this reason the wise have said: man is the first of thought and the last of action." Man stands first in the order of intention — he was the very design and aim that stood firm in the Tenth Intellect's thought from the outset — yet last in the order of execution, for the whole long unfolding of the cosmos, the motions and the densities and the spheres, all serve finally to bring man into being as the goal in which the work culminates. He is first conceived and last accomplished: the first of thought, the last of action.

### The Sorting into Form and Matter, Male and Female

The text now turns to what became of the accumulated, commingled mass once it had taken on its three dimensions. `Thumma inna dhalika al-shay' lamma tarakama ba'duhu fawqa ba'd wa imtazaja ba'duhu bi-ba'd, sara hayula wa surah` — "then indeed that thing, when some of it had accumulated upon other parts and some had mixed with others, became prime matter (hayula) and form (surah)." Out of the single mingled mass two principles emerged: the form and the underlying matter. And the division between them followed the inner quality of the constituents. Within the mixture, the parts that had been superior — those that had held correct belief, whose influence and effect were the greater — rose to become the form, while the parts that were weaker, of lesser effect, sank to become the matter that the form would act upon.

The text states the criterion exactly: `fa-al-surah ashaffuhu wa altafuhu, wa al-hayula aghmazuhu wa akthafuhu` — "so the form is its most transparent and its most subtle, and the prime matter is its most obscure and its most dense." Form is the clearest and finest portion of the commingled stuff; prime matter is its murkiest and densest portion. The intellects that had stood on sound belief, that were luminous and subtle, became the formative principle; the obscure and heavy residue became the receptive matter.

Upon this sorted stuff the divine grace then went to work: `'amilat al-'inayat al-ilahiyyah ila ikhtira' minha` — "the divine grace worked toward originating from it." From this matter-and-form the divine grace fashioned a creation, and it did so by a fundamental polarity. `Ja'alatha 'alatan fa'alatan ka-al-dhakar, wa huwa 'alam al-aflak` — "it made some of it an active instrument like the male, and that is the world of the spheres." One portion was constituted as an active, agentive instrument, on the analogy of the male — and this is the world of the spheres ('alam al-aflak), the agent that acts upon what lies below it. `Wa ashya'an minha munfa'ilatan ka-al-untha, wa huwa 'alam al-ummahat` — "and other things of it passive like the female, and that is the world of the mothers." The complementary portion was constituted as passive, receptive, on the analogy of the female — and this is the world of the mothers ('alam al-ummahat), the elemental world that receives the action of the spheres.

So the whole was divided as a male is divided from a female: the spheres taking the place of the active, generating principle, and the mothers — the elemental world — taking the place of the receptive, conceiving principle. And then everything was set in its proper order: `wa rattabat kulla shay'in minha fi mawdi'ihi al-la'iq bihi` — "and it arranged everything of it in the place befitting it." Each thing among them was disposed in the station that suited it; in the world of the mothers, whatever had to be placed wherever, was placed there exactly according to the original intention, so that the arrangement matched the design.

### The Cross of the Dimensions and the First Group Made into Spheres

Why this division should have produced the spheres in particular is explained through a geometric figure. `Wa dhalika annaha lamma lazimat al-ab'ad al-thalathah wa ijtama'at 'ala hay'at al-khutut al-salib kama sawwarnahu li-yu'ana ju'ilat al-aflak` — "and that is because, when the three dimensions had become necessary and had gathered into the figure of the lines of the cross, as we have depicted it so that it may be seen, the spheres were made." The three dimensions — length, breadth, and depth, born of the three motions — became necessary and then assembled into the form of a cross (salib): one line for length, one crossing it for breadth, and depth descending beneath their intersection. The text refers to a drawn figure of this cross, depicted so the reader can see it.

And from the purest portion of the commingled, accumulated mass, the spheres were fashioned: `ju'ilat al-aflak min asfa dhalika al-shay' al-mumtazij al-mutarakim` — "the spheres were made from the purest of that mingled, accumulated thing." Out of the whole mixture of form and matter, the part that was wholly pure and clean was drawn off, and from it the spheres were made.

`Wa hum al-firqat al-ula` — "and they are the first group." This is a crucial identification: the spheres are none other than the first of the three groups, the group that had recognized the truth. The first group was the one `al-lati wahhadat al-mubdi'a wa sabbahat al-'aql al-awwal wa qaddasathu wa i'tarafat bi-haqqih` — "which affirmed the oneness of the Originator, and glorified the First Intellect, and sanctified it, and acknowledged its right." This group had professed the Originator's oneness, had glorified and hallowed the First Intellect, and had acknowledged the First Intellect's true right and rank — its dependent precedence beneath the Originator. This was the sound creed that set the first group apart.

Yet the first group, for all its right belief, did not escape the fall, and the text explains the painful reason with great care. `Li-annaha lamma khatabaha al-'aql al-'ashir ma'a al-firqatayn al-akhiratayn ba'da tawbatihi wa tanassulihi wa taklifihi khalasahum, asarrat bi-israrihima wa istakbarat bi-istikbarihima wa inhadarat bi-inhidarihima, fa-sarat min al-kasa'if 'arifatan 'an al-lata'if` — "because, when the Tenth Intellect addressed it together with the two latter groups, after his repentance, his self-divestment, and his being charged with their deliverance, it insisted with their insistence, and was arrogant with their arrogance, and descended with their descent, so it became one of the dense things, ignorant of the subtleties."

The fall of the first group was therefore a fall by association, an entanglement. When the Tenth Intellect, after his repentance and his being tasked with deliverance, addressed all three groups together, the first group did not at that moment hold to its own sound recognition. The two erring groups insisted on the equality-doctrine — that the Tenth Intellect (standing in the First Intellect's station) and the Originator are equal, with no precedence between them — and when the Tenth Intellect summoned the groups, they answered, in effect, "No: the First and the two are equal." The first group, drawn into their insistence, insisted with them; drawn into their arrogance, was arrogant with them; and so descended along with their descent. By this entanglement it became one of the dense things (al-kasa'if), losing its awareness of the subtleties (al-lata'if), departing the world of fineness for the world of density.

But the descent of the first group was not the descent of the others, for at the very edge of the fall its true nature reasserted itself. `Sha'arat bi-ma hiya fihi wa nadimat wa talabat al-istiqalah wa al-tawbah` — "it became aware of the state it was in, and it regretted, and it sought release and repentance." As it was falling, the first group woke to its own condition; it perceived where its entanglement had brought it, was seized with regret, and begged for release and for repentance. This awakening came in the very act of falling — late, but not too late to alter its destiny.

The repentance, however, could not undo the descent already underway. `Fa-lam yumkin dhalika li-kawniha qad sarat hawiyatan fi al-inhidar` — "but that was not possible, because it had already become a plunging thing in the descent." It could no longer be restored to its original station, for it had already become a body plunging downward in the fall; the momentum of descent could not simply be reversed. And so a middle outcome was decreed: its repentance arrested its fall where it stood, halting it partway and forbidding it to plunge any lower. `Wa ju'ilat aflakan wa kawakiban wa burujan` — "and it was made into spheres, and stars, and constellations." The first group, caught between its right creed and its borrowed fall, between its late repentance and its irreversible descent, was fixed at the height it had reached and constituted as the spheres, the stars (kawakib), and the zodiacal constellations (buruj). Its repentance was answered not by full restoration but by exaltation to the luminous, celestial rank — high above the lower world, yet no longer in its original station.

### The Order of Densities and the Two Remaining Groups

From the two groups that remained — the polytheists and the atheists — the rest of the cosmos was drawn out. `Wa istukhrijat min hatayn al-firqatayn... 'an al-mushrikin wa al-mulhidin` — "and there was extracted from these two groups... from the polytheists (al-mushrikin) and the atheists (al-mulhidin)." These were the remaining two of the three: the group that had associated a partner by holding the Originator and the First Intellect to be equals, and the group that had denied Allah altogether. From the very group that had denied the creator there issued, in turn, both the polytheists and the atheists, and from these two remaining cohorts the lower order of the cosmos was extracted, the spheres of the first group having already been set above.

It is essential to grasp the sequence here, because the densification of the cosmos did not happen all at once but step by step. When the command to descend was first given, the fallen had not yet become dense. The command of descent is an order, like an admonition — and indeed it was the admonition that should have produced repentance first, but here the repentance came afterward. At the very issuing of the command "Descend!" a very large portion cried out `khata'un kallamam` — "a mistake has been made, a mistake by us" — recognizing their error before density had overtaken them. That portion's error had been upon the celestial sphere, and from them the world of life ('alam al-hayat) was manifested, prior to densification. Only after the command of descent had been given, and time had passed, did density form. And once density set in, it set in by degrees, thing after thing, producing the ordered series of the spheres.

The text frames the gradualness explicitly: the ordering of the world of spheres is like the gradual creation of a human being, `shay'an ba'da shay'in` — "thing after thing." As a man wishing to build a house first draws up a plan in his mind before laying a single stone, so the first stage of the spheres was a thing of conceptual, imaginary density rather than solid mass. The order of densities runs thus. First, the encompassing celestial sphere — the falak al-aflak, the falak al-muhit — of imaginary or conceptual density (kasafat wahmiyya); dense, yet only in the manner of a plan held in the mind, not the density of solid bodies. Then, denser than it, the sphere of the constellations (falak al-buruj). Then the seven planetary spheres. Then, denser still, the four mothers — fire, air, water, and earth. From the encompassing sphere down through this descending order, the three groups under study were arrayed, each stratum denser than the one above it: there is a difference between the dense and the extremely dense, between the less dense and the more dense, and these gradations map the descending series. It is like the parts of a single body — the feet, the heart, the muscled limbs — where each part takes the action proper to it according to the intention by which it was made.

### The Sun: First-Formed Heart of the World of Spheres

With the order of densities established, the text turns to the formation of the world of spheres in detail, and again the governing image is the gradual making of a human being. `Wa kana ibtida' tartib 'alam al-aflak ka-ibtida' khalq al-insan, shay'an ba'da shay'in` — "and the beginning of the ordering of the world of spheres was like the beginning of the creation of man, thing after thing." Just as in the human a drop of fluid first takes form, then becomes a clot of blood, then flesh, then the limbs are articulated one after another, so too the world of spheres was ordered little by little, stage upon stage.

And the first thing to be acted upon and formed in this world was the Sun. `Fa-awwaluhu munfa'ilun wa mutakawwinun minhu al-Shams` — "so the first of it to be acted upon and formed from it is the Sun." The term munfa'il means that which receives an effect or is acted upon, as against fa'il, the agent that acts. The world of spheres as a whole performs the governance (tadbir), and the Sun was the very first to receive that governance and to be formed under it. Of all the world of spheres, the first creation, the first to accept the effect, was the Sun.

The reason for the Sun's primacy is its correspondence to the heart. `Allati hiya mawsulu nuqtat al-barkar wa mawsulu qalb al-insan` — "which is connected to the point of the compass and connected to the heart of man." The Sun is the center, joined to the central point from which a compass is struck — the nuqtat al-barkar, the pivot-point of the circle. And it is connected to the heart of man: as the heart is the first-formed and central organ of the human body, so the Sun is the heart of the world of spheres, the first-formed and central member. If the world of spheres be regarded as a single great human, its heart is the Sun.

`Wa hiya markazu al-hararati al-ghariziyyati al-munba'ithati minha ila ma fawqaha min al-kawakib wa tahtaha min al-kawakib wa al-ummahat` — "and it is the center of the innate heat, which is emitted from it to the planets above it and the planets below it and the mothers." The Sun is the center of the innate, natural heat (al-hararat al-ghariziyya), and from it that heat radiates outward — upward to the three planets above it and downward to the three planets below it, and downward further still to the four mothers. The three planets above the Sun are Saturn (Zuhal), Jupiter (Mushtari), and Mars (Mirrikh); the three below it are Mercury ('Utarid), Venus (Zuhrah), and the Moon (Qamar). To all of these, and to the elemental mothers as well, the Sun's innate heat is conveyed. The Sun is reckoned the fourth of the seven planets in the ordering of the spheres, with three above and three below — yet though it is fourth in rank, its formation was first, for it is the heart, and the heart is formed before all the other organs.

`Wa hiya aslu tilka al-harkati al-awwala` — "and it is the origin of that first motion." The Sun is, moreover, the origin of the first motion — that primal motion in the world of spheres from which the motions of all else proceed, and from which, ultimately, time and space took their rise. The Sun's heat is the very source of generation; it is the cause of coming-into-existence, and from its motion all the beings that are to come into being receive their impulse. Its turning is so ordered that human souls might receive its effect and through it make their progress, passing in time through the encompassing sphere and ascending to the higher world — for this is the essential purpose of the whole apparatus. And though the Sun appears a ball of pure light, it too is made of density (kasafat); those densities will descend, take human form, and being thus embodied will then be elevated and rise upward through the encompassing sphere. The Sun revolves on its own axis at the center while the seven planets revolve around it, and the mover that turns it — as we shall see — is the encompassing sphere.

### Sun, Moon, and Saturn: The Limbs of the Absolute Body

The correspondence between the world of spheres and the human body extends beyond the heart. The text now maps three great bodies to three regions of the human frame. The Sun, as established, is connected to the heart and is the center of innate heat. `Thumma falak al-qamar alladhi huwa mawsulu al-ra's wa markazu al-rutubati wa al-burudah` — "then the sphere of the Moon, which is connected to the head and is the center of moisture and coldness." The Moon (Qamar) stands at the level of the head and is the center of moisture and coldness — the very opposite of the Sun's heat. `Thumma Zuhal alladhi huwa mawsulu al-qadamayn wa markazu al-burudati wa al-yubs` — "then Saturn, which is connected to the two feet and is the center of coldness and dryness." Saturn (Zuhal) is connected to the two feet, the lowest member, and is the center of coldness and dryness. Saturn is the lowest of the bodies, as the feet are the lowest of the limbs, and its picture, the text notes, is accordingly placed at the bottom.

`Thumma istamarra baqi dhalika shay'an ba'da shay'in hatta sara ka-al-jismi al-wahid` — "then the rest of it continued, thing after thing, until it became like a single body." The remaining members were ordered in the same gradual way, one after another, until the whole world of spheres became like one single body. `Wa yuqalu lahu al-jismu al-mutlaqu muqabilun li-jismi al-insan` — "and it is called the Absolute Body, the counterpart of the human body." This entire integrated world of spheres bears the name al-jism al-mutlaq, the Absolute Body, and it stands as the macrocosmic counterpart to the body of the human being: heart, head, and feet answering to Sun, Moon, and Saturn, and all the rest disposed between.

### The Encompassing Sphere as the Spirit and Mover of the Absolute Body

If the world of spheres is a single body, then it must have a spirit, and the encompassing sphere is that spirit. `Wa ju'ila al-falaku al-muhitu lahu ruhan li-kawnihi altafuhu wa ashrafuhu, wa huwa al-muharriku li-hadha al-jismi al-mutlaq` — "and the encompassing sphere was made a spirit for it, on account of its being the most subtle and noble of it, and it is the mover of this Absolute Body." Because the encompassing sphere (falak al-muhit) is the most subtle and the most noble of all the spheres, it was constituted as the spirit (ruh) of the Absolute Body, and as its mover (muharrik). There is a direct link between the encompassing sphere and the world of spheres, however vast the apparent distance; the encompassing sphere is spread over the whole, and by it all the spheres are kept in motion. It is the mover of the Absolute Body, the one that imparts motion to the entire frame.

`Wa al-khaliqu lahu fi jawfihi fi kulli yawmin wa laylatin qalabatan wahidatan ilahiyyatan` — "and the Creator turns it in its core, every day and night, with a single divine turn." The Creator turns the encompassing sphere in its very interior — its jawf, its inmost belly — once in each day and night, with a single divine revolution (qalabah ilahiyya). No fixed span of years is assigned to the forming of the world of spheres or the Absolute Body, for that forming proceeded gradually, thing after thing, slowly over its long unfolding; but the great daily turning itself is a single divine cycle (dawra ilahiyya) completed once in every day and night.

`Wa huwa 'aynu al-makani wa al-usquss` — "and it is the very essence of space and the foundation." The encompassing sphere is the essence of place itself and the foundation upon which all rests; space takes its origin from the encompassing sphere, which is at once the principle of location and the underlying ground (al-usquss). As the outermost and subtlest sphere, containing all within it and contained by nothing, it is fitting that it should be the source of space and the moving spirit of the whole.

### The Reward of the Sun and the Scale of Justice

The encompassing sphere holds the first rank in nobility, and the Sun follows it. `Wa yatluhu al-Shamsu fi al-sharafi wa al-fadl` — "and the Sun follows it in nobility and virtue." Immediately after the encompassing sphere in honor and excellence comes the Sun, and the reason is rooted in the drama of the fall and repentance. `Li-annaha awwalu man nadima wa taba ba'dahu wa hum fi hali al-inhidar, kama sabaqa bi-al-qawl` — "because it was the first to repent and seek forgiveness after it, while they were in the state of descent, as has been said before." When the command of descent fell and the fallen were plunging downward, the first to repent of all was the encompassing sphere; and the first to repent after it, while still in the state of descent, was the Sun. `Wa hiya awwalu man sabbaha al-'aqla al-awwala wa wahhada Allaha ta'ala ba'da al-falaki al-muhit` — "and it was the first to glorify the First Intellect and to declare the oneness of Allah, exalted is He, after the encompassing sphere." After the encompassing sphere, the Sun was the first to glorify the First Intellect, to sanctify it, and to proclaim the oneness of Allah, exalted is He.

This precedence in repentance earned the Sun its glorious recompense. `Wa ju'ila laha hadha al-nuru al-sati'u alladhi mala'a al-samawati wa al-arda mathubatan fi ma fa'alathu` — "and there was made for it this radiant light which filled the heavens and the earth, as a reward for what it did." The Sun's brilliant, far-flung light, which fills the heavens and the earth, was bestowed upon it precisely as a reward (mathubah) for its early repentance and its early profession of the truth. `Wa sabaqa minha` — "and it preceded from it" — that is, this radiant light is the recompense for what preceded from the Sun, for the good it did in repenting and glorifying before all the rest of the descending bodies.

And what holds for the Sun holds, in due measure, for every other luminous body. `Wa kadhalika al-anwaru 'ala qadri dhalika fi al-kawakibi wa al-buruji, kullun minha ju'ila lahu 'ala qadri ma sabaqa minhu` — "and likewise the lights, according to that measure, in the planets and the constellations; for each of them was made according to what preceded from it." The lights of all the stars and zodiacal signs are apportioned exactly according to the measure of the repentance that issued from each. The light of a given star is the precise reward of the repentance that star displayed. What we see with the outward eye is only the shell (sadaf); the luminous reward bound within answers to a hidden moral history.

This is grounded in the standing scale of justice. `Li-anna mizana al-'adli qa'imun la yughadiru saghiratan wa la kabiratan illa yuthabu 'alayha wa yu'aqabu` — "because the scale of justice is established; it leaves neither a small thing nor a large thing but that it is rewarded for it or punished." The mizan al-'adl, the balance of divine justice, stands erect over all; it lets pass neither the small nor the great deed without recompense — reward for what merits reward, punishment for what merits punishment. Every measure of repentance and every measure of obstinacy is weighed and answered, down to the least. And so the text breaks into glorification: `subhana man hadhihi al-qudratu qudratuhu wa hadhihi al-san'atu san'atuhu, wa ta'ala 'amma yaqulu al-zalimuna 'uluwwan kabiran` — "glory be to Him whose power is this power, and whose craftsmanship is this craftsmanship; and exalted is He far above what the wrongdoers say, a great exaltation."

### The First Depth, the Splitting Space, and the Eight Temperaments

The text returns to the geometry of the cross to trace the origin of temperament. `Thumma inna hadhihi al-khutut al-mutakawwinah 'ala hay'at al-salib al-sabiq taswiruha... sara laha wasatun min al-ashya' al-mutarakimah ka-tarakum al-khutut ba'duha fawqa ba'd, wa huwa al-'umq al-awwal` — "then these lines, formed in the figure of the cross whose depiction preceded, came to have a center, from things accumulated like the accumulation of lines one upon another, and that is the first depth." Where the line of length and the line of breadth meet — at the intersection of the cross — a center formed out of the accumulated matter, matter heaped like lines piled one upon another. This center is the first depth (al-'umq al-awwal), arising precisely at the meeting-point where the force of length and the force of breadth converge.

`Fa-infaqa, fa-summiya al-jawwa al-munfaqih` — "so it split, and so it was named the splitting space." At that point of convergence the matter split open, and the space thus opened was named al-jaww al-munfaqih, the splitting space — for there, where length met breadth, the matter parted and a depth opened within it. `Wa qad istawa fihi al-mizaju wa al-muntazij` — "and in it the temperament and the tempered became balanced." Within this splitting space, by reason of its enclosure, the temperament (mizaj) and that which is tempered (muntazij) came into balance and equilibrium. This is the very place from which the temperament and the tempered, so often spoken of, take their origin.

From this balancing there arose the eight temperaments — the combinations of the primary qualities. The pairings are: heat with dryness, heat with moisture, coldness with moisture, and coldness with dryness, reckoned out to eight combinations in all. Physicians know this matter well, for it is the foundation of their reading of the pulse, where length, breadth, and depth, and the qualities of heat and coldness, dryness and moisture, are discerned. These eight are the established, primary temperaments, and from them every material thing is composed. `Wa in'aqada minha ma in'aqada` — "and from it was formed what was formed." From these eight temperamental combinations all matter whatsoever is constituted and solidified; every body, down to a single drop or a single grain of sand, is formed by the joining of these qualities. Heat with dryness, heat with moisture, coldness with moisture — from such pairings all matter coheres, and being joined together, all things are established.

The forming of these dimensions and depths is bound to the moment of the divine command "Descend" (ihbitu) — yet not as a thing that came only then, for the three dimensions had already arisen gradually, thing after thing. With the command "Descend," as soon as the divine intention was set, the temperaments took form, just as a man's resolve forms first in his heart and is then enacted. The essences (dhawat) that were present obtained, by their descent, a definite form. And it is to be noted that the depth which appeared at the encompassing sphere was a conceptual depth (wahmi) — the encompassing sphere's density being itself conceptual — so that the first depth there was a depth of the conceptual order, even as the later densities grew solid.

### The Rock (Sakhrah): The Densest, Made from the Third Group

The descending order of densities reaches its floor in the earth, which the text identifies as the rock. `Wa kanat al-ardu allati hiya al-sakhrah... wa hiya aksafu jami'i dhalika wa azlamuhu wa akdaruhu` — "and the earth, which is the rock... and it is the densest of all of that, and the darkest, and the murkiest." The earth here is the sakhrah, the rock — and it is the densest, darkest, and most turbid of all that came to be. By sakhrah is not meant loose soil; the soil is something else, comparatively pure, made of various particles — celestial particles, particles touched by repentance, particles of those who doubted and wavered, particles of the rock of the spheres — that have come to rest upon the rock. The sakhrah itself is the bedrock upon which the entire world sits; it is this very world upon which we dwell. Even the Sun has its core, which is likewise a rock of its kind. But the great rock that is the earth's foundation is the densest and murkiest body of all.

Its origin lies in the worst of the three groups. The earth-rock was formed from the third group (firqa-e-thalithah), the group that denied the creator and committed the gravest disbelief. `Allati lam tuwahhid al-mubda'a ta'ala wa lam tantazim bi-shay'in min al-'uqul` — "which did not affirm the oneness of the Originator, exalted is He, and was not ordered by anything from the intellects." The third group neither professed the oneness of the Originator nor submitted itself to any of the intellects; it denied the creator outright and adhered to no intellect at all. From this group, the most obstinate and benighted of the three, the rock — the densest and darkest of bodies — was constituted. That is why the sakhrah accepts nothing and yields to nothing: it is the sediment of pure denial.

`Wa baqiya altafu dhalika mudiran biha` — "and the most subtle of that remained revolving around it." Around this rock there remained, revolving, the subtler bodies — the four mothers. `Wa huwa al-naru wa al-hawa'u wa al-ma'u` — "and it is the fire, and the air, and the water" — these together with the soil, the four mothers, revolve around and above the rock, being more subtle than the rock itself.

### The Mapping of the Spheres to the Three Sects

The text now draws together the whole cosmology into a single map of the three original groups onto the three great strata of being. The principle is that the structure of the cosmos is the frozen record of the three sects' creeds. `Wa hiya al-firqatu al-thaniyah` — "and they are the second group" — said of the four mothers, fire, air, water, and earth. The second group is the one that believed in the Originator, exalted is He, but did not believe in the First Intellect (al-'Aql al-awwal) nor in the First Emanation (al-Munba'ith al-awwal). It is from this middle group — those who affirmed Allah but withheld recognition from the First Intellect and the First Emanation — that the four elemental mothers were made.

The full threefold mapping stands thus. The first group is the one that believed in Allah and in the First Intellect, acknowledging the First Intellect's right; from this group came the world of spheres — the encompassing sphere, the sphere of the constellations, and the planetary spheres. The second group believed in Allah and in the First Intellect but not in the First Emanation; from it came the four mothers — fire, air, water, and earth (the elemental ummahat). The third group believed in none of the three — neither affirming the Originator's oneness nor any intellect — and denied the creator; from it came the rock, the sakhrah. So the spheres, the mothers, and the rock are the petrified outcomes of the three creeds: the highest belief made the highest, subtlest bodies; the partial belief made the elemental middle; and the denial made the dense, dark floor of the world.

A note on the encompassing sphere and the sphere of the constellations clarifies the upper structure. Above the world of the seven planetary spheres stands the falak al-buruj, the sphere of the constellations, which holds all the fixed stars; these stars revolve each in its own orbit. Above the falak al-buruj is the falak al-muhit, the encompassing sphere, called the Atlas — the most subtle of all, of conceptual density, containing nothing within it. The distinction between the fixed stars and the planets is exact: the stars (kawakib in the sense of fixed stars) revolve each upon its own orbit within the sphere of the constellations, while the seven planets (sayyarah, the wandering ones) revolve around the Sun as their center. There is, in the world of spheres, but a single Sun and a single Moon; the rest of the lights belong to the constellations. The descending order — encompassing sphere, sphere of constellations, seven planetary spheres, four mothers, and at the very bottom the rock — is a single integrated descent from the subtlest to the densest.

### The Conceptual Revolution, the Subtle Smoke, and the Shells of the Planets

A final episode completes the formation of the planetary bodies, and it turns again on the obstinacy of the rock. `Thumma inna al-falaka al-dara 'ala al-wasti dawratan wahmiyyah` — "then the sphere revolved upon the center a conceptual revolution." The sphere — the encompassing sphere — turned about the center in a conceptual, imaginary revolution (dawra wahmiyya); this first revolution was itself of the conceptual order. `Fa-ramat jami'u al-kawakibi bi-ash'atiha 'ala al-sakhrati min jami'i jihatiha` — "so all the planets cast their rays upon the rock from all its directions." As the sphere turned, all the planets — for their shells had not yet been formed — cast their rays down upon the rock from every side.

`Fa-lam tajid munfadhan laha` — "and they did not find a passage for themselves." The rays found no way into the rock. `Wa kana hawlaha dukhanun latifun lam yan'aqid bi-in'iqadiha wa lam yakhtalit bi-al-ummahat` — "and around it was a subtle smoke which had not solidified with its solidification and had not mixed with the mothers." At the beginning of creation there remained about the rock a subtle smoke (dukhan latif) — a fine vapor that had neither hardened into the rock when the rock solidified nor merged with the elemental mothers. After everything else had been formed, this little subtle smoke remained suspended around the rock, belonging neither to the rock nor to the mothers.

That this smoke should remain thus distinct was no accident but a deliberate divine provision. `Wa dhalika bi-qasdin min Allahi 'inayatan li-kawnihi min al-firqati al-awla` — "and that was by an intention from Allah, a grace, on account of its being from the first group." The smoke was kept apart by an intentional divine grace, because it was of the first group — the repentant, luminous group. This is, the text specifies, the second intention from the Tenth Intellect: the first intention had been the production of length, breadth, and depth; this preservation of the subtle smoke is the second intention, a further deliberate working of the intentional grace ('inayat) through the 'Ashir.

Now the rays of the planets, having found no entrance into the rock, returned to their sources, and in returning they gathered up their own affinity. `Fa-lamma balaghat tilka al-ashi'atu ila al-sakhrati... ittahada biha dhalika al-dukhanu li-al-munasabati allati baynahu wa baynaha` — "so when those rays reached the rock... that smoke united with them, on account of the affinity that was between it and them." The subtle smoke, being itself of the first group like the rays of the repentant planets, recognized its own kind and united with the rays; the image is of the owner of a house coming to reclaim his own belongings — the rays found their own proper matter lying upon the rock and took it up. `Fa-raja'at kullu shu'a'in ila aslihi wa qad ittahada biha qistun min dhalika al-dukhan` — "so every ray returned to its origin, and a portion of that smoke had united with it." Each ray went back to its source, carrying with it a portion — not the whole, but a portion — of the subtle smoke.

From this union the bodies of the planets were formed. `Fa-takawwanat shu'a'u kulli kawkabin wa ma ittahada biha min dhalika al-dukhani sadafatan li-dhalika al-kawkabi al-'a'idi ilayh` — "so the ray of every planet and what united with it of that smoke became a shell for that planet which was returning to it." The ray of each planet, together with the portion of smoke that joined it, became a sadaf — a shell — for that planet. The smoke that had lain upon the rock became the shell of the planet. `Fa-sara jami'u dhalika ajraman li-tilka al-kawakibi al-wahmiyyah fa-labisathu` — "so all of that became bodies for those conceptual planets, and it clothed them." The rays and the smoke together became the bodies (ajram) of the planets — those planets being conceptual (wahmi) in their inner reality — and these bodies clothed them as a garment.

This is why what we behold with the outward eye is only the shell. The shell (sadaf) is like the shell of a pearl: the outer casing is the sadaf, and within it lies the pearl, the inner luminous reality. The body of each planet is its shell; our sight cannot pierce to the conceptual light within, for that inner light, though luminous, has taken on density, even as the encompassing sphere is dense — so that the light we see is itself a dense light, the outward garment of a hidden brilliance. The Sun, the Moon, and the rest present to us their shells; the inner light is conceptual and beyond the reach of the apparent eye.

And so the text closes its account with gratitude: `wa al-hamdu lillahi alladhi hadana li-hadha al-ma'rifati wa manna 'alayna bi-al-tahqiqi ma kana hunalik` — "and praise be to Allah who guided us to this knowledge and favored us with the verification of what was there" — and with blessings upon Muhammad and his pure progeny.

### The World of Light and the Necessity of the Rock

It remains to draw out the meaning of this whole cosmology for the human soul, which is its final purpose. The world of spheres ('alam al-aflak) is, in its inner truth, the world of the penitents — the nadimin, those who repented. It is composed of those who, having sinned in the luminous world of origination, repented there and in consequence became dense, descending from their original subtlety into the celestial bodies. The 'alam al-aflak is thus a world of light precisely because it is the world of those who turned back; its luminosity is the visible token of their repentance.

The four mothers descend and rise according to their natures. The elements descend easily but rise only with difficulty, and the reason is the rock. The sakhrah accepts nothing; it is stubborn and yields to no influence, and so the ascent that must pass over or up from the rock is hard. The human being's own descent is upon the rock: he comes down to the sakhrah, to this dense and obstinate world, and it is precisely from here — from the floor of denial, the densest and darkest of all bodies — that he must make his progress upward. The whole apparatus of the spheres, the Sun's radiating heat, the turning of the encompassing sphere, the apportioned lights of the stars — all of it is ordered so that the human soul, descending upon the rock, may receive the effect of these heavenly governors, be purified through the great trial and test of the da'wah bound to place and time, and ascend at last, passing through the encompassing sphere, back to the higher world from which it fell. Man is the first of thought and the last of action: conceived from the beginning as the goal of the whole descent, he is the last to be brought forth, and in his ascent from the rock the long work of deliverance, obligated upon the Tenth Intellect, finds its completion.

## The Elements and the Frame of the Cosmos

### The Second Motion of the Spheres and the Birth of the Breathable Air

The fifth chapter of the first book of secrets bears the title *sakhrah* — the rock — and within it the discourse turns to a second motion of the spheres, distinct from the first. In the first motion, when the fixed stars (*thawabit*) cast their rays toward the rock and those rays were reflected back, the shell (*sadaf*) was formed. Now a second movement of the spheres occurs, and from it a wholly different result issues. The encompassing sphere (*falak al-muhit*) — upon which sits the world of life (*'alam al-hayat*), the dwelling of those brought near (*muqarribin*), whose governances and arrangements (*tadabir*) are ceaselessly at work — commanded that the stars cast their rays a second time toward the rock from every direction:

`wa ramat bi-ashi'atiha ila 's-sakhrah min jami'i jihatiha`
(And it cast its rays toward the rock from all its directions.)

But the rock would not receive them. It is the dense remnant of the third group — the deniers, who accepted neither the Originator nor the First Intellect nor any of the sent ones — and so it possesses no quality of acceptance (*qubuliyyat*). It accepts no arrangement that descends from the encompassing sphere. The rays, finding no opening to penetrate it, were turned back:

`fa-lam tajid munfadhan fiha fa-'adat tilka 'l-ashi'ah turidu amakinaha`
(And it did not find an opening in it, so those rays returned, seeking their places.)

By the law of reflection, a ray seeks to return to the very place from which it emanated:

`fa-'adat 'ala a'qabiha li-da'fi quwaha`
(So they returned upon their heels, due to the weakness of their powers.)

Here, however, a decisive difference enters. In the first motion the rays had been thrown with great force, and so, on rebounding from the rock, they retained strength enough to travel all the way back to their origins. The arranger (*mudabbir*) had made that first motion exceedingly swift. The second motion, by contrast, was weaker:

`wa kawni 'l-harakati thaniya duna tilka 'l-harakati 'l-ula`
(And the second motion being lesser than that first motion.)

Because the reflecting impulse was now feeble, the returning rays could not reach their points of origin. They were cut short, stopping midway between the rock and the place from which they had come:

`fa 'nqada'at duna amakiniha`
(So they were cut off short of their places.)

And in that suspended middle region, the spent rays met and mingled with what lay there:

`wa 'mtazajat bi-ma ma'aha min jism al-hawa'`
(And they mixed with what was with them of the body of the air.)

The effect of the air reached them, and from this mixing of the weakened, reflected rays with the body of the air a new sphere came into being — a sphere that had not existed before. It became distinct (*munfarid*) from all the bodies around it: from the encompassing sphere above and from the four elemental spheres below. This is the sphere of the breeze (*kura-e-nasim*), which is what we call the atmosphere — the breathable layer in which a living creature can draw breath. It is not the same as the sphere of air, which lies at another level; the sphere of the breeze sits within the sphere of air, reaching only as high as the reflected rays ascended. By one account it extends roughly one hundred miles, and by virtue of it — by the oxygen within it — a human being remains alive and able to breathe. From the mingling of the rays with the body of air this atmosphere was created.

What distinguishes this sphere is its moderation of temperament:

`fa-sara ma mazaja hadhihi 'l-ashi'ata mu'tadila 't-tab'`
(So what mixed with these rays became of a moderate temperament.)

The air that already existed could not by itself sustain life; it was too extreme. But through the admixture of these spent rays a balance (*i'tidal*) entered its constitution, and only with that balance did life become possible — for excess heat destroys a living thing, and excess cold destroys it no less. The encompassing sphere maintains its link with the lower spheres precisely through these descending rays; it sends them down from above, and through them, life reached the realms below:

`wa kanat hayatan li'l-nabat wa'l-hayawan`
(And it was a life for the plants and the animals.)

It became a life for all the animals and the plants; from the oxygen of the sphere of the breeze a creature receives its vitality. This breathable sphere is also given another name:

`wa yuqalu laha al-bahr al-sayyal`
(And it is called the flowing sea.)

It is named the flowing sea — the liquid, streaming ocean of air — and it is described as encompassing the earth on every side:

`wa hiya 'llati tuhitu bi'l-ardi min jami'i jihatiha`
(And it is that which encompasses the earth from all its directions.)

So the architecture of the layers may now be set out: above the rock lies the sphere of earth (*kura-e-turabi*); above or with it the sphere of water (*kura-e-ma'*), which itself encompasses the earth from every direction; above that the sphere of air (*kura-e-hawa'*); and within the sphere of air, reaching as high as the rays attained, the sphere of the breeze; and above all of these the sphere of fire (*kura-e-nar*).

### The Springs of the Earth and the Sphere of Water

From the sphere of the breeze, the discourse continues, a further consequence flowed downward into the body of the earth. The breeze, descending into the hollows and caves of the earth, met the waters there:

`wa 'mtasala bi-hi jism al-nasim ila 'l-magharati fi 'l-ard`
(And the body of the breeze connected with it toward the caves in the earth.)

There, in the caverns of the earth, the breeze and the waters piled one upon another:

`wa tarakama ba'duhu fawqa ba'd`
(And some of it piled upon some.)

They congealed and pressed together, colliding and mixing, until they became water:

`fa 'n'aqada wa tazahama ... wa sara ma'an`
(So it congealed and they pressed together ... and it became water.)

And from this process the perpetual springs appeared:

`fa-zahara 'uyunun la 'nqita'a la-ha 'an marri 'z-zaman`
(And springs appeared, for which there is no interruption with the passing of time.)

These are springs that the passage of ages does not exhaust. They are continually replenished, for the same breeze that produced them goes on feeding them, and the waters of the rains augment them still further:

`wa huwa yamudduhu bi-ma yattasilu min dhalika ka-dhalika wa-ma yazidu yattasilu bi-hi min miyahi 'l-amtar`
(And it aids it with what connects from that, likewise, and what increases connects with it from the waters of the rains.)

The springs receive perpetual aid from above — from the sphere of the breeze that first generated them — and to this is added all that connects to them afterward: the rains, the runoff, the flowing of other springs. Whatever falls or flows joins the store and increases it, so the supply is never cut off. In this way the visible water of the earth, welling up in inexhaustible springs and fed forever by atmosphere and rain, took its place in the cosmic frame.

### The Absolute Body: Prime Matter and Form

With the second chapter of this fifth section the author turns from the genesis of the spheres to their classification, opening with the formula of dependence on divine grace:

`wa aqulu bi-tawfiqi 'llahi ta'ala wa 'awni waliyyihi bi-ardi salamu 'llahi 'alayhi inna 'alama 'l-jirm wa 'alama 'l-jism yuqalu 'alayhi al-jism al-mutlaq wa jami'uhu yuqalu 'alayhi al-hayula wa 's-surah`
(And I say, with the grace of God the Exalted and the help of His saint, peace of God be upon him, that verily the world of substance and the world of the body is called the Absolute Body, and all of it is called prime matter and form.)

Two worlds are named here. The world of substance ('alam al-jirm) and the world of body ('alam al-jism) are together designated the Absolute Body (*al-jism al-mutlaq*). The world of substance is itself a body, for it begins at the encompassing sphere, where there is already an imaginal density (*kathafat wahmiyyah*) — a density, but a density of the imaginal order rather than gross corporeality — and in that sense it may rightly be called the Absolute Body. The world of body, by contrast, is the world of the elements, a fully corporeal realm. Taken together, these two worlds are also called prime matter (*hayula*) and form (*surah*):

`wa jami'uhu yuqalu 'alayha al-hayula wa 's-surah`
(And all of it is called prime matter and form.)

The assignment is precise: the world of substance is named *surah* in its higher aspect and the world of the elements is named *hayula*. For the author specifies that prime matter, in reality, is the world of the mothers:

`fa'l-hayula 'ala 'l-haqiqati 'alam al-ummahat`
(So the prime matter, in reality, is the world of the elements.)

That is to say, *hayula* is fire, air, water, and earth:

`allati hiya 'n-naru wa'l-hawa'u wa'l-ma'u wa'l-ard`
(Which are the fire, the air, the water, and the earth.)

And the form, *surah*, is the world of the spheres, extending from the encompassing sphere down to the moon:

`wa 's-suratu huwa 'alam al-aflak min al-muhiti ila akhiri falaki 'l-qamar`
(And the form is the world of the spheres, from the encompassing sphere to the end of the sphere of the moon.)

The deeper point of this classification is to mark where corporeality (*jismaniyyat*) first entered the order of being. Above lies the world of light ('alam al-nur), and beneath it the world of life ('alam al-hayat); beneath that the world of spheres ('alam al-aflak), whose highest member is the encompassing sphere. It is at the encompassing sphere — the topmost member of the world of spheres, called the Absolute Body — that corporeality began. Before that point there was no body at all. The world of body, the elements, follows below it as the realm of density (*kathafat*). So both worlds, substance and body, are the Absolute Body, and from this juncture corporeality took its origin: above it, the non-corporeal; from it and below it, the dense world of things.

### Fathers, Spouses, and Offspring: The Greater Souls

The author now reads the cosmic frame through the imagery of a household, mapping the structure of generation onto the structure of the spheres and elements. The spheres stand in the station of fathers (*aba'*), and the elements in the station of wives or spouses (*azwaj*). And just as the Shari'ah permits a man four wives, so the mothers are four — fire, air, water, and earth. From the union of these fathers and these spouses come offspring (*awlad*), and these are the things born into the world: the minerals (*ma'dan*), the plants (*nabat*), and the animals (*hayawan*).

The author then deepens this scheme by identifying each world with one of the universal souls. The world of the mothers, he says, is in reality entirely a vegetative soul:

`wa huwa bi'l-haqiqati jami'un nafsun namiyah wa yuqalu laha an-namiyat al-kubra`
(And it, in reality, is entirely a vegetative soul, and it is called the Greater Vegetative Soul.)

The world of the elements is the Greater Vegetative Soul (*al-namiyat al-kubra*), and its boundary is fixed:

`wa hadduha min falaki 'l-qamari ila 'l-markaz`
(And its limit is from the sphere of the moon to the center.)

Its limit runs from the sphere of the moon down to the center — the center of the rock. This is a point to hold firmly in mind. The Greater Vegetative Soul is the original source and station of every vegetative soul (*nafs namiyah*) whatsoever, in whatever creature it appears. When a vegetative soul attaches itself to a growing thing — a child, a plant — it draws upon this region between the moon's sphere and the center as its true homeland. In humanity the vegetative soul is open and manifest; in plants it is present but hidden (*namiyah makhfiyyah*); in some plants the sensitive soul (*hissiyyah*) lingers as well; and in the minerals the vegetative soul is gone altogether. The center of the rock is thus called the center of the vegetative soul, for it is there that this soul finds its deepest, most hidden root.

The world of the spheres, by contrast, is the form (*surah*), and these spheres are called the fathers:

`wa 's-suratu huwa 'alam al-aflak ... wa yuqalu laha al-aba'`
(And the form is the world of the spheres ... and they are called the fathers.)

And the world of the spheres is in reality entirely a sensitive soul:

`wa huwa bi'l-haqiqati jami'uhu nafsun hissiyyah wa yuqalu 'alayhi al-hissiyyat al-kubra`
(And it, in reality, is entirely a sensitive soul, and it is called the Greater Sensitive Soul.)

So the two great souls have their two domains. The Greater Sensitive Soul (*al-hissiyyat al-kubra*) extends from the encompassing sphere down to the sphere of the moon; the Greater Vegetative Soul extends from the sphere of the moon down to the center of the rock. The fathers — the spheres — are the seat of sense; the mothers — the elements — are the seat of growth; and from their conjunction the offspring of mineral, plant, and animal are born into the world.

### How Motion Forged the Four Elemental Spheres

The author now returns to explain, in physical terms, why the four elemental spheres took the particular natures they did. The cause in every case is the motion of the sphere, and the law is that the nearer a thing lies to the source of motion, the more intensely it is affected. When the encompassing sphere first revolved and moved with the universal motion, it imparted to the matter directly connected with it an intense and total heat:

`wa lamma dara 'l-falaku fi awwali dawranihi wa taharraka 'l-harakat al-kulliyyah aksabahu wa-ma yattasilu bi-hi min jismihi 'l-hayula hararatan shadidatan kulliyyah`
(And when the sphere revolved in its first revolution and moved with the universal motion, it imparted to it and to what was connected to it of its body of prime matter an intense, universal heat.)

This is the first revolution, the imaginal first casting of rays. Where the motion was greatest — immediately beneath the sphere — its effect was greatest, and motion produces heat (*hararah*). And with this intense heat came dryness:

`wa ma'aha yubs fa-sara kuratan-nar wa yuqalu laha kurat al-athir`
(And with it dryness, so it became the sphere of fire, and it is called the sphere of ether.)

So the matter closest to the sphere, receiving the most intense heat and a complete dryness, became the sphere of fire (*kurat al-nar*), also named the sphere of ether (*kurat al-athir*), characterized by extreme heat and dryness.

Farther from the source, the motion's effect diminished:

`thumma ba'udati 'l-harakatu min awwali 'd-dawrani fa-aksaba 'l-muttasila bi-ha min dhalika 'l-jism hararatan duna tilka 'l-hararati 'l-awwal fa-sarat hararatun mu'tadilah`
(Then the motion became distant from its first revolution, so it imparted to what was connected to it of that body a heat less than that first heat, so it became a moderate heat.)

This moderate heat, by virtue of its very moderation, drew moisture after it:

`wa tabi'aha rutubatun li-i'tidaliha wa qila 'alayha kurat al-hawa'`
(And moisture followed it due to its moderation, and it was called the sphere of air.)

Thus arose the sphere of air (*kurat al-hawa'*): moderate heat joined with moisture. And farther still, where the motion had grown remote and the parts of matter had come to rest, the opposite qualities prevailed:

`thumma ba'udati 'l-harakatu 'ani 'l-falaki fa-kanat ajza'uhu sakinah fa-aksabathu burudatan wa yubsan wa qila 'alayhi kurat al-zamharir`
(Then the motion became distant from the sphere, and its parts became static, so it imparted to it coldness and dryness, and it was called the sphere of the frozen.)

So at the greatest remove, where the parts lay motionless, coldness and dryness (*burudat wa yubs*) took hold, and this became the sphere of the frozen (*kurat al-zamharir*).

The author illustrates the whole principle with a vivid image:

`wa'l-mithalu fi hadhihi 'l-harakat ka-mithli rajulin yamkuru fi ma'`
(And the metaphor for these motions is like that of a man who moves in water.)

It is like a man swimming in water. The water nearest to him moves with all its force:

`fa-ma qaruba ilayhi fa-huwa yataharraku bi-kulliyatihi wa'lladhi dunahu adwanu minhu wa'lladhi ba'uda minhu ghayru mutaharrik`
(So what is near to him moves with all its parts, and what is below it is lesser than it, and what is far from it does not move.)

The wave closest to the swimmer surges at full strength; a little farther out it moves more slowly; and far away the water lies still. So it is with the spheres of the elements: the closer to the source of motion, the more violent the effect; the farther, the more it subsides into stillness. And the crucial lesson the author presses is this — that all of these elemental spheres were created not from the interaction of the elements with one another, but from the motion of the spheres themselves. It is the revolving of the *aflak* that forged the spheres of fire, air, and the frozen.

The author then closes the loop with the genesis of the breathable atmosphere, which belongs to the second motion. When that second, weaker motion stirred and the stars cast their rays toward the rock and, finding no passage, returned upon their heels:

`wa lamma taharrakati 'l-harakatu 'th-thaniyatu wa ramati 'l-kawakibu bi-ashi'atiha ila 's-sakhrah min jami'i jihatiha wa lam tajid munfadhan fiha 'adat 'ala a'qabihi ... ila ba'di kurati 'z-zamharir`
(And when the second motion moved and the stars cast their rays toward the rock from all its directions and did not find an opening in it, they returned upon their heels ... to a part of the sphere of the frozen.)

These reflected rays were cut off near the sphere of the frozen, and from their intensity a new sphere was made:

`fa-sarat min hiddatiha kuran ... fa-sarat kuratan baridatan ratibatan mu'tadilatan bi-ha hayatu 'n-nabati wa'l-hayawan`
(So from their intensity they became a sphere ... so it became a sphere that was cool, moist, and moderate, by which is the life of the plants and the animals.)

So a sphere cool, moist, and balanced came into being — the sphere of the breeze, by one report some hundred miles thick, the sphere of oxygen by which a human being breathes, and by which life was granted to plant and animal alike. Thus the two motions are distinguished and remembered: in the first motion the shell (*sadaf*) was formed; in the second motion the breathable atmosphere was created.

### The Arrangement of the Pillars and the Attention of the Intellects

The third chapter of the fifth section opens with the elements at last arranged into a stable order, at which point they receive a new name and a new dynamism:

`wa lamma tarattabati 'l-ummahatu ka-dhalika wa qila 'alayha al-arkan wa tasa'ada dukhanuha wa tamazajat wa nazara kullu 'aqlin min al-'uquli 'l-mujarradah allati sabiqa dhikruha ila falakin min hadhihi 'l-aflak`
(And when the elements were arranged thus and were called the pillars, and their smoke rose and they mixed, every intellect from the abstract intellects, whose mention preceded, looked toward a sphere from these spheres.)

When the four mothers were set in order they were called the pillars (*arkan*) — the four pillars — and their smoke rose and mingled. And at this point each of the abstract intellects (*'uqul mujarradah*) directed its gaze toward one of the spheres. The spheres in question are nine: the encompassing sphere (*falak al-muhit*), the sphere of the constellations (*falak al-buruj*), and the seven planets. The tenth body, the earth, stands apart. So among the ten abstract intellects, each turned its attention to one sphere: the First Intellect's attention rested on the encompassing sphere; the second intellect's on the sphere of the constellations; the third on Saturn; the fourth on Jupiter; and so on, in descending order. The tenth intellect's attention was directed not to a planet but to the earth itself.

The author insists upon this distinction:

`ila falakin min hadhihi 'l-aflak`
(toward a sphere from these spheres.)

The planets are not the earth. There are nine spheres in all — nine heavens — and beneath them the elements, and beneath those the earth as the tenth creation, set apart. The earth (*ard*) is not a planet; it is separate.

Then divine providence assigned to the seven planets their governing office:

`wa ja'ala sab'ata kawakiba mudabbiratan li-'alami 'l-ummahat wa'l-mawalidi kama sa-nadhkuru dhalika fi'l-fasli 'th-thani`
(And it made the seven planets arrangers for the world of the elements and the generations, as we will mention in the second chapter.)

It was divine providence ('inayat-e-ilahiyyah) that disposed every star, every sphere, and every pillar in its fitting place, and it made the seven planets the managers and arrangers (*mudabbir*) of the world of the elements and of the generations (*mawalid*). And the world of the elements receives here its formal name:

`wa qila li-'alami 'l-ummahat al-'alam al-kawn wa'l-fasad`
(And it was said of the world of the elements, the world of generation and corruption.)

The world of the elements is the world of generation and corruption ('alam al-kawn wa'l-fasad) — the realm in which things ceaselessly come to be and pass away, and over which the seven planets preside as governors.

### Why the Whole Frame Was Built for the Rock

Having laid out the structure of spheres, elements, and rock, the author poses the great question of purpose. Why was man, the final creature, brought down to the rock and not placed somewhere finer? The one who arranged man's descent could have lodged him in a nobler station — in the sphere of the constellations, say, or in some elevated place. The whole laborious arrangement — the making of the elements, the ordering of the spheres, the building of layer upon layer — could have been spared if a fairer dwelling had been the goal. What good, after all, comes up from the rock?

The answer lies precisely in the rock's resistance. Things descend easily from the spheres, for descent is downhill and the lower realms receive what comes to them. But nothing can climb up from the rock, because the rock accepts nothing; it has no quality of acceptance, and so it cannot be a launching place for ascent. Man is set in the world of mud — the lowest, densest station. From the spheres, things can descend; onto the rock, things cannot easily climb. Descent is easy; ascent is difficult; and the rock will not cooperate. This, paradoxically, is the very design. Man is placed at the bottom of the order, in the place from which rising is hardest, so that his ascent must be earned through the long discipline of the cosmic process. The entire frame of spheres, elements, and rock exists for the sake of the rock — that is, for the sake of the earth and the man who descends upon it — and the whole purpose of the arrangement is to make his ascent possible.

### The Three Divisions and the Forming of Man's Limbs

The author then sets out the cosmic process as unfolding in three great divisions. The first division is the establishment of length, breadth, and depth — the three dimensions of bodily extension. The second division is the creation of the shell (*sadaf*). The third division is the creation of the trenches or valleys (*khudd*). Each of these is a stage in the gradual preparation of the world to receive its final creature.

The sequence may be traced from the top down. The world of spheres begins with the encompassing sphere — the *falak al-muhit* — followed by the sphere of the constellations; and the spheres themselves are the penitents (*nadimin*). Beneath the nine spheres lie the elements — fire, air, water, earth — and beneath them the rock. From the mutual collision and mixing of all these, the shell (*sadaf*) was first created; then the sphere of the breeze; then the sphere of the frozen; then water, until the whole flowing sphere of water came to be, with its lightning and its electrical phenomena. After this the mountains (*jibal*) arose, with lightning (*barq*) and smoke (*dukhan*), and finally the trenches and valleys (*khudd*) were carved.

The purpose of all this carving and layering is the gradual purification of the final creation. Unless man passes through this long process, being purified slowly and by stages, he cannot be reformed. The author makes a striking point here about the limitation of the original luminous world. In the world of the beginning there was no constraint of time, and God expended a vast effort — He tried with the utmost intensity — to reform the fallen, yet they would not be reformed. The original luminous world, unbounded by time, could not accomplish their purification, precisely because reform there required no passage through trial. Only by being put through trials over time, and only when those who fail are tested and made to feel the consequence, can reform be accomplished. And so the trenches are dug and the whole apparatus is built, that the final creature might be purified gradually through the trials that the timeless luminous world could not impose.

The author adds a further, intimate detail. The degree of delay each part underwent in this descent corresponded to the limb it would later become in the human frame. According to the delay that befell a part, so was its place assigned in the body that man would receive: some part became the heart, some the earth-bound foot, some one organ, some another. The structure of the human body thus records, limb by limb, the history of the descent.

### The Seven Planets as Managers and the Promise of Ascent

The fourth chapter turns to the governance of the seven planets over the world of generation and corruption. When the seven planets were appointed managers of this world, the term of governance allotted to each was fifty-one thousand years and a little more — the surplus being denoted by the word *nif*, "a little." These are solar years of three hundred sixty-five days, our ordinary worldly years. The planets were given this office so that they might cast their effects upon the world of generation and corruption — the world of the elements, the world of *kawn wa'l-fasad* — and thereby elevate the corrupt parts within it.

The mechanism of this elevation runs upward, stage by stage. The planets act upon the elements and send down into them the powers of the spheres, so that the matter of the elements is raised: it ascends from sphere to sphere until it reaches the encompassing sphere (*falak al-muhit*); from the encompassing sphere it passes to the world of life ('alam al-hayat); and from the world of life it rises toward the world of light ('alam al-nur). It is from this point that purification begins. Yet the author is careful to specify that at this stage what is happening is not yet the purification of human souls but the preparation for it — the preparation for the Millennial Resurrection (*qiyamat al-alfiyyah*).

This Millennial Resurrection is the means of raising beings upward. By its occurrence they come to participate in the Sensible Divinity (*lahut al-mahsus*), and from there the bearer of the Sensible Divinity takes them and ascends with them, one nested temple (*haykal*) after another. They enter the temple of the Imam; from the temple of the Imam they pass to the temple of the Qa'im; from the Qa'im's temple to a still greater temple; from the temple of the Hujjah they come to share in the temple of the Mawla; and from there, finally, they ascend from the world of life toward the world of light. It is, the author observes, a very long process — temple within temple, station above station — by which the elevated being climbs from the lowest mud to the highest light.

### The Seven Planets and the Two That Retained Inauspiciousness

The seven planets are named in their order:

`Zuhal, Mushtari, Mirrikh, Shams, Zuhrah, 'Utarid, Qamar`
(Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, the Sun, Venus, Mercury, the Moon.)

These are the seven planets made managers of the world of generation and corruption. Now all of these stars, the author teaches, are fashioned from the penitent ones (*nadimin*) — from the repentant (*ta'ibin*), those who turned back to God. But although every one of them was made from repentance, two among them bore a lingering stain, and this for a reason rooted in their history. The companions of these two had been people of inauspiciousness, the Iblisi ones; these two planets had repented only after keeping such company, and so the influence of that company always remained with them.

The Sun did not have this quality; the Moon did not have it; Jupiter, Venus, and Mercury did not have it. But Saturn (Zuhal) and Mars (Mirrikh) did, for among their company were some who had listened to the words of Iblis before they repented. Hence two effects clung to these two planets: the quality of inauspiciousness (*nuhusat*) and, with it, the further qualities that the author will detail. The repentance was genuine, but the trace of the prior association persisted, marking Saturn and Mars as the two inauspicious ones among the seven managers of the world.

### The Lesser Cycle: How Fifty-One Thousand Years Are Composed

The author now explains how each planet's reign of fifty-one thousand years is internally structured, and how the reigns sum into a complete cycle. Governance began with Saturn (Zuhal), because it is the highest of the planets — the most elevated among them. And its lofty station was assigned to it not arbitrarily but on account of its specific qualities: its coldness, its dryness, its inauspiciousness, and its power to annihilate everything it encounters. Saturn's nature is to finish, not to make — to demolish rather than to build. The author likens it to a demolition crew: before a new building can be raised, a demolition squad comes first to tear down what stands and to clear the ground. Saturn is the cosmic demolition squad. Its governance (*tadbir*) lies in its hand to demolish everything first.

But the author is emphatic that this destructive office is Saturn's action, not its essence. The destruction belongs to its function, which is specific to it; it is not that Saturn is, in its very essence, an evil thing. He draws the comparison to architecture: when a building is to be made, the first task is not construction but the leveling of the ground. Leveling, too, is part of demolition — it is included in the same work of clearing — and it is Saturn that performs this leveling. Yet in its own reality Saturn is *tawwab*, one of the repentant (*ta'ibin*), drawn from the penitents; it is not in its essence the inauspicious thing its function makes it appear.

The reason such a function can issue from an essentially penitent planet lies in the nature of the world of spheres as a whole:

The entire world of spheres ('alam al-aflak) is of a fifth nature (*tabi'atun khamisah*). There are four natures — heat, dryness, moisture, and coldness — but the world of the spheres possesses a fifth, special nature that is the combining source from which those four emerged. The four natures were released, as it were, out of the spheres; the world of spheres holds within itself the single nature from which all four were brought forth. It is this fifth, combining nature that is signified by the term *khamisah*, "the fifth." And from this single combining nature each of the four natures issued and was distributed among the spheres.

Accordingly, each planet is associated with its proper action by way of approximation. A relation was established between each planet and the particular action that issues from it — not because that action exhausts the planet's essence, but by an approximate correspondence. Since Saturn was appointed to level everything first, the action of inauspiciousness (*nuhusat*) — the action of bringing down — is the action related to it.

Now to the internal structure of the reign. Saturn ruled alone (*mufarrad*) for the first one thousand years, governing the world by itself with no partner. Then, in each successive thousand-year span, one of the remaining planets served as its minister (*wazir*). The first of Saturn's ministers was Jupiter (Mushtari). Jupiter's action is heat and moisture, and it is called the Greater Fortune (*sa'd al-akbar*). By its temperament it softened the dryness of the first millennium — and the author pauses on the precise sense of the Arabic word: *layyana* means "it softened," from which comes *mulayyin*, the term physicians use for a softening agent. Jupiter, as the softener, lightened the harsh dryness that Saturn's solitary reign had imposed. It sent down into the earth heat and moisture, and it fermented within the earth every praiseworthy thing.

Here the author introduces a key distinction between two kinds of sowing. There is *takhmir* and there is *khalq*. Takhmir means sowing — the bringing of certain parts and the placing of them in the earth, like the laying of a leaven (*khamir*). The leaven is laid first; then, as when a seed is sown, a plant grows, and fruit comes, and the final result appears. Takhmir is the sowing of the seed-parts that will later issue in living things. So Jupiter, as Saturn's first minister, fermented every praiseworthy thing into the earth — that is, sowed within the soil the seeds of all that is good.

A word on the genesis of the planets themselves: the author notes that the manner in which the spheres of Saturn, Jupiter, and the rest were made is not given in detail, but the principle is clear. When the initiator (*hads*) acted in the luminous world ('alam nurani), the parts belonging to the penitents (*nadimin*) and the repentant (*ta'ibin*) became dense and descended into these bodies. The dense parts of the penitents came down and constituted the planets.

Through Jupiter's ministry the rains dried up and the earth was made pure and drenched. Where Saturn's solitary reign had brought dryness, Jupiter's accompanying ministry brought the rains and saturated the earth with water through the second millennium.

Then came the third millennium, in which Saturn's government was joined by the ministry of Mars (Mirrikh) — both of them inauspicious stars together. From their conjunction coldness and dryness were generated, and through it the evil and vile things were fermented. Here the author marks a second technical term alongside *takhmir*: *takhannuth*. Takhmir is the sowing of good things; *takhannuth* is the sowing of corrupt and vile things. So while Jupiter's ministry had sown the praiseworthy, Mars's ministry sowed the corrupt — the leaven of the evil and base things was laid down in the earth.

This pattern continues through the remaining ministers in turn, each serving one thousand years, until the last of Saturn's ministers is the Moon (Qamar). In the final millennium of Saturn's reign, the Moon is its minister and Saturn its king. Then the office of governance passes on: after Saturn's seven thousand years and a little more — that is, after its full term of fifty-one thousand years — Jupiter becomes king in its place, ruling alone for the first thousand years just as Saturn had done, and then taking each remaining planet as its minister in turn, beginning with Mars. So Saturn's reign of fifty-one thousand years is reached as seven spans of roughly seven thousand years each — the planet ruling first alone, then with each of the others as minister in succession — and the same pattern governs every planet's reign that follows.

The author works the arithmetic. Each of the seven planets reigns for fifty-one thousand years and a little more; seven such reigns yield three hundred fifty-seven thousand years, and with the accumulated surplus (*nif*) of three thousand the total comes to three hundred sixty thousand solar years — or, reckoned in lunar years, four hundred thousand. This complete revolution of all seven planets is one cycle (*dawr*), and it is named the lesser cycle (*kawr al-asghar*). The figure of three hundred sixty thousand carries the resonance of the three hundred sixty degrees of a full circle, the rounding of a complete turn.

### The Measure of a Thousand Years and the Manifestation of the Soul

The author pauses to connect these vast spans to the scriptural measure of time and to the destiny of the individual soul. The years in which these reigns are counted are solar years, reckoned by the sun and not by any other star, each of three hundred sixty-five days, a year of day and night such as we know. And against this stands the Qur'anic scale: a thousand years is, with God, equal to a single day — as Allah the Exalted has declared, that one day with Him equals a thousand years.

Upon this measure the author builds a teaching about the manifestation of the soul. The growth and unfolding of a person across a thousand years need not be exactly a thousand years — it may run a little shorter or a little longer. But the decisive correspondence is this: a person's manifestation in the world answers to his manifestation in the luminous world ('alam nurani), where his pledge of allegiance (*bay'at*) was taken. The hand upon which the *bay'at* was given in the luminous world is the real and governing reality. And so a person's appearance in this world is timed to his appearance there. If his manifestation is not completed within a thousand years, then a part of it is certainly manifested — for the unfolding does not fail entirely; some portion of it definitely comes to light within that span. We cannot fix it by saying, for instance, that one was born in such-and-such a year of the Hijri calendar; what matters is the correspondence to the luminous original, and within the thousand-year measure some manifestation always occurs.

The author also reminds us that, at this stage, all of this is still preparation. The process of fermentation (*takhmir*) is still under way; humanity is not yet born. The whole vast labor of the planetary ministries is the readying of the ground — the preparation of the house of purity (*safa khanah*) — so that man may in time ascend from below.

### The Greater Cycle and the Total Resurrection

Just as the lesser cycle is the complete revolution of the seven planets, so the cycles themselves are gathered into a still vaster measure. The greater cycle (*kawr al-a'zam*) is the lesser cycle multiplied by itself: three hundred sixty thousand years (or, in lunar reckoning, four hundred thousand) multiplied by its like. This squaring of the lesser cycle yields the immense span of the greater cycle.

At the completion of one greater cycle a momentous loosening occurs:

`thumma tanhallu rubutu 'l-aflak`
(Then the bonds of the spheres will loosen.)

The bonds of the spheres will loosen — the ropes that bind them, the magnetic pulls, the central attraction by which the spheres are held in their order. These magnetic bindings of the spheres relax when one greater cycle — four hundred thousand multiplied by four hundred thousand years — is complete. This event is the total resurrection (*qiyamat al-kulliyya*). The resurrection of the greater cycle is the total resurrection, in which the entire world of things is annihilated. Yet the encompassing sphere (*falak al-muhit*) is untouched by it; the encompassing sphere remains safe and continues to dispense its governance. It is the world of spheres beneath it — the bound and revolving spheres with their apogees (*awj*) and perigees (*hadid*) — whose bonds are loosed and whose order is dissolved, while the encompassing sphere abides and governs on.

The author grounds this in scripture. The cosmos returns to its beginning exactly as it was first initiated:

`ka-ma bada'na awwala khalqin nu'iduhu wa'dan 'alayna inna kunna fa'ilin`
(As We began the first creation, We shall bring it back; a promise upon Us. Indeed, We are doers.)

As creation was first initiated, so it is brought back from its first. When the world of things is annihilated, the elements are finished — the mothers and all that came of them — and then creation begins anew: the elements are made again, and the process recommences from its origin.

### Where Our Scattered Parts Go: Death and the Gathering of Returns

This raises the searching question of what becomes of our own substance when the world is annihilated. Those parts that were worthy of elevation and had already ascended remain elevated; those who had reached the world of life have gone up and continue to ascend, for the world of life is very far and the climb goes on. When the final Imam comes — he upon whom the last ones depend — the subtle things worthy of elevation rise with him first, while what remains, the dregs, stays behind: those not yet worthy of elevation, those who adopted slackness. They are like a long line of beings drawn up before a window; the window closes, and the remainder must wait.

To make this intelligible the author unfolds a profound teaching about the dispersal and re-gathering of the single soul. One entity that existed in the spiritual world ('alam ruhani) does not come into the world as a single intact unit; it is broken into many pieces, and its parts are elevated across successive cycles — some parts in one cycle, others in a second, others in a third. Its substance is scattered. The unit that came into the world is assembled from fragments: all of a person's constituent parts are present in fire, air, water, and earth, and from these one receives some portion at the start, and throughout life one goes on acquiring the remaining portion that is one's share. A person begins as a mere point, not even an infant, and across a whole lifetime gathers and gathers his substance to himself, drawing in matter and releasing it again — taking in and giving off, by tons over a lifetime.

At death, only vapors leave the body; the great, dense mass of a being's substance remains in the world. What actually ascends at death is only the subtle soul (*nafs*) and the sensory soul (*nafs hissi*). A small portion is elevated; the major dense part remains behind, to be elevated later. And when is it elevated? At the coming of the Imam, on whose hand that elevation is attained. The scattered parts are gathered with the Imam; all the Imams, in turn, were gathered with the Qa'im; and there the parts that had been dispersed across different returns (*karrat*) were assembled and brought to one place. Then, when a Qa'im greater than him comes, the gathering proceeds further still, from station to station.

The instrument of this re-gathering is the spirit. The author asks: how, then, are we to be reassembled, given that our substance is so scattered? The answer is that one's spirit (*ruh*) has become a magnet for one's own parts. Wherever a part may lie, the spirit draws it from there. What is released from a person at his death is his own; the rest belongs to another's return, and yet another's, and through the successive returns (*karrat*) all are at last drawn together — gathered with the Imam, then with the Qa'im, then with a greater Qa'im — until the dispersed parts of a single being are united into one thing, one single unit. It is, the author marvels, a most strange and wonderful arrangement.

### The Return of Governance to the First Sphere

At the close of a cycle, governance reverts to its starting point and the world is remade as it first was:

`thumma ya'udu 't-tadbiru ila 'l-falaki 'l-awwali ka 's-surati 'l-ula`
(Then the governance will return to the first sphere, like the first form.)

The governance returns to the first sphere, in the likeness of the first form — and this after the devastation of all the inhabitants of the surface of the earth. A devastation comes upon the face of the earth and ruins all that is upon it. Fine clouds and heavy clouds, snow and sleet spread across the world, and the cold grows severe as in the first state, example after example, forever and ever — for it is Saturn's office to bring on the cold. This returning of governance to the first sphere, and the spreading of cloud and snow and bitter cold, fulfills the divine word that what was begun shall be brought back: as God began the first creation, so He restores it, a promise binding upon Him.

### The Resurrection at Each Cycle and the Reckoning of Deaths

The author now refines the timing of resurrection across the vast cycles. Resurrection (*husayb*) is initiated at the beginning of every cycle — but not in the very first cycle. In the first greater cycle, the first three hundred sixty thousand years that passed, there was no resurrection; it was only preparation. Resurrection begins at the head of every subsequent cycle. So the author, having described the general state of death, is now specifying which cycles bear a resurrection and which do not.

And the cycles themselves are gathered into a yet larger reckoning. The greater cycle is not the final unit; there are many deaths, and within the scale of deaths the greater cycles are counted: four hundred thousand greater cycles (*kawr al-a'zam*) make one death (*mawt*). After the passing of each such death — at the close of every cycle within it — this devastated state recurs, and resurrection (*husayb*) is initiated at the beginning of each cycle.

What happens at the end of a death is a total governance (*tadbir kulli*). It resembles the lesser annihilation in that the world of things is finished, but it reaches higher: within the world of spheres themselves all things are transformed. In this total governance the shells (*sadaf*) of the planets and the constellations are elevated and changed — the spheres are themselves transmuted. Then the first resurrection comes, initiated from the earth, just as the world's being was initiated from the earth in the first cycle; and the resurrection continues thereafter, the generations propagating onward upon this renewed state, as a line continues by pollination.

### The Elaborated Account of Saturn's First Three Millennia

The fifth chapter returns to the planetary ministries to render in detail what the earlier passage had stated only in summary. When the first millennium belonged to Saturn (Zuhal) as the sole manager of its governance, without any of the remaining planets, then there occurred in the world the severe cold, the extreme cold; the accumulated snow; the clouds, both light and heavy; the thick fog; the abundance of rains pouring as from the mouths of waterskins; the occurrence of mountains and stones; and the darkness of the atmosphere — for the clouds and the fog became a barrier between the earth and the sun. So in Saturn's solitary first millennium the world was a place of bitter cold, heaped snow, overcast and fog, torrential rain, the raising up of mountains and rock, and a darkened sky shut off from the sun.

When this first millennium ended and the second millennium entered, Saturn's minister in governance was Jupiter (Mushtari). Then the rains dried up, the stones and the mountains broke apart, the atmosphere grew warm, and the earth became a plain. Every region and island was disposed as the earlier statement had described, across the twelve islands that God had appointed; and the earth remained wet and soaked to the end of the second millennium, in which Jupiter was Saturn's minister. So the second millennium reversed the harsh dryness and bitter cold of the first: the rains ceased, the broken mountains settled, the air warmed, and the earth became a moist plain stretched across the twelve islands.

Then the third millennium came, and Saturn's governance was joined to the ministry of Mars (Mirrikh). In this millennium were generated the crawling creatures of the earth (*harashat*) — the burrowing things that live in the ground — and the scorpions (*'aqarib*), the snakes (*hayyat*), the geckos (*wazagh*), the lizards (*zab*) that dig into the earth, the Banat Wardan, the locusts (*jarad*), and every creature of the water. The author dwells on a feature of these creatures: they did not come as fermentations (*khama'ir*), as in the first cycle, but were generated whole. In the first cycle the leaven had been laid — the *khamirah* made — so that life issued in stages; but here the creatures arose as complete organisms, each a whole being made and brought forth entire, rather than as a single cell or a mere ferment.

### Predators, Hard Minerals, and the Conjunction of the Two Inauspicious Ones

After the crawling things and the creatures of the water came a further generation of fiercer creatures and harder substances. There came the predatory animals (*siba'*), the wolves (*zi'ab*), and the beasts armed with claws and tusks — the disobedient and arrogant ones, those that tear their prey apart. These are the clawed ones (*zawat al-makhalib*), the final parts to be gathered from the base elements of fire, air, water, and earth, taking shape as the tearing beasts. Whether the creatures we observe now or the beasts of ages long past, all fall under this category of the rapacious and harmful.

With them was generated every hard thing — that is, the hard minerals — and especially those originating from iron and copper. Iron and copper were created in this period, in the cycle of Mars and Saturn, by reason of their toughness. And from this the author draws a ruling: it is for this reason that wearing iron and copper, and the like such as brass, is held forbidden in prayer. For the generation of these vile and evil things proceeded from the putrefactions and filth of the earth, and their appearance came about through the coming together of the two inauspicious ones — Saturn and Mars — and the participation of each with the other in governance. These two are the two magnets most specific to the vile and the base among the temperaments and the mixtures, and so the harshest minerals issued from their conjunction; hence the unfitness of iron and copper for the state of prayer.

### Ra's and Dhanab: The Two Slow Ones Kept on High

To the ministry of the two inauspicious planets the author joins the ministry of the two slow ones — Ra's and Dhanab, the head and the tail of the dragon. Their story is distinctive. They belong to the things of the disobedient and the arrogant, and yet they were not kept down among the base; they were kept high, beneath the sphere of the moon. The reason lies in their history: they are the parts of those who, at the very beginning, hastened in the repentance of the 'Ashir — who came forward early to repent — but who afterward became the most disobedient of all. Because of that initial repentance, God dealt with them by a special arrangement, lodging them on high rather than casting them among the base, so that they are not reckoned among the base ones.

These two are called together the *jawzahar*, and also the two knots (*'uqdatayn*). The author makes their nature plain: Ra's and Dhanab are not planets with ascended physical bodies but imaginary calculations. No physical body has risen up to constitute them; they are an imaginary reckoning — Ra's the head and Dhanab the dragon, its tail, just as a serpent has a head and a tail. When the spheres were being arranged and these two were set in their places, a portion of vileness reached them — for they belong to those who repented but whose end was upon disobedience and arrogance, and the influence of their company fell upon them. They are not counted among the spheres proper, yet they move on high, beneath the sphere of the moon, near the two Hujjats, revolving in the celestial circle though they are neither of the spheres nor with them, neither of the elements nor with them.

Rather, they belong to those who persisted and were arrogant — the third group, who accepted neither the divine Oneness nor God Himself but followed the 'Ashir — and this third group is what is formed in the rock and is the base for the rock. The third group descended into the rock because of its vileness, its tyranny, and its darkness — the darkness of a cold, humid blackness that is receptive and ready to accept what is cast into it. So Ra's and Dhanab are in essence things of the rock and its third group, yet they have been kept aloft.

### The Two Opposites, the Eclipses, and the Preservation of Divine Justice

Because they are things of the rock raised on high, Ra's and Dhanab stand as the two opposites for the two worlds — for the world of earth and nature on the one hand, and for the world of light on the other. They are especially the adversaries of the two luminaries (the two shining ones), the Sun and the Moon (*Shams* and *Qamar*), by reason of the greatness of their vileness and their arrogance. From them proceed the killings and the vile things that spread through the world; they are the directors of such slaughter and corruption, descending from their high station to manifest these vile things in the world. They remained as they are, except for offspring of their own kind, by a special intent from the original divine plan, whose place the author defers to explain.

The two luminaries are their enemies, and the visible sign of their enmity is the eclipse. The rays of the Sun do not reach the earth unhindered, for a barrier is set against them; and this barrier is the solar and lunar eclipse (*kusuf* and *khusuf*). The effects of the two knots (*'uqdatayn*) become a screen between the luminary and its light. And of the two, the dragon — Dhanab, the tail — is more severe than Ra's, the head; its effect upon the luminaries is the harsher.

Finally, the author returns to the wisdom in their elevated station. God kept these two on high because of their initial repentance, for the sake of preserving His own justice. Though evil was afterward generated from them, they had, in that first moment, repented; and so, in justice, that early repentance is honored with a high place, that the justice of God might stand established. The discourse closes, as it opened, in the language of praise and benediction:

`al-hamdu li'llahi 'lladhi yahdi bi-hujaji 'l-haqqi ila tahqiqi 'adlihi wa wahdaniyyatihi`
(Praise be to God, who guides by the proofs of the truth to the verification of His justice and His Oneness.)

The proofs of creation bear witness to the truth of His religion and His command; and blessings and peace are sought upon the Messenger Muhammad, upon his pure and good progeny, and upon his rightly guided guides — the whole vast frame of spheres, elements, and rock standing forth at last as a single testimony to the justice and the Oneness of God.

## The Governance of the Spheres

### The architecture of the second surah

With *Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim* we open the second surah of *Kitab al-Anwar*. This surah comprises five chapters, and the first of those chapters in turn contains five sections. The opening words tell us that the surah *gathers* its five chapters within itself, and that the first chapter *gathers* five sections — and it is worth pausing on the very verb the text employs, *yajma'u*, "it gathers," for its grammar already carries a teaching.

The root *jama'a* is transitive: it means simply "to gather," to bring things together. It does not, of itself, mean "to be gathered." For the passive, intransitive sense — "to be gathered," to come together of one's own accord — Arabic shifts the verb into the *tafa''ul* pattern and produces *tajamma'a*. Thus *jama'a* is the active gathering done to a thing, while *tajamma'a* is the gathering that befalls or happens within a thing. And when one moves in the other direction — to *cause* a gathering to occur — the verb becomes *ajma'a*, "to bring everyone to agree, to bring all together," from which is derived the noun *ijma'*, the consensus in which all were caused to assemble as one. So the surah's first verb, *yajma'u*, says that this second surah actively gathers its five chapters, and the first chapter actively gathers its five sections, into a single ordered whole. The morphology is not an aside; it teaches that what follows is a deliberate gathering of an immense process under a single governing design.

That governing design is the administration of the heavens across the great cycle of time. What this surah unfolds is nothing less than the slow preparation of the cosmos for the coming of man — the sowing of seeds, the formation of minerals and plants and animals, and at last the genesis of the one who would walk the earth. Before any of it, we must understand who governs.

### Zuhal's grand cycle and its planetary companions

When the third millennium of the cosmic reckoning was completed, Zuhal — Saturn — took up the administration (*tadbir*) of the fourth millennium, and in that fourth millennium the Sun, Shams, became its companion. The Arabic of the text reads: `Wa lammā tamma al-alf al-thālith, istaqbala Zuhal tadbīr al-alf al-rābi' bi-murāfaqat al-shams lahu` — "And when the third millennium was completed, Zuhal faced the administration of the fourth millennium, with the companionship of the Sun to it therein."

Zuhal is not the governor of a single millennium only. Zuhal administers a full grand cycle of fifty-one thousand years, and this enormous span is divided into successive thousand-year periods, each one accompanied by a different planet that shares the administration as a minister shares the rule of a king. Zuhal remains the sovereign throughout; the companions rotate. The first such period Zuhal held alone; thereafter each thousand brought a new planetary companion to its side.

In the celestial correspondence between the spheres and the parts of a single living body, the Sun, Shams, occupies the station of the heart, *qalb*. As the heart is the sovereign organ of the body, so the Sun is the sovereign among the planets. The Sun's two essential qualities are heat, *harara*, and dryness, *yubusa* — and these qualities, sent down to the forming earth, will prove decisive in everything that the fourth millennium brings forth.

We should fix the order of the companionship for the whole of Zuhal's cycle, because the text will return to it: Zuhal first administers alone; then Shams becomes its companion; then Zuhra (Venus); then 'Utarid (Mercury); then Mushtari (Jupiter); then Mirrikh (Mars); then Qamar (the Moon). This sequence — Zuhal, Shams, Zuhra, 'Utarid, Mushtari, Mirrikh, Qamar — is the skeleton on which the entire account of creation hangs.

### The fourth millennium: the leavening of kings, gold, and ruby

In the fourth millennium, under the joint administration of Zuhal and Shams, came the *takhmir* — the leavening — of the kings, of gold, and of ruby. The text reads: `Wa takhmīr al-mulūk wa al-dhahab wa al-yāqūt` — "and the leavening of the kings and the gold and the ruby." This leavening does not mean that kings, gold, and ruby were born in the fourth millennium. It means that their seeds were sown into the dimensional world; their leaven was set; they were not yet brought forth.

To understand this we must hold apart four technical terms that mark the stages by which a thing comes into being. The first is *khamira*, the leaven — the seed being sown into the earth. The second is *tawallud*, birth or generation — the seed sprouting and growing. The third is *takwin*, formation or development — the progressive unfolding of what has been born. The fourth is *in'iqad*, conception, the coming-together that initiates the whole. These are not synonyms; they are sequential stages, and the text uses them with precision.

*Khamira*, then, is the sowing of the seed. The image is exact: consider a tree. From the tree come leaves, then flowers, then fruits, and from the fruit at last emerges the seed. That seed is taken and placed into the earth — and the act of placing it there is the *khamira*, the leavening. *Tawallud*, by contrast, is what happens next: from the buried seed the tree is born. But here is the crucial qualification — when the tree is born, the *whole* tree is not born at once. Only those parts of it destined for the present sphere come forth in the present sphere. Many spheres remain ahead, and in each successive sphere new parts will be born, new things manifested from the same original leaven. One might, in modern idiom, call this genetics: the genes were laid down in the leavening, and they express themselves stage by stage across the journey. The seeds of the kings, the gold, and the ruby were thus sown in the fourth millennium, their full manifestation deferred to spheres yet to come.

This is the very truth carried in the couplet that Hazrat Fazlullah Shah Mukarram habitually recited at the close of his discourses: `Khuld se tukhm-i 'amal ban ke yahān āye the, yahān rahen jaise ke hain wādi-i gulshan men shajar` — "From Paradise they came here having become the seed of action; here they remain as trees do in the valley of the garden." The seed of action was sown above, in *khuld*, in Paradise itself; having become that seed it descended, and here it remains as a tree remains rooted in the garden valley. The couplet continues: `Bū-i gul ban ke abhī aur kahīn jānā hai. Abhī ashjār men bāqī hain lagne ko thamar` — "Having become the fragrance of the flower, we must now go elsewhere; fruits are yet to come forth upon the trees." The teaching is precise: it is the fragrance of the flower that travels onward, not the flower itself, and not the seed. The seed stays sown; the flower stays on the tree; only the fragrance is carried away. And what is that fragrance? It is the form and the sensory soul — that subtle essence which moves on to the next sphere while the rooted parts remain behind. The fruits not yet borne on these trees will come in their season, and when they come their fragrance too will spread, sphere after sphere, until the original is at last completed. This is why the saint sealed his teaching with these lines: they compress the whole doctrine of the journeying soul into a single image of a garden.

### The four terms of coming-into-being, and the Realm of Intentions

If *khamira* is the sowing and *tawallud* the birth, then *takwin* is the formation that follows — and it too is a progressive thing. *Takwin* derives from *kaun*, being or becoming; it is the becoming-actual of what was born. Its order is fixed: first the vegetative soul is formed, then upon it the sensory soul is formed, and then the rational soul is formed; and through knowledge and action the whole is advanced and carried forward. So *takwin* is precisely development — the development of the part, limb by limb, soul by soul, until the rational creature stands complete and capable of growth through knowledge and deed.

Now we may see why the leavening of *kings* in particular belongs to this era. The *takhmir* is what came down into the dimensional world; in the Realm of Intentions, the '*alam al-niyyat*, the kings as such did not yet exist — only their leaven was set there and sown here. But why kingly leaven, of all things? Because in the Realm of Intentions there were certain entities who, through the accumulation of good deeds, generated within themselves kingly qualities, qualities of ownership and dominion. The good they had done there is what bears fruit here. The reward for those deeds is now received in this world as polity, *siyasat*; as dominion; as caliphate, *khilafa*; and as the comforts and bounties of worldly life. What was earned in that place is what proves useful in this one. The leaven of kings, therefore, was the worldly sowing of an intention formed above — and it was sown here, upon the earth.

And it was sown in *this* era for a precise reason. This was the era in which Shams accompanied Zuhal, and Shams is the king among the celestial spheres. Because the kingly planet was present as companion, the leaven that came down to earth was the leaven of kings. The royal sowing matched the royal companion.

Here a subtle distinction must be drawn between the intention and its leaven. An intention, once made in the Realm of Intentions, is fixed and unchanging — written, as it were, as a mark carved upon stone, never to alter. But the leavens that derive from a single such intention do not stay fixed; they continuously change and develop across the successive spheres. The intention is one; the leavens it generates are many and ever-unfolding. How do they change? Just as we said of the tree: when the leaven was put into the earth, a tree grew from it, and from the tree only a fragrance rose — that and no more. The whole has not yet developed from it. Further development awaits the spheres still to come. And what is decreed for this sphere becomes, in turn, the decree governing the spheres that follow.

This yields a governing principle of the whole journey: the intention made in one sphere becomes an *action* in that same sphere; and the intention of that sphere — the one the traveler forms within it — becomes the *administration*, the action, of the spheres to come. Intention first, then administration: each sphere converts the intentions of the last into the working order of the next. This is why the one who walks the straight path, the *mustaqim al-sayr*, must traverse three hundred and sixty thousand spheres, *karrat*, each single sphere lasting one thousand years. This vast traverse is necessary; it is the very shape of the soul's appointed road.

### The provisions prepared before the journey

What this entire account describes is the *first manifestation* — the spheres themselves have not yet happened; this is their beginning, the first emergence from the realm of *khuld*. Before the soul sets out on its manifestation, before it descends to begin its passage through the spheres, Allah the Most High prepared in advance every provision that man would require. The image is homely and exact: before a traveler sets out upon a long journey, he assembles all his supplies beforehand. So too here — whatever man would need across his immense road was created and made ready before he himself appeared. The minerals, the plants, the animals, the very ground and water of his forming — all of it was laid up against his coming.

### Clearing skies and the first warmth

Returning to the fourth millennium and the leavening of gold and of ruby — `Wa al-yāqūt`, "and the ruby" — the text turns to the condition of the atmosphere. `Wa tahallalat tilka al-ghuyūm wa al-dabāb` — "and those clouds and that deep fog dissolved." In Zuhal's first, solitary period the heavens had been choked with dense clouds, deep fog, and snow in the space above the earth. These did not clear in the second millennium. But now, by the third and into the fourth, under the ministry of the Sun, they began to dissolve and thin. `Fa-safā al-jaww` — "so the atmosphere became clear." The Sun's heat was burning away the long cold murk of Zuhal's solitude.

With this clearing came a new condition: `Wa zaharat sukhūnatun qarībatun muqārinatun shiddat al-bard` — "and there appeared a near warmth, accompanying the intensity of the cold." Alongside the fierce cold there now appeared a warmth that was *close*, a near heat. The cold belonged to Zuhal, whose nature is cold; the warmth arose from the ministry of Shams. So the two natures of the two governing planets were both present at once — Zuhal's deep cold and, set against it, the Sun's approaching warmth.

### The first animals and the noble minerals

In this clearing, warming world the first creatures appeared. `Fa-tawalladat al-hayawānāt al-sighār dhawāt al-arba'` — "so there were born the small four-legged animals." These were the small quadrupeds: rabbits, foxes, cats, mice, and the like — `wa ghayr dhālika mimmā yasghuru khalquhu min dhawāt al-arba'ī`, "and others besides, of those whose creation is small, among the four-legged." So if one asks when the mouse was born, the answer is: in the fourth millennium, under the Sun's companionship. And it is no accident that these creatures appeared as four-legged in the *fourth* millennium; the number itself echoes the era. But every one of these animals is a forbidden, *haram* animal — and this too has its cause. It is Zuhal that holds the administration, and these creatures came under Zuhal's companionship; the harshness of Zuhal's nature stamps them as forbidden. No permissible animals existed yet at all; the lawful beasts would have to wait for a gentler companion.

In the same fourth millennium were also formed the noble minerals. `Wa tama'danat al-ma'ādin al-sharīfa min dhahab wa fiḍḍa wa jawāhir shaffāfa 'alā ikhtilāf anwā'ihā ilā an inqaḍā al-alf al-rābi'` — "and the noble minerals were formed: gold, and silver, and transparent jewels of various kinds, until the fourth millennium ended." The verb *tama'danat* means precisely that they took on the form of minerals, became fully mineral. The noble minerals were gold and silver, and from this it is to be understood that gold and silver were the origin of all minerals — the noble metals from which the rest derive. Beyond the two metals there were the transparent jewels, of many kinds: crystal, *billor*; carnelian, *aqiq*; ruby, *yaqut*; and emerald, *zumurrud*. All the clear gems were formed in their varieties, and this minering continued until the fourth millennium came to its close.

But the great predatory beasts had not yet appeared. The predators — *siba' al-dawari*, the beasts of prey — and creatures of their kind, such as the dinosaurs, would not come in this gentler era. They belong to the first era of Zuhal's harsher rule and would only emerge there. The fourth millennium produced small forbidden quadrupeds and noble minerals; it did not produce the great hunting beasts.

### The fifth millennium: the coming of plants and the lawful animals

`Thumma istaqbala Zuhal tadbīr al-alf al-khāmis bi-murāfaqat al-Zuhra` — "then Zuhal took up the administration of the fifth millennium with the companionship of Zuhra." In the fifth millennium Zuhra, Venus, became Zuhal's companion, and Zuhra is an auspicious star, a *sa'd*. Until now the text had spoken of animals and minerals but not of plants; with the fifth millennium, plant life at last begins.

`Fa-ibda'at fīhi al-amṭār al-mu'tadila fī awqāt ghayr dā'ima` — "so there began in it the moderate rains, at non-permanent times." In Zuhal's solitary era the pattern of water had been extreme: either it rained continuously for a full thousand years, or it did not rain at all. Now the rains became moderate and intermittent — falling at intervals that did not endure, sometimes present and sometimes not. `Wa jarat riyāhun ghādhiyatun illā annahā shadīdat al-bard` — "and there blew nourishing winds, except that they were intensely cold." These winds gave nourishment and moisture, *ghadhiya*, yet they remained bitterly cold, for Zuhal's cold still pervaded the world even as auspicious Zuhra warmed it.

Now plant life unfolded. `Wa jarat al-anhār wa nabatat al-ashjār wa nabatat al-azhār` — "and the rivers flowed, and the trees grew, and the flowers bloomed." `Wa ta'allaqat al-anwār` — "and the buds began to open." Here the text distinguishes two words: *azhar* are the flowers, the opened blossoms, while *anwar* (singular *nawr*) are the buds, the blossoms not yet unfurled — flower as against bud. `Wa intasharat al-rawā'ih al-ṭayyiba al-zakiyya` — "and the good, pure fragrances spread." `Wa zaharat al-fawākih al-ladhīdhat al-ṭu'ūm` — "and there appeared fruits, delicious in taste." So the fifth millennium brought flowing rivers, growing trees, opening flowers and buds, spreading fragrances, and sweet-tasting fruits: the full inauguration of the vegetable world.

With the plants came a new order of animals. `Wa tawalladat al-hayawānāt al-mahmūda` — "and there were born the praiseworthy animals." Where the fourth millennium had brought only the disliked, forbidden creatures, the fifth — under auspicious Zuhra — brought the praiseworthy, *mahmuda*, the good animals. `Wa al-muhallala al-nāfi'a` — "and the permissible, beneficial ones." These are the lawful and useful beasts: cattle, sheep and goats, horses, and camels, `mimmā salima min al-sharr mu'īnun li-l-bashar` — "of those that are safe from evil and are helpers to mankind." They are the animals safe from harm and given as aids to the human race.

These animals too were born from the caves. From the *maghara*, the caves, the lawful beasts emerged — and this is no incidental detail, for in the same manner the Dhu Sa'id al-Da'i and his parts would later emerge from the caves. The pattern of birth-from-the-cave that governs the animals here is the very pattern that will govern the coming of the one who appears in the world; precisely how this cave-birth works the text leaves to its own time, but the correspondence is deliberate.

`Wa ṭāba al-hawā'` — "and the air became pure and clean." The atmosphere itself was now wholesome. The creatures spread across the face of the earth, among the trees and over the mountains; they ate from the fruits and gathered the grains — *hubub* meaning the grains — and `ṭāra fī al-arḍ`, "flew about in the land," ranging freely across it. `Ilā an tamma hādhā al-alf al-khāmis` — "until this fifth millennium was completed."

### The sixth millennium: the fertilizing winds and the doctrine of pollination

`Wa istaqbala Zuhal tadbīr al-alf al-sādis bi-murāfaqat 'Utārid wa shirkatuhu lahu fī al-tadbīr` — "and Zuhal took up the administration of the sixth millennium with the companionship of 'Utarid and its sharing with him in the administration." In the sixth millennium Mercury, 'Utarid, became the companion and shared in the governance.

`Fa-kathura fīhi hubūb al-riyāh` — "so the blowing of the winds became numerous in it." The verb *kathura* is intransitive — "they became many" — complete in itself, requiring no object; one does not ask "many of what did he make," for nothing is made many here, the winds simply became many of their own accord. (Had the sense been "he made them many," the transitive *akthara* or *kaththara* would stand in its place, just as *jalasa*, "he sat," is intransitive while *ajlasa*, "he made to sit," is transitive. But the text says *kathura*: the winds increased of themselves.) `Al-muhyiyya wa al-ghādhiya al-mulqiha li-l-nabāt` — "the life-giving, the nourishing, the pollinating winds for the plants." These winds gave life, gave nourishment, and pollinated the plants and the trees alike.

This introduces the doctrine of pollination, *liqah*. Pollination is the meeting of male and female — in plants as in animals — from which a third thing is born. Such winds carry one plant's connection to another, joining male and female so that from their meeting a new thing comes forth; the winds are *mulqiha*, the bearers of this fertilization to plant and tree. And the principle holds across the living world: in animals and in plants, the male and the female meet, and from their meeting issues the third. So crucial is this that some plants cannot accomplish it unaided — the date palm, for instance, does not self-pollinate; it requires that a human being perform its pollination by hand. Where nature does not join male and female of itself, man must complete the meeting.

`Wa kathurat fīhi al-hayawānāt al-ghādhiya li-l-ajsām al-bashariyya` — "and there became numerous in it the animals that nourish the human bodies." The beasts that feed and sustain the human frame multiplied in the sixth millennium. Here the text uses *ajsam*, the plural of *jism*, and we should note the distinction the term carries: *jism* (plural *ajsam*) is set apart from *jasad* (plural *ajsad*), with *jism* being the word that applies to the human body proper. The animals multiplied to nourish those human *bodies* that were soon to come. The full discourse on this, the text tells us, will be resumed in the section that follows — `Wa sa-ya'tī bāqī al-qawl fīhi fī fasl tālī` — but its purpose is already plain: before man came, his nourishment was prepared, both the bad and the good, laid up in advance against his arrival.

Across these first planetary companionships — Shams in the fourth millennium, Zuhra in the fifth, 'Utarid in the sixth — a single mercy governed the age. Four thousand years and more passed under companions who were all auspicious. Zuhal remained throughout the king, but every one of its ministers in this stretch was a good minister; no harshness descended from the ministers, for none of them was a malefic star. The harshness latent in Zuhal's own nature was held in check by the goodness of its companions, and so the world was prepared in gentleness for the creature it awaited.

### All creation as a prelude to man

`Wa lam yabqa shay'un min al-hayawān wa al-ashjār illā zahara, muqaddaman li-l-jins al-basharī` — "and there remained nothing of animal or tree that did not appear, as a prelude to the human species." Every kind, every species of animal and tree — whether permissible or forbidden, praiseworthy or disliked — had now appeared. Not one was left unmanifested. And all of it came forth as a *muqaddaman*, a prelude, a forerunner laid down in advance of the human race. The human himself had not yet come; but the entire living world was set in place before him, so that he might enter a world already furnished for his need.

`Thumma ubtudi'a fī tadbīr al-insān` — "then the administration of man was initiated." After all else had been prepared, the governance of man himself at last began.

### The genesis of man: the ascending vapor and the precedence of remorse

The text now turns to the very origin of the human being, and its language is dense and exact: `Wa dhālika annahu sa'ada man altaf al-miyāh al-'adhbati wa al-bihār bukhārun laṭīfun mu'tadlun min aṣfāhā wa a'dhabihā wa aṭyabihā wa a'dalihā, wa mā huwa qad uzina lahu min fudūl al-dhukūr ilā iqāmat al-ajlāfiyya, wa istahaqqa dhālika limā sabaqa minhu min al-nadam 'alā mā kāna fahuwa fī hāl al-inhidār.`

Man's genesis began when a subtle, moderate vapor — *bukhar latif mu'tadil* — ascended from the waters. The verb *sa'ada*, "it ascended," is intransitive, needing no object; the thing that rose did so of itself. (To *make* something ascend would be *as'ada* or *sa''ada*, yielding the verbal nouns *is'ad* and *tas'id*; but here the vapor simply *ascended*.) And it ascended not from any water, but from the most subtle part of the sweet waters and the seas — `min aṣfāhā wa a'dhabihā wa aṭyabihā wa a'dalihā`, from their purest, their sweetest, their most pleasant, and their most balanced. Explicitly it was *not* from saline water; it rose from the sweet, non-saline waters, from the very finest of them.

And this vapor was the one for which permission had been given — `mā huwa qad uzina lahu min fudūl al-dhukūr ilā iqāmat al-ajlāfiyya` — granted leave from the remnants of the males toward the establishment of the physical forms. It deserved this precedence, `wa istahaqqa dhālika limā sabaqa minhu min al-nadam`, "and it merited that because of what had preceded from it of remorse" — remorse, *nadam*, over the prior state it had been in while it was in the condition of falling, *inhidar*. Here is the heart of the matter: everything had now appeared, and the one who had shown remorse *first* and most, the highest among them, is the one whose parts appeared first and highest. What is the cause of this precedence? When these entities were in the state of falling — when the command "descend" was given and there occurred what we may call the Big Bang, the great *inhidar*, the downward plunge at the command to descend — then, in that very state of falling, whoever repented first earned that his parts should manifest first and most exalted. Priority in remorse during the descent became priority in manifestation. The soul quickest to turn back in regret as it fell is the soul that rises first and highest in the forming world.

### Adam al-Ibda'i and his veil

This is the place to grasp a distinction central to the whole doctrine. There is Adam al-Ibda'i, the Primordial Adam, and he belongs to the Realm of Life, the '*alam al-hayat*. And there is his veil, his *hijab* — his counterpart who actually appears in the world — and that is the Dhu Sa'id al-Da'i. Adam al-Ibda'i abides in the Realm of Life; the Dhu Sa'id al-Da'i is the one who manifests below as his veil.

The same pattern is seen in the Prophet himself. What is his name? In heaven, Ahmad; on earth, Muhammad. And the meaning is precisely this: Muhammad is the *hijab*, the veil, of Ahmad. The heavenly reality bears one name; its earthly veil bears another; and the earthly manifestation is the veil through which the heavenly reality appears. So Muhammad is to Ahmad as the Dhu Sa'id al-Da'i is to Adam al-Ibda'i — the manifest veil of the hidden, exalted reality.

### The cloud, the rain, and the pure receiving land

`Fa-in'aqada minhu sahābun laṭīfun` — "so from it a subtle cloud was formed." From the ascended vapor there gathered and conceived a subtle cloud, *sahab latif*. `Thumma indafa'a mataran qalīlan 'alā arḍin naqiyyat al-turba` — "then it propelled itself as a light rain upon a land of pure soil." The verb *indafa'a* is the reflexive of *dafa'a*, "to push away"; *indafa'a* means "to propel oneself, to thrust oneself forward." There was a potency within the cloud, and out of that inner impulse it drove itself forward, becoming a light rain that fell upon a land whose soil was pure.

And the purity of that receiving land is described in detail. `Salīmatan min kulli ṭa'min ghayr ṭayyib` — it was safe from every unpleasant taste. `Naqiyyatan lawn` — pure in its color. `Sahīhat al-turāb` — of sound, light dust. `Mutakhalkhilatan` — loose and uncompacted. The word *mutakhalkhal* derives from *khalkhal*, the loose anklet worn on the foot, which is neither fastened to the foot nor joined to anything else — so the soil too was loose, not bound together, not the kind of dry earth that turns hard and clotted when water touches it, but free and friable, with no prior stickiness in it. It was, in sum, a soil of perfect purity, light, clean-colored, untainted in taste, loose in texture, free of every defect and every previous adhesion.

`Fīhā maghārātun ghayr 'amīqa` — "in it were caves that were not deep." This pure land held caves, but shallow ones, not deep — `bal qarībat al-'umq`, "rather, of shallow depth." And this shallowness is itself a standard set by Allah. For the great ones whom Allah formed — such as the Dhu Sa'id al-Da'i — Allah fixed the measure of the grave at no more than six cubits in depth; and the caves of this pure land are of that same shallow measure. The grave of the exalted is not a deep pit; the caves that would receive the forming of man were shallow, of a depth near the surface, by the standard Allah himself laid down.

### The two waters: the womb of the earth

Now the text describes how the forming substance came together within those caves. `Fa-baqiya fī tilka al-maghārāt min dhālika al-mā'` — "and there remained in those caves some of that water," the water that had come down from the clouds. `Wa kāna nazīr manī al-imra'a` — "and it was the like of a woman's semen." Here is the great analogy on which the genesis of man turns. The water already standing in the caves was the like of a woman's seed, *nazir mani al-imra'a*. It was the earlier water — `mimmā kāna min tilka al-thulūj wa al-amṭār al-mutaqaddima al-sā'ira fīhā`, "of what had been of those snows and the preceding rains that flowed within them." This standing water had gathered there from the snows and the rains of the earlier epochs, flowing into and collecting within the caves; it was the residue of all that ancient precipitation.

`Fa-ikhtalaṭa bimā tafajjara fī tilka al-maghārāt` — so this water mixed with what had welled up within the caves. The water that sprang and gushed forth from the earth itself — the water of the former snows and rains — was the woman's part; while the rain now descending fresh from the clouds was the man's part, the man's semen. The man's seed came down from above; the woman's seed was already present below, risen from the springs of the earth. The semen of the woman was there first, present in the earth from the previous rains and snows; the semen of the man arrived afterward, falling now from the heavens.

`Fa-imtazaja ma'an imtizājan kulliyyan` — "so the two mixed together a complete mixing." The two waters did not merely touch; they merged in a total, thoroughgoing *imtizaj kulli*. `Fa-takawwana fī tilka al-maghārāt kamā yatakawwan al-nuṭfa fī al-arhām` — "and there was formed in those caves, just as the drop of semen, the *nutfa*, is formed in the wombs." With the complete mixing of the two seeds, formation occurred in those caves exactly as the drop of semen is formed within the wombs — for the caves themselves had now become wombs, *arham*. The cave was the womb; what took shape in it took shape as an embryo takes shape in the mother's womb. This is the second stage of man's making; before it stood the *khamira*, the leavening; now begins the formation in the cave-womb.

### The inner heat and the wedding of the planets

`Thumma askhanahu wa rahmāhu harāratun bāṭinatun 'arḍiyyatun min asfalihī` — "then an inner, earthly heat from below it heated it and showed it mercy." An inner heat, *harara batina ardiyya*, rose from beneath and warmed the mixture — the heat of the earth's own womb acting from below, just as the heat of the womb acts upon the embryo it carries.

And why was the earth's inner heat so apt at this moment? `Li-anna fī dhālika al-waqt kānat al-kawākib fī buyūt sharafihā` — "because at that time the planets were in the houses of their honor." At the very moment of man's formation, all the planets stood in their houses of dignity, *buyut sharafiha* — their most noble stations. The image is that of a wedding: when a child is born, all the household come dressed in their finest and gather for the celebration; so the planets, at this birth, were each arrayed in its house of honor as guests in their best attire at a wedding. The birth taking place was the birth of the Dhu Sa'id al-Da'i, and for it the whole heaven put on its honor.

`Wa kāna al-shamsu fī burj al-dalw` — "and the Sun was in the house of Aquarius." The Sun in particular stood in *burj al-dalw*, Aquarius, which is for the Sun its most noble house. And this placement had a precise physical consequence. `Wa li-dhālika bāṭin al-arḍ kāna hāmiyan` — "and for that reason the inner earth was intensely hot." Because the Sun occupied Aquarius, its house of honor, from which its power to send heat to the earth is at its greatest, the inner earth was made *hamiyan*, intensely hot — the word *hamiyan* itself pointing back to the Sun's heat-sending power. Yet — `Wa sukhūnatuhā layyinatan mu'tadila` — "and its heat was gentle and moderate." The inner earth was intensely hot, and at the same time its heat was soft and balanced: neither scorching nor cold, but a strong yet gentle and temperate warmth. The Sun's exaltation made the heat abundant; the providence governing the birth made that abundant heat moderate and kind.

### The rising and falling of the water, and the completion of the semen

Under this gentle, abundant heat the forming water began a ceaseless motion. `Fa-rakhā dhālika al-mā' ṣā'idan yajlib al-harra wa yantafi' al-harāra` — "so that water rose, ascending, drawing the heat to itself and benefiting from the heat." Drawn upward by warmth, the water climbed. `Wa lammā balagha ilā mawḍi'in lāhika fīhi bardan askalahu` — "and when it reached a place where coldness met it, that cold made it heavy." Aloft, the cold seized it; the wind made it acquire coldness, and the cold weighted it down. `Fa-raj'a munhadiran ilā qarārihī` — "so it returned, descending to its resting place." Made heavy by cold, it sank again to where it had begun.

`Wa lam yazal kadhālika mutaraddidan ṣā'idan wa munhadiran` — "and it did not cease to be thus, wavering, ascending and descending." The water wavered endlessly between the two, *mutaraddidan sa'idan wa munhadiran*: rising to draw heat, falling under cold, rising again, falling again, without rest. And `Wa huwa yatakassaf wa yanfa'il shay'an ba'da shay'in` — "and all the while it was densifying and being acted upon, thing after thing." Through this tireless oscillation the water grew progressively denser and thicker; its volume lessened as its substance thickened; it was acted upon little by little, stage after stage. `Ilā an ṣāra duhniyyatan raṭiban sayyālan mu'tadilan` — "until it became an oily, moist, flowing, moderate substance." At last, by this repeated ascent and descent, the water became a substance oily, moist, flowing, and balanced — like the semen of a man. With this, the creation of the semen was completed: a praiseworthy, moist, temperate oil, the very likeness of a man's seed, made ready in the cave-womb of the earth.

### Locating the birth in the cycle: 'Utarid, not Qamar

It remains to fix this birth precisely within Zuhal's grand cycle of fifty-one thousand years. The first fifty-one thousand years belong to the companionship of Zuhal; within them the first thousand was Zuhal alone, then each succeeding thousand brought its companion in the fixed order — Zuhal, Shams, Zuhra, 'Utarid, Mushtari, Mirrikh, Qamar. The birth, the formation, of the Dhu Sa'id al-Da'i occurred within this grand cycle, specifically in the sixth millennium, during the era of 'Utarid, Mercury. The whole year is reckoned Zuhal's year, for the fifty-one thousand years are Zuhal's; but within them, the formation of the Dhu Sa'id al-Da'i falls in the sixth thousand, in the era of 'Utarid.

And here a common misconception must be corrected. It is widely supposed that the creation of the Dhu Sa'id al-Da'i occurred in the era of Qamar, the Moon. But this is an error: no book of the Da'wat Shareefa mentions Qamar in this connection at all. Everywhere, without exception, the mention is of 'Utarid. The complete formation of the Dhu Sa'id al-Da'i occurs in the era of 'Utarid; it is in the *subsequent* era of Qamar that he emerges from the cave. Formation in 'Utarid; emergence in Qamar — the two must not be confused, and the formation, which is the creation proper, belongs to 'Utarid.

One clarification about the reckoning itself: the "thousand," *alf*, in these accounts is not to be taken as a literal span of one thousand years, but as an epoch — one *alf* meaning one epoch. The numbers mark eras, not arithmetical millennia. Whether the whole matter unfolded across a literal seven thousand years or across seven epochs, it is the epoch-sense that is intended.

And so the rule for the believer is plain. Pending any explicit statement from Mawla on the question, the believer's conception must rest upon the book: the birth of the Dhu Sa'id al-Da'i happened in the sixth millennium, in the era of 'Utarid. Until a word from Mawla should come to settle it otherwise, this is what we hold and this is what we believe — that in the sixth, in the era of Mercury, the formation was complete, and from the cave he would emerge in the era of the Moon that followed.

## The Moon, the Sensory Soul, and the Great Cosmic Cycle

### The Drying of the Primordial Oil and the Onset of Form

We now take up the third *fasl* of the second *risala* of the first *bab*, where the discourse turns from the primordial waters to the first stirrings of the human shape within them. The narrative resumes from the state already described, in which the water that had streamed down from the sky and welled up from the earth had gathered and settled into its appointed condition. The author writes:

`Wa lamma saara dhalik al-ma' 'ala tilka al-halah al-mutaqaddama dhikruha, wa balaghat al-shamsu ila burj al-jauza, sakhuna al-hawa', wa habbat riyah al-bawarih, wa hamma zahir al-ard, fa-ja'ala dhalika al-duhn yajiffu shay'an ba'da shay'in.`

"And when that water came into the state whose mention has preceded, and the sun reached the zodiacal sign of al-Jauza, the air grew hot, the *bawarih* winds blew, the surface of the earth was heated, and that oil began to dry little by little."

A precise astronomical event sets this whole process in motion. The sun, which had previously stood in the sign of al-Dalf, now advanced into the sign of al-Jauza. A *burj* is the name of a zodiacal constellation, and al-Jauza is one such house among the twelve. With the sun's entry into this sign, the climate of the young earth was transformed. The air grew hot, and across the land swept the *bawarih* — the scorching winds of high summer, *al-riyah al-harrah fi al-sayf*, the hot winds of the summer season. Under their breath the surface of the earth itself grew warm.

The consequence of this heating was decisive. The oily, fatty substance that had been gathering — the *duhn* that earlier stages of the narrative had described forming upon and within the primordial matter — now began to dry, drop by drop and degree by degree, *shay'an ba'da shay'in*, little after little. The author continues that it began to congeal and to set: `wa yan'aqidu fi tilka al-sukhunah al-batina wa al-zahira` — "and it congealed in that heat, both the inner heat and the outer." For the heat pressed upon it from two directions at once: it came down from above, borne on the summer winds and the sun's ascendancy, and it rose up from below, out of the warmed body of the earth. Caught between the two, the oil solidified and set firm, `ila an balagha al-ghayah fi al-in'iqad` — "until it reached the very extreme of congealing." It hardened thoroughly, and with this hardening there appeared in it a faint firmness, `wa al-salabah al-yasirah` — "and a slight solidity." Where there had been only a viscous, flowing matter, there was now the beginning of a body that could hold a shape.

And with this firmness, the matter `akhadha min tariqat al-taswir` — "took to the path of image-making," began to take on a form. A figure was now being engendered out of that congealed substance, as a child's form is engendered and slowly brought into being.

### Divine Providence Shapes the Human Form Out of Heat and Cold

It is at precisely this juncture that the author insists the process is not a blind mechanical accident but a directed act of God. The setting of the oil and the first emergence of a shape are the occasion upon which the providence of God descends to govern the work:

`fa-'amidat al-inayat al-ilahiyyah takhtit al-surah wa takwinaha min al-harr wa al-bard kama sha'a 'azza wa jalla.`

"So the divine providence undertook to outline the form and to compose it out of heat and cold, as He, the Mighty and Majestic, willed."

Here is the teaching of *al-inayat al-ilahiyyah*, the divine solicitude. The two elemental qualities of heat and cold — *al-harr wa al-bard* — are the very instruments through which the human shape is sketched and built up. Providence takes them in hand and, working with their interplay, draws the outlines of the form, marks out its sides, and gives it its composition and structure. Nothing in this happens of its own accord; the whole forming proceeds *kama sha'a 'azza wa jalla*, exactly as God in His might and majesty willed it. The drying oil, the summer winds, the heating earth — these are the visible means, but the forming hand is the hand of divine providence.

### Each Star Governs the Making of a Particular Member

The author next reveals the heavenly order presiding over the construction of the body. The making of the human frame was not entrusted to a single agent but parcelled out among the stars, each one charged with a specific portion of the work:

`wa kana kullu kawkabin mutawalliyan li-shay'in min a'da'ihi tilka al-ajsam.`

"And every star was entrusted with the governance of some one of the members of those bodies."

This is a foundational principle of the cosmology being unfolded: every *kawkab*, every star or planet, is a *mutawalli* — a deputed overseer — placed in charge of forming a particular limb or organ of those primordial bodies. The human frame is, on this reading, a distributed work of the heavens, each of its members the special concern of a particular celestial governor. No part of the body comes into being without its appointed stellar custodian. The body is thus a kind of map of the heavens, each *'udw* answering to the star that fashioned it.

### The Engraving of the Human Form: Utarid with Zuhal and Qamar

The construction of the body reaches its culmination when the governance of the heavens arrives at a particular configuration. The author writes:

`fa-lamma intahat al-tadbir ila 'Utarid bi-murafaqat Zuhal wa al-Qamar, naqashat surat al-insaniyyah.`

"And when the governance reached 'Utarid, with the companionship of Zuhal and al-Qamar, the human form was engraved."

This is the moment of *naqsh surat al-insaniyyah* — the impressing, the engraving, the chiselling of the human form. It occurred when the *tadbir*, the great governing cycle of the planets, had passed through its long succession and come at last to 'Utarid (Mercury). And it occurred not under Mercury alone but with the *murafaqat*, the accompaniment, of two further planets: Zuhal (Saturn) and Qamar (the Moon). The precise junction was the closing phase of Saturn's accompanying turn and the opening phase of the Moon's — the final reach of Zuhal and the first reach of Qamar converging upon Mercury's governance. At that conjunction the human shape was at last graven into the prepared matter. The body whose members the stars had severally fashioned was now stamped, as a whole, with the form of humanity.

### Rising From the Earth Little by Little: The Quranic Growth

Once the form was complete, the body began to rise. The author describes its emergence in vivid, gradual terms:

`fa-lamma kamulat, irtafa'a al-jismu min mawdi'ihi shay'an ba'da shay'in, ila an sara qa'idan 'ala 'iliyayatihi, wa zaqanuhu 'ala rukbatayhi, wa qad lazima zira'ayhi ila ma waliyahu min jismihi, wa huwa mujtami'un 'ala hadhihi al-hay'ah al-madhkurah.`

"And when it was completed, the body rose from its place little by little, until it came to be seated upon its haunches, its chin upon its two knees, its two arms wrapped about what was nearest to it of its body, gathered up in this described posture."

The forming body lifted itself from the earth as a fetus matures in the womb — by slow, imperceptible degrees, the way a child grows across its nine months in the mother. It rose *shay'an ba'da shay'in*, a little after a little, until it came to sit upon its haunches, with its chin resting upon its two knees and its arms folded in close around the body that was nearest to them — the very posture of a child curled within the womb. It was gathered and folded into this single compact attitude.

Of this, the author tells us, the Noble Book itself has given report, in the words of God Most High:

`wa Allahu anbatakum min al-ardi nabatan.`

"And God grew you from the earth as a [progressive] growth." (Quran 71:17)

This is the scriptural anchor of the whole account. The first man did not come into the world by the ordinary birth of a child from a mother's womb; rather, he was *grown* out of the earth itself, sprouting and rising from his place as a plant rises from the soil — *nabatan*, a growing-forth. The Quranic verb of growing, *anbata*, is taken at its full weight: the earth was the matrix from which the human being was brought up, member by member, into his folded, seated, fetal posture, exactly as the verse declares.

### The In-Breathing of the Life-Giving Spirit: From Vegetative to Sensory Soul

Up to this point the growing body had been governed by one kind of soul. The decisive transition came at the moment of its true birth into the world, when a new and higher animating principle was breathed into it. The author writes:

`thumma nafakha fihi al-ruh al-muhyiyah al-ilahiyyah al-muttasilah bihi min ash'iat al-shams wa al-qamar.`

"Then He breathed into it the life-giving divine spirit, connected to it from the rays of the sun and the moon."

To grasp this we must distinguish two souls. While the being was still being shaped and grown — while it was, as it were, within the womb of the earth — what governed it was the *nafs al-namiya*, the vegetative or growth soul. This is the soul of mere growth and nourishment, the principle that draws sustenance and increases the body's mass, exactly as it governs the child in the mother's womb. The center and origin of this vegetative soul is the Moon, *Qamar*. As the things that are grown out of the earth have the Moon for their wellspring, so the soul that governs the womb-bound, earth-grown body traces its home to Qamar.

But when the being is at last born forth into the world, a second soul is conferred upon it: the *nafs al-hissiyyah*, the sensory soul. This is the *ruh al-muhyiyah*, the life-giving spirit, divine in origin, which God breathes into the form at its emergence. And this sensory soul is connected — *muttasilah* — to the rays of two heavenly bodies at once: the rays of the sun (*aftab*) and the rays of the moon (*qamar*). With its appearance the being passes from a merely growing thing into a sentient, moving, feeling creature. The vegetative soul belonged to the womb and to the Moon; the sensory soul belongs to the world and is linked above all to the Sun, though woven also of the Moon's light.

### Amir al-Mu'minin's Definition of the Sensory Soul

The nature of this sensory soul the author does not leave to inference, for it was given an authoritative definition by Amir al-Mu'minin, 'Ali ibn Abi Talib — *salawatullah 'alayh* — when a questioner asked him concerning the *nafs al-hissiyyah*. To him 'Ali replied with a definition of remarkable precision, naming the soul's nature, its origin, the moment of its union with the body, its seat, and its proper operation:

`hiya quwwatun falakiyyah, asluha al-aflak, wa ittihaduha 'inda al-wiladat al-jismaniyyah, wa maqarruha al-qalb, wa fi'luha al-hiss wa al-harakah wa al-sawlah wa al-ghalabah.`

"It is a celestial power; its origin is the celestial spheres; its union [with the body] is at the time of bodily birth; its seat is the heart; and its action is sensation, movement, assault, and dominance."

Let us take this definition member by member, for each clause carries a teaching. First, the sensory soul is a *quwwah falakiyyah*, a celestial power — not an earthly or material faculty but a force descended from the spheres. Second, *asluha al-aflak*: its origin and root lie in the celestial spheres, the seven heavens, and within that totality its fundamental tie is to the Sun, *Shams*, which is the heart of the spheres. Third, *ittihaduha 'inda al-wiladat al-jismaniyyah*: its union with the body occurs precisely at the moment of physical birth — when the child enters the world, the influences of the sun begin to act upon it, and it is in this moment of emergence, not before, that the sensory soul is joined to it. It is not required that the sun's rays fall upon the newborn directly; it is enough that, upon coming into the world, the effects of the sun begin to operate upon it, and through them the sensory soul is born within. Fourth, *maqarruha al-qalb*: its established seat is the heart. The heart is the center and meeting-point of all things in the body; and just as Shams is the heart of the celestial spheres, so the human heart is the place upon which the influence of that solar heart of the heavens is cast. From the heart, then, the sensory soul radiates outward into the whole body. Fifth and finally, *fi'luha al-hiss wa al-harakah wa al-sawlah wa al-ghalabah*: its operation is fourfold — to sense and feel, to move, to assault or advance, and to dominate or overpower. These four are the characteristic acts of the sensory soul, and though they take their origin from the heart, they spread thence to every part of the body.

### The Cause of the Soul's Departure and Its Return to the Sun

If the sensory soul enters at birth, the question naturally follows: what causes it to depart? Here 'Ali's definition is completed by an account of its separation and return:

`sababu iftiraqiha ikhtilaf al-mawadd, fa-idha faraqat 'aadat ila ma minhu badat.`

"The cause of its separation is the disharmony of the materials; and when it separates, it returns to that from which it began."

The body is compounded of four cardinal materials — *al-mawadd* — which are the four humors: *safra* (yellow bile), *sawda* (black bile), *balgham* (phlegm), and *damm* (blood). So long as these four remain in their proper proportion and concord, the soul abides in the body. But when *ikhtilaf al-mawadd* arises — when a disharmony enters among them, so that they fall out of their mutual fitness and balance — this discord becomes the occasion of death, and at death the sensory soul separates from the body. And once separated, it does not perish but returns: *'aadat ila ma minhu badat*, "it returns to that from which it began." As a traveller, however far his journey, makes his way home in the end, so the sensory soul retraces its path to its source. Its source is the celestial spheres, and within them the Sun; for the sensory soul began from the Sun's rays, and to the Sun it returns when the body that housed it dissolves. *Badat* here means precisely "that from which it commenced" — its origin and beginning. (We may note the symmetry the author draws: the vegetative soul has its home in Qamar, the Moon, and the sensory soul has its home in Shams, the Sun; each, at the dissolution of the body, makes its way back to its own celestial origin.)

### The Nourishment of the Earth-Born Body and Its Swift Maturing

The author now returns to the bodily life of this first earth-grown man, for the connecting of the sensory soul to the body sets the whole organism into vital motion:

`wa lamma ittasala bihi hadhihi al-nafs al-hissiyyah, darat fi jami' al-jism wa ahsha'ihi wa a'da'ihi, wa tanaffasa min kharayhi, wa tanassama al-nasim al-mu'tadil.`

"And when this sensory soul connected with it, it circulated through the whole body, its inward parts and its limbs; and it breathed through its two nostrils, and inhaled the temperate breeze."

With the soul's joining, life coursed through every region of the frame — through its viscera and its members alike. The being breathed through its nostrils and drew in the mild, temperate air, the *nasim al-mu'tadil*, the balanced breeze that is the breath of life. And with this breathing its movement began to grow, its body to expand and increase, and its sensation to quicken — for through inhaling that temperate breeze the body grew, spread, and came to feel, just as a growing child does.

As for its sustenance, the author describes a wonderful mechanism. The body fed itself upon the very oil out of which it had been formed:

`wa rijlahu yajzibani baqiyyat dhalika al-duhn alladhi takawwana minhu dhalika al-jism, kama yajzibu hajar al-maghnatis al-hadid li-l-mushakalah wa al-munasabah allati baynahuma.`

"And its two legs drew up the remainder of that oil from which the body had been composed, just as the lodestone draws iron, by reason of the resemblance and affinity that lie between them."

There remained in the earth a residue of the primordial oil from which the body had been made, and the man's two legs drew this oil upward into the body as nourishment. The author likens this drawing to the action of the lodestone — *hajar al-maghnatis*, the magnet — upon iron. The magnet attracts iron toward itself not by force imposed from without but by an inner kinship, a *mushakalah* and *munasabah*, a resemblance and suitability between the two. The lodestone is itself of the nature of iron; and because of this affinity it draws iron to itself. In just this way, by the affinity between the body and the oil from which it was made, the legs drew that oil up — `wa yas'adu fi sa'ir al-badan`, "and it rose into the rest of the body." (Here the author observes the natural bond between the feet and the brain: there is an ancient connection between the soles and the seat of the mind, so that when one's feet are massaged a tranquillity steals over the brain and one is drawn toward sleep. This affinity between sole and brain is of a piece with the drawing of nourishment up from the legs.)

The body also took its nourishment by rolling upon the earth — `yatamarraghu` — turning over and over in its place in the dust, *taratan ba'da ukhra*, time after time, so that the matter of the earth might cling to it and be absorbed; and it drew the oil in also through its pores, *min masamin*, the pores scattered over the whole of its body, the hands and nose and every part alike. By all these means it was nourished and made to grow:

`wa huwa yaghdhihi wa yunmihi ila an istakmala tis'ata ashhurin wa qa'imatayni wa sab'in yawman.`

"And it nourished it and made it grow until it completed nine months — two hundred and seventy days."

Nine months — that is, two hundred and seventy days, thirty multiplied by nine — were fulfilled. Then, the author continues, `thumma fataha fahu wa talaba al-ghidha' min famihi al-muhayya' lahu` — "then it opened its mouth and sought the food prepared for it through its mouth." Having completed its nine months, the being now took its nourishment by mouth, food that had been made ready for it by the earth and the rain. Its body continued, moreover, to absorb the last of that primordial oil little by little as food, until at length it stood and walked. And here the author marks the prodigious vigor of this earth-born creature: within the space of a single year it stood and walked, and `wa huwa fi jussati mithla waladi arba'a sinin` — in the bulk and stature of its body it was like a child of four years. So robust was it that an infant of mere days had the frame of a four-year-old, and within a year had risen and walked. This swift and powerful maturing the author attributes to the surpassing strength of its two parents:

`wa dhalika li-quwwati al-abawayni alladhayni huma al-aflak wa al-ard.`

"And that was by reason of the strength of the two parents, which are the celestial spheres and the earth."

The newborn of the ordinary world is the child of a mother and a father; but this first man had for his parents the heavens and the earth themselves. His father was the celestial spheres, his mother the earth — and from such mighty progenitors he drew a constitution of extraordinary health and strength. Because the nourishment he required was given him in full from these two parents, he was born sound and strong and grew with this astonishing rapidity.

Thereafter he turned for his food first to the fig and the grape — `fa-'amada ila al-tin wa al-'inab` — for these are fruits easily digested, and it is for this that God swore "by the fig and the olive." And beyond these, all the rest had already been made ready for him: `wa qad kana uhiyya lahu sa'ir al-fawakih wa al-hubub wa al-tayr wa al-an'am wa kaffat al-hayawan wa al-nabat ajma'` — "and there had been prepared for him all the other fruits and grains, the birds and the cattle, and every animal and plant entire." The whole creation of fruit and grain, beast and bird and herb, stood ready as his provision. He fed upon all of it and ruled over it, *fa-ightadha bihi wa tahakkama fihi*, by reason of the loftiness of his rank and the majesty of his station. For he was, in the famous phrase, `awwala al-fikrati wa akhira al-'amal` — "the first in conception and the last in execution." The creation of man was the first thing intended and the last thing wrought. God made the minerals, made the plants, made the animals, made the stars — and all of these He made for the sake of man, that man might draw benefit from them. Man therefore had to be fashioned last of all. It is as when one would build a house: first he draws the plan, then lays the foundation, then gathers the cement and the materials and sets everything in place — and only at the very end does the one who is to dwell in it enter. The whole arrangement is made first, for the sake of the resident who comes last. So too the whole order of creation was prepared beforehand, and man, for whose sake it was all prepared, came at the end.

### The Sevenfold Governance of the Planets and the Long Period Before Counting

Having traced the making of the body and the soul, the author now sets the whole event within its vast chronological frame — the great cosmic cycle of planetary governance. He states the governing law:

`wa lamma tawallat al-kawakib al-sab'ah mutawalliyatan li-amri al-kawn fi tadbirihi, sara li-kulli kawkabin ahad wa khamsuna alfa sanatin, wa kanat ibtida' al-tadbir min Zuhal.`

"And when the seven planets undertook the governance of the affair of the world in its ordering, there fell to each planet fifty-one thousand years; and the beginning of the governance was from Zuhal."

This is the doctrine of the *tadbir*, the planetary governance of the cosmos. The seven planets rule the affairs of the world in succession, and to each is allotted a turn of fifty-one thousand years — *ahad wa khamsun alf sanah*. The sequence begins with Zuhal (Saturn) and proceeds in order: after Saturn's fifty-one thousand years comes the turn of Mushtari (Jupiter); then Mirrikh (Mars); then Shams (the Sun); then Zuhra (Venus); then Utarid (Mercury); and finally Qamar (the Moon). Each in its appointed millennia governs the unfolding of the world.

It was within this immense succession that the human form was at last impressed. The author has already told us that the *naqsh surat al-insaniyyah*, the impressing of the human form, took place in the governing turn of Utarid, *bi-murafaqat Zuhal wa al-Qamar* — with the companionship of Saturn and the Moon. Reckoning the cycle from its beginning in Saturn, through Jupiter, Mars, the Sun, and Venus, and into the turn of Mercury, the author places this event roughly after two hundred and fifty thousand to three hundred thousand years of celestial revolution had elapsed. By the time the governance had passed through its long sequence and arrived at Mercury — with Saturn's accompanying turn at its close and the Moon's at its beginning, the conjunction described before — some quarter of a million years and more of the great cycle had run.

Of this immense span the author makes a crucial qualification, lest it be confused with the reckoned ages of sacred history. All these revolutions of the heavens before the appearance of man are an *uncounted* period — a *fatrat*, an interval that is not numbered in the sacred reckoning. They are not part of the counted cycle at all. For the *dawr al-kashf*, the cycle of disclosure, is reckoned only from the beginning of human creation onward; and the manifestation of the *Juz' al-Ibda'i*, the originated portion, likewise belongs to the era that opens with man. The long ages of celestial turning before man are merely the *fatrat*, the preparatory interval during which the prerequisites of the human species were arranged in the world — the minerals, the plants, the animals, all the conditions that man would require. These are *celestial changes*, arrangements antecedent to the *Juz' al-Ibda'i*, and they are not entered into the count. The first ages are the longest of all, fifty-one thousand years to each planet; and it is only with *ibtida' al-khilqat al-insaniyyah*, the beginning of human creation, that the counted cycle of disclosure begins. Everything before is *fatrat* — uncounted preparation.

### The Beginning of the Seventh Millennium: Zuhal With the Moon

When man at last appeared, a new phase of governance opened. The author writes:

`wa lamma zahara al-insan, ibtada'a Zuhal 'inda zuhurihi bi-tadbiri alfi al-sabi'i bi-musharakat al-Qamar.`

"And when man appeared, Zuhal began, at his appearance, the governance of the seventh millennium, with the participation of al-Qamar."

At the very moment of man's emergence, Saturn — Zuhal — commenced the *tadbir alf al-sabi'*, the governance of the seventh thousand-year period, and it did so *bi-musharakat al-Qamar*, with the participation of the Moon. Thus the appearance of the human being coincides with Saturn's entry upon this seventh millennial governance, the Moon sharing in the work. This participation of Qamar is fitting, for the seventh station answers to the final reach of the body's making — the year that faces the consummation of the human form, *li-kawnihi muqabilan* for that completing creation. The Moon, the governor of growth and the home of the vegetative soul, thus presides together with Saturn over the threshold at which man steps forth.

### The Twelve Islands and the Variety of the Human Races

With man's appearance the human form arose not in one place only but across the whole earth, and in a stature befitting that primordial age. The author writes:

`wa zaharat hadhihi al-ashkhas al-bashariyyah bi-l-qamati al-alfiyyah fi kulli jaziratin min al-jaza'ir ithna 'ashar, 'ala ikhtilafi ajnasiha, min 'Arabin wa Fursin wa Hindin wa Rumin wa Sindin wa Habashin wa Barbarin wa Khazarin wa Zayali'in wa Saqalibatin wa Turkin wa Zanjin wa ghayri dhalik.`

"And these human individuals appeared, with the millennial stature, in each of the twelve islands, according to the variety of their races: Arabs, Persians, Indians, Romans, Sindhis, Ethiopians, Berbers, Khazars, Zayalis, Slavs, Turks, Zanj, and others."

The first human forms arose with the *qamah alfiyyah*, the "millennial stature" of that founding age, and they arose across twelve great islands or regions — *jaza'ir* — scattered through the world, not gathered in any single locale. These twelve answered to the variety of the human races, the *ajnas*: there appeared the Arab and the Persian, the Indian and the Roman, the Sindhi and the Ethiopian, the Berber and the Khazar, the Zayali and the Saqaliba (the Slavs), the Turk and the Zanj (the black peoples) — and others beyond these twelve, for the author adds *wa ghayri dhalik*, "and others besides." These regions were spread across the four quarters of the earth — Arabia in one place, Persia in another, India in yet another, and so on — each race appearing in its own island, with still further peoples in regions beyond the named twelve. From every such region newborns came forth; and among them, the author notes, were also those in whom evil and wickedness and regret had their root — the portions of the *nadimin*, the regretful — so that the full range of human kinds, the noble and the base alike, was represented in this primordial dispersal.

### The Island of Sarandib and the Twenty-Eight Most Perfect Individuals

Among all these islands one stands supreme. The author singles out the island of Sarandib for a particular distinction:

`wa zahara fi jaziratin Sarandib, li-annaha min khatti al-istiwa', shukhusun kathirun, min jumlatiha thamaniyatun wa 'ishrun, hum khulasatuhum wa jami'u khilqatihim, wa altafuha wa ashrafuha wa akmaluha, nihayatu al-jinsi al-bashari.`

"And there appeared in the island of Sarandib — because it lies upon the line of the equator — many individuals, among whom were twenty-eight who were the purest of them and of all creation, the most refined, the most noble, the most perfect: the pinnacle of the human species."

Sarandib — the island traditionally identified with the place of Adam's Peak — lies upon *khatt al-istiwa'*, the line of the equator. By reason of this favored situation, *shukhusun kathirun*, a great multitude of individuals, appeared there. The number that arose was large; but from among that whole multitude, *min jumlatiha thamaniyatun wa 'ishrun*, twenty-eight individuals stood out as wholly exceptional. These twenty-eight were the *khulasah*, the very distillation and choicest extract, not only of the people of Sarandib but of all creation. While noble and excellent individuals had appeared in each of the twelve islands, none rivalled these. They were *altafuha wa ashrafuha wa akmaluha* — the most refined, the most noble, and the most perfect of all created beings — *nihayat al-jins al-bashari*, the very limit and summit of the human species, the pinnacle beyond which the human kind does not rise.

### The Twenty-Eight: The Letters and the Lunar Mansions

These twenty-eight are not an arbitrary number, for they correspond to two sets of twenty-eight that the author binds together:

`wa hum al-huruf al-mu'jam, wa hiya al-manazil, wa hiya amthaluhum, wa hum mansubun ilayha.`

"And they are the letters of the alphabet, which are the [lunar] mansions, which are their likenesses, and they are attributed to them."

First, the twenty-eight correspond to the *huruf al-mu'jam*, the twenty-eight letters of the Arabic alphabet. These are called the "mute" letters — *mu'jam* signifying the voiceless, the unpointed and silent — over against man himself, who is the speaking one. The twenty-eight individuals are set in correspondence with these twenty-eight letters of speech. Second, they correspond to the *manazil*, the twenty-eight mansions of the Moon — the stations through which the Moon passes in its monthly journey. The Moon completes its cycle in twenty-eight days, traversing one mansion each day. (There is, the author notes, a slight irregularity in the Moon's course: by reason of a difference in its orbit about the earth, the lunar month at times runs to some twenty-nine and a half days, so that the months are reckoned now at twenty-nine days and now at thirty, giving a discrepancy of about a day and a half in the month. This variation has been present from the beginning; it lies in the orbit itself, not in any change over time. Yet the mansions proper are twenty-eight.) These twenty-eight mansions are the likenesses of the twenty-eight individuals — *hiya amthaluhum* — and the individuals are attributed to them, *mansubun ilayha*. Thus the twenty-eight perfect persons, the twenty-eight letters of speech, and the twenty-eight stations of the Moon stand in a single correspondence, the human summit answering to the alphabet of speech and to the monthly mansions of the Moon.

### Perfection Without Maturation and the Turning Toward the Tenth

These first and most perfect individuals attained their completeness in a manner utterly unlike the ordinary growth of mankind. The author writes:

`lam yablugh dhalika al-'alam rutbat al-ihtilam illa wa hum ashkhasun fi halat al-kamal min al-ajsam wa al-aqwal wa al-af'al.`

"That world did not reach the rank of maturation save that they were individuals already in a state of perfection in body, speech, and action."

That entire world did not so much as arrive at *rutbat al-ihtilam* — the rank of bodily maturity, the ordinary coming-of-age through which a human being passes to adulthood — before these individuals were already complete. Their growth was so swift, by virtue of the *tadbir* that had governed their making, that they attained perfection at once: perfection of body (*al-ajsam*), perfection of speech (*al-aqwal*), and perfection of action (*al-af'al*). They did not climb by slow degrees to maturity; they stepped forth already mature and whole in every dimension of the human being.

And in this state of completeness they directed their gaze upward. The author continues:

`bal balaghu ma'a al-kawni al-ibtida'i bi-'uqulin ibtida'iyyah, wa hasharu bi-hashratihim ila al-'Ashir bi-musa'adatin min al-'inayati al-ilahiyyah.`

"Rather they attained, together with the originated world, with originative intellects; and they turned, in their whole assembly, toward the Tenth, with the assistance of the divine providence."

Endowed with primordial, originative intellects (*'uqul ibtida'iyyah*) befitting that first world, they turned their collective vision and aspiration — *bi-hashratihim* — toward the *'Ashir*, the Tenth. And this turning was accomplished *bi-musa'adatin min al-'inayati al-ilahiyyah*, with the aid and support of the divine providence. The same providence that had outlined their forms now lifted their gaze to the highest object of contemplation. We are again reminded that nothing in this consummation is achieved by the creature's unaided power; the turning toward the Tenth is itself a gift of *al-inayat al-ilahiyyah*.

### The Tenth (al-'Ashir): Adam al-Hayat and the Place Where the First Intellect's Rays Fall

The section closes with the doctrine of the Tenth, toward whom the twenty-eight turned. The author identifies him:

`alladhi huwa jaddu al-jaddi al-akhir, al-mutawalli li-amri kawni al-tadbir, al-muqabil li-l-'aql al-awwal fi 'alam al-ibda', idh huwa matrahu shu'a'atihi.`

"He who is the ancestor of the final ancestor, the one entrusted with the affair of the world of governance, the one set opposite the First Intellect in the world of origination, for he is the place where its rays fall."

The *'Ashir*, the Tenth, is *jadd al-jadd al-akhir* — the ancestor of the final ancestor. He is Adam al-Hayat, Adam of the Living, the one who carries out the *tadbir*, the governance of the world. He has attained the perfection of his rank and, having entrusted the governance to the one beneath him, occupies this tenth station. He is *al-mutawalli li-amri kawni al-tadbir*, the one charged with the affair of the world of governance. And he holds a station of the highest dignity in the cosmos: he is *al-muqabil li-l-'aql al-awwal fi 'alam al-ibda'*, the one set opposite the First Intellect (*al-aql al-awwal*) in the world of origination (*'alam al-ibda'*). His defining glory is given in the final clause: *idh huwa matrahu shu'a'atihi* — "for he is the place upon which its rays fall." He stands in the world of origination directly over against the First Intellect, in the very position upon which the rays and outpourings of the First Intellect descend. He is the receiving-place of the light of the First Intellect — the locus where the radiance of the *'aql al-awwal* alights and is gathered.

This new ancestor — *al-jadd al-jadid* — who appeared in the world of nature (*'alam al-tabi'ah*) is thus at once the summit toward which the twenty-eight perfect individuals aspired and the channel through which the highest light of origination reaches the world of nature. He is the ancestor, the trustee, and the imam; through him the assistance of the heavens descends, and toward him the pinnacle of the human species turns its gaze. With the appearance of this final, perfected ancestor — the Tenth, set opposite the First Intellect and bathed in its rays — the long preparation of the cosmos reaches its consummation, and the human story, no longer mere uncounted *fatrat* but the very beginning of the counted cycle of disclosure, is at last begun.

## Man, the Quintessence of the Two Worlds

### The Most Noble of His Kind: Man Among the Kingdoms of Being

When the second teacher, al-mu'allim al-thani, moved his pen from the second tablet and caused his whole essence to grow and ripen within it, that essence was at last obtained and brought to its arrival. From this point the higher world, al-'alam al-a'la, began to be manifested upon him, and he became the seat and locus of the highest and choicest specimen within each of the kingdoms of existence. He became the place of the red ruby, al-yaqut al-ahmar, from among the mineral gems; the place of pure gold, al-dhahab al-ibriz, from among the malleable bodies that are wrought and pounded; the place of the date palm, al-nakhlah, from among the plants; and the place of man, al-insan, from among the animals.

The author sets this out in his own words: `illa minhā mahal al-yaqūt al-ahmar min al-jawāhir al-ma'daniyyah al-musaffāt, wa mahal al-dhahab al-ibriz min al-ajsām al-mutarriqah, wa mahal al-nakhlah min al-nabāt wa al-insān min al-hayawān bal huwa ashraf wa altaf wa a'lā` — that is, his place is that of the red ruby among the purified mineral gems, and the place of pure gold among the wrought bodies, and the place of the date palm among the plants, and of man among the animals; rather he is more noble, more subtle, and more exalted than all of them.

Each term in this comparison carries a precise weight. The red ruby is reckoned the most precious among the gems that nature forms within the earth; pure gold — ibriz means exactly that, gold that is unalloyed and complete, gold at one hundred percent purity — is the most perfect among the malleable metals that the smith hammers and works; the date palm stands as the noblest among the plants; and man, by common acknowledgement, is the best and highest among the animals. So when the author lines these four together, he is assembling four parallel images of supremacy, each one the crown of its own order of being.

Yet the comparison is not made to set this person beside the ruby and the gold as their equal. The chain of analogies serves a single purpose: to declare his nobility, his sharafiyyah. The author is emphatic that even these summits of their respective kingdoms fall short of him — `bal huwa ashraf wa altaf wa a'lā`, "rather he is more noble, more subtle, and more exalted." He is more noble than the ruby, more subtle than the gold, higher than the palm and the most excellent man. The analogies, in other words, name the ceiling of the created orders only in order to say that he stands above that ceiling.

This excellence belongs to a particular person within the company of the twenty-two. Out of those twenty-two persons, al-ashkhas al-thaniyah wa al-'ishrin, there is one single person, shakhsan wahid, who is the purest of them, asfahum, the most subtle of them, altafahum, the most excellent of them, afdalahum, and the most noble of them, ashrafahum. It is this distinguished individual whom the four images crown, and it is to him that the higher world is manifested.

### The Essence and Choicest of Both Worlds

Summing up this entire description of the most noble person, the author delivers his governing thesis: `wa 'alā jumlat al-qawl fa-huwa zubdat al-'ālamayn al-jirmānī wa al-jismānī wa safwatuhumā` — "and in summary of the statement, he is the quintessence of the two worlds, the celestial and the physical, and the choicest of them both."

The phrasing is exact and deliberate. He is the zubdah — the cream, the distilled essence — of the two worlds together: the celestial world, al-'alam al-jirmani, which is the world above and contains within it all the higher orders of being, and the physical world, al-'alam al-jismani, the world of bodies below. He is not merely a creature of one of these realms but the very essence and choicest extract of both. The celestial world is everything that lies above; the physical world is everything that lies below; and the purest thing distilled out of both of them is the form of the natiq, the form upon which revelation, wahy, descends. He is their safwah, their elect and clarified portion, the point at which the two worlds meet and yield their finest.

### The First Man's Witness to the Oneness of the Creator

From the description of his rank the author turns to the act by which this person came to know his Lord, and here lies one of the central teachings of the passage. The author writes: `thumma innahu nazara bi-dhātihī ilā dhātihī wa ilā abnā' jinsihī wa mā fawqahū` — "then, indeed, he looked, by his own self, at his own self, and at the sons of his kind, and at what was above him."

The force of bi-dhatihi is essential and the author dwells on it. The man looked at his own self by his own self — that is, without any speaker to instruct him, without any teller, without anyone's aid, without any external teacher at all. For who was there to teach the first man? There was none. His teacher was his own self. He looked at himself, then at the sons of his kind, then at what stood above him: `wa mā fawqahū min samā' marfu'ah wa arz mawdu'ah wa nujūm sayyārah wa anhār jarrārah wa falak dawwār wa layl wa nahār` — the raised heaven, the laid-out earth, the moving stars, the flowing rivers, the revolving firmament, and night and day.

Contemplating himself, the sons of his kind, and all these worlds of differing kinds — the heaven, the earth, the stars, the rivers, in all their variety of species and persons — he reasoned his way to a Creator. He perceived that for himself and for his kind and for all these manifold worlds there must be a Maker who is not similar to them and does not resemble them in any way. There is a Creator, he concluded, and that Creator is unlike anything He has made.

Upon this conclusion he performed the great act of negation and affirmation: `fa-nafā 'anhū wa 'an abnā' jinsihī sā'ir tilka al-mawjūdāt al-ilāhiyyah wa athbatahā li-mubdi'ihim wa mūjidihim ta'ālā` — "so he negated from himself and from the sons of his kind all those divine existents, and affirmed them for their Originator and their Creator, may He be exalted." He stripped divinity away from every created existent — from himself, from his kind, from the heavens and stars and all the rest — and affirmed divinity for their Originator and Maker alone. Then he bore witness to His oneness, wahdaniyyah, and glorified Him and held Him holy: he negated divinity from all that is created and affirmed it for the One who originated it, the Most High.

### The Second Perfection and the Knowledge of What Was and What Will Be

Through this witnessing, the man's connection with the higher realities was established. The author continues: `wa shahida lahū bi-al-wahdāniyyah wa sabbahahū wa qaddasahū fa-ittasala bihi al-ma'ādin al-ilāhiyyah wa al-anwār al-sha'sha'āniyyah wa al-tā'idāt al-malakūtiyyah wa al-mutahhid bi-dā'irat al-'aql al-'āshir`. By bearing witness to the divine oneness and glorifying and sanctifying his Creator, he was joined to the divine sources, al-ma'adin al-ilahiyyah; to the radiant, dazzling lights, al-anwar al-sha'sha'aniyyah; and to the angelic supports, al-ta'idat al-malakutiyyah, united with the sphere of the Tenth Intellect.

The Tenth Intellect looked upon him, supported him, and aided him, and through this aid he reached the second perfection, al-kamal al-thani, and his essence was made to blaze with a universal illumination: `wa balagha al-kamāl al-thānī wa ja'alahū min ashwāq shu'ā'ihī fa-ashraqat dhātuhū bi-ishrāq kullī`. He was furnished with good counsels, al-mawa'iz al-hasanah, and he was given to look upon both the hidden things and the present things. From the unseen and the witnessed he came to comprehend the whole range of the arts and disciplines: `wa ittala'a min al-mughayyabāt wa al-hādirāt 'alā jamī' al-funūn wa 'alima mā kāna wa mā sayakūn` — "and he was informed of the unseen and the present concerning all the arts, and he knew what was and what will be," ma kana wa ma sayakun.

This knowledge of "what was and what will be" pertains to the physical world, al-'alam al-jismani. It is precisely in connection with the world of bodies that the knowledge of all that has happened and all that is yet to happen is here being affirmed of him. He was made to know the entire span of the temporal world, past and future, through the illumination granted to him at the second perfection.

### His Station in the World of Bodies: The Likeness of the First Intellect

Having attained this illumination, the man assumed his station within the lower realm. The author writes: `wa qāma fī 'ālam al-jismī muqābil al-'aql al-awwal fī 'ālam al-'aql wa maqām al-'aql al-'āshir fī maqāmihī munfaridan bi-rutbat al-wahdah wa maqām al-'azamah mawsūman bi-al-ibdā' fī hādhā al-'ālam`. He stood in the world of bodies in a station corresponding to the station of the First Intellect within the world of intellect.

The parallel is structural. Just as the First Intellect holds the rank of oneness at the summit of the intellectual world, so this person holds the answering rank in the world of bodies; and just as the Tenth Intellect governs from its own station, so he stands in its likeness here below. He is unique in the rank of oneness, munfaridan bi-rutbat al-wahdah, occupying the station of greatness, and he is marked with origination, mawsuman bi-al-ibda', within this world — he is the one through whom origination is brought into the world of bodies. From this station of solitary oneness and stamped origination, he summoned the twenty-seven persons to the oneness of Allah: `wa da'ā tilka al-ashkhās al-sab'ah wa al-'ishrīn ilā tawhīd Allāh`.

His call was to three connected things: `wa tanzīh wa tajrīd` — to the oneness of Allah, tawhid; to His purification from all likeness, tanzih; and to the abstraction of Him from all that is created, tajrid. And the twenty-seven answered him at once, hastening, and they responded to his call obediently: fa-ajabuhu musri'ina wa labbaw da'watahu mut'i'ina.

### The Covenants, the Laws, and the Marriages That Populated the World

Having gathered their assent, the man bound the twenty-seven by solemn obligation: `wa akhadha 'alayhim al-'uhūd al-mu'akkadah wa al-mawāthīq al-mughalladhah al-mushaddadah`. He took from them confirmed covenants, al-'uhud al-mu'akkadah, and reinforced, strengthened, and tightened pacts. He then taught them what he himself was joined to and from whom — `wa 'allamahum mā ittasala bihi wa ilayhi min al-mutahhidīna bihi` — instructing whoever had accepted his call in the realities to which he was connected; and he gave them to look upon the unseen sciences and the secrets of the kingdom, al-'ulum al-ghaybiyyah wa al-asrar al-malakutiyyah.

After this he turned to the ordering of the religious law. The author records: `ba'da an awdaha lahum al-halāl wa al-harām wa 'allamahum al-qadāyā wa al-ahkām wa qannana lahum qawānīn al-diyānah wa afādahum jamī' mā yahtājūna ilayhi min al-ifādah wa amarahum an yash'urū al-'ālam nikāh tilka al-harā'im al-lātī qad sunna lahum`. He clarified for them the lawful and the forbidden, the halal and the haram; he taught them the cases and the rulings, al-qadaya wa al-ahkam; he legislated for them the laws of religion, qawanin al-diyanah; and he benefited them with everything of which they stood in need.

Among his commands was one made necessary by the founding circumstance of the world's settlement. He ordered them to make the world aware of, and to enter into, marriage — nikah — with those women otherwise forbidden, al-hara'im, for whom in this beginning the sunnah was now established and made permissible. By the sunnah he set down, marriage that would in later cycles be forbidden was at this founding moment made lawful to them, so that the world might be populated and the human kind might spread. He entrusted them with all of this — the rulings, the sciences, the obligations — committing every command to their keeping as a favor he bestowed upon them, and he laid up in their minds whatever he had been commanded to convey.

### The Sixteen Chosen and the Eleven Preserved in the Islands

From the twenty-seven the man then selected his hierarchy. The author writes: `wa ikhtāra minhum sittata 'ashara shakhsan, afdaluhum bābun wa al-thānī lahū hujjah wa dā'iyayn ahaduhumā dā'ī balāgh wa al-thānī dā'ī mutlaq wa ithnā 'ashar ja'alahum hujaj al-layl wa tarakahum bi-hadratihi lā yufāriqūnahu`. He chose sixteen persons. The most excellent of them he made a Bab; the next he gave a Hujjah; and two he made da'is — one of them a da'i balagh, a da'i of conveyance, and the second a da'i mutlaq, an absolute da'i. These four — the Bab, the Hujjah, the da'i balagh, and the da'i mutlaq — are his immediate du'at, the inner four. The remaining twelve of the sixteen he made the hujaj al-layl, the proofs of the night, and he kept them in his own presence, not parting from him.

Counting with the man himself at the head, this company numbers seventeen: he is the first, and with him are the sixteen he chose, of whom four are his du'at and twelve are the hujaj al-layl in his presence. The author then turns to those who were not kept at the center: `hādhā ihdā 'ashara shakhsan wa hum al-bāqūna fī al-jazā'ir`. The remaining persons — eleven of them — are the ones dispersed in the islands, al-jaza'ir. With a twelfth stationed in the island in which the man himself resides, these constitute the persons posted abroad, and `rutbatuhum mahfūzah fī jamī' al-jazā'ir ilā al-ān wa ilā ākhir al-zamān` — their rank is preserved in all the islands until now and until the end of time.

### The Mission in the Islands and the Proofs of the Day

Each of those stationed in the islands was charged with carrying the mission in his appointed place. The author writes: `wa amara kulla wāhidin minhum bi-al-da'wah lahū fī jazīratihi al-latī ja'ala ilayhi wa ja'ala lahum hujajan nahāran ithnā 'ashar`. He commanded every one of them to conduct the da'wah, the summons, on his behalf in the island assigned to him, and he furnished each with twelve hujaj al-nahar, twelve proofs of the day. These twelve are the subordinate proofs posted in the islands beneath the island-chief, and they too bear the rank of hujaj — the proofs of the day, as distinct from the proofs of the night kept in the central presence.

So the island-chief took up the da'wah on the man's behalf, peace be upon him, as he was commanded, and he restrained all the people of the islands from what he had forbidden them: `wa qāma bi-al-da'wah lahū salāmu Allāhi 'alayhi kamā umira wa anzajara jamī' ahl al-jazā'ir 'ammā nahāhum`. Concerning the standing of these proofs of the day the author makes a particular point: `wa hādhā al-hujaj 'āriyūna 'an afwāl al-bātil al-muhiss wa marfu' 'anhum af'āl al-sitr wa al-takāyid zāhiran li-'ulūwwi darajātihim`. These proofs are stripped bare of the afflictions of perceptible falsehood, and the acts of veiling, sitr, and of deception, takayid, are lifted from them outwardly, on account of the loftiness of their ranks. Because they are men of such elevation, the burdens of concealment that the law of veiling imposes are removed from them; the rulings of sitr that bind ordinary ranks do not fall upon them. Their station places them above the ordinary mechanism of concealment by which the da'wah otherwise shields itself.

The author adds that these twelve hujaj al-nahar are the most excellent of all the twenty-eight — `wa hum afdal jamī' al-thamāniyah wa al-ishrīn` — and that the hierarchy ascends in degrees above them: above the twelve stand the four, and within the four the Bab, and then the Imam. `wa Bābuhum wa sā'isuhum al-Imām 'alayhi al-salām`: their Bab, and the one who governs them, is the Imam, peace be upon him. The hujaj al-nahar are themselves established and well-grounded, and they are acquainted with all these stations — `wa hujaj al-nahār 'ārifūna bi-hadhihi al-maqāmāt kullihā illā annahum dūna maqām hā'ulā'i` — except that they stand below the station of those higher ones. They are aware of every rank above them even while remaining beneath it.

### The Twenty-Eight as the Possessors of Knowledge in the Qur'an

The author now gathers the full company and identifies it with a Qur'anic designation. The whole assembly numbers twenty-eight — the man at the head together with the twenty-seven — and they are all engaged in the mission and learned in its realities: `wa qā'imuna bi-al-dā'irah wa muta'allimūna jamī'an`. Of this company he declares: `hā'ulā'i al-thamāniyah wa al-ishrīn hum al-ulū al-'ilm al-ladhīna dhakarahum Allāh min ahl al-shahādah` — "these twenty-eight are the possessors of knowledge, ulu al-'ilm, whom Allah mentioned, from among the people of witnessing."

The Qur'anic verse to which he points is this: `Shahida Allāhu annahū lā ilāha illā huwa wa al-malā'ikatu wa ulū al-'ilm` — "Allah bears witness that there is no god but Him, as do the angels and the possessors of knowledge." The author reads the three witnesses of this verse onto the hierarchy he has described. Allah, who bears witness here, is the First Intellect; the angels, al-mala'ikah, who bear witness alongside Him, are the other intellects; and the possessors of knowledge, ulu al-'ilm, are these twenty-eight persons. The verse, so understood, names the three tiers of witnessing being together — the First Intellect, the subsequent intellects, and the twenty-eight bearers of the realities in the world of bodies.

### Originated Without Procreation, and the Self-Taught Founder of the Law

The author specifies the mode of existence proper to these twenty-eight: `wa hā'ulā'i al-thamāniyah wa al-ishrīn shakhsan al-ladhīna hum 'alā al-jasad al-ibdā'iyyah al-mawjūdūna bi-ghayri tanāsul`. They are upon originating bodies, al-jasad al-ibda'iyyah, and they exist without procreation, bi-ghayri tanasul. They do not come to be through the ordinary lineage of generation; their bodies are bodies of origination, brought into being by the direct creative act rather than by the chain of birth.

Concerning their head, the author returns to the founding act of negation and affirmation: `kamā sabaqa bihi al-qawl anna al-nāfī al-muthbit ra'īsuhum al-wāhid al-ladhī aqāma al-sharī'ah wa huwa muqābil al-mubdi' al-awwal al-nāfī al-muthbit al-muwahhid`. As was already stated, the negator-affirmer is their one head — al-nafi al-muthbit, the one who negates and affirms — and he is the very one who established the shari'ah. He is the unifier, al-muwahhid, the one who taught the oneness of God: he negated every god besides Him, saying illa hu, "except Him," and affirmed Him alone. He who performs this negation and this affirmation is the founder of the sacred law; and in the first cycle, the first dawr, the legislator who did this was Adam. He stands opposite the First Originator, al-mubdi' al-awwal, mirroring in the world of bodies the negating, affirming, unifying reality of the highest realm.

What marks this head out is the source of his knowledge: `al-ladhī ta'allama al-tawhīd min ghayri mu'allim yutaqaddamahu wa lā yasbaquhu... bal ahjamahu min dhātihi bi-dhātihi`. He learned the oneness of God with no teacher preceding him and none coming before him; rather he apprehended it from his own self by his own self — min dhatihi bi-dhatihi. Where every other rank receives tawhid from one above it, the head of the twenty-eight grasped it unaided, from himself and by himself, which is why the four images of supreme nobility were fitted to him at the outset.

### The Recurrence of the Twenty-Eight with Every Imam

This pattern of the twenty-eight is not confined to the first cycle. The author teaches that the company manifests anew with every Imam, in a fixed and recurring shape. The first four of the twenty-eight are the Dhu'l-Sayf Da'iyah — the Imam who is the bearer of the sword and the summoner — together with the three who stand in his presence: the Bab, and two da'is. These four are the Imam's house: the Dhu'l-Sayf Da'iyah, his Bab, his Hujjah, and his two da'is, the da'i mutlaq and the da'i mahdud. Beyond these stand the twelve of the islands.

The governing teaching the author draws from this arrangement is the perpetual recurrence of the number. With every Imam, the manifestation of the twenty-eight comes to pass. When the Imam appears as Dhu'l-Sayf Da'iyah, he stands at his station with twenty-seven individuals about him, completing twenty-eight. When the Qa'im comes, peace and blessings upon him, he too appears with the full twenty-eight. Every bearer of the sword who comes brings this same company. Allah has established a fixed pattern, a naqsh, and upon this pattern the order of the hudud will recur perpetually, always and without end.

### The Six Days of Creation and the Reliance on La ilaha illa Allah

The author then connects this recurring structure to the temporal frame of creation. `wa khalaqa al-'ālam fī sittat ayyām` — Allah created the world in six days. These six days, however, are Allah's days, and one day with Allah is not a day of our reckoning: one yawm with Allah is equal to one thousand years. Reckoned thus, the creation of the world unfolded across six thousand years; in this span of six thousand years the creation that is spoken of took place, and the particular era within it — the era associated with 'Utarid, Mercury — is a matter the author leaves for further inquiry and research.

Behind the whole structure stands the kalimah upon which the choice of faith relies, for its reliance rests upon La ilaha illa Allah. This testimony carries twenty-eight letters and qualities, and in every circle of the hierarchy the number twenty-eight is necessarily present, mirroring the twenty-eight letters of the kalimah. Wherever the full hierarchy stands, it is structured by this word. The author maps its parts onto the ranks: the kalimah divides into nafi and ithbat, negation and affirmation. The ithbat, the affirmation, is the Dhu'l-Sayf Da'iyah; the nafi, the negation, is the Bab. Beyond these two stand the common letters — the alif, the lam, the ha — which are shared. The alif belongs to the Dhu'l-Sayf Da'iyah; the others answer to the two further classes of persons.

For there are, in this reading, three kinds of person who are always present: first, the Imam himself, who is the Dhu'l-Sayf Da'iyah; second, those who dwell within his presence, his hadrah, whether Bab or Hujjah; and third, those who dwell outside the presence, in the islands. These three classes endure perpetually. Above this triad the author counts the four words, al-kalimat al-arba': the Dhu'l-Sayf Da'iyah, his Bab, those who dwell in his presence, and one further. Then he counts the seven — the Dhu'l-Sayf Da'iyah with his Bab, his Hujjah, his da'is, and the proofs of the day — and the twelve, who are the twelve islands. In every act of worship the same testimony shines forth: in the prayer, in the fast, in purification, in each observance there is the manifestation, the jalwah, of La ilaha; and so here too the same word structures the entire hierarchy of being.

### The Word Between the Originator and the Originated

Returning to first principles, the author asks what the governing kalimah ultimately is, and answers with a teaching of the highest order: `bayna al-mubdi'i wa al-mubda'i kalimah` — "between the Originator and the originated is a Word." Between the Mubdi', the First Originator, and the mubda', the first thing originated, there stands a Word; and the thing that is higher than the first originated is light, nur — the light of the kalimah La ilaha illa Allah. This Word, this light, occupies the place between the Originator and His first origination, and it stands above the First Originated itself.

It is to this Word, as light, that the most noble person was joined, and from this point the author opens his great exposition of the Pillar of Light.

### The Pillar of Light: The Knowledge Flowing from Allah to the Tenth Intellect

The author writes: `thumma innahu ittasala bi-hādhā al-shakhs al-fādil al-sharīf al-kāmil 'amūd al-nūr, ittasala 'amūd al-nūr alladhī huwa liman ittasala bihi al-fawz wa al-ni'mah wa al-kawn fī jumlat ahl al-rahmah` — "then he was connected with this virtuous, noble, perfect person; the Pillar of Light was connected; the Pillar of Light, such that to whoever connects with it belong success and grace, and being among the people of mercy."

He then defines the Pillar precisely: `wa huwa al-'ilm al-muttasil min Allāh ta'ālā ilā al-'aql al-awwal` — "and it is the knowledge connected from Allah, the Exalted, to the First Intellect." The Pillar of Light, amud al-nur, is knowledge: it is that same light, the flowing of knowledge from Allah down to the First Intellect, in keeping with the principle that between the Originator and the originated there is a Word. It is the perfect knowledge, descending from its source toward the First Intellect.

From the First Intellect it descends further, link by link, through the whole chain of intellects: `wa minhu ittasala bi-al-'aql al-thānī al-tālī lahu, wa minhu ilā al-thālith, wa halumma jarran ilā al-'aql al-'āshir al-muntahī ilayhi amr al-tabī'ah`. From the First Intellect it is joined to the Second Intellect that follows it, and from that to the third, and so on in succession until it reaches the Tenth Intellect, to which the command of nature, amr al-tabi'ah, finally terminates. The Tenth Intellect is the one to whom the affair of the world of nature is entrusted, al-mawkul ilayhi amruha, the governor and administrator of that world. It is the one that stands in the world of nature in the station of the First Intellect, al-qa'im fiha maqam al-'aql al-awwal, and that is unique in the rank of unity, al-munfarid bi-rutbat al-wahdah.

The author offers a vivid image of the Pillar's nature: it is the perfect intellect, al-'aql al-kamil, and yet it is not a being, a dhat, but a light, beginning from the First Intellect — `saba'atu ashbāhin nūrāniyyah`, seven luminous specters. Within it lies perfect knowledge; it is the light of Allah, His support and aid, ta'id, and His knowledge, 'ilm; and everything is connected to it. He likens it to a channel through which everything flows: one needs only to tap into it. We too are connected to this channel, but on account of our darkness, our zulmah, we are unable to draw from it as we ought.

### The Strong Rope and the Firmest Handhold

Whoever does succeed in connecting with the Pillar of Light, through the noble covenant, attains the highest reward, and this connection is the very rope of Allah. The author teaches that the Pillar is the strong rope extended from the side of Allah, the Exalted, the rope that joins toward all the ranks, the hudud; and this is what is named the firmest handhold: `fa-qad istamsaka bi-al-'urwat al-wuthqā lā infisāma lahā` — "he has grasped the firmest handhold, which never breaks." Whoever lays hold of the Pillar of Light has grasped a rope whose breaking is impossible; this firmest handhold, al-urwat al-wuthqa, is the very strand of the amud al-nur.

The reward of this connection is stated plainly. The Pillar of Light belongs to whoever connects with it: to him belong success, fawz, and grace, ni'mah, and he becomes one of the people of mercy, ahl al-rahmah. The connection is made through the noble covenant, the 'ahd al-sharif, and the one who takes that covenant is the last one in the chain, the respondent at the lowest rank — yet through the covenant his connection runs all the way up: from the Tenth Intellect, which is our immediate point of contact, through the whole succession of intellects to the First Intellect, and beyond the First Intellect to the Exalted Originator by way of the kalimah La ilaha illa Allah. The taking of the noble covenant is the beginning, the ibtida', of this ascent. The Pillar is thus a single, continuous, unbroken chain that runs from the constellations of the firmament, falak al-buruj, up to the First Intellect, and above which stands the kalimah itself; it is one chain, the chain of La ilaha illa Allah.

### Spiritual, Not Physical: The Pillar Descends upon the Rational Soul

The author is careful to forestall any material misconception of the Pillar of Light. It has, he insists, no physical station within it whatsoever; it is above all physical density, above kasafah. It is light — and not even light of the order we know, for the light of the sun is itself a kind of physicality, whereas this nur is a far higher light that has never descended into density at all. How could it have entered density, when it is the very thing connected from Allah, the Mubdi' the Exalted, to the First Intellect? Our own connection is with the Tenth Intellect; and by what means can we come to recognize the Exalted Originator? Only through connection with that light.

Because it is spiritual and not physical, the Pillar does not descend upon our body. The author teaches: `fa-huwa lā yanzilu 'alā al-jasad wa innamā yanzilu 'alā al-nafs al-nātiqah` — it descends not upon the body but upon the rational soul, the nafs natiqah. The rational soul, in turn, has a connection with our body; and since at the moment of the noble covenant the nafs natiqah is present, it remains bound up with the Pillar from that moment. The descent of the Pillar, then, is a descent upon the soul that reasons and bears witness, the soul that was present and engaged at the taking of the covenant, and it is through that soul that the body is touched.

### The Magnet: Drawing the Forms of the Beneficiaries to the Forms of the Benefactors

The Pillar of Light is, the author further teaches, connected from every station to all its ranks and to those who answer its call: `wa huwa al-muttasil min kulli maqāmin ilā kāffat hudūdihi wa mustajībī da'watihi`. From every station it runs to all that station's hudud and to the respondents of its call. And it performs a magnetic work: `wa huwa al-jādhib li-suwar al-mustafīdīn ilā suwar al-mufīdīn` — "and it is the attractor of the forms of the beneficiaries to the forms of the benefactors."

The Pillar acts as a beneficial magnet. By its operation, the form of the beneficiary — the one who receives — is drawn and joined to the form of the benefactor, the one who gives. After death, the rational soul, the nafs natiqah, of the beneficiary is drawn to the rational soul of the benefactor; the magnet pulls it across. The author is explicit in his term: `wa huwa maghnātīsuhum al-mumsik li-suwarihim wa nufūsihim` — "and it is their magnet, the holder of their forms and their souls." The Pillar of Light is a magnet, a medium in this sense, that attracts the forms of the beneficiaries toward the forms of the benefactors.

But it does more than attract; it also holds and preserves. It is the magnet that holds fast their forms and their souls together: `al-hāfiz li-kulli shay'in minhum`, the preserver of every single thing of theirs — both their forms and their souls. When a form has ascended, it ascends together with its companion, the sensory soul. The author corrects himself here on a fine point: the soul carried up with the form is not a physical thing but the sensory soul, the nafs hissiyah. This nafs hissiyah, having become attached to the form and having had luminosity created within it, accompanies the form in its ascent. To keep the two together — the form and its sensory soul — fixed in one place is the office of the Pillar of Light. It gathers them, holds them, and preserves them, so that neither can be undone.

### What Is Preserved and What Is Lost: The Form, the Sensory Soul, and the Rational Soul

From this preserving work the author draws a careful distinction concerning what can and cannot be corrupted. A form that has ascended can no longer be ruined; a sensory soul that has become good and has separated from the body can no longer be ruined; what has been made pure is permanently fixed and cannot fall back down. This is one of the divine arrangements, one of the tadabir: Allah has ordained that whatever has become pure can never afterward descend.

Yet the author qualifies this with reference to the case of one who occupies a living rank where the outcome is still open — where there remains the possibility of becoming good or bad, and where the reward of goodness is still a condition not yet secured. If such a person turns bad and his high rank is corrupted, then the portion of form in him that had become pure is extracted from him, and it is his sensory soul that suffers corruption. The form belonging to the rank itself is not corrupted — that has been made, and the part that became pure remains pure. What is corrupted is the sensory faculty, the nafs hissiyah, which falls into corruption through the bad company the person has kept and through which he ruins his own form. The rational soul, the nafs natiqah, cannot be corrupted at all: if it has ascended, it has left the man and gone up, and it is beyond ruin.

The author distinguishes the souls in their order of ascent. The blaming soul, the nafs lawwamah, does not itself ascend; if it makes progress, it becomes the sensory soul, the nafs hissiyah, and ascends from there — so the form that is carried up is the nafs hissiyah. There are persons of a very high rank whose nafs lawwamah does ascend as well, but the discussion here concerns ordinary people. Beneath these stands the commanding soul, the nafs ammarah: the nafs ammarah bi-su' commands evil, while the nafs ammarah bi-khayr commands good, and these are two distinct things. The fuller treatment of how all this relates to the bodily structure, the haykal, the author defers to a later chapter devoted to it.

### Divine Providence: The Pillar That Conveys to Each His Appointed Portion

The Pillar of Light is, the author teaches, what is named the divine providence and the divine power: `wa yuqālu 'alayhā 'ināyat al-ilāhiyyah wa al-qudrat al-ilāhiyyah`. It is the preserver, the divine providence, the Pillar of Light, the divine power — all of these together. And it extends its preserving care down to the smallest portion of sustenance destined for each person.

The author states it thus: `wa huwa al-hāfiz li-al-'uzw wa al-qit'ah 'alā sāhibihā hattā lā ya'kul dhālika illā al-ma'bar alladhī sāra nafs al-kabsh fīhi` — "and it is the preserver of the limb and the piece for its owner, so that none eats it except the passage, al-ma'bar, in which the soul of the ram has traveled." The Pillar preserves every limb and every piece of nourishment for the one whose appointed portion it is, ensuring that none consumes it except the very passage, the ma'bar, the human channel through which that portion is destined to pass. The soul of the slaughtered ram is present in its parts, and its effect carries through; the Pillar of Light draws the portion and brings it to the person whose share it is destined to be. The water of ablution, wudu, that comes to one's hand is precisely the water appointed for it; the morsel of food that comes and touches the body is exactly the portion destined to be one's own. So great is the task the Pillar of Light performs: it ensures that one's own thing reaches oneself and not another.

The author dwells on the mechanism with a homely example. Food is placed in a dish; a person extends his hand toward it. Is it the action of the hand that draws those particular parts to him, or are those parts already destined to be his, so that the hand merely goes to where they are? The answer is the latter: the portion is his by appointment, his share that must reach him, and what protects it from straying elsewhere is the Pillar of Light. It is not the reaching of the hand that produces the result, but the plan of Allah. And the saying of Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim is what sets the Pillar's effect in motion: by pronouncing it, the operation of the amud al-nur begins and the appointed portion comes; otherwise, even a portion that is genuinely ours may take long to reach us. The ma'bar is the place of passage, the channel through which one's own thing flows: `al-ma'bar alladhī sāra nafs al-kabsh fīhi`, the passage in which the soul of the ram has traveled.

The Pillar's preserving care extends likewise to the leftovers of the saints: `wa huwa al-hāfiz li-fuzālat al-awliyā'`. It is the preserver of the fuzalat al-awliya', the remainders left over from the meals and affairs of the friends of Allah, which the Pillar of Light guards so that they reach specific, appointed people. And it is the very thing that distinguishes one portion from another and that recovers it from wherever it has gone: `wa al-mumayyiz lahā min ghayrihā wa al-mustakhrij lahā min haythu kānat`. Though things become mingled in air, water, and earth, the Pillar separates each portion from the rest; even after the passage of a thousand years, the very limbs of a particular person reappear in the same way, and it is the Pillar of Light that has preserved and distinguished them. It distinguishes a portion from all that is not its own, and it extracts it from wherever it may be — whether in fire, air, water, earth, or mineral — drawing it out and bringing it to its rightful owner. This is what is called divine providence, 'inayat ilahiyyah, the divine arrangements, al-tadbirat, and the divine decrees, al-muqaddarat al-ilahiyyah, all of which belong to the Pillar of Light.

### The Pillar Is Reserved for the People of the Covenant and of Wilayah

This entire capability, the author makes clear, is not given indiscriminately. It is bound to wilayah, devotion. He asks in whom this capacity lies and answers: in the awliya', the friends of Allah, the noble elders — those in whom the leaven of wilayah is present, who are for that reason called wali. The awliya' are those who love; the friends of Allah are defined by their devotion. And the wilayah of Mawla Ali is necessary for this, the author teaches, because the Pillar of Light is for one purpose and one alone: it is only and exclusively for those who take the noble covenant, the 'ahd al-sharif, and who possess wilayah. Without the covenant and the devotion, there is no connection to the Pillar; with them, the whole providential operation of the amud al-nur is opened to the believer.

### Flowing Knowledge, Pervading Light, Spirit, and Word

The author then gathers up the many names of the Pillar of Light: `wa yuqālu 'alayhā al-'ilm al-jārī wa al-nūr al-sārī wa al-rūh wa al-kalimah wa jamī' suwar al-wujūd al-rūhāniyyah wa al-jismāniyyah`. It is called the flowing knowledge, al-'ilm al-jari; the pervading light, al-nur al-sari; the spirit, al-ruh; and the Word, al-kalimah — the kalimah La ilaha illa Allah. It is named, too, all the forms of spiritual and physical existence.

And all these forms, with their followers, from the highest rank to the lowest, are joined to it: `wa jamī' suwar al-wujūd al-rūhāniyyah wa al-jismāniyyah wa atbā'uhum min al-'aql al-awwal ilā al-mustajīb bihi muttasilūna wa ilayhi ma'tūfūn` — all the forms of spiritual and physical existence and their followers, from the First Intellect down to the respondent, are connected by it and joined to it. The whole order of being, from the First Intellect to the answerer of the call at the lowest rank, hangs upon this single Pillar.

The bond that joins them is love, for wilayah is love. As Hasan Shakir Sahab observed in a discourse, the Prophetic saying holds: `al-mar'u yuhsharu ma'a man ahabba` — "a person will be resurrected with the one he loves." Whoever loves another from his heart establishes thereby his connection with that one, and on the Day of Gathering he will be raised together with him. By the love of the heart the connection is made, and through that connection the lover is joined to the same Pillar of Light as the one he loves.

### The Forms That Cling Like Flies to Honey

The author closes his exposition of the Pillar with a striking image of how firmly the forms adhere to it: `wa mathaluhu ka-mathali 'ūdin mamdūdin qad asābahu al-'asal. Fa-idhā waqa'a 'alayhā al-dhubāb lam yakid yubārihu` — "and its likeness is the likeness of an extended stick of wood that has been touched by honey; so when flies land upon it, they can hardly leave it."

Just as flies that alight upon a stick smeared with honey stick fast and can scarcely depart from it, so the forms cling to the Pillar of Light and do not leave it: `wa kadhālika al-suwar lā tubārihu hādhā al-'amūd`. The forms do not depart from this Pillar; all of them remain connected to it, for it is a powerful magnet, and with the forms their souls too are bound. The reason is that the Pillar is their carrier, their attractor, and the one that elevates them in their ascent: `li-anna huwa al-hāmil lahā wa al-jādhib wa al-murtaqī lahā 'inda su'ūdihā`. It carries the forms, draws them, and raises them up at the moment of their ascension — and so they cling to it as the fly clings to the honeyed stick.

### The Snatching of the Form from the Hypocrite, the Apostate, and the One Who Incurs Wrath

The same magnetic Pillar that draws and preserves the forms of the worthy also withdraws the form from those who fall away. The author teaches: `wa min dhālika anna al-munāfiq wa al-murtadd wa al-maghdūb 'alayhi tuslabu sūratuhu tilka al-ilāhiyyah allatī kānat 'indahu li-ta'alluqihā bi-dhālika al-'amūd al-munāsib lahā, wa ta'ūdu nafsuhu nāqisatan shā'i'atan fī jismihi` — "and from that: the hypocrite, the apostate, and the one who has incurred wrath — his form is snatched away, that divine form which was with him, because of its connection to that pillar appropriate to it; and his soul returns, deficient and base, pervading his body."

From the hypocrite, al-munafiq; from the apostate, al-murtadd, the one who changes his religion; and from the one upon whom wrath has fallen, al-maghdub 'alayhi — the wrath being that of the Lord, the Rabb — the divine form is snatched away. The good form that had been created in such a person, the form by which his connection to the appropriate Pillar had been established, is withdrawn by that very Pillar. The author illustrates the principle of a good form being removed and reassigned with the case of Dawood and the wife of Uriah: a good form was created and, it was said, was taken out and given to another — to Zainab, the daughter of Hukman, for her training. In the same way, the Pillar of Light takes back the form. The Pillar has no owner of its own; the form it withdraws is held in reserve, to be given to whomever it must be given. And the soul of the one from whom the form was snatched returns deficient, naqisah — that is, base — pervading his body once more. The Pillar of Light, which holds and attracts the divine forms — `li-anna dhālika al-'amūd huwa al-mumsik li-tilka al-suwar al-ilāhiyyah al-jādhib lahā` — is the very agent of both the holding and the withdrawal, preserving the form in the worthy and reclaiming it from the fallen.

### The Turning Away of the Forms in the State of Janabah

Finally, the author treats the effect of marital intimacy upon the connection with the Pillar of Light, and the restoration of that connection through purification. He teaches: `wa matā talaba wāhidun min al-hudūd aw al-mustajibīn mubāsharat ahlihi malat 'anhu tilka al-suwar wa inharafat 'anhu` — "and whenever one of the ranks or the respondents seeks intimacy with his spouse, those forms incline away from him and deviate from him." When any one of the hudud or the mustajibin — even at the very time when he is engaged in the work of benefiting and being benefited — has intimacy with his spouse, the divine forms incline away and turn aside from him.

This turning away persists until purity is restored: `hattā lā tu'āyinu min dhālika shay'an illā waqt al-firāgh wa 'ādat ka-hālatihā` — "until it does not behold anything of that except at the time of completion, and it returns to its state." The Pillar does not behold anything of him while he remains in the state of janabah, the state of ritual impurity, of non-purity; only at the time of completion, when purity is regained, does the form return to its original state. And this withdrawal and return, the author insists, is itself a grace: `lutfan min Allāh ta'ālā wa rahmatan lahum` — "as a kindness from Allah, the Exalted, and a mercy for them." It is a kindness from Allah toward His servants and a mercy for them, by which He looks upon them. So, the author adds, glorified and exalted is He above what the wrongdoers say: `fa-subhānahu wa ta'ālā 'ammā yaqūlu al-zālimūna 'uluwwan kabīra` — far above the great saying of the unjust is He.

The form that was disconnected reconnects when the person performs ghusl, the full ritual washing; and for women likewise, the reconnection follows ghusl. This, the author notes, is why prayer is forbidden during menstruation: the woman has not performed ghusl, and so she remains in a state of impurity. The means by which the Pillar reconnects is, above all, the kalimah — the saying of A'udhu billah min al-shaytan al-rajim, Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim, which is the greatest of words, accompanied by the intention of purifying oneself.

The author closes with the Prophetic ruling that governs the thoroughness of this purification: the Prophet, peace be upon him, said `fī kulli sha'ratin janābah` — "in every hair there is janabah." In every single hair there is ritual impurity. One must therefore wash with water so abundantly that every hair is reached, washing the hair very thoroughly — and women especially, who have long hair, must wash their hair well. And the clothes that were worn at the time of intimacy should not be worn at the time of prayer.
