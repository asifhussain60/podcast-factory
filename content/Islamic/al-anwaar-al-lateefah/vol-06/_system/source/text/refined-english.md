## The Letters of the Quran and the Foul Soul

### The Sanctity of the Letters and the Danger of Alteration

There is no lighter matter in the religion than the way a man carries the speech of Allah upon his tongue, and yet none is treated more lightly. When a reciter changes the vowels of the Qur'an — when he replaces a *zabar* with a *zer*, or a *zer* with a *pesh*, when he drops a word, abandons a word, or swallows the words so that they are no longer fully pronounced — an alteration enters into the speech of Allah. By the time that speech reaches the servant who listens, it is no longer exactly what was revealed. And that alteration, small as it may seem, becomes a cause for sin. This is the gravity that hangs over the whole science of recitation: the words are not the reciter's own, to be handled as he pleases. They are a trust whose every letter, every mark of vocalization, must be conveyed intact.

For this reason a man must be extraordinarily careful whenever he speaks the Qur'an, writes it, or pronounces it. The danger is not theoretical. Among those who have copied out the Holy Qur'an, errors of the text are common; many who write it down do not grasp the meaning and so write something else entirely in place of the true word, and in this way changes creep into the word of Allah. Among those who recite, scarcely a reciter is free of mistakes. The remedy is vigilance — the most extreme care that one is able to muster — in saying, in writing, and in speaking the sacred text.

The principle that governs all of this is a strict one: the letters of the Qur'an must never be changed. A clear illustration is the practice, found among some, of inserting a saying about the virtue of a chapter into the chapter itself — reciting within *Yasin*, for instance, a phrase praising *Yasin* as though it belonged to the *surah*. Such an insertion is an addition, a *ziyadah*, and an addition to the Qur'an is a sin. The man who does it may believe he is honoring the chapter, but in truth he has tampered with revelation. Here a necessary distinction must be drawn. The words of separate prayers and supplications may differ from one another; a supplication is the speech of the servant, and its wording is not fixed by revelation. But the letters of the Qur'an stand on entirely different ground. They may not be added to, subtracted from, or altered in any way, because they are the very speech of God.

The completeness demanded extends down to the full and clear pronunciation of each letter. While reading the Holy Qur'an the entire letter must be sounded out, completely and distinctly; one must not let a *zabar* slide into a *zer*, nor pronounce a letter so faintly that it is lost. When a vowel is turned in this way, the meaning does not merely shift — it changes completely. Few among those who recite ever perceive this, because few understand the meaning of what they read. Consider the way a single letter can carry an entire sense. In Arabic poetry the word for a donkey is written `gadha`, and the question of how it is written is not idle. One writes it with a *dal* and then with the letter that has two eyes — the form `ھ` of the Hindi tongue — and reads it accordingly. But Arabic does not possess that two-eyed letter at all; what Arabic possesses is the plain `ha`. The two-eyed letter belongs to Hindi, not to Arabic. The reciter who carries the habits of one tongue into the other will pronounce wrongly without ever suspecting it, and the meaning will be deformed in his mouth. He must keep firmly in mind, then, the proper way each letter is to be read in the language to which the Qur'an belongs.

Because errors are so easily made and so rarely noticed, it is good that all in the gathering read aloud rather than reserving recitation to a few. When everyone reads, their faculties are opened, their qualities are drawn out, and the barrier that keeps a man from reading in company is removed. More than that — how else are mistakes to come to light? If a man reads aloud, his error becomes audible and can be corrected; or, if he prefers, he may come privately and read so that his fault may be pointed out to him in confidence. Either way, the purpose is correction, and correction requires that the reading be heard.

### The Prayer of Completion

When a man has read the Qur'an through to its end, he closes with the prayer of completion, the *khatm al-Qur'an*, and the opening words of that prayer set the tone for everything the science of recitation guards. He begins: `Sadaqa Allahu-l-'Adhim alladhi la ilaha illa huwa-r-Rahman-ur-Rahim` — Allah, the Great, has spoken the truth; there is no god but Him, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful. Allah has spoken the truth, the reciter affirms, such that there is no god besides Him. He is `al-Rahman al-Rahim`, the Most Gracious and the Most Merciful; He is *Mannan*, the Bestower, and *Karim*, the Generous. `laysa kamithlihi shay'un` — there is nothing whatever like unto Him; no thing in all creation furnishes an example of Him. `Wa huwa-s-Sami'-ul-'Alim` — and He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing. Then the witness is sealed: `Wa ballaghat rusuluhu risalatihi wa nahnu 'ala dhalika min al-shahidin` — and His messengers delivered His messages, and we are among those who bear witness to that. The closing phrase is `irghaman lil-jahidin`, in defiance of the deniers. The word `irgham` carries the image of crushing the nose into the dust; it is a rebuke flung at those who have denied — a declaration that they have denied wrongly, uttered as one might grind the noses of the deniers into the ground.

From this affirmation the one who completes the Qur'an turns to petition. `Allahumma-nfa'na bil-Qur'an al-'Adhim wa barik lana fi-l-ayati wa-dh-dhikr al-hakim` — O Allah, grant us benefit from the Great Qur'an, and grant us blessing in its verses and in the wise remembrance. `Wa taqabbal minna qira'atana wa du'a'ana, ya Rabbi, ya Mawla, innaka anta-s-Sami'-ul-'Alim` — and accept from us our recitation and our supplication, O Lord, O Master; indeed You are the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing. `Wa la tadrib bihi wujuhana ya Ilah al-'alamin wa ya Khayr al-nasirin` — and do not strike our faces with it, O God of the worlds and Best of helpers. Then comes the great series of requests that the Qur'an itself become a living spring within the believer: `Allahumma-j'al al-Qur'ana rabi'a qulubina wa shifa'a sudurina wa dhahaba humumina wa ghumumina wa takfiran li-khatayana wa tamhisan li-dhunubina` — O Allah, make the Qur'an the spring of our hearts, a healing for our chests, a remover of our worries and sorrows, an atonement for our sins, and a purification for our wrongdoings. The reciter asks further that it be a breadth and a blessing within his sustenance, `sa'iqana wa qa'idana`, his driver from behind and his leader in front, `wa dallana wa dalilana ilayka`, his guide and his proof toward God Himself — `wa ila jannatika jannat al-na'im`, toward the gardens of bliss, `wa darika dar al-salam`, toward the abode of peace. There he asks to be set `ma'a-lladhina an'amta 'alayhim min al-nabiyyina wa-s-siddiqina wa-sh-shuhada'i wa-s-salihin` — in the company of those upon whom God has bestowed His favor: the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous. `Wa hasuna ula'ika rafiqa` — and how excellent are these as companions. The petition is sealed with the verse `Dhalika-l-fadlu min Allahi wa kafa bi-Llahi 'alima` — that is the bounty from Allah, and Allah is sufficient as a Knower.

The completion prayer then turns, remarkably, to confess the very faults of recitation that the whole science is meant to prevent, asking God to overlook them: `Allahumma taqabbal minna khatm al-Qur'an, wa tajawaz 'anna ma kana minna fi tilawat al-Qur'an, min khata'in aw nisyanin aw tahrifi kalimatin 'an mawadi'iha aw taghyiri harfin aw taqdimin aw ta'khirin aw ziyadatin aw nuqsanin aw ta'wilin 'ala ghayri ma anzaltahu aw raybin aw shakkin aw ta'jilin 'inda tilawatihi aw sur'atin aw zayghin lisani aw wuqufin bi-ghayri waqfin aw idghamin bi-ghayri mudghamin aw izharin bi-ghayri bayanin aw maddin wa tashdidin aw hamzatin aw jazmin aw i'rabin bi-ghayri ma kana katabtahu minna 'ala-t-tamami wa-l-kamal` — O Allah, accept from us the completion of the Qur'an, and overlook whatever has come from us in its recitation: mistakes, forgetfulness, the alteration of words from their proper places, the changing of a letter, bringing a word forward or holding it back, adding or omitting, interpreting it otherwise than You revealed it, doubt, uncertainty, haste in reciting, undue speed, a slip of the tongue, stopping where there is no stop, merging where there is no place to merge, clarifying where there is no place to clarify, errors in elongation and emphasis, in *hamza*, in *jazm*, or vocalization other than as You wrote it — and accept it from us in completeness and perfection.

This is a confession of the whole catalogue of recitational error, and it is worth dwelling on what it names. There is plain mistake and there is forgetfulness — forgetting the word. There is *tahrif al-kalimah*, the alteration of a word from its place: the word displaced, dropped out before its time, changed in its very substance. There is *taqdim* and *ta'khir*, the bringing forward of what should come after and the setting back of what should come before, so that order itself is overturned. There is *ziyadah*, addition, and *nuqsan*, diminution. There is interpreting the text other than as it was revealed; doubt; uncertainty; haste and excessive speed in the reciting; the tongue's slip; stopping at a place where there is no permitted stop, and merging two letters where no merging belongs, and pronouncing a letter plainly where it ought to have been merged. And there are the specific failures of *madd*, of *tashdid*, of *hamza*, of *jazm*, and of *i'rab* — the vocalization given other than as it was written. To recognize all these as faults is itself the beginning of careful reading.

The prayer asks God to grant the reciter the rank of one who reads the Qur'an as it deserves: `wa-rzuqna fadla man qara'ahu mu'addiyan haqqahu ma'a-l-i'rabi wa-l-qalbi wa-l-lisan` — and grant us the merit of one who recites it fulfilling its right, with proper vocalization, with the heart, and with the tongue. The phrase `mu'addiyan haqqahu` — one who fulfills its right — is the heart of the request: that the right owed to the recitation of the Holy Qur'an be discharged, and that the reciter be made into such a man. Three things together constitute that right: *i'rab*, the heart, and the tongue. The vocalization must be correct, the heart must be present, and the tongue must form the letters truly. The prayer continues, `wa-h-balana fihi-l-khayra wa-s-sa'adata wa-sh-shahadata wa-l-bisharata wa-l-imana` — and grant us in it goodness, felicity, martyrdom, glad tidings, and faith — and then, `wa la takhtim lana bi-sh-sharri wa-sh-shaqawati wa-d-dalalati wa-t-tughyan` — and do not seal our end with evil, wretchedness, misguidance, and transgression.

The prayer reaches toward the grave and the resurrection. `Nabbihna qabl al-manaya min nawm al-ghaflati wa-l-kaslan` — awaken us before death from the sleep of heedlessness and laziness. `Wa amminna min 'adhab al-qabri min su'al Munkar wa Nakir` — and grant us security from the punishment of the grave, from the questioning of Munkar and Nakir. `Wa min akl al-didan` — and from being eaten by the worms; *didan* are the worms, and this consuming belongs to the grave. `Wa bayyid wujuhana yawm al-ba'th` — and make our faces white on the Day of Resurrection. Here the science of pronunciation again presses in: many recite this as `bayz`, saying `bayz wujuhana`, when the *tashdid* must be sounded clearly so that it is `bayyid` — make our faces bright. `Wa a'tiq riqabana min al-niran` — and free our necks from the Fires, with the *zabar* properly placed upon the *alif*. `Wa min sharri-l-hisabi wa-l-khusran` — and from the evil of the reckoning and the loss. `Bi-rahmatika ya Arham al-rahimin` — by Your mercy, O Most Merciful of the merciful. Even here a question of *madd* arises: why does the elongation fall upon the *ya* of `ya Arham`? Because two *alif*s have come together in one place, and the command in such a place is to stretch.

### The Disciplines of the Tongue: Madd, the Guttural Letters, and Tashdid

The completion prayer, by confessing its faults, opens onto the science of recitation itself — *ilm al-tajwid*, the science by which each letter is given its due. Three of its disciplines deserve to be set out in full, for they are precisely the ones most often neglected.

The first is *madd*, elongation. The very word means to pull, to stretch. The question is where the stretch belongs, and the answer is governed by a rule. *Madd* arises when one of the letters of softness — *alif*, *waw*, or *ya* — is followed by an *alif* or a *hamza*. When such a letter is met by an *alif* or a *hamza*, the elongation comes, and its purpose is to make both letters distinct: the first letter clear, and the letter that follows clear as well. So in `ula'ika` the syllable is drawn out because after it a *hamza* has come; in `yasha'u` the *alif* is stretched because after the *alif* a *hamza* follows, and since the *hamza* is itself a kind of *alif*, written in the form of a *hamza*, the elongation is needed to keep the two from collapsing into one; in `qa'idina` likewise a *hamza* follows the soft letter, and so the *madd* appears before it. The general rule the reciter should carry with him is this: wherever a *hamza* stands, look to what comes before it — if *alif*, *waw*, or *ya* (the letters of *'illah*, of softness) precede it, a *madd* occurs there and both letters are clarified. So when one reads in the completion prayer `ya Arham al-rahimin`, the *madd* upon the *ya* is explained by the two *alif*s meeting; and when one reads `shifa'an`, the *hamza* — written in the form of an *alif* — commands that the word be stretched. Whenever a reader meets a *madd* in the Holy Qur'an, he should ask himself why it has come, and he will find that it has come for just this reason.

The second discipline concerns the letters of the throat, the *huruf halqi*. There are six guttural letters, and they must issue properly from the throat: among them are *ha* and *kha*, and *'ayn* and *ghayn*, and the rest of the six. These letters, taken together, demand that the tongue and throat give each its true point of articulation; when *ba* and *dal* come, likewise, they must be read clearly and not slurred. There was once a book studied for exactly this purpose — the *Tajwid*, the manual of *ilm al-tajwid* — in which the proper formation of every such letter is explained in fine detail, this way and that way, so that none is sounded carelessly. Yet many who recite read in such a manner that what they are reciting cannot be understood. The remedy is simply that they read well, giving each guttural its place.

The third discipline is *tashdid*, the doubling of a letter. A *tashdid* holds a letter for the duration of two — two units of time — and through that double duration the letter must be pronounced with full clarity. The common fault is that the final letter is dropped: a man says `tashdidin` and the *dal* at the end never emerges, when it ought to be sounded. So too with `iyya`: for how long is the *ya* held? For two durations, and it must be pronounced clearly throughout. The same care attends `sabbar`. There the *ba* carries a *tashdid*, and the structure of that doubling reveals what *tashdid* truly is: first the *ba* carries a *jazm* — `sab` — and then the *ba* carries a *zabar*; two things have been joined, a *ba* with a *jazm* and a *ba* with a *zabar*, and by their joining the word becomes `sabbar`. The reciter who lets the doubled letter collapse into a single sounding has lost the very thing the *tashdid* was meant to preserve.

### The Twenty-Eight Letters and Their Origin in the Realm of Spirits

Where do the marks of vocalization — *i'rab*, which is to say *zabar*, *zer*, and *pesh* — come to rest? They are placed upon letters. And how many letters are there? There are twenty-eight. This number is not arbitrary, nor is it merely the count of an alphabet; it answers to a far older reckoning. The twenty-eight letters stand opposite the twenty-eight of the *Qisa-i Ibda'iyah*, the originated drama of creation: with the *Qisa-i Ibda'iyah* there are twenty-eight. As for who it was that manifested these twenty-eight letters in material form within the world — who first set them down as the letters of the alphabet, from *alif* to *ya* — that was the *Juz'-i Ibda'i*, the originated part. The *Juz'-i Ibda'i* taught these letters and brought them into bodily, written existence. And from where did the *Juz'-i Ibda'i* receive them? From the First Intellect, the *'Aql al-Awwal*; and the First Intellect drew them from the *kalimah*, from `La ilaha illa Allah` — there is no god but Allah. Within that *kalimah* there are twenty-eight. The twenty-eight letters issued from there.

The same letters, traced inward, came forth from the realm of spirits and from the *kalimah* `La ilaha illa Allah`, and this is the reason that they constitute the original Arabic — the language in which the alphabet first arose. It is this, and not accident, that lies behind the Qur'an's naming of its own tongue. The name of no other language appears in the Holy Qur'an. The German tongue is not named there, nor France, nor Telugu; none of these has its name in the Book. What the Qur'an names is Arabic alone: `bi-lisanin 'arabiyyin mubin`, in a clear Arabic tongue. In a clear Arabic tongue it was sent down, for Allah chose this language for His own expression — `bi-lisanin 'arabiyyin mubin`.

### The Vocalizations as the Ranks of the Emanated

The placing of the *i'rab* upon the twenty-eight letters is no mere grammatical convenience; it is the cosmic record of how the emanated beings used their freedom. The marks were set upon the twenty-eight letters in the World of Intentions, the *'alam al-niyyat*. There too the number twenty-eight is found, for the First Emanation, the *Munba'ith al-Awwal*, likewise possesses twenty-eight; and every circle that was ever formed, in whatever intellect, has within it a seating of twenty-eight letters. Even the circle of the Imam, peace be upon him — though it is said to number a thousand, and a thousand it is — when its members are added in their various groupings reduces at last to twenty-eight. This is why twenty-eight is a uniquely special number, recurring in every circle of every intellect.

Before the World of Intentions the letters stood bare, without vocalization. Why then were the *i'rab* placed upon them at all? The answer lies in the threefold endowment given to every emanated being. Each was given three things. The first was life, which is to say existence. The second was knowledge — the knowledge of the powers that lie within existence. And the third was free will, freedom itself. In that realm, those who used their freedom to come foremost, to press to the front without delay — the ones who put their free will instantly into action and came *pesh-pesh*, to the very forefront — received a *pesh* upon their vocalization. The one who, out of the same circle, took a delay remained with a *zabar*. And of those who remained with *zabar*, some did not hold even that station but sank lower, going under into *zer*; they remained in the state of *zer*. So the three movements correspond to three ranks of response to the same gift of freedom. The highest movement is *pesh*; below it is *zabar*; below that is *zer*. *Pesh*, then *zabar*, then *zer* — these are the marks of how foremost or how laggard each emanated being chose to be.

Beyond these three lie the compound forms. After *pesh*, *zabar*, and *zer* comes *jazm*. What is *jazm*? It is a *zabar* that has been bent: in essence it is *zabar*, but its movement, instead of running straight, has been turned into a circle and made crooked. Its course was not straight; it became bent, and that bending is the *jazm*. Then comes *madd*, which is two movements joined. The doubled forms show this joining at work. In `maddo` there are two *dal*s: upon the first *dal* there is a *jazm*, and upon the second a *pesh* — so `maddo` joins a *jazm* with a *pesh*. In `madda` there is *jazm* and then *zabar*. In `maddi` there is *jazm* and then *zer*. In each case the *jazm* does not remain alone in its single state but is joined to another mark, so that what was crooked is bound to what is straight. One was crooked, one was straight, and the elongation marries them.

This marrying is the inner meaning of *madd* itself. Wherever a *madd* falls there is a command to stretch, and the reason for the stretching is always the same: two entities of the same nature have come together whose distinction in rank must nonetheless be made clear. The elongation forces that clarity. It must be so, because Allah the Almighty, in creating two entities of one and the same nature, has decreed that there will surely be some difference between them — two individuals of the identical rank cannot be appointed to the very same status; there will always be some distinction. So when two *alif*s come together, the *madd* appears to clarify which is which: had the second *alif* not come, there would have been no *madd*. Where a *hamza* — written in the form of an *alif*, as in `shifa'an` — meets the soft letter, the word must be stretched and read with elongation. The same logic governs `rabbun`: the *ba* carries a *jazm* and then a doubled *pesh*; while `rabbi`, the Lord in the genitive, carries the *jazm* and then a *zer* at its end — again two things joined, the crooked bound to the straight. The man who reads the Holy Qur'an attentively, asking at each *madd* why the elongation has come, will find that the vocalization itself is telling the story of rank and distinction written into the letters from the World of Intentions.

### The Call of the Maqamat to Mortals and to the Concealed Forms

From the letters of the Qur'an the discourse turns to those who carry the call of the Qur'an into the world — the Maqamat, the stations of the Imams, peace be upon them. The text under exposition reads `fa du'a'u al-maqamati 'alayhim al-salam` — the call of the Maqamat, peace be upon them — directed `li-l-ins alladhina hum al-bashar wa-l-jinn alladhina hum hadhihi al-suwar`: to the humans, who are the mortals, and to the jinn, who are these forms. The Imams issue their summons in two directions at once. They call the humans, who are mortal beings; and they call the jinn, who are described as `hadhihi al-suwar`, these forms — forms that have reached the state of jinn and to whom, in that state, the invitation is now extended.

The purpose of this calling is *tawqif* — to make aware. The word must be read carefully, for it is not *tawfiq*, divine success, but *tawqif*, the act of bringing to awareness. The text gives the aim: `li yarja'a man yarja'u wa yusirra man yusirru` — so that he who is to turn back may turn back, and he who is to insist may insist. (A point of grammar belongs here: the *'ayn* of `yarja'a` carries a *zabar* because of an implicit `li an`, a hidden "so that," while `man yarja'u` keeps its *pesh* as a present-tense verb naturally does.) The whole design of the call is to set each one before a choice — to make him aware so that whoever is destined to return is given the moment in which to return, and whoever will insist upon his denial is left to insist. They are placed in that state of awareness and offered the two roads: to turn back or to remain fixed in their obstinacy.

How can the jinn answer such a call at all? Faith, after all, is established through three things together: by word, by bringing the truth into the heart, and by action. So how is a jinn to discharge these? The answer is that those jinn who do accept the call are not left in the abstract; services are entrusted to them. Within the rank of the *Shams* they are assigned duties, and once assigned they are named the *mustaqdimin*, the forerunners, and the *jinn-e-mahmud*, the praised jinn. Their acceptance is sealed by service: each is told, in effect, "this service has been given to you," and within the *Shams* they perform it.

There is a fixed number to this congregation. How many were mentioned in the *Shams*? One hundred and forty thousand. There stands in the *Shams* a permanent gathering of one hundred and forty thousand. When one of them moves on from his place, or falls, another becomes his successor, his *khalifa*, and steps into the vacancy, so that the number never diminishes and the hundred and forty thousand always remain. These are the praised jinn, the *jinn-e-mahmud* and *mustaqdimin*, and the services concerning them are continually performed.

### The Miracle as the Establishment of Proof

The call and the response form a single event whose deeper purpose is the manifestation of a miracle. The text continues: `wa fi du'a'i hadhihi al-suwar al-khabisah wa ijabati al-mujibu minhum izharun li-l-mu'jizati yuzhiruhu al-maqamu 'alayhi al-salam li man hawlahu min waliyyin wa ziddin wa munafiqin` — in the calling of these wretched forms, and in the response of the one who answers among them, there is a manifestation of the miracle, which the Maqam, peace be upon him, reveals to those around him: to the ally, the opponent, and the hypocrite. The calling of these foul forms to the right path, and the answer of those who answer, is itself a sign — a miracle the Imam displays before the three kinds of men who surround him: the *wali* who is his friend, the *zidd* who is his adversary, and the *munafiq* who feigns faith while hiding enmity.

The purpose of revealing the miracle is then stated: `li yakuna dhalika akbara li iqamati hujjah wa asra'a li idhhari al-mahajjah` — so that this may be greater for the establishment of the proof and swifter for the revealing of the clear path. The miracle makes the *iqamat al-hujjah*, the establishment of the proof, weightier and more decisive, and it brings the *mahajjah*, the clear and open road, into view more quickly. Its fruit is twofold: `li yastayqina alladhina utu al-kitab wa yazdada alladhina amanu imana` — so that those who were given the Book may attain certainty, and those who have already believed may increase in their faith. The People of the Book are brought to conviction, and the believers' faith is deepened. And the ultimate purpose is the one given in the scriptural phrase: `wa li yahlika man halaka 'an bayyinatin wa yahya man hayya 'an bayyinatin` — so that he who is to perish may perish by a clear proof, and he who is to live may live by a clear proof. Whether a man falls into ruin or rises into life, it will be upon evidence that leaves him no excuse.

### The Calling of the Jinn of Yemen

The discourse now narrates the great historical instance of this miracle. As the Messenger of Allah, may Allah's blessings be upon him, once did, he sent the companions of Amir al-Mumineen, peace be upon him, toward Yemen — sending with Amir al-Mumineen three companions — and he gave them a command concerning the jinn: `wa amarahum bi du'a'i al-jinn alladhina hum bi wadi Zahr wa Musayyab`, he commanded them to call the jinn who dwelt in the valley of Zahr and Musayyab. He made the companions aware of the jinn's very names, telling them: in that desolate well there are jinn, and these are their names; you are to call them by these names. The naming was essential to the calling, for the summons was to be addressed to particular jinn by their own names in the abandoned well.

The Prophet then declared the stakes. Whoever among them received an answer from the jinn would be the *wasi*, the successor, of the Prophet — and this incident is set down in great detail in the work of Sayyidna Hatim, in the book *Saluni*. He further ordered the sequence in which they were to call: `yad'uhum awwalan Abu Bakrin` — let Abu Bakr call them first; `fa in istajabu lahu wa illa da'ahum 'Umar` — if they answer him, well; if not, let 'Umar call them; `fa in istajabu lahu wa illa da'ahum 'Uthman` — and if they answer him, well; if not, let 'Uthman call them; `fa in istajabu lahu wa illa da'ahum 'Aliyyun ibn Abi Talib` — and if they answer him, well; if not, let them be called by Ali ibn Abi Talib. Then he set down the consequence: `wa'lamu anna man ajabuhu minkum fa huwa wasiyyi fikum wa khalifati 'alaykum` — and know that the one whom they answer from among you is my *wasi* among you and my *khalifa* over you. And he added the binding clause of obedience: `wa man ata'ahu fa qad ata'ani wa man 'asahu fa 'asani wa 'asa Allaha ta'ala` — whoever obeys him has obeyed me, and whoever disobeys him has disobeyed me and disobeyed Allah the Exalted.

The text then states the Prophet's underlying intent: `wa dhalika azzalahum 'ala ta'ati Amir al-Mumineen salawatullahi 'alayhi wa 'ala ma'rifatihi` — and this was to urge them toward obedience to Amir al-Mumineen, the blessings of Allah be upon him, and toward his recognition. The whole arrangement was designed to spur the companions to obey Amir al-Mumineen and to come to true *ma'rifah*, recognition, of him. `Fa madaw 'ala halihim ila an balaghu San'a` — so they proceeded in their condition until they reached San'a. It is in San'a that the Well of Alam is found; the descent the text will later distinguish, into the Well of Dhat al-Alam, also belongs to San'a, while the present episode concerns the valley of Zahr and Musayyab. `Wa kharaju ila haythu umiru` — and they went out to the place where they were commanded.

A grammatical point arises in this very phrase and is worth preserving, for it teaches the indeclinability of certain words. The particle `ila` is one of the letters that govern the genitive, giving a *zer*, a *kasra*, to the noun that follows. There are seventeen such governing letters: *ba, ta, kaf, lam, waw, mundhu, mudh, khala, rubba, hasha, min, 'ada, fi, 'an, 'ala, hatta,* and *ila* — and `ila` is the last of these seventeen. By rule, then, `ila` ought to put what follows it into the genitive, so that one would expect `ila haythi`. Yet the word `haythu` does not change. The reason is that words fall into three kinds — *ism*, the noun; *fi'l*, the verb; and *harf*, the particle — and a *harf* is *mabni*, indeclinable; it does not change, taking neither *zabar* nor *zer* nor *pesh*. `Haythu` is of this indeclinable nature, and so it is always read `haythu`, never `haytha` or `haythi`, even when `ila` stands before it. Hence `ila haythu umiru` — and they went out to where they were commanded.

`Fa hatu fi ahadi al-mawazi'i al-wadi` — so they encamped in one of the places of the valley. Then the calling began, in the order the Prophet had fixed. `Wa taqaddama Abu Bakrin` — Abu Bakr went forward first, alone, toward the appointed place; `wa 'urrifahu wa ashabuhu fi mahattihim` — he had been made aware of it, given the recognition of where to go, while his companions remained in their encampment. He went out by himself, as the method had been shown, and `wa hatafa bi a'la sawtihi ya fulan ya fulan ya fulan` — he called out in his loudest voice, "O so-and-so, O so-and-so, O so-and-so," naming the jinn. `Fa lam yujibhu ahadun` — but no one answered him; the verb is *ajaba, yujibu*, to answer, and none answered. `Fa 'ada ila ashabihi kha'iban` — so he returned to his companions, disappointed, having lost hope.

`Wa taqaddama 'Umar ila dhalika al-mawzi'i duna sahibihi` — and 'Umar went forward to that place, leaving his companions behind, `wa hatafa bi a'la sawtihi ya fulan ya fulan ya fulan` — and called out in his loudest voice, "O so-and-so, O so-and-so, O so-and-so." `Fa lam yujibhu ahadun fa 'ada ila ashabihi kha'iban` — but no one answered him, so he too returned to his companions, disappointed. `Wa taqaddama 'Uthman fa fa'ala ka fi'li sahibayhi` — and 'Uthman went forward and did exactly as his two companions had done before him; `wa lam yujibhu ahadun fa 'ada ila ashabihi kha'iban` — and no one answered him, so he returned to his companions, disappointed, unsuccessful, his hope lost.

`Thumma taqaddama 'Ali ibn Abi Talib ila dhalika al-mawzi'i wa taba'ahu ashabuhu min haythu yazunnuna annahu la yarahum nazirin` — then Ali ibn Abi Talib went forward to that place, and his companions followed behind him from where they supposed he would not see them watching, `li ma yakunu min amrihi wa amrihim` — to observe what would come of his affair and theirs. `Ila an hatafa bi a'la sawtihi` — until he called out in his loudest voice, "O so-and-so." `Fa ajabuhu` — and the jinn answered him. `Thumma da'a ya fulan` — then he called again, "O so-and-so," and again he was answered; `wa hum yastajibuna lahu` — and they kept responding to him, one after another, as he called name after name. `Thumma sallama 'alayhim` — then he greeted them with peace. `Fa qalu wa 'alayka al-salam wa rahmatullahi wa barakatuh` — and they replied, "And upon you be peace, and the mercy of Allah and His blessings," addressing him as `ya Amir al-Mumineen`, O Commander of the Faithful, with `wa 'alayka al-salam wa rahmatullahi wa barakatuh`.

`Thumma da'ahum ila al-shahadatayn` — then he called them to the two testimonies. The two testimonies are `La ilaha illa Allah, Muhammadun rasulullah` — there is no god but Allah, Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah. `Fa shahida man shahida minhum lillahi ta'ala bi-l-wahdaniyyah wa li Muhammadin bi-l-risalati wa-l-'ubudiyyah` — so those among them who testified bore witness to Allah the Exalted with Oneness, and to Muhammad with Messengership and servitude. The servitude is what is meant by `Ashhadu anna Muhammadan 'abduhu wa rasuluh` — I bear witness that Muhammad is His servant and His Messenger; `'abduhu wa rasuluh`, His servant and His Messenger. And they bore witness further `wa li 'Aliyyin Amir al-Mumineen bi-l-wilayah` — and to Ali, the Commander of the Faithful, with the *wilayah*, his authority and guardianship. Thus the jinn who testified affirmed three things at once: the Oneness of Allah, the Messengership and servitude of Muhammad, and the *wilayah* of Ali as Amir al-Mumineen.

`Wa tashakhkhasa lahum minhum man tashakhkhasa` — and there took bodily form for them, from among the jinn, the one who took bodily form; `wa mada ma'ahum ithnan` — and two of them went along with Ali. Two individuals took on material form and accompanied him. `Wa huma 'Abdullah Mard-shah wa 'Abdullah 'Ard-shah` — and they were named 'Abdullah Mard-shah and 'Abdullah 'Ard-shah. `Ila an wasalu ila Rasulillahi salawatullahi 'alayhi` — until they reached the Messenger of Allah, the blessings of Allah be upon him. (Sayyidna Hatim has narrated this miracle of Amir al-Mumineen in a very thick published book, in which the incident is detailed; it is found also in *Saluni*.) `Fa jaddadu islamahum bayna yadayhi` — so they renewed their Islam before him; `wa hasuna islamuhum` — and their Islam became excellent; `wa 'adu ila ashabihim` — and they returned to their companions, going back, that is, to the Well of Alam.

The chapter closes with the verdict on this affair: `wa kana hadha al-amru min akbari al-mu'jizat li-l-natiqi wa-l-wasiyyi salawatullahi 'alayhima fa ma zadahum ula'ika al-munafiqin illa bu'dan wa kufran` — and this affair was among the greatest of miracles for the Natiq and the Wasi, the blessings of Allah be upon them both; yet it increased those hypocrites in nothing but distance and disbelief. The hypocrites here are the first, the second, and those who went and were not answered: the very greatness of the miracle, which drew the jinn to faith, drove them only further from God and deeper into denial.

The text then distinguishes a separate episode, lest the two be confused: `wa ka nuzuli Amir al-Mumineen 'alayhi al-salam ila bi'ri Dhat al-'Alam marratayn li munajatihi al-jinn huna` — and like the descent of Amir al-Mumineen, peace be upon him, into the Well of Dhat al-Alam twice, for his communion with the jinn there. This is not the incident of the Well of Alam but a different one: the San'a episode of Wadi Zahr and Musayyab is one thing, and this descent into the Well of Dhat al-Alam, twice, is another. `Wa qatlihi min qatala minhum bi-l-islam man aslama` — and his slaying of those among them whom he slew, and the Islam of those among them who submitted. Into that well Amir al-Mumineen went down twice to commune with the jinn, killing those of them he killed and accepting the Islam of those who accepted Islam.

### Ham ibn al-Him and the Jinn Who Submitted to Every Natiq

A further instance is narrated to the same purpose. `Wa ka wusuli al-Ham bin al-Him ila hadrati Rasulillahi salawatullahi 'alayhi wa huwa qa'idun fi masjidihi wa-l-muslimuna hawlahu` — and like the arrival of Ham, son of Him, in the presence of the Messenger of Allah, the blessings of Allah be upon him, while he sat in his mosque with the Muslims around him. `Fa sallama 'alayhi bi sawtin yusma'u wa la yura lahu shakhsun` — so Ham greeted him with a voice that was heard but whose person was not seen; the voice reached the gathering, yet no form was visible. `Fa qala lahu Rasulullah salawatullahi 'alayhi man anta` — so the Messenger of Allah said to him, "Who are you?" `Fa qala ana Ham ibn Him ibn Laqis ibn Iblis` — and he answered, "I am Ham, son of Him, son of Laqis, son of Iblis."

He went on to recount his long history of submission: `wa kuntu qad aslamtu li-l-nabiyyi Nuhin thumma li Ibrahim thumma li Musa thumma li 'Isa 'alayhim al-salam` — and I had accepted Islam for the Prophet Nuh, then for Ibrahim, then for Musa, then for 'Isa, peace be upon them. `Wa ana ashhadu an la ilaha illa Allah wa annaka Muhammadun 'abduhu wa rasuluh` — and I bear witness that there is no god but Allah, and that you, Muhammad, are His servant and His Messenger. `Fa kana hadha min akbari al-mu'jizat` — so this was among the greatest of miracles. `Fa ma zadahum dhalika illa nufura` — yet it increased them in nothing but aversion; the disbelievers were only driven further away.

The text then traces Ham's ascents and descents through the eras of the prophets. `Wa kana al-Ham ba'da islamuhu li-l-nabiyyi Nuhin sa'ida ila rutbati al-shams bi wasatiti al-qamar kama sabaqa bihi al-qawl` — and Ham, after his Islam for the Prophet Nuh, ascended to the rank of the *Shams* through the intermediary of the *Qamar*, the moon, as the statement has already established. There he remained at that high rank, `ila an zahara Ibrahim 'alayhi al-salam` — until Ibrahim, peace be upon him, appeared. `Wa anhadara al-Ham min haythu kana fa aslama li Ibrahim 'alayhi al-salam` — and Ham descended from where he was and accepted Islam afresh for Ibrahim, peace be upon him.

A grammatical observation belongs to the name Ibrahim and is preserved here. One reads `li Ibrahima`, with a *zabar*, and not `li Ibrahimi` with a *zer*, even though the preposition `li` governs the genitive. The reason is that Ibrahim is a non-Arab name — a Hebrew name — and such names are indeclinable, *ghayr munsarif*. Ibrahim, Ishaq, and Yaqub are all of this kind: even in the genitive case they take a *zabar*, a *fatha*, rather than the *zer* one would otherwise expect. So it is `li Ibrahima 'alayhi al-salam`.

`Wa 'ada ila mawzi'ihi` — and Ham then returned to his place. `Wa kadhalika li Musa wa 'Isa 'alayhim al-salam` — and the same occurred for Musa and 'Isa, peace be upon them: at the appearance of each Natiq, Ham descended from his rank to accept Islam anew for that Natiq, then returned to his station. `Ila an zahara Muhammadun salawatullahi 'alayhi` — until Muhammad, the blessings of Allah be upon him, appeared. `Wa anhadara hatta aslama lahu` — and Ham descended until he accepted Islam for him; `tuwuffiya` — he was then made to die; `wa 'ada fi asra'i waqtin ila qamati al-saniyah` — and he returned, in the swiftest of time, to the lofty stature, as the statement has already established.

The reason for this final descent and submission is then made plain: `li anna la budda li kulli man aslama minhum fi al-adwari al-maziyah an yuslima li Muhammadin salawatullahi 'alayhi alladhi huwa al-natiqu al-sadis` — because it is necessary for everyone among the jinn who accepted Islam in the eras that have passed to accept Islam for Muhammad, the blessings of Allah be upon him, who is the sixth Natiq. No earlier submission suffices on its own; each must be renewed for the sixth Natiq. `Alladhi tamma bi tamamihi khalqu al-jism al-dini` — by whose completion the creation of the religious body, the *jism al-dini*, is completed. With the coming of Muhammad, the sixth Natiq, the fashioning of the religious body reaches its perfection.

`Wa yartaqi fi al-maratib ila an yablugha rutbata al-bab allati hiya haddu al-'ismah` — and such a believing jinn ascends through the ranks until he reaches the rank of the Bab, the Gate, which is the limit of infallibility. This swift ascent applies to the *mustaqdimin*, the forerunners, who because of their long ages of service can attain a rank higher than that of ordinary believers. The reasoning is this: such a one may even have begun as a disbeliever, but having then served and performed services through long ages, he earns as his reward an ascent to the rank of the Bab, like the rank of the noble Prophet, the blessings of Allah be upon him. And the rank of the Bab is the limit of *'ismah*, infallibility: for both the Imam possesses *'ismah* and the *Bab al-Abwab*, the Gate of Gates, possesses *'ismah*. So it is indeed possible that such a server should become higher than ordinary believers — because for ages upon ages he performed service. The passage seals this with a prayer: `Ballaghana Allahu wa iyyakum ila dhalika al-majma'i al-sharifi bi haqqi Muhammadin wa alihi al-tahirin salawatullahi 'alayhim ajma'in` — may Allah bring us and you to that noble assembly, by the right of Muhammad and his pure progeny, the blessings of Allah be upon them all.

### Saluni: Ask Me Before You Lose Me

To this is joined a remembrance of Amir al-Mumineen's own declaration of knowledge. When he was seated upon the pulpit in Kufa, a man came to him, and Amir al-Mumineen had proclaimed from that pulpit: `Saluni qabla an tafqiduni` — ask me before you lose me. This incident is recorded in *Saluni* and likewise in *Tawil al-Da'a'im*. (The questioner was not Ibn al-Kawwa, who was a different man, a hypocrite; this was another.) A person came forward and said, "O Amir al-Mumineen, I wish to ask you a question." He said, "Ask." The man said, `Ayna Jibril` — where is Jibril at this moment? Tell me where the angel Jibril is right now. Amir al-Mumineen, peace be upon him, looked upward toward the sky, and downward toward the earth, and turned to the left and to the right and behind him, searching in every direction; and not finding Jibril anywhere, he concluded: `Ana azunnu anta Jibril` — I think that you are Jibril. I looked to the sky, he said, and to the earth, and to my left and my right, but I could not find Jibril; so I know that you yourself are Jibril — and you are so-and-so, son of so-and-so, naming his lineage in full. At this the questioner let out a cry and rose up on high. The incident is narrated in great detail in *Saluni*, and it stands as a sign of the knowledge of Amir al-Mumineen, who knew the angel's presence and lineage though the angel had come in disguise.

### The Foul Form and the Truth It Sometimes Speaks

The exposition now turns to the foul form, the *surat al-khabitha*, and to the puzzle of how an evil thing can ever speak the truth. The text reads: `wa hadhihi surat al-khabith al-mufariqa li-l-nufus min al-ajdadi al-muttasila bi kulli jismin khabith, fa sarat nihayatan khabithatan li-l-shar muqabalatan li-l-nihayati al-sharif allati hiya nihayatu al-khayr kama sabaqa fi hadha al-qawl` — this is the evil form, separated from the souls, from the bodies, joined to every evil body; so it became the utmost evil for wickedness, standing in opposition to the noble end, which is the utmost of goodness, as has preceded in this statement. This foul form, released from the souls and bound to every evil body, has become the extreme limit of evil; and it stands opposite the noble extreme, which is the utmost good. As the end of goodness works for those who are good, this evil form works against them; it is the adversary of the most noble, who are the utmost of good.

Then comes the difficult truth about such forms: `wa sidqu qawliha fi ma tukhbiru bihi min al-ka'inat wa al-hawadithi al-jariyah, li ma kana min khidmatiha fi shari'ati al-natiq wa khidmatiha li ba'di ahli al-haqq bi sidqin minha wa nasihah` — and the truth of its speech in what it announces concerning beings and ongoing events is owing to what was once its service in the *shari'a* of the Natiq, and its service to some of the people of truth, with truthfulness on its part and sincere counsel. The point is that even the foul forms sometimes report things that are true; their predictions sometimes come to pass. The reason is not that they are good, but that in the era of the Natiq, in the Natiq's *shari'a*, they once rendered service, once spoke truth, once gave sincere advice and tended to some of the people of truth — and the truth they earned in that service is now paid back to them. (The teacher likens the receiving of true report to a screen fixed within, upon which an image can still appear.)

The principle beneath this is fundamental: `al-sharra la asla lahu fi al-ibda'` — evil has no root in the origination of things. Because evil has no original ground in creation, the good that a being once did is never annihilated by its later corruption. At one time a person spoke truth and served; afterward he was corrupted. Does his earlier truth then go to waste? It does not. Truth knows no decline. For the truth he spoke he will be rewarded; for the services he rendered he will be rewarded; for whatever good deed he did he will receive its recompense. So even a corrupted, foul form retains the credit of its former truth, and that credit is discharged to it in the form of the true reports it is occasionally able to give. It speaks the truth at times and not at others; in its place it sometimes says what is true. And this belongs to the *shari'a* of the Natiq — not of the Masih — for a being may become corrupt during the Natiq's own era, or corrupt anywhere; yet if alongside its corruption it performed some act of piety, that act earns its reward, and it is not punished for the truthful word but rewarded for it.

The justice in this is then named explicitly: `wa kana sidquha min hadha al-sabab 'adlan lillah li kawnihi la yudi'u saghiratan wa la kabiratan min khayrin wa la sharrin` — and its truthfulness, for this reason, is an instance of the justice of Allah, since He does not let go to waste anything small or great, of good or of evil. This is established by the verse: `fa man ya'mal mithqala dharratin khayran yarah, wa man ya'mal mithqala dharratin sharran yarah` — so whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil shall see it. Whatever a man does, good or evil, small or great, he will be shown its consequence; and so the small good of the foul form is repaid in true speech, even as its evil is requited.

These foul forms are then identified with a saying of the Prophet about the desert ghouls: `wa hum al-ghilan al-zahira allati dhakaraha Rasulullah salawatullahi 'alayhi bi qawlihi: la yaqulu al-ghilan illa al-a'rab wa al-sibyan, wa jami'u awza'ihim wa 'aza'im la ta'malu illa min khawfi sharrihim` — and they are the apparent ghouls which the Messenger of Allah, the blessings of Allah be upon him, mentioned in his saying: the ghouls do not affect any but the bedouins and the children; and all their charms and incantations work only out of fear of their evil. The *ghilan* are troops of jinn, ghouls of the desert in their outward appearance. They prey chiefly upon the bedouins and upon children — those who are illiterate, and the young, are the ones most affected by them. As for their truthful predictions, a man should not on that account take their word as proof: their evil shows itself as a kind of prediction, and that prediction sometimes turns out true; but it turns out true only because they are being recompensed for the services they once performed in the *shari'a* of the Natiq and for the true things they once spoke when they supported the truth. So when their word does come true, a man must not conclude that they are among the *awliya*, the saints — they are not; they are merely being paid the wage of their former truth. As for the amulets and incantations, these are written and given only out of fear of the ghouls' evil; the *awliya* of Allah give such amulets precisely because of the harm these forms can do, as protection against their evil.

The Prophet's commands regarding protection follow from this. `Wa lam yu'mar bi-l-qira'ati wa al-ta'awwudh 'inda al-nawm illa minhum` — and recitation and the seeking of refuge at the time of sleep were commanded only on account of them. What is the seeking of refuge that is recited at sleep? It is `Qul a'udhu bi-rabbi al-nas` and `Qul a'udhu bi-rabbi al-falaq`, together with the supplication `Allahumma la tu'minni makraka wa la tunsini dhikraka wa la taj'alni min al-ghafilin` — O Allah, do not make me feel secure from Your plan, and do not let me forget Your remembrance, and do not place me among the heedless. The meaning of `la tu'minni makraka` is: do not leave me secure from Your strategy. *Makr* here means strategy or design — and at night the strategies of the desert ghouls are also at work; so the servant prays that he not be left exposed, that Allah's own design shelter him, and that he not be made safe in a false security. `Wa la tunsini dhikraka` — and do not let me forget Your remembrance; may Your remembrance remain ever fresh in me. `Wa la taj'alni min al-ghafilin` — and do not place me among the heedless. It has further been taught that after this a man should form the intention, "I shall wake at such-and-such an hour," for then the cell of that intention becomes fixed within the brain and he wakes at the appointed time; and if it should happen that he does not wake, Allah the Exalted has already forgiven him, for he did intend it but was overtaken by drowsiness — as Sayyidna Ghulam Husain Sahab has explained in *Sahifa-e-Chahalsawa*.

The same seeking of refuge is prescribed in foul and darkened places: `wa kadhalika al-ta'awwudh fi al-mawadi'i wa al-mawazi' al-muzlima wa al-biqa' al-wasikha min khawfi sharrihim` — and likewise the seeking of refuge in dark locations and filthy spots, out of fear of their evil. The word `biqa'` is the plural of `buq'ah`, a spot or place. In urinals, in dark places, in filthy places, one should pronounce the *ta'awwudh* out of fear of the evil forms that gather there. On reaching such a place a man should say `A'udhu billahi min al-rijs al-najis` — I seek refuge in Allah from the impure filth. *Ta'awwudh* means precisely this: to seek refuge; and the amulets, in their way, seek the same refuge in written form. The passage closes with the prayer `'Adhana Allahu min sharrihim wa min sharri aslafihim bi haqqi Muhammadin wa alihi salawatullahi 'alayhim ajma'in` — may Allah protect us from their evil and from the evil of their predecessors, by the right of Muhammad and his family, the blessings of Allah be upon them all.

### Praiseworthy and Blameworthy: The Two Kinds of Jinn and the Two Kinds of Men

The exposition concludes by sorting both jinn and humans into the praiseworthy and the blameworthy. `Fa-lladhina yuslimuna min hadhihi al-suwar al-muqamat al-'aliya salamullahi 'alayhim hum al-jinn al-mahmudun, wa-lladhina baqu 'ala 'isyanihim hum al-jinn al-madhmumun` — so those who submit, from among these forms of the high stations, peace of Allah be upon them, are the praiseworthy jinn; and those who remain in their disobedience are the blameworthy jinn. The verb is *aslama, yuslimu*, to submit. Those of these high forms who bring Islam for the Imam, peace be upon him, are the *jinn-e-mahmud*, the praised; those who persist in their rebellion are the *jinn-e-madhmum*, the blamed.

The text then confronts a denial common within the community itself: `wa kathiran mimman yunsabu ila al-da'wat al-hadiya salamullahi 'ala sahibiha la yara bi-l-jinni bal yukadhdhibu bi dhalik` — and many of those who are counted among the Rightly-Guiding Mission, the peace of Allah be upon its master, do not believe in the jinn but rather deny them outright. Many who are attributed to the *Da'wat al-Hadiya* do not credit the existence of the jinn, nor the hidden workings of these beings, but deny the whole matter. The cause is given: `wa dhalika li qillati ma'rifatihim wa istighna'ihim bi nufusihim wa tarkihim li muwalat al-hududi al-mansubin min qibali awliya' Allah ta'ala` — and that is owing to the scantness of their knowledge, their self-sufficiency in their own souls, and their abandonment of allegiance to the hudud who are appointed by the friends of Allah the Exalted. It is the poverty of their *ma'rifah*, then, that breeds the denial. Many within the *Da'wat al-Hadiya*, possessing little knowledge, suppose that the matter of the jinn is mere talk with nothing behind it; they are content with their own understanding, certain that whatever they have thought must be right; and they have let go of their loyalty to the appointed *hudud*. From these three failings — little knowledge, self-conceit, and abandoned allegiance — flows the conclusion that the jinn do not exist at all.

A subtle point is then resolved concerning the purpose served by the evil spirits. A question arises: the *arwah-e-khabitha*, the evil spirits, are retained so that they may discharge the dues of their past deeds — the *qisasat*, the retributions, and the *baqiyat*, the remaining accounts, of their era — yet are they not, by their evil, only multiplying their own evil? The answer turns on the nature of this world as a world of choice. Increase, whether of good or of evil, belongs properly to the world of bodies, for that alone is the world of choice, where a man may do good or evil and so augment his goodness or his evil. The foul forms, however, are no longer in such a world of accruing deeds; rather, what is given to them is the right to exact their *qisasat*, the dues owing to them from their own time. The evil they work is permitted only against those for whom there are such *baqiyat*, outstanding accounts; they cannot work it against everyone, but only against those toward whom a debt remains. Why, then, do we recite `a'udhu billah` and seek refuge? Because if some fault should issue from us in our dealings with them — if something should pass between us through our own error — we ask that Allah the Exalted, by His grace and favor, keep us safe from it. This is the world: in it people speak harshly, things happen between one and another, and a man can be only so careful. So we seek refuge that we be guarded from the consequences of our own slips.

The deniers of the jinn are then shown to misread the very texts that speak of them. `Wa yaquluna inna al-jinn hum ahl 'ilm al-batin wa al-ins hum ahl 'ilm al-zahir` — and they say that the jinn are the people of inward knowledge and the humans the people of outward knowledge, `wa ma hunalika shay'un ghayra dhalik` — and that there is nothing there beyond that. Their error is to take this saying as *ta'wil*, interpretation, alone — as though to call the jinn the people of inward knowledge were merely a figure with no further reality. But it has not been said in an interpretive sense only; the interpretation is true, and a real physical existence is true as well. Both are correct together: it is true that the jinn are the people of inner knowledge and humans the people of outer knowledge, and it is also true that the *jinn-e-mahmud* and the *jinn-e-madhmum* genuinely exist, just as humans exist.

This double truth is then unfolded. `Wa la 'amri inna ahl al-batin hum al-jinn li ijnanihim wa sitrihim 'an al-batin, wa hum yanqasimuna 'ala qismayn: mahmud wa madhmum` — and by my life, the people of the inward are indeed the jinn, so called because of their concealment and their veiling from the inward; and they divide into two kinds, the praiseworthy and the blameworthy. The very name jinn derives from *ijnan* and *satr*, hiddenness and concealment, for they are veiled from sight. And of the two kinds, the text describes the praiseworthy: `wa al-mahmudu hum al-muntazimuna fi silki al-haqq wa hududihi al-mustamsikuna bi shara'i'i al-tawhid wa awza'ihi al-'arifuna bi ma'alimi al-manazil wa hum falasifat al-ilahiyyun` — and the praiseworthy are those ordered upon the thread of truth and its ranks, who hold fast to the laws of *tawhid* and its statutes, who know the signposts of the stations; and they are the divine philosophers. These are the good among the inward folk: arranged within the thread of truth and its hierarchy, clinging to the ordinances of the divine Oneness, acquainted with the marks of the high stations — the *falasifat al-ilahiyyun*, the philosophers of the divine.

The blameworthy form the second kind: `wa al-qismu al-thani hum al-falasifat al-mu'attilun li-l-wasa'it alladhina hum al-nuqaba' wa al-awsiyya' wa al-a'imma wa hududihim salawatullahi 'alayhim` — and the second kind are the negating philosophers, who deny the intermediaries, namely the *nuqaba'*, the *awsiyya'*, the *a'imma*, and their *hudud*, the blessings of Allah be upon them. These are the *mu'attilun*, the ones who strip away and deny the mediating ranks — the chiefs, the successors, the Imams, and their hierarchy. `Wa ha'ula'i adallu dalalatan min al-firaq` — and these are more astray in error than all the other sects: the negating philosophers stand at the furthest extreme of misguidance. `Wa hum al-abalisa wa al-shayatin` — and they are the devils and the satans. The passage prays: `'Adhana Allahu minhum bi haqqi Muhammadin wa alihi al-tahirin salawatullahi 'alayhim ajma'in` — may Allah protect us from them, by the right of Muhammad and his pure family, the blessings of Allah be upon them all.

The same division is then drawn among humankind. `Wa amma al-ins fa hum ahl al-zahir wa hum yanqasimuna qismayn: mahmud wa madhmum` — and as for the humans, they are the people of the outward, and they too divide into two kinds, the praiseworthy and the blameworthy. `Wa al-mahmudu hum al-qa'imuna bi awza'i al-shari'a allati lam yubdilha al-mubtilun wa lam yughayyirha al-dallun wa hum biha qa'imuna ma'a 'iffatin wa taharatin wa salamati al-nafs min bughdi ahl al-haqq wa ittiba'i al-nafs allati bi-l-su'i ammaratun, wa hum qalilun` — and the praiseworthy are those who uphold the statutes of the *shari'a* which the falsifiers have not altered and the misguided have not changed, holding to it with chastity, with purity, and with a soul kept sound from hatred of the people of truth and from following the soul that commands evil, the *nafs al-ammara bi-l-su'*; and they are few. These good ones of the outward folk maintain the unaltered ordinances of the law in chastity and purity, their souls free of malice toward the people of truth and free of obedience to the evil-commanding self — and, the text stresses, they are few.

The blameworthy among men are the most carefully drawn of all, for their evil wears the mask of piety. `Wa al-madhmumu hum al-qa'imuna bi awza'i al-shari'a al-munasibuna li ahl al-da'wat al-hadiya al-muzharuna li-l-nusuk al-mubtinuna li-l-ifk al-mubaddaluna li-l-shar'i al-muhibbuna li akthari al-waza'` — and the blameworthy are those who uphold the statutes of the *shari'a* but are hostile to the people of the Rightly-Guiding Mission, who make an outward show of devotion while concealing falsehood within, who alter the law and love to multiply burdens. Outwardly these men are attached to the *shari'a* and display the marks of asceticism and worship; inwardly they harbor enmity toward the people of the *Da'wat al-Hadiya*, and falsehood lodges in their hearts. They are *al-muzharuna li-l-nusuk*, the displayers of piety, and *al-mubtinuna li-l-ifk*, the concealers of falsehood; *al-munasibuna li ahl al-da'wat al-hadiya*, set in hostility against the people of the Mission; and *al-mubaddaluna li-l-shar'*, the alterers of the law.

What this alteration of the law looks like in practice is given a concrete example, which closes the section. Allah has commanded, in the verse of ablution, `famsahu bi-ru'usikum wa arjulikum` — so wipe your heads and your feet. The command is to wipe, *mash*, the head and the feet. Yet these men wash their feet instead. One sees a man at his ablution pour four or five mugs of water over his feet, washing them rather than wiping them. Now the law is not violated merely by the washing: for us it has been said that one who wishes to wash his feet may wash them. The error lies in the belief. If a man washes his feet supposing that washing is what Allah has made obligatory — that a fuller purity is thereby commanded, and that wiping is not the true ordinance — then he has changed the law in his understanding, and his prayer is not valid. He who washes in the conviction that washing, rather than wiping, is the obligation has invalidated his own prayer. The passage seals the whole discourse with a prayer of protection: `'Adhana Allahu wa jami'a ikhwanina minhum wa min muwalat al-awliya' 'anhum` — may Allah protect us and all our brethren from such men, and from the loss of allegiance to the friends of Allah on their account.

## The Angels, the Souls, and the Law of Retribution

This section opens the third chapter of *Sawabit al-'Adad*, the section devoted to proving the origin and the return — `Al-Bab al-thalith min Sawabit al-'Adad fi Ithbat al-mabda' wal-ma'ad`. The first discussion within it is headed `Al-Fasl al-awwal minha fi-ma yash-hadu min sihhatihi ma awradnahu min al-qawl fi ma'ad al-nufus al-mutawaliyah wal-nufus al-khabithah al-'asiyah` — "The first section thereof concerning that which testifies to the soundness of what we have cited concerning the return of the loving souls and the evil, disobedient souls." Everything that follows is offered as testimony to the soundness of what has come before regarding two destinies: the homecoming of the souls who love and uphold the truth, and the descent of the souls that are evil, disobedient, and sinful. The chapter turns first to the authority of one of the great teachers of the tradition, the Author of the Epistles, before unfolding, with great precision, the doctrine of the soul's ascent, its perversion, its punishment, and at last its renewal.

### The Virtuous Soul Who Becomes an Angel in Actuality

The discussion rests upon a statement of the virtuous personage, the Author of the Epistles — `sahib al-rasa'il`, may the blessings of Allah be upon him — taken from his epistle known as `al-Jami'ah`. The text reads: `thumma inna al-insan al-fadil al-'amil al-'alim la yazalu hatta yara a'malahu al-hasanat, wa-akhlaqahu al-jamilah, wa-ma yazharu minhu, wa-yasiru 'anhu, fi husni al-siyasati allati yatahayya'u laha biha li-iqamat al-shari'ah, wa-talaqqi al-wahy min al-mala'ikah, thumma idha faraqa al-'alama sara malakan bil-fi'l, wa-dakhala fi zumrat al-mala'ikah.` In translation: "Then indeed, the virtuous, active, and knowledgeable person persists until he sees his good deeds, his beautiful morals, what appears from him, and what proceeds from him in the excellence of conduct by which he is prepared for the establishment of the *shari'ah* and the reception of revelation from the angels. Then, when he departs from the world, he becomes an angel in actuality and enters into the company of the angels."

The teaching here is dense and deliberate. The person in view is at once virtuous, active, and knowing — `al-fadil al-'amil al-'alim` — and he does not cease in his striving. He persists until the fruits of his life become visible to him: his good deeds, his beautiful character, and all that issues from him in the excellence of conduct. Through this excellence of conduct, `husn al-siyasah`, he is made ready — `yatahayya'u` — for two great offices: the establishment of the sacred Law, `iqamat al-shari'ah`, and the reception of revelation from the angels, `talaqqi al-wahy min al-mala'ikah`. He prepares himself, by the discipline of a whole life, to be a vessel fit to receive what descends from above and to translate it into a governing order below.

The decisive phrase is what follows his departure from the world. Until that moment he was an angel only in potentiality — `malak bil-quwwah` — bearing within himself the latent capacity for the angelic rank but not yet possessing it in act. Upon leaving the world he becomes an angel in actuality, `malak bil-fi'l`, and enters into the company of the angels, `zumrat al-mala'ikah`. The transition from potency to act is the whole of the matter: the latent angel becomes the actual one, and is admitted into the assembly of those who are, in their very essence, angelic. This is the homecoming the chapter sets out to prove.

The commentary that the Author of the Epistles' words allude to a definite class of people: the People of Truth, `ahl al-haqq`, who uphold the truth in the two worships. The text states: `wa-qawluhu salawat Allahi 'alayhi isharatan ila ahl al-haqq al-qa'imina bil-haqq fi al-'ibadatayn al-zahirah wal-batinah, al-muttasilina bil-maqamat al-ilahiyyah, fa-inna mufaraqatahum li-hadha al-'alami yasiruna…` — "And his statement, blessings of Allah be upon him, is an allusion to the People of Truth who uphold the truth in the two worships, the outward and the inward, who are connected to the divine stations. For indeed, upon their departure from this world, they proceed…" The two worships, `al-'ibadatayn al-zahirah wal-batinah`, must be carefully distinguished. The outward worship is action, `'amal`; the inward worship is knowledge, `'ilm`. The People of Truth are those who establish both — who are upright in deed and grounded in gnosis — and who, by this twofold uprightness, remain connected to the divine stations, `al-maqamat al-ilahiyyah`. To be connected to the divine stations is to remain in living attachment to the *hudud kiram*, the noble ranks of the spiritual hierarchy through whom the divine descends. When such people leave this world, they reach the high and noble presence, `al-hadrat al-'aliyyah al-sharifah`, and it is precisely of them that it is said: `sara malakan bil-fi'l, wa-dakhala fi zumrat al-mala'ikah` — "he became an angel in actuality and entered into the company of the angels."

Here a careful reader must keep two distinct things in view. The phrase `sara malakan bil-fi'l` speaks of the soul, the `nafs`; and the entry into the company of angels concerns both the soul and the form, the `surah`. The luminous form and the soul together are received into the angelic assembly. And what, precisely, is this assembly that the virtuous soul enters? The text clarifies: `wa-zumrat al-mala'ikah ahlun li-dhalika al-majma' al-sabiq dhikruhu` — "And the company of the angels are worthy of that assembly whose mention has preceded." The assembly of angels, `zumrat al-mala'ikah`, into which the soul is gathered is none other than the noble temple, `al-haykal al-sharif`, that earlier discussions had already named. The angelic company and the noble temple are one and the same reality, approached from two sides. The virtuous soul, having become an angel in act, takes its place within that sacred edifice of light.

### The Opposite Who Becomes the Imam of Error

Having set forth the destiny of the virtuous, the Author of the Epistles turns to the opposites, `al-addad`, until he reaches this statement: `fa-li-jahalatihi bi-mubtadi'ihi wa-awliya'ihi sara 'aduwwan li-rabbihi, kharijan 'an ta'atihi, wa-idda'a li-nafsihi ma la yastahiqquhu wa-la yaliqu bihi, wa-huwa imam al-zalalati wa-ra'is al-jahalah` — "So, because of his ignorance of his Originator and His friends, he became an enemy to his Lord, departing from His obedience, and he claimed for himself what he does not deserve and what is not befitting him, and he is the Imam of error and the chief of ignorance."

The root of this whole catastrophe is ignorance, `jahalah` — but a specific ignorance, ignorance of one's Originator, `mubtadi'`, and of His friends, `awliya'`. From that ignorance everything else follows. The man who does not know his Originator nor recognize His friends becomes an enemy to his Lord and steps outside His obedience. Then comes the fatal act: he makes a claim, `idda'a`, for himself — laying hold by claim of what he neither deserves nor befits. The very form of the word is instructive. `Idda'a` is built upon `infa'ala` from the root `da'a`, "he called"; `idda'a` therefore means that he called toward himself, summoning to his own person honors and ranks that are not his. By this false summons he becomes the Imam of error, `imam al-zalalah`, and the chief of ignorance, `ra'is al-jahalah` — not merely a man astray, but a leader of others into error, a head and source of the very ignorance from which he sprang.

The fate of such a man at death is the precise inversion of the virtuous soul's homecoming. The text continues: `fa-idha faraqa al-'alama bi-jismihi, sara shaytana nafsihi, wa-nafsuhu la yufariqu jismihu, wa-habata ila asfal al-safilin, yuwaswisu bil-ghawayah fi sudur al-nas, wa-yuhi ila amthalihi min al-mutaraddina al-ajsam, zukhrufa al-qawl ghurura, kama yuhi al-mala'ikah ila anbiya' al-ta'id, fa-yahbitu ilayhim bi-awamirihi wa-nawahihi, wa-yatanazzalu bi-amri rabbiha 'ala man yasha'u min 'ibadihi, kama akhbara subhanahu: nazala bihi al-ruh al-amin.` Rendered into English: "So, when he departs the world with his body, he becomes the shaitan of his own soul, and his soul does not leave his body, and he descends to the lowest of the low, whispering error into the breasts of men, and inspiring his likes from among the degraded bodies with specious words as a delusion, just as the angels inspire the prophets of support. So he descends upon them with his commands and his prohibitions, and he descends with the command of his Lord upon whomever He wills of His servants, as He, the Glorified, informed: The Trustworthy Spirit descended with it."

Three movements of this descent must be held distinct. First, this man becomes the shaitan of his own soul — `shaytana nafsihi`. Second, and crucially, his soul does not leave his body. Where the virtuous soul's luminous form separated and ascended, this man's soul does not depart at all; instead it sinks. He falls to the lowest of the low, `asfal al-safilin`. The reason is exact: the soul, which ought to have acquired a luminous form and risen with it, has not done so. Materiality has only thickened in it. The soul has, in effect, become wedded to the body, and so it descends rather than ascends. Third, from this lowest station he does a dark work that mirrors, in inversion, the work of the angels. As the angels whisper into the breasts of men the inspiration of guidance, he whispers error, `ghawayah`, into their breasts. As the angels grant revelation to the prophets of support, the `anbiya' al-ta'id`, so he grants a counterfeit revelation, `wahy`, to his own likes among the degraded bodies, the `mutaraddina al-ajsam`. And the matter of this revelation is `zukhrufa al-qawl ghurura` — embellished, ornamented speech offered as a delusion, words dressed up and adorned to deceive. He descends upon his followers with commands and prohibitions of his own, a perverse legislation set against the true Law. The Qur'anic phrase invoked to describe the descent of true revelation — `nazala bihi al-ruh al-amin`, "The Trustworthy Spirit descended with it" — stands as the very pattern that this man's satanic descent counterfeits.

### Ruh and Nafs: The Command and the Soul

This passage occasions a question of capital importance, for the words *Ruh* and *Nafs* — spirit and soul — are everywhere used, and the careless reader treats them as one. The teaching is that, in common usage and in many scriptural places, the two are indeed synonymous; they are often interchanged. But a precise distinction must be drawn.

The *Nafs*, the soul, is of several kinds. There is the vegetative soul, `nafs al-namiyah`; the sensory soul, `nafs al-hissiyah`; and the rational soul, `nafs al-natiqah`. These are three real and distinct faculties, descending from three distinct sources and governing three distinct functions, as will be detailed below. The *Ruh*, by contrast, is not one of these souls at all. The *Ruh* is the command of the Lord. The Qur'an declares: `Qul al-ruhu min amri Rabbi` — "Say: 'The Spirit is of the command of my Lord.'" Say, O Prophet, that the *Ruh* is from the `amr`, the command, of my Lord. The *Ruh* is thus the divine support — `ta'idat` — that descends from Allah upon the rational soul. It is not the rational soul itself but that which the rational soul first receives. The vegetative and sensory souls do not receive it; only the rational soul, `nafs al-natiqah`, attains it. The very first thing the rational soul attains of the divine supports descending from Allah is what is called *Ruh*. For this reason the *Ruh* is identified with the first perfection, `kamal al-awwal`: it is the inaugural gift of support, the opening descent of the divine command into the prepared soul. And since the divine command, the `amr Rabbi`, fully arrives when a person's end is sealed with good — `khatima bil-khayr` — and the luminous form, `surat nuraniyyah`, is completed at that very moment, in one valid sense the completed luminous form may itself be called *Ruh*.

From this distinction a precise ruling on death follows. Is death the departure of the soul from the body, or the departure of the spirit from the body? It is the departure of the *Nafs*, the soul, from the body — not the departure of the *Ruh*. The *Ruh* is the command; the command does not "leave" in the way a soul leaves a body. The scripture itself preserves this distinction by its choice of words. Where the dying believer is addressed, the word used is *nafs*: `Ya ayyatuha al-nafsu al-mutma'innah` — "O soul at peace." But where the divine command is named, the word is *ruh*: `qul al-ruhu min amri Rabbi`. So too with sleep: when Allah speaks of souls being taken in slumber, this is a state of the *nafs*, the soul — a temporary withdrawal of the soul, not of the command. Common speech collapses *Ruh* and *Nafs* together; Allah has kept them apart, and the doctrine of the return requires that we keep them apart as well, for it is the soul that is judged, that ascends or descends, while the *Ruh* names the descending command and the first perfection it confers.

### What His Ignorance Was: Tawhid, Tanzih, and Tajrid

The Author of the Epistles' phrase, "because of his ignorance of his Originator and His friends," is now opened more fully. The text explains: `wa-qawluhu 'alayhis salam: fa-li-jahalatihi bi-mubtadi'ihi wa-awliya'ihi yuridu bihi al-didd alladhi huwa ghayru 'arifin billahi ta'ala wa-la bi-hududihi, salamu Allahi 'alayhim, al-mudihina li-'ulum al-tawhid wal-tanzih wal-tajrid, wal-mu'arrifina lahu fi kayfiyyat al-ibtida' wa-haqiqat al-ma'ad wa-intiha'ih` — "And his statement, peace be upon him: 'So, because of his ignorance of his Originator and His friends,' he intends by it the opposite, who is one who is not a gnostic of Allah, the Exalted, nor of His *hudud*, peace of Allah be upon them, who clarify the sciences of *tawhid*, *tanzih*, and *tajrid*, and who make Him known in the manner of the beginning, the reality of the return, and its end."

The man's ignorance, then, is no small defect of learning. He is no gnostic of Allah, `ghayru 'arifin billah`, and no gnostic of His *hudud* — those noble ranks who are the very ones that clarify the sciences of `tawhid`, `tanzih`, and `tajrid`, and who make Allah known in three respects: in the beginning, `ibtida'`; in the reality of the return, `haqiqat al-ma'ad`; and in its end, `intiha'`. To be ignorant of the *hudud* is therefore to be cut off from the only channel through which these sciences and this knowledge of Allah are conveyed.

These three sciences must be understood with precision, for they are the substance of true gnosis. *Tawhid* does not mean, as the unschooled suppose, simply to "count" Allah as one — to call Him one rather than two or three. We cannot comprehend Allah under any number at all, neither as one nor two nor three. The true meaning of *tawhid* concerns the *hudud*: Allah has created every `hadd` unique in its essence, so that no `hadd` can be the like of any other. Were any two ranks identical, the hierarchy itself would collapse, for it is precisely the difference between each `hadd` and the next that constitutes the ordered descent. He has therefore created every rank as a singular, unrepeatable essence — and this making-unique, this irreducible distinctness of each created rank, is the meaning of *tawhid*. *Tanzih* is the declaration of Allah's transcendence: to hold Him utterly beyond all attributes, to declare that we cannot comprehend Him under any quality whatsoever. And *tajrid* is the stripping away of every quality so as to affirm that the bringing-into-existence of essences, `ijad al-zawat`, belongs to none but the First Originator, `Mubdi' al-Awwal`. The creation of beings is in no one's power save His; *tajrid* isolates this prerogative and assigns it to the Originator alone. These three together — the uniqueness of each rank, the transcendence of the Lord, and the exclusive creative prerogative of the Originator — are the sciences the *hudud* clarify and the ignorant opposite does not know.

The phrase "he became an enemy to his Lord" must likewise be read with the proper referent. By "his Lord," `rabbihi`, in the words `fa-sara 'aduwwan li-rabbihi`, is meant the Imam of the Time, `imam al-zaman`. The enmity is not an abstract enmity toward the Deity but a concrete enmity toward the living Imam. And the false claim — `wa-idda'a li-nafsihi ma la yastahiqqu` — is then specified exactly: `yuridu al-shirk fi maqdari dhalika al-imami wa-da'wa rutbatihi, fa-huwa imam al-zalalati wa-ra'is al-jahalati, kama qala Allahu ta'ala: wa-ja'alnahum a'immatan yad'una ila al-nar, wa-yawm al-qiyamati la yunsarun` — "he intends partnership in the station of that Imam and the claim to his rank. So he is the Imam of error and the chief of ignorance, as Allah, the Exalted, said: 'And We made them leaders inviting to the Fire, and on the Day of Resurrection they will not be helped.'" The false claim, in other words, is *shirk* — associationism — but `shirk` of a particular and grave kind: partnership in the station of the Imam and a claim to the Imam's own rank. The man who lays claim to the Imam's station has set himself up as a partner where there can be no partner. He thereby fulfills the Qur'anic word about those whom Allah made leaders inviting to the Fire, who shall find no help on the Day of Resurrection. This statement, moreover, applies universally — `wa-yantaridu hadha al-qawlu fi jami'i addad al-maqamat wa-addad hududihi jami'an, 'alihim wa-danihim`, "and this statement applies to all the opposites of the stations and the opposites of all of His *hudud*, the high among them and the low among them." Every opposite of every rank, from the highest to the lowest, falls under this same condemnation. (The verb `yantaridu`, from the form `infa'ala`, is intransitive: it means the statement "extends" or "applies onward" of itself, requiring no object — as `intaqala` means "he transferred," `infataha` "it opened," and `inqalaba` "it changed," each carrying an agent but no object. So the saying extends across all the opposites without remainder.)

### The Satanic Soul, the Sensory Soul, and the Three Centers

What, then, is the "satanic soul" that the opposite is said to become? The text is exact: `wa-qawluhu 'alayhis salam: wa-idha faraqa al-'alama bi-jismihi, sara shaytana nafsihi, yuridu bi-nafsihi dhalika al-tasawwur al-khabith alladhi kana yatasawwaruhu min 'ada'i imami zamanihi wa-hududi dinihi, wa-khulumihi al-zulmaniyyah, wa-af'alihi al-qabihah, li-anna al-mufariqa minhu huwa hadha al-tasawwur la ghayr, wa-huwa yusamma nafsan shaytaniyyatan, kama tusamma tasawwur ahl al-haqqi nafsan malakiyyatan, 'ala ma sabaqa bihi al-sharh wal-qawl` — "And his statement, peace be upon him: 'And when he departs the world with his body, he becomes the shaitan of his own soul,' he intends by 'his soul' that evil conception that he used to form from the enmity of the Imam of his time and the *hudud* of his religion, and his dark qualities and his ugly deeds. Because the departing entity from him is this conception and nothing else, and it is called a satanic soul, just as the conception of the People of Truth is called an angelic soul, according to what has preceded in the explanation and the statement."

The "satanic soul," then, is not the man's soul in its entirety but precisely the evil conception, `al-tasawwur al-khabith`, which he had formed within himself out of his enmity to the Imam of his time and the *hudud* of his religion, together with his dark qualities and his ugly deeds. This conception, and this alone, is what departs from him — `al-mufariqa minhu huwa hadha al-tasawwur la ghayr`. It is the departing conception that is named a satanic soul, exactly as, on the side of the virtuous, the conception of the People of Truth is named an angelic soul. The whole doctrine turns on this symmetry: a man's enduring conception, his settled inward image of reality, is what crystallizes at death into either an angelic or a satanic form, while the soul that formed it remains behind in the body.

For the sensory soul does not depart. The text continues: `wa-amma nafsuhu al-hissiyyah, fa-ghayru mufariqatin li-jismihi, wa-li-dhalika qala 'alayhis salam: wa-nafsuhu la yufariqu jismihu, isharatan ila al-hissiyyati allati la tufariqu jismihu, bal tastahilu bi-istihalatihi ila al-hadd al-sharif` — "As for his sensory soul, it does not depart from his body, and for that reason he, peace be upon him, said: 'And his soul does not leave his body,' as an allusion to the sensory soul which does not leave his body, but rather transforms with his transformation towards the noble rank." The sensory soul, `al-nafs al-hissiyyah`, does not separate from the body; rather it undergoes transformation, `istihala`, together with the body's own transformation, moving toward the noble rank, `al-hadd al-sharif`. It does not depart but is transmuted in place, ascending with the body's ascent toward the noble station and the acquisition of essence.

This invites a careful exposition of the three souls and their origins, a point to be held firmly in mind. All the bodily constituents of a person are dispersed throughout the world. From among them, the first that must develop is the vegetative soul, `nafs al-namiyah`. It descends from the moon — from the sphere of the moon, `falak al-qamar`, which is its center. This vegetative soul combines with the seminal drop of the mother and father; when that drop settles in the womb, the connection of the vegetative soul with it is established. Before that it was merely an extract, `sulalah`; after settling as a drop, it becomes the vegetative soul. For nine months this vegetative soul remains in the womb and develops. When the child comes into the world, then through the rays of the sun it acquires the sensory soul, `nafs al-hissiyah`. The vegetative and the sensory soul become one and develop together, as has been explained. Finally, when the person accepts the noble covenant, the `ahd-e sharif`, at that moment he acquires the rational soul, `nafs al-natiqah`. The rational soul descends from `Mujrima`, which is its center.

Thus there are three centers, and they must not be confused: the center of the vegetative soul is the moon; the center of the sensory soul is the sun, `shams`; and the center of the rational soul is `Mujrima`. The vegetative soul descends from the sphere of the moon, the sensory soul from the sun, and the rational soul from Mujrima — three souls, three sources, three governing spheres.

What happens to these souls at death follows from their natures. When a virtuous person passes from the world, it is his sensory soul together with his vegetative soul that have, throughout his life, been preparing his luminous form. At death the luminous form, `surat nuraniyyah`, separates; and the sensory and vegetative souls, to the extent that each had followed the rational soul, contribute their share, and the luminous form ascends. What does not follow the rational soul is left behind in the grave. This is the process of nature, proceeding for each person according to how he has lived — one man with more of the vegetative, one with less, each according to his own measure. The luminous form ascends to the person's higher rank, the `hadd-e 'ali`, and there it remains adjacent to that rank — `mujawar` — merging with the form of that `hadd`, `mumazaj`. Why does it merge, rather than simply rest beside it? For a reason of preservation. If the soul of that higher `hadd` should ever go astray, the good form can then be detached and saved. Should the `hadd-e 'ali` go astray, his own evil soul becomes corrupt; but the good form that had merged with him, being good, is not allowed to perish — for Allah does not waste good — and so it is removed from there and separated. The soul becomes separate and the luminous form becomes separate, each going to its proper destiny. This whole mechanism has been explained before, and it is precisely inverted in the case of the opposite: for him the satanic form becomes separate, while his soul falls and joins itself to the body. His soul retains both the vegetative and the sensory components, but it sinks rather than rises.

### The Wandering of the Evil Form Through the Barzakh

The chapter now traces the journey of the evil form once it has departed. The text reads: `wa-qawluhu: wa-habata ila asfal al-safilin, yuwaswisu bil-ghawayah fi sudur al-nas, yuridu bi-dhalika anna mufaraqata hadhihi al-surat al-khabithah li-jismiha wa-nafsiha wa-masiraha tatilu bayna al-falaki wal-ard, fa-hiya ta'taridu kulli min yasma'uha wa-yu'ayinuha bi-halat mufaraqatiha li-jismiha wa-nafsiha` — "And his statement: 'And he descends to the lowest of the low, whispering error into the breasts of men,' he intends by that that the departure of this evil form from its body and its soul, and its journey, wanders between the celestial sphere and the earth. So it presents itself to everyone who hears it and sees it in the state of its departure from its body and its soul."

The evil form, having separated from its body and soul, does not simply vanish. Its journey, `masir`, prolongs itself in the region between the celestial sphere and the earth — that intermediate realm, the barzakh, through which it roams. And in that wandering it presents itself to people. To whoever hears it and sees it, it appears — sometimes startling the beholder in the form of a human, `bi-hay'ati insan`, and at other times in a satanic form, `fi hay'at shaytan`, beautifying the ugliness of its deeds and making its most abominable acts and speech seem fair. So the text: it appears `wa-taratan yamza'u man yara-ha bi-hay'ati insanin wa-taratan saniyyatan fi hay'at shaytanin yuzayyin qabiha af'alihi wa-aqbahihi wa-qawlihi` — "and at times startling whoever sees it in the form of a human, and at other times in the form of a shaitan, beautifying the ugliness of its deeds and its most ugly deeds and its speech."

It appears, too, in the form of a female or male jinn, and it visits the sleep of its own kind. The text continues: `wa-qawlan liman yara-ha bi-hay'ati jinniyyatin aw jinniyyin, wa-taratan fi tara'iha fil-nawm li-amthaliha min ahl al-nufus al-khabithah, fa-yuhi ilayhi ma waswasat-ha lahu fima yujrihi min al-haqqi wa-ahlihi, wa-taratan min ittihadiha bi-ahadi al-ajsadi al-khabithah al-muzlimah, wa-qawniha yantiqa 'ala lisanihi bil-kufriyyat wal-fawahishi wal-hawadithi al-ka'inat` — "And as a statement to whoever sees it in the form of a female jinn or a male jinn, and at times in its appearance in sleep to its likes from among the people of evil souls, so it inspires it with what it whispered to it concerning what it carries of enmity toward the truth and its people, and at times from its union with one of the evil, dark bodies, and its being such that it speaks on its tongue with blasphemies, abominations, and future events." It thus appears in sleep to those of evil soul like itself, inspiring into them the very whispers that carry men away from the truth and its people. And at times it unites with one of the evil, dark bodies — `ittihad`, an actual union — so that it speaks through that body's tongue, uttering blasphemies, `kufriyyat`; abominations, `fawahish`; and predictions of future events, `hawadith al-ka'inat`.

### Why the Hearers Believe: True Service Repaid in True Prediction

This raises a pressing question, for the evil form's predictions often come true, and men are misled by them. The text states the hearer's reaction: `wa-yaqulu man yasma'u nutqahu fil-umuri al-ka'inati wal-fawahishi wal-kufriyyat: huwa sadiqun fi jami'i ma yantiqa bihi wa-yukhbiru 'anhu` — "And he who hears its speech concerning future events, abominations, and blasphemies says: 'He is truthful in all that he speaks and informs of.'" The one who hears it speak of coming events, mixed though they are with obscenity and disbelief, concludes that the speaker is wholly truthful in all he says and announces.

But why should these dark forms speak truth at all? Here a precise teaching is given. These ugly forms, in their own time — in the era of the *natiq*, when they had once rendered service — had spoken true things and dealt rightly. For that earlier truthfulness and service they are now compensated: they are granted the faculty of speaking accurately about the matters of existence, the `umur al-ka'inat`, so that their predictions come true. The truths they utter are, in reality, the arrangements and dispositions of the Imam of the Time, `tadabir al-imam al-zaman`. How do such vile beings, `khaba'is`, come to have this unveiling, `kashf`, of good things? Precisely because they lack physical constraint. Their spirits have a freedom that embodied beings do not have, and they exploit it. And the arrangements of the Imam are pure arrangements that nevertheless contain within them the dues of retribution, `qisasat`. Because these forms had once, in service to their Imam, spoken truth, a recompense is owed to them; the dues must be settled; and so a measure of good dealing, `husn al-mu'amalat`, is shown them in the form of accurate prediction. Their true sayings are a wage paid out against an old service, even as their corruption proceeds.

This is why the predictions seen in dreams are likewise believed. The text continues: `wa-kadhalika ma yura 'anhu fi al-nawm` — "and likewise what is seen from him in sleep." These vile spirits, `arwah-e khabisah`, descend in the state of sleep and utter things, some of which are true, so that the dreamer takes the vision for a miracle, `karamat`. But this is a deadly error. The text warns: `fa-yakunu dhalika sababan li-halakihi wa-halaki mimman ta'allama min amthalihi min ahl al-i'tiqadat al-fasidah` — "so that becomes a cause for his destruction and the destruction of others who learned from the likes of him, the people of corrupt beliefs." Such a thing becomes a cause of destruction, `halak`, for the spirit itself, which is in any case bound for ruin, and for the destruction of all who learn from such spirits and adopt their corrupt beliefs, `i'tiqadat fasidah`. The vile spirit will be destroyed regardless; but those who hear it, believe it, and act upon it have their own beliefs corrupted thereby. For the danger lies exactly in the mixture: `nutquhu fi umur al-ka'inat` is interwoven with `fawahish` and `kufriyyat`, truths braided together with abominations and disbelief. Because some of what the spirit says proves true, men conclude that the rest must be true as well — and how many things, said with apparent authority, later proved utterly false. To follow such a being is destruction.

Against this snare the straight path is set: `'ati'ullaha wa-'ati'u al-rasula wa-uli al-amr` — "Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger, and those in authority." Obey Allah; obey the Messenger; and obey those to whom the command has been entrusted, the `Uli'l-amr`. This is the straight path, `sirat al-mustaqim` — not the mixed truth-and-falsehood of the wandering spirit, but obedience to the established sources of authority.

### Refuge from the Whisperer: Human and Jinn

The text now draws the consequence for the spirit's enmity. Its predictions and its destruction of men together intensify its hatred: `fa-yakunu bi-dhalika ishtidadu 'adawatihi li-ahl al-haqq wa-a'zamu li-bughdihi wa-akdadu li-bughdihi` — "so by that is the intensification of his enmity for the People of Truth, and a greater and more confirmed hatred." Its enmity for the People of Truth grows fiercer and its rancor more deeply confirmed. `Hadha huwa al-iha'u wal-waswasatu wal-ghawayah` — "this is the inspiration, the whispering, and the misguidance." This is precisely the threefold work of the evil form: to inspire falsehood, `iha'`; to cast doubt, `waswasah`; and to lead astray, `ghawayah`.

It is against exactly this enemy that refuge is commanded. The Mu'awwidhatayn are recited: `qul a'udhu bi-rabbi al-falaq` — "Say: 'I seek refuge in the Lord of the daybreak'" — and `qul a'udhu bi-rabbi al-nas, min sharri al-waswasi al-khannas, alladhi yuwaswisu fi suduri al-nas` — "Say: 'I seek refuge in the Lord of mankind, from the evil of the whisperer who withdraws, who whispers into the breasts of mankind.'" And the doctrine is precise about the identity of this enemy: the whispering adversary operates behind the scenes, and it is of two kinds. Both human beings and jinn act as the whisperer; the whispering comes from the men of evil and from the jinn alike. From both of them, therefore, refuge is sought in the two protective Surahs, the `Qul` — for the danger is not from a single source but from a hidden enemy among men and among the jinn together.

### Gathering at the Two Knots: Devils in Actuality

The wandering form does not roam forever; it has a gathering place. The text reads: `thumma innahu yazdadu ithman wa-awzaran wa-shaytanatan wa-kufran wa-bu'dan, min jumlat iqamatihi muttahidan bi-hadha al-bayt al-khabith al-muzlim` — "then he indeed increases in sin and burdens, and satanism and disbelief and distance, during the whole of his stay, united with this vile, dark house." Throughout the time it remains united with the vile, dark body it has possessed, it goes on increasing in sin, in burdens, in satanism, `shaytanah`, in disbelief, and in distance from the truth. It is a partner to those who set themselves against the Messengers and the command of the *Uli'l-amr*, joining in their incitement and in the spreading of the call of falsehood, the call of `ghayr-e haqq`.

Then, upon leaving that possessed body, it moves to a definite station. The text continues: `fa-'inda mufaraqatihi lahu wa-masirihi fi ufuq al-barzakh al-falakiyy al-muzlim alladhi huwa al-'uqdatan, wa-huma magnatisuhu al-khabith al-muzlim al-jadhibah lahu, yazdadu shaytanatan wa-bu'dan` — "so upon his separation from him and his journey to the horizon of the dark celestial barzakh, which is the two knots, and they are its vile, dark magnet that attracts it, it increases in satanism and distance." When the evil form separates from the jinn or body it had inhabited, its journey takes it to the horizon of the dark celestial barzakh — the two knots, `al-'uqdatan`. These two knots act as a vile, dark magnet, `magnatis`, drawing the form to themselves; and arriving there, the form increases yet further in satanism and in distance.

Here a question of geography arises: are there places these vile spirits cannot reach, or do they pervade everything? The answer draws a sharp line. Inside the noble Ka'bah itself, angels descend, and the vile spirits have no station there. But around the Ka'bah there are many places that they do claim as their own. Their station is at the markers, the `amyal` — the mile-posts; and at the place of swift running between Safa and Marwah; and at the place of the pelting, where the pebbles are cast near the idols. These are their centers. So while whisperings, `waswasah`, may intrude even at the sacred sites, the spirits' own station is not set within the Ka'bah but at these peripheral markers — Safa, Marwah, and the place of pelting — for they withdraw from where the angels descend. By the same principle, they have no place within the sacred presences, the `hadarat`, where the divine descends; their existence is confined to the marked and vile places, not to the noble presences themselves.

At the two knots the forms gather with their own kind. The text: `wa-yajtami'u bi-dhalika bi-amthalihi min sufara' al-khabith al-sabiqah 'alayhi wal-warid 'alayhi, fa-yazdaduna bi-ijtima'ihim shaytanatan wa-kufran wa-bu'dan wa-nukran, thumma yanhadiruna ila al-ard wa-yasiruna ila al-qamat al-alfiyyah, wa-hina'idhin yakununa shayatin bil-fi'l` — "and he gathers there with his likes from the vile envoys, those who preceded him and those who come upon him, so they increase by their gathering in satanism, disbelief, distance, and denial, then they descend to the earth and proceed to the thousand-year stature, and at that point they become devils in actuality." Gathered at the two knots with the vile envoys, `sufara' al-khabith` — those who came before and those who come after — they increase, by the very fact of their assembly, in satanism, in disbelief, in distance, and in denial, `nukran`. Then they descend again to the earth and reach the thousand-year stature, `al-qamat al-alfiyyah`. And at that point they become devils in actuality, `shayatin bil-fi'l`. Until then they had been devils only in potentiality, `shayatin bil-quwwah`, working through another's body and tongue; now they themselves become full devils in act and manifest openly in the world. The earlier-described speaking through a possessed man was the work of the devil in potency; once it has gathered at the two knots, magnified its satanism, and descended again at full stature, it is a devil in actuality.

What such a devil then does upon the earth is governed by a strict law. The text continues: `wa-tazdadu waswasatuhu wa-ghawayatuhu wa-sadduhum 'an aksar al-'awalim 'an al-hidayah` — "and his whispering and his misguidance increase, and their blocking of most of the worlds from guidance." Its whispering and misguidance grow, and it blocks most of the worlds from receiving guidance. But it cannot act against everyone. The whole process, gathered into one law, runs thus: when a living person, in his worldly life, opposes the truth, his evil form, the `surah khabisah` he had created, separates from him at death; his soul spreads through his body and sinks; and the form goes roaming through the world. Its task is to find every gathering set against the People of Truth and to aid and incite it against the truth. When its time is up, it goes to the two knots, where its satanism is intensified; then, at the appointed moment, it descends again into the world. And it descends at a particular juncture — when an Imam appears — in order to take its retributions, its `qisasat`. The text marks this: `ka-man taqaddama min azdad awliya' Allah 'alayhim al-salam` — "like those who preceded from the opponents of the Friends of Allah, peace be upon them." It cannot act against all and sundry, but only against those with whom dues, `baqiyat`, remain pending. It attaches itself to whoever opposes the People of Truth, inciting and aiding them, until the time comes to exact the outstanding retributions — and no further. The forms appear as sorcerer, as murderer, in whatever shape suits the settling of accounts; they discharge their `qisasat`; and only at the end of the cycle, when the Qa'im appears, are they at last confronted and finished.

The rank these devils occupy is named precisely: `wa-hadhihi al-rutbah al-ka'inuna fiha ha'ula'i al-shayatin hiya asfal al-safilin, li-annaha la rutbata tahtaha wa-la akhbatha minha` — "and this rank in which these devils are is the lowest of the low, because there is no rank beneath it, nor anything viler than it." The station of these devils is `asfal al-safilin`, the lowest of the low — and it is so named for a strict reason: there is no rank beneath it, and nothing more vile than it. It is the absolute floor of being. Against the evil that emanates from this rank, refuge is sought in the same breath: `hamana Allahu 'anha bi-muwalat awliya'ihi wa-ajarana Allahu min nafasatihim wa-hamazatihim. Innahu 'ala kulli shay'in qadir` — "May Allah protect us from it through the love of His Friends, and may Allah give us refuge from their evil breaths and their evil suggestions. Indeed, He is powerful over all things." We ask protection from the lowest of the low through the love of the Friends of Allah, and refuge from the evil breaths of these devils, their `nafasat` — the vile exhalations by which they blow evil — and from their `hamazat`, their goading suggestions, the proddings by which they incite to sin. Indeed He is powerful over all things.

### The Disobedient Souls and the Lesser Punishment

The chapter now turns from the wandering forms to the disobedient souls themselves and the punishment prepared for them. The text of the Author of the Epistles reads: `wa-idha balaghati al-anfus al-'asiyah akhira 'adhra' al-silsilah al-mu'addah li-'adhabiha, ghasat biha mala'ikat al-'adhab, wa-zabanaha malik al-ghadban` — "and when the disobedient souls reach the last of the links of the chain which is prepared for their punishment, the angels of punishment plunge them into it, and the wrathful angel drives them." When the disobedient souls, `al-anfus al-'asiyah`, reach the final link of the chain, `akhira 'adhra' al-silsilah`, that has been prepared for their punishment, the angels of punishment, `mala'ikat al-'adhab`, plunge them in, and the wrathful angel, `malik al-ghadban`, drives them onward. The text then describes the next stage: `fa-'ttahadat biha lata'if al-'adhab, wa-'nhadarat biha ila surat al-zulmah bi-mujarradiha` — "so the subtleties of punishment unite with it, and it descends with them to the form of darkness in its pure state." The subtleties of punishment unite with the soul, and it is brought down to the form of pure darkness, `surat al-zulmah bi-mujarradiha` — pure, meaning without the body, a darkness stripped of body and complete in itself.

There follows the dreadful perception within that darkness: `fa-tatara'a laha fi dhatiha kullama lahazat nafsuha ashkhass silsilat allati salakat fiha wa-ju'ilat biha, wa-amwaj jahannam, wa-marratan tarfa'uha ila wahj al-athir, wa-marratan tahbutu ila bard al-zamharir, wal-shayatin tahtalitu biha, wal-mala'ikah tarjumuha bil-shuhub min thughur al-'adhab, wa-tarmiha duhuran min kulli janib, wa-la yazalu kadhalika da'buhum ma damati al-samawatu wal-ard tarjufu` — "so there appears to it, in its essence, whenever its soul perceives them, the figures of the chain in which it traversed and was placed, and the waves of Hell; and one time it raises it to the heat of the ether, and another time it brings it down to the cold of *zamharir*; and the devils mingle with it, and the angels stone it with meteors from the frontiers of punishment, and they cast it away, repulsed, from every side; and their condition will not cease to be thus so long as the heavens and the earth tremble." In its own essence the soul beholds, whenever it turns to perceive, the very figures of the chain it had traversed and the waves of Hell, `amwaj jahannam`. It is raised to the searing heat of the ether — `wahj al-athir`, the heat of the sphere of fire — and dropped to the freezing cold of `zamharir`. Devils mingle with it; angels stone it with meteors, `shuhub`, from the frontiers of punishment, `thughur al-'adhab`; and it is driven off, repulsed, from every side. It seeks to reach the luminous stars but is driven back by piercing flames — recalling the verse `idha al-sama'u inshaqqat, wa-adhinat li-rabbiha wa-huqqat`, "When the sky is split asunder, and has harkened to her Lord, and it is fitting." And this condition does not cease so long as the heavens and the earth tremble and quake.

The Author of the Epistles' words about "the last of the links of the chain prepared for their punishment" are now expounded: `isharah minhu ila akhir al-naw'ayn min anwa' al-'adhab, li-anna al-silsilah hiya al-adrak al-sab'ah allati yu'azzabu fihi al-'usat bi-anwa' al-'adhab, wa-yalbasuna fi kulli naw'in min anwa'iha sakina qamisan, kama sabaqa bihi al-qawl, wa-hadha huwa al-'adhab al-adna` — "a sign from him to the end of the two types of punishment, because the chain is the seven stages in which the disobedient are punished with types of punishment, and they wear in every type of its types a thick shirt, as was previously stated; and this is the lesser punishment." The "chain" is the seven stages, `al-adrak al-sab'ah`, through which the disobedient are punished with diverse torments, donning in each stage a thick shirt as was earlier described. This is the lesser punishment, `al-'adhab al-adna`. And it is the very punishment Allah named in His Mighty Book: `khudhuhu fa-ghulluhu thumma al-jahima salluhu thumma fi silsilatin dhar'uha sab'una dhira'an fa-slukuhu` — "Seize him and shackle him. Then into the Hellfire cast him. Then into a chain whose length is seventy cubits, insert him." Seize the man and shackle him; cast him into the blaze of Hell; then thread him into a chain whose links measure seventy cubits, and drive him through it. The seven stages of the chain, and the punishment of the four peripheries, `atraf`, together constitute this lesser torment.

It is essential to see how this lesser punishment relates to the wandering of the form already described, for the two are distinct processes. The evil form, after departing, roams the world, sows corruption, joins the people of enmity, increases their hostility, and then goes to the two knots, where it remains until its time to descend and take its `qisasat` in the era of some Imam, after which it returns to its place at the two knots. That is the path of the *form*. The souls and bodies, meanwhile — the `nufus` and `ajsam` left in the world once the form has separated — undergo the worldly punishments: the seven links of the chain and the punishment of the peripheries are inflicted upon them. Only afterward, at the appearance of the Qa'im, do the form and the soul meet again, when the great judgment is rendered.

### The Judgment of the Qa'im and the Greater Punishment

The "plunging" by the angels of punishment is now placed in its sequence. The text reads: `yudillu dhalika 'ala annahu ba'da hudurihim bayna yaday al-Qa'im 'ala dhikrihi al-salam wa-hisabihi iyyahum wa-tashkhisihi li-kulli suratin ma laha li-ahli dawrihi wa-tabkitihi iyyahum 'ala al-ifsad wal-ta'nif, thumma qatlihim wa-'stihalatuhum bukharan wa-su'uduhum ila ufuq al-'uqdatan wa-masiruhum tahta al-sakhrah allati hiya asfal al-safilin` — "this indicates that this is after their being present before the Qa'im, peace be upon his mention, and his reckoning of them, and his particularizing for every form what is due to it for the people of its cycle, and his rebuking them for their corruption and harshness; then their being killed, and their transformation into vapor, and their ascent to the horizon of the two knots, and their journey beneath the Sakhrah, which is the lowest of the low." The plunging of the souls by the angels of punishment, then, takes place after a definite event: their being brought to stand before the Qa'im. He renders their reckoning, `hisab`; he specifies for every form what is its due for the people of its cycle, `tashkhis li-kulli suratin ma laha`; he rebukes them for their corruption and their harshness, `tabkit 'ala al-ifsad`. Then comes their killing, their transformation into vapor, `istihala bukharan`, their ascent to the horizon of the two knots, and their journey beneath the Sakhrah — the rock — which is the lowest of the low.

The text then names the agents of this final consignment: `taslim al-'uqdatayn wa-ash'at Zuhal wal-Mirrikh lahum ila al-sakhrah` — "the handing over by the two knots and the rays of Saturn and Mars toward the Sakhrah." The two knots and the rays of Saturn, `Zuhal`, and Mars, `Mirrikh`, hand the evil forms over toward the rock. It is through Saturn and Mars that those punishing rays reach there — into the middle of the rock, where the Sun, `shams`; the Moon, `qamar`; Venus, `zohra`; and Mercury, `'atarud`, have no access. The benevolent and luminous planets cannot enter that place; only the rays of the maleficent planets and the two knots reach it. And the duration is given: `thumma masiruhum fi wasat al-sakhrah, mu'adhdhabina fiha ma damati al-samawatu wal-ard… wa-huwa madda al-qawl al-a'zam alladhi huwa thalathumi'at alfin wa-sittun alfan sana, madrubah fi mithliha` — "then their journey into the middle of the Sakhrah, being punished therein as long as the heavens and the earth endure… and this is the extent of the Greatest Cycle, which is three hundred and sixty thousand years, multiplied by its like." The punishment in the middle of the rock, `wasat al-sakhrah`, lasts as long as the heavens and the earth endure — the span of the Greatest Cycle, `al-qawl al-a'zam`, which is three hundred and sixty thousand solar years (four hundred thousand by the lunar reckoning), multiplied by itself. Against this the prayer is offered: `ajarana Allahu min dhalika bi-haqqi Muhammadin wa-alihi al-tahirin, salawatullah 'alayhim ajma'in` — "May Allah protect us from that, by the right of Muhammad and his pure progeny, the blessings of Allah be upon them all."

The structure of the two punishments must be held clearly. The lesser punishment, `'adhab al-adna`, comprises the seven stages of the chain and the punishment of the four peripheries, the `atraf`. Many criminals turn back after only two or three stages; only the very worst reach the final stage and pass through the peripheral torments of the inhabited quarter, the `rub'-e maskun` — west, east, north, and south. The greater punishment, `'adhab al-akbar`, is the rock, the `sakhrah`, and it comes only after the Qa'im appears. After the peripheral punishments are complete, the evil souls and bodies are present before the Qa'im, who judges them; fire descends from their own forms, burning body and soul; they are reduced to ash; and they enter the rock through its gates, `abwab`. The lesser punishment precedes; the rock follows; and between the confrontation, `muqatilah`, at the Qa'im's appearance, the believers complain that corruption issues from these dead bodies and pray against them, whereupon fire from the heavens descends from the forms themselves to consume body and soul.

### The Wrathful Angel: The Tenth Intellect and Its Gates

The chapter now expounds the phrase "and the wrathful angel drives it," `wa-zabanaha malik al-ghadban`. (This is from the epistle of the Author of the Epistles, `sahib al-rasa'il` — Maulana Ahmad, the ninth Imam in the line: after the seventh, Maulana Muhammad bin Isma'il, came Maulana 'Abdullah, then Maulana Ahmad, then Maulana Husain.) The verb `zabana` means to drive away, to repel; `malik al-ghadban` repels and drives the forms before him. And the commentary identifies this wrathful angel with a supreme reality: `fa-hadhihi isharatun ila al-'aql al-'ashir, alladhi huwa al-mudabbir li-jami'i 'alam al-tabi'ah, wal-malik laha, wal-murattib li-kulli shay'in minha fi martabatihi, wa-huwa ghazban 'ala kulli 'asin lillahi ta'ala wa-li-awliya'ihi` — "this is an indication to the Tenth Intellect, who is the administrator of the entire world of nature, and its owner, and the arranger of everything from it in its rank, and who is wrathful against every sinner against Allah, the Exalted, and against the Friends of Allah."

The wrathful angel, then, is the Tenth Intellect, `al-'aql al-'ashir`. He is the administrator, `mudabbir`, of the entire world of nature, `'alam al-tabi'ah`; its owner, `al-malik laha`; and the arranger, `al-murattib`, who sets everything within it in its proper rank. (One must distinguish `murattib`, the one who arranges — the agent — from `murattab`, that which is arranged — the object; the Tenth Intellect is the arranger, the `fa'il`, never the merely arranged.) And he is wrathful, `ghazban`, against every sinner against Allah and against the Friends of Allah. The same Intellect that orders the world of nature in justice is, by that very justice, wrathful against every transgressor of it.

The Tenth Intellect is, moreover, a gate. The text continues: `wa-huwa bab 'alam al-quds, kama anna al-shams bab 'alam al-tabi'ah, wa-kama anna hujjat tilka al-maqam bab dini` — "and he is the gate to the world of sanctity, just as the sun is the gate to the world of nature, and just as the *hujjah* of that station is a religious gate." The Tenth Intellect is the gate, `bab`, to the world of sanctity, `'alam al-quds` — even as the sun is the gate to the world of nature, and even as the `hujjah` of every Imam is the religious gate, `bab dini`, of his Imam. There is thus a triple correspondence of gates: the *hujjah* opens the way in religion, the sun opens the way into the world of nature, and the Tenth Intellect opens the way into the world of holiness.

This same gate bears two names according to whom it serves. The text states: `wa-huwa al-mushar ilayhi bi-Ridwan al-khazin al-jinan, idh ilayhi intiha'u kulli nafs salihah` — "and he is the one indicated by Ridwan, the treasurer of the gardens of paradise, since to him is the end of every righteous soul." When this gate serves the righteous, it is named Ridwan, the treasurer of the gardens of paradise, `khazin al-jinan` — the keeper of the heavens. There are thus two names for the one office: Ridwan and malik al-ghadban. Malik al-ghadban is for the sinners, the disobedient; Ridwan is for the righteous souls, `nufus salihah`. Both, however, are servants of the Tenth Intellect, the `'ashir`, and to him is the end and destination of every soul, for to him every soul at last returns — the righteous through Ridwan, the sinner through the wrathful angel.

The sun, the gate of the world of nature, is also named for Ridwan, and for a reason: `wa-qad yusamma al-shams allati hiya bab al-tabi'ah ayDan bi-Ridwan, li-annahu maghnatis kulli shay'in mahmud, wa-ilayha masir al-nufus al-ruhiyyah al-mardiyyah, wa-ilayha masir nufus ahl al-zahir al-salihah al-khayyarah` — "and the sun, which is the gate of nature, is sometimes named after Ridwan, because it is a magnet for every praiseworthy thing, and to it is the destination of the pleased spiritual souls, and to it is the destination of the righteous, good souls of the people of the outward." The sun is a magnet, `maghnatis`, for every praiseworthy thing; all good things are drawn toward it. To it journey the spiritual souls that are pleasing, `al-nufus al-ruhiyyah al-mardiyyah`, and the righteous, good souls of the people of the outward, `nufus ahl al-zahir al-salihah al-khayyarah`. And conversely, all evil things are drawn toward Saturn, `Zuhal`, and Mars, `Mirrikh`.

It is upon this principle — that good is magnetized to the sun and evil to Saturn and Mars — that the auspicious times are fixed. The auspicious hour is determined by the planetary hours, `sa'at`. On Friday, the day of Jumu'ah, the first hour belongs to Venus, `Zuhra`; then follows the hour of Mercury, `'Atarid`; then the Moon, `Qamar`; then, after the Moon, the hour of Saturn, `Zuhal`, begins; then Jupiter, `Mushtari`; then Mars, `Mirrikh`. The hours are observed in this order, and the time is stated after reciting Bismillah.

Yet the name malik al-ghadban falls also upon Saturn itself. The text declares: `wa-qad yaqa'u ayDan ism malik al-ghadban 'ala Zuhal, innahu al-mutawalli li-ihbat nufus al-'usat ila tahta al-sakhrah bi-ash'atihi wa-ash'at al-'uqdatayn` — "and the name *malik al-ghadban* also falls upon Saturn, for he is the one in charge of causing the souls of the sinners to fall beneath the rock by his rays and the rays of the two knots." Saturn bears the name malik al-ghadban because he is the one charged with the falling — `ihbat`, the causing-to-fall — of the sinners' souls beneath the rock, accomplished by his own rays and the rays of the two knots. The maleficent planet thus becomes, under this name, the very instrument that casts the disobedient souls down into the rock.

### Memory as Torment and the Permanence of Light

The Author of the Epistles' words, "and it became darkness in its pure state," `wa-sarat zulmatan bi-mujarradiha`, are now glossed: `isharatun ila kawniha fi wasat al-sakhrah, tataqalla fi al-'adhab al-alim` — "an indication to its being in the middle of the rock, burning in the painful torment." The form's becoming pure darkness signifies its being in the middle of the rock, where it burns in the painful torment, having been emptied of body.

And his words, "there appears to it, in its essence," are opened to reveal the special character of the torment of the rock. The text reads: `wa-qawluhu yutara'a laha fi dhatiha kullama ahdarat nafsaha ashkhas al-silsilati allati salakat fiha wa-ju'ilat biha 'inda… innaha ma'a ma hiya fihi dhakirah tahaqquqan li-dhalika al-'adhab al-adna alladhi salakat fihi wa-jami'u atrabiha, thumma kawnuhum ila al-'adhab al-akbar ba'da dhalik` — "and his saying, 'there appears to it, in its essence, whenever its soul presents itself, the figures of the chain in which it traversed and was placed'… indeed, along with what it is in, it is remembering, as a realization of that lesser torment in which it traversed, it and all of its peers, then their being destined for the greater torment after that." Within the rock the soul retains a complete memory. Along with the present torment it now endures, it is remembering, `dhakirah` — vividly recollecting the lesser torment, `'adhab al-adna`, through which it had already passed, recalling the chain and every stage of it, and recalling all its companions, `atrab`, who passed through with it. And it remembers, too, that beyond the lesser torment came the greater, `'adhab al-akbar`, into which it then entered. The recollection of how it traversed the chain of the lesser torment and how it came into the greater is itself a torment — for this remembering is no neutral act of recall but a renewed suffering.

The text underscores that nothing is forgotten: `wa-inna jami'a ma salaku fihi wa-usilu ilayhi bi-'isyanihim lil-maqamat al-'aliyah wa-hududihim salamu Allahi 'alayhim ghayru gha'ibin 'anha jami'u dhalik` — "and indeed all that they traversed in and were brought to through their disobedience to the lofty stations and their *hudud*, peace of Allah be upon them, none of that is absent from it." All that the punished souls traversed, and all that they were brought to through their disobedience, `'isyan`, to the lofty stations, `al-maqamat al-'aliyah`, and their *hudud* — none of it is absent from them. They retain the entire memory of every kind of torment they have passed through, by reason of their disobedience to the high Imam and his ranks; and that unfading memory is woven into the very fabric of the punishment.

The Author of the Epistles' words about the waves of Hell are likewise expounded: `wa-qawluhu, wa-amwaj jahannam marratan tarfa'uha ila bahr al-athir, wa-marratan tahbutu biha ila bard al-zamharir, isharatun minhu, salawatullahi 'alayhi, ayDan ila tadhakkurihim kawnahum fi nahiyat al-shimal allati hiya markaz al-zamharir, wa-nahiyat al-junub allati hiya markaz al-athir, wa-masirihim ila hunalika ba'da istifa'ihim 'adhab al-adrak` — "and his saying, 'And the waves of Hell, one time raise it to the sea of ether, and another time bring it down to the cold of *zamharir*,' is an indication, God's blessings be upon him, also to their remembering their being in the northern direction, which is the center of *zamharir*, and the southern direction, which is the center of ether, and their journeying there after they have fully received the torment of the cycles." The waves of Hell that raise the soul to the heat of the ether and drop it to the cold of `zamharir` point also to a remembered geography. The northern direction, `nahiyat al-shimal`, is the center of `zamharir`, the sphere of extreme cold; the southern direction, `nahiyat al-junub`, is the center of the ether, the sphere of heat. The punished souls journey to these regions after they have fully received the torment of the cycles, `'adhab al-adrak`. This northern cold and southern heat are constant centers, not the mere alternation of the seasons; though the natural world reflects something of the same polarity in its great turnings — the long winter and summer of the poles, the seasonal migration of creatures across the earth — the centers themselves are fixed seats of cold and heat to which the punished are borne.

The "devils falling with it" are then identified: `wa-qawluhu, wal-shayatin tahwi maha, isharatun ila 'ulama'ihim wa-ru'asa'ihim al-mudillina lahum, al-mawkulina maham bi-hadhihi al-'adhabat al-karihah` — "and his saying, 'and the devils fall with it,' is an indication to their scholars and their chiefs who lead them astray, who are entrusted alongside them in these loathsome torments." The "devils" that fall together with the punished souls are their misleading scholars, `'ulama'`, and their chiefs, `ru'asa'`, who had led them astray; these are entrusted alongside the souls they misled, sharing with them these loathsome torments. The leaders who misguided are made to fall with those whom they misguided.

### The Stoning of Meteors and the Driving-Off

The phrase "and the angels stone it with the meteors of torment," `wal-mala'ikah tarjumuha bi-shuhub al-'adhab`, is now given its profound interpretation. The text reads: `wa-huwa ma yasri ilayhim min al-la'nat al-'uqul al-mujarradah fi kulli waqtin wa-hin, wa-min tajdid al-'adhab wa-shiddat al-an… 'indama ya'innu dhikruhum wa-dhikr ru'asa'ihim fa-yal'anuhum al-hudud wal-mu'minun, wa-yanshuruna masawihim wa-yad'una 'alayhim wa-la yarhamuna, fa-yashtaddu 'alayhim al-'adhab wa-yatajaddadu, idh al-hudud hum al-mala'ikah bil-quwwah` — "which is what flows to them from the curses of the disembodied intellects at every time and moment, and from the renewal of the torment and the severity of the pain — when their mention, and the mention of their chiefs, arises, so the *hudud* and the believers curse them, and spread their vices, and pray against them, and show no mercy, so the torment intensifies upon them and is renewed, since the *hudud* are angels in potentiality."

The stoning of meteors, then, is not merely a celestial event but the descent upon these forms of what flows to them from the curses, `la'nat`, of the disembodied intellects, `al-'uqul al-mujarradah` — the pure intellects, cleansed of all body and materiality, who are charged with sending torment upon the punished and who curse them at every time and moment. And it is, further, the renewal of the torment that occurs whenever the mention of these sinners and of their chiefs arises among the living: then the *hudud* and the believers curse them, spread abroad their vices, `masawi`, pray against them, and show them no mercy. By this the torment is intensified and renewed upon them — for the *hudud* are angels in potentiality, `al-mala'ikah bil-quwwah`, and their living curse takes effect upon the punished as a genuine stoning. The shooting stars, `shihab thaqib`, that fall in the world, striking where the administrator, `mudabbir`, directs them, are the outward sign of this inward law: the torment that descends upon these forms flows from the curses of the pure intellects above and from the curses of the *hudud* and believers below.

The text draws out the cosmic correspondence: `wa-yakunu bi-dhalika saryan ash'at al-shams bi-shiddat harr al-athir, wa-ash'at al-qamar bi-shiddat bard al-zamharir, wa-hum al-mala'ikah bil-quwwah, wa-ayDan li-anna al-athir alladhi yaqa'u 'ala hararat al-shams wal-zamharir 'ala bard al-qamar` — "and by that there is the flowing of the rays of the sun with the intensity of the heat of the ether, and the rays of the moon with the intensity of the cold of *zamharir*, and they are the angels in potentiality; and also because the ether falls upon the heat of the sun, and the *zamharir* upon the cold of the moon." So too the rays of the sun flow with the intensity of the ether's heat, and the rays of the moon with the intensity of the `zamharir`'s cold; for the *hudud* are angels in potentiality and stand in correspondence with sun and moon. The ether is joined to the sun's heat, and the `zamharir` to the moon's cold, so that the very luminaries become channels of the torment.

Finally, the Author of the Epistles' words, "and they are pelted with it, being driven off from every side," `wa-yurma biha duhuran min kulli janib`, are expounded: `isharatun ila ibraziha ila hadhihi al-biqa' al-khabithah al-mu'addah li-'adhabiha wa-tazyiliha min ard al-maskunah allati hiya bil-idafati ila tilka al-biqa' al-khabithah al-karihah jannatun muzakhrafah, ajarana Allahu wa-jami'a ikhwanina minha bi-haqqi Muhammadin wa-alihi al-tahirin` — "an indication to their being brought forth to these filthy plains prepared for their torment, and their removal from the inhabited land, which, in relation to those filthy, loathsome plains, is a decorated paradise. May Allah protect us and all our brethren from it, by the right of Muhammad and his pure progeny." Their being driven off from every side, `duhuran`, signifies their being brought out to the filthy plains, `al-biqa' al-khabithah`, that have been prepared for their torment, and their being removed from the inhabited land, `al-ard al-maskunah`. And the inhabited land, set beside those filthy and loathsome plains, is by comparison a decorated paradise, `jannatun muzakhrafah` — the very place where men now dwell, however far it is from the true paradise, is yet a garden when measured against the plains of torment to which these forms are exiled. May Allah protect us and all our brethren from such a fate, by the right of Muhammad and his pure progeny.

It is at this point that the limit of human knowledge is reached, for what lies beyond — the further dispositions of the administering Intellect — is known to the Mudabbir of the world alone. As the poet Iqbal said: `Khirad kya hai, chiragh-e rahguzar hai, ke jis se admi rawshan basar hai. Darun-e khanah hangame hain kya kya, chiragh-e rahguzar ko kya khabar hai` — "What is intellect? It is a lamp of the pathway, by which a man's sight is illumined. What tumults rage within the house? What does the lamp of the pathway know?" Intellect, `khirad`, is a lamp — but a lamp of the path, not of the house. It is set upon the road so that a man may find his way in and out with ease; by it his sight is illumined. But of the tumults within the house, `darun-e khanah`, the lamp of the pathway has no knowledge. So too the human intellect lights the way along the visible road of doctrine, yet of the inmost dispositions of the unseen it knows only what it is given to know.

### The Inheritance of the Former Scriptures

The section closes with an authority that anchors the whole teaching in the inherited revelation of the prophets. It is related — a narration met with in *Saluni* — that when Amir al-Mu'minin, peace and blessings be upon him, became the ruler, `wali`, and led the prayer, he recited at Fajr the Surah `Sabbih isma rabbika al-a'la`: `Sabbih isma rabbika al-a'la alladhi khalaqa fa-sawwa wa-lladhi qaddara fa-hada` — "Glorify the name of your Lord, the Most High, Who created and proportioned, and Who destined and then guided." On the second day he recited the same Surah; on the third, the same; and for a whole month he continued to recite it. The people asked him whether he remembered no other Surah, that he recited `Sabbih isma rabbika al-a'la` without change. He answered them by pointing to its closing verses: `Qad aflaha man tazakka wa-dhakara isma rabbihi fa-salla, bal tu'thiruna al-hayata al-dunya, wal-akhiratu khayrun wa-abqa, inna hadha la-fi al-suhuf al-ula, suhufi Ibrahima wa-Musa` — "He has certainly succeeded who purifies himself, and mentions the name of his Lord and prays. But you prefer the worldly life, while the Hereafter is better and more enduring. Indeed, this is in the former scriptures, the scriptures of Abraham and Moses."

The whole point lay in the words `inna hadha la-fi al-suhuf al-ula` — "indeed, this is in the former scriptures." Whatever this Surah declares, said the Amir, is contained already in the ancient scriptures, the `al-suhuf al-ula`, which are the scriptures of Abraham and Moses, `suhuf Ibrahima wa-Musa`. These scriptures were given in inheritance to all the prophets; and he himself had received them in inheritance from the Prophet. From those scriptures of Abraham and Moses he was drawing the things that are part of that inheritance — and it was for the sake of those inherited wonders that he recited the Surah continually, because the marvels contained in the legacy of Abraham and Moses are great. The recitation was no mere repetition but a continual drawing upon an inherited treasure, the same legacy of the former scriptures through which the origin and the return, the angels and the souls, and the law of retribution have all been made known.

## Retribution, Inheritance, and the Word of Unity

### The Scale of Justice Established Over Every Servant

This portion of the discourse, drawn from the fourth chapter (*bab al-rabi'*) of the seventh pavilion (*surad*) and continuing into the fourth pavilion, resumes a theme that had reached its natural conclusion in the preceding lectures: the doctrine of *mabda' wa-ma'ad*, the beginning and the end, considered with respect to three classes of human being — the *wali*, the friend of God; the *zahiri*, the merely exoteric believer who lives at the surface of religion; and the *didd*, the opponent who stands against the truth. The thread that binds these three together, and which now becomes the governing principle of the entire section, is the scale of justice — *mizan al-'adl* — which Allah, exalted is He, has established and set in motion over all His servants without exception.

The foundational statement is unambiguous. The scale of justice that Allah has erected over His creatures `lā yughādiru ṣaghīratan wa-lā kabīratan illā aḥṣāhā` — it leaves behind neither a small deed nor a great one without having enumerated it, counted it, and entered it into the reckoning. Nothing escapes this scale. Whether a person is a friend of God, an exoteric believer, or an outright adversary, the justice of Allah encompasses him entirely; to each is given precisely in the measure of what he intends and what he does. This is not a justice administered by some arbitrary or shifting official who differs from age to age. It is an appointed, established law of Allah — and whoever is appointed to administer it administers exactly this same unvarying decree. The law is implemented; the scale is upright and standing.

The principle of recompense built into this scale is stated in the Arabic of the text: `wa-jāzā ʿalayhā, in khayran fa-khayrun, wa-in sharran fa-sharrun` — and He recompenses according to it: if there be good, then good shall be the reward; and if there be evil, then evil shall be the requital. Whoever performs anything, however small or however large, will receive its recompense in kind. Do a good deed, and good returns to you; commit an evil, and the evil returns upon you. There is here no escape and no exception, for the operation is, in a sense, automatic: the one who does good reaps its benefit of itself, and the one who does evil suffers its loss of itself.

This is the doctrine the section labors to expound at every level, from the cosmic to the most minute, and it is worth dwelling on its full reach before turning to its difficult consequences. The justice of Allah is operative over everything. As the soul performs, so the soul bears. There is no act of good or evil, however hidden, however trivial it may appear in the eyes of men, that the scale does not register and to which it does not assign its exact and proportionate return.

### The Friends of God Are Not Exempt From Retribution

The most arresting application of this principle — and the one that most tests the believer's understanding — is that even the very highest among God's chosen are not removed from its operation. The discourse recalls a matter already mentioned in earlier lectures: the passing of the *natiq*, the speaking-prophet, who departed this world poisoned, and the passing of his *wasi*, his testamentary heir, Amir al-Mu'minin, who was killed. The Arabic preserves the formulation: `wa-dhakarnā intiqāl al-nāṭiq dawr masmūman wa-naqlat waṣiyyihi bil-mawt... maqtūlan` — we have mentioned the passing of the speaking-prophet of the cycle in a state of being poisoned, and the passing of his heir by death... slain. And likewise, the text continues, `wa-kadhalika mā wālānā al-Ḥasan wal-Ḥusayn wal-Mustanṣir wal-ʿĀmir, ṣalawāt Allāh ʿalayhim ajmaʿīn, alladhīna minhum man intaqala masmūman wa-minhum man intaqala maqtūlan` — and likewise our masters al-Hasan, al-Husayn, al-Mustansir, and al-'Amir, the blessings of Allah be upon them all, among whom were those who passed away poisoned and those who passed away slain.

These are not random tragedies, nor merely the cruelty of wicked men allowed to run its course. The text insists upon their deeper cause: `wa-inna dhālika jarā ʿalayhim bi-mūjib al-ḥikmah wa-qaḍiyyat al-ʿadl qiṣāṣan` — and indeed that befell them by the requirement of wisdom and the demand of justice, as retribution (*qisas*). The teacher pauses here over a fine point of the Arabic, distinguishing `inna`, which means "indeed," from `idhā`, which means "if" — the reading must be "and indeed that befell them," for it is an affirmation of a settled reality, not a conditional possibility. What befell these exalted figures came upon them as *qisas*, as the exacting of a recompense, `ʿammā kāna min kulli wāḥidin min ahl dawrihi` — for that which proceeded from every single one of the people of their cycle.

The doctrine is then stated in its most uncompromising form: `wa-dhāka anna al-qiṣāṣ wāqiʿun ʿalayhim, salām Allāh ʿalayhim, ṣafwat Allāh min khalqihi wa-umanāʾahu ʿalā waḥyihi wa-khulafāʾahu fī arḍihi wa-shuhadāʾahu ʿalā ʿibādihi, fa-lā suqūṭa lahu ʿan shayʾin min ʿālam al-ṭabīʿah jumlatun, wa-lā manāṣa li-ṣaghīrihi wa-lā kabīrihi wa-qalīlihi wa-kathīrihi` — and that is because retribution falls upon them, the peace of Allah be upon them, who are the chosen elect of Allah from His creation, His trustees over His revelation, His vicegerents in His land, and His witnesses over His servants; yet there is no falling away of it from anything whatsoever in the world of nature, in its entirety, and there is no escape from it for its small or its great, its little or its much. The very fact that they are God's elect, His *umana'* over revelation, His *khulafa'* upon the earth, His *shuhada'* over His servants — this exalted rank does not lift them out of the scale; the scale operates upon them precisely as it operates upon the world of nature in its entirety.

To drive home how absolute this is, the text reasons from the greater to the lesser: `idh law kānat al-musāmaḥah jāʾizatan li-aḥadin min dhālika aw shayʾin minhu, la-kāna Awliyāʾ Allāh ʿalayhim al-salām aḥaqqu wa-awlā bil-musāmaḥah min jamīʿ al-khalqah` — for if pardon (*musamaha*), the overlooking and remission of the debt, were permissible for anyone in any of this or any part of it, then surely the Friends of Allah, peace be upon them, would have been the most deserving and the most worthy of pardon of all creation. The argument is decisive. If the law admitted of any waiver, the Friends of God would have been the first to receive it; they would have stood at the head of the line for mercy. That they did not — that retribution was exacted from them in full — proves that no waiver exists. The very same law that governs all creation governs the Friends of God as well, and they too were held to account in the matter of *qisas*, whether the debt arose from their own actions or from the people of their cycle who stood with them in their age.

### Deeds Reach Across the People of an Era

This last point opens onto a further and subtler teaching: that a person's deeds are not sealed within his own person but extend to and are accounted against the people of his era. The retribution due from one age may therefore be exacted in another. The discourse illustrates this with the supreme example of Imam Husayn, upon him be peace. It was said of him — and the teaching is preserved as a settled doctrine — that Mawla Ali had fought certain battles, and in those battles a number of men were killed, men from among the forebears of those who later confronted Husayn. The recompense for those slayings was exacted not from Ali himself but from Imam Husayn, upon him be peace, in his own later age.

The principle is general: a person's actions are not confined to himself; they touch the people of his era, and the people of his era are held to account in turn. If they themselves committed the deed, the recompense returns to them directly; if it was committed by the people of their era, then the people of the era bear it; and a relationship is established between an event and those gathered around it through which the reckoning is completed. So it is said that in the time of Imam Husayn a veil, a concealment, descended — and the cause of that veil reached back to events of the time of Mawla Ali, the same matter manifesting again in a later cycle to bring its account to settlement. Nor does this transfer pertain to evil deeds alone; the giving and taking across the eras is of good and of evil alike. A good deed, too, can come to a man's aid across the span of an age; one's good deed comes in handy, and the scale credits it no less faithfully than it debits the evil. The justice of Allah is operative over everything, and `fa-lā suqūṭa lahu ʿan shayʾin min ʿālam al-ṭabīʿah` — there is no exemption from it for anything whatsoever in the world of nature. Nothing in the world of nature can slip beyond its grasp. As you do, so shall you bear.

The text gathers up this universal sweep in a single sentence: `wa-idh kāna al-qawl kadhālika, fa-lā manāṣa li-aḥadin min al-khalāʾiq ʿan dhālika, sharīfihim wa-mashrūfihim wa-ʿazīzihim wa-dhalīlihim wa-rafīʿihim wa-waḍīʿihim` — and since the matter is so, there is no escape for anyone among created beings from this, neither for their noble nor their lowly, neither for their mighty nor their humble, neither for their high-ranking nor their base. The scale spares none on account of rank or station. And the reason is given immediately: `idh mīzān al-ʿadl qāʾimun, wa-laysa lillāhi Taʿālā wa-lā li-aḥadin min ʿibādihi muḥābātun fī fiʿlin yafʿaluhu, khayran kāna aw sharran` — for the scale of justice is standing and upright, and there is no favoritism (*muhabat*) belonging to Allah, exalted is He, nor to any of His servants, in any act that he performs, whether it be good or evil. The word *muhabat* means precisely this favoritism, this showing of partiality — and it is excluded absolutely. Neither Allah nor any servant of His shows favor in the matter of good and evil; the deed and its recompense are bound together by a justice that cannot be bent.

This is sealed by the word of the Quran: `kamā qāla Taʿālā fī muḥkam kitābihi: fa-man yaʿmal mithqāla dharratin khayran yarah, wa-man yaʿmal mithqāla dharratin sharran yarah` — as the Exalted has said in the decisive verses of His Book: "So whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil shall see it." Even the weight of a mote of dust, of good or of evil, is registered and returned. The text affirms that this verse falls upon all creation together — `wa-hādhihi al-āyah wāqiʿatun ʿalā al-khalq ajmaʿīn` — and that everything a person does of good or evil returns upon him as its recompense.

### The Difference Between Evil and Sin, and the Tenfold Reward

A careful question arises here, and the discourse takes it up directly. The verse speaks of an atom's weight of *khayr* (good) and an atom's weight of *sharr* (evil); it does not speak of *gunah* (sin). Are evil and sin then the same thing? The answer drawn out is that they are not. *Sharr*, evil, and *gunah*, sin, are distinct: sin is born from evil. As good is the soil from which good deeds grow, so evil is the soil from which sin springs; sin issues from evil as a particular fruit issues from a tree.

And the rates of recompense differ between the two columns of the scale. A good deed earns a reward multiplied tenfold; an evil deed earns a single recompense, one for one. The reward for good is tenfold, the requital for evil is onefold — yet, and this must not be lost, there is most certainly a recompense for both. Neither column goes unpaid. The difference lies in the severity and the multiplication of the return, not in whether a return is rendered at all. Both good and evil are infallibly recompensed; good is simply rewarded with a generous abundance that evil's bare single measure does not receive.

### Two Kinds of Sin: Against God and Against His Servants

This leads to one of the central rulings of the section, repeated and emphasized because so much turns upon it. There are two distinct kinds of sin, and they are forgiven in entirely different ways. The first is a sin against Allah Himself — a transgression of a command that Allah has given to be obeyed. This kind of sin Allah forgives through the servant's seeking of forgiveness (*istighfar*); repentance washes it away, and it is done. We say as much in our prayers, asking forgiveness of God for what we have failed Him in.

But the second kind is a sin against the servants of God — a wrong done to another human being, a right of His servants violated, a debt of retribution incurred toward a fellow creature. This kind Allah does not forgive through the wrongdoer's *istighfar* alone. It remains owing until one of two things occurs: either the wronged servant himself forgives the debt, or Allah, exalted is He, arranges some means by which the recompense reaches the wronged party in another way, through which the account may be discharged and the sinner's burden lifted. And if neither the servant's pardon nor a divinely arranged means brings the dues to their owner, then *qisas* — retribution — must take place. This is the very meaning of retribution as it operates between the servants: the rights owed among them cannot simply be cancelled by repentance to God, for they are not God's to remit; they belong to the wronged, and they must reach the wronged.

There is one explicit limit upon this. Acts done under the rulings of the sacred law (*ahkam al-shari'a*) do not carry this debt. If a man is lawfully commanded to be killed — sentenced by a valid order — and is then killed in execution of that order, no retribution becomes obligatory upon the one who carries it out. The shari'a's making the act lawful removes the debt that would otherwise attach to a killing. Where the law has declared an act permissible and it is performed accordingly, *qisas* does not follow.

### What the Lawful Transaction Removes From the Scale

The text now states the general rule of which this is an instance: `wa-kullu mā yafʿaluhu al-insān min khayrin aw sharrin ʿāʾidun ʿalayhi mukāfātan bihi illā mā aḥallathu al-sharīʿah wa-sallama mā ahlāluhā fī qiymatihi ʿan tarāḍin bihi wa-inqiyādin lahu, ka-mithl al-nikāḥ al-jārī ʿalā kitāb Allāh wa-sunnat rasūlihi, ṣalawāt Allāh ʿalayhi, fa-lā qiṣāṣa fīhi` — and everything that a person does of good or evil returns upon him as its recompense, except for that which the shari'a has made lawful and whose price it has caused to be delivered, by mutual consent (*tarad*) and submission (*inqiyad*) — like marriage conducted upon the Book of Allah and the practice of His Messenger, the blessings of Allah be upon him, in which there is no retribution.

The principle here is that a transaction freed of debt requires three things together: the act must be made lawful by the shari'a; its consideration or price must actually be delivered; and there must be genuine mutual consent and submission between the parties. Where all three hold, no *qisas* attaches. The teacher draws the comparison with ordinary lawful trade. When goods, houses, lands, garments, and the like change hands by mutual consent, through buying and selling for a known and agreed price, no retribution is reckoned in it — for it was done by willing agreement, not by theft and not by stealth, given and taken in open consent and obedience to the terms. Yet the lawfulness of the shari'a must accompany the mutual consent; consent alone is not enough. Adultery (*zina*), the teacher notes pointedly, can also occur by mutual consent — and yet retribution is most certainly reckoned in it, precisely because the shari'a has not made it lawful. The lawful marriage and the unlawful coupling may both involve consent; what distinguishes them, and what removes or imposes the debt of *qisas*, is whether the shari'a has sanctioned the act and whether its proper consideration has been rendered.

### Why the Imams' Lawful Actions Still Incurred Retribution

A profound difficulty now confronts the believer, and the discourse states it as the objection of a hypothetical questioner: `wa-laʿalla qāʾilan yaqūlu: fa-inna qatl al-maqāmāt li-man qatalūhu, salām Allāh ʿalayhim, wa-jald man jaladūhu wa-ḥasb man ḥasabūhu, bi-mā amara Allāh Taʿālā bihi` — and perhaps a sayer may say: the killing by the *maqamat*, the Imams, of those whom they killed, the peace of Allah be upon them, and their flogging of those whom they flogged, and their stoning of those whom they stoned, by that which Allah, exalted is He, commanded — these were acts the Imams performed under divine command. The objection presses: these actions were pronounced lawful by Allah's noble Book in many of its verses, were issued from His Messenger, and were enjoined by all the Imams of His religion — `man naṭaqa bihi kitābuhu al-karīm fī kathīrin min āyātihi, wa-ṣudūr anābiya min rasūlihi, wa-kāffat aʾimmat dīnihi` — so why, then, was retribution made obligatory in these acts, when it was dropped entirely from the case of marriage — `fa-lima wajaba fīhi al-qiṣāṣ, wa-usqiṭa ʿan al-nikāḥ` — given that He, exalted is He, leaves behind neither a small thing nor a great thing without enumerating it?

In marriage, the questioner reasons, the companionship that takes place occurs with mutual consent, and so no *qisas* attaches. Why should the lawfully commanded killings, floggings, and stonings of the Imams not be similarly exempt, since they too were lawful by divine command?

Before resolving the objection, the discourse pauses on the example of stoning for adultery, for it sharpens the same puzzle from another angle. The punishment of *zina* is that the offender is stoned — yet the wrong, if it was a wrong of two persons, was committed by two; how is it that the whole community stones the one offender? The answer given is that the stoning is carried out by the command of Allah, and it is commanded collectively because *zina* is an act from which collective harms are bred. Adultery is an act of just such a kind: from it nothing arises but shamelessness and a corruption that spreads through the whole body of the community. To remove those collective harms — so that such evils do not take root and multiply among the people — Allah has established this command, that the offense of two be answered by the hand of all. Although it is an act of two individuals, its punishment is made collective in order to lift the collective harm. And this, the text adds, was not a command peculiar to Islam: in every earlier religion — among the Jews, among the Christians, among all of them — stoning for adultery was likewise the command of Allah.

The discourse then turns to the deeper resolution of the objection, and it does so by way of a story preserved from Mawla Ali, upon him be peace, in the Battle of the Camel (*Jamal*). As Amir al-Mu'minin's armies were advancing toward the field, there was a pregnant woman sitting at her doorway. When the armies arrived and passed, the trampling and stamping of the horses' hooves so alarmed her that she went into labor and gave birth, and the child died. Afterward her tribe came to Amir al-Mu'minin demanding the blood-money (*diya*) for her loss. The matter was presented: this had occurred in the press of a confrontation — who, then, was to pay the blood-money? Amir al-Mu'minin ruled that it should be paid from the public treasury (*bayt al-mal*); the *diya* would be discharged from the common funds of the Muslims, since it was the galloping of the Muslims' horses that had caused the death, in which the woman herself bore no fault. Yet before settling it, he asked a question that seemed, on its face, beside the point: "Tell me first — did the mother die before the child, or the child before the mother?" The reason for this question belongs to the law of inheritance, and the discourse returns to it below; here it suffices to mark that Ali's lawful conduct of a just war nonetheless produced a death that had to be answered, and that even a perfectly lawful military advance generated a debt the just ruler took pains to discharge.

The resolution of the great objection is this. Retribution remained obligatory upon the Imams' lawful actions — the killing, the flogging, the stoning — precisely because, in those actions, there was no consent from the party against whom they were carried out. The Arabic states it directly: `wa-amara Allāh Taʿālā bihi fī hādhihi al-ashyāʾ allatī dhakarnāhā min qatlin wa-jaldin wa-ḥasbin, lammā lam yaqaʿ bihā riḍan min al-maqtūl wa-lā sullima lahu qīmatuhu fī dhālik, maʿa iḥlāl al-sharīʿah lahā` — and Allah, exalted is He, commanded it in these things we have mentioned, of killing and flogging and stoning, when there occurred in them no consent from the one killed, and his price was not delivered to him in that matter, together with the shari'a's making the act lawful. The criminal who is being stoned does not consent to his stoning; the man who is being killed has not agreed to his death and has not been paid the value of his life. The shari'a has indeed made the act lawful — and so the agent who performs it is righteous in performing it — yet because the one acted upon neither consented nor received any consideration, the debt of retribution is not extinguished. One cannot say to the offender, "I shall give you the value, and you let me stone you"; that exchange does not occur. The lawfulness of the act protects the doer from sin in doing it, but the absence of consent on the part of the one who suffers it means that the account between them remains open and must be settled — either it was already owed from before, or it falls due in time to come.

This is why the text concludes: `wa-li-dhālika waqaʿa ʿalayhim al-qiṣāṣ bi-qiṣāṣ al-maqāmāt` — and for this reason retribution fell upon them, by the retribution of the *maqamat*, the Imams. The Imams themselves did not escape this law; how then could an ordinary person hope to escape it? The discourse marks how delicate and exact this whole relation of *qisas* is, so fine that even the blink of an eyelid, the slightest involuntary motion, is itself a tiny settlement of an account. And what of the twig whose prick draws blood, or the obstacle that trips a foot? That twig, too, was a soul — a soul that had taken the form of a twig, and that had its own giving and taking to discharge. So minute and so universal is the reach of the scale.

### Afflictions as Pending Settlements

This brings the discourse to a saying of the Messenger of Allah, the blessings of Allah be upon him, which casts the smallest mishaps of daily life in the light of the scale: `mā yuṣību aḥadakum khadshat ʿūdin wa-lā ikhtilāj ʿirqin wa-lā nakbat qadamin illā bi-dhanbin, wa-mā yaʿfū Allāh ʿanhu akthar` — no scratch of a twig afflicts any one of you, nor the throbbing of a vein, nor the stumbling of a foot, except by a sin (*dhanb*); and what Allah forgives is far more than that.

The smallest hurts — the prick of a twig, the involuntary twitching of a vein, the stumble of a step — none of these befalls a person except in connection with some sin. There is a hidden bond between the injury and the transgression; the twig, the vein, the stumbling foot are the very instruments through which a prior debt is being quietly exacted. These afflictions, then, are nothing other than pending retributions being applied, the scale collecting in small coin what is owed. And yet the Prophet's saying ends with a vast mercy: what Allah forgives is far more. The sins that are committed and then forgiven outnumber by far the ones that are exacted through such small afflictions; the discharge through suffering is the exception, the forgiveness the rule.

### Unanswered Prayers and Unfulfilled Sacrifices Stored for the Hereafter

The question whether a debt incurred in the age of concealment is repaid within that age or only in the age of manifestation prompts the teacher to relate a treasured teaching on the fate of unanswered supplications, which belongs to the same economy of the scale: nothing offered to Allah is lost; what is not requited in this world is stored and multiplied for the next.

A human being, throughout his life, makes a great many supplications (*du'a*), and many of them are not answered in this world. On the Day of Judgment, when such a person is brought to his reckoning and good deeds are found placed in his scale that he does not recall performing, he will protest: "I did not do these good deeds — have they been entered in my scale by mistake?" And he will be told: "These are the supplications you made that were not answered in the world; this reward is the recompense for them." Hearing this, the man will wish that none of his supplications had ever been answered in the world at all — so that the entire reward for them might have been laid up for him on the Day of Judgment. The unanswered prayer, far from being lost, is the most profitable of all, its full reward preserved untouched until the day it is most needed.

The teacher recalls the high esteem in which this teaching was held by the blessed master Sayyidna, who so cherished the saying that he directed it be recorded in writing among the virtues of supplication, drawing upon the book of Sayyidna Ghulam Husayn Sahib. He remembers the master in his last days — the prayer-rug laid out, the call to prayer given, the cotton pillow set by his side — directing that the book be read to him, listening to four or five lines, and correcting a passage with his own pen, saying, "It is good that you have come." The same principle was applied to the sacrifice (*qurbani*): if a person offers a sacrifice with the intention that a sick person be healed, and that sick person nonetheless passes away, does the benefit of the sacrifice come to nothing? The answer recorded is that he most certainly receives its benefit; and if that benefit has not reached him in this cycle, then in the cycle to come he will be granted its reward multiplied. What is intended for God's sake is never wasted; if its requital does not arrive in this turn of the wheel, it arrives, and arrives increased, in the next.

### The Lawful Marriage and the Rights It Settles or Leaves Owing

Returning to the case of marriage, the text states precisely what a man's lawful discharge of his obligations accomplishes: `fa-li-yaʿlam anna taslīm al-rajul mā yuslimu lil-marʾah min ḥāḍirin wa-mahrin wa-qabḍin wa-tarāḍin baynahumā maʿa iḥlāl al-sharīʿah lahu, law qad amāta ʿanhu ḥaqq al-qiṣāṣ fī dhālik` — so let it be known that a man's delivering to the woman what he delivers of the present gift (*hadir*) and the dower (*mahr*), with the taking of possession (*qabd*) and the mutual consent between them, together with the shari'a's making it lawful for him, has indeed put to death and nullified the right of retribution in that matter. When the union is contracted with consent, with the lawful sanction of the shari'a, and with the actual delivery of the present gift and the dower into the woman's possession, the question of *qisas* simply does not arise; the right has been satisfied at the source, exactly as in a lawful sale concluded for a known price.

But the text is equally precise about the opposite case: `wa-law sallama alladhī sallama min ḥāḍirin wa-mahrin bi-ghayr iḥlāl al-sharīʿah lahu, la-lazima fīhi al-qiṣāṣ` — and were he to deliver what he delivers of the present gift and dower without the shari'a's making it lawful for him, retribution would be obligatory in it. The mere transfer of goods, absent the shari'a's sanction, does not purchase exemption. And further still: `wa-law waṭiʾahā maʿa taḥrīm al-sharīʿah min ghayr dhālika bi-ghayr an yusallima lahā shayʾan min ḥāḍirin wa-mahrin, la-wajaba fīhi al-qiṣāṣ` — and were he to have intercourse with her, with the shari'a's prohibition standing and without delivering to her anything of a present gift or dower, retribution would be obligatory in it. The senior elders state this plainly: there must be both the shari'a's permission and the actual delivery of the dower; where either is missing, the debt of *qisas* falls upon him. The teacher notes, with a touch of irony toward a lax custom, that some people in their final days have the dower forgiven by the wife — she says, "I have remitted the dower" — so that the obligation is discharged by her pardon rather than by payment; but the principle stands that an undelivered dower, absent her remission, leaves the right owing.

### The Master Who Served His Slave

It is in this context that the discourse states the doctrine that the same retribution which befell the Imams in their lawful actions may be exacted within the very same cycle or carried over from a previous one. The Arabic frames it: `kamā wajaba al-qiṣāṣ ʿalā al-maqāmāt fī afʿālihim hādhihi al-madhkūrah` — just as retribution became obligatory upon the *maqamat* in these aforementioned actions of theirs. It is not necessary that the deeds for which the Imams were recompensed were performed in the same cycle in which the recompense fell; the deed may belong to an earlier cycle and the *qisas* be taken in this one — as in the case of Mawla Ali's battles, whose recompense, it is related, Imam Husayn had to render. So the settlement may occur in the same age or reach back across the ages.

And here, lest it be thought that this giving and taking concerns only debts of evil, the teacher unfolds a luminous example of the same law operating in the register of good — the maxim `khadam al-mawlā ʿabdahu`, "the master served his slave." He recalls an account preserved of Sayyidna Badruddin, blessed and sanctified: one night a certain gardener had served the master, and when the master rose for the dawn prayer and found the gardener asleep, he himself began to press the man's feet. The gardener woke in alarm — "Master, you are pressing my feet!" — and the master answered, "You served me by night; I serve you by day." This is the outward shell of the maxim. Its inner chapter is this: the prophets of old served the Imams. The Imams were hidden, veiled in concealment, while the prophets and messengers served them — bearing hardships in their cause, flogged, slain, given poison, while the Imams remained hidden. Then, when the appointed time came — the time of the Prophet, upon him be peace — all the prophets were gathered there with him. And who then served, who stood as the deputy and requiter of the hidden ones? It was Mawla Ali Murtada, upon him be peace. He served the Prophet, and through him all the outstanding accounts were settled. These were recompenses long owed; for retribution, as the teacher insists, does not occur only in matters of evil — the giving and taking runs through good and evil alike, and Ali discharged every one of those debts. When the prophets, in their hour of difficulty, called out, it was upon Ali that they called.

So too the lawful violence of the Imams generates the same kind of unconsented debt that must be settled. The shari'a has made it lawful for them — and yet, in a battle, does the sword stay its edge? Sometimes the horse bolts and tramples a bystanding woman; one cannot always halt it. And so its recompense must be rendered, exactly as Amir al-Mu'minin rendered the blood-money from the public treasury for the woman who died at the door. The teacher adds a candid aside: the questioner who raised this whole difficulty was assured by Sayyidna that every such question is already answered in the books; it is only because we have not read them that the answers do not reach us and do not encompass us. With the matter resolved, the section on retribution closes upon the recitation of `Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm`.

### The Numerical Structure of the Word of Unity

The discourse now turns from the law of recompense to the inner architecture of the word of confession itself — `lā ilāha illallāh`, "there is no god but Allah" — and to the way its very letters and their numerical values encode the deepest realities. The word is examined under the categories of the negative and the positive, of letters that bear a dot (*nuqta*) and letters that bear none, and of the numbers three, four, seven, and twelve that emerge from its composition.

The dotted letters in question are identified: the `yā`, which carries dots; and the contrast between `ʿayn` and `ghayn`, of which one is dotted and one bare. A fine master, the teacher recalls, composed a verse upon the open mouth of the letter `ʿayn`:

`Wajh-i is ʿayn ki munh khulne ki tujh se keh dun, kufr-i kafir ko yeh chahta hai ke ek dam niglun` — "Let me tell you the reason for this `ʿayn`'s open mouth: it desires to swallow the disbelief of the disbeliever in a single gulp." The open shape of the `ʿayn` is read as a mouth poised to devour unbelief whole.

The numerical reckoning is then unfolded. The three are the three letters `ʿayn`, `lām`, `yā`. The four arise from `lām` and `yā` together: `lām` has the value thirty and `yā` the value ten, which sum to forty. The seven belongs to the `ʿayn` alone, whose value is seventy. And the twelve arises from a composition of the letters' own constituent letters — the `yā` and `nūn` of the spelled-out `ʿayn`, the `alif` and `mīm` of the spelled-out `lām`, and the `alif` of the spelled-out `yā`: five elements, which together with the seven yield twelve. So the single confession, read through its letters and their values, opens into the structured numbers three, forty, seventy, and twelve, combined in several ways.

### The Seventy Special Characteristics Gathered in Ali

The mention of the seventy associated with the `ʿayn` carries the discourse to a teaching preserved in the *majalis* — the assemblies of the masters, of which the teacher notes that the collection in hand begins at the seventy-sixth and seventy-seventh assembly, the earlier ones being sought from Yemen for publication. In one of these assemblies it is related that in the person of Janab-i Ali there were gathered seventy special characteristics (*khususiyat*) such as could not be combined in any other human being.

A special characteristic, in the precise sense intended, is a distinction to which both Allah and His Messenger have borne testimony; only such a witnessed distinction counts as a *khususiyya*. When the men of knowledge surveyed and tallied these characteristics, they found that in no single person could more than ten of them be combined — one man might possess two, another three, but ten was the utmost any one person could gather. In Ali, however, seventy were combined together. The matter was enumerated, and examples of these characteristics were given in proof.

Among the examples adduced is a celebrated sermon. People had come to Ali, and by chance they had been disputing among themselves which letter occurs most frequently in the Arabic language. All agreed it was the *alif*; and from this they concluded that no oration could be composed without the *alif*. Then the Master of Creation arrived and delivered, on the spot, a sermon of roughly three pages in which the letter *alif* does not occur even once. This was done extempore — and the teacher fixes the term: extempore is *fil-badih*, which in the Arabic of the science is called *murtajjal*, the unpremeditated improvisation of a finished oration. The sermon is recorded in the foremost book, and likewise in the *Nahj al-Balagha*, and in the present book as well. And there is a second of its kind: a sermon of roughly thirteen pages, also delivered extempore, in which not a single dotted letter appears.

To these is joined the testimony of Umar, who said, `Lawlā ʿAlī lahalaka ʿUmar` — "Were it not for Ali, Umar would have perished." When the occasions of this saying were compiled, it was found that he uttered it seventy times. Thus the number seventy, which the letter `ʿayn` carries in the word of unity, finds its echo in the seventy characteristics gathered in the bearer of that name and in the seventy confessions of his indispensability upon the tongue of Umar.

### The Reward That Returns to the Luminous Structures While the Retribution Remains

The discourse now opens the third section of the fourth chapter of the fourth pavilion, which returns to the actions of the Imams and the *hudud* with a crucial addition: that even as the debt of *qisas* stands, a great reward for those same actions accrues to them. The Arabic states it: `inna afʿāl al-maqāmāt wa-awliyāʾihim ʿalayhim al-salām allatī faʿalūhā min mujāhadat al-azdād wa-qatli man qutila minhum wa-ḥaddi man ḥaddawhu min al-umam wa-ḥasbi man wajaba ʿalayhim al-ḥasab... ʿāʾidun ajruhu ʿalā hayākilihim al-nūrāniyya wa-ʿalā nufūsihim` — indeed the actions of the Imams and their friends, peace be upon them, which they performed — of striving against the opposites (*azdad*), and killing of them whom they killed, and applying the *hadd* upon those of the communities upon whom they applied it, and stoning those upon whom stoning became obligatory — the reward thereof returns to their luminous structures (*hayakil nuraniyya*) and to their souls, and to the souls of those whom they sent forward for that from among their friends, polishing their essences (*mujawhirin li-dhawatihim*), elevating their degrees and their ranks in the Abode of Purity (*dar al-safa*) and the elevated dwelling.

The teaching to be grasped is twofold and held in a fine balance: the *qisas* remains fixed in its place, and yet the reward is also given. For acting upon the command of Allah, the command of the Messenger, and the command of those in authority over them (*Uli'l-amr*) — for the slayings they performed, the striving they undertook, the *hudud* they applied, the stonings they carried out at command — a reward is earned that does not lapse. The teacher recalls the wisdom of the command that, when an offender is to be put to death for fornication, a company of Muslims must be present to witness it: this was enjoined so that, seeing the punishment, no person in the future would commit the like. The one who carries out the killing kills, but he does not kill out of personal enmity (*tarad*); he kills by command — by the command of Allah, who created the man and who is the giver of his life. Both realities therefore stand together: the reward of the deed returns to him, and the retribution of the deed remains upon him. How delicate, the teacher marvels, is this relation of *qisas* — that the very same act, performed in obedience to the sacred law, is at once a meritorious deed whose reward returns (`ʿāʾidun ajruhu`) and a deed whose *qisas* is yet owed, whether to the people of that age or, in time to come, by a fresh reckoning before the *Mudabbir*, the Governor. The reward is immense precisely because the agent is executing a divine command and striving to purge impurity from the world; and the grammatical pronoun in `ʿāʾidun ajruhu` returns, the teacher is careful to specify, to the reward of the one who performed the deed — that is, to the Imam — its merit returning to his luminous structures and to the souls of the friends he sent forward in the work.

The principle that killing is no good thing in itself, and yet may be rewarded, is then made explicit: `li-anna tilka al-afʿāl allatī faʿalūhā ṭalaban li-thawāb Allāh Taʿālā wa-amrihi bi-dhālika tabāraka wa-taʿālā wa-iḥlāl al-sharīʿa lahu lam yakun ajruhu bi-ḍāʾiʿin bal ʿalayhi al-qiṣāṣ al-awfā` — because those deeds which they performed, seeking the reward of Allah, exalted is He, and by His command for that, blessed and exalted, and to establish the shari'a — their reward is not wasted; rather, upon them is the fullest requital (*al-qisas al-awfa*). To take a life is not in itself a good; yet when it is done in self-defense (*dafa'-i hifazat*), in the protection of one's own life — when a man comes to kill you and you kill him in defense of your soul — or when it is done by the command of Allah and His Messenger or those in authority, then its reward stands. And its *qisas* stands also. The two are not at war; the seeking of God's reward in the act preserves the reward from being lost, while the fullest requital is exacted all the same.

### Upon Whose Body the Retribution Falls, and Why

The text now specifies upon what, in the case of the opposites, the retribution falls: `wa-kāna qiṣāṣuhum ʿalā kathāʾifihim salām Allāh ʿalayhim bi-amr Allāh Taʿālā bihi wa-iḥlāl al-sharīʿa lahu li-karāhiyat nufūs al-azdād bimā fuʿila bihā wa-qillat raḍāʾihim bihi` — and their retribution falls upon their dense bodies (*katha'if*), by the command of Allah, exalted is He, and the shari'a's making it lawful for them, on account of the aversion (*karahiya*) of the souls of the opposites to what was done to them and the scarcity of their pleasure in it. The *qisas* settles upon the dense bodies of the opposites — upon the gross forms in which their progress has been arrested — and it does so by Allah's command and the shari'a's sanction. The ground assigned for this is the aversion of the opposites' very souls to what was done to them: they did not suffer it with consent or pleasure. They were attacked, they were driven out, an assault was made upon them — and so what is done in legitimate defense (*mudafa'at*) stands on one footing, while what is done by those who themselves muster an army and unleash bloodshed and strife stands on quite another. The distinction is between the one who repels aggression and the one who initiates it; the retribution upon the dense bodies of the opposites is bound up with the involuntary, unwilling character of what they endured.

### The Question of Inheritance and the Order of Death

The text grounds even this in the universality of the scale: `li-anna mīzān al-ʿadl qāʾimun min Allāh Taʿālā fī al-qiṭmīri wa al-naqīr` — because the scale of justice is established from Allah, exalted is He, even in the *qitmir* and the *naqir*. The *qitmir* is the thin membrane upon the date-stone; the *naqir* is the small groove or hollow in the stone. The scale's reach extends to the most minute matters imaginable, down to the membrane and the groove of a single date-stone — there is no recess of creation, however small, in which justice is not erected and operative.

It is in connection with the incident of the trampled woman, narrated above, that the discourse now unfolds the law of inheritance, for it was to settle the inheritance that Amir al-Mu'minin had asked whether the mother or the child died first. The first principle is that the law of inheritance does not arise during a person's lifetime; what a person gives while living is a gift (*hiba*), not an inheritance, and the question of inheritance arises only at the moment of death. Connected to this is the ruling on a gift made in terminal illness (*marad al-mawt*): such a gift has no legal consideration. Whatever a person wishes to give as a gift he must give before the onset of his final illness; a gift made during *marad al-mawt* is not given effect. And even a valid *hiba* carries a strict condition: possession (*qabd*) must actually be transferred to the recipient. If possession is not given — if the thing remains in the donor's own hand and he has merely written that it is a gift — then the gift is not accepted in law.

The order of heirs is then set out. The first heirs of a person are his parents, if they are living. Each parent receives a one-sixth share (*sudus*) — the mother a sixth, the father a sixth. After the parents come the husband and the wife, each with a right in the other's estate. The wife's share has two aspects: an eighth part (*thumun*) where there are children, and a fourth part (*rub'*) where there are none. The husband's share, correspondingly, is a fourth where there are children and a half — the larger portion — where there are none, the shares being adjusted according to the presence or absence of offspring.

Upon the wife's eighth-share the teacher recalls the famous retort of Janab Abdullah ibn Abbas: `Laka al-thumunu min al-ʿushri wa li-l-kulli tamallakti` — "Yours is an eighth of a tenth, yet of the whole you have taken possession." The word *thumun* means an eighth part. The occasion was the dispute over the burial of Imam Hasan, upon him be peace, beside the Messenger of Allah, when the Banu Umayya, at the instigation and command of Hakam ibn al-As, shot arrows to prevent the burial and declared that they would not permit Imam Hasan to be laid to rest beside the Prophet. To those who claimed the Prophet's house as their own, Ibn Abbas replied that the Prophet had at his passing ten wives, and the share of all ten wives together in the house was but a *thumun*, an eighth — "yours is an eighth of a tenth" — while the remainder was the right of Janab-i Fatima, passing to the children; "yet of the whole you have taken possession," he charged, "you have seized it by force."

The wise point that Mawla Ali drew from the order of death in inheritance is then unfolded in full, for it shows how everything turns upon the sequence. Consider the mother and her child. If the mother dies first, the child becomes her heir — and then, when the question turns to the child's own heirs, his first heirs are his parents. But the mother has already died during the child's lifetime, so she takes nothing of the child's estate; and the father, if he is present, inherits, though it is not always known whether the father survives. After the parents, the child's heirs are his brothers and sisters — and if the child had no children of his own and no wife, the whole of his estate passes to his brothers and sisters, and then to the paternal uncle and the paternal uncle's children. Reverse the order, and the result is wholly different: if the mother is living and the child dies first, the child takes nothing from the mother, and the mother's own relatives inherit — the mother's father and mother, and her husband if she has one, and her other children if she has any. So great a difference is wrought by the mere sequence of the two deaths; and it was precisely to determine this that Amir al-Mu'minin asked, in the case of the trampled woman, which of the two — mother or child — had died first.

### The Imams' Actions as Retribution for Past Cycles

A further question is now posed in the text: `wa-li-qāʾilin an yaqūl, fa-qad ʿalimnā anna kathīran min al-maqāmāt wa al-ḥudūd ʿalayhim al-salām jāhadū al-azdād wa-qatalū minhum man qatalū wa-ḥaddū man ḥaddū wa-ḥasabū man ḥasabū thumma intaqalū ilā dār karāmat Allāh Taʿālā min ghayri qatlin wa-lā sammin` — and a sayer may say: we have known that many of the Imams and the *hudud*, peace be upon them, strove against the opposites, killed of them whom they killed, applied the *hadd* upon whom they applied it, and stoned whom they stoned — and then passed away to the abode of Allah's generosity without being killed and without poison. If retribution attaches to such actions, why did these die peacefully, in their beds, untouched by the violent death their deeds seemed to require?

The answer is given: `fa-l-yaʿlam anna afʿāl tilka ṣalawāt Allāh ʿalayhim fī azdādihim wa-ʿalā adwārihim ikhtiṣāṣun minhum bi-ḥasbi mā taqaddama min afʿālihim fīhim fī adwār salafat wa-ʿuṣūr taqaddamat` — so let him know that the actions of those, the blessings of Allah be upon them, against their opposites and within their cycles, are a taking of retribution from those opposites according to what had preceded of the opposites' own actions against them in cycles that had passed and ages gone by. The Imams' actions against their opposites in the present cycle are themselves the exacting of *qisas* for deeds the opposites had committed in earlier cycles and former times. The reckoning runs backward across the ages: the opposites are now being requited for what they did long before.

The teacher pauses over the word *qisas* itself, tracing it to its root `qaṣṣa` — *qaf*, *sad*, *sad* — from which arise *iqtisas*, the taking of retribution; the verb *aqassa*, "he took retribution"; and, through the form *bab mufa'ala*, *muqasasa*, which denotes the mutual, reciprocal taking of retribution between two parties. The seventeen augmented forms (*abwab-i mazid*) of the Arabic verb each shift the meaning, turning a simple taking into a giving, a mutual exchange, a reciprocity; and *qisas*, in its fullest sense, is just such a mutual settling of accounts.

The reason this reckoning is gathered into the Imams' time is then stated: `fa-awjabat ʿināyat al-ilāhiyya ijtimāʿahum fī zamān hādhihi al-maqāmāt wa-al-ḥudūd li-yakūna minhum lahum al-qiṣāṣ ʿammā salafa ʿadlan min Allāh Taʿālā wa-iqāmatan li-mā faʿalūhu min al-maʿāṣī` — so the divine providence (*'inaya ilahiyya*) necessitated their gathering together in the time of these Imams and *hudud*, so that there might be exacted from them, for them, the retribution for what had passed — as justice from Allah, exalted is He, and as a requital establishing the account for the sins they had committed. The teacher notes the precise sense of *ijtima'*, gathering: from the root that gives *ajma'a*, "he gathered," and *ijma'*, the consensus, and the passive *ijtumi'a*, "they were gathered." Divine providence so arranges matters that the opposites of every age are gathered together into the very time of the Imams against whom their past offenses were committed, so that the long-deferred retribution may be exacted from them there — a perfect justice from Allah, and an establishment of the reckoning for the sins of cycles past.

This is sealed by a verse of the Holy Quran: `wa-subḥāna man lā yaʿzubu ʿanhu mithqālu dharratin illā aḥṣāhā, wa-wajadū mā ʿamilū ḥāḍiran, wa-lā yaẓlimu rabbuka aḥadā` — and glory be to Him from whom not an atom's weight is hidden but that He has enumerated it; and they found present what they had done; and your Lord wrongs no one. Not the weight of a mote escapes His enumeration; on the day of reckoning, men find laid before them, present and undeniable, the very deeds they performed; and in all of it the Lord wrongs no one, for the whole is exact justice.

### The Grammar of Ali and the Ranks of the Soul

The closing words of that verse furnish the occasion for one of the section's most luminous teachings, drawn again from Mawla Amir al-Mu'minin. The verse ends `wa-lā yaẓlimu rabbuka aḥadā` — "and your Lord wrongs no one" — and the teacher asks why *ahad* here takes the accusative form *ahada*, the *zabar*, while *rabbuka* takes the nominative, the *pesh*. The answer lies in a grammatical maxim that Mawla Ali himself composed, a maxim that is at once a rule of Arabic syntax and a parable of the soul's ascent.

`Kullu fāʿilin marfūʿun` — every doer (*fa'il*) is in the nominative case (*marfu'*), raised and elevated. In the verse, *rabbuka*, "your Lord," is the doer, the one who does not wrong; and being the doer, He is raised. `Kullu mafʿūlin manṣūbun` — every object (*maf'ul*) is in the accusative case (*mansub*); and *ahada*, the one wronged, is the object, and so it is given the accusative *nasb*, the *zabar*. `Wa-kullu majrūrin maksūrun` — and every object of a preposition (*majrur*), every word drawn along by a particle of pulling, is in the genitive case (*maksur*), broken, bearing the *kasra*.

The esoteric reading of this triple maxim is the heart of the teaching. *Kullu fa'ilin marfu'un* — the one who moves forward, who acts, who is the very first to act, is the one who is elevated and raised up. *Kullu maf'ulin mansubun* — the one who, on seeing the doer, strives to become like him, who imitates and follows the one who acts, is the object: he is *mansub*, supported, given a support upon him. *Wa-kullu majrurin maksurun* — the one who must be pulled, for whose sake effort must be expended to draw him toward the good, is the one who is *maksur*, broken, given a break. Thus the three cases of the grammarian become three ranks of souls: the one who acts of himself and is raised; the one who imitates the actor and is supported; and the one who must be dragged toward the good and is broken in the drawing. So the verse, read through Ali's grammar, both declares that the Lord wrongs no one and pictures the hierarchy of souls in their ascent. The lecture closes upon the verse once more — `wa-wajadū mā ʿamilū ḥāḍiran`, they found present what they had done — and upon the recitation of the Fatiha.

### Passing the Hand Over the Face and the Five Facing Five

The discourse opens the fourth section of the fourth chapter of the fourth path with a question of practice that carries the deepest cosmology: why do we pass our hand over our face when we recite `Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm`? The answer is found in the doctrine of connection (*ittisal*).

In the human body there are five higher limbs (*a'la*) and five lower limbs (*adna*). Among the lower, the right hand is the highest of all; and with this, the highest of the lower limbs, we make a connection (*ittisal*) with the highest part of the upper body — the face. Passing the right hand over the face joins the loftiest of the lower five to the loftiest region of the upper five, and so unites the body's two pentads into one. From this bodily gesture a meaning is drawn that reaches into the unseen: just as the five lower limbs are joined to the five higher in the body, so the five luminous essences (*zawat*) of the Luminous Realm (*'alam-e-nurani*) are perpetually joined to the five essences of the world below. The five of the Luminous Realm, named in the Quran's symbolism, are *'aql* (Intellect), *nafs* (Soul), *jadd* (Fortune), *fatah* (Opening), and *khayal* (Imagination); the five of this world are the Imam, the Bab, the Hujjat, the Da'i, and the Ma'dhun. The passing of the hand over the face at the Bismillah is a sign and enactment of the unbroken connection between these two ranks of five.

But why, the questioner presses, do we make this gesture only at the Bismillah and not at every utterance? The answer leads to a comparison of the two highest words. The word `Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm` is the highest of summaries, the very essence of all that is gathered into a brief formula. Yet higher still than the Bismillah is a word — `lā ilāha illallāh`, "there is no god but Allah," which is wholly without dots in the deepest sense intended. This word comprises twenty-eight letters, of which fifteen are dotted and thirteen are undotted; the dotted ones, when counted out, are `bā`, `tā`, `thā`, `jīm`, `khā`, `dhāl`, `zā`, `shīn`, `ḍād`, `ẓā`, `ghayn`, `fā`, `qāf`, `nūn`, and `yā` — fifteen in all, leaving thirteen without dots.

Within the Bismillah itself a further structure is disclosed: `khamsatun bi-izāʾi khamsah` — five set facing five. Five repeated letters stand in correspondence with five non-repeated letters, and the non-repeated are the more excellent. The non-repeated letters of the Bismillah — the *bā* of *Bism*, the *sīn* of *Bism*, the *hā* of *Allāh*, and the *nūn* of *al-Raḥmān*, with the *yā* of *al-Raḥīm* — each occurs but once, a single letter of distinction in its word, while the *alif*, the *lām*, the *mīm*, and the *nūn* recur many times across the formula. The five non-repeated letters correspond to the five individuals of the Luminous Realm — *'aql*, *nafs*, *jadd*, *fatah*, *khayal* — those who do not need to make an appearance (*zuhur*) in the world; and the five repeated letters correspond to the five who must appear in the world again and again. And just as in the Bismillah the repeated and the non-repeated letters are joined together to make one single word, one *kalima*, so the five of the Luminous Realm and the five of the physical realm are perpetually connected and form one single *kalima*.

The teaching is then gathered into a threefold correspondence. Just as in `Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm` five repeated and five non-repeated letters combine into one word; and just as in the human body five higher and five lower limbs combine into one body; so in the Spiritual Realm (*'alam-e-ruhani*) the luminous and the worldly individuals combine — all of them becoming one in that realm, one single *kalima*. The word of the Bismillah, the human body, and the Spiritual Realm thus stand in exact correspondence, three mirrors of a single pattern. It is for this reason, the teacher notes, that the esoteric interpretation (*ta'wil*) we possess begins with `lā ilāha illallāh` and only thereafter takes up `Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm` — as in the treatise of Sayyidna Mu'ayyad, whose *ta'wil* opens with the word of unity, because everything is contained and present within it.

### La ilaha illallah as the Light of the Word

This brings the discourse to the supreme origin of the revealed word. `Lā ilāha illallāh`, the teacher affirms, is from God Almighty Himself; it is the light of the word (*nur-e-kalima*). The Bismillah, by contrast, and all the verses of the noble Quran, descended from the Circle of the First Intellect (*da'irat 'aql-e-awwal*), the Circle of Origination (*da'irat ibda'*); from there they were revealed. But the entire source and wellspring of all of them is `lā ilāha illallāh`. The word of unity is not one revealed verse among others; it is the light from which the Bismillah and every Quranic verse take their descent, the first principle to which the whole of revelation traces back.

### Deeds Unsanctioned by Law That Darken the Soul

The fifth movement of the discourse returns from the heights of the word of unity to the dark consequence of unlawful action, and here the doctrine of the descending forms is unfolded. The text states: `wa-ammā sāʾir al-ashyāʾ allatī bihi tafʿal min ghayri an tuḥillahā al-sharīʿah, aw yaʾmura bihā aḥad al-maqāmāt aw baʿḍ ḥudūdihi, wa-mithla qatli mayit al-insān min abnāʾi jinsihi, wa-nahbi man yanhubuhu, wa-ghaḍbi man yaghḍibuhu, wa-akhdhi man yaʾkhudhuhu ẓulman, wa-yabtaghu ghashman, fa-inna jawhara nafsihi yuẓlimu muʿajjalan` — as for all the other things that a person does which the shari'a has not made lawful, nor has one of the Imams or any of his *hudud* commanded — such as the killing of a human being from among his own kind, and the looting (*nahb*) of whomever he loots, and the usurping (*ghadb*) of whomever he usurps, and the taking by oppression (*zulm*) of whatever he takes, and the seeking of gain through treachery (*ghashm*) — then the essence of his soul (*jawhar al-nafs*) is darkened swiftly.

These are deeds that no command of the Imam sanctioned and no ruling of the shari'a made lawful: a man kills one of his own kind, or plunders, or seizes by force, or takes by oppression, or wins by treachery. When a person commits such an act — neither obeying a station of authority nor following the law — the essence of his soul grows dark, and grows dark quickly. And the text adds: `wa-yakūnu tilka al-afʿāl ḥājizatan bayna nafsihi wa-bayna sabīl al-rushd` — and those actions become a barrier between his soul and the path of guidance (*sabil al-rushd*). The unlawful deed does not merely stain the soul; it erects a wall between the soul and the road of right guidance, so that the man no longer finds the opportunity to come to the path. The barrier shuts him out.

A question is raised here: when the Imam imposes a prescribed punishment (*hadd*) upon a man — for instance, putting him to death for a killing — is the man's entire sin thereby erased, or does some portion of it remain? The answer is that this depends upon the retribution of the *Mudabbir*, the Governor, and upon the states of the cycles (*adwar*) and the returns (*karrat*) — in which return, and in what manner, the man committed his deed. For in a single great cycle each Imam has three hundred and sixty returns (*karrat*); and what a man did in each return, and how his account stands with the *Mudabbir*, determines whether the sin is wholly erased or not. It may be erased; it may not. The matter is too fine to be settled by the outward *hadd* alone.

And here the saying of the Prophet, upon him be peace, is brought to bear: `mā aṣarra man istaghfara, wa-law kāna sabʿīna marrah` — he has not persisted in sin who seeks forgiveness (*istighfar*), even were it seventy times. The one who, after sinning, turns and seeks forgiveness is not counted among those who persist in sin; even if his lapse and his repentance recur seventy times, he is not of the persistent. The number seventy is named, the teacher explains, because seventy marks the limit, the point at which the matter is gathered up and included among the seventy names. Yet a crucial qualification is restated: this forgiveness through *istighfar* avails for sins incurred toward Allah, not for sins incurred toward His servants. As many sins as a person commits in relation to Allah, He forgives them upon the seeking of forgiveness; but the wrongs done to His servants Allah does not forgive until that servant forgives, or until such means are arranged that the expiation reaches the wronged party — as by the method of *Bitaq*, by which Allah causes the recompense to reach whomever it must reach, so that the sinner's account may be cleansed.

### The Soul Driven Into the Paths of Error

The text now describes, in a chain of vivid participles, the full ruin that the darkening of the soul's essence works upon it: `mukassifatun lahā, sāʾiqatun lahā ilā ṭuruq al-ghawāya, mūriṭatun lahā fī arbāq al-maʿāṣī, mūbiqatun lahā fī qabāʾiḥ al-aʿmāl, dāʿiyatun lahā ilā manāfir al-ḥudūd, wa-muqṣiyatun lahā ʿan al-ḥaqq, muwaqqiʿatun lahā fī nāri jahannam, allatī lā tarjiʿu lahā raḥmatun, mukhalladatun bi-ʿadhābin alīm` — those deeds eclipse the soul and drive it into the paths of error (*turuq al-ghawaya*); they ensnare it in the snares of sins; they destroy it in the foulnesses of evil deeds; they call it toward the hatred of the *hudud*; they thrust it far from the truth; they cast it down into the fire of Hell, for which no mercy returns, abiding there in a painful torment. The darkened soul is hurried along the roads of error, caught in the nets of sin, made to loathe the divine limits and the people of truth, driven from the truth, and flung at last into the fire of Jahannam.

Yet at the very edge of this terror the discourse marks a hidden mercy, drawn from the precise wording of the Quran. Of the disbelievers it is said: `lam yakuni alladhīna kafarū min ahl al-kitābi wa-al-mushrikīna fī nāri jahannama khālidīna fīhā` — those who disbelieved among the People of the Book and the polytheists are in the fire of Hell, abiding therein — `ulāʾika hum sharr al-bariyya`, those are the worst of creatures. The word *khalidina*, abiding, is used of them — but the word *abada*, "forever," is not. Whereas of the believers it is said: `wa-alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti... ulāʾika hum khayr al-bariyya`, and those who believed and did righteous deeds — those are the best of creatures — `jazāʾuhum ʿinda rabbihim jannātu ʿadnin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru khālidīna fīhā abadā`, their reward with their Lord is gardens of perpetual residence beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein forever. For the righteous, paradise and within it eternity (*abada*); for the disbelievers, the fire — but without the word "forever." This withholding of *abada* from the abode of the fire, while it is granted to the gardens of the blessed, is a mercy from Allah, a token that the abiding of the damned does not bear the same seal of unending permanence as the eternity of the saved.

The passage closes upon the affirmation that the darkened soul has no escape from the reckoning: `wa-lā manāṣa lahā baʿda dhālika min qiṣāṣin ʿalayhā wa-lā ʿalā jismihā jamīʿan` — and there is no escape for that soul, after all this, from the retribution upon it and upon its body entirely. The teacher seals the section with a prayer: may Allah protect us and deliver us from the paths of destruction, by the right of Sayyidna Muhammad and his pure progeny, the blessings of Allah be upon them all.

### The Descending Garments of the Soul

The fifth and final chapter of the fourth section turns to the precise mechanism by which the retribution for grave human sins is exacted — the doctrine of the descending garments or forms (*qumus*) through which the unredeemed soul passes. The text opens: `wa-kadhālika afʿāluhā al-qabīḥa mithla al-zinā, wa-mā shākalahu, fa-lā budda min al-qiṣāṣi ʿalayhi maʿa ẓulam jawhar al-nafs wa-takassufihā wa-inhidārihā ʿalā mā sabaqa min al-qawl` — and likewise its vile acts, such as adultery (*zina*) and what resembles it, then there is no escape from the retribution upon it (*la budda min al-qisas*), together with the darkening of the soul's essence, and its eclipse (*takassuf*), and its descent (*inhidar*), as was stated before. The phrase *la budda* the teacher glosses with care: *budda* means a way out, an escape; *la budda* means there is no way out — the retribution will surely come, inescapably. For vile acts such as adultery, *qisas* is unavoidable, and it is accompanied by the darkening of the soul's essence, its eclipse, and its downward descent through the forms.

The descent begins in the human form itself: `ḥattā yakūnu al-qiṣāṣu muʿajjalan fī Qamīs al-Riss alladhī huwa qamīs al-bashar al-munhadira` — to the extent that the retribution is hastened in the Garment of the Riss (*Qamis al-Riss*), which is the garment of the descending human. The descending forms are named in sequence — Riss, then Waqs, then 'Aks, then Khars — and the first, the Qamis al-Riss, is the human form, the descending human shape; in this very first stage much of the *qisas* for major human sins (*kabair*) is completed. Beyond the human form lie the further stages: the second is the form resembling the animal, the monkey-like; then a tearing, sea-dwelling form; then the plant; then, when the soul is stripped away, the mineral — for the soul must pass through seven stages in all in its descent.

The text continues: `wa-rubbamā baqiya minhu shayʾun fī Qamīs al-Waqs, wa-huwa al-tarkīb al-māthil lil-bashar alladhī huwa al-qirdu wa-al-nasnāsu wa-al-adaru wa-al-ʿadabu wa-ashbāhu dhālik` — and sometimes a portion of it remains for the Garment of the Waqs (*Qamis al-Waqs*), which is the composition resembling the human — namely the monkey (*qird*), the *nasnas*, the *adar*, the *'adab*, and their like. The remaining retribution for adultery passes into the Qamis al-Waqs, the human-like animal composition, because of a particular reason given below. The teacher describes the *nasnas* — not the bear but a manlike species — as `nasnās jinsun min al-khalq fī ʿarabi al-shimāl`, a species of creation in the regions of the north; `lahum khalqun mughayyar`, they have an altered form; `yuqālu inna wujūhahum fī ṣudūrihim`, it is said that their faces are upon their chests; and `wa-yuqālu inna al-wāḥida minhum yuthbatu ʿalā rijlin wāḥidah`, it is said that one of them stands upon a single leg. And in the state of copulation in which such creatures are, the retributions for adultery are exacted upon them.

The text proceeds: `thumma fī Qamīs al-ʿAks, wa-ʿalā qadri kathrati dhālika al-fiʿl min qillatihi` — then in the Garment of the 'Aks, and according to the measure of the abundance or scarcity of that act. The recompense a soul receives in these descending realms is proportioned to how much of the deed it did by its own choice: the more adultery a man committed, the more retribution he receives; the less he did, the less. The particle *min* here is one of comparison, weighing abundance against scarcity.

### The Distribution of Retribution Across the Forms

A further refinement is given for the case in which one woman is sinned with by many men: `li-annahu rubbamā yaʿbathu bil-marʾati qawmun kathīrun, fa-yakūnu al-qiṣāṣu minhum mutafarriqan fī Qumus al-inhidār` — because sometimes a large group of men has intercourse with a single woman, so that the retribution from them is distributed across the garments of descent (*qumus al-inhidar*). The *qisas* is not gathered into a single stage but scattered through several: `minhā shayʾun fī Qumus al-Riss, wa-shayʾun fī Qumus al-Waqs, wa-aktharu dhālika yakūnu fī Qumus al-Waqs` — some of it in the garments of the Riss, and some in the garments of the Waqs, and most of it in the garments of the Waqs.

The reason most of the retribution falls in the Waqs, the monkey-like form, is stated: `li-anna al-munākaḥah bil-qurūd wa-ṣifātihā aktharu min al-munākaḥah bi-sāʾir al-ḥayawān` — because copulation among monkeys, and its qualities, is greater than copulation among all other animals. The lustful quality is more abundant in them than in other beasts; and so the surplus of a much-repeated human sin descends into the form whose own nature most answers to it. The text continues: `wa-kadhālika fa-inna munākaḥat ahl Qumus al-Riss aktharu min munākaḥat ahl al-Qumus al-Sawiyya, allatī hiya fī al-ḥaqīqati qumus al-bashariyya` — and likewise, the copulation of the people of the garments of the Riss is greater than the copulation of the people of the upright garments (*Qumus al-Sawiyya*), which are in reality the garments of humanity. Those who have already begun the descent into the Riss are more inclined to the act than those still in the upright human form; the descended state intensifies the very disposition that brought about the descent. And whatever retribution remained beyond this, `thumma mā baqiya min al-qiṣāṣi fī dhālika kāna fī Qumus al-ʿAks` — then what remained of the retribution in that regard was in the garments of the 'Aks — and likewise in the Khars (*al-khars*) and in all the descending animals. When the soul, in the human form, had the power of choice and performed such deeds, the effect of those deeds remains upon it through every one of the descending garments it enters.

### The Impermanence of the Garments and the Thousand-Year Stature

The teacher now sets all these descending forms against the one form that possesses a kind of permanence. These other forms, he explains, are temporary: a human soul enters into them, passes through the garments, and then returns; they are not its abiding state. Only the Thousand-Year Stature (*Qamat-e-Alfiyyah*) possesses a permanence — and even this is a contingent permanence, a blessing granted to every person for a limited time, after which the soul departs and returns to it, sometimes again, sometimes not, though return it must.

Why is this form called the Thousand-Year Stature, and in what sense is it primary? Because the luminous forms (*nurani zawat*) that were created in the unseen were created upon the pattern of the Qamat-e-Alfiyyah. The question is posed that at the time of their creation the Qamat-e-Alfiyyah did not yet exist — how, then, could they have been created upon it? The answer is that the luminous essences created in that realm were themselves upon the form of the Qamat-e-Alfiyyah, and that when Allah willed to create the Qamat-e-Alfiyyah in the world, He created it in accordance with that prior luminous form. So in truth the Qamat-e-Alfiyyah came first — first there in the realm of the luminous essences, and first here in the physical world as well. The degraded forms — the human, the animal, the mineral — are all a later matter, a subsequent thing. This whole world of density and atoms is the degraded world, and the descent through it proceeds by stripping away, one by one, the powers of the form: in the animal realm one faculty is taken; in the next, a second, a third, a fourth; in the plant stage only growth remains, sensation having been all but withdrawn; in the mineral, even that is finished; in the Khars, sensation is taken away. Stage by stage, the density and the atoms are removed and the powers stripped, until the form that was created whole upon the Thousand-Year Stature is reduced to the barest residue.

### Why Retribution Applies Despite Consent in the Descending Forms

A final and subtle difficulty is resolved. In the descending animal forms, the acts of copulation occur with consent and even with the payment of a price — so why should retribution still apply there? The text answers: `wa-innamā waqaʿa al-qiṣāṣu fī dhālika wa-al-ʿadhābu ʿalayhi maʿa mā annahu min marāḍāt wa-taslīm qīmatihi, li-kawnih waqaʿa bi-ghayr taḥlīl al-sharīʿa lahu, fa-kāna dhālika kadhālik` — and the retribution and the punishment occurred for that despite its being with mutual consent (*marada*) and with the delivery of its price (*taslim qima*), because it occurred without the shari'a's making it lawful; so the matter was thus.

The animals, the teacher explains, have consent in their unions — and the soul that has descended into them had, in its human life, performed the original act with consent and with payment, just as a man pays a prostitute and the act is done by mutual agreement. Yet because that original human act was performed without the shari'a's permission, the retribution and the punishment follow the soul into the descending forms regardless of the consent that attends the act there. The lawfulness that would have removed the debt was absent at the root; and so, exactly as in the human realm of choice the unsanctioned act incurred its penalty, in the animal realm the penalty is exacted still — though the form of the punishment becomes somewhat different in the descended state.

The chapter — and the section — closes with a final distinction concerning who descends into which order of lower states. The descending garments here described (the *darakat*) pertain to adultery and its likes — and the text is explicit that the doctrine of these descending forms has been expounded specifically with regard to *zina* and what resembles it: `al-zinā wa-mā shākalahu`, adultery and the acts similar to it, such as the usurping of another's wealth, theft, and robbery. The opponents of the Imam (*azdad*), however, do not descend into these descending garments (*darakat*) at all; they descend rather into the lower levels (*adrak*), a different and deeper order of degradation. Thus the *darakat* of the descending forms belong to the carnal and acquisitive sins of ordinary men, while the adversaries of the Imam are consigned to the *adrak* below them. With this the lecture closes upon the recitation of `Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm` and the prayer upon Muhammad and his progeny.

## The Farthest Mosque and the Ascent of the Faithful

### Three Lights Upon a Single Date: The Twenty-Seventh of Rajab

Among the dates recorded in the epistle, two are written plainly: the *Mab'ath al-Nabi*, the commencement of the Prophet's mission, and the *Wiladat 'Ali*, the birth of Mawla 'Ali. A third event, the *Laylat al-Isra'*, the Night of the Journey, is not entered there in the same way; the epistle gives only the first two, while the night of the ascension is something that "it is said" (`yuqalu lahu`) belongs to this same date, a tradition famous among the common people and reported in the discourses of the elders. When these three are gathered together, what emerges is a single date bearing three lights, and the relationship among them is precise and worth establishing with care.

The birth of 'Ali came first. Between his birth and the Prophet's mission lies a span of years that must be reckoned exactly. The Prophet, upon whom be blessing and peace, was himself born in the *'Am al-Fil*, the Year of the Elephant — the year of Abraha and his host, of which the Qur'an asks, `Alam tara kayfa fa'ala rabbuka bi-ashabi 'l-fil` (Have you not seen how your Lord dealt with the Companions of the Elephant?). 'Ali was born thirty years after the Prophet, in what the reckoning here calls the thirtieth year measured from that same starting point. And at the time the Prophet received prophethood, in the fortieth year of his life, 'Ali was ten years old — for the mission, the *bi'that*, and 'Ali's birth fell upon the very same calendar date, the twenty-seventh of Rajab, separated by ten years. Thus when 'Ali was ten, the Prophet was forty, and the day that had been the day of 'Ali's birth ten years earlier became, on its anniversary, the day of the Prophet's mission. Sayyidna Ja'far has stated that it was on the morning of the twenty-seventh of Rajab that the *bi'that* took place.

And then, by the same reasoning that fixes the date, the *Laylat al-Isra'* also fell upon the twenty-seventh of Rajab. There is in circulation another report, from Mashhad, that names the fifteenth of Sha'ban for the night of the journey, but that is a separate statement belonging to a different occasion; the epistle here does not mention the fifteenth of Sha'ban at all, and the tradition that the night of the ascension belongs to the twenty-seventh of Rajab is the one famous among the people and the one this exposition follows. So a single date carries three weights at once: 'Ali's birth thirty years before the Prophet's mission, the Prophet's mission when he was forty and 'Ali ten, and the Night of the Ascension. What a composition this is — three of the greatest events of the cycle drawn together upon one morning of one month.

Before turning to the night itself, a word on the disposition with which such a night is to be received. When the meanings of the night are present in a person's mind, he performs both the *zahir* and the *batin* — both the outward act of worship and the inward understanding of it. From this two forms are generated within him: a form of knowledge-in-action, the imprint that action leaves, and a form of knowledge itself. And what is the provision a person carries to the Hereafter? It is knowledge and action together. First the form of action is fashioned, and upon it the form of knowledge is built; the two together are the believer's store for the world to come. It is in this spirit — action joined to its meaning — that the night of the ascension is to be read.

### The Night Journey: What the Word *Isra'* Carries

The very name of the event instructs. The discourse here is drawn, as the text declares, from the *Asas al-Ta'wil* of Sayyidna al-Qadi al-Nu'man: `Dhikr wa-ta'wil laylat al-isra' allati qila laha laylat sab' wa-'ishrin min Rajab. Manqulun min kitab Asas al-Ta'wil` (The mention and interpretation of the Night of the Journey, said to be the twenty-seventh night of Rajab, quoted from the book Asas al-Ta'wil). The word *isra'* derives from the root *sara*, which means specifically to journey by night. *Sara* is "he travelled by night"; the form *isra'* is built on the causative pattern *if'al*, so that where *fa'ala* means "he did," *af'ala* means "he caused to do." Hence `asra bi-'abdihi` means "He caused His servant to journey by night." The Qur'an even reinforces the sense by adding *laylan*, "by night," in the verse `Subhana 'lladhi asra bi-'abdihi laylan` (Glory to Him who caused His servant to journey by night) — so the nocturnal character is named twice over, in the verb and in the explicit word. Even in common Urdu usage a trace of the root survives: the *sara'i*, the wayside inn where one spends the night, takes its name from this very meaning of passing the night in a place.

The discourse then sets before us the foundational saying that frames the whole night: `Qala Muhammadun salla Allahu 'alayhi, arqahu 'llahu 'azza wa-jalla ila 'ilm malakutihi wa-atla'ahu 'ala hudud samawatihi wa-'allamahu 'ilm ghaybihi wa-faddalahu bi-dhalika 'ala jami' anbiya'ihi wa-qarrabahu min al-hudud al-'ulwiyyah ladayhi` (It is said of Muhammad, may Allah bless him, that Allah the Almighty and Glorious raised him to the knowledge of His Kingdom, and informed him of the limits of His heavens, and taught him the knowledge of His unseen, and by that favored him over all His prophets, and brought him near to the celestial limits with Him). On this night, then, Allah raised the Prophet up into the knowledge of His *malakut*, His Kingdom; He made him aware of the *hudud al-samawat*, the limits of the heavens; He taught him the *'ilm al-ghayb*, the knowledge of the unseen; by all of this He gave him precedence over the entire body of the prophets; and He brought him near to the *al-hudud al-'ulwiyyah*, the celestial limits, that stand near Him — and this nearness, the text notes, was attained by potentiality, `bi 'l-quwwah`.

The great *du'at* have drawn from this a meaning concerning the number forty. The mission came in the fortieth year, and from this they understood that the Prophet had completed forty stations of prophethood, and only then was made a *rasul*, a messenger. From a *nabi* he became a *rasul*, and having become a messenger he gradually surpassed, one after another, all the rightful messengers before him — until, on the *Laylat al-Isra'*, his superiority over every prophet was established outright. This is the superiority of *risalat*, messengership, raised above all the rightful messengers. He kept ascending and ascending, station upon station, until he reached the very summit of the matter.

### Fifty Prayers Reduced to Five

It belongs to the account of this ascent that when the Prophet had risen through the heavens and reached the fourth heaven, Musa descended to meet him. Musa was *Kalim Allah*, the one who spoke with God, and he was given to speech; he asked, "O Prophet of God, what gift have you brought back from God?" The Prophet answered that God had made fifty prayers obligatory upon his community, and that this was the gift he carried. Musa replied with a counsel born of his own long experience of a people: "What have you done? You should have asked Allah to lighten the prayers, for I have seen that so long as no duties were prescribed upon a community they remained in one condition, but once duties were prescribed they grew neglectful, and with neglectfulness their sins multiplied. You should have petitioned Allah." So the Prophet returned, and one prayer was remitted, then a second, then a third, and so on as they were lightened, until only five remained. Still Musa urged him: "O Prophet of God, ask that these five also be forgiven, so that only the bare essentials remain." But the Prophet said, "I am ashamed before Allah — shall my community not perform even five prayers in gratitude to Allah?" `Istahya rasul Allah salawat Allah 'alayh` — the Messenger of God showed modesty before his Lord. And he declared the five prayers to be a gift from Allah in which there is a blessing for the community.

Who, then, was the more correct — the Prophet or Musa? The judgment given here is that the Prophet was the more correct in accepting the five. For the blessings that are obtained from prayer are very many: a person's mind finds peace through it, and the illuminations, the supports, and the encounters that come to one in prayer are far greater than whatever hardship a person bears in performing it. To have the burden lifted entirely would have been to forfeit those very benefits. The Prophet's modesty before God and his refusal to bargain the five away preserved for the community a treasury of blessing greater by far than the relief of fewer obligations.

### "Glory to Him Who Took His Servant by Night": The Transcendence of the Creator

The Qur'anic verse that names the journey is its own theology: `wa-dhalika qawluhu subhanahu alladhi asra bi-'abdihi laylan min al-masjid al-haram ila al-masjid al-aqsa alladhi barakna hawlahu li-nuriyahu min ayatina innahu huwa al-sami' al-basir` (And that is His saying — Glory to Him who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque whose precincts We have blessed, that We might show him of Our signs; indeed He is the All-Hearing, the All-Seeing). The discourse first establishes the plain sense and then guards it against a popular error. The journey was by night, as both the verb *asra* and the added word *laylan* confirm; the servant was taken from al-Masjid al-Haram to al-Masjid al-Aqsa, from the Sacred precinct to the Farthest, whose surroundings God has blessed.

The crucial clause is `li-nuriyahu min ayatina` — "that We might show him of Our signs." The common people say, "He saw God there." But Allah in His own glorious word does not say that He showed Himself; He says that He showed *of Our signs*, the *ayat*. God was not seen; God's signs were seen. And the verse itself answers the question of whether one should believe the people's report or believe Allah's own declaration. The journey was a showing of signs, not a vision of the Essence.

The word *subhan* at the head of the verse carries the whole burden of transcendence. As the discourse continues: `wa-qawluhu subhana 'lladhi asra bi-'abdihi laylan fa-subhan tanzih al-bari jalla dhikruhu 'an sifat khalqihi wa-simat bariyyatihi wa-'abdihi` (And His saying, "Glory to Him who took His servant by night" — for *subhan* is the declaration of the Creator's transcendence, may His mention be exalted, above the attributes of His creation and the marks of His creatures and His servant). To pronounce *subhan* is to declare the *tanzih* of the Maker — to lift Him utterly above the attributes that belong to created things. Whatever attributes creatures possess do not apply to Him in any degree. So far does this transcendence reach that even the attribute of being encompassed — of being grasped and comprehended — does not apply to Him. And here the discourse cites the luminous saying of Mawlana 'Ali: `al-'ajzu 'an dark al-idrak idrakun` (The inability to attain comprehension is itself comprehension). To become incapable of reaching the grasp of His comprehension is, in reality, the attainment of comprehension. The truth of the matter is this: we know Him only through our own existence; we recognize that there must be a Creator for us, and beyond that — what He is like, in what manner He is — we do not reach. Through seeing His favors and His bounties we understand that He exists; from the attributes of His creation and the marks of His creatures we are led to affirm Him, while He Himself remains beyond the attributes of all creation.

This same transcendence is what the *sura* of unity proclaims: `Qul huwa 'llahu ahad` (Say: He is Allah, the One). The command "Say" is addressed to the Prophet, and it is issued, through an intermediary, by the *Mubdi'*, the First Originator, who is the one commanding. Some have understood the pronoun *huwa*, "He," to indicate the *Mubdi' ta'ala* directly; others have said that *huwa* indicates the *Mubdi' al-awwal*, the First Originator, that what is meant by "Allah" is the essence of that First Originator, and that *ahad*, "One," gathers up all the ranks that come after. When one passes into the inner stages of this sura, into its ranks and its hidden meaning, the realm of unity is established and one becomes settled in the station of *huwa*. The Prophet is commanded: "Say — He is Allah, the One."

A question arises from the geography of the verse. The Prophet was taken from al-Masjid al-Haram in Mecca to al-Masjid al-Aqsa, identified with Bayt al-Muqaddas — yet the station of Bayt al-Muqaddas is higher than Mecca. Why, then, is the motion described as going from the higher place to the lower? The answer lies wholly in the *ta'wil*: the referents of al-Masjid al-Haram and al-Masjid al-Aqsa, and of the *Buraq* as well, are not the geographical sites but the spiritual ranks they signify, and these will be unfolded shortly. The journey "from high to low" in the outward map is, in its inner meaning, a journey of an entirely different order.

### "His Servant," Not "the Prophet": Servitude Before Messengership

The discourse fixes attention on a single chosen word in the verse: God did not say that He took the *nabi*, nor that He took the *rasul*; He said `asra bi-'abdihi` — He took His *'abd*, His servant. When the Prophet had attained a special distinction within servitude itself, then it was that He took His servant. This is no accident of phrasing. When the testimony of messengership is pronounced, the believer says, `Ashhadu anna Muhammadan 'abduhu wa-rasuluhu` (I bear witness that Muhammad is His servant and His messenger) — placing *'abduhu*, "His servant," before *rasuluhu*, "His messenger." Servitude is set above and before messengership, for it is the ground out of which messengership rises. So when God carried him on the night journey, He carried him precisely as *'abd*, the servant who had perfected his servitude, and it was that perfected servitude that qualified him for the journey.

And as for the corporeal reality of that travelling: the question is whether the Prophet, in a human state, was moved bodily from one place to another. The answer the discourse gives is layered. The Prophet, upon whom be blessing and peace, comprises several things at once: there is his veil, the body; there is the corporeal body; and there is the spiritual body. On that night each of these progressed — for the body and for every dimension of him, all the progressions took place together. The journey was not the displacement of a body alone, but the simultaneous elevation of every rank of his being, and it was accomplished by potentiality. He is the All-Hearing and the All-Seeing.

### *Layl* as the Veil: Travelling in the Inner World

Here the *ta'wil* turns inward. `Wa-asra bi-'abdihi laylan wa-al-sara al-sayr bi 'l-layl` (And He took His servant by night, and *al-sara* is travelling by night) — He took the servant, not the prophet nor the messenger, for the reason already given. And then: `Wa 'l-layl fi 'l-ta'wil mithal al-sitr wa 'l-kitman li 'l-batin` (And the night, in interpretation, is an example of the veil and the concealment of the inner dimension). The "night" of the verse is, esoterically, the *sitr* and the *kitman* — the veiling and the keeping-secret of the inner reality. The discourse continues: `ya'ni 'azza wa-jalla li-tarbiyatihi annahu akhfahu wa-sayyarahu fi 'l-'alam al-batin ma baynahu wa-bayna al-mutimm min dawrihi wa-huwa sadis al-a'immah` (meaning that Allah, Almighty and Glorious, concealed him and made him travel in the inner world, between himself and the Completer of his cycle, who is the sixth of the Imams).

So the meaning is that God concealed the Prophet — veiled him under the night of secrecy — and caused him to journey within the inner world, in the space between himself and the *mutimm*, the Completer of his era. And the *mutimm* of his cycle, the sixth of the Imams, is identified here as Janab Abu Talib. The journey "from Mecca to Bayt al-Muqaddas" reported in the tradition is, in this reading, the journey of the Prophet within the batin toward this Completer. The seventh after him is the one to whom all virtue culminates; the travel between the Prophet and this completing rank is the inner substance of the night.

A note on the very name of the destination is woven in here, recalling a teacher's grammatical correction. A scholar from Egypt, dark of complexion and given to wearing a red coat, who taught Arabic in the MA program, insisted on the precise construction: is *Bayt al-Muqaddas* an *idafi* (genitive) construction or a descriptive one? If one says `al-bayt al-muqaddas`, that is adjective-and-noun — "the house which has been sanctified." If one says `bayt al-maqdis`, that is the *idafi* construction — "the house of sanctity." The distinction is the same that governs the opening of the Qur'an: in `Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim`, `bismillah` ("the name of Allah") is an *idafi* construction, while `al-Rahman al-Rahim` ("the Compassionate, the Merciful") is descriptive, the attributes of Allah, which is why the *kasra* falls upon them. From beginning to end of the glorious Qur'an, two grammatical points keep the mind in order: which word is the noun, which the verb, which the particle; and whether a sentence is nominal or verbal — for if nominal, one looks for subject and predicate, and if verbal, for verb, subject, and object. Mawlana 'Ali himself laid down this very science: all words are of three kinds — noun, verb, and particle — and from them sentences are formed, and sentences are of two kinds, nominal and verbal. A nominal sentence must have subject and predicate; a verbal sentence must have verb and subject, with the object following as the sense requires. Whoever holds this in mind as he reads finds his mind trained of itself and reads the Arabic rightly. The clause `ja'a fi 'l-khabar` (it has come in the report), by which the tradition is introduced, is itself a verbal sentence — and what it reports is that He caused the Prophet to be taken from Mecca to Bayt al-Muqaddas.

The inner meaning is then made explicit: `wa-ma'nahu annahu arqahu fi 'l-'ilm ila ma baynahu wa-bayna mutimm dawrihi wa-jumi'a lahu min al-'ilm wa 'l-hikmah ma yakunu fihim wa-ma yajri 'alayhi umuruhum wa-kayfa takunu hududuhum 'ala ma urida min al-hudud al-'ulwiyyah allati ra'aha bi-nafsihi al-hissiyyah` (And its meaning is that He elevated him in knowledge to what is between him and the Completer of his cycle; and there was gathered for him of knowledge and wisdom what is in them, and what runs upon them of their affairs, and how their limits would be, according to what was intended of the celestial limits, which he saw with his own sensory self). The Prophet was raised in knowledge up to the level that lies between himself and the *mutimm* of his era; there was gathered for him the knowledge and wisdom contained in the hudud, the manner in which their affairs run, and the disposition of their ranks — the very system of the *al-hudud al-'ulwiyyah*, the celestial limits, which he beheld. Going there, the knowledge of the Kingdom was opened to him: how, in the *malakut*, in the whole universe, the plans of creation are executed. The inner knowledge of that administration was given to him, and it is a great knowledge indeed — for we are as frogs in a small well who imagine the well to be the whole universe, when in truth the universe is vast, and the Imam must carry out all its plans.

### Feeding the Indigent, the Orphan, and the Captive

To this knowledge of the universal administration the discourse attaches the interpretation of a famous verse: `wa-yut'imuna al-ta'ama 'ala hubbihi miskinan wa-yatiman wa-asiran` (And they feed, for the love of Him, the indigent, the orphan, and the captive). The outward occasion is the fast of the five — 'Ali, Fatima, Imam Hasan, Imam Husayn, and Fidda — who fasted and then, at the hour of breaking the fast, gave their bread to a *miskin* who came, then to a *yatim*, then to an *asir*. But the inner sense reaches into the very administration of the universe just spoken of: the Imam's call encompasses the whole cosmos, and the feeding of these three is the freeing of three classes of soul through the call of the *khamsat al-ashab*, the five companions.

The *miskin*, first, is not merely a pauper. The word has two senses: one is the destitute, but the other is the one from whom hearts find *sukun*, peace and rest. The *miskin* here is the *da'i*, the summoner, from whom people find peace of heart. He had come confined — he did not possess the right to give protection, the right of granting refuge — and by feeding him, that is, by the call of the five, he was freed from his confinement and the right to grant protection was bestowed upon him. Each of the *panjatan pak* rose in turn and gave his portion: 'Ali gave his piece of bread, then Fatima, then Imam Hasan, then Imam Husayn, then Fidda, and the two children also gave; and each one, by his own call, freed such confined souls. This was the deed.

The *yatim*, second, is literally one whose parents have died, but inwardly the *yatim* is the *durr-i yatim*, the unique orphan-pearl — one who has no equal. He was a person of very high rank who had for some reason been halted and held back; his "parents" — those who supported and upheld him — had been finished, so that he stood as a *yatim*, deprived of his backers. The command came for him as for a unique pearl, and his orphanhood was ended by his being fed: he was a high one whose supports had fallen away, and the call restored him.

The *asir*, third, is one who has been imprisoned, held in a place where the *'ulama al-su'*, the scholars of evil, held control — a place where he could neither benefit others nor be benefited, where he stood in a state of captivity. He was given permission, and by it he was freed from that captivity. So the three whom the five fed are the *da'i* in whom hearts find peace, the high-ranking one whose supports were finished, and the one imprisoned where evil scholars ruled — each set free by the call of the five companions. From what station in the universe each of these came is itself unfolded in the *ta'wil* that follows.

### "The Heart Did Not Lie" and "The Sight Did Not Swerve": A Vision of the Soul

The discourse turns to the verses of the *Najm* that describe what the Prophet saw: `wa-min dhalika qawluhu 'azza wa-jalla ma kadhaba al-fu'adu ma ra'a ila qawlihi ma zagha al-basaru wa-ma tagha laqad ra'a min ayati rabbihi al-kubra` (And from that is His saying — "The heart did not lie about what it saw," up to His saying, "The sight did not swerve, nor did it transgress; indeed he saw of the greatest signs of his Lord"). Here again the Qur'an is precise about the object of the seeing. God took him to see the great signs, and what he saw was the *ayat rabbihi al-kubra*, the greatest signs of his Lord — not the Essence. The common people say, "He saw God"; but as with the verse of the journey, the text is again present with al-Masjid al-Haram and al-Masjid al-Aqsa, and it speaks of signs, not of a vision of God.

What was the faculty of this seeing? `ya'ni bi-dhalika kulla 'l-hudud al-'ulwiyyah araha bi 'l-basar alladhi lam yazigh wa-huwa basar al-nafs al-safiya min al-kadar` (He means by that all the celestial limits, which He showed him with the sight that did not swerve — and it is the sight of the pure soul, free of turbidity). The "sight that did not swerve" is the *basar al-nafs al-safiya*, the vision of the pure soul, cleansed of all turbidity. By it the Prophet was shown all the *al-hudud al-'ulwiyyah*. This was a seeing of the soul, not of the bodily eye in bodily absence.

That this vision was of the soul, and not a removal of his body from its place, is confirmed by a narration from one of his wives. `wa-li-dhalika ruwiya 'an ba'd azwajihi annaha qalat laylata usriya bihi wa 'llahi ma bata illa bayna sahri wa-nahri` (And for this reason it is narrated from one of his wives — it is narrated from 'A'isha — that she said, "On the night he was taken on the journey, by Allah, he passed it nowhere but between my chest and my neck"). On the very night of the *isra'*, she testifies, he did not leave his place beside her; he remained the whole night between *sahri* and *nahri*, between her chest and her throat. The meanings of *sahri* and *nahri* are to be examined, but the force of the report is plain: since his body never departed, the journey and the vision were of the soul, the pure soul whose sight does not swerve.

### The Buraq as the Flashing Knowledge

Now the *ta'wil* of the *Buraq* itself, which was deferred earlier, is unfolded. `wa-fi 'l-khabar innahu lamma balagha ila bayt al-maqdis atahu jibra'il bi-markub dun al-baghl wa-fawqa 'l-dawabbah yusamma al-buraq fa-rakibahu` (And it is in the report that when he reached Bayt al-Maqdis, Jibra'il came to him with a mount smaller than a mule and larger than a beast of burden, named al-Buraq, and he rode it). The wording itself is instructive: Jibra'il brought the Buraq only after the Prophet had reached Bayt al-Maqdis — not in order to carry him from Mecca to Bayt al-Maqdis. The mount was smaller than a mule and larger than a common beast of burden, a thing of middle measure, named al-Buraq, and he mounted it.

The inner meaning: `wa-dhalika fi 'l-ta'wil huwa 'l-'ilm al-mubriq al-mudi' alladhi huwa 'l-bayan fawqa 'ilm al-lahiq wa-dunahu 'ilm al-imam wa-rukubuhu iyyahu istila'uhu 'alayhi` (And that, in interpretation, is the flashing, illuminating knowledge which is the clarification — above the knowledge of the successor and below the knowledge of the Imam — and his riding it is his mastery over it). The Buraq is the *al-'ilm al-mubriq*, the flashing, lightning-bright and illuminating knowledge, which is the *bayan*, the clarifying exposition. It stands above the knowledge of the *lahiq*, the successor, and below the knowledge of the Imam, who here is Abu Talib. And his riding the Buraq signifies his *istila'*, his mastery and command over that knowledge — to mount it is to be its master.

And the ascent that follows the mounting? `wa-su'uduhu ila 'l-sama' fi 'l-ta'wil ittila'uhu 'ala malakutiha wa-'ilm ma fiha` (And his ascension to the heaven, in interpretation, is his being informed of its kingdom and the knowledge of what is in it). The ascension to the heaven is his being made aware of the *malakut* of the heaven and the knowledge of all that it contains; the Kingdom of the heavens was opened to him.

### Abraham and the Kingdom of the Heavens and the Earth

This same opening of the *malakut* is grounded in the case of Ibrahim. `wa-min dhalika qawluhu 'azza wa-jalla fi ibrahim salawat Allah 'alayh wa-kadhalika nuri ibrahim malakut al-samawat wa 'l-ard wa-li-yakuna min al-muqinin` (And from that is His saying concerning Ibrahim, peace upon him — "And thus did We show Ibrahim the kingdom of the heavens and the earth, that he might be of those who are certain"). God showed Ibrahim the *malakut al-samawat wa al-ard*, the Kingdom of the heavens and the earth. The discourse will give the two halves of this showing distinct meanings — but first it situates the whole within the order of the highest ranks.

The ranks of imamate, of messengership, and of successorship Ibrahim established; yet above all of these stands the station of *true speech*, the divine utterance — above prophethood, above messengership, above imamate, above successorship. Who are the bearers of this true speech? They are three: Hunayd, Ibrahim, and Hashim. These three are the bearers of the *nutq ilahi*, the divine utterance, in this era. And the Prophet, upon whom be blessing and peace, surpassed even their rank — and he surpassed it through the *hijabiyyah*, the rank of the veil, which he attained on the night of the ascension. In that strange and lofty domain of the *hijabiyyah*, his rank rose above theirs.

The discourse will return below to where the celestial showing of Ibrahim points, distinguishing the heavenly Kingdom from the earthly; but here it has already given the first sense: `wa-isharatuhu ila malakut al-samawat wa 'l-ard, yuridu bi-dhalika al-'alam al-ruhani ba'da 'uluwwi rutbatihi wa kamali ma'rifatihi` (And his indication toward the kingdom of the heavens and the earth — he intends by it the spiritual world, after the loftiness of his rank and the perfection of his knowledge). The "Kingdom of the heavens and the earth" shown to Ibrahim is the *'alam al-ruhani*, the spiritual world, disclosed to him after his rank had been raised high and his knowledge perfected.

### The Prophet Leading the Prophets: A Call to All the Nations

Among the reports of the night is that the Prophet led all the prophets in prayer. `wa-ja'a fi 'l-khabar annahu salla bi-jami' al-anbiya' wa-salatuhu bihim fi 'l-batin da'watuhu li-jami' al-umam` (And it has come in the report that he prayed with all the prophets, and his leading them in prayer, in the inner sense, is his call to all the nations). His imamate over the prophets in that prayer is, esoterically, his summons addressed to every nation. When the prayer was to be established, the angels announced, "The Master of the Messengers has come," and the Prophet led the prayer for all the prophets.

And who gave the call to prayer at that gathering? `wa-annahu bu'itha li-jami'ihim kama dhukira tamam al-kalam fihi wa-ghayrihi. wa-ghayruhu min al-anbiya' innama bu'itha ila qawmihi bi-a'yanihim` (And that he was sent to all of them, as the completion of the discourse on this and other matters has been mentioned; whereas the others among the prophets were sent only to their own particular peoples). The *mu'adhdhin*, the one who called to that prayer, was Ibrahim, who gave the call standing at the station of Safa and Marwa — the explanation of Safa and Marwa being the one rendered in the matters of the Hajj. So the muezzin was Ibrahim and the Imam was the Prophet, leading all the prophets. And the deep significance is this: the Prophet was sent to all the prophets and all the nations, whereas every other prophet was sent only to his own specific people. His imamate over them in that prayer is the sign of the universality of his mission.

The discourse returns to the verse to draw out the "signs": `wa-qawluhu li-nuriyahu min ayatina min ayatihi al-zahirah allati arahu subhanahu hududan allati akhanaha baynahu wa-bayna anbiya'ihi wa-mala'ikatihi fa-ra'a ayatan kubratan minhum` (And His saying, "that We might show him of Our signs" — of His manifest signs, which the Glorious One showed him, limits which He had appointed between Himself and His prophets and His angels, so that he saw a great sign among them). The signs shown were the *hudud* that God had set between Himself and His prophets and His angels, and the Prophet beheld a great sign among them, with his pure, insightful heart, and heard with his hearing ear — and that is the meaning of `innahu huwa al-sami' al-basir` (Indeed He is the All-Hearing, the All-Seeing).

### Al-Masjid al-Aqsa as the Completer, and the Blessing as Support

The destination of the journey is now interpreted in full. `wa-qawluhu ila 'l-masjid al-aqsa wa-qad dhakarnahu mithal al-mutimm al-mahzur 'alayhi 'l-mufatahah` (And His saying, "to the Farthest Mosque" — and we have already mentioned that it is an example of the *mutimm*, the Completer, upon whom initiation is forbidden). Al-Masjid al-Aqsa, the Farthest Mosque, is the symbol of the *mutimm*, the Completer of the cycle, upon whom *mufatahah* — initiation, the opening of one to another — is forbidden, because he is the seal and term of the cycle and there is none above him within it to initiate him further.

And the blessing named in the verse? `wa-qawluhu alladhi barakna hawlahu fa 'l-barakah hiya 'l-ta'yid wa 'l-mufatahah fa-kanat dhalika li-man qablahu wa-man ba'dahu` (And His saying, "whose precincts We have blessed" — for the blessing is the support and the initiation, and that was for those before him and those after him). The *barakah*, the blessing of the precincts, is the *ta'yid* and the *mufatahah* — the divine support and the initiation — and this blessing was granted both to those who came before and to those who came after.

The closing clause of the verse then yields the doctrine of establishing the lower limits in the image of the higher. `wa-qawluhu li-nuriyahu min ayatina ya'ni annahu idha ittala'a 'ala dhalika min hudud al-'ulwiyyah nasaba bi-idha'iha hududan sufliyyah` (And His saying, "that We might show him of Our signs," means that when he was informed of that of the celestial limits, he set up, in correspondence with them, terrestrial limits). When the Prophet had been made aware of the system of the *al-hudud al-'ulwiyyah*, the celestial limits, he established, in opposition and correspondence to them, the *hudud sufliyyah*, the terrestrial limits — for the system of the celestial order that he beheld there, that very same system he set up in his call upon the earth.

A word on Jibra'il at the summit belongs here, for his station was not fixed but varied: as the Prophet's rank was elevated, Jibra'il's position too changed. The discourse does not specify who Jibra'il was at the highest station to which the Prophet ascended; but it does mark the station beyond which even Jibra'il could not pass — the station of `qaba qawsayni aw adna` (at the distance of two bow-lengths, or nearer). When Jibra'il reached that limit he said, "O Prophet of God, I cannot go beyond this." The verse of Sa'di speaks of just this: that were he to advance a hair's breadth further, his wings would burn.

### The Prophet's Transmission to His Foundation

The night's knowledge was not given to be kept; it was given to be conveyed. `fa-abda'a salawat Allah 'alayh min dhalika al-'ilm alladhi utiyahu ila asasihi bi-ma yujib an yaqbadahu minhu ilayhi fa-'allamahu iyyahu wa-ahda ilayhi an yu'addi min dhalika ila man yusi ilayhi bi-sirrihi wa-yuqimuhu min ba'dihi bi-ma yajib li-mithlihi wa-in yanqulahu kadhalika wahidan ila wahid` (So he conveyed of that knowledge which he was given to his foundation, what it was necessary for him to receive from him, and taught it to him, and instructed him to transmit of it to the one to whom he bequeaths his secret, and to establish him after him with what is fitting for his like, and to pass it thus, one to one). The Prophet conveyed to his *asas*, his foundation — who is 'Ali — the knowledge of the *ta'wil* of the *Laylat al-Isra'* that he had received, conveying to him precisely what was due to be conveyed. He laid out the whole order of the universe: that this Imam would have such-and-such a measure, and that Imam such-and-such — an entire cosmos arranged. And he instructed his foundation in turn to convey from it, after him, to the one whom he should appoint as the bearer of his secret, and to establish that one after himself with what befits his rank, and so to transmit it one to one, hand to hand, down the chain of successors. `wa-kadhalika awda'ahu wa-amarahu an yu'addi 'anhu min amr zahir al-shari'ah` (And likewise he entrusted it to him and commanded him to convey on his behalf the matter of the outward Shari'ah) — so that alongside the inner knowledge, the foundation was charged also with conveying the outward law.

### The Twenty-Two Cycles of the Twenty-Seventh of Rajab

The discourse turns from the night journey to the rite that commemorates the Prophet's mission upon this date. One who performs a deed on this day has, it is said, the reward of twenty years. When asked what should be done, the answer was given: twenty-two *rak'at* should be prayed, with twenty-two surahs. And after the *salam* that closes the prayer, one should recite *al-Hamd* seven times, *Inna Anzalna* seven times, and *Ayat al-Kursi* seven times — which together make twenty-one, and with *Qul al-Hamd* completing them, twenty-two; so in the twenty-two *rak'at* there are, by this reckoning, twenty-two recitations. Then a supplication is added, and on the morning of that day one says, `Ba'atha Muhammad salawat Allah 'alayh` — Allah sent forth Muhammad — on the twenty-seventh of Rajab, as a summoner to the religion of Islam.

The choice of the month is itself a sign. Rajab is an example for the rank of the Imam — the month of imamate. As the Prophet declared, "O 'Ali, Rajab is the month of every Imam who rises after you," for it is the month of the Imams. "Sha'ban is my month, Ramadan is the month of Allah, and Rajab is the month of the Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt." Thus the three months ascend in order: Rajab belonging to the Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt, Sha'ban to the Prophet, and Ramadan to Allah Himself.

### The Thirty Days of Rajab and the Twenty-Seven Hidden Imams

The Prophet's saying continues into the symbolism of the days of the month: "And the Imams of the progeny of Isma'il ibn Ibrahim, peace be upon him, were thirty." That is, the thirty days of Rajab correspond to thirty Imams from the progeny of Isma'il ibn Ibrahim. And there is a further division within the days: the nights of Rajab are founded upon the progeny of Isma'il, and its days upon the progeny of Ishaq — its nights for the line of Isma'il, its days for the line of Ishaq. And the rising of whoever rises from the progeny of Ishaq is itself founded upon the master of his era from the progeny of Isma'il, to whom the command is entrusted.

The placement of the *mab'ath* on the twenty-seventh now discloses its meaning. The Prophet's mission fell on the twenty-seventh — and the deeper sense, drawn from his very birth, is that his *wiladat*, his coming forth, was after twenty-seven Imams from the progeny of Isma'il who had been in the veil, concealed. The *mab'ath* upon the twenty-seventh thus signifies twenty-seven hidden Imams preceding the Prophet. And what of the days remaining? After the twenty-seventh, three days remain of the month — and these three remaining days are an indication of three Imams who remain after the Prophet's coming forth: 'Abd al-Muttalib, 'Abdullah, and Abu Talib. During the time of these three Imams the Prophet was born; they are the three to whom the three remaining days of Rajab point. And among these three, the one chosen from among the Imams is 'Abd al-Muttalib.

The discourse adds that in the twenty-two *rak'at* of this prayer, when the prayer is completed, the recitations are themselves examples of the limits — celestial and terrestrial — by which the call of imamate is established. The names of these ranks were given in order: Imam, *bab*, *markaz*, *saff*, the *bab* of the circle, *hujjat*, the *hujjat-i sayyarah*, the *da'i-i balagh*, the *da'i-i ahram*, the *ma'dhun al-mutlaq*, and yet two or three further ranks — the whole ladder of the hudud. And in this same context it is affirmed that the *asas* is 'Ali and the *natiq* is the Prophet, and that the birth of the *asas* fell on the twenty-seventh of Rajab — the very date that is to be confirmed and held fast as the established date of this commemoration.

### The Three Imams and Their Connection to Both the Prophet and 'Ali

The three Imams indicated by the three remaining days — 'Abd al-Muttalib, 'Abdullah, and Abu Talib — raise a question of relationship: after whom do they come, and to whom is their *nisbah*, their connection — to the Prophet or to Mawla 'Ali? The answer is that their connection is to both at once. To grasp how, one must distinguish, in speaking of "the Prophet," whether one means the *Hijab* or the *Muhtajib*; and one must read the three through the doctrine of the three streams, the *majari* — whether a matter belongs to the highest stream (*majra-e a'la*), the middle (*majra-e awsat*), or the lowest (*majra-e adna*) — for the connection of the three answers to the stream in which the discussion stands. And there is a subtlety of writing preserved here: in a certain book the phrase `'Abdullah 'indallah` was written with two dots removed, the upper dot and the lower both lifted away, so that the reader must understand what stands beneath; for as it is said, `kila-huma ayatun wahidah` (both of them are a single sign) — as Hasan is Husayn, as Mahdi and Qa'im are both a single sign, so here too two names resolve into one reality.

The station above these is the `'Ayn 'Azimah`, which is the station of the *Muhtajib* in the *'alam-e hayat*, the realm of life. Its glory, its *shan*, is of another order, and of it is said: `Ana al-awwal, ana al-akhir, ana al-zahir, wa al-batin` (I am the First, I am the Last, I am the Manifest, and the Hidden). Those who are in this world were made its servants, its *khadim*. The figures of the world stand as servants to the *Muhtajib* of the 'Ayn 'Azimah.

As for the manner of the connection of the three Imams: they were not all alive at one moment, and the question is whether their connection to the Prophet is by way of his birth or his mission. The answer given is that it is by way of his birth, not his *bi'that*. For the Prophet had already become the bearer of prophethood before his outward birth — `Kuntu nabiyyan wa Adamu bayna al-ma'i wa al-tin` (I was a prophet while Adam was yet between water and clay). At the time of his coming forth, the three Imams were present, and in this way the connection holds: when he was made — when he stood as prophet — these three Imams were present with him. And the symmetry is exact: just as, had 'Abd al-Muttalib and 'Ali both passed in the Prophet's time, the Prophet-Imam and his era would still have been present, so the three stand on 'Ali's side as on the Prophet's. With 'Ali there were three, and on the Prophet's side there were three — the three Imams bound by birth and by prophethood to both the natiq and his asas.

### The Ladder of the Ranks: From Respondent to Divine Utterance

A question is raised about the 124,000 prophets and where they stand in relation to the bearers of divine utterance. To answer it, the whole ladder of the ranks (*maratib*) must be set out in order. At the lowest is the *mustajib*, the respondent who first answers the call. Above him is the *mu'min*, the believer. Then the *mukasir*, the disputer who breaks down objections. Then the *ma'sum*, the infallible. Then the *da'i*, the summoner — and the rank of *da'i* itself has its grades, the *da'i mahsur*, the limited summoner, and the *da'i balagh*, the summoner of conveyance. Then the *hujjah*, the proof. Then the *bab*, the gate. Then the *Imam*. These are ten ranks.

After these ten, a higher order begins: *Nubuwwah* (prophethood) and *Risalah* (messengership), *Imamah* (imamate) and *Wasiyyah* (executorship) — four ranks. And above these four stands the rank of *nutq ilahi*, the divine utterance, which is counted among the ranks of *Qa'imiyyah*. These four ranks lie below the level of the *nutq ilahi*. Now within the level of divine utterance, three are established in this era — the *thalathah*, the three: Imam Hunayd, peace be upon him, then Ibrahim, peace be upon him, and then Hashim. Three bearers of *nutq ilahi*. And whoever is the seventh in the era of the Prophet is the *sahib nutq ilahi*, the possessor of the divine utterance. So the principle of ten reappears once more: Hunayd, Ibrahim, Hashim — three — and then seven Imams, making ten; and the rest are *qurun*, eras. As for the 124,000 prophets, they occupy the lower levels, below the rank of the *nutq ilahi*. Thus the great multitude of the prophets, for all their dignity, stand beneath the supreme rank of the divine utterance, which belongs to the few.

A point is here put to rest concerning the chronology of the Prophet's household. Some object that during the lifetime of Mawlana Khadija the Prophet took no second wife, and that the *hijrat* and the *mi'raj* fell within her lifetime — so whence the report of 'A'isha lying beside him on the night of the journey? The answer is that such a narration was created and then denied; it does not stand. Had it been allowed to grow any further, the *hur*, the celestial maidens, would have been brought into it as well. The sound point remains the one established above: the journey was of the soul, and the body never left its place.

### "Knowing Nothing": The Universal Need for Instruction

The fourth chapter opens by extending the teaching to the whole of humankind. `fa-naqulu anna jami' hadha al-'alam al-bashari min natiqin aw wasiyyin wa-imamin wa-tabi'ihim wa-ghayrihim min sa'ir al-nas, muftaqiruna fi bad'i wujudihim ila al-ta'lim` (So we say that the whole of this human world — whether a natiq, or a successor, or an Imam, or their followers, or others from all people — are needy, at the beginning of their existence, of instruction). Without exception — the *natiq* no less than the *wasi*, the Imam no less than the common man — every human being stands, at the start of his existence, in need of *ta'lim*, of being taught.

This neediness is grounded in scripture: `wa-bi-dhalika nadakh-khala al-kitab al-karim bi-qawlihi ta'ala: wallahu akhrajakum min butuni ummahatikum la ta'lamuna shay'an` (And for this the Noble Book is brought in, by His saying, "And Allah brought you out from the wombs of your mothers knowing nothing"). Allah brought every one of us forth from our mothers' wombs knowing nothing whatever. `fa-'ammat hadhihi al-ayah jami' al-khalq al-bashari, sharifihi wa-mashrufihi` (And this verse became general for the whole human creation, its noble and its ignoble alike). The verse makes no exception: it embraces every human being, those granted nobility and those who are lowest of the low. All come forth in ignorance, and all require teaching.

### The Difference Lies Only in Capacity

If all begin in ignorance, wherein lies the difference among them? `wa-innama baynahum al-tafawut fi al-dhaka wa al-fitnah wa al-fikr wa al-qubul` (And the difference between them is only in intelligence, sagacity, thought, and acceptance). The disparity among people is not in the starting point — all start at nothing — but in four faculties: *dhaka*, intelligence; *fitnah*, sagacity; *fikr*, thought; and *qubul*, the capacity for acceptance.

And the noblest of all are those in whom these faculties are most abundant. `wa-dhalika inna ashraf al-ashraf al-muntakha 'alayhim al-salam, natiqu dawrina wa-abuhu ibrahim, wa-ashraf al-awsiya' wa-al-a'immah 'alayhim al-salam, amir al-mu'minin 'ali salamu-llahi 'alayhi wa-'alayhim, wa-hum mimman ta'allama wa-raqa fi al-ma'arif shay'an ba'da shay'in ila an balagha ghayat al-nutq` (And that is because the most noble of the noble, peace upon them — the natiq of our era and his father Ibrahim — and the most noble of the successors and Imams, Amir al-Mu'minin 'Ali, the peace of God upon him and upon them, are among those who learned and progressed in the branches of knowledge, thing after thing, until they reached the utmost limit of utterance). The most noble of the nobles — the *natiq* of our era, the Prophet, and his father Ibrahim — and the most noble of the successors and Imams, Amir al-Mu'minin 'Ali, were themselves among those who learned and rose in knowledge bit by bit, step after step, until they reached the very pinnacle of utterance.

They too were born knowing nothing, but their speed in learning was incomparable. The image given is plain: one man travels on foot and another by aircraft, and between the two there is a vast difference, though both move along the road. So it is with the noblest souls: their *'iddat*, their preparedness — that is the very word used for it — was far greater than others'. Their intelligence, their sagacity, their thought, and their acceptance were of a wholly higher measure, and so they traversed the ranks of knowledge with a swiftness that none could match, until they came to the summit of the divine utterance.

### The Two Kingdoms Shown to Abraham

The certainty granted to Ibrahim is the scriptural witness to this doctrine of learning and ascent. `wa-yash-hadu bi-dhalika qawlu-llahi ta'ala fi ibrahim: wa-kadhalika nuri ibrahima malakuta al-samawati wa-al-ard wa-li-yakuna min al-muqinin` (And the saying of Allah concerning Ibrahim bears witness to this: "And thus did We show Ibrahim the kingdom of the heavens and the earth, that he might be of those who are certain"). The showing has two halves, and each is interpreted. `wa-isharatuhu ila malakut al-samawati wa-al-ard, yuridu bi-dhalika al-'alam al-ruhani ba'da 'uluwwi rutbatihi wa-kamali ma'rifatihi` (And his indication toward the kingdom of the heavens and the earth — he intends by it the spiritual world, after the loftiness of his rank and the perfection of his knowledge). The *malakut al-samawat*, the Kingdom of the heavens, is the *'alam al-ruhani*, the spiritual world, disclosed to Ibrahim after his rank had been exalted and his knowledge perfected.

The earthly half points the other way — downward and backward, to the very ranks from whom he first learned. `wa-bi-malakut al-ard al-hudud alladhina akhadha 'anhum fi bad'i amrihi` (And by the kingdom of the earth, the limits from whom he took at the beginning of his affair). The *malakut al-ard*, the Kingdom of the earth, is the *hudud*, the spiritual ranks, from whom Ibrahim received in the beginning of his affair. And these earthly hudud are precisely the "star, moon, and sun" of the famous passage: `wa-'ana hum bi-qawlihi ta'ala: falamma janna 'alayhi al-laylu ra'a kawkaban, qala hadha rabbi, falamma afala qala la uhibbu al-afilin. falamma ra'a al-qamara bazighan qala hadha rabbi, falamma afala qala la-in lam yahdini rabbi la-akunanna min al-qawm al-dallin` (And he meant them by His saying: "So when the night covered him he saw a star and said, 'This is my Lord'; but when it set he said, 'I love not those that set.' Then when he saw the moon rising he said, 'This is my Lord'; but when it set he said, 'If my Lord does not guide me I shall surely be among the people who go astray'"). And the conclusion: `wa-sa'ir al-ayah, wa-ha'ula'i hum al-hudud al-jismaniyyun alladhina tawallaw tarbiyatahu wa-ta'limahu fi bad'i amrihi, wa-ta'allama minhum` (and the rest of the verse — and these are the bodily limits who undertook his upbringing and his teaching at the beginning of his affair, and he learned from them).

So the *la uhibbu al-afilin*, "I love not those that set," is not Ibrahim's repudiation of created heavenly bodies as false gods in the popular telling, but his progression through the *hudud jismaniyyun*, the bodily limits, who took charge of his upbringing and teaching at the start of his affair, and from whom he learned. Initially the *hadd* of one teacher stood at the station of his "star"; then he came to the "moon," and the one at the station of the moon was Bab Sharif; and so onward to the sun. The setting of each is his passing beyond that rank to the next, until he was guided to the summit. The earthly Kingdom shown to Ibrahim is thus the disclosure of the very teachers — star, moon, and sun — from whom he had first received.

### 'Ali's Instruction and His Likeness to 'Isa

The same law of learning governed Amir al-Mu'minin. `wa-ta'allama amir al-mu'minin fi bad'i amrihi min rasulillahi salawatullahi 'alayhima wa-min khadijata bint khuwaylid wa-ghayriha min al-hudud` (And Amir al-Mu'minin learned, at the beginning of his affair, from the Messenger of God — peace of God upon them both — and from Khadija bint Khuwaylid, and from others besides her among the limits). 'Ali received his instruction in the beginning from the Prophet, and from Khadija bint Khuwaylid, who was his teacher and nurturer, and from yet others among the *hudud*.

Because of this attribution to Khadija, a likeness was drawn. `wa-li-dhalika nasaba ilayha wa-muth-thila bi-'isa ibn maryam` (And for this he was attributed to her and likened to 'Isa son of Maryam). 'Ali was attributed to Khadija, and a parallel was drawn between him and 'Isa ibn Maryam: Maryam corresponds to Khadija, and 'Isa to 'Ali, in the manner of a comparison. `kama qala fihi al-kitab al-karim: wa-lamma duriba ibnu maryama mathalan idha qawmuka minhu yasiddun` (As the Noble Book says of him: "And when the son of Maryam was set forth as an example, behold, your people turned away from it"). When the example of the son of Maryam is given, the people turn from it in derision — and 'Ali is likened to 'Isa ibn Maryam in just this way. The Prophet himself confirmed the comparison, saying, "O 'Ali, were I to describe of you those things which I have not until now described, the people would revere the hoof of your horse as they revere the hoof of 'Isa's donkey" — a famous hadith that places 'Ali in the very station of veneration accorded to the Messiah.

And the breadth of what 'Ali received from the Prophet is captured in his own celebrated saying. `thumma qawluhu 'alayhis salam: 'allamani rasulullah alfa babin min al-'ilm, infataha li min kulli babin alfa bab` (Then his saying, peace upon him: "The Messenger of God taught me a thousand chapters of knowledge, and from each chapter a thousand chapters opened up for me"). The Prophet taught 'Ali a thousand doors of knowledge, and from every one of those doors a thousand further doors opened to him. There is even a grammatical nicety in the wording. The verb *infataha* belongs to the *infi'al* pattern, the reflexive form, which requires no object; for verbs are of two kinds, intransitive (*fi'l-e lazim*), needing only a verb and subject, and transitive (*fi'l-e muta'addi*), needing a verb, subject, and object. *Infataha li*, "it opened for me," is intransitive: it takes no object, and the *alfa bab*, the thousand doors, is its subject, which is why the case-ending falls upon it. It is like *inhazama*, "he was defeated," which cannot take an object — for one says not *inhazama al-ahzab* but `hazama al-ahzaba wahdahu` (He defeated the parties alone), where *hazama* is the active "He gave defeat." A scribal error long propagated in the copies, writing *inhazama* where the Qur'an itself reads *hazama*, is corrected here by reference to the verses of the Book — in the second section of the fifth chapter, from the fourth evidence — for nowhere in the glorious Qur'an does *inhazama* occur; it is always *hazama*.

### "I Took from Five and Delivered to Five": The First Five

The discourse now expounds one of the great sayings of the *natiq*, the key that unlocks the whole genealogy of his knowledge and his trust. `wa-min al-dalili 'ala dhalika qawlu natiqi dawrina 'alayhis salam: akhadhtu min khamsatin wa-sallamtu ila khamsatin wa-bayni wa-bayna rabbi khamsah` (And among the proofs of that is the saying of the natiq of our era, peace upon him: "I took from five, and I delivered to five, and between me and my Lord are five"). Three fives are named, and each must be identified.

The first five, those from whom he took: `fa-al-khamsah alladhina akhadha 'anhum al-nutuqa' al-khamsah al-mutaqaddimuna 'alayhi, alladhina ta'allama kutubahum al-munazzalah 'alayhim, wa-shara'i'ahum allati shara'uha, wa-awda'ahum allati wada'uha, haqa'iqaha wa-ta'wilatiha wa-ma'aniha` (Thus the five from whom he took are the five speaking-prophets who preceded him, whose revealed books he learned, and their laws which they legislated, and their deposits which they laid down — their realities, their interpretations, and their meanings). These five from whom the Prophet took are the five preceding *nutuqa'*, the speaking-prophets before him — Adam, Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, and 'Isa. From them he learned their revealed books, the laws they established, the deposits they laid down, and he verified their realities, their interpretations, and their meanings. From the five speaking-prophets of the cycles before him he received the whole inheritance of revelation.

### The Five Between Him and His Lord: His Earthly Teachers

The third five — those between him and his Lord — are named next, for the discourse turns to them before the five to whom he delivered. `wa-al-khamsah allati baynahu wa-bayna rabbihi hum al-hudud al-diniyyah alladhina akhadha 'anhum wa-ta'allama fi bad'i amrihi minhum, wa-raqquhu maratib al-din shay'an ba'da shay'in ila an balagha dhirwataha` (And the five between him and his Lord are the religious limits from whom he took and learned at the beginning of his affair, who elevated him through the ranks of the religion, thing after thing, until he reached its peak). The five between the Prophet and his Lord are the *hudud al-diniyyah*, the religious ranks, from whom he took and learned in the beginning of his affair, and who raised him through the ranks of the religion bit by bit until he reached its summit. `wa-hum ubayy wa-zayd ibn 'amr wa-'amr ibn nufayl wa-maysarah wa-khadijah bint khuwaylid` (And they are Ubayy, and Zayd ibn 'Amr, and 'Amr ibn Nufayl, and Maysarah, and Khadija bint Khuwaylid). These five — Ubayy ibn Ka'b, Zayd ibn 'Amr (not to be confused, and the name 'Amr read as 'Amr, not 'Umar), 'Amr ibn Nufayl, Maysarah, and Khadija bint Khuwaylid — are the religious hudud who instructed the Prophet and elevated him through the ranks. And Khadija, who gave the Prophet instruction, is also the teacher of 'Ali — the same Khadija to whom 'Ali was attributed in his likeness to 'Isa.

These five, in turn, belong to one master. `wa-hum hududu sahib al-waqt al-muqim lahu, al-mu'li rutbatahu, al-musallim ilayhi, wa-huwa 'ammuhu abu talib` (And they are the limits of the Master of the Time who established him, elevated his rank, and entrusted to him — and he is his uncle, Abu Talib). The five hudud are the hudud of the *sahib al-waqt*, the Master of the Time, who established the Prophet, raised his rank high, and entrusted his affair to him; and this Master of the Time is the Prophet's uncle, Abu Talib. The phrase `wa-huwa 'ammuhu abu talib` is itself a nominal sentence, subject and predicate — "and he is his uncle, Abu Talib." So the five teachers are Abu Talib's hudud, and Abu Talib is the Master of the Time who set the Prophet in his station.

### The Five to Whom He Delivered

The second five, those to whom the Prophet delivered his trust, are now named. `wa-amma al-khamsah alladhina sallama ilayhim fa-hum wasiyuhu amir al-mu'minin, wa-abnahu al-hasan wa-al-husayn, wa-al-baqir wa-huwa muhammad ibn 'ali, wa-muhammad ibn isma'il salawatullahi 'alayhim ajma'in` (And as for the five to whom he delivered, they are his successor Amir al-Mu'minin, and his two sons al-Hasan and al-Husayn, and al-Baqir, who is Muhammad ibn 'Ali, and Muhammad ibn Isma'il, peace of God upon them all). The five recipients are 'Ali his successor, Hasan and Husayn his two grandsons, al-Baqir who is Muhammad ibn 'Ali, and Muhammad ibn Isma'il. From the vast store of glory that the Prophet possessed, he bestowed upon these five the things of imamate — and here a distinction is to be borne in mind between the things of the *Imamah al-mustawda'*, the deposited imamate, and those of the *Imamah al-mustaqarr*, the established and permanent imamate. The treasures that 'Abd al-Muttalib had gathered together with Abu Talib — in whom the imamate and executorship were combined, and the prophethood and messengership combined — these the Prophet conveyed to 'Ali, Hasan, and Husayn.

The entrustment to Hasan and Husayn rests upon a famous saying. `wa-taslimuhu li-amir al-mu'minin 'alayhis salam, wa-al-hasan wa-al-husayn 'alayhima al-salam, bi-qawlihim: al-hasan wa-al-husayn imaman qama aw qa'ada, wa-abuhuma khayrun minhuma` (And his delivering to Amir al-Mu'minin, peace upon him, and to al-Hasan and al-Husayn, peace upon them, by the saying: "al-Hasan and al-Husayn are two Imams, whether they rise up or sit, and their father is better than the two of them"). The Prophet declared Hasan and Husayn two Imams — already Imams, in his own lifetime, whether they should rise up or remain seated — and declared their father better than the two of them. The wording is precise: it does not say "after me," for the Prophet was yet present when he said that they *are* Imams; both had become Imams during the Prophet's own time, and 'Ali had become better than imamate itself.

The entrustment to al-Baqir came by another channel. `wa-taslimuhu li-al-baqir 'alayhim al-salam, bi-qawlihi li-jabir ibn 'abdillah: innaka sa-talhaqu waladan min waladi, hadha, fa-aqri'hu minni al-salam, wa-qul lahu: qala laka jadduka ya baqir, ya baqir al-'ilm, abqirhu baqran` (And his delivering to al-Baqir, peace upon them, by his saying to Jabir ibn 'Abdillah: "You will meet a child of my child — this one — so convey my peace to him, and say to him: Your grandfather says to you, O Baqir, O cleaver of knowledge, cleave it open"). The Prophet told Jabir ibn 'Abdillah that he would live to meet a descendant of his, a child of his child — pointing toward Husayn and his line — and charged him to convey the Prophet's salam to him and to deliver the message: "Your grandfather says to you, O Baqir, O cleaver of knowledge, cleave the knowledge wide open" — that is, lay it open and expound it thoroughly. The name "Baqir," resembling the matter of cleaving, marks him as the one who would split knowledge open.

And the entrustment to Muhammad ibn Isma'il came through the *adhan* itself. `wa-taslimuhu li-muhammad ibn isma'il, bi-shahadatihi lahu bi-al-risalah fi al-adhan, 'inda qawlihi: ash-hadu anna muhammadan rasulullah, li-anna shahadatahu salawatullahi 'alayhi li-nafsihi ghayru ja'izin, wa-innama kanat al-shahadatu li-muhammad ibn isma'il 'alayhim al-salam` (And his delivering to Muhammad ibn Isma'il, by his testimony of messengership for him in the call to prayer, at the saying, "I bear witness that Muhammad is the Messenger of God" — because his own testimony for himself is not permissible, and indeed the testimony is for Muhammad ibn Isma'il, peace upon them). In the words of the *adhan*, the first testimony of messengership points to the Prophet, but the testimony cannot be the Prophet's own witness for himself, which is impermissible; therefore the testimony `ash-hadu anna muhammadan rasulullah` is also a witness borne for Muhammad ibn Isma'il, whose name resembles the Prophet's. The Imam whose name resembles the Prophet's receives from the Prophet; the Imam whose name resembles 'Ali's receives from 'Ali; and in this way every Imam receives his portion. The five named here leave unnamed 'Ali Zayn al-'Abidin and Ja'far al-Sadiq — not because they did not receive, for all the Imams receive, but because the special attention here falls upon those whose names answer to the Prophet's and 'Ali's.

### Why al-Baqir and al-Sadiq Are Singled Out

A question follows from the omission of Zayn al-'Abidin and Ja'far al-Sadiq from this particular reckoning, and it is answered with care. `wa-amma shahadat al-a'immati 'alayhim al-salam wa-sa'ir al-muslimin, fa-hiya laka, li-annaka natiqun sadisun` (And as for the testimony of the Imams, peace upon them, and the rest of the Muslims, it is for you, because you are the sixth natiq). The testimony of the Imams and of all the Muslims belongs to the Prophet, who is the sixth *natiq*. But why was al-Baqir singled out for the spreading of knowledge, and not Zayn al-'Abidin who preceded him? Because the era of Imam Zayn al-'Abidin was not an era of dissemination, not a time of *nashr*, the spreading abroad of knowledge. It was Imam Muhammad al-Baqir who spread it — for he came upon a time when the Banu Umayyah had been broken and were fighting among themselves, and in that opening he found the opportunity to narrate the hadith of the Prophet and to lay knowledge open. And why is Ja'far called al-Sadiq? Because he narrated the teachings of his father with *sidq*, with truth; for that reason he is al-Sadiq, the truthful. The one who in fact narrated and opened the knowledge was al-Baqir, and Ja'far is the truthful transmitter of what his father had opened.

### The Forty-Nine Imams and the Seven Week-Heads

The exaltation of Muhammad ibn Isma'il among the recipients leads into a doctrine of the whole succession after the Prophet. How many Imams will there be after the Prophet? Seven weeks — forty-nine Imams — for the forty-ninth is the seventh of the seventh week. And the heads of the seven weeks, the *hafti*, are superior to all the rest. In the first week stands Mawlana Muhammad ibn Isma'il; in the second, Mawlana Ma'add al-Mu'izz; in the third, Imam al-Tayyib; in the fourth, Mawlana Ma'add al-Thani; then Mawlana Ahmad; then Mawlana 'Ali; in the sixth week, Bayazid; and in the seventh week, the son of the present living Imam. These seven week-heads are superior to all the other forty-two Imams.

But within these seven, a further height is marked: four among them are the most superior of all — the four answering to the four words of the *kalimah*, `la ilaha`. And the manner of their gradation is illustrated by a couplet of Janab Hali, who said, in effect: "I draw a line; you, without touching this line, make it greater or smaller." For one cannot diminish a thing by attacking it directly; rather, by drawing a longer line beneath it the first line becomes small, and by drawing a shorter line above it the first becomes great — the original line untouched, yet made lesser or greater by what is set beside it. So it is with these ranks, graded by relation rather than by alteration. Of the four highest, two are the most superior of all, and of these two the rank of Muhammad ibn Isma'il is unique: he is `sahib kasbat al-ilahiyyah`, the possessor of the divine acquisition — one set against all, alone in his rank.

### The Double Testimony and the Seventh *Natiq*

The two testimonies of messengership in the *adhan* are now read together. `wa-shahadat al-a'immah wa-sa'ir al-muslimin fa-hiya li-al-payghambar, wa-qawnu shahadah musannatun fi al-adhan, li-ma kanat shahadah al-awwala 'alayhi` (And the testimony of the Imams and of all the Muslims is for the Prophet; and the testimony is made twofold in the call to prayer, because the first testimony was to point to him). `wa-al-thani li-muhammad ibn isma'il, alladhi huwa mutimm al-dawr, wa-sahib al-rusul, wa-ilayhi ishara mawlana al-mu'izz salawatullahi 'alayhi fi du'a' yawm al-sabt, idh huwa al-natiq al-sabi'` (And the second is for Muhammad ibn Isma'il, who is the completer of the cycle and the master of the apostles, and to whom our master al-Mu'izz pointed in the supplication of Saturday, since he is the seventh natiq). The first testimony of the *adhan* points to the Prophet; the second points to Muhammad ibn Isma'il, who is the *mutimm al-dawr*, the completer of the cycle, the master of the apostles, and the seventh *natiq* — indicated by Mawlana al-Mu'izz in the supplication of the Saturday prayer.

The dignity of these completers is then measured against the masters of the cycles. The six *mutimmin* — Imam al-Hasan, Imam al-Husayn, Imam 'Ali Zayn al-'Abidin, Imam Muhammad al-Baqir, Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, and Imam Isma'il — though they are Imams of one rank within the seventh part of the week, are so lofty that in comparison with the *sahib-e dawr*, the master of the cycle, they stand as equals to him; the Imams of the second week do not compare to them, but compare rather to the seventh. So high is the rank of the completer.

### The Qa'im al-Qiyamah: A Universal Intellect

Above even the master of the apostles stands the Qa'im. `wa-amma qa'im al-qiyamah 'alayhi dhikruhu al-salam, fa-amruhu a'la min dhalika, li-annahu ghayru muntazimin fi silk al-rusul, li-kawni kulli wahidin minhum 'udwan min a'da'ihi` (And as for the Qa'im of the Resurrection, upon him be the mention of peace, his affair is loftier than that, for he is not strung within the chain of the apostles, because each one of them is a limb among his limbs). The Qa'im al-Qiyamah is not counted within the chain of the apostles at all, for his rank is higher than theirs; rather, each apostle is a limb among the limbs of the Qa'im, a member of his body. `idh al-qa'im al-kulli alladhi qad sara 'aqlan kulliyyan, ka-ahad al-'uqul al-ibda'iyyah al-mujarradah, wa-huwa al-qa'im bi-tadbir al-'alam` (Since the Universal Qa'im, who has become a Universal Intellect, like one of the primordial abstract intellects, is the one who maintains the administration of the world). The Qa'im has become an *'aql kulli*, a Universal Intellect, like one of the *al-'uqul al-ibda'iyyah*, the primordial, abstract, originated intellects; and he is the one who upholds the *tadbir al-'alam*, the administration of the entire world.

`ka-ma sabaqa min amthalihi al-qa'imina bi-tadbir 'alam al-tabi'ah fi rutbat al-'ashir` (As has preceded from his likes, who maintain the administration of the natural world in the rank of the Tenth). Just as it has been said before, his likes — the administrators of the world of nature in the rank of the Tenth, the representatives of the Tenth Intellect — maintain the natural order, so the Qa'im al-Qiyamah maintains the administration of the entire era. The administration of the whole age is his.

### The Third of the Divine Speakers

Finally, Muhammad ibn Isma'il is placed among the supreme bearers of the divine utterance. `wa-muhammad ibn isma'il min sa'ir al-nutuqa' al-ilahiyyin, alladhina hum muhammad wa-ibrahim salamu-llahi 'alayhim ajma'in, wa-thalith lahuma` (And Muhammad ibn Isma'il is from the divine speakers, who are Muhammad and Ibrahim — peace of God upon them all — and he is the third to the two of them). Muhammad ibn Isma'il belongs to the *nutuqa' ilahiyyin*, the divine speakers, alongside Muhammad and Ibrahim, being the third to the two of them. `idh kullu wahidin minhum majmal al-maqamat taqaddamathu, wa-sar al-nutuqa' du'at al-a'immah azmanihim, wa-kullu wahidin minhum 'udwan min a'da' al-imam al-tali, li-imam 'asrihi ka-ma sabaqa bi-al-qawl` (Since each one of them is a summary of the stations that preceded him, and the speakers became the summoners of the Imams of their times, and each one of them is a limb from the limbs of the following Imam, the Imam of his era, as has preceded in the saying). Each *natiq* is a *majmal al-maqamat*, a summation of all the stations that came before him; the *nutuqa'* are the *du'at*, the summoners, of the Imams of their times; and each of them is a limb among the limbs of the Imam who follows, the Imam of his era. The divine utterance, then, is a rank distinct from and higher than that of the *natiq* as ordinarily understood: in this era its bearers are the *thalathah* — Imam Hunayd, Ibrahim, the Prophet, and Muhammad ibn Isma'il — and all the seventh-of-the-week Imams who have come are possessors of the divine utterance; yet among them the highest rank belongs to the Prophet, to Ibrahim, and to Muhammad ibn Isma'il.

### The Infant Imam and His Three Teachers

A question naturally arises from the foregoing, and the fourth chapter poses it directly. `la-qa'ilin an yaqula: fa-idha kana Mawlana Aamir, radiyallahu 'anhu, qad intaqala ila dari rahmatillah, wa-Mawlana Imam al-Tayyib Abu 'l-Qasim, salawatullah 'alayh, fi halati t-tufuliyyah, fa-man al-mu'allim lahu?` (One might ask: when Mawlana Aamir, may God be pleased with him, has passed to the abode of God's mercy, and Mawlana Imam al-Tayyib Abu'l-Qasim, peace upon him, is in the state of infancy — then who is his teacher?). If Imam al-Tayyib is an infant at his father's death, who instructs him in the period of instruction? And who is the *mufid*, the bestowing benefactor, for those who migrate toward the Imam — whether openly (*zahir*) or inwardly (*batin*) — in the state of his concealment? For there are those who migrate physically and those whose supplications reach the Imam in the hidden way, and both stand in need of one who imparts.

The answer is given. `fa-l-ya'lam annahu min waqti nassi abihi 'alayhi wa-satrihi lahu la yufariqu l-bab hujjatan wa-da'iyan` (So let it be known that, from the time of his father's designation upon him and his concealment, the gate, the proof, and the summoner-of-conveyance do not leave him). From the moment of the *nass*, the explicit designation by his father, and of the *satr*, the concealment that follows, three figures never depart from the infant Imam: the *bab*, the gate; the *hujjah*, the proof; and the *da'i al-balagh*, the summoner of conveyance. `wa-hum al-mufiduna liman ittasala bihim wa-quddira lahu 'l-muhajara ila bayna yadayhi` (And they are the bestowers for whoever connects with them and for whom migration to his presence is destined). These three are the *mufidun*, the bestowers of benefit, for whoever attaches himself to them and for whom migration into the Imam's presence is decreed.

`wa-hum al-mu'allimuna lahu fi awani t-ta'lim ma yajib ta'limuhu, wa-l-muraqquna lahu fi rutbati d-din` (And they are the ones who teach him, in the period of instruction, what must be taught, and who elevate him in the rank of religion). These three are the very ones who teach the infant Imam, during the period of instruction, all that must be taught him, and who raise him, the *muraqqun*, through the rank of religion. The word *muraqqun* derives from *raqa*, to ascend: one who ascends is *raqi*; one who causes another to ascend, who grants promotion, is *muraqqi* — from the form *raqqa, yuraqqi, muraqqi* — and from it comes *al-muraqqun*, with the *ya'* dropped, "those who elevate him." So the three are at once his teachers and the agents of his elevation in the ranks of the religion.

`wa-li-dhalika nitu bihi wa-stataru bi-stitarihi` (And for that reason they were bound to him and concealed by his concealment). For this they were joined to the Imam — the word *nitu* answering to the Urdu *nata*, relationship, in the passive: "they were connected to him" — and they became hidden along with his being hidden, sharing in his *satr*.

### The Succession of the Teachers and the Imam's Return as Benefactor

The continuity of this teaching across the deaths of the teachers themselves is then secured. `wa-kullama 'anat nuqlatu wahidin minhum lam yantaqil hatta aqama man yakhlufuhu fi maqamihi wa-yanubu manabihi ila an yablugha salamullah 'alayh` (And whenever the passing of one of them draws near, he does not pass until he has appointed one who will succeed him in his station and deputize in his place, until [the Imam] reaches [his maturity], peace upon him). Whenever the death of any one of the three — the *bab*, the *hujjah*, the *da'i al-balagh* — draws near, that one does not depart until he has appointed a successor to take his station and act as his deputy, so that the chain of teaching is never broken, and it continues thus until the Imam reaches his maturity.

And the timing of these deaths is not in the teachers' own hands. To the question whether the passing of all three lies in their own power, the answer is plain: it is not in their power, but comes when Allah decrees the time for it — yet still they do not pass until they have appointed a successor in their place. The decree of death belongs to Allah; the duty of appointing a successor belongs to the one who departs.

`wa-ya'udu mufidan li-hududihi, mufayyidan barakatihi wa-ta'yidatihi wa-mawaddihi` (And he returns as a bestower for his ranks, pouring out upon them his blessings, his supports, and his reinforcements). When the Imam has reached his maturity, the relation reverses: he who was taught now returns as *mufid* for his own hudud — for the very three who taught and elevated him — pouring out upon them his *barakat*, his *ta'yidat*, and his *mawadd*: his blessings, his supports, and his reinforcements. The infant who received now becomes the benefactor who bestows.

This reversal is illustrated in the case of Sayyidna Ja'far. His *mufid* had been Sayyidna Muhammad ibn al-Fayd; when Ja'far reached twenty years of age, Muhammad ibn al-Fayd entrusted all his affairs to him, and thereafter Ja'far became the *mufid*. The occasion is remembered: once, while Sayyidna Muhammad ibn al-Fayd was teaching and Ja'far was present, listening or studying, a matter arose that Muhammad ibn al-Fayd said he did not understand. At that moment Ja'far offered an interpretation — "perhaps what is meant by this is thus" — and gave some reference for it. Muhammad ibn al-Fayd was overjoyed and declared, `Al-an sarat al-mawaddu Ja'fariyyah` (Now the reinforcements have become Ja'fari) — now the support has passed to Ja'far. So the one who had received the support became, in his turn, the source of it. And this whole pattern, the text recalls, `kama sabaqa min al-qawl fi jaddayhi Muhammad wa-Ibrahim` (as has preceded in the saying concerning his two grandfathers, Muhammad and Ibrahim) — it had already been established in the case of the Imam's two grandfathers, the Prophet and Ibrahim, who likewise took from teachers and then became the benefactors of their ranks. This is the very pattern of "I took from five, and I delivered to five, and between me and my Lord are five," with which the chapter began.

### The Distribution of the Prophetic Forms

Within the pattern of the Prophet's taking and giving, a further distribution is recorded — the parceling out of the spiritual *suwar*, the forms, of the five preceding speaking-prophets among the Prophet's own household and among his teachers. `wa-tawalla 'l-hudud al-madhkurah li-tarbiyatihi` — the aforementioned hudud took charge of the upbringing — and the forms were assigned thus. The *surat* of Adam was given to Sayyidna Ubayy ibn Ka'b. The *surat* of Nuh was given to Fatima — and it is for this reason that she performed *nawha*, lamentation, for the form of Nuh, who wept much over his people, was hers. The *surat* of Ibrahim was given to 'Ali. The *surat* of Musa was given to Imam al-Hasan. And the *surat* of 'Isa was given to Imam al-Husayn.

The assignment of 'Isa's form to Husayn explains the likenesses between them. The first is that 'Isa remained six months in his mother's womb, and Husayn likewise remained six months in the womb. The second is that 'Isa was persecuted — *marqub*, pursued — and Husayn too was persecuted and martyred. The third, added later, is that the mothers of both were *batul*, chaste and wholly devoted to God: Maryam the mother of 'Isa, and Fatima the mother of Husayn — `ummuhuma batulat`, their two mothers are *batul*. And a fourth likeness belongs to what followed the martyrdom: as the apparition of 'Isa appeared three days after his being taken, so too the apparition of Imam Husayn appeared after his martyrdom — Sayyidna Ghulam Husayn mentions these likenesses, and the matter is recorded in the book *Ibtida' al-Da'wah*. Thus the Prophet, having received the realities of the five prophets, distributed their forms — to Ubayy, to Fatima, to 'Ali, to Hasan, and to Husayn — so that each bore the likeness of the prophet whose form he carried.

### The Imam, the Sun, and the Three Planets

The relation of the Imam to his three teaching-hudud is then given a cosmic figure. `wa-ha'ula'i 'l-hudud al-thalathah hum al-mawsulat al-thalathah min al-kawakib allati takhtassu bi-sh-shams` (And these three ranks are the three connections from the planets which are particular to the sun). The three ranks — the *bab*, the *hujjah*, and the *da'i al-balagh* — are connected to three planets that draw their light from the sun. These three planets are Saturn, Jupiter, and Mars. `wa-kawnuha muddatan lahum wa-liman dunaha min al-kawakib` (And it being a provider for them and for those below them among the planets) — the sun, which is feminine in the grammar, is the provider of support to these three and to the planets below them.

`idh al-Imam, 'alayhis salam, mawsulun bi-sh-shams wa-'l-mumidd lahu wa-li-l-hudud al-thalathah as-sabiqah 'alayhi fi 'l-wujud alladhina kanu mutawallina li-tarbiyatihi fi hali sigharihi wa-ta'limihi, thumma 'ada mufidan lahum mufayyidan barakatihi wa-ta'yidatihi 'alayhim` (Since the Imam, peace upon him, is connected to the sun and is the provider of support to him and to the three ranks preceding him in existence, who undertook his upbringing and teaching in his state of infancy; then he returned as a bestower for them, pouring out his blessings and supports upon them). The Imam is connected to the sun, and he is the *mumidd*, the giver of support, to the three ranks that preceded him in existence — the very three who undertook his upbringing and instruction in his infancy. Then, having matured, he returns as their *mufid*, pouring out upon them his blessings and his supports — the same reversal once more, now figured in the heavens, the sun feeding the planets that had, as it were, watched over its rising.

`wa-s-salamullah 'alayh mawsulu 'l-qalb fi 'l-jism fa-l-mumidd lima fawqahu min al-hawassi wa-lima tahtahu` (And he, peace upon him, is connected to the heart in the body, the provider of support to the senses above it and below it). The same relation is figured a third way, in the body: the Imam is connected to the heart, and as the heart in the body gives support to the senses both above it and below it, so the Imam provides support to the ranks above and beneath him. Sun in the heavens, heart in the body, Imam in the hierarchy — the one supplying life and support to all that depends upon it.

### The First Await the Last: The Great Gathering

The discourse turns to a saying about the order of the great gathering at the end. `yantazir bi-awwalikum quduma akhirikum` (He awaits, by means of your first ones, the arrival of your last ones). The wording is exact and must not be misread: it is not "your first ones await" (*yantazir awwalakum*) but "he awaits by means of your first ones" (*yantazir bi-awwalikum*) — those who have arrived first are kept waiting, on account of the first, until the last shall come. The first arrivers at the great assembly wait there for all the others to come and join, ready to welcome them; their final elevation is bound up with the coming of those who follow. As the first cannot make his ascent until he establishes another as *qa'im* for himself, so the elevation of the gathered is completed only when all have assembled. When at last all gather in the station of the *'Ashir*, the Tenth, and each has completed his *taraqqi*, the *'Ashir* awaits the coming of the last so that the assembly may become great and glorious in its fullness. The first await the last, by means of the first, so that the whole company may ascend together.

### The Concealment of Martyrdom and the Pending Retribution

A question was raised concerning the *hijabat*, the veiling that operates in the matter of martyrdom: what is the form of the *hijab* that comes — is it within the body, a covering laid over a prophet, or is it of some other nature? The doctrine, explained in other books as well, is this. The *nasut sharif*, the noble human form, and the pure soul are not subject to *qisas*, to retribution; the *surat*, the form, is pure and clean, and the *nafs*, the soul, is pure and clean. Upon what, then, does *qisas* fall? Only upon that which belongs to the earth — the earthly part. So when the time comes for retribution to be exacted, and some person is present in the world upon whom a pending claim of *qisas* rests, a *tahrik*, an impulse, is given to the *hijab*. The *mudabbir*, the administrator who effects this, is the Imam, peace upon him. He causes the form whose dues are still pending to come and become *muttasil*, joined, with the one from whom retribution is to be taken.

And in what shape does this pending form appear? In the shape of any human being who stands before the slayer. The slayer believes that he is martyring Imam Husayn — it appears to him that he is killing al-Husayn — whereas in truth he is not martyring Imam Husayn at all. It is rather that very *hijab*, the form with whom the pending retribution lies, which has come and become joined to the event. The one whose *qisas* was outstanding is present at that moment and takes his due, while the slayer imagines he strikes the Imam. The Imam, as administrator, connects the pending-retribution form to the event, so that the earthly debt is discharged upon the earthly part, and the noble form and pure soul remain untouched.

### Abbas, Muslim ibn Aqil, and the Forms That Appear as Others

The same mechanism is traced through the *fida'i*, the devoted ones, of Imam Husayn. The *fida'i* of Imam Husayn is Hazrat Abbas; and the *fida'i* of Hazrat Abbas is Janab Muslim ibn Aqil. In the case of Muslim ibn Aqil, the Imam connected to that event those with whom giving-and-taking of retribution was pending. The slayer thinks, "I am martyring Muslim ibn Aqil," whereas the one with whom his *qisas* was outstanding in the world was present at that moment, and it was that *nasut*, that form, which became the participant in the event. So too the *hijab* of Hazrat Abbas that came took the shape of Imam Husayn — for the form is a *shakl-e misal*, an exemplary form that can take any shape whatever; in any shape it can appear.

This phenomenon of one figure appearing as another — *shubbiha lahum*, "it was made to seem so to them" — is illustrated by several reported wonders. Of Mawlana Aminuddin it is told that when a gathering was held, several hosts each claimed that he had taken food with them on the same day: one said, "Janab Aminuddin ate with us"; another said, "He ate with us"; and so several individuals each insisted he had been present at their meal. Of Amir al-Mu'minin it is told that he led the funeral prayer for Salman al-Farsi in Mada'in, several miles — indeed a thousand miles — distant from Madina, going there and leading the prayer though he was elsewhere. And of 'Isa it is told that Judas was changed to resemble him — `shubbiha lahum`, the likeness was cast upon another — so that the one seized in his place was made to appear as 'Isa. In each case a single figure is present where, by ordinary reckoning, he could not be, or another is made to bear his likeness; the form is not bound by the limits that bind common bodies.

### "Ask Me Before You Lose Me": Salman, Uthman, and al-Baqi'

The matter of Salman's funeral in distant Mada'in is woven into a celebrated exchange. A man in Iraq was delivering discourses, and the claim was made on Ali's behalf, `saluni qabla an tafqiduni` (ask me before you lose me). A woman who had come from Madina rose and put a question: Amir al-Mu'minin left Madina and went to Mada'in and led the funeral prayer for Salman al-Farsi, performing the *janaza* and burying him — yet when 'Uthman was killed in Madina, while Amir al-Mu'minin was himself present in Madina, why did he not lead 'Uthman's funeral prayer?

The questioner was answered first with a counter-question. "O woman," he said, "from where have you come?" She said, "From Madina." He asked, "Did your husband give you permission to leave Madina and come to Iraq?" She replied with a question of her own, sharp and turning: "Tell me, how did 'A'isha go from Madina to Basra to make war? Did she go with the permission of the Prophet?" The question was full of turns, and he — wearing a very large *'amama* — took off the turban and cast it down. These are historical incidents.

The true reason for Ali's not leading 'Uthman's prayer is then given. The Muslims did not permit 'Uthman to be buried in Jannat al-Baqi'. Those who had performed the washing — among them Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr and others — could find no place for him within al-Baqi', for the Muslims would not allow it. There was a graveyard adjacent to al-Baqi', the graveyard of the Jews, and 'Uthman was buried there. Only later was that graveyard expanded and incorporated into al-Baqi'. In earlier times, when the *ziyarat* was performed, the graves of Janab Fatima bint Asad and Janab 'Uthman ibn Maz'un — Mawlana 'Uthman ibn Maz'un — lay in Jannat al-Baqi' but outside the wall, beyond the fence, the two graves together; and the fence was afterward built so as to bring them within. The reason for 'Uthman's burial in the Jewish graveyard lay partly in such connections of lineage, and there were several reasons besides; in those days, moreover, women were not permitted to visit the graves or to enter Jannat al-Baqi' — and this remains the case even now. So that the *ziyarat* might be performed, with the women of the party present, a large building stood opposite; they climbed to the top of it, and from there performed the *ziyarat* of the graves that lay before it.

### The Permissibility of Companion-Names

A point of practice closes the discussion, arising from the mention of 'Uthman ibn Maz'un. He was a very special companion of the Prophet, very pious and God-fearing. When 'Ali had sons born to him — among them Hazrat Abbas ibn 'Ali — his fourth son he named 'Uthman, and having named him, he said, "I have named him after Hazrat 'Uthman ibn Maz'un, the companion." For no other name did he make such a declaration; for this name he made it expressly, attaching it to the pious companion. From this the ruling follows: names drawn from such companions may be kept. From that tradition the name Abu Bakr may be kept; the name 'Abd al-Rahman, and many names of that kind, may be kept. The qualification is the worth of the one after whom the name is taken — as 'Ali named his son after the pious 'Uthman ibn Maz'un, so the keeping of such names is permissible. It is recalled in this connection that a *da'i* named Abu Sufyan also passed — Abu Sufyan al-Hulwani and Abu Sufyan al-Khalafi, the two whom 'Ubayd Allah al-Mahdi had sent forth from Mahdiyya — bearing the name Abu Sufyan; for the name, taken from a worthy bearer, carries no taint of the one ill-remembered by it.

## The Dawn and the Straight Path

The closing of these lectures turns from exposition to recitation. Having traveled through the inner sciences, the gathering returns to the words of the Qur'an itself — first to the oath of the dawn and the summons of the reassured soul, and then to the opening of the Book and its prayer for the straight path. The teaching here is the Qur'an recited in full, and so it is set down here whole, in the name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.

### The Oath of the Dawn

The recitation opens with Surat al-Fajr, which swears its first oaths upon the dawn and the sacred nights, calling the soul to reflect on the rise and fall of the mighty:

`Wal-fajr. Wa layalin 'ashr. Wash-shaf'i wal-watr. Wal-layli idha yasr. Hal fi dhalika qasamul li dhi hijr.` — "By the dawn, and by ten nights, and by the even and the odd, and by the night when it passes. Is there not in that an oath for one of understanding?"

The oath sworn, the surah turns to the nations of old, recalling how the Lord dealt with those who transgressed and filled the earth with corruption — 'Aad of Iram of the lofty pillars, the like of which was never created in the land; Thamud, who carved out the rocks in the valley; and Pharaoh, owner of the stakes:

`Alam tara kayfa fa'ala rabbuka bi 'aad. Irama dhatil 'imaad. Allati lam yukhlaq mithluha fil-bilad. Wa thamoodalladhina jab-us-sakhra bil-wad. Wa fir'awna dhil-awtad. Alladhina taghaw fil-bilad. Fa aktharu fihal-fasad. Fa sabba 'alayhim rabbuka sawta 'adhab. Inna rabbaka la bil-mirsad.` — "Have you not considered how your Lord dealt with 'Aad — with Iram of the lofty pillars, the like of which were not created in the land — and with Thamud, who carved out the rocks in the valley, and with Pharaoh, owner of the stakes, all of whom transgressed within the lands and increased therein the corruption? So your Lord poured upon them a scourge of punishment. Indeed, your Lord is ever in observation."

From the fate of nations the surah turns to the soul of every person, exposing how a man misreads both honor and hardship — taking ease as a sign of his worth and want as a sign of his disgrace — while neglecting the orphan, the poor, and just inheritance, and loving wealth with an immense love:

`Fa ammal-insanu idha mab-talahu rabbuhu fa akramahu wa na''amahu fa yaqoolu rabbi akraman. Wa amma idha mab-talahu fa qadara 'alayhi rizqahu fa yaqoolu rabbi ahanan. Kalla bal la tukrimoonal-yateem. Wa la tahaaddoona 'ala ta'amil-miskeen. Wa ta'kuloonat-turatha aklal-lamma. Wa tuhibboonal-mala hubban jamma.` — "And as for man, when his Lord tries him and is generous to him and favors him, he says, 'My Lord has honored me.' But when He tries him and restricts his provision, he says, 'My Lord has humiliated me.' No! But you do not honor the orphan, and you do not encourage one another to feed the poor, and you consume inheritance, devouring it altogether, and you love wealth with immense love."

Then the scene breaks open to the Day itself, when the earth is leveled, pounded and crushed, and the Lord comes with the angels rank upon rank, and Hell is brought into view — the Day on which a man at last remembers, though the remembrance can no longer avail him, and wishes he had sent something forth for his life:

`Kalla idha dukkatil-ardu dakkan dakka. Wa ja'a rabbuka wal-malaku saffan saffa. Wa jee'a yawma'idhim bi jahannam. Yawma'idhiy-yatadhakkarul-insanu wa anna la hudh-dhikra. Yaqoolu ya laytani qaddamtu li hayati. Fa yawma'idhil-la yu'adhdhibu 'adhabahu ahad. Wa la yoothiqu wathaqahu ahad.` — "No! When the earth has been leveled — pounded and crushed — and your Lord has come and the angels, rank upon rank, and Hell is brought into view that Day; on that Day man will remember, but how will the remembrance avail him? He will say, 'Oh, I wish I had sent forth good for my life.' So on that Day none will punish as severely as His punishment, and none will bind as severely as His binding."

And the surah closes — as the whole journey of these lectures closes — upon the reassured soul, al-nafs al-mutma'inna, called home to its Lord, well-pleased and pleasing, gathered among His servants and into His Paradise:

`Ya ayyatuhan-nafsul-mutma'innah. Irji'i ila rabbiki radiyatam-mardiyyah. Fadhkuli fi 'ibadi. Wadhkuli jannati.` — "O reassured soul, return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing to Him. And enter among My righteous servants, and enter My Paradise."

### The Prayer for the Straight Path

After the dawn and the homecoming of the soul, the recitation turns to the opening of the Book, Surat al-Fatiha — the praise of the Lord of the worlds, the Most Gracious and Most Merciful, Master of the Day of Judgment, and the prayer of those who worship Him alone and seek help from Him alone to be guided to the straight path:

`Al-hamdu lillahi Rabb al-'alamin. Al-Rahman al-Rahim. Maliki Yawm al-Din. Iyyaka na'budu wa iyyaka nasta'in. Ihdina al-sirat al-mustaqim. Sirat alladhina an'amta 'alayhim, ghayr al-maghdubi 'alayhim wa la al-dallin.` — "All praise is for Allah, the Lord of the worlds, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful, Master of the Day of Judgment. It is You we worship and You we ask for help. Guide us to the straight path — the path of those upon whom You have bestowed favor, not of those who have earned Your anger nor of those who are astray."

So the lectures end where the believer's every prayer begins: with the asking for guidance, ihdina al-sirat al-mustaqim, upon the path of those whom God has favored, and away from those who earned His anger, al-maghdubi 'alayhim, and those who went astray, al-dallin.
