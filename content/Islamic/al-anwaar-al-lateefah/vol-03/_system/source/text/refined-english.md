## The Pillar of Light and the Sixth Speaker

### Jibra'il as a Part of the Pillar of Light

The whole purpose of what follows is to establish a single luminous truth: that Jibra'il, peace be upon him, is himself a part of the Pillar of Light (amud al-nur). The discussion of the angel, his form, and his descent upon the Prophet is not a digression into angelology; it is a continuation of the explanation of the Pillar of Light and its origin (mabda') and return (ma'ad), and it serves to clarify the manner in which the luminous reality reaches the sixth Speaker of the cycle.

This is confirmed and given further clarification by the saying: `an al-Nāṭiq al-dawr ṣalawātullāhi 'alayhi kāna fī ibtidā'i amrihi idhā 'āyana sūrat al-malak alladhī yatajallā lahū al-musammā bi-Jibrā'īl, ghashiya 'alayhi min 'aẓm nūrihi alladhī yu'āyinuhū, ḥattā khālat al-aḍdād wa ahl al-fikr wa ahl al-kufr wa al-'inād annahū junna, wa anna dhālik al-ghashyah ṣara'` — that the Speaker of the cycle, the blessings of Allah be upon him, in the very beginning of his affair, when he would behold the form of the angel that manifested before him, the form named Jibra'il, would be overcome from the greatness of the light that he beheld. So overwhelming was that brilliance that a state of swooning would descend upon him, and he would fall down unconscious from the sheer magnitude of the light that confronted his sight.

The Speaker (al-Natiq) of the sixth era is Muhammad al-Mustafa, the blessings of Allah be upon him. At the dawn of his prophetic mission, when the angelic form appeared to him, the greatness of its light was such that he would be overpowered by it. The Arabic word ghashiya names precisely this: he was overcome, he fell, his outer senses were eclipsed before a light too immense for the body to sustain. This was not weakness; it was the natural response of a created form encountering the overflowing radiance of a higher rank, the radiance of one who is himself a portion of the Pillar of Light. (S276)

This brings us to the matter of how that fainting was interpreted, for the same event drew opposite verdicts from opposite men. To the adversaries — to the people of mere reasoning, the people of disbelief, and the people of stubborn opposition — the Prophet's being overcome looked like a sickness of the body or the mind. They said a jinn had taken hold of him; they said the swooning was junna, possession, or sara', epilepsy, a falling sickness. The same outward sign that the unbelievers read as affliction was read by another man entirely as the very signature of prophethood. (S277)

That man was Waraqa bin Nawfal, may Allah's mercy be upon him. He was no ordinary witness: `wa kāna 'āliman kāhinan muntaẓiran li-ẓuhūr al-Nāṭiq al-sādis` — he was a scholar and a soothsayer, and he was awaiting the appearance of the sixth Speaker. Living in the age that still belonged to the dispensation of 'Isa, peace be upon him, Waraqa was watching and waiting for the rising of the sixth Natiq, Muhammad al-Mustafa, the blessings of Allah be upon him. Where the obstinate saw only an ailment, Waraqa, equipped with knowledge and with the expectancy of a man who knew the signs, recognized in the fainting the unmistakable mark of the descent of revelation. He understood that this was not a body seized by a jinn but a vessel overwhelmed by the light of the angel — and through the angel, by the Pillar of Light itself. (S277)

### Waraqa's Test and the Modesty of Angels

Because Waraqa knew the difference between an angel and a jinn, he was able to furnish Khadijah, may Allah be pleased with her, with a precise test by which the nature of her husband's visitor might be discerned. He said to her: `idhā 'āyanti dhālik al-amr fa-ajā'ahu fa-atrukīhi 'alā fakhidhik al-yumnā wa qūlī lahū hal tarā shay'an` — when you observe that matter coming upon him, when the visitation arrives, then seat him upon your right thigh and ask him, "Do you see anything?" Khadijah was to repeat the procedure with deliberate stages: `fa-atrukīhi 'alā fakhidhik al-yusrā wa qūlī lahū hal tarā shay'an, thumma ḥawwilihī 'alā sadrīki wa sa'alīhi kamā sa'alti awwalan, thumma ikshifī ra'saki wa sa'alīhi kamā sa'alti awwalan` — then seat him upon your left thigh and ask him as before; then turn him toward your breast and ask him as you had asked the first time; then uncover your head, uncover the hair of your head, and ask him once more in the same way. (S278)

The diagnostic hinge of the whole test was the response that would follow the uncovering: `fa-in kāna majnūnan fa-yaqūlu na'am, fa-in kāna nabiyyan mursalan Nāṭiqan fa-yaqūlu mā arā shay'an, li'annahū wallā 'an 'awratiki` — for if the one present with him were a jinn, possessing him, the visitor would say "yes," he would acknowledge what was uncovered; but if he were a Prophet, a sent messenger, a Speaker, then his visitor would say, "I see nothing," because he turns away from your private parts. The whole logic rests upon a single truth about the nature of the higher beings: a true angel, the messenger of the true Natiq, will avert his gaze from what must be concealed and will report that he sees nothing, whereas a jinn, having no such reverence, will admit to seeing the head and hair when they are uncovered. The angel's modesty is itself the proof of his angelic nature. By this means, Khadijah could establish with certainty that the form attending upon her husband was no jinn but the bearer of revelation, the very angel who is a part of the Pillar of Light. (S278)

From this principle — that angels do not look upon private parts — flows a ruling of the sacred law and the explanation of a famous incident from the early history of the community. As for the ruling: it is for this reason that a woman who prays without covering her hair has her prayer rendered unacceptable. The hair, in the context of prayer, falls under what must be veiled from the gaze of the attending angels, just as the awrah must be veiled; and since the angels turn away from what is uncovered, the connection of acceptance is broken where the covering is neglected. The same principle that lets Khadijah test her visitor also governs the conditions of a valid prayer. (S279)

And it is the same principle that explains the survival of 'Amr ibn al-'As at the Battle of Siffin. When Amir al-Mu'minin, the blessings of Allah be upon him, pursued 'Amr ibn al-'As to slay him, `kashafa 'an saw'atihi` — 'Amr uncovered his private parts. He had heard, by way of Khadijah's report of Waraqa's teaching, that the angels do not look upon the private parts; and knowing that 'Ali was connected to the Pillar of Light through the attending angelic reality, he reasoned that by exposing himself he would cause that luminous connection to avert its gaze and thereby spare his life. 'Ali turned his face away, and 'Amr escaped death. The poet Abu Firas, may Allah have mercy on him, alluded to the shamefulness of this stratagem when he said: `wa lā khayra fī daf' al-radā bi-madhammatin kamā raddahū yawman bi-saw'atihi 'Amr` — there is no good in averting destruction by means of a disgrace, just as 'Amr one day repelled it with his private parts. The verse carries its own judgment: it is better to be slain than to ward off death through an act of shame. To avert one's destruction by such a disgrace, as 'Amr did, is no honor but a stain. Yet the incident illustrates, with grim clarity, the operative law: that the luminous connection turns away from what is uncovered, and that this turning-away is real enough to be exploited even by a man like 'Amr. (S279)

### The Severing of the Connection and the Reality of Revelation

What unites the test, the ruling, and the incident at Siffin is a single doctrine concerning the Pillar of Light itself. The matter under discussion has been throughout the Pillar of Light: that in a state of nakedness, the Pillar of Light does not come, and where the private parts appear before it, its connection is cut off. The discussion now turns explicitly to the angel, for it is through Jibra'il that the Prophet's link to the Pillar of Light is constituted. This is a connection of light, an ittisal: the Prophet possesses an ittisal with the Pillar of Light, and the means of that ittisal is Jibra'il, peace be upon him. The angel is the one who brings the Pillar of Light, the one who establishes and conveys the connection with it. (S280)

Hence Jibra'il in this context is identical, in function, with the Pillar of Light: he is the one who carries it, the one who establishes the link to it. And the connection, real and luminous as it is, can be severed: the Pillar of Light is cut off when private parts appear before it. The connection that was joined becomes disjoined; the established ittisal is broken. This is why the same modesty that proves the angel an angel also governs the validity of prayer and explains 'Amr's escape — in every case it is the integrity of the luminous connection that is at stake, the connection that the appearance of the awrah severs. (S280)

The Noble Book itself alludes to this Pillar of Light. As the text proceeds — `al-Faṣl al-rābi' min al-bāb al-thānī min surādiq al-thānī, wa qad ramaza al-kitāb al-karīm bi-hādhā al-umūr, wa huwa qawluhu ta'ālā: yunazzil al-rūḥ min amrihi 'alā man yashā'u min 'ibādihi` — the fourth chapter of the second section of the second pavilion declares that the Noble Book has alluded to these matters in the saying of the Exalted: "He sends down the Spirit (al-ruh) by His command upon whomever He wills of His servants." The Spirit (al-ruh) here named is the Pillar of Light, sent down by divine command upon the chosen among His servants. This descent is the same reality named elsewhere in the descent of the angels and the Spirit; and the Spirit is the means by which the connection of the Pillar of Light is established. (S281)

For this Spirit is also the very reality of revelation: `wa huwa ayḍan ḥaqīqat al-waḥy` — it is the reality of revelation (haqiqat al-wahy). The Pillar of Light itself is revelation. From it knowledge flowed and light became manifest; and it emerged from the word of `Lā ilāha` — from the testimony of the oneness of Allah. That same word is called light, the flowing light (al-nur al-sari), the manifest light. The Pillar of Light, the descending Spirit, the reality of revelation, and the luminous word of tawhid are, in this teaching, names for a single overflowing reality, the wellspring from which revelation pours upon the hearts of the chosen. (S281)

### The Three Modes of Revelation: Jadd, Fath, and Khayal

That overflowing reality reaches its recipients not in one manner only but in three. The text continues: revelation flows in three kinds (alladhi yajri 'ala thalathat aqsam), and the Quran establishes this in the verse `wa mā kāna li-basharin an yukallimahullāhu illā waḥyan aw min warā'i ḥijābin aw yursila rasūlan fa-yūḥiya bi-idhnihi mā yashā'` — "And it is not for any human being that Allah should speak to him except by revelation (wahy), or from behind a veil (min wara'i hijab), or by sending a messenger (rasul) who reveals, by His permission, what He wills." Three avenues, then, and no more: revelation, the veil, the messenger. And these three correspond exactly to three ranks that stand above every recipient of the divine address — the ranks of Jadd, Fath, and Khayal. (S282)

To grasp this, one must understand the structure that obtains above every Imam, and indeed above every Natiq, every Prophet, every 'Ayn 'Azimah, and every Wali. Above each of them stand three individuals of a higher rank, through whom the commands of the One above are conveyed downward. The first is Jadd, the second is Fath, the third is Khayal. These are the three intermediaries that stand between Allah and the Imam — for the converse is not direct, but mediated through these three luminous ranks. They are the channels by which the divine commands descend, whether the commands of the sacred law in the time of a law-giving Natiq, or the commands of the spiritual path (tariqah), or the affairs of prophethood, or the affairs of spiritual Imamate — for whatever mission a recipient is entrusted with, the commands that govern it descend through these same three ranks, each according to the dignity of the office it serves. (S283)

The first and highest of the three is the rank of Jadd. `Al-awwal minhā rutbat al-Jadd, alladhī kallafa-hullāhu ta'ālā al-waḥy` — the first of them is the rank of Jadd, to whom Allah the Exalted has assigned the office of revelation. Jadd is the rank that operates by wahy, by revelation in the strict sense, and its grace flows from the Holy Spirit (Ruh al-Quds) without any intermediary, connected at every time and moment to the chosen messenger (al-mab'uth al-mukhtar). This is the supreme channel: there is no intermediary between Jadd and the Ruh al-Quds. The commands that reach Jadd reach him directly, unmediated, from the 'Ayn 'Azimah, the Mighty Source; and Jadd in turn is joined to the one who has been sent upon a mission, the chosen one — such as the Prophet, and such as every Imam of the time. Because Jadd receives without an intermediary, the rank of revelation is the highest of the three; what comes by wahy comes by the most direct and exalted route. The Ruh al-Quds from which Jadd draws may be understood as the Nafs 'Azimah, the Mighty Soul, and it can also be understood as the Tenth — but in either case the defining feature of Jadd is the absence of any mediation between it and that holy source. (S283)

These three — Jadd, Fath, and Khayal — are not bodies but realities: abstract realities, belonging to the world of the pure intellects, marked by oneness and abstraction (wahid, tajrid). They stand above the Imam in rank, and it is through them that the Imam receives his support from the 'Ayn 'Azimah, the Imam of Imams, together with the twelve Hujjats: all of it reaches the Imam by way of Jadd, Fath, and Khayal. The arrangement is universal and proportionate. Whatever the degree of the recipient, his three higher ranks are the three degrees immediately above his own: for an Imam of the first degree, the three ranks before that; for one of the seventh degree, the three ranks above the seventh; for the Qa'im, the three ranks above his. And the arrangement is particular to each recipient who exists simultaneously. When an Imam and a Messenger are present in the same age — as with Mawlana 'Ali and the Messenger of Allah — their Jadd, Fath, and Khayal are distinct: 'Ali's are his own, and the Messenger's are his own. (S283)

It must be stressed that these three higher ranks are not the physical lineage of the recipient. The physical grandfather, uncle, and father of the Prophet — 'Abd al-Muttalib, Abu Talib, and 'Abdullah — illustrate by analogy the relation of grandfather (jadd), father (walid), and the one descended, but the true Jadd, Fath, and Khayal are of a higher rank altogether, not bound to any particular physical kinship. The same holds for every recipient: for the Muntakha, the chosen one; for the Awliya'; for the 'Ayn 'Azimah — for each of these, above his own station stand the three ranks of Jadd, Fath, and Khayal, of a rank loftier than his, through which everything is received. This is necessary for every one of them without exception. And of the three, `wa huwa 'alā al-marātib` — Jadd is the highest of the ranks; the rank of Jadd stands above the rest. (S283)

The second mode is the rank of Fath. `Wa al-thānī rutbat al-Fath alladhī huwa min warā'i ḥijābin, wa huwa idrāk al-ashyā' bi-l-fikrat al-ṣāfiyah wa al-amthāl al-maḍrūbah` — and the second in rank is Fath, which is the mode of "from behind a veil," and it consists in the perception of things through pure thought (al-fikrat al-safiyah) and through struck parables (al-amthal al-madrubah). Fath is the channel that conveys meaning not openly but within the veil of an image or an example, so that the one who is meant to understand understands, while the matter remains screened to the rest. (S284)

The Quran works in this very manner again and again. The stories of Musa and the others, wherever they appear, are conveyed by struck parables: the matter is explained through an example. So, in the account of Khidr and Musa, building a wall and boring a hole in the boat are amthal madrubah — outwardly one thing, inwardly another, intelligible to the one equipped to read them. By "knowledge," water is meant; the parable carries a meaning beneath its surface. The principle may be illustrated plainly. If one says, "Look, a palm tree is approaching," the words on their surface speak of a tree, but their meaning is that a tall man is coming. If one says, "A Dunlop tire is coming," the meaning is that a stout man approaches. If one says, "A swift horse is coming from the front," the meaning is that a rider is coming, riding fast. The child who has not the key will ask, "Where is the tire?" — but between two who share the understanding, the veiled meaning passes intact and action follows upon it. So it is with Fath: it is perception through pure thought, the giving of a ta'wil, an inner interpretation; the command comes within the veil of words, not announced plainly in public, but conveyed within the veiling garment of the parable to the one prepared to receive it. (S284)

The third mode is the rank of Khayal. `Wa al-thālith rutbat al-Khayal, wa huwa al-malak al-mursal, fa-yūḥiya bi-idhnihi mā yashā'u, al-mumaththal basharan sawiyyan, dhū al-quwwah allatī tuwāṣiluhu min Dār al-Quds` — and the third is the rank of Khayal, who is the angel that is sent, revealing by His permission what He wills, appearing in the likeness of a well-proportioned man (basharan sawiyyan), possessed of a power that connects him from the Abode of Holiness (Dar al-Quds). This perception comes in either of two forms: `ammā qawlan bil-sam'i, wa ammā tashakhkhuṣan bi-ru'yat al-'ayn` — either as a speech that is heard by the ear, or as a form made manifest and seen by the eye. The voice may be heard alone, or the form may be seen, and both may occur together. (S285)

The reality of Khayal is illustrated by incidents transmitted within the community. Sayyidna 'Ali used to relate that, in the time of the court of Aurangzeb, when he was governor of Gujarat, there occurred a debate concerning religion, and an order was given to take two men outside and put them to death. As they were being led away, two horsemen appeared before them; and Sayyidna Muhammad 'Izzuddin manifested in that form and addressed the two, saying: `lā takhāfā wa-lā taḥzanā, innanī ma'akumā asma'u wa arā` — "Do not fear and do not grieve, for I am with you both, hearing and seeing." A figure appeared, and his voice was heard, and the two were delivered: brought into the marketplace, then released amid the commotion there and told to go, so that they came out unharmed. The appearance of the form and the audible address are precisely what is meant by the rank of Khayal — a manifestation perceived by the eye and the ear at once. (S285)

Another such incident is related of the first of Rajab. The son of Qutb-i Shahid — Qutb bin Dawud, whose shrine is well known — had been put to death by Aurangzeb, his corpse left to be torn at by dogs. Sayyidna 'Ali Sahib was urged by all to leave Ahmedabad for some other place, but he answered, "Until I receive the command from my Master, I will not depart from here." On the night of the first of Rajab he stood in prayer, and during the prayer the command reached him that he should depart. Thus he heard a voice conveying a command — that he should now leave this place. At the time of the Fajr prayer he reached the mosque of the common people, prayed the dawn prayer at its earliest hour, and departed for the countryside of Gujarat, where he remained for six months. When Aurangzeb's men came searching to seize and kill him, the household directed them to the mosque, and the Imam there told the pursuers, "He prayed the prayer here and left a little while ago." So they searched behind him in vain while he had already reached the countryside. Here, as before, the command came as a heard voice — and this, too, is a manifestation in the rank of Khayal: a vision that may appear, or a voice that may be heard, by which the divine direction is conveyed to its recipient. (S285)

### The Flowing Limit from the Spirit of Holiness

What, then, is the source of the power that animates Khayal? The text answers: `wa hādhihi al-quwwah allatī tuwāṣiluhu min Dār al-Quds, wa huwa al-ḥadd al-sārī min Rūḥ al-Quds, fa-idhā al-Quds mubda' al-awwal kamā sabaqa bihi al-qawl` — and this is the power that connects him from the Abode of Holiness, and it is the flowing limit (al-hadd al-sari) from the Spirit of Holiness (Ruh al-Quds); and the Holiness in question is the First Originated (al-mubda' al-awwal), as has preceded in the discourse. The power that joins Khayal to its source comes from the Dar al-Quds, the Abode of Holiness, and it is a flowing limit issuing from the Ruh al-Quds. And the Ruh al-Quds is the First Intellect. (S286)

From the First Intellect there extends a chain, and from this chain the luminous limit is constituted — the same luminous limit through which Khayal is either heard as a voice or seen as a form. The whole apparatus, therefore, resolves into a single luminous continuity: the First Intellect at its summit, the flowing limit descending from it, the Abode of Holiness as the immediate source of the angel's power, and Khayal as the terminus at which that flowing reaches perception. It is one chain of light, and the angel sent in the rank of Khayal is its visible or audible end. (S286)

### "The Flowing Title Became Attached to the Flowing"

To this very Pillar of Light the second Da'i, Sayyidna Ibrahim bin al-Husayn al-Hamidi, may Allah sanctify their souls, gave a memorable testimony. `Wa ilā hādhā al-'umūd ishārah Sayyidnā Ibrāhīm bin al-Ḥusayn qaddasallāhu rūḥahumā, qāla 'inda naqlatihi Sayyidnā 'Alī bin al-Ḥusayn: an al-laqab al-sārī ta'allaqa bil-jārī` — and toward this Pillar there is an indication from our master Ibrahim bin al-Husayn, may Allah sanctify their souls, who said, at the passing of our master 'Ali bin al-Husayn: "The flowing title became attached to the flowing" (al-laqab al-sari ta'allaqa bi'l-jari). At the time when Sayyidna 'Ali bin al-Husayn passed from this world, Sayyidna Ibrahim bin al-Husayn al-Hamidi declared this: that the flowing title became united with the flowing. The meaning is an indication toward the Pillar of Light — that upon his passing, the Pillar of Light drew him into itself. (S287)

To round out the historical setting: Sayyidna 'Ali bin al-Husayn had been appointed by Sayyidna Dhu'ayb, and upon his passing Sayyidna Ibrahim bin al-Husayn al-Hamidi succeeded to the office of Da'i. It was at that juncture that Ibrahim al-Hamidi spoke his luminous phrase. And the text adds: `wa lam yulā'im jānib al-ṭabī'ah 'ālī wa-l-dānī` — that he was not suited to the side of nature, neither in its high aspect nor in its low; the world of nature did not befit him, for his form had been drawn upward into the Pillar of Light. The flowing title — the luminous designation — attached itself to the flowing reality, and the departed was gathered into the stream of light. (S287)

### The Manifest Presence and the Gate of the Veil

The text then describes what befalls the one who is thus drawn into the flowing grace: `lammā ittaṣala bil-'ināyat al-sāriyah, warada 'alā ahl al-na'īm... ilā ḥaḍrat al-ẓāhirah, majma' al-ajzā' al-ṭāhirah... al-muntaẓir li-wujūdihim wa wurūd amthālihim wa atbā'ihim wa ashbāhihim, ḥattā idhā wafā al-'adad wa balaghat muddat al-amad, ẓaharat al-qubbat al-'abbāsiyyah` — when he became connected with the flowing grace, he came forth to the people of bliss, in the radiant, sublime, manifest presence, the gathering-place of the pure parts (majma' al-ajza' al-tahirah), toward which the whole of existence in the present world is journeying, awaiting their coming-into-being and the arrival of their likes, followers, and counterparts — until, when the number was fulfilled and the term of the duration was reached, the Abbasid dome (al-qubbat al-'abbasiyyah) appeared. (S288)

This manifest presence is the gathering-place of the luminous spirits, the assembly of the ready souls; to it the departed one ascended when he was joined to the flowing grace. He came to dwell among the people of bliss in that radiant gathering, which is the destination toward which all of present existence is moving, and which awaits the arrival of those who are like its members, who follow them and resemble them. And there is a sequence to these abodes: when the number is complete in one of the paradises, the next paradise appears. `Bi-anwār al-shā'sha'ah wa tajallā bi-hādhihi al-'umūd, wa ẓahara bil-ḥaqq fī awān al-ẓuhūr, fī ḥaḍīrat al-quds 'inda bāb al-ḥijāb` — by the radiant lights, and it was manifested through this Pillar of Light, and it appeared with truth at the time of manifestation, in the enclosure of holiness (hadirat al-quds), at the gate of the veil (bab al-hijab). When the number of one paradise is fulfilled and its term is reached, the next paradise manifests by the radiant lights and is made to appear through this Pillar of Light. (S288)

The "radiant lights" are the luminous, radiant spirits — the souls that are made ready and that reach the Pillar of Light, the paradise of the Pillar of Light. They are all related to the Pillar of Light, the place where the flowing light is, where the flowing knowledge is; and their connection is with the gate of the veil — the veil of the Imam of the time, at its gate. The sacred enclosure (hadirat al-quds) denotes the Imam's presence; yet the one who arrives does not arrive within that presence itself, but reaches as far as the gate of the veil. This is the station named Jannat al-Ma'wa, the Garden of Abode: the soul drawn into the Pillar of Light comes to the gate of the inner Imam's veil and there finds its abode. (S288)

A subtle clarification must accompany this, lest the Pillar of Light be misconceived. `Fa-lammā istaqarra dhālika ayyuhā al-akh al-mu'min, fa'lam anna hādhā al-'umūd` — so when this is established, O believing brother, know that this Pillar is wholly spiritual, not physical. It is not physical light, for physical light is a dense and gross thing; the real light, the light of the Pillar of Light, is spiritual luminosity. Luminous here means precisely that it is not physical brightness but a subtle, spiritual radiance. Even so, this spiritual light is not beyond perception; the luminous reality can become manifest. Just as the form of Sayyidna Muhammad 'Izzuddin appeared, as Sayyidna 'Ali Sahib related, so the spiritual light can be apprehended — Sayyidna Muhammad 'Izzuddin standing in the rank of Khayal, Sayyidna Ja'far in the rank of Fath, and Sayyidna Sulayman in the rank of Jadd. The three ranks are thus borne by particular luminous personages, and through them the spiritual light becomes accessible to perception even while remaining spiritual in its essence. (S285)

### The Ascent of the Rational Soul

What, then, becomes of the soul that journeys toward this Pillar? Is the departing rational soul (nafs natiqah) merely stored within the Pillar of Light, or is it taken up into it? The answer is that it joins: a connection is established. The light of the departing soul is joined to the Pillar of Light. If the soul has had a good end, then its connection is bound to the Pillar of Light, and its virtues become attached to it, each according to its portion. `Al-nafs al-natiqah` that ascends with a praiseworthy end does not merely lie inert in some receptacle; it is connected, and its lights and merits are woven into the Pillar of Light in the measure that is its due. The reward of the soul is precisely this joining of its luminosity to the luminous reality, in the degree to which its share entitles it. (S289)

### Origination and Emanation: al-Ibda' and al-Inbi'ath

The discourse now turns to the structure of the worlds and the manner in which rank gives rise to rank. The virtuous person — the second ibda'iyyah, who stands at the rank of the Imam — established his mission until he brought forth from it a son who deputizes in his place and stands in his position. `Salāmu'llāhi 'alayh` — peace of Allah be upon him — established his mission until he desired to bring forth from it his son, such a son as would be his deputy (na'ib manab) and his successor (qa'im maqam). This son is designated al-inbi'ath, the Emanated, just as the father is designated al-ibda', the Origination. The father is called al-ibda' because whatever exists in this lower world stands as a counterpart to al-ibda' (the Origination) and al-inbi'ath (the Emanation) in the greater, luminous world. (S290)

The two names answer to one another like an original and its issue. We call the originating one, in one sense, al-ibda', and we call his son al-inbi'ath, the munba'ith, the Emanated. These belong to the world of nature ('alam al-tabi'ah), while their luminous counterparts — al-mubda' and al-munba'ith in the world of the intellects — belong to the luminous world ('alam al-nurani); the one is the worldly image of the other. The son is named al-inbi'ath because he is, as it were, drawn out of the father as a letter is drawn out of its source. Just as, when we write the letter alif and pronounce it, we say "one," yet within the very utterance there is the sound issuing from it — so the son is issued from the father, the second perfection (kamal-i thani) attained by beholding the Exalted Originator (mubda'-i ta'ala) and the First Originated (al-mubda' al-awwal). The son is named al-inbi'ath just as the father is named al-ibda', because both, in this world, are counterparts to al-ibda' and al-inbi'ath in the world of the intellect. (S290)

This correspondence is fixed and recurring. `Wa-kawnu hādhā al-walad awwala munba'ithin fī hādhā al-'ālam` — the being of this son is the first Emanated (munba'ith al-awwal) in this world; he is the first to be emanated in the world of nature. And `wa-kadhālika kullu maqāmin ba'dahumā fa-yuqālu lahā al-ibdā'` — and likewise every rank that comes after those two is called al-ibda'. So in the world of nature, the second ibda'iyyah stands at the rank of the Imam and is the counterpart of the First Originated (al-mubda' al-awwal), while his son stands at the rank of the first Emanated (munba'ith al-awwal). Every Imam, within his own circle, occupies the rank of the First Intellect, and the first within that circle is at the rank of al-ibda', while the sons of the Imam are at the rank of al-inbi'ath. After these two — after the second ibda'iyyah and his son — every subsequent rank is given the same name and is called al-ibda'; `wa-kullu man yastakhriju min da'watihi alladhī huwa waladuhu fa-huwa al-inbi'āth` — and everyone who is brought out from his mission, who is his son, is the al-inbi'ath, on account of the correspondence and fittingness that obtains between them. Origination and Emanation, then, are not single events but a repeating pattern that propagates down every rank, each father an ibda' to the inbi'ath he extracts from his own mission. (S291)

### The Passage to the Rank of the Tenth Intellect

Having brought forth his son and conferred upon him the office, the virtuous person does not remain at his former station. `Thumma naṣṣa 'alā waladihi hādhā bi'l-imāmah, wa-intaqala ilā dār karāmat Allāh Ta'ālā, fa-kāna khālifan fī rutbat al-'aql al-'āshir` — then he conferred upon his son the ruling of Imamate (nass), and passed on to the abode of the generosity of Allah the Exalted, and became a successor in the rank of the Tenth Intellect (rutbat al-'aql al-'ashir), for the governance (tadbir) of the world of nature. After conferring the nass upon his son, that is, he ascended to the rank of the Tenth; he took the place of the last individual of the circle of the Tenth, attaining that rank for the governance of the world of nature. (S292)

And there he remains, governing, `ilā an yūjada mithluhu man yakhlufuhu fī dhālika al-maqām al-'ālī` — until there is found one like himself who will succeed him in that lofty station. This is a strange and weighty saying: one like himself, not merely his son. For the son's vicegerency is only a partial vicegerency; the son is not the equal of the father in this respect. For a second ibda'iyyah truly like the first to be born — one who, like the first, comes at the beginning of the mawr (the great cycle) — can only arise at the last mawr; he cannot come before it. The successions in between establish only partial vicegerencies. The qawr, the sub-cycle, is a matter of immense scale: four hundred thousand qawr gather to make one mawr, and within the mawr the second part is born, who is called the vicegerent of the first mawr — himself a vast vicegerency, but partial in its manner. The first is greater than the last, for he has precedence; yet it is the universal vicegerent, arising at the last mawr, who attains the universal vicegerency. The one who takes the rank of the Tenth, in this passage, takes the place of the last individual of the Tenth's circle — for the circle of the Tenth is reckoned by its last member, who is assigned the entire governance of the world of nature. (S292)

### The Successive Ascent of the Intellects

When the second ibda'iyyah passes to the rank of the Tenth, a chain of ascents is set in motion among the intellects, each in turn rising to the rank above it as the levels below are made ready and ascend. The Tenth (al-'ashir), with those under its authority, passes to the rank of the Ninth Intellect; and the Ninth, with those under its authority, to the rank of the Eighth; and the Eighth, with those subordinate to it, to the rank of the Seventh; and the Seventh to the Sixth; and the Sixth to the Fifth; and the Fifth, with those under it, to the Fourth; and the Fourth, with those under it, to the Third; and the Third, with those subordinate to it, to the Second Intellect — `allatī hiya martabat Jannat al-Ma'wā allatī laysa fawqahā martabatun illā martabat al-'aql al-awwal, allatī hiya martabat al-waḥdah, lā yanbaghī li-aḥadin min al-'uqūl siwāhu, wa-hiya Sidrat al-Muntahā` — which is the rank of the Garden of Abode (Jannat al-Ma'wa), above which there is no rank except the rank of the First Intellect, which is the rank of oneness (martabat al-wahdah), unfitting for any of the intellects besides it, and which is the Lote-tree of the utmost boundary (Sidrat al-Muntaha), to which no other can reach. (S293)

The Second Intellect, then, occupies the rank of Jannat al-Ma'wa, and it is itself the first Emanated. The literal sense of Jannat al-Ma'wa is the garden in which is everyone's dwelling-place, for ma'wa means abode. Above it stands only the First Intellect, the rank of oneness, the Sidrat al-Muntaha, the boundary beyond which no intellect may pass. Each of these ascents from rank to rank occurs in its own time: the Ninth rises to the Eighth, and the Eighth to the Seventh, only when that which is below has been prepared and has itself ascended. The movement is driven from below, by the second perfection (kamal-i thani): when the second ibda'iyyah takes the place of the Tenth, one level of ascent is accomplished; and the next great ascent awaits the last mawr, when the whole world ascends and the universal vicegerent takes the place of the first mawr and rises higher. So the whole hierarchy of intellects rises, stage by stage, the lower feeding the higher, until all converge toward the Second and, beyond it, the unreachable First. (S293)

To this twofold summit — the Sidrat al-Muntaha and the Jannat al-Ma'wa beside it — all the rewarded souls ascend. `Ilayhā yantahī jamī'u al-muthābīna min al-rūḥānīn wa'l-jismānīn, 'alā ḥasbi hādhā al-tanaqqul shay'an ba'da shay', 'alā murūr al-adwār wa'l-akwār` — to it terminate all the rewarded ones, from the spiritual and the physical, according to this transition, bit by bit, over the passing of the cycles and the aeons (al-adwar wa'l-akwar). In just this manner all who have earned reward, whether among the spiritual or the physical, find their transition, passing on one after another with the passing of cycles and aeons. (S294)

And it is to these two ranks that the Noble Book alludes: `wa-bihimā ramaza al-kitābu al-karīmu bi-qawlihi Ta'ālā: 'inda Sidrat al-Muntahā, 'indahā Jannat al-Ma'wā` — and to both of them the Noble Book alluded in the saying of the Exalted: "At the Lote-tree of the utmost boundary, near it is the Garden of Abode." Near the Sidrat al-Muntaha is the Jannat al-Ma'wa; and the repetition of 'indahā, "near it," twice is for emphasis — close to it, hard beside it, lies the Garden of Abode. The two scriptural names mark the two highest stations of the ascent, the boundary and the garden that rests against it. (S294)

This is the very paradise of which the Messenger, the blessings of Allah be upon him, spoke: `inna fī al-jannah mā lā 'aynun ra'at wa-lā udhunun sami'at wa-lā khaṭara 'alā qalbi bashar` — "Indeed, in paradise there is what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and what has not occurred to the heart of a human." Such is the paradise it is: no eye has beheld it, no ear has heard of it, no human heart has so much as conceived it. `Li-'aẓīmi mā a'addahu'llāhu Ta'ālā fīhi li-awliyā'ihi min al-'ulā wa'l-raf'ah wa'l-sanā' wa'l-masarrah wa'l-kibriyā'` — by reason of the greatness of what Allah the Exalted has prepared therein for His friends: of highness, loftiness, eminence, joy, and grandeur. So great is what Allah has appointed in that paradise for His friends — exaltation, sublimity, splendor, gladness, and majesty — that `wa-lā sabīla ilā waṣfi mā hunālik illā taqrīban` — there is no way to describe what is there except by approximation. It can only be expressed approximately, never fully. (S295)

Of this same greatness, the most that can be said is found in what is reported in the Torah. `Wa-kafā min al-qawli fīhi mā warada fī al-Tawrāt min qawli'llāhi Ta'ālā: aṭi'nī yā ibn Ādam, aj'alka mithlī, ḥayyan lā tamūt, 'azīzan lā tudhall, ghaniyyan lā taftaqir` — and it suffices, of what may be said concerning it, what has come in the Torah from the saying of Allah the Exalted: "Obey Me, O son of Adam, and I will make you like Me — living, you will not die; mighty, you will not be humiliated; rich, you will not become poor." And the Quran adds: `inna fī dhālika la-dhikrā li-man kāna lahu qalbun aw alqā al-sam'a wa-huwa shahīd` — "Indeed, in that is a reminder for whoever has a heart, or who lends his ear while he is a witness." This matter is a reminder for the one who possesses a heart, or who directs his hearing toward it and is present and attesting. The promise of being made "like" Allah — undying, undefeated, never impoverished — is the scriptural measure of what awaits the friends of Allah in that paradise; and the one who has a living heart, or who attentively listens as a witness, will take it as a reminder. (S296)

### The Qa'im al-Qiyamah and the Single Stroke

There is one alone for whom the passage to the rank of the Tenth occurs not by the long, stage-by-stage ascent but at a single stroke. `Wa-hādhā al-shakhs al-fāḍil al-muntaqil ilā rutbat al-'āshir, lā yatahayyā li-aḥadin min al-maqāmāt mā tahayyā lahu min al-intiqāl ilā rutbat al-'āshir dafa'atan wāḥidatan` — and this virtuous person who has passed to the rank of the Tenth — it is not prepared for any of the stations what was prepared for him of passing to the rank of the Tenth at a single stroke (daf'atan wahidatan). For no other station is it possible to become the deputy or successor of the Tenth at one stroke, except for the second ibda'iyyah, who stands at the beginning of the mawr. (S297)

The reason is given: `li-annahu ūjida fī juz' al-ibdā'iyyah mubda'an ba'da al-muddat al-ṭawīlah, qā'iman bi'l-waḥdāniyyah ka-maqām al-mubda' al-awwal` — because he was brought into existence, in the part of origination, as an originated one after the long period, standing in oneness (qa'iman bi'l-wahdaniyyah) like the rank of the First Originated (al-mubda' al-awwal). Just as the circle of the First Intellect is a circle of unity, so the circle of the second ibda'iyyah is a circle of unity — but in the world of nature, for nature. Between these two, no circle of unity intervened anywhere: neither in the world of intentions, nor in the world of the command; the circle of unity belongs to the First, and the circle of nature to this one, and even among the ten ranks there is no other circle of unity. Because the second ibda'iyyah was originated standing in oneness, like the First Originated, the passage to the Tenth at a single stroke was prepared for him alone. (S297)

And this culminates in the Qa'im al-Qiyamah. `Qad 'uniya fīhi ḥattā ṣāra 'an ḥiddatihi zubdat al-'ālamīn jamī'an wa-siwāhum min al-maqāmāt al-sharīfah, lā yatahayyā lahu dhālika illā Qā'im al-Qiyāmah 'alā dhikrihi al-salām` — he was the object of this concern until, by his sharpness (hiddah), he became the quintessence (zubdat) of all the worlds, all of them, and of the noble stations besides them; and this is not prepared for any except the Qa'im al-Qiyamah, peace be upon his mention. The Riser of the Resurrection is the one who comes at the last mawr — `ḥattā yalija al-jamalu fī sammi al-khiyāṭ`, until the camel passes through the eye of the needle. The last mawr (al-mawr al-akhir) has its qa'im, the second ibda'iyyah; and the very last qa'im of that final mawr is the Qa'im al-Qiyamah. He alone is the quintessence of all the worlds, the summary and cream of all of them. (S297)

In him gather all the lights without exception. `Li-ijtimā' al-anwār kullihā 'indahu, a'nī anwār al-nuṭaqā' wa'l-usus wa'l-a'immah 'alayhim al-salām` — because of the gathering of all the lights with him, I mean the lights of the Speakers (al-nutaqa'), the foundations (al-usus), and the Imams, peace be upon them. With the Qa'im al-Qiyamah are gathered the lights of every Speaker, every Asas, and every Imam. `Li-anna kulla maqāmin min hādhihi al-maqāmāt bi'l-iḍāfah ilayhi juz', wa-huwa lahā kull, kamā sa-nubayyinuhu inshā'allāh fī mawḍi'ihi` — because every station of these stations, in relation to him, is a part (juz'), and he, in relation to them, is the whole (kull), as we shall explain, if Allah wills, in its place. Relative to the Qa'im al-Qiyamah, every other station is but a part, while he himself is the whole that gathers them. (S298)

A clarification guards against misunderstanding the dignity of the Tenth's rank. The frozen titles of the ranks — that this rank is the rank of the second ibda'iyyah, and so forth — are not bestowed because of any sharpness or perfection inherent in the title itself; rather, the position and designation have been made so exalted in the world of intentions. These are, as it were, the seats prepared in the world of intentions: whoever sits in them sits according to his own deeds and intentions. The chairs are set in place; the dignity belongs to the office, and the occupant fills it according to what he has earned. The one born at the beginning of the first mawr (al-mawr al-awwal) holds all the points of the mawr with him until the final resurrection, for his sphere of governance extends across the entire mawr — not merely his own, but all the mawrs to come; the governance of all of them is his, and for this reason he is reckoned the First Originated (al-mubda' al-awwal) in this world, his governance being universal and enduring to the very end. (S297, S298)

### The Son, the Mission, and the Salvation of Souls

The discourse returns to the son who was designated by nass. `Thumma anna walada hādhā al-shakhs al-fāḍil al-manṣūṣi 'alayhi, al-qā'imi fī maqāmihi khalafan li-'ālim al-dīn, aqāma al-da'wata fī 'ālamihi, wa-da'ā ilā tawḥīd Allāh Ta'ālā wa-'ibādatihi, li-khalāṣi tilka al-nufūs al-ghariqati fī baḥr al-hayūlā, wa-asr al-ṭabī'ah, wa-istikhrāji man yakhlufuhu minhā fī maqāmihi kamā fa'ala wāliduhu al-mustakhraju lahu min da'watihi` — then the son of this virtuous person, the one designated by nass, standing in his station as successor to the scholar of religion, established the mission in his world, and called to the oneness of Allah the Exalted (tawhid) and His worship, for the salvation of those souls drowned in the sea of primeval matter (bahr al-hayula) and in the prison of the physical world (asr al-tabi'ah), and for bringing forth from it the one who would succeed him in his station, just as his father did, the father himself having been extracted for him from his mission. (S299)

The son, then — the second ibda'iyyah upon whom the nass was conferred — succeeds as vicegerent for the world of religion and establishes the mission in his own world. He summons the servants to the oneness of Allah, so that the souls trapped and submerged in the seas of hayula, the primeval matter, may be granted salvation, and so that those imprisoned in the captivity of the physical world may be delivered. And he, in turn, extracts the one who will succeed him in his station, exactly as his own father had done. (S299)

`Fa-idhā istakhraja minhu waladahu wa-awfā mā 'alayhi min al-khidmah, intaqala ilā ufuqi martabat al-'āshir` — so when he has brought forth from his mission his son and discharged the service that lay upon him, he passes to the horizon of the rank of the Tenth (ufuq martabat al-'ashir). The word here is ufuq, horizon — the court, the seat — for his universal vicegerency is not yet present; he will have a place in the court of his own father, who occupies the rank of the Tenth. His duties, therefore, are reduced to two alone: to deliver those drowning in the sea of hayula, and to appoint his successor — the governance of the physical world, two governances and no more. `Allatī qad ṣārat martabatan murattabatan li-abīhi, fa-yaṣīru wāqifan hunālik` — which had become a rank specially arranged for his father, and there he comes to a stand. He becomes the son who halts at that horizon, the very son alluded to under the figure of the walad-i ta'am in the doctrine of origin and return (mabda' wa ma'ad): the walad-i ta'am signifies the one who is the universal vicegerent. (S299)

A clarification on the universal and the partial accompanies this. As the Shaykh used to say, wherever the universal (kulli) is spoken of, the partial (juz'i) is necessarily implied, and wherever the partial is spoken of, the universal is implied; to say "universal" does not mean there is no partial — for if there were no partial, there could be no universal at all. The son here appointed as successor, upon whom the ruling was conferred, is from the father's own lineage, while the twenty-eight are gathered with him; the bab, too, who is not among the twenty-eight, has a special rank and will ascend within their structure. The son in question may be understood as a physical son, who, when the full service is done, passes to the horizon of the Tenth's rank — the rank that was arranged for his father, the second ibda'iyyah — and there he stands. (S299)

`Wa-man intaqala min maqāmin ba'dahu intahā ilayh` — and whoever passes from a rank after him terminates with him. Whoever passes from any rank after him comes to a stop with the son; in the ranks of the Tenth themselves the meeting takes place, and whoever comes after him will have a seat in his court. (S299)

### The Barzakh and the Stopping of the Stations

`Wa-lā tazālu al-da'watu qā'imatan wa-kullu maqāmin yantaqilu yaṣīru mawqūfan ma'ahu ilā wafā'i 'iddatihim wa-inqiḍā'i muddatihim` — and the mission does not cease to stand, and every station that passes becomes stopped (mawquf) with him until the completion of their number and the elapsing of their period. The mission remains forever established; every rank, as it passes, becomes halted with the second ibda'iyyah until their number is complete and their term has elapsed. And the place where they are halted is the barzakh, the isthmus. (S300)

`Alladhī ramaza bihi al-kitābu al-karīmu bi-qawlihi Ta'ālā: wa-min warā'ihim barzakhun ilā yawmi yub'athūn` — to which the Noble Book alluded in the saying of the Exalted: "And behind them is a barzakh until the day they are resurrected." The word warā', literally "behind," means after them: after their passing there is a barzakh, until the day of their resurrection. When all of them, together with the universal structures, have passed into the barzakh, a day will come on which they are raised. This is the praiseworthy barzakh (al-barzakh al-mahmud), prepared for the people of good — that horizon of the Tenth which serves as the barzakh for all the Imams and all the qa'ims. From it, the qa'ims dispense to their succeeding qa'ims, to the Imams, to the superiors; there is a process by which every master (sahib) receives his portion, every qa'im his thousand years, from that station, from the barzakh. As for the blameworthy barzakh, the discourse upon it will come in its place, if Allah the Exalted wills. (S300)

### The Completion of the Number and the Rising of the Qa'im

The text specifies the number and the period precisely. The completion of their number is that they fulfill one hundred and twenty-four thousand forms (mi'ah wa arba'ah wa 'ishrun alf surah), and their period is seven thousand years. For their entry into the barzakh, the completion of their number is the completion of one hundred and twenty-four thousand forms (surahs), and the completion of their period is seven thousand years. Throughout the cycle of unveiling (dawr al-kashf), they remain for seven thousand years, and within that span the hundred and twenty-four thousand forms are completed; then they all pass into the barzakh. One hundred and twenty-four thousand forms in seven thousand years. (S301)

`Fa-'inda wafā'ihā yaqūmu qā'imuhum mustakhrajan min da'wati abīhi ka-istikhrājihim` — then, upon its completion, their qa'im rises, extracted from the mission of his father, just as they were extracted. At the completion of the seven thousand years, their qa'im rises — the qa'im of the cycle, the one in whom the hundred and twenty-four thousand forms are fulfilled — and he is brought forth from his father's mission, even as they were brought forth. Among the many points the second ibda'iyyah of the first mawr holds, one is the point of the qa'im; that point of the qa'im stands at the rank of the father, who establishes him. (S301)

`Fa-yattaṣilu bihī hādhihi al-maqāmāt al-mawqūfah fī al-barzakh` — then these stations that were stopped in the barzakh connect with him. The qa'im of the seven thousand years rises, and all the stations that had been halted in the barzakh — the seats of the hundred and twenty-four thousand forms of those seven thousand years — connect with him. These the second ibda'iyyah bestows upon the qa'im, the qa'im of the seven thousand years. (S301)

### The Luminous Structure of the Qa'im

Once the halted stations connect with the qa'im, they become the very members of his luminous form. `Fa-yakūnu kullu wāḥidin minhum 'uḍwan min a'ḍā'i haykalihi al-nūrānī al-laṭīf` — then each one of them becomes a limb among the limbs of his luminous, subtle structure (haykal nurani latif). Each of the hundred and twenty-four thousand becomes a limb of that subtle, luminous body. `Fa-yakūnu laṭīfuhu lahum ka'l-rūḥi li'l-jism` — and his subtle self is to them as the spirit (al-ruh) is to the body. The qa'im's subtle self stands to them as the spirit stands to the body: they become the body, and he becomes the spirit. By his coming-together with them — the spirit's joining with the body, the qa'im's joining with all the forms of the seven thousand years — they attain the power to ascend to that lofty rank, attaining one level in the circles of the Tenth, having stopped first in the barzakh; for the circles of the Tenth are with the second ibda'iyyah, and from these they gain one rank. (S302)

`Fa-yaṣīrūna wa-iyyāhu haykalan nūrāniyyan ilāhiyyan` — then they and he become one luminous, divine structure. They and he together become a single luminous, divine structure. `Fa-'inda mufāraqatihi li-jismihi wa-intiqālihi min hādhihi al-dār, yaṣīru 'aqlan qudsāniyyan mujarradan ilāhiyyan qad jarā fīhi mā jarā fī al-'uqūl al-mujarradah al-'āliyah` — and upon his parting from his body and his passing from this abode, he becomes a holy, abstract, divine intellect ('aql qudsani mujarrad), in which flows what flowed in the high, abstract intellects. When he departs his body and passes from this house, he becomes an abstract, holy, divine intellect, in which there flows what flowed in the exalted, abstract intellects above him. (S302)

`Fa-lā yakūnu baynahu wa-baynahum farqun illā bi-rutbat al-sabq lā ghayr` — and there is no difference between him and them except by the rank of precedence (rutbat al-sabq), nothing else. Between this qa'im and those above him there remains no difference whatsoever except the rank of precedence — the priority of having come first — and nothing besides. This is the meaning of the saying that the universal is universal and the partial is partial: with this universal qa'im all the believers, too, are present. (S302)

Yet a difficulty must be addressed: if all the believers have ascended, have they thereby become greater than the Imams or qa'ims yet to come? The answer is that the rank of precedence persists. When the seventh qa'im comes, all seven qa'ims are joined to the structure of the seventh, and there a hierarchy is established — a hierarchy of Imams, a hierarchy of believers — with the seventh; and afterward, when the qa'im of the cycle of concealment (sitr) manifests, he gathers everyone, and there no further hierarchy obtains. But the rank of precedence remains a thing apart. In the world of intentions, if we are behind another, we necessarily remain behind — unless an effort is made within the cycles, for the cycles possess the capacity for change, and one may move forward, in the world of intentions, from one's appointed point. (S302)

### The Hundred and Twenty-Four Thousand Prophets and the Known Day

To this noble, great structure the Speaker of the cycle himself alluded. `Wa-li-dhālika qāla nāṭiqu al-dawr ṣalawātu'llāhi 'alayh: inna li'llāhi Ta'ālā mi'at alf nabiyyin wa-arba'at wa-'ishrīn alf nabiyyin, mushīran ilā hādhā al-haykal al-sharīf al-'aẓīm` — and for this reason the Speaker of the cycle, the blessings of Allah be upon him, said: "Indeed, for Allah the Exalted there are one hundred thousand prophets and twenty-four thousand prophets," alluding thereby to this noble, great structure (haykal). The figure of the one hundred and twenty-four thousand prophets points to the great luminous body composed of the halted forms gathered in the qa'im. (S303)

And the Noble Book alludes to the same: `wa-ilayhi ashāra al-kitābu al-karīmu bi-qawlihi Ta'ālā: qul inna al-awwalīna wa'l-ākhirīna la-majmū'ūna ilā mīqāti yawmin ma'lūm` — and to it the Noble Book pointed in the saying of the Exalted: "Say: indeed, the first and the last will surely be gathered for the appointed time of a known day." Say, O Prophet: the first ones and the last ones will surely be gathered for the appointed time of a known day. This is said for the qa'im of the seven thousand years, the qa'im of the cycle of unveiling; it applies to the last qa'im. `Fa-huwa 'alā dhikrihi al-salām al-mīqātu al-mujtama'u ilayhi` — so he, peace be upon his mention, is the appointed time toward which they are gathered. He is the last; the first depart and the last arrives, and toward him all are gathered. (S303)

`Wa'l-yawmu al-ma'lūmu huwa dawruhu, a'nī dawr al-kashfi alladhī huwa khamsūna alf sanah` — and the known day is his cycle, I mean the cycle of unveiling (dawr al-kashf), which is fifty thousand years. The "known day" is his cycle, the dawr al-kashf, which measures fifty thousand years. `Wa-bi-dhālika naṭaqa al-kitābu al-karīmu ḥaythu qāla Ta'ālā: fī yawmin kāna miqdāruhu khamsīna alf sanah` — and to this the Noble Book gave voice where the Exalted said: "In a day whose measure is fifty thousand years." This is the final cycle of unveiling. (S304)

`Wa-'inda qiyāmi hādhā al-qā'imi 'alā dhikrihi al-salām yaqa'u al-ḥisābu bayna yadayhi wa-ilayhi al-thawābu wa'l-'iqābu` — and at the rising of this qa'im, peace be upon his mention, the reckoning (hisab) takes place before him, and to him belong the reward and the punishment. Upon the rising of this qa'im — the qa'im of the fifty thousand years — the reckoning occurs before him, and into his hands are placed the portions of reward and of punishment. (S304)

The chain does not end with him. `Thumma yastakhriju min da'watihi man yakūnu lahu waladan yakhlufuhu fī maqāmihi, wa-yunaṣṣu 'alayhi wa-yantaqilu ṣā'idan ilā martabat al-'aql al-'āshir` — then he brings forth from his mission one who will be a son to him, succeeding him in his station, and he is designated by nass, and he ascends to the rank of the Tenth Intellect. This qa'im of the unveiling confers the nass upon his son and passes upward to the rank of the Tenth. `Fa-yakhlufu dhālika al-shakhs al-awwal alladhī ṣāra mudabbiran li-'ālam al-ṭabī'ah, wa-yaṣīru 'iwaḍan minhu mudabbiran li-'ālam al-kawn wa'l-fasād` — then he succeeds that first person who had become the governor (mudabbir) of the world of nature, and becomes, in his place, the governor of the world of generation and corruption (kawn wa'l-fasad). The son succeeds the first one who had governed the world of nature and becomes, in his stead, the governor of the world of generation and corruption, in a partial manner. (S304)

`Wa-yas'adu dhālika ilā rutbat al-'aql al-tāsi'` — and that one ascends to the rank of the Ninth Intellect, just as those before him had ascended. `Wa-taraqqā al-'uqūl min rutbatin ilā rutbatin, ka-taraqqīhim awwalan 'alā hādhā al-namaṭ, yakūnu al-amru abadan sarmadan` — and the intellects ascend from rank to rank, just as they ascended at first, in this same pattern; the matter is forever and ever (abadan sarmadan). The intellects pass on, one after another, in exactly this manner, and so the matter continues without end. (S304)

So the whole pattern repeats and renews itself across the aeons: each qa'im extracting his son, conferring the nass, ascending to the rank of the Tenth; each Tenth, with its subordinates, rising to the Ninth, and the Ninth to the Eighth, and so onward toward the Second and the unreachable First — the cycles and aeons passing, the rewarded souls converging upon the Garden of Abode beside the Lote-tree of the utmost boundary, and the affair enduring forever and ever. And praise be to Allah, the Guide who leads, by His limits (hudud), to His oneness (tawhid), the Bestower upon His servants by His grace and His generosity; and may Allah's blessings be upon His Messenger, our master Muhammad, and upon his family, the pure Imams.

## The Cycles of Concealment and Unveiling

### The Recurring Qa'im and the Seven Thrones of the Dawr al-Kashf

We now take up the second chapter of the third gate of the second pavilion (`al-Fasl al-Thani min al-Bab al-Thalith min al-Suradiq al-Thani`), where the Natiq's discourse turns to the great rhythm by which the resurrection renews itself across the long ages of unveiling. The governing statement is this:

`wa la yazal 'ala wafa'i kulli saba'at alaf sana qa'imun yaqumu ka-mithli hadha al-qa'im al-mutaqaddim 'alayhi al-qawl ila wafa'i khamsina alf sana.`

(And so it continues that, upon the completion of every seven thousand years, a Qa'im arises like this Qa'im about whom the statement was previously made, up to the completion of fifty thousand years.)

The meaning unfolds in this way. Just as there was mentioned before that great Qa'im of the fifty-thousand-year cycle of unveiling, so too, upon the completion of each span of seven thousand years, there arises one who performs the act of standing (qiyam), a Qa'im after the likeness of the one whose statement has already passed, and this renewal continues onward until the term of fifty thousand years is fulfilled. The Qa'im of the seven thousand years is established upon the very pattern of the Qa'im of the fifty thousand years; the lesser is fashioned in the image of the greater.

The mechanism of this renewal must be grasped from the foundation. After the primordial body of the first cycle — the jussa ibda'iyyah — has passed on, its own offspring, who is the Imam, becomes its successor in a partial (juzwi) capacity. In the horizon of the newly generated order (the ufuq of the nashi'at), a single seat is established for this successor, and upon that seat a Qa'im resides, presiding until the seven thousand years of the dawr al-kashf are brought to completion. During that interval, the one hundred and twenty-four thousand forms appear, and the Qa'im of the seven thousand years is firmly set upon his station within the dawr al-kashf. When his establishment is complete, the jussa ibda'iyyah entrusts to him the forms gathered over those seven thousand years — the forms of the Imams of that span. He, the Qa'im of the seven thousand, becomes the keeper of those entrusted forms; from them his own luminous structure (haykal) is composed. Thus he takes his place upon one throne of the seven thrones (kursi), one seat which is the very essence and distillation of the seven thousand years. In the same manner, throne after throne is established across the fifty thousand years, until all seven thrones are filled by their seven Qa'ims.

The text then names the culmination directly:

`wa qama al-Qa'im al-Sabi' fa-yakunu lahu min al-quwwah wa al-sharaf wa al-fazl ma lam yakun li-ahadin taqaddamahu min sittatin mutaqaddim dhikruha`

(And when the seventh Qa'im stands, he will have such power, honor, and grace as none of the preceding six, whose mention has passed, possessed.)

When the seventh Qa'im of the dawr al-kashf rises — the last Qa'im of unveiling — there belongs to him a power, an honor, and a grace not granted to any of the six who preceded him. He does not merely gather the harvest of his own seven thousand years; he stands above six exalted persons, the six Qa'ims who came before, each of whom in turn gathered the forms of his own seven-thousand-year cycle. First each Qa'im acquires the things of his own era, drawing into himself the full yield of his span of unveiling; and then the six Qa'ims of the dawr al-kashf — the Qa'ims who are themselves the Imams of their ages — are themselves gathered to the seventh. The forms they bear, which they received from the jussa ibda'iyyah, pass through their intermediary realm (barzakh) and ascend into the seventh.

The reason for this surpassing rank is given:

`li-annahu yaqumu lahum maqam al-khalq al-akhar alladhi huwa al-ruh li-al-jism wa huwa muntaha dawr al-kashf`

(Because he stands for them in the station of the "other creation," which is the spirit for the body, and he is the culmination of the cycle of unveiling.)

For this last Qa'im stands for the preceding six in the station of the khalq al-akhar, the "other creation" — that final, second creation of which the body is made worthy. He is to them as the spirit is to the body: the six Qa'ims constitute, as it were, the body, and he is the spirit that animates and completes them. He is the muntaha, the culmination and term, of the entire cycle of unveiling. In him the dawr al-kashf reaches its perfection and finds its end.

### The Governance of the Primordial Body Over the Whole Cosmos

A question naturally arises concerning the reach of the jussa ibda'iyyah's authority. It has been said that the governance of all things (tadbir-i kull) rests in the hand of the primordial body. Does this extend even to the fixed laws of nature — that two and two make four, that two parts of hydrogen joined to one of oxygen yield water? And does it reach further still, down to the partial and lowly phenomena that afflict the natural order, such as viruses and plagues?

The answer affirms the totality of this governance. The tadbir of the jussa ibda'iyyah of the first cycle extends over the entire kawr — over the whole expanse of the cosmic cycle and all that is generated within it. The four elements appointed in the natural order — fire, air, water, and earth — together with the natures established in them — moistness, coldness, dryness, and heat (rutubat, burudat, yubusat, hararat) — the whole of this elemental architecture proceeds under that governance. And not only the universal laws but the partial ones as well: when viruses come, when plagues come, these too belong to that same appointment. For what are such afflictions? They are particles, and specifically corrupted particles (kharab ajza') — fragments that have fallen into disorder. They become connected to a person and cling to him; they arrive as viruses and pestilences, and this too is a form of governance.

Yet the manner of their coming is to be understood precisely. Such an affliction operates like a magnet. In a prior sphere of existence, a man maintained some connection with that corrupted particle, and by reason of that earlier bond, the magnet of affinity draws it to him here, so that it inevitably comes to meet him. Unless he should, by some supplication, fashion a counter-governance — petitioning that in time to come he not acquire that thing — the meeting will occur, for the affinity demands it. This is possible: a du'a may avert what magnetic affinity would otherwise compel. But the remaining retributions (qisasat) will most certainly come to pass, for they belong to the order of justice that cannot simply be waved aside.

Here the discourse touches a ruling of grave consequence. Allah, the Exalted, forgives the sins that are owed to Him alone — His own due He may remit by His mercy. But the wrongs that lie between servant and servant, the debts and trespasses of one creature against another, Allah does not simply cancel; only the servant who was wronged can forgive them. The matter rests upon the servants themselves. The one against whom the offense was committed must himself forgive, or else some path of compensation must be opened — a way by which recompense reaches the one with whom the enmity stood, so that in place of strict retribution (qisas) another form of settlement arrives. These are the methods by which the ledger between servants is balanced. The debt (qarz) must be paid in one currency or another; it does not dissolve of its own accord.

So we return to the principle: the jussa ibda'iyyah, the primordial body of the cosmic cycle, holds its governance over the entirety of the kawr. With it, a seat is formed for its offspring — a partial successor, a juzwi of the juzwi — and when the seven thousand years are accomplished and that successor performs the qiyam of the dawr al-kashf, he goes and takes his place upon one of the seven thrones. The jussa ibda'iyyah entrusts to him the Imams of those seven thousand years; from them his haykal is formed; and so, throne upon throne, the seven seats are established across the fifty thousand years of unveiling.

### The Onset of Weakness and the Beginning of the Dawr al-Sitr

The text now marks the great hinge upon which the ages turn — the passing of the seventh Qa'im and the descent of the world from openness into concealment:

`wa 'inda nuqlatihi wa-inqida'i dawrihi ibtida'u dawr al-sitr`

(And near his passing and the completion of his cycle, there begins the cycle of concealment.)

With the intiqal, the passing-over, of that seventh Qa'im, and with the completion of the cycle that belonged to the six, the dawr al-sitr — the cycle of concealment — takes its beginning. The text continues:

`fa-yaqumu waladuhu salamullahi 'alayhi ba'dahu fa-yaqi'u fi al-da'wah zu'fun li-anna dawr al-kashfi qad intaha ila amadih wa balagha al-kitabu ajalahu wa nihayat haddihi`

(So his son rises after him, may the peace of Allah be upon him, and a weakness befalls the mission, because the cycle of unveiling has reached its term, and the Book has reached its appointed end, and the limit of its boundary.)

After the seventh Qa'im, his offspring rises to perform the qiyam in his place; but now a weakness (zu'f) enters into the mission (da'wah). The reason is that the dawr al-kashf has reached its term (amad), has attained its perfection (kamal), and the Book has reached the fullness of its measure and the very end of its limit. The cycle of openness has completed itself, and the order begins to recede.

`wa la yazal yatamada dhalika al-zu'fu shay'an ba'da shay'in wa yazdadu khumulan wa dusuran ila an tantamisa al-da'watu wa tabqa fi halat tasattur`

(And that weakness continues to take hold little by little, thing after thing, and obscurity and lassitude increase, until the mission is eclipsed and remains in a state of concealment.)

That weakness does not arrive all at once but takes root gradually, increasing thing after thing, one degree following upon another. With it grows khumul — a withdrawal into obscurity and neglect — and dusur, a word the Natiq pauses to unfold from its root. Dusur carries the sense of poverty and tightness of means; it is connected to the blanket that one draws over oneself, to the soiled and unkempt state of one who wraps himself away from the world — for from this same root comes the address al-mudaththir, "the one wrapped in a cloak." Here it signifies the spreading of laziness, languor, and negligence over the affairs of the mission. So the obscurity and the lassitude increase together, until at last the da'wah is eclipsed and effaced (tantamisa) and there remains only a state of concealment (tasattur).

`wa la yatazahharu bi-shay'in minha illa khafiyyatan bayna ahliha wa ramzan wa isharatan`

(And nothing of it becomes manifest except secretly, among its own people, through symbol and indication.)

In this concealed condition, nothing of the mission shows itself openly. It survives only in hiddenness, among its own people, transmitted through symbol (ramz) and allusion (isharah). The haqa'iq, the inner realities, become veiled; what circulates openly is something else entirely.

`wa la yakunu 'ilmun zahirun illa 'ilm al-hindisa wa al-nujum wa ghayr dhalika min 'ulum al-riyadiyyah`

(And there is no manifest knowledge except the science of geometry and the science of the stars and the rest of the mathematical sciences.)

This is the distinguishing mark of the dawr al-sitr: in it there exists no apparent knowledge save the mathematical sciences — engineering and architecture, astronomy, and their kindred disciplines of calculation and measure. The exoteric sciences spread and flourish, while the inner realities withdraw into hiding. These are the special characteristics of the cycle of concealment.

`wa yakunu al-a'immatu salamullahi 'alayhim fi ghayat al-taqiyyah wa al-sitr wa takunu aydi al-azdad ghalibatan 'aliyatan wa umuruhum 'ala ahsan-i ha jariyatan ila wafa'i thalathat alaf sana`

(And the Imams, may the peace of Allah be upon them, are in the utmost dissimulation and concealment, and the hands of the opponents are dominant and exalted, and their affairs proceed in the best of states until the completion of three thousand years.)

Throughout this cycle the Imams, peace be upon them, dwell in the extremity of prudent dissimulation (taqiyyah) and concealment (sitr). The hands of the opponents (al-azdad) are dominant and lifted high; the adversaries of the truth hold the upper hand. This dominance of the opposition runs its course in this best of states, for them, through the first three thousand years of the cycle.

### The First Natiq of Concealment and the Establishment of Adam

With the order now driven into concealment, the text introduces the chain of those who will carry the mission through the long night of the dawr al-sitr:

`wa qama awwal mutaqa'idi dawr al-sitr wa huwa Adam 'ala dhikrihi al-salam`

(And the first of the mainstays of the cycle of concealment arose, and he is Adam, upon whose mention be peace.)

The first of the mutaqa'idin — those who stand as the pillars and mainstays of the dawr al-sitr — rose to his station, and he is Adam, upon whose mention be peace. A point of reckoning must be clarified here, since some had understood that after the fifty thousand years of unveiling there would follow a separate span of three thousand years of interval (fatrat). The correct understanding is otherwise. The three thousand years of fatrat are not appended after the dawr al-kashf as a distinct era; rather, they are the last three thousand years of the dawr al-kashf itself. For within the dawr al-kashf two kinds of forms appear. One kind is grounded in reality, the forms proper to unveiling. The other kind, appearing in those final three thousand years, are such forms as will become the future bearers of the dispositions and thoughts of the dawr al-sitr; these come into process during that closing interval. That closing interval of the dawr al-kashf is what is named the dawr al-fatrat — and the word is fatrat, with the vowel a upon the first letter, carrying the sense of lassitude and the slackening of the order, not fitrat. With this the reckoning holds together: fifty thousand years of unveiling, of which the last three thousand are the interval, and seven thousand years of concealment.

`fa-yakunu al-amru bi-khilafi ma kana 'alayhi fi al-thalathat alaf al-mutaqaddimah wa yustaqbalu saba'at alaf sana`

(So the matter will be contrary to how it was in the preceding three thousand years, and seven thousand years will be received in what is to come.)

The affair of this new cycle will run contrary to how matters stood in the preceding three thousand years, and seven thousand years lie ahead — the seven thousand years of the dawr al-sitr — concerning which the statement will come in its place, if Allah the Exalted wills.

Now the third chapter takes up the founding of the law in the cycle of concealment:

`thumma innahu 'ala wafa'i hadhihi al-thalathat alaf sana yuqimu imamu dhalika al-zaman naatiqan wa huwa Adam 'alayhi al-salam`

(Then, upon the completion of these three thousand years, the Imam of that age establishes a Natiq, and he is Adam, upon him be peace.)

This must be read with care, for it overturns a hasty assumption. It is not Adam who is the Imam; rather, the Imam of that age establishes a Natiq, and that Natiq is Adam, peace be upon him. The Imams of that time set up the Natiq, and the Natiq whom they establish is Adam. And the Imam who establishes him is Janab Imam Hunayd, upon him be peace and blessings. We must mark a structural truth here: in the dawr al-kashf, this manner of arrangement does not obtain at all — there are no Natiqs in the cycle of unveiling. There the station is that of the mustawda'in, the entrusted ones. Adam belongs among these mustawda'in; he is established as Natiq by the command of his Imam.

`fa-yuqimu lahu hududan wa yuqanninu lahu qawaninan wa yusunnu sununan wa yad'u ila 'ibadatillahi ta'ala wa tawhidihi bi-amri dhalika al-imam alladhi huwa sahib al-zaman salamullahi 'alayhi`

(So he establishes for him limits, and lays down for him laws, and enacts ordained ways, and calls to the worship of Allah the Exalted and to His oneness, by the command of that Imam who is the master of the age, may the peace of Allah be upon him.)

The Imam of the age establishes hudud for Adam, lays down laws for him, and enacts for him ordained ways (sunan); and Adam, in turn, calls the servants of Allah to the worship of Allah, the Exalted, and to His oneness — all of this by the command of that Imam who is the Sahib al-Zaman, the master of the age. Janab Adam's own name in the body is Abdullah bin Tayjun bin Taykhum, and his Imam, who commands him, is Hunayd.

This is the universal pattern of the Natiqs across the cycle of concealment: each Natiq governs not by his own authority but by the command of the Imam of his time. And each Natiq, once established, undertakes the founding of a religious order:

`wa yashra'u shar'an wa yaza'u wad'an zahiran yaj'aluhu rumuzan wa isharatin ila ma'rifati hududillahi al-ruhaniyyah wa al-jismaniyyah al-dallati 'ala tawhidillahi`

(And he institutes a sacred law and lays down an outward observance, making it symbols and indications pointing to the knowledge of the limits of Allah, spiritual and corporeal, which indicate the oneness of Allah.)

Each Natiq institutes a shari'ah, choosing and laying down a path, and establishes an outward rite. But these outward observances are not ends in themselves; he fashions them as symbols (rumuz) and indications (isharat) pointing toward the knowledge (ma'rifah) of the limits of God — the hudud, both the spiritual (ruhaniyyah) and the corporeal (jismaniyyah) — and these hudud in turn point to the oneness of God (tawhid).

The text adds a remark on the character of the cycle's opening: the beginning of that matter, of that cycle, is from weakness moving toward strength (`wa ibtida'u dhalika zu'fun sa'irun ila quwwatin`). Adam stands established in the station of the sulalah — the gathered essences — for the reason that:

`li-annahu yaqumu maqam al-sulalah`

(Because he stands in the station of the extract.)

The sulalah is the first of the embryonic stations, followed by the nutfah (the drop), then the 'alaqah (the clinging thing), then the mudghah (the morsel of flesh), then the 'izam (the bones), then the lahm (the flesh), and finally the khalqan akhar (the other creation). The sulalah resides in both the father and the mother: those essences that are gathered together are the sulalah. The essences destined to appear from fire, air, water, and earth — Allah, the Exalted, brings them and deposits them in the father and the mother, and these deposited essences are called the sulalah. The word itself comes from sulul, a chain, signifying that which is drawn out and pulled forth. Adam, then, occupies the station of the extract at the dawn of the cycle, the first gathering of essences from which all that follows will be drawn.

Adam's term runs its course:

`wa la yazalu dhalika kadhalika ila an yuqima lahu asasan yakhlufuhu fi ummatihi wa-intaqala ila dar karamihillahi ta'ala wa jarat al-imamatu mutasalsilatan min imamin ila imamin ila wafa'i dawrihi`

(And so it continues thus until he establishes for himself a foundation who succeeds him in his community, and he passes on to the abode of the honor of Allah the Exalted, and the Imamate runs on in succession from Imam to Imam until the completion of his cycle.)

The cycle continues in this fashion until Adam establishes for himself an asas — a foundation — who succeeds him in his community (ummah). Adam then passes on to the abode of Allah's honor, and the Imamate runs on in unbroken succession, from one Imam to the next, until the completion of Adam's cycle. The asas of Janab Adam was Shith. His first vicegerent had been Habil, but Habil passed away; afterward Shith was born, and Janab Shith became the foundation.

### The Succession of the Natiqs Through the Cycle of Concealment

The text now lays out the great chain of Natiqs who carry the mission through the dawr al-sitr, each succeeding the last, each established by the command of the Imam of his age, each confronted by an appointed opponent:

`wa qama ba'dahu al-naatiq al-thani wa huwa Nuh 'alayhi al-salam wa qiyamuhu 'an amri imami zamanihi wa huwa Hud 'alayhi al-salam wa zidduhu Awj bin Unaq la'anahullahu`

(And after him the second Natiq arose, and he is Nuh, upon him be peace, and his standing was by the command of the Imam of his age, and he is Hud, upon him be peace, and his opponent was Awj ibn Unaq, may Allah curse him.)

After Adam, the second Natiq arose, and he is Nuh, peace be upon him. His qiyam was by the command of the Imam of his age, who is Hud, peace be upon him. His opponent was Awj ibn Unaq — written also as Unuq — whom Allah cursed; the opponent of Janab Nuh was of the line of Qabil. Nuh, like Adam before him, laid down laws and instituted a sacred order different from the first, and called toward an outward worship that was, in its inner sense, symbols and indications pointing to the limits of Allah, the spiritual and the corporeal, indicating the knowledge of the oneness of Allah and His transcendence (tanzih), until the fulfillment of the service laid upon him. Then Nuh established his wasi, Sam, peace be upon them both, leaving him behind in his community as the interpreter of the inner meaning (batin) of his sacred law; he performed the designation (nass) upon him, and passed on to the abode of Allah's honor, while the Imamate ran on, connected and successive, until the completion of his cycle.

A clarification belongs here on the two chains that run in parallel. The chain of the Imamate proceeding from Imam Hunayd runs son after son (waladan an waladin); this is the chain of the mustaqarr, the established line, and it runs by hereditary succession. But the chain of the Natiqs — from Adam to Nuh, from Nuh to Ibrahim — is the chain of the mustawda'in, the entrusted ones, and in this chain hereditary descent from one to the next is not a condition. The Natiqs are linked by the office of the mission, not necessarily by blood.

Concerning the opponent, a condition is fixed: every Natiq must have his zidd, his opponent. As the Prophet, upon him be peace, declared, for every prophet there is an enemy — `li-kulli nabiyyin 'aduwwun` — a devil who stands against him. Every light casts a shadow; a shadow is necessary to every luminous thing. And in the dawr al-sitr the opponents appeared precisely so that each prophet might be confronted by his adversary. There are the manifest opponents, like Abu Lahab and Abu Jahl, plain to all; and there are those who worked their opposition from within, practicing hypocrisy under cover of belonging.

The chain continues:

`wa qama Ibrahim 'alayhi al-salam 'an amri imami zamanihi salamullahi 'alayhima alladhi huwa Salih wa kana zidduhu al-Namrud bin Kan'an`

(And Ibrahim, upon him be peace, arose by the command of the Imam of his age, may the peace of Allah be upon them both, who is Salih, and his opponent was Namrud ibn Kan'an.)

Ibrahim, peace be upon him, performed his qiyam by the command of the Imam of his age, who is Salih — and note that the prophet himself is named Ibrahim while the Imam who commands him is Salih, so that two distinct persons must be kept apart in the mind. Ibrahim's opponent was Namrud ibn Kan'an. He laid down laws and instituted a sacred order in which he placed symbols and indications pointing toward the knowledge of the limits of Allah, the spiritual and the corporeal, which indicate the knowledge of the oneness of Allah and His transcendence, until the completion of the service laid upon him.

A precise observation arises at this point in the text. In Adam's cycle, only the word tawhid appeared. Here, in Ibrahim's cycle, two words appear together: tawhid and tanzih. But a third term, tajrid, is not mentioned. This distinction is not incidental, and the three terms must be carefully separated. Tawhid is the recognition of the createdness of all the hudud — to know that every one of the limits is a created thing, and to affirm thereby the oneness of the Creator who is beyond them. Tanzih is to purify Allah from all attributes and from all names — to declare Him free of every quality and designation. And tajrid is to abstract: to strip away and pull apart the state of origination (ibda') from all creatures. This last is a state that can be established upon no one, for none possesses the capacity to perform ibda'; to abstract that state away from all created things entirely is what is called tajrid, and it stands at a height that none can reach. Thus tawhid, tanzih, and tajrid form an ascending order of declaration, and the text grants them to the cycles measure by measure.

Ibrahim established his wasi, Isma'il, peace be upon him, and passed on to the abode of Allah's honor, the Imamate running on in succession through his progeny until the completion of his cycle.

`wa qama Musa 'an amri imami zamanihi alladhi huwa 'Ud salamullahi 'alayhima wa kana iblisuhu Fir'awn la'anahullahu qa'iman bi-iza'ihi`

(And Musa arose by the command of the Imam of his age, who is 'Ud, may the peace of Allah be upon them both, and his Iblis was Fir'awn, may Allah curse him, standing opposite him.)

Musa performed his qiyam by the command of the Imam of his age, who is 'Ud — 'Ud ibn 'Udad — peace be upon them both. His devil was Fir'awn, may Allah curse him, who stood opposite him and did as those before him had done, until the completion of Musa's cycle.

`wa qama 'Isa 'an amri imami zamanihi`

(And 'Isa arose by the command of the Imam of his age.)

'Isa performed his qiyam by the command of the Imam of his age, who is Khuzayma. So the line of the Imams who command the Natiqs runs thus: Adam is established by Hunayd; Nuh by Hud; Ibrahim by Salih; Musa by 'Ud; 'Isa by Khuzayma; and the Prophet Muhammad by Abu Talib. 'Isa did as those before him had done, until his cycle was completed.

`wa qama Muhammad 'an amri imami zamanihi alladhi huwa Abu Talib 'alayhima al-salam wa kana lahu ziddani: Abu Lahab wa Abu Jahl la'anahumallahu`

(And Muhammad arose by the command of the Imam of his age, who is Abu Talib, upon them both be peace, and he had two opponents: Abu Lahab and Abu Jahl, may Allah curse them both.)

And the Prophet Muhammad, upon him be the most excellent blessings and peace, performed his qiyam by the command of the Imam of his age, who is Mawlana Abu Talib, peace be upon them both. For him there were two manifest opponents: Abu Lahab and Abu Jahl, may Allah curse them both. He fulfilled the service laid upon him and perfected the laws of his sacred order, which are symbols and indications pointing to the limits of Allah, the spiritual and the corporeal, which indicate the oneness of Allah, the Exalted, and His transcendence. Then he established his wasi, Mawlana Ali ibn Abi Talib, the blessings of Allah be upon them both, and passed on to the abode of Allah's honor.

A point of Arabic grammar illuminates the precision of the text here. The verb qama means "he stood, he arose" and is intransitive (lazim), requiring no object; but aqama means "he caused to stand, he established" and is transitive (muta'addi), demanding an object. Thus when the text says aqama wasiyyahu — "he established his wasi" — the object (the wasi) is grammatically necessary and present, whereas qama Amir al-Mu'minin — "Amir al-Mu'minin stood" — requires no object, for the verb is intransitive. This grammatical exactness mirrors the doctrinal exactness: the Natiq is established (the passive recipient of the Imam's command), while he himself stands and acts.

`wa qama Amir al-Mu'minin 'Aliyy-ibn Abi Talib salawatullahi 'alayhi ila wafa'i muddatihi wa nassa 'ala waladihi al-Hasan mustawda'an thumma 'ala waladihi al-Husayn mustaqarran salamullahi 'alayhima`

(And Amir al-Mu'minin Ali ibn Abi Talib, the blessings of Allah be upon him, stood until the completion of his term, and designated his son al-Hasan as the entrusted one, then his son al-Husayn as the established one, may the peace of Allah be upon them both.)

Amir al-Mu'minin, Ali ibn Abi Talib, the blessings of Allah be upon him, stood until the completion of his term, and performed the designation (nass) upon his son Imam Hasan as a mustawda' — an entrusted Imam — and then upon his son Husayn as a mustaqarr — an established Imam. May the peace of Allah be upon them both, and the Imamate ran on in succession thereafter.

We may now gather the parallel ranks of the wasis who interpreted the inner meaning for each Natiq. Adam's wasi was Shith, who followed after Habil; Nuh's was Sam; Ibrahim's was Isma'il; Musa's was Harun; 'Isa's — though the text does not explicitly name him in that place — was Sham'un al-Safa; and Muhammad's was Ali ibn Abi Talib, who designated Hasan as mustawda' and Husayn as mustaqarr. This roster of wasis completes the structure: every Natiq is paired with the foundation who interprets the batin of his law and carries the Imamate forward.

### The Question of Precedence and the Hujjats of Sayyidna Ja'far

A subtle matter of precedence (awwaliyyat) arises from this succession. The Natiqs were established first in the order of the luminous world but appeared later here in the corporeal world. The Prophet Muhammad is a case in point: in the sequence of the Natiqs he is the sixth to appear, yet his light was the first nur. How then is precedence to be reckoned?

The principle is decisive: the basis of precedence is not the light itself, but who reaches the homeland (watan) first. In the order of the world, Adam came first, then Nuh, then the others — but mere chronological appearance in the dunya is not the ground of awwaliyyat. The basis is not shown in the order of manifestation. The true measure is this: who reaches the watan, the homeland, first? The one who arrives first is the first. And so, despite the Natiqs being established first in the luminous order but appearing later in the world, the first remains the first; precedence is fixed by the arrival at the homeland, not by the sequence of bodily appearance.

And toward where do they all proceed and gather? They gather in the Qa'im — in the luminous structure (haykal) of the Qa'im of the Qa'ims. And where does the Qa'im gather? In the haykal of the jussa. And the jussa gathers as the jussa of the cosmic blueprint (Mawr). The final progress runs upward toward the tenth — the Ashir — and from the Ashir the cosmic body (al-Jussa al-Mawriyyah) ascends, taking, like a hudhud, the place of the ninth (Tasi'), then the eighth, ascending station by station until, before the Day of Unity (Dahr-i Wahdat), it reaches the Garden of Refuge (Jannat al-Ma'wa). For none can reach the Lote-Tree of the Boundary, the Sidrat al-Muntaha. The twenty-sixth blessed Da'i, Shah Sahib, expressed this ascent in words of longing: that such strength had been granted to his wings that he reached the nest, and that long had it been since he departed his home.

Here a note on the transmission of the very book before us belongs, for it bears upon the authority of this text. The book Anwaar al-Latifah was copied by Sayyidna Da'ud bin Ahmad, who is the first mansub — the first appointed deputy in rank — buried in Ahmedabad, near the resting place of Sayyidna Ali Sahib, beside Sayyidna Ibrahim. He was of the time of Sayyidna Hasan Khan Sahib, the Da'i Ghayr Munfarid, who passed away in the year 1107. When Sayyidna Hasan Khan Sahib became the non-individual Da'i, he appointed Sayyidna Da'ud bin Ahmad as mansub. Da'ud bin Ahmad himself passed away in 1108, in the month of Safar, having led the mission individually as the first mansub in rank for one year. This is the very Da'ud bin Ahmad who copied this excellent book from an old manuscript.

And within this copied text, Sayyidna Ja'far has left his marks. Where there was a point of debate, where proofs (hujjat) were to be drawn forth, Sayyidna Ja'far derived them by the method of istimbat — the extraction of a hidden meaning — from the original text, and a mark was made at each such place. In two places these marks appear: "a mark is here, a mark is here." This is Sayyidna Ja'far's mark, indicating where, from the subtle point (latifah) embedded in the lights (anwar) of the book, he made his derivation.

The meaning of istimbat is worth pausing over. The word derives from the root nabata — nun, ba, ta — whose original sense is to draw water up from a well, hence to bring forth what was hidden. Istambata thus means to reveal something concealed, to extract a proof (dalil) from a text. In the technical sense, it is what a jurist (faqih) or scholar ('alim) does when, by his own understanding, he extracts the inner, esoteric (batini) meaning of the Quran and the Hadith, bringing forth an excellent and correct opinion (ra'yan hasanan). The form istimbat follows the pattern istif'al from the root nabata. So the marks of Sayyidna Ja'far point precisely to the places where, from the latifah present in the lights of this book, he performed his istimbat — his extraction of inner proof — and the copyist Da'ud bin Ahmad faithfully reproduced those pointers from the original manuscript, that we who read after him might know where the proofs were drawn.

### The Line of Imams to Muhammad ibn Isma'il and the Tayyibi Cycles

The Imamate, having passed through Hasan and Husayn, runs on in its appointed succession down to the seventh Natiq of this dispensation:

`wa jarat al-imamatu mutasalsilatan ila tamami dawr al-A'imma alladhi huwa Muhammad ibn Isma'il`

(And the Imamate ran on in succession until the completion of the cycle of the Imams, which is Muhammad ibn Isma'il.)

The Imamate continued in succession until the completion of the cycle of the Imams, which is Mawlana Muhammad ibn Isma'il, peace be upon him. The line runs: Imam Hasan, Imam Husayn, then Imam Zayn al-'Abidin, then Imam Muhammad al-Baqir, then Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, then Mawlana Isma'il, and seventh is Mawlana Muhammad ibn Isma'il, the blessings of Allah be upon them all. He is of a most exalted rank.

`thumma qama awwal al-a'immat al-khulafa'i wa huwa 'Abdullah ibn Muhammad ibn Isma'il salamullahi 'alayhim jami'an`

(Then the first of the Imams of the Caliphate arose, and he is Abdullah ibn Muhammad ibn Isma'il, may the peace of Allah be upon them all.)

A question arises whether Muhammad ibn Isma'il and the other Imams of this cycle stand together with Mawlana Husayn, or whether Husayn holds a station apart. The answer is that Mawlana Husayn's status is a separate one. For the Prophet, upon him be blessings and peace, said concerning Imam Hasan and Imam Husayn: "O Ali, you two will be the first to come to me at the Hawd al-Kawthar." By this saying he declared them included with him, with the Prophet, among the Panjatan Pak, the Five Pure Ones. And Mawlana Muhammad ibn Isma'il will be joined with them; his rank is very high. In the cycle of unveiling his ranks will be exalted, for he acquires them from the horizon (ufuq) of the Prophet, from Imam Hasan and Imam Husayn.

Then the first of the Imams of the Caliphate (a'immat al-khulafa') arose, and he is Abdullah ibn Muhammad ibn Isma'il, may the peace of Allah be upon them all. This cycle of the caliphal Imams continued onward:

`fa-ntarada dhalika ila wafa'i dawrihim bi-Mawlana al-Imam al-Mu'izz li-Din-illah salawatullahi 'alayhi`

(So that continued until the completion of their cycle with Mawlana al-Imam al-Mu'izz li-Din-illah, the blessings of Allah be upon him.)

So that cycle continued in this manner until their term was completed with Mawlana al-Imam al-Mu'izz li-Din-illah, the blessings of Allah be upon him. Just as the seventh Qa'im gathers the six before him, so does each culminating Imam gather his predecessors.

`wa qama awwal dawr al-ashhad wa huwa Mawlana al-'Aziz billah salamullahi 'alayhim ajma'in wa-ntarada dhalika ila wafa'i dawrihim bi-Mawlana al-Imam al-Tayyib Abi'l-Qasim Amir al-Mu'minin salawatullahi 'alayhi wa 'ajma'in`

(And the first of the cycle of the witnesses arose, and he is Mawlana al-'Aziz billah, may the peace of Allah be upon them all, and that continued until the completion of their cycle with Mawlana al-Imam al-Tayyib Abi'l-Qasim Amir al-Mu'minin, the blessings of Allah be upon him and upon them all.)

Then the first of the cycle of the witnesses (dawr al-ashhad) arose, and he is Mawlana al-'Aziz billah, the peace of Allah be upon them all; and that cycle continued until its completion with Mawlana al-Imam al-Tayyib Abi'l-Qasim Amir al-Mu'minin, the blessings of Allah be upon him.

`wa qama awwal a'immat al-abdal wa huwa waladu Mawlana al-Tayyib 'alayhi al-salam wa yantaridu al-amru kadhalika ila wafa'i dawrihim bi-sabi'ihim 'alayhim al-salam`

(And the first of the Imams of the substitutes arose, and he is the son of Mawlana al-Tayyib, upon him be peace, and the affair continues thus until the completion of their cycle with their seventh, upon them be peace.)

Then the first of the Imams of the substitutes (a'immat al-abdal) arose, and he is the son of Mawlana al-Tayyib, peace be upon him; and the affair continues in this way until the completion of their cycle with their seventh, peace be upon them.

Within this Tayyibi era, the very opening formula of revelation, the Bismillah, is given an interpretation referred to the persons of the Imams:

`Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim`

In this interpretation, said of the dawr al-tayyibi: Allah is Mawlana Muhammad ibn Isma'il; the name (al-ism in bi-ism) is Imam al-Tayyib, the twenty-first Imam, peace be upon him; and al-Rahman al-Rahim, the two attributes, are Ma'add and Ma'add — that is, the fourteenth Imam, al-Mu'izz, and the twenty-eighth Imam, Ahmad. Thus the supreme persons of the Tayyibi line are read into the divine formula: Muhammad ibn Isma'il standing as the locus of the divine name, Imam al-Tayyib as the name itself, and the two Ma'adds as the attributes of mercy.

The grammar of the formula deepens the doctrine. Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim admits of two constructions: a genitive construction (tarkib-i idafi), "the name of Allah," and a descriptive construction (tarkib-i tawsifi), "Allah's attribute." The genitive construction stands far higher than the descriptive, for the name is greater than the attribute. So in bi-ism, the ism — the name — is Imam al-Tayyib, while Allah is Mawlana Muhammad ibn Isma'il; and al-Rahman al-Rahim are the attributes, Ma'add and Ma'add. The exalted ranks of these persons are such that they themselves have become the name of Allah and His attributes — a station expressed in the prayer of self-effacement: "May I be so erased that only You remain; may Your existence be proven by my negation."

`thumma qama hujjat-u Qa'im al-Qiyamah 'ala dhikrihima al-salam wa wufiyat saba'at alaf al-sana allati hiya dawr al-sitr alladhi huwa al-dunya`

(Then the Hujjah of the Qa'im of the Resurrection arose, upon whose mention be peace, and the seven thousand years which are the cycle of concealment, which is the world, were fulfilled.)

After the line of al-Tayyib reaches its term, the Hujjah of the Qa'im al-Qiyamah arises, upon whose mention be peace; and with this the seven thousand years which are the dawr al-sitr — which is the dunya, the world itself — are fulfilled. As the Natiq of the cycle, peace be upon them, said: "The age of the world is seven thousand years" (`'umr al-dunya saba'at alaf sana`), pointing to this very cycle of concealment. These seven thousand years run from Adam, peace be upon him, the first mainstay of the cycle of concealment, to the rise of the Hujjah of the Qa'im — the full span of the world's hidden age.

### The Seventh Natiq, the Veil of Ayn Azimah, and the Conferral of Rank

We come now to a station of great subtlety, where the office of the Natiq al-Sabi' — the seventh Natiq — is unfolded in two senses that must be carefully distinguished. The text speaks of the Imams who, having passed away, are stationed in the horizon of the Tenth Intellect (ufuq al-'aql al-'ashir), awaiting the coming of the Qa'im:

`mawqufina fi ufuq al-'aql al-'ashir kama sabaqa bihil-qawl ila qiyam al-Qa'im 'alayhi al-salam wa huwa al-natiq al-sabi' al-qa'im wa muqamuhu fi khalq al-akhar alladhi huwa al-ruh li-al-jism`

(Stationed in the horizon of the Tenth Intellect, as the statement preceded concerning them, until the standing of the Qa'im, upon him be peace, and he is the seventh Natiq, the Qa'im, and his station is in the final creation, which is the spirit for the body.)

The Imam who passes away is stationed upon the 'aql al-'ashir, the Tenth Intellect — which is to say, upon the supplementary part (jus'-i idha'i) that occupies the horizon of the tenth station. There the Imams of the cycle of unveiling abide, their statement directed toward the Qa'im, until their Qa'im appears. And who is the Qa'im? The Imam of the Imams is called the Qa'im. He is the Natiq al-Sabi', the seventh Natiq, and his station is in the khalq al-akhar — the final creation, the creation of the hereafter — wherein he will establish the resurrection. He is to that final creation as the spirit is to the body; his status is that of the soul.

Now the two senses of "seventh Natiq" must be held apart. In one sense, Sayyidna Muhammad ibn Isma'il is called the Natiq al-Sabi'. But the original Natiq al-Sabi' is the Qa'im al-Qiyamah, who is to appear in the future. How then is the title given to Muhammad ibn Isma'il? The resolution is this: because the rank of Muhammad ibn Isma'il became very elevated — higher than the Qa'ims of the Imams — therefore that point (nuqtah) which constitutes the rank of the Natiq al-Sabi' was conferred upon him. He, Muhammad ibn Isma'il, is in truth the Natiq al-Sadis, the sixth Natiq, and the gatherer of the six speaking-prophets (nutuqa'); but because his station rose so high, the point of the seventh's rank was bestowed upon him.

The structure of the ascent must be traced precisely. The Prophet, upon him be blessings and peace, is the sixth Natiq. The rank of the seventh has become greater even than that of the Prophet; the seventh's station comes after that of the Natiq al-Sadis. The Natiq al-Sadis is Sayyidna Muhammad ibn Isma'il, and the original Natiq al-Sabi' is the future Qa'im. In one sense the Natiq al-Sabi' is the one who gathers all six speaking-prophets — this is one meaning of the title. And the Natiq al-Sabi' referred to here as Sayyidna Mawlana Muhammad ibn Isma'il is also a gatherer of the six. But his rank of 'Ayn Azimah is high; he has reached the station of veiling (hijabiyyah), which stands higher than the rank of the Natiq al-Sabi'.

`wa qad balagha al-hijabiyyah allati hiya a'la min al-natiq al-sabi'`

(And he has reached the station of veiling, which is higher than the seventh Natiq.)

By attaining hijabiyyah, Muhammad ibn Isma'il has gained superiority (fadilat) over all the entrusted Imams (mustawda'in) and all the established Imams (mustaqirrin), up to and including the Qa'im. For this reason Sayyidna Mawlana Muhammad ibn Isma'il is higher and more elevated than them all.

The Prophet's own final station must here be understood, for it is the summit of this hierarchy:

`maqam al-Nabi al-akhir huwa al-hijabiyyah al-maqam al-mahmud`

(The final station of the Prophet is the veiling, the praiseworthy station.)

The Prophet, Janab Payghambar, upon him be blessings and peace, occupies in his final station the hijabiyyah — which is the maqam mahmud, the Praiseworthy Station — the veil of 'Ayn Azimah. At this station of veiling, the Prophet holds superiority over all the entrusted Imams and all the established Imams, up to the Qa'im. Below the hijabiyyah lies one point, the nuqtah of the Natiq al-Sadis; and at that point of the sixth Natiq stands Janab Mawlana Muhammad ibn Isma'il. After that comes a further point, the nuqtah of the Natiq al-Sabi', which belongs to the Qa'im al-Qiyamah. From that point the Qa'im was elevated; being elevated, he made 'Ayn Azimah his minister (wazir), and he himself became the standing — the qiyam — of that entire cycle, the Qa'im of that whole dawr. In this manner does the elevation of the points (nuqat) occur: a rank ascends, the office below it becomes its ministry, and the ascended one becomes the master of the cycle.

### The Great Gathering: The Connection of All Stations to the Qa'im

The passing of the seventh Qa'im opens the beginning of the dawr al-sitr's new arrangement, but the text returns now to the great gathering by which the Qa'im draws all the noble stations into himself. The nafs of the master of the Qa'im — the soul of the Qa'im who is the spirit to the body — becomes the locus of a vast connection:

`wa ittasalat bihi hadhihi al-maqamat al-sharifah ka-ittisal suwar al-mushtami'ati 'indal-bab bi-nafsil-imam al-mawlud`

(And these noble stations became connected to him, like the connection of the forms gathered near the Bab with the soul of the born Imam.)

All these noble stations become connected to him in the very manner that the forms (suwar) gathered near the Bab connect with the soul (nafs) of the Imam who is born. Near the bab al-abwab, the gate of gates, all the forms gather; and once gathered, they become connected with the soul of the newly born Imam. In exactly this fashion, all the forms that are there — and the original resident (muqim) of these forms is the future Qa'im, the Qa'im of the dawr al-sitr — become connected to the Qa'im. The Qa'im who came before is in reality the resident; he is the partial (juzwi) successor of the supplementary part (jus'-i idha'i), and the universal (kulli) successor of the previous one, who is the Qa'im of the dawr al-sitr. Across all these fifty-seven thousand years, whatever forms were elevated gathered with the Imams there, and those Imams in turn became connected with the Qa'im, just as the forms gather with the born Imam near the Bab.

`fa-yakunu nafsuhu ruhan li-hadhihi al-maqamat fa-tasiru hiya wa iyyahu suratan wahidah, haykalan nuraniyyan, wa 'aqlan qudsaniyyan`

(So his soul becomes a spirit for these stations, so that they and he become a single form, a luminous structure, and a holy intellect.)

So the soul of that born Imam becomes a spirit (ruh) for all these stations that have reached there. And then they and he — his soul and all of them together — become a single form (suratan wahidah), a luminous structure (haykal nurani), and a holy intellect ('aql qudsani). The forms of the fifty-seven thousand years — of the dawr al-kashf and the dawr al-sitr alike — all come and become his luminous structure. The soul of the born Imam and the forms of the fifty-seven thousand become one thing, one intellect. Here the question of "first" and "last" loses its force entirely: all the Imams who appeared throughout the dawr al-kashf and the dawr al-sitr, and with them all the believers, and all the hudud — all of them, joined to the luminous structure of their own Imam, become participants in the luminous structure of the Qa'im. There an ocean is gathered, and the small things are gathered there too. One luminous structure is formed, and one holy intellect, complete in its limbs (tamm al-a'da') and perfect in its senses (kamil al-hawass) — for there the arrangement and completion of the limbs takes place, and the perfection of all the senses.

This gathering is patterned upon the seven horizons that preceded it from the cycle of unveiling:

`as-sab'u ufuq allati taqaddamatuhu min daur al-kashf`

(The seven horizons which preceded him from the cycle of unveiling.)

Just as the master of the dawr al-kashf had a cycle of fifty thousand years, and just as a great gathering happened there among the seven Qa'ims, so here an even greater gathering takes place. The seven horizons are the seven Qa'ims of the dawr al-kashf; as they gathered there, so this born Imam attains his rank by the same pattern of gathering.

`fa-yashtami'u ilayhi kaffat ahl daur al-sitr, al-awwalina minhum wa al-akhirin`

(So all the people of the cycle of concealment are gathered to him, the first of them and the last of them.)

To this born Imam, who is the first Imam of the dawr al-kashf, are gathered all the people of the dawr al-sitr — the first of them and the last of them, without exception. The scripture confirms this gathering:

`qul innal-awwalina wal-akhirin la-majmu'una ila miqati yawmin ma'lum`

(Say: indeed, the former and the later peoples will surely be gathered for the appointed time of a known Day.)

`wa yuhasibu kulla wahidin minhum wa yuwaqifuhum 'ala ma taqaddama min qalilin wa kathir`

(And he will take account of every single one of them, and make them stand upon what has passed, be it little or much.)

He takes the account of each one of them, and makes each aware of all that has passed, whether little or much.

`wa yatashakhasu kullu imamin wa hududihi bi-ahl dawrihim`

(And every Imam and his limits will be personified with the people of their cycle.)

Every Imam, together with his hudud, will be personified and made manifest with the people of his own cycle, so that each may be given his reward (jaza) and his punishment ('adhab). Every person will see his Imam and will see his hudud. The scripture attests:

`wa ji'a bil-nabiyyina wal-shuhada'i wa qudiya baynahum bil-haqqi wa hum la yuzlamun`

(And the prophets and the witnesses will be brought forward, and a just decision will be passed between them, and they will not be wronged.)

The prophets and the witnesses are brought forward, a just decision is made among them, and no injustice is done to them.

`wa yuwaqifuhum 'ala jami'i af'alihim kama nataqa bihi al-kitab al-mubin mukhbiran min qawlihim`

(And He will make them stand upon all their deeds, as the clear Book spoke of it, informing of their saying.)

He makes them aware of all their deeds, as the Clear Book has spoken, informing of their cry. For at that station all who are present will cry out:

`ya waylatana, mali hadhal-kitab la yughadiru saghiratan wa la kabiratan illa ahsaha`

(Woe to us! What is this book that leaves neither a small thing nor a great one, but has accounted for it?)

This is the book that will be given — concerning which we always pray in the presence (hudur): `Allahumma a'tini kitabi bi-yamini wa la tu'tinihi bi-shimali` (O Allah, give me my book in my right hand, and do not give it to me in my left hand), and `Allahumma la tu'tini kitabi bi-shimali wa la min wara'i zahri` (O Allah, do not give me my book in my left hand, nor from behind my back). The book leaves out neither small thing nor great, but has enumerated all; every sin and every good deed is reckoned within it.

Within this enumeration there is a hidden treasure that the scripture of supplication discloses, concerning the virtues of du'a. A person makes many supplications in this world, yet his supplications are not accepted, and then he passes away. When he is presented in the court of Allah, the Exalted, some of his rewards are placed in his scale of deeds — rewards for which he finds no corresponding act. He will say: "O Lord, I did not perform these deeds for which I am being rewarded." It will be said to him: "These were your supplications which you made in the world, which We did not accept at that time; now We are giving you their recompense." When the believer beholds the magnificence of this stored reward, he will cry out in longing that none of his supplications had ever been hastened in their acceptance, and that the whole of them had been kept and treasured up for this day. The Arabic of his saying is preserved:

`wa yaqulu ya rabbi layta innaka lam tu'ajjil li shay'an wa akhkhartahu li kullahu ila hadhal-yawm`

(And he will say: O my Lord, would that You had not hastened anything for me, but had delayed all of it for me until this day.)

The servant, in the world, wishes his prayer accepted as soon as he utters it; but a day will come when he wishes the opposite — that his prayer had not been answered then, but had been laid up entirely against the reckoning. So the unaccepted supplications are not lost: they are stored, and rewarded at the gathering, with a recompense whose magnificence makes the believer regret that any of his prayers were ever answered in haste.

`wa huwa al-rab al-muthib wal-mu'aqib fa-hina'idhin yasiru al-thawabu kulli, wa yaqumu al-'iqab al-kulli`

(And he is the Lord who rewards and who punishes, so at that time the reward becomes total, and the total punishment is established.)

The Qa'im, peace be upon his mention, is himself the book that leaves out neither small nor great — every point will be clear in him. He is the Lord who gives the reward and who gives the punishment. And at that time the reward is rendered in its totality (thawab kulli) and the punishment in its totality ('iqab kulli). The discussion on both these states — reward and punishment — will come later in its place, if Allah the Exalted wills.

A distinction must be drawn between the two cycles regarding this reckoning. In the dawr al-kashf, the manner of reward and punishment that constitutes the resurrection does not operate as it does in concealment, because there the forms are manifested in such a way that no outstanding retributions (qisasat) remain upon them; in the cycle of unveiling, only those persons come for whom there are no past liabilities, no retribution, no remaining animosity. But in the dawr al-sitr, through the seven thousand years, the deeds are of a kind from which reward (thawab) and punishment ('iqab) arise, for there the give-and-take between servants persists. Allah, the Exalted, forgives His own due, but the give-and-take that lies between the servants He does not simply cancel; the servant who was wronged must come and forgive, or some path must be made — as through ransoms (fikakat), where, in place of strict retribution, another form of the owed thing reaches the one with whom the enmity stood. The word fikak comes from fakk, "he freed him from bondage": just as a captive is freed, so the debt is settled by an equivalent reaching its rightful claimant. Until such settlement, some give-and-take remains for someone; and so in the dawr al-kashf only those free of all retribution may enter, while the full settling belongs to the resurrection of the dawr al-sitr.

### The Two Spans of Formation: Seven Thousand and Ten Thousand Years

We turn now to a question of measure that the text poses directly: why does the perfected form of the Qa'im require ten thousand years to be prepared in the cycle of concealment, while in the cycle of unveiling the form is perfected in seven thousand? The governing statement reads:

`wa innama li-safa'i ahl al-dawr al-kashf wa-izhar al-'ulum al-hikmiyyah 'ala ru'us al-ashhad, kamulat al-surah allati hiya dhalika al-'aql al-nurani wa-al-haykal al-tufsani fi muddat-i sab'at-i alaf-i sanah. Wa-li-kathafat-i ahl al-dawr al-sitr wa-shukukihim wa-takaddurihim wa-kathrat-i imtihanihim, kana kamalu hadhihi al-surah allati hiya huwa qa'im al-qiyamah, salamullah 'ala dhikrihim jami'an, li-'ashar-i alaf-i sanah.`

(And it is only on account of the purity of the people of the cycle of unveiling, and the manifestation of the sciences of wisdom upon the heads of the witnesses, that the form which is that luminous intellect and the perfected structure was completed in the span of seven thousand years. And on account of the density of the people of the cycle of concealment, and their doubts, and their turbidity, and the abundance of their trials, the completion of this form, which is the Qa'im of the Resurrection — may the peace of Allah be upon their mention, all of them — was over ten thousand years.)

The principle is this. In the dawr al-kashf, every seven thousand years one Qa'im appears; but in the dawr al-sitr, the Qa'im appears only after ten thousand years. The reason lies in the very atmosphere of the two cycles. In the dawr al-kashf there is purity (safa') among the people, and the sciences of wisdom (al-'ulum al-hikmiyyah) are manifested openly upon the heads of the witnesses, declared without concealment. In such a clear medium, the perfected form — which is the luminous intellect ('aql nurani) and the perfected structure (haykal) — is completed in seven thousand years. But in the dawr al-sitr the people are characterized by density (kathafat), by doubts (shukuk), by turbidity (takaddur) and corruption, and by the abundance of their trials (kathrat al-imtihan). On account of all this thickness and disturbance, the completion of the form that is the Qa'im al-Qiyamah requires ten thousand years rather than seven.

The arithmetic of the ten thousand must be made exact. In the dawr al-kashf a resurrection (qiyamah) occurs every seven thousand years, so that seven Qa'ims arise across the fifty thousand years, fashioning seven forms, each prepared in seven thousand years. But in the dawr al-sitr one Qa'im comes after ten thousand years, and this ten is composed of the seven thousand years of concealment proper plus the three thousand years of interval (fatrat). The three thousand fatrat years, though they fall chronologically at the close of the dawr al-kashf, belong by their disposition to the domain of the thought of the dawr al-sitr; they are reckoned with concealment, not with unveiling. Thus the seven thousand of pure concealment and the three thousand of turbid interval together make the ten thousand years over which the single Qa'im of the dawr al-sitr is prepared. Where there is no turbidity and no doubt, and the sciences are spread openly, seven thousand years suffice; where the turbidity of the interval, the doubts, and the abundance of trials are present, ten thousand are required.

`ma'a anna kulla wahidin min hadhihi al-usul al-mustakhrajah min 'alam al-tabi'ah fi dawr al-kashf li-sab'at-i alaf-i sanah wa-fi dawr al-sitr li-'ashar-i alaf-i sanah`

(Along with the fact that each one of these principles, extracted from the world of nature, in the cycle of unveiling is for seven thousand years, and in the cycle of concealment is for ten thousand years.)

This holds together with the fact that each one of these foundational principles (usul), which are extracted from the world of nature ('alam al-tabi'ah), takes seven thousand years in the dawr al-kashf and ten thousand years in the dawr al-sitr — the same difference of medium producing the same difference of duration.

### The Partial and Universal Qa'im of the Resurrection

The text now establishes the universal Qa'im at the summit of all reckoning, and then unfolds the doctrine that every rank in the hierarchy is itself a partial resurrection for those beneath it.

`wa huwa qa'imun qiyamat al-kulliyyatin, ilayhi al-ma'abu wa-al-hisabu wa-ladayhi al-thawabu wa-al-'iqab`

(And he is the standing one of the universal resurrection; to him is the return and the reckoning, and with him are the reward and the punishment.)

He is the Qa'im of the universal resurrection (qiyamat al-kulliyyah). To him is the return (ma'ab) and the reckoning (hisab), and with him are the reward (thawab) and the punishment ('iqab). Every seven thousand years there is a resurrection — whether in the dawr al-kashf or the dawr al-sitr; in the latter, after ten thousand years there is a resurrection — and in the hand of the Qa'im are the reward and the punishment.

`wa huwa al-musharu ilayhi bi-al-ilahiyyah fi hadhihi al-dar`

(And he is the one indicated by divinity in this abode.)

He is the one toward whom the indication of divinity (ilahiyyah) is made, in this abode. For his own seven thousand years he stands as the Ilah — that station of divinity, that central circle around which all others revolve, bewildered as to how he attained this station. (A textual note: some copies read fi hadhihi al-dar, "in this abode," using the feminine dar; others read fi hadha al-dawr, "in this cycle," using the masculine dawr — and both are correct in their meaning, for dar is feminine and dawr masculine, and the copy descends from a Da'i's hand in which the feminine form was written.)

`wa huwa al-natiqu bi-dhalika yawma ijtima' al-khala'iq bayna yadayhi`

(And he is the speaker of that on the day of the gathering of the creatures before him.)

And he is the speaker (natiq) of that matter on the day when the creatures are gathered before him. Every seven thousand years there is one Natiq who assumes this capacity — for the office of the Natiq is the position of prophethood — and he speaks with authority concerning reward and punishment, declaring to whom each is due. That Qa'im speaks that matter on the day when the creatures are gathered before him.

`wa kullu natiqin wa kullu wasiyyin wa kullu imami al-zaman huwa qa'im al-qiyamah juzwiyyah`

(And every Natiq, and every wasi, and every Imam of the age, is a Qa'im of the resurrection in a partial sense.)

Though the Imam of the age is, in the universal sense, the future Qa'im al-Qiyamah, yet every Natiq, every wasi, and every Imam of the age is also, in his own way, a Qa'im al-Qiyamah — an establisher of the resurrection — but in a partial (juzwi) sense. He too is a Qa'im al-Qiyamah in one sense, because for all the people who come in his cycle, he is the very locus of their reward and their requital. So it was said to the Prophet: "Be a witness (shahid)" — you will be a witness over the deeds of all.

`wa kadhalika man yatluuhu min babin wa-hujjatin wa-da'in wa-ma'dhunin. Wa-kullu wahidin minhum qa'im al-qiyamah li-intihai man yantahi ilayhi min al-suwar al-mufariqah al-ka'inah 'indahu`

(And likewise the one who comes after him, whether a Bab, a Hujjah, a Da'i, or a Ma'dhun. And every one of them is a Qa'im of the resurrection, for the termination of those whose end is with him, of the separated forms that come to be in his presence.)

And so likewise for the one who comes after the Imam — whether a Bab, a Hujjah, a Da'i, or a Ma'dhun. Every one of these ranks is a Qa'im al-Qiyamah for those persons whose end is with him: for those who return to him, the separated forms (al-suwar al-mufariqah) that come to be in his presence.

`li-annahu huwa al-qa'imu biha. Wa-kullu wahidin min ha'ula'i qa'im al-qiyamah juzwiyyah. Wa-alladhi huwa fawqahu qa'im al-qiyamah kulliyyah`

(Because he is the one who establishes them. And every one of these is a partial Qa'im of the resurrection, while the one who is above him is a universal Qa'im of the resurrection.)

Each rank is a Qa'im al-Qiyamah because he is the one who establishes those who return to him. And every one of these ranks is a Qa'im al-Qiyamah in a partial sense, while the rank that stands above him is, in relation to him, the Qa'im al-Qiyamah in a universal sense. So the partiality and universality are relative: wherever the text said "universal," the rank above that is in turn partial to a still higher one; and wherever it said "partial," the rank above is its universal.

`'ala ma sabaqa bihi al-qawl ila an yantahi al-jami'u ila qa'im al-kull al-qiyamat alladhi huwa sahib al-qiyamat al-'uzma wa-al-batshat al-kubra`

(According to what the statement has preceded, until all of them reach the Qa'im of the entire resurrection, who is the master of the greatest resurrection and the greatest seizure.)

So the ascent continues, rank giving way to higher rank, until all of them reach that Qa'im who is the Qa'im of the entire resurrection — the master of the Greatest Resurrection (al-qiyamat al-'uzma) and the Greatest Seizure (al-batshat al-kubra). The scripture seals this:

`yawma nabtishu al-batshat al-kubra, inna muntaqimun`

(The Day when We will inflict the greatest seizure; indeed We are avengers.)

The word batsh signifies a violent striking, a seizing with force, a pouncing upon. To stand against the enemy and strike with overwhelming might — "when you strike, you strike as tyrants (jabbarin)" — this is batsh. So the verse means: the Day when We will inflict the greatest seizure, We are indeed among the avengers, taking a severe and crushing requital. And the discourse closes upon glorification:

`fa-subhana man hadhihi al-sunnatu sunnatuh wa-hadhihi al-qudratu qudratuh wa-la ilaha illa alladhi awjada mujidu hadhihi al-khala'iq 'ala tabayuni anwa'iha. Wa-al-hamdu lillahi rabb al-'alamin. Wa-salla Allahu 'ala rasulihi Sayyidna Muhammadin wa-alihi al-tayyibin al-tahirin.`

(So glorified is He whose way this way is, and whose power this power is; and there is no god but the One who originated, the Originator of these creatures upon the diversity of their kinds. And praise be to Allah, Lord of the worlds, and may Allah bless His Messenger, our master Muhammad, and his pure and immaculate progeny.)

Glorified is the Being whose way this is and whose power this is; there is no god but the One who originated and gives existence to these creatures in all the diversity of their kinds. All praise belongs to Allah, the Lord of the worlds, and may Allah's peace be upon His Messenger, Sayyidna Muhammad, and his pure progeny.

### The Primordial Body and the Manifestation of the Assigned Points

The text now returns to a foundational doctrine: that all the points which will appear up to the universal resurrection are already present, from the beginning, within the primordial initial body — the jussa ibda'iyyah of the first blueprint (mawr-i awwal). Their manifestation is only a matter of appointed time.

All the points that will appear until the qiyamat al-kulliyyah are present in the jussa ibda'iyyah of the first mawr. The points are there from the start; their manifestation (zuhur) occurs as the time for each arrives. The principle of governance follows from this: whoever holds the mawr for a given cosmic cycle (qawr) holds the governance (tadbir) of that qawr. Whoever, in the luminous world ('alam-i nurani), accepted the governance of a particular scope, holds the governance to that very scope: if his charge is one qawr, his governance extends to one qawr and no further; if ten qawrs, then ten; if four hundred qawrs, then four hundred. The measure of governance corresponds exactly to the measure of what each one accepted in the luminous world.

The one who holds the mawr governs its qawr — for the mawr is the blueprint of the form and its completion — until he prepares a successor (janashin) like himself, whereupon he is elevated from his station. This holds for both the partial and the universal. A universal Qa'im (qa'im-i kulli) prepares a universal successor like himself; a partial Qa'im (qa'im-i juzwi) prepares a partial successor like himself. The partial succession runs through the son; but "one like himself" does not mean merely his son in the universal sense — it means one who carries the full measure of his charge. If his Qa'imship is for fifty-seven thousand years, he must prepare a Qa'im for fifty-seven thousand years; if for a cycle (dawr) of four hundred thousand years, he must prepare one like himself for four hundred thousand years. Only then is he elevated. Until that successor is prepared, the small points of the cycle remain latent, manifesting as the opportunity arises, each at its appointed time.

This correspondence between the assigned order and its manifestation extends to the relation between the Imam and his subordinate ranks. In the World of Command ('alam-i amr), all the stations were assigned — those of the twelve, those of the seven. The question arises whether this same correspondence obtains in the World of Intention ('alam-i niyyat) and the Physical World ('alam-i jismani). When the creation of the physical and natural worlds took place, there was a correspondence: the mawr-i awwal is alone, yet a correspondence was established. So here too the Imams hold the governance of the hudud, and with the Imams stands the governance of the Hujjah, and a correspondence of governance occurs — for it is necessary that whatever happens in the heavens find its reflection upon the earth.

`fa-yakunu nafs sahib al-qa'im qiranan li-babihi`

(So the soul of the master of the Qa'im becomes a conjunction with his Bab.)

In the World of Intention, when the Imam, peace be upon him, stepped forward, there came forward, in correspondence, one who was called the Bab. When the Imam manifests, his Bab manifests with him; they manifest together in a dual capacity (qiran), a conjunction. The original point is there, and according to it the manifestation occurs. Likewise, when the Qa'im al-Qiyamah comes, he has his own individual governance; but the other points subordinate to him — the 'ayn, the mim, and his jadd, fath, and khayal — are all shown in a dual capacity. So too his physical kin reflect the original assigned points: Mawla Abd al-Muttalib is the jadd of the Qa'im; Mawla Abu Talib is the fath; his mothers are the mothers of the Qa'im. All of these stand in a conjunction (qiran) with their luminous originals, a correspondence reflecting the original assigned points down into the world of manifestation.

### The Two Ways of Human Existence: From the Earth and Through Lineage

The discourse now descends from the cosmic to the human, to unfold how the gnostic human form comes into being. Two ways of man's existence are established at the outset:

`wa-awdahna fi dhalika ma awdahna min zuhur al-qamat al-'arafiyyah al-insaniyyah nabatan min al-ard, kama qala Allahu ta'ala: wallahu anbatakum min al-ardi nabata`

(And we have clarified concerning that what we have clarified regarding the manifestation of the gnostic human form as a plant from the earth, as Allah the Exalted said: And Allah caused you to grow from the earth as a plant.)

The gnostic human form (qamat al-'arafiyyah al-insaniyyah) manifests as a plant (nabat) from the earth, as Allah, the Exalted, said: "And Allah caused you to grow from the earth as a plant." This is the first way. The text then announces its intention to treat the second:

`fa-nuridu an natakallama 'ala wujudihi wa-al-munakahatihi wa-taraqqi nafsihi rutbatan rutbatan, li-yahiqqa li-qari'i kitabana hadha wujud al-shakhs al-basari aydan mutasalsilan`

(So we intend to speak about his existence, and about inter-marriage, and the ascent of his soul rank by rank, so that the existence of the physical person in a continuous series may be confirmed for the reader of this book of ours.)

We intend now to speak of man's existence by way of inter-marriage and lineage (munakahat), and of the ascent of his soul rank by rank, so that the reader of this book may be assured of the existence of the physical person also in a continuous, unbroken series. The word munakahat derives from the root nakaha, "to marry," whose reciprocal verbal noun tanakuh means "to marry one another." This belongs to a recognized Arabic pattern of mutual action: as musafahah is to greet and join hands with one another, musadaqah to be truthful with one another, munasabah to have a connection with one another, muhadathah to converse with one another (for a single person one says hadatha, but when two or more speak together it is muhadathah), and mula'anah to curse one another — so munakahat is to enter into marriage with one another. There are seventeen augmented patterns of the verb, and this reciprocal pattern is one of them; it is the pattern employed here to denote the joining of lineages.

`kama qala Allahu ta'ala: wa-laqad khalaqna al-insana min sulalatin min tin. Thumma ja'alnahu nutfatan fi qararin makin`

(As Allah the Exalted said: And indeed We created man from an extract of clay. Then We made him a drop in a firm resting place.)

As Allah, the Almighty, said: "And indeed We created man from an extract of clay (sulalatin min tin); then We made him a drop (nutfah) in a firm resting place (qararin makin)." So one may know that man's existence is from two ways: from a plant grown out of the earth, and from munakahat, from lineage and the joining of marriages. The first way — growth as a plant — occurs through the Da'is and their like, in the spiritual gardens; and the second, through marriage and descent, follows after.

### The Dispersal of the Formal Apparitions and Their Purification by Trial

The text now narrates the cosmic descent by which the formal apparitions fell into density and were dispersed for purification:

`fa-naqulu: inna tilka al-ashbah al-suwariyyah lamma waqa'at fi al-ab'ad al-thalathah bima kana minha min al-zallati wa-al-khati'ati wa-ba'udat wa-takaththafat wa-takaddarat, 'amadat al-'inayat al-ilahiyyah ila tabdidiha wa-tafriqiha fi al-aflak wa-al-ummahat 'ala ma sabaqa minhu al-qawl`

(So we say: indeed those formal apparitions, when they fell into the three dimensions on account of the slip and the error that came from them, and became distant and dense and turbid, the Divine Providence proceeded to disperse them and separate them among the celestial spheres and the mothers, according to what the discussion has preceded.)

Those formal apparitions (al-ashbah al-suwariyyah), when they fell into the three dimensions (al-ab'ad al-thalathah) by reason of the slip (zallah) and the error (khati'ah) that came from them, became distant, dense (takaththafat), and turbid (takaddarat). Once they had become distant, density became a necessity for them — the density of the spheres became necessary. On account of that density and turbidity, they were no longer fit to remain in the world of spirits, and so the command was given: "Descend." This is the Big Bang: when it occurred, density began to appear in them. The Divine Providence (al-'inayat al-ilahiyyah) then intended to disperse them — tabdid, to scatter them — and to separate them among the celestial spheres (aflak) and the mothers (ummahat). But this scattering was no mere dispersal; it was undertaken for a special purpose, the purpose of their purification.

`li-tasfu bi-tilka al-imtihanat wa-al-a'rad wa-al-'adhab kama yasfu al-dhahab al-ibriz bi-ta'riqihi fi al-nar`

(So that they may be purified through those trials and accidents and punishments, just as pure gold is purified by being melted in the fire.)

So that their purification might come about through those trials (imtihanat), tribulations, and punishments — just as pure gold (al-dhahab al-ibriz) is purified by being placed and melted in the fire. The trials and punishments were imposed upon them for precisely this end: that as gold, when melted, becomes pure ibriz, so they too should be refined into gold. They had fallen into density, and this density had to be removed; therefore they were cast into trials.

`thumma ista'rafat li-jam'ihi wa-lammihi ba'd al-'adhab wa-al-imtihan, wa-izharihi ila al-qamat al-'arafiyyah min al-ard nabatan`

(Then Providence undertook the gathering of it and the collecting of it, after the punishment and the trial, and its manifestation into the gnostic form from the earth as a plant.)

Then, after the punishment and the trial had done their refining work, the Divine Providence undertook to gather and collect what had been prepared, and to manifest it as the gnostic form (qamat al-'arafiyyah) from the earth, as a plant. Before reaching this point, the apparitions underwent their trials: they were cast into the spheres, the mothers (ummahat), and the rock (the mineral foundation); the world of life serves as their veil.

`thumma akhadhat al-'inayah al-ilahiyyah fi ikhraj ma tahayya'a ba'da dhalika bi-tariqah thaniyah, hiya bi-khilaf tilka al-tariqat al-uwla wa-aqrabu minha wa-asra'u li-wijdanih`

(Then the Divine Providence took up the bringing-forth of what had been prepared, after that, by a second way, which is contrary to that first way, and closer than it and faster for its coming-into-being.)

Then the Divine Providence took up the task of bringing forth, by a second way, that which had been prepared in the spheres, the mothers, and the rock. This second way is contrary to the first — it is other than it — and it is closer (aqrab) and faster (asra') for the thing's coming-into-existence. The first way moved through the long slow ages of the spheres and mothers, where the embryonic substance remained for thousands of years; this second way is much swifter. Through it, the very things that had been processed in the spheres and the mothers came at last to the earth and became manifest in one place, with humanity.

### The Three Kingdoms as Food and the Forming of the Physical Veil

The text now describes how the prepared substance is gathered into the three kingdoms and consumed by the primordial body as food, becoming flesh and blood:

`fa-hasarat jami'a ma tahayya'a li-al-su'ud wa-al-zuhur, wa-istahaqqat li-dhalika fi al-mawalid al-thalathah, allati hiya al-ma'dan wa-al-nabat wa-al-hayawan`

(So it encompassed all that had been prepared for the ascent and the manifestation, and it became fit for that in the three kingdoms, which are mineral and plant and animal.)

The Divine Providence encompassed all that had been prepared for ascent and manifestation — all those for whom manifestation in the world was to occur — and it became worthy of that matter in the three kingdoms (al-mawalid al-thalathah): mineral (ma'dan), plant (nabat), and animal (hayawan). Now Allah wills to make them manifest, and so He brings them forth by way of the mineral, the plant, and the animal.

`wa-ja'alat-hu ghidha'an li-tilka al-ashkhas al-zahirah min al-magharat bi-al-jussas al-ibda'iyyah. Fa-da'at-ha ila dhatiha wa-qtadhat biha wa-sayyarat-ha min jumlat-i ajsamih lahman wa-daman, jami'an li-al-taba'i al-arba'`

(And the Divine Providence made it food for those apparent persons from the caves, with the primordial bodies. So the primordial body called those foods to its own self, and ate them, and made them, according to the parts of its body, flesh and blood, gathering the four natures.)

The Divine Providence made the mineral, plant, and animal into food (ghidha') for those apparent persons emerging from the caves, by means of the primordial bodies (al-jussas al-ibda'iyyah). The primordial jussa called those foods to its own self (da'at-ha ila dhatiha), and ate them (iqtadhat biha), and converted them into the substance of its body as flesh (lahm) and blood (dam), gathering together the four natures (al-taba'i al-arba'). The four natures are these: yellow bile (safra), black bile (sauda), blood (dam), and phlegm (balgham). And the body so formed is not the primordial body itself but a veil over it: that which manifests in the world is a physical veil (hijab jismani) over the primordial body.

`wa-huwa altafu ma-qtadhat bihi wa-sta'malathu, li-annaha 'inda-qtida'iha bi-dhalika ta'khudhu al-latif wa-tad'u al-kathif`

(And it is the most subtle of what it ate and used, because at the time of its eating, it takes the subtle and leaves the dense.)

The food which that human form obtained and made part of its body is the most subtle (altaf) of all foods, the most lofty and noble — just as a believer is careful regarding lawful and unlawful food, so does the primordial jussa exercise this discernment. For at the time of its eating, the jussa takes the subtle (latif) and leaves out the dense (kathif). When we eat, both the dense and the subtle are present in our food, intermingled and not distinguished in the eating; but when the residue exits, it exits with its denseness, the subtle having already been taken.

### The Descent of the Subtle Extract and the Forming of the Single Entity

The text now traces the physiology of conception with great precision — how the subtlest extract is drawn from both parents, refined in the brain, and descends into the womb to become a single entity:

`wa-idha kana waqt al-mulamasah bayna al-rajul wa-al-mar'ah, imtahadha al-jisman, fa-nsalla 'anhuma altafu ma fiha`

(And when the time of contact between the man and the woman occurs, the two bodies are stirred, and there descends from them both the most subtle of what is in them.)

When the time of intimate contact (mulamasah) between the man and the woman occurs, the two bodies meet and are stirred, and from them both there descends the most subtle (altaf) of what is in them. It emerges from both of their bodies — and the text is precise that it is from both, 'anhuma, "from the two of them," answering directly the matter raised earlier: that the generative extract is not collected in the man's spine alone but in the woman's as well; from both it is drawn forth.

`fa-sara sa'idan ila al-dimagh li-an yuqassiruhu wa-yu'iduhu abyad ba'da an kana daman ghamitan, wa-huwa maslulun min jami' a'da' al-jism, saghirih wa-kabirih, 'aliha wa-daniha`

(So it ascends to the brain so that it may refine it and return it white after it was dense blood, and it is drawn from all the organs of the body, its small and its great, its high and its low.)

This subtle extract ascends to the brain (dimagh), for the function of the brain is to refine. The brain refines it and returns it white (abyad), after it had been dense, dark blood (daman ghamitan). And it is drawn (maslul) from all the organs of the body — from its small organs and its great, from its high and its low. Concerning this universality of its source, our Master the Messenger of Allah, upon him be peace, said that in every hair there is janabah, the ritual impurity for which the full washing is commanded — and this is the reason: that the extract is drawn from every hair, from every part of the body without exception. The gland (gudd) affected by this process, which the brain refines and trains, is the reference here; the brain takes the dense blood drawn from every organ and refines it, whitening it.

`fa-idha sara munqasiran, inhadara min al-zawjayn jami'an ila mustaqarrihi alladhi qad huyyi'a lahu, wa-huwa rahm al-imra'ah, fa-yasiru jumlatun wahidatan, jami'atan li-al-arba' taba'i`

(So when it has become refined, it descends from the two spouses together to its resting place which has been prepared for it, which is the womb of the woman, and it becomes a single entity, gathering the four natures.)

When it has become refined, it descends from the two spouses together (min al-zawjayn jami'an) to the resting place prepared for it, which is the womb of the woman (rahm al-imra'ah). Here again the text affirms what was clarified before: that the descent is from both spouses, 'anhuma — the most subtle of what is in them both is extracted. And there, in the womb, it becomes a single entity (jumlatun wahidatan), gathering the four natures: the man's nature and the woman's nature combine and become one single entity.

`wa-fi zimn tilka al-jumla hayatun hayulaniyyatun kamilatun, fiha ka-kumun al-nar fi 'ud al-zinad. Wa-hiya al-musammat bi-al-tabi'ah wa-bi-al-khamirah al-ibda'iyyah wa-bi-al-nafs al-namiyyah wa-bi-al-surah aydan`

(And within that entity is a complete vegetative life, hidden in it like the latency of fire in the wood of a flint. And it is that which is named the nature, and the primordial leaven, and the growing soul, and the form as well.)

Within that single entity there resides a complete vegetative life (hayat hayulaniyyah kamilah), hidden within it like fire latent in the wood of a flint (ka-kumun al-nar fi 'ud al-zinad) — present but not yet kindled. And this life is called by four names: the nature (tabi'ah); the primordial leaven (khamirah ibda'iyyah); the growing soul (nafs namiyyah); and the form (surah) — by which is meant the growing, developing form.

### The Settling in the Womb and the Fermentation of the Entity

`fa-idha kanat hadhihi al-jumla kamilat al-taba'i, mu'tadilah fi mizajiha, qad udhina laha bi-al-zuhur, wa-wafaqat ma'daniyyan naqiyyan, tahiran, mu'tadilan, wa-huwa al-rahm, fa-istaqarrat fihi`

(So when this entity has become complete in its natures, balanced in its temperament, and has been given permission to manifest, and it corresponds with a mineral place that is pure, clean, and balanced, which is the womb, then it settles in it.)

When this entity has become complete in its natures (kamilat al-taba'i) and balanced in its temperament (mu'tadilah fi mizajiha), and has been granted permission to manifest (udhina laha bi-al-zuhur), it corresponds with a mineral place that is pure, clean, and balanced (ma'daniyyan naqiyyan, tahiran, mu'tadilan) — and that place is the womb (rahm). The entity settles there (istaqarrat fihi).

`wa-qabalaha al-tali' wa-al-gharib wa-al-'ashir wa-al-rabi' bi-hukm al-kawn`

(And it is faced by the ascendant, the descendant, the tenth, and the fourth, by the decree of the cosmos.)

It is faced, by the decree of the cosmos (bi-hukm al-kawn), by the four cardinal astrological houses: the ascendant (tali'), the descendant (gharib), the tenth (ashir), and the fourth (rabi') — the four points that govern its existence.

`wa-qad sarat mutahayyi'atan, munfa'ilatan lima yaridu 'alayha, mutakhammiratan fi al-rahm, ba'daha bi-ba'din, ka-takhmir al-anfahati wa-al-laban, al-sa'iri jubunan`

(And it has become prepared, receptive to what comes upon it, fermenting in the womb, one part with another, like the fermentation of rennet and milk, which becomes cheese.)

The entity has become prepared and receptive to what descends upon it, fermenting (mutakhammirah) in the womb, part with part, like the fermentation of rennet and milk (takhmir al-anfahati wa-al-laban), which thereby becomes cheese (jubun). Just as rennet acting upon milk transforms it into cheese, so does the forming entity ferment and curdle within the womb into its determinate shape.

### The Forming of the Heart First, and the Astral Fathers and Mothers

The fourth chapter of the second pavilion takes up the order in which the organs are formed, beginning with the heart:

`fa-idha sarat hadhihi al-jumla mutahayyi'atan, qabalaha al-kawakib fa'ilatan fiha, wa-jtama'a dhalika al-latif al-sha'i' fiha, wa-huwa zubdatuha, ka-jtima' zubdat al-laban`

(So when this entity is at this stage, the planets face it, acting upon it, and that subtle thing which radiates within it gathers, and it is its essence, like the gathering of the essence of milk, which is butter.)

When the entity reaches this stage, the planets (kawakib) face it and act upon it, and that subtle radiant element within it gathers together — and this is its essence (zubdah), like the gathering of the essence of milk, which is butter (zubdat al-laban). It is the subtle part of every nature (latif kulli tabi'ah) among these natures; so it coalesces and becomes a single entity.

`thumma inna awwala ma yanfa'ilu min tilka al-jumla allati hiya al-nutfah, nuqtat al-birkar, allati hiya al-qalb. Wa-tab'uhu tab'u fi'l al-shams, alladhi huwa al-hararah wa-al-yubus, wa-huwa al-jadhib li-tilka al-jumlat al-latifah, fa-yasiru baytan laha wa-markazan`

(Then the first thing that is formed from that entity which is the drop is the point of the compass, which is the heart. And its nature is the nature of the action of the sun, which is heat and dryness, and it is the attractor of that subtle entity, so it becomes a house for it and a center.)

The first organ to be formed from that entity which is the drop (nutfah) is the point of the compass (nuqtat al-birkar) — the fixed central point from which the circle is drawn — and this is the heart (qalb). The heart is formed first of all. Its nature is the nature of the sun's action (fi'l al-shams), which is heat and dryness (al-hararah wa-al-yubus). The heart is the attractor (jadhib) of that subtle entity, drawing it to itself, so that it becomes a house (bayt) for it and a center (markaz). Only after the heart is established do the remaining organs form:

`thumma yanfa'ilu sa'ir al-a'da' ba'da dhalika al-'azw. Wa-al-aba' allati hiya al-kawakib hiya al-fa'ilah li-dhalika. Wa-al-ummahat, al-hawazin, alladhina hum al-rahm`

(Then the rest of the organs are formed after that organ. And the fathers, which are the planets, are the agents for that. And the mothers, which nurse, are the womb.)

After the heart, the rest of the organs are formed. And the fathers (aba'), which are the planets (kawakib), are the agents acting upon this formation; while the mothers (ummahat), which nurse, are the womb. Each organ of the forming child has a guardian (mutawalli) in the spheres — for every organ there is an overseer among the celestial bodies:

`wa-kullu 'azwin min hadhihi al-a'da' yumiddu ma-nfa'ala min a'da' tilka al-nutfah mumathilan lahu, ittiba' kulli 'azwin min a'da' tilka al-nutfah mithla tab'i 'azwi ummiha. La tughadiru wa-la tubayinu fi shay'in minha`

(And every organ from these provides aid to what was formed from the organs of that drop, corresponding to it, every organ of that drop following the nature of the organ of its mother. It does not omit nor differ in anything from it.)

Every organ among these provides aid to the corresponding organ formed from the drop, each organ of the drop following the nature of the organ of its astral mother — and the correspondence misses nothing and differs in nothing. And the nourishment passes through this maternal correspondence:

`wa-jami' ma taqtadihi al-ummu yaftariqu 'ala a'da'iha fi a'da'iha. Wa-kullu 'azwin min a'da' tilka al-nutfah yattasilu bihi qistuhu min dhalika al-ghidha' bi-wasatat-i 'azwi ummiha`

(And all that the mother consumes is distributed among her organs and among the organs of the child. And every organ from the organs of that drop receives its share of that food through the medium of its mother's organ.)

All that the mother takes in is distributed among her own organs and, through them, among the child's organs; and every organ of the drop receives its appointed share (qist) of that nourishment through the medium of its corresponding maternal organ. Nothing is missed, nothing misdirected; each organ is fed through its own celestial channel.

### The Sun of the Sphere and the Sun of Religion

The text closes this passage with a luminous analogy between the sun's governance of the cosmos and the Natiq's governance of the world of religion:

`thumma inna sawari tilka al-jumlat al-latifah tumiddu al-a'da' al-'alawiyyah min al-qalb ila al-ra's, wa-kullu 'azwin bima yastahiqquhu, wa-tumiddu al-a'da' al-sufliyyah min al-qalb ila al-qadamayn`

(Then the channels of that subtle entity provide aid to the higher organs from the heart to the head, each organ according to what it merits, and they provide aid to the lower organs from the heart to the two feet.)

The channels (sawari) of that subtle entity provide aid to the higher organs, from the heart up to the head, each organ according to what it merits; and they provide aid to the lower organs, from the heart down to the two feet. The heart, the central point, supplies above and below alike.

`kama anna al-shams allati hiya qalb al-falak tumiddu lima 'ala'iha min al-kawakib wa-al-aflak wa-lima huwa dunaha hadhwan bi-hadhwin`

(Just as the sun, which is the heart of the celestial sphere, provides aid to what is above it of the planets and spheres, and to what is below it, step by step.)

Just as the sun, which is the heart of the celestial sphere (qalb al-falak), provides aid to what lies above it among the planets and spheres, and to what lies below it, step by step (hadhwan bi-hadhwin) — for the sun is the fourth, with three above it and three below it, even as the body has its three parts above the heart and its three below, all of them aided by the central heart.

`wa-kadhalika shams 'alam al-din, alladhi huwa al-natiq, salamullah 'alayh, yumiddu bi-al-'ulum al-ilahiyyah wa-al-ta'yidat al-malakutiyyah man fawqahu min hududin wa-man dunahu min hudud al-din. Wa-sa-ya'ti al-qawlu 'ala dhalika fi mawdi'ihi, insha'Allah ta'ala`

(And likewise the sun of the world of religion, who is the Natiq, peace be upon him, provides aid with the divine sciences and the angelic confirmations to those above him of the limits and those below him of the limits of religion. And the discourse on this will come in its place, if Allah the Exalted wills.)

And in just this manner, the sun of the world of religion (shams 'alam al-din), who is the Natiq, peace be upon him, provides aid through the divine sciences (al-'ulum al-ilahiyyah) and the angelic confirmations (al-ta'yidat al-malakutiyyah) to the hudud above him and to the hudud of religion below him. As the sun, heart of the celestial sphere, supplies light and power to all the bodies above and below it step by step, so the Natiq, sun of the religious cosmos, supplies the divine sciences and the heavenly confirmations to the ranks ranged above and below him in the hierarchy of religion. The discourse on this will come in its proper place, if Allah the Exalted wills.

### The Spiritual Discipline of the Bismillah and the Wiping of the Face

There remains to be unfolded a spiritual discipline tied to the formula of the Bismillah and to the act of wiping the hand over the face — a practice rich with the symbolism of the ranks. When the believer recites Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim, the first lesson of spiritual training is to wipe the hand over the face; and this gesture carries a precise meaning that must be held in the mind for it to bear fruit.

The foundation lies in a correspondence between the formula of the Bismillah and the formula of the witness, La ilaha illa Allah. The phrase La ilaha illa Allah possesses many qualities — twenty-eight in one reckoning — and among them is this: that within it the letter lam occurs five times (in La, in ilah, in illa, and twice in Allah), and likewise the letter alif occurs five times, while the letter ha occurs twice as a repetition. The full splendor of the Bismillah is present within La ilaha illa Allah, but manifested in a different manner. Among the forty-nine letters, the alif is the highest, and it stands as the non-repeated (ghayr mukarrar); the lam, by contrast, stands for the repeated (mukarrar).

From this the discipline of wiping the face draws its meaning. Leaving aside the face, the five recur upon us five times over: the five fingers of the right foot, the five of the left foot, the five of the right hand, and the five of the left hand — and the five of the face — so that five times five make twenty-five. Of all these, when we wipe the hand over the face, we wipe with the right hand, never the left, and with the highest of the five. This signifies the connection of the lower with the higher: the joining of the five lower ranks (adwan) with the five higher ranks (ashraf) — the adwan being those who, among the four, are the greatest below, and the ashraf being the loftier five above.

The five of the face do not come as repeated; there is no repetition of the heart among them. Who are they? They are the Intellect ('aql) — which is the First Intellect, 'Aql-i Awwal — the Soul (nafs), which is the Second Intellect, 'Aql-i Thani; and then jadd, fath, and khayal, which are their counterparts established in the world, the three confirmations (ta'yidat) by which the hudud are connected to the Imam. These five — 'aql, nafs, jadd, fath, khayal — do not come as repeated; they have become established and have attained the second perfection (kamal-i thani). In the world there is no repetition of them. But the five of the hand are the repeated: upon this hand and that, upon this finger and that, their manifestation occurs in the world — and these are the Imam, the Bab, the Hujjah, the Da'i, and the Ma'dhun. The hand's five are manifested and repeated in the world; the face's five are non-repeated, perfected in their second perfection.

Therefore, when one wipes the hand over the face, one must hold this concept in the mind — the connection of the five lower manifested ranks (Imam, Bab, Hujjah, Da'i, Ma'dhun) with the five higher non-repeated ranks ('aql, nafs, jadd, fath, khayal) — so that the gnostic form is established and imprinted upon one's spiritual being. Merely wiping the hand accomplishes nothing of itself; but when the wiping is done with this concept present, it becomes imprinted upon the spiritual form, and the discipline bears its fruit. This is the inner sense of the gesture that opens every recitation of the Bismillah: the lower ranks reaching upward to be joined with the higher, the manifested with the perfected, so that the believer's own form is drawn into the luminous order it imitates.

## The Souls and the Planetary Spirits

### The Seven Governors of the Womb

The fourth chapter now turns to the most hidden of the book's themes: the descent of the soul into the body, and the celestial governance that presides over the forming of a human being in the dark. Its opening words speak of the fetus issuing `min al-kawakib al-sab'a` — from the seven planets — and being completed in its measured period within the body of the embryo. The teaching here is that the seven planets, in a fixed order, take turns governing the growth of the drop that has been deposited in the womb, each presiding over it for one month, until the seven have passed in their succession and the child is brought forth complete.

The seven planets, taken in the order of their governance over the womb, are these: first `Zuhal` (Saturn), then `Mushtari` (Jupiter), then `Mirrikh` (Mars), then `Shams` (the Sun), then `Zuhra` (Venus), then `'Utarid` (Mercury), and finally `Qamar` (the Moon). Each of these seven planets, in its turn, exercises its governance (`tadbir`) over the `nutfah`, the drop that is to be wrought into a child, for the span of a single month. The fetus passes from one to the next, `bi-'ayanin min kulli jihatin`, until it has traversed the full circuit of the seven. As the text declares: `Summa min al-kawakib sab'ati… shahrin wahidin… hatta takhuta min al-sab'ati, wa-tukmiluha fi hadhihi al-muddati` — seven of the planets, each for one month, until it traverses the seven and He completes it in this period. And then: `wa-kharaja mauludan kamilan ila fushat al-ard fi al-shahr al-sabi'` — and it comes forth as a complete newborn into the expanse of the earth in the seventh month.

It is born complete in the seventh month precisely because the governance of that month belongs to the Moon, `Qamar`. The seventh in the sequence is the Moon, and under the Moon's governance the child reaches the fullness that permits its emergence. This is why so many children are in fact born in the seventh month: the lunar governance, when its effects are present, is the natural occasion of birth.

The order of governance over the months may be traced step by step. In the first month, the governance belongs to Saturn. In the second, to Jupiter. In the third, to Mars. In the fourth, to the Sun. And here a particular wonder is to be marked, for it is under the Sun's governance, in the fourth month, that the power of the heart is created within the fetus, and with it the power of movement. The formation of the heart and the beginning of its motion occur in the fourth month, under the governance of the Sun — and this is precisely why the heartbeat of the fetus first becomes perceptible at that time. In the first three months nothing of the heart's movement is apparent; the only outward sign is the cessation of the mother's menses, which is a matter of another order. But when the fourth month arrives, the movement of the child's heart begins to be felt, for in the cycle of the Sun the heart is brought to completion and its motion is set going. Thereafter come the fifth month, the sixth, and the seventh, and in the cycle of the Moon, when the lunar effects are fully present, the child may be safely born.

### The Eighth Month and the Star of Death

But the natural rhythm of the seventh-month birth is not invariable, for the text continues: `wa-rubbama 'arada lahu 'aridun fa-lam yakhruj fi al-shahr al-sabi'` — and perhaps some contingency befalls it, so that it does not come forth in the seventh month. Should such a thing occur, the newborn is not delivered in the seventh month but `fa-istaqarra fi batni ummihi ila al-shahr al-thamin` — it settles and remains within its mother's womb until the eighth month. And here a grave danger arises, for `wa-fihi ya'udu al-tadbiru ila Zuhal` — in the eighth month the governance returns to Saturn.

Saturn is `nahs al-falaki`, the inauspicious star of the celestial sphere, the bringer of ill-fortune among the planets. Its action, as the text declares, is `al-burudatu wa-al-yubs` — coldness and dryness — `alladhi huwa tab' al-mawt`, which is the very nature of death. Cold and dry together are the temperament of death itself. Therefore: `fa-in zahara dhalika al-janinu fihi, zahara mayyitan` — if that fetus should appear in the eighth month, it appears dead. The eighth month brings death upon it; its limbs all become as though paralyzed and lifeless, on account of the nature it has received from Saturn. The cold, dry temperament that Saturn impresses upon the eighth-month child is the temperament of the corpse, and so the child born in that month appears already as one deceased.

Yet if the child is not born in the eighth month either, but endures still longer, the danger is past and life returns. As the text says: `fa-in istaqarra fi batni ummihi ila al-shahr al-tasi', sara fi tadbir al-Mushtari, alladhi huwa fi al-falaki al-sa'd al-akbar` — but if it settles in its mother's womb until the ninth month, it comes under the governance of Jupiter, which is in the celestial sphere the greater good fortune. Jupiter is called `al-sa'd al-akbar`, the greater of the auspicious planets. Here it is fitting to set down the offices the planets bear in this celestial reckoning. The two luminaries, the `nayyirayn`, are the Sun and the Moon. The two fortunes, the `sa'dayn`, are Jupiter and Venus — Jupiter the greater good fortune (`sa'd al-akbar`) and Venus the lesser (`sa'd al-asghar`). And Mercury is `katib al-falak`, the scribe of the sphere.

Jupiter's action is the opposite of Saturn's in every respect. Its action is `al-hararatu wa-al-linu` — heat and softness, or moisture — `allati hiya tab'u al-hayat`, which is the nature of life. Where Saturn is cold and dry, the temperament of death, Jupiter is hot and moist, the temperament of life; for life itself is generated from heat and moisture. Therefore `zahara al-janinu hayyan` — the fetus, born in the ninth month under Jupiter's auspicious and life-giving governance, comes forth alive.

This whole teaching rests upon a principle that may be stated thus: `inna wujuda al-nafsi bi-wujudi mawdu'iha` — the existence of the soul depends upon the existence of its substrate. The soul subsists only where its surrounding conditions are sound. There must be heat, there must be moisture, in their right proportion. If these are not duly furnished to the forming life, the soul cannot hold, and it perishes. The substrate must be temperate and whole, or the soul that would inhabit it cannot remain.

### The Stations of the Souls

Here a question naturally arises: do the souls have stations? They do. The vegetative soul, the `nafs al-namiyya`, has its station in the womb of the mother, for it is there that growth is governed. The sensory soul, the `nafs al-hissiyya`, has its station once the child is born into the world; its effect abides thereafter in the living person. These two souls, the vegetative and the sensory, are the lower powers of the human being, and the chapter now proceeds to define each in turn through the words of the Commander of the Faithful.

### The Vegetative Soul: A Natural Power

The text records: `wa-ma'na qawli Amir al-Mu'minina 'Aliyyibni Abi Talibin 'alayhi al-salam li-l-sa'il alladhi sa'alahu 'an al-namiyyati ma hiya` — and the meaning of the saying of the Commander of the Faithful, 'Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, to the questioner who asked him concerning the vegetative soul, what it is. The questioner asked, "What thing is the vegetative soul?" and the answer of Amir al-Mu'minin is preserved as a precise definition, framed point by point.

He said: `inna al-nafs al-namiyyata quwwatun tabi'iyyatun` — the vegetative soul is a natural power. It is, before all else, a power, and "vegetative soul" is the name given to that power. Its first attribute is its origin: `asluha min taba'i'i al-arba'a` — its origin is from the four natures. The four natures are heat and cold, moisture and dryness; from the conjunction of these four the vegetative power arises. Its second attribute is its moment of union: `wa-ittihaduha 'inda masqati al-nutfah` — and its union is at the place where the drop of seed is deposited. The vegetative soul joins its substrate precisely at the depositing of the `nutfah` in the womb. Its third attribute concerns its substance: `wa-mawadduha min nata'iji al-aghdhiyati al-latifah` — and its substance is drawn from the products of refined foods. From these refined foods it draws the material for all its growth; the mother takes nourishment, the subtle essence of it is drawn out, and it joins to the forming child. Its fourth attribute is its action: `fi'luha al-numuwwu wa-al-ziyadatu` — its action is growth and increase. To grow is to move forward in development; to increase is to become greater in measure. Its fifth attribute is the cause of its departure: `sababu iftiraqiha ikhtilafu al-mawadi` — the cause of its separation is the disharmony of substances. When the foods that sustained it cease to reach it duly, when there is imbalance in the materials, the vegetative soul withdraws. And its sixth and final attribute is its return: `wa-idha faraqat, 'adat ila mawdi'in minhu badat` — and when it separates, it returns to the place from which it first began. And whence did the vegetative soul begin? From the Moon. To the lunar station, therefore, it returns when it departs.

### The Greater Vegetative Soul and the Animal

This return to the Moon opens onto a profound reality of the haqa'iq, for the text continues: `li-anna jami'a ma tahta falaki al-qamari mahkumun ila nuqtati al-ardi allati hiya al-sakhratu, wa-yuqalu 'alayha al-namiyyatu al-kubra` — because all that is beneath the sphere of the Moon, down to the point of the earth which is the rock, is governed [as one], and it is called the Greater Vegetative Soul. Everything that lies below the sphere of the Moon, extending all the way down to the point of the earth — which is the Rock, `al-sakhrah` — constitutes a single governed reality, and this whole expanse beneath the lunar sphere is named the `namiyyat al-kubra`, the Greater Vegetative Soul. The vegetative power that comes to the child is drawn from this vast reservoir below the Moon; it is, as it were, a portion taken from the great vegetative ocean and brought forth by the governance of God to be enfolded upon the forming life.

From this Greater Vegetative Soul, every lesser vegetative soul branches forth: `wa-minha yatafarra'u kullu namiyyatin sughra` — and from it branches out every lesser vegetative soul. However many vegetative souls there may be, they are all portions of the single greater reality beneath the sphere of the Moon. And the text then reveals what the lesser vegetative soul truly is: `wa-'ala al-haqiqati fa-inna al-namiyyata al-sughra hiya al-hayawan` — and in reality, the lesser vegetative soul is the animal. The `namiyyat al-sughra` is the `hayawan` — that is to say, it is the living creatures, all that lives the animal life below the human rank. The lesser vegetative soul, considered in its true nature, is none other than the animal soul.

### The Greater Sensory Soul and the Ranks of Souls

Just as there is a Greater and a Lesser Vegetative Soul, so too there is a Greater and a Lesser Sensory Soul, and the text sets them in parallel: `wa-kadhalika al-hissiyyatu al-kubra min al-muhiti ila falaki al-qamar` — and likewise, the Greater Sensory Soul extends from the encompassing sphere to the sphere of the Moon. The Greater Sensory Soul, `hissiyyat al-kubra`, occupies the whole region from the `muhit`, the encompassing or outermost sphere, down to the sphere of the Moon. Where the Greater Vegetative Soul filled the region from the Moon downward to the Rock, the Greater Sensory Soul fills the region from the Moon upward to the encompassing sphere — the highest of the heavens. It is from there that the sensory reality begins, and it extends down to the lunar sphere.

The Lesser Sensory Soul, by contrast, is defined as the human soul itself: `wa-al-hissiyyatu al-sughra nafsu al-insan al-natiqat al-mukallaf` — and the Lesser Sensory Soul is the rational, obligated soul of the human being. Where the Lesser Vegetative Soul was the animal, the Lesser Sensory Soul is the human being — the one who speaks, the soul that bears the burden of religious obligation. A small portion of that vast sensory ocean, which stretches from the encompassing sphere down to the sphere of the Moon, is granted to the human being, and this granted portion is what is called the `nafs al-hissiyya`.

The text then completes the rising scale by treating the rational soul, the `natiqa`: `wa-al-natiqatu al-kubra hududun 'alayhimu al-salam` — and the Greater Rational Soul is the Hudud, peace be upon them. The greater rational souls are the Hudud, the ranks of the religious hierarchy, upon whom be peace. And the lesser: `wa-al-natiqatu al-sughra huwa al-mustajibu` — and the Lesser Rational Soul is the respondent, the `mustajib`, the one who takes the noble covenant. With this the threefold scheme is complete: vegetative, sensory, and rational, each in a greater and a lesser degree, mapping the whole graded order of being from the encompassing sphere down to the rock of the earth, and from the animal up through the obligated human soul to the respondent who answers the call of truth.

### A Word Plainly Spoken Without Symbol

The author pauses here to mark the gravity of what he has set down, for these are realities that the tradition has guarded jealously. As the text declares: `wa-hadhihi al-ashya'u lam yakun yulsha'u 'anha illa ramzan, wa-min lisanin ila udhnin` — and these things were never disclosed except by allusion, and from a tongue to an ear. They were transmitted by symbol alone, spoken from mouth to ear, never committed to writing in plain form. Yet here they are written plainly, and the reason is given: `lakin qad sabaqa al-sharhu fi awwal al-kitabi bi-itrah al-rumuzi wa-al-isharati wa-al-i'rab 'an al-haqa'iqi wa-al-ma'ani min ghayri ramzin wa-la ta'wilin` — but the explanation has already been established at the beginning of the book, by setting aside symbols and allusions, and by giving plain expression to the realities and meanings without symbol or allegorical interpretation. Because the questioner had requested that the matter be explained to him without `ramz` (symbol) and without `ta'wil` (allegorical interpretation), the author has written it forth in the language of the realities alone — `haqa'iq` and `daqa'iq`, the language of realities and of subtleties, the latter being more difficult still than the former. This is plain speech in a domain where plain speech is rare, and the reader is to receive it as a trust.

### The Newborn and the Sensory Power from the Sun

The chapter now moves to the moment of birth and the descent of the sensory soul, the second of the souls Amir al-Mu'minin defined. The text reads: `thumma innahu idha zahara al-mawludu ila fushat al-ardi min batni ummihi, wa-sara shakhsan tamman kamilan, ittasalat bihi quwwatun hissiyyatun min shu'a'i al-shams` — then indeed, when the newborn appears into the expanse of the earth from its mother's womb, and becomes a complete and perfect person, a sensory power from the rays of the sun connects with it.

When the child emerges from the narrowness of the womb into the wide expanse of the earth — `fushat al-ard`, the open vastness of the world — and becomes a complete, perfected person, a sensory power joins itself to it from the rays of the Sun. The Sun is `qalbu al-falak`, the heart of the sphere, and `markazu al-hissiyya`, the center of the sensory reality. Just as the Moon is the center, the `markaz`, of the vegetative power, so the Sun is the center of the sensory power. A distinction is to be drawn here between the star (`kawkab`) and the sphere (`falak`): the sphere is the orbit in which the star revolves, while the relationship of the sensory soul is with the star, the celestial body itself, which is the center from which sensation flows. The great center is an ocean; from that ocean a drop is granted to this child. First, from the Moon, the vegetative portion is granted; and now, from the Sun, the sensory portion is granted.

The sensory power, having joined the newborn, does its work: `fa-sha'at fi al-badani kullihi, wa-ba'athat fihi al-harakati` — so it spreads throughout the entire body, and sends movements into it. The power diffuses itself through the whole body and rouses movement within it; sensation and motion are awakened. `Wa-ittahadat min nafsi al-namiyyati, fa-sabaghatha wa-sarata shay'an wahidan` — and it unites with the vegetative soul, so it dyes it, and the two become a single thing. The sensory soul joins to the vegetative soul already present, and the two are so blended that they become one — as when two distinct colors are mixed and a single color results. The sensory and vegetative souls, in the living person, are one fused reality.

And the two together draw their sustenance from a single source: they meet a portion `min nasim` — from the breeze. `Fa-zadat bihi quwwatuhuma, wa-sara ghidha'an lahuma mithla ghidha'i al-ajsami min anwa'i al-at'imati` — so by it the power of both increases, and it becomes a nourishment for both of them, like the nourishment of bodies from the various kinds of foods. The temperate breeze, the `nasim`, becomes the food of the joined vegetative and sensory souls, increasing the strength of both, just as bodies are nourished by the various kinds of food. As the body lives by what it eats, so these subtle souls live by the breath of the temperate air.

### The Sensory Soul: A Celestial Power

Here the author invokes again the saying of Amir al-Mu'minin, for the reality just described is the very meaning of his second definition: `wa-hadhihi haqiqatu qawli Amir al-Mu'minina 'alayhi al-salam li-l-sa'il alladhi sa'alahu 'an al-nafsi al-hissiyyati ma hiya` — and this is the reality of the saying of the Commander of the Faithful, peace be upon him, to the questioner who asked him concerning the sensory soul, what it is. As he defined the vegetative soul, so he defined the sensory, in parallel terms.

He said: `inna al-nafs al-hissiyyata quwwatun falakiyyatun` — the sensory soul is a celestial power. Where the vegetative soul was called a natural power (`quwwatun tabi'iyyatun`), the sensory soul is a celestial power (`quwwatun falakiyyatun`). The term "natural" belongs to what lies beneath the sphere of the Moon, in the natural world; the term "celestial" belongs to the spheres above, the `aflak`, which are the celestial powers. And whereas the source of the vegetative soul was the Moon, the source of the sensory soul is the Sun, which dwells among the spheres. Then he set out its attributes in the same sixfold order. Its origin: `asluha al-aflaku` — its origin is the celestial spheres. Its moment of union: `ittihaduha 'inda al-wiladati al-jismaniyyati` — its union with the human being is at the time of the physical, bodily birth, which is to say at the emergence into the world, as distinct from the vegetative soul's union at the depositing of the seed. Its seat: `maqarruha al-qalbu` — its station is the heart; the sensory soul resides in the heart of the body. Its action: `fi'luha al-hissu wa-al-harakatu wa-al-sawlatu wa-al-ghalabatu` — its action is sensation, movement, assault, and victory. It senses, it moves, it advances with dominance (`sawlah`), and it achieves the upper hand (`ghalabah`). Its cause of departure: `sababu iftiraqiha ikhtilafu al-mawadi` — the cause of its separation is the disharmony of substances. The body parts from the sensory soul through imbalance in the humors: when heat, moisture, cold, or dryness exceeds or falls short of its right measure, illness and disorder arise, and if the imbalance grows past remedy, the soul departs. So long as the materials remain in temperate proportion the matter is sound; once they pass beyond moderation, the union dissolves. And its return: `wa-idha faraqat, 'adat ila mawdi'in minhu badat` — and when it separates, it returns to the place from which it began. As a traveler returns to his home, so each soul returns to its origin: the home of the vegetative soul is the Moon, and the home of the sensory soul is the Sun. Each goes back whence it came.

### Al-Mu'ayyad's Lament: Why Were You Drawn Down?

The author now corroborates this whole doctrine of descent with the words of one of the greatest authorities of the tradition. The text continues: `wa-dhalika maw'izun haqiqun wa-qawlu sayyidina al-Mu'ayyadi arwaha Allahu ruhahu fi majalisihi` — and that is a true admonition, [confirmed by] the saying of our master al-Mu'ayyad, may God sanctify his soul, in his Majalis. Sayyidna al-Mu'ayyad — whose name is Hibatullah ibn Musa al-Shirazi, al-Mu'ayyad being his honorific title — held the rank of `Bab al-Abwab`, the Gate of Gates. In one of the sessions of his Majalis he gave voice to a meditation on this very descent of the soul.

He said: `wa-layta shi'ri lima sulliltum min al-aflaki wa-al-ajrami, wa-udrijtum fi mada'iqi al-aslabi wa-al-arhami, wa-ukhrijtum min diqi dhalika al-ghisha'i ila sa'ati hadha al-fida'` — and would that I knew why you were drawn out from the celestial spheres and the heavenly bodies, and made to descend, stage by stage, into the narrow places of the loins and the wombs, and brought out from the narrowness of that membrane into the expanse of this abode. The lament turns on three words. `Sulliltum` means that you were extracted, drawn forth, from the high places — from the spheres (`aflak`) and the bodies of the stars (`ajram`, the substance of each `jirm` or star). `Udrijtum`, from the root meaning to advance stage by stage, says that you were brought down by graded descent into the `mada'iq`, the narrow places — the plural of `maziq`, from `diq`, narrowness — the cramped confines of the loins and the wombs. And `ukhrijtum` says that you were then brought forth from the narrowness of the fetal membrane out into the open expanse of this worldly abode. It is the cry of one who looks back upon the soul's fall from the breadth of the heavens into the straits of the body, and wonders at its meaning.

### The Drop and the Four Mothers

The author then unfolds al-Mu'ayyad's allusion in the technical language of the haqa'iq. The text reads: `isharatun minhu 'alayhi al-salam ila al-nutfati al-muntabiyati 'alayha al-nafs al-namiyyatu al-maslulatu min ghidha'i al-ardiyyati al-ka'inati min al-mawalidi al-thalathati, allati hiya al-ma'danu wa-al-nabatu wa-al-hayawanu, al-maslulati min al-ummahaati al-arba'ati, allati hiya al-narru wa-al-hawa'u wa-al-ma'u wa-al-ardu, wa-hadhihi al-ummahaatu quwa al-aflaki` — an allusion from him, peace be upon him, to the drop of seed upon which the vegetative soul is enfolded, that soul being drawn from terrestrial nourishment, which exists out of the three kingdoms — namely the mineral, the plant, and the animal — which in turn is drawn from the four mothers, namely fire, air, water, and earth; and these mothers are the powers of the celestial spheres.

The chain of dependence is to be traced carefully. The `nutfah`, the drop, has the vegetative soul enfolded upon it. That vegetative soul is drawn from terrestrial nourishment. That nourishment, in turn, exists out of the three kingdoms — the `mawalid al-thalathah`, namely the mineral (`ma'dan`), the plant (`nabat`), and the animal (`hayawan`). These three kingdoms are themselves drawn from the four mothers — the `ummahat al-arba'ah`, namely fire, air, water, and earth. And these four mothers, finally, are the powers of the celestial spheres (`quwa al-aflak`). Thus the line of descent runs from the powers of the spheres, down through the four elemental mothers, down through the three kingdoms of generated things, into the terrestrial nourishment, into the vegetative soul enfolded upon the drop — and so the highest heavens reach down by graded mediation into the seed that becomes a child.

The author quotes al-Mu'ayyad further: `wa-ansha'a fika ma'nan batinan, dhata ayatin dhahira, muhitan bi-al-ardi wa-al-samawat` — and He originated within you a hidden, inward meaning, possessed of manifest signs, encompassing the earth and the heavens. This `isharatun ila hadhihi al-quwwati al-hissiyyati al-muttahidati bi-al-janini 'inda al-wiladati al-jismaniyyati` — this is an allusion to that sensory power which unites with the fetus at the time of the physical birth. The hidden inward meaning, whose signs are manifest and which encompasses earth and heaven, is the sensory power that joins the child at its bodily birth — the very power from the rays of the Sun that the chapter has already described.

### The Fall of the Luminous Essences

At this point the discourse opens onto the grandest theme of all: the origin and fall of every existing thing. The question is raised whether the various souls — vegetative, sensory, rational — combine to form a single essence in the physical world, together with the density of the body. The answer carries the whole doctrine of descent.

In the beginning, what existed was the essence, the `dhat`, which in the world of spirits was an `ashbah nuraniyya` — a luminous specter or likeness. For these the word `surah`, form, is not used; their quality is described as `shabih`, likeness. God Almighty created only spiritual essences, `zawat ruhaniyya`. He did not create minerals as such, nor plants, nor animals; what He created was the essences alone. Upon these essences He bestowed three powers: `'ilm` (knowledge), `hayat` (life), and `ikhtiyar` (choice). He granted them existence (`wujud`); within that existence He gave them knowledge of it; and then He gave them choice, that they might use their existence as they willed. These three gifts — knowledge, life, and the freedom of choice — were granted to every essence.

Now the one who, despite possessing knowledge, did not rightly use his choice, began to fall; while the one who used his powers correctly remained at his station of exaltation. When the essences delayed, when they did not accept the word, when they did not answer the call (`da'wah`), a flaw was wrought in their spiritual existences. With that flaw, they took on the form of souls: the `dhat` became `nafs`. What had been a luminous likeness above became a soul below. And the soul itself fell by degrees: it became the sensory soul (`nafs hissiyya`); falling further, it became the vegetative soul (`nafs namiyya`); falling further, it entered into the density of the body; falling further, it took the form of an animal; falling further still, it descended into the plant; and falling lowest of all, it descended into the mineral. All of these — soul, body, animal, plant, mineral — are in truth fallen spiritual essences. They are all `zawat ruhaniyya` whose forms have been altered by descent. The mineral that we tread, the plant, the animal, the very density of the body: not one of these was created as such; each is a luminous essence fallen from its first estate, wearing now a changed form.

### Salvation as the Return to Luminous Specters

If the fall is the descent of the luminous essences into the changed forms of souls and bodies and the kingdoms of nature, then salvation is their return. As the teaching declares, `najat` — salvation — is precisely the return of all things to being `ashbah nuraniyya` once more, luminous specters as they were at first. Every thing is a fallen spiritual essence; all are parts of one mother, however far they have fallen, even to the point where we look upon them and feel revulsion. The portions are scattered: the part that is with you, you know; the parts that are not with you are with others.

The gathering of these scattered portions is the work of the Governor's power. The portion that a person received in the sacred realm (`Quds`) is his portion; what he did not receive there is not his, but another's; and it is the power of the Governor (`Mudabbir`) that holds and gathers each one's portions. A man's fall has divided him into many things — a vegetative soul has come, a rational soul, a sensory soul, then the body, then even its waste products; these are the stages of his descent. And the question is whether these will return as one unit or at different times. The answer is that the unit is held with the `Mudabbir`: all of a person's units are with the Governor. And the place of gathering is the Qa'im. At the Qa'im a person's portions will be gathered — the sensory souls and much else assembled there, and there the unit will be formed. Then a greater Qa'im will come, and there a further gathering and a further unit will be formed. It is a wondrous governance of the Mudabbir, whereby the various souls from their different stations are so drawn together that the one essence capable of ascent, when it ascends, becomes a single essence — all of it gathered, the whole prime matter (`hayula`) becoming one essence. So the scattered drops are gathered into the ocean from which they fell, and the dispersed parts of the human being are reassembled at the Qa'im, and at the greater Qa'ims, ascending ultimately toward the Ashir and the Garden of Abode (`Jannat al-Ma'wa`) — though none in this ascent reaches the Lote-tree of the Utmost Boundary, the Sidrat al-Muntaha.

### Created in the Form of a Human Being

The chapter now sets down a saying of extraordinary depth: that the essences were created in the human form before any human being existed. The form of the Alif — the `qamat alifiyya`, the upright stature — is the very form in which God Almighty created the luminous essences. Consider the human being: there is a head, there are feet, there are limbs, there is everything in its arrangement. Now the essence that arrived first occupies the station of the head, or of the heart; and the essence that came last occupies the station of the feet. As they were arranged in that first creation, so they will ascend in that same arrangement; it will become the human form again. The one who arrived first, the precedent one (`sabiq`), is in the station of the heart; the one who came later is in the station of the feet; and all are ordered in the human shape.

Reflect upon the wonder of this single word: created in the human form, `shukhusan 'ala shakli al-insan` — persons in the shape of a human being. When did the human form yet exist? Human beings were created much later in time. The meaning is that there, in the world where He created them, it was upon the human form that He created the essences — by His decrees and destinies, before ever the bodily human existed. And when you ascend, you will acquire again the same form you bore before, but now as a complete light: the foremost in the station of the heart, another in the station of the head, another in the station of the feet. The human form is thus the eternal pattern of the luminous world, projected into time only afterward in the bodies of men.

This is corroborated, the author notes, by what Sayyidna al-Mu'ayyad has written concerning the cause of creation, for he says that God Almighty brought into existence (`wujud`) out of non-existence (`adam`) — and `adam`, non-existence, is not a place — `shukhusan 'ala shakli al-insan`, persons in the form of a human. The human form came later in the order of bodies, but at that primal moment God created the essences in this very shape, by His ordainments.

### The Twenty-Eight Parts and the Word of Tawhid

This human form, into which the essences were created, is composed of twenty-eight parts (`ajza'`) — and these twenty-eight parts correspond to the twenty-eight letters upon which the formula `La ilaha illa Allah` is built. The head corresponds to `illa Allah`, and the whole body to `La ilaha`: the head is the essence, the affirmation of God, while the body is the negation that precedes it. Within this form the parts are further ordered: there are three parts, and four parts, and seven parts, and twelve apertures (the openings called `jharoka`, the windows of the body). The twelve apertures are these: the two eyes, the two ears, the two nostrils, the mouth, the two nipples, the navel, the two openings of the chest, and the two lower orifices. So the human form, in its very members, spells out the word of divine unity, and the twenty-eight letters of `La ilaha illa Allah` are inscribed in the twenty-eight parts of the human shape.

### Why Did God Create? Al-Mu'ayyad in Taj al-Aqa'id

Having spoken of the cause of creation, the author turns to Sayyidna al-Mu'ayyad's treatment of the deepest form of that question in his work Taj al-Aqa'id: why did God Almighty create at all? Did He create out of some need — to display a favor, to take some work or benefit from His creatures, or to be worshipped? Al-Mu'ayyad refutes each of these in turn, returning to the refutation again and again across whole chapters of the book. God has no need that worship should supply; His majesty (`kibriya'`) is not increased by any work His creatures perform; there is nothing He stands to gain by taking labor from them. One after another these proposed causes are set aside.

In the end, al-Mu'ayyad concludes that we cannot know the cause of God's creating. We can grasp only this much: that God, by His grace (`fadl`) and His kindness, brought existence into the world out of non-existence. The cause itself is beyond our knowing; the only thing within our reach to understand is that it was an act of pure grace and kindness, bringing what was not into being. This, the author commends, is set down in beautiful words by Sayyidna al-Mu'ayyad, and Taj al-Aqa'id is a wondrous book that the reader is urged to study.

### The Rational Soul as Luminous Form

The fifth chapter of the fourth section now takes up the formation of speech and the rise of the rational soul, the third and highest of the souls that come to dwell in the human being. The text reads: `thumma al-mawludu yata'allamu min abawayhi al-nutqa bil-munaghat, shay'an ba'da shay'in, ila an yasira balighan nutqihi` — then the newborn learns speech from its two parents through babbling, little by little, until its speech reaches maturity. The child learns from its mother and father; through the cooing and babbling of infancy (`munaghat`), gradually and by degrees, it comes to speak, until its power of speech matures and reaches its perfection.

And then the text adds: `fa-idha 'ullima kitaba Allah ta'ala, izdada fi al-nutqi wa-kamalihi` — and when it is taught the Book of Allah the Exalted, it increases in speech and in its perfection. To know is `'alima`; to be made to know, to be taught and informed, is `'ullima` — to be made into one who knows (`'alim`). When the child is taught the Book of Allah, its speech and its perfection are alike increased; the learning of God's Book carries the matured power of speech to a higher completion.

The text proceeds to the decisive connection: `fa-idha ittasala bil-mu'allim al-sadiq, al-mutawallid lil-mukatharah ahl al-zahir, wa-kassara 'alayhi, wa-shawwaqahu ila al-dukhul fi da'irat ahl al-haqq, wa-khada 'alayhim imam dhalika al-zaman 'ahdahu al-karim, wa-dakhala fi haram Allah al-amin, jawara nafsahu nuqtatun min al-nur, wa-ta'allaqat tilka al-nuqtatu bi-'amud al-nur, alladhi huwa sarin min Imam al-zaman 'alayhi al-salam` — and when he connects with the true teacher, who counters the multitude of the people of the outward and presents his proof against them, and rouses in him a longing to enter the circle of the people of truth, and the Imam of the time takes from him his noble covenant, and he enters the secure sanctuary of Allah — then a point of light neighbors his soul, and that point connects with the Pillar of Light which flows from the Imam of the time, peace be upon him.

Several stages are gathered here. The person connects with the true teacher (`mu'allim al-sadiq`), the one who counters the crowd of the people of the outward (`ahl al-zahir`) and presents his proof against them — `kassara 'alayhi`, he establishes his demonstration upon him. The true teacher then rouses in him a longing (`shawwaqahu`) to enter the circle of the people of truth (`da'irat ahl al-haqq`). By the means of the Imam of the time, the noble covenant (`'ahd al-karim`) is taken from him, and he enters the secure sanctuary of Allah (`haram Allah al-amin`). At that moment his soul is neighbored by a point of light (`nuqtatun min al-nur`) — the soul is the object, and the point of light is the subject that comes to dwell beside it. And this point of light connects with the Pillar of Light (`'amud al-nur`), which is flowing from the Imam of the time.

The word "neighbors" (`jawara`) is to be weighed precisely. To be a neighbor is to remain in the vicinity without union (`ittisal`); the point of light abides in the soul's proximity, not fused with it. The point of light is a luminous thing, not a bodily thing; it is present with the whole body and has no particular bodily station, for its origin is the sensory soul (`nafs hissiyya`), and its association is with the rational soul (`nafs natiqah`) seated in the heart. So long as the person endures to a good end, this point of light remains a neighbor to his soul, and that point is connected to the Pillar of Light — the definition of which Sayyidna has given at great length, across three or four pages, as a most lofty matter. The Pillar of Light is itself flowing from the Imam of the time, peace be upon him; this is the statement of al-Sadiq.

### The Increase of the Point of Light

The point of light, once established, may grow. The text reads: `fa-kullama izdada [intifa'u] imam dhalika al-zaman wa-hududihi, wa-min sifati al-taharah wa-ta'at wali amrihim, izdadat tilka al-nuqtatu wus'an wa-nuran, wa-izdada ishraqu tilka al-nafsi wa-tajawharuha shay'an fa-shay'in` — and the more a person increases in benefiting from the Imam of that time and his Hudud, and in the qualities of purity and obedience to their Wali al-Amr, the more that point increases in vastness and in light, and the more the illumination of that soul and its substance increase, little by little.

The growth is proportioned to benefit. The more the person profits from the knowledge (`'ilm`), the gnosis (`ma'rifah`), and the love (`mahabbah`) of the Imam of the time and his Hudud, and the more he walks in chastity (`'iffah`), purity (`taharah`), and obedience to the Wali al-Amr, the Guardian of the Command — by that measure the point of light increases in vastness (`wus'`) and in light (`nur`), and the illumination (`ishraq`) of the soul increases, and the soul becomes more substantial, more truly a substance (`tajawhur`), little by little. This illumination is happening from the Pillar of Light, conveyed through the point — the rational soul that the person has received being illumined ever more from its source.

### The Form Which Is the Rational Soul

The author now identifies what this luminous point truly is. The text reads: `wa-tilka al-nuqtatu al-nuraniyyatu allati hiya surah, allati tasawwaraha dhalika al-shakhsu al-mustafidu min mufidihi, wa-hiya al-nafs al-natiqah` — and that luminous point, which is a form, a form imaged by that benefiting person from his benefactor — and it is the rational soul. The luminous point is a form (`surah`); the rational soul (`nafs natiqah`) and this form are one and the same thing. The soul that the person received, once it became connected to the Pillar of Light, is the luminous form, and from it a form of knowledge and of action alike is shaped. This form is imaged by the beneficiary (`mustafid`) from his benefactor (`mufid`) — the disciple receiving the image of the soul from the one who bestows it. And lest the reader wonder whether the form and the rational soul are two things, the author states plainly: `wa-hiya al-nafs al-natiqah` — and it is the rational soul. The form is the rational soul.

This too was the subject of a question put to Amir al-Mu'minin, peace be upon him. As the vegetative soul was defined as a natural power (`quwwatun tabi'iyyah`) and the sensory soul as a celestial power (`quwwatun falakiyyah`), so the rational soul is defined as an intellectual power. He said: `hiya quwwatun 'ilmiyyatun, jawharun shaffafun, 'alimatun bil-quwwati wa-al-fi'l, hayyatun bil-tab'` — it is an intellectual power, a transparent substance, knowing in potentiality and in actuality, living by nature. The rational soul is a transparent substance, a substance in which there is no coarseness, for the rational soul is pure; all that is good is contained within it, while coarseness entered into the sensory soul, and greater coarseness into the vegetative, and greater still into the body, the density mounting through the parts of the body. The rational soul knows both in potentiality and in actuality — `'alimatun bil-quwwati wa-al-fi'l` — that is, it knows both in capacity and in practical realization. And it is living by nature (`hayyatun bil-tab'`); it is alive, and it can never die.

### Evil Has No Origin in the Primal Creation

That the rational soul can never die rests upon a great principle: once the rational soul has been formed, it cannot be destroyed. As the text states it: `al-sharru la asla lahu fi'l-ibda'` — evil has no origin in the primary creation. In the first creation of the world, evil possesses no root whatsoever. For what is evil? It is distance from the good; to be remote from the good is what we call evil. Evil is not itself a thing. What is a lie? The absence of truth. What is darkness? The absence of light. So all the things we call bad are, in their essence, not things at all, but distances — as when we sit in the light and, the further we withdraw, the more the darkness increases; the darkness is no thing, but only our remoteness from the light.

By this principle, even our coarseness is not a thing. Coarseness is not a substance; it can therefore be finished and undone. It came upon us as a temporary condition, because there, in the first estate, we did not obey but delayed, and from that delay coarseness was wrought in us. The opposite of subtlety is coarseness, and coarseness is precisely delay in attaining subtlety. When we move forward upon the path in the right direction, the coarseness will come to an end. We do nothing to the coarseness but advance along its path, and it is finished — for it was never a thing in the first place, only a distance to be closed.

### Truth, Sustenance, and the Body Man Made

This principle leads to a startling consequence concerning the body itself. As the text declares it: Allah did not create the body; you created it. God Almighty created only truth (`haqq`) and sustenance (`rizq`). What is truth? That which has happened, the event that has truly occurred, is truth; and a lie is the mixing of falsehood into it — and the one who did the mixing is you, while the truth abides in its place. So too with the body: God created sustenance, and it was man who turned that sustenance into things of excretion, into base and corrupt things, into the body's density. Man is therefore the cause of his own coarseness. The body, the falsehood, the density — these man made of the pure sustenance and truth that God provided. And because man is their cause, man can also be their undoing: this coarseness is removed by progressing upon the path.

### The Spirit from the Command of the Lord

A question is raised concerning the spirit: what is the `ruh`? The answer is given from the Holy Quran: `qul al-ruhu min amri rabbi` — say, the spirit is from the command of my Lord. The spirit is from the command of our Lord; the command that is received, and with it the support of its angelic forms in which illumination occurs — that is what is called the `ruh`. Yet the word `ruh` is also applied to the rational soul (`nafs natiqah`). For the rational soul too the term `ruh` is used; and when the rational soul ascends to its height, then the command of the Lord (`amr-i rabb`) is at work. That is what is meant by the command of the Lord.

### The Rock and the Perfume: How the Coarse Is Saved

There remains the relation between the subtle rational soul and the coarse sensory soul it dwells beside. The rational soul is subtle; the sensory soul is exceedingly coarse; and the rational soul is drawn to make the sensory soul like itself. This drawing of the subtle upon the coarse is the very work of salvation: the rational soul wishes the sensory soul to become as it is, and that becoming is `najat`.

The author illustrates this by recalling the famous verses of Sa'di concerning a piece of clay, or a stone, that bore perfume upon it. Fragrance had come into its very body, and when it was asked how this fragrance had come to it, it answered that it had spent some days in the company of fragrance, and so the fragrance had passed into it — `ay gul, ba tu khar zand`, the rose and the thorn together. Just so, when the rational soul abides with the sensory soul, then slowly, by long company, the sensory soul too is gradually transformed into the rational soul. As the stone takes on the scent of the perfume by prolonged nearness, so the coarse sensory soul takes on the nature of the subtle rational soul by abiding with it — and this transformation is salvation.

### The Universal Soul: A Divine Power

The chapter now rises to the highest of the souls, the Universal Soul, the origin and ocean of the rational soul. The text states of the rational soul: `wa-qala hiya quwwatun 'ilmiyyatun, jawharun shaffafun, hayyatun bil-tab', asluha al-nafs al-kulliyyah` — and he said it is an intellectual power, a transparent substance, living by nature, whose origin is the Universal Soul. The origin of the rational soul is the `nafs al-kulliyyah`, the Universal Soul; the rational soul has become a part of it.

Where does the Universal Soul reside? In the world of life (`'alam al-hayat`). It must be understood that in the world of lights (`'alam al-anwar`) — the region from the Moon up to the Rock having been the world of the souls below — there is no soul at all; there are only essences and spirits. The first thing God created was not a soul; nowhere is it said that He created a soul or a vegetative power; He created luminous specters (`ashbah nuraniyya`). From the delay of those specters, the form that was wrought became the soul. This came to be after the command of origination (`ibda'`), with the command of origination. The Universal Soul, then, is the soul of the body in the world of life, and it is toward the Universal Soul that all these lower souls are ultimately oriented.

The Universal Soul is not something received in the way the rational soul is received. Rather it is an origin, an ocean. As the firmaments are the origin of the sensory soul, as the Moon is the origin of the vegetative and the Sun of the sensory, just so the Universal Soul is the ocean from which the rational soul is drawn. In the world of life there exists the ocean of universal souls, and the soul that we receive is a part of it, a drop of it. Are all the essences then one unit? In the world of life all the essences are one unit, one world, one ocean — yet each remains in its place, and the drop, though taken from the ocean, is still reckoned a drop. The ocean is the whole; the drop is counted as a drop; and both descriptions hold together.

What, then, is the relation of the Pillar of Light to the Universal Soul? The connection is from it: the connection of the entire Pillar of Light is from the Universal Soul. The Universal Soul's connection is from the Pillar of Light; the Pillar's connection is from the Tenth (`'ashir`); and the connection of the Tenth is from the First (`awwal`). So the chain of light reaches from the First, through the Tenth, through the Pillar, to the Universal Soul, and thence to the rational soul of the believer.

### The Definition of the Universal Soul

Amir al-Mu'minin was questioned about this Universal Soul as the last of his definitions. The text reads: `wa-bud'u ittihadiha 'inda al-wiladati al-diniyyah` — and the beginning of its union is at the time of the religious birth. Three births are to be distinguished: the birth in the womb is the birth of the vegetative soul; the birth into the world is the birth of the sensory soul; and the religious birth — at the time of the noble covenant (`'ahd-i sharif`) — is the birth of the rational soul. The Universal Soul's union with the human being begins at this religious birth.

The text continues: `maqarruha al-'ilmu wa-al-haqiqah, mawadduha al-ma'arif al-rabbaniyyah` — its seat is knowledge and reality, its substance is the divine gnoses. The abode of the Universal Soul is knowledge and reality: to know the soul and to know its reality is its dwelling-place, its station of rest. And its substance, its sustenance and support, is from the divine gnosis (`al-ma'arif al-rabbaniyyah`): the more knowledge a person acquires, the more this soul is supported and supplied.

Then its departure: `sababu iftiraqiha ikhtilafu al-alat al-jismaniyyah` — the cause of its separation is the disharmony of the bodily instruments. When the four bodily temperaments fall into imbalance, the soul departs the body. `Wa-idha faraqat, raja'at ila ma minhu bada'at` — and when it separates, it returns to that from which it began. The rational soul, separating, returns to the Universal Soul from which it came.

And the manner of this return discloses a profound identity. The Universal Soul is the essence of the Imam of the time, peace and blessings be upon him; his noble form (`haykal`) is the ocean of the Universal Soul, and it is he who takes the departing soul. This is the very `sultan`, the authority, spoken of in the verse: `la tanfudhuna illa bi-sultan` — you will not pass through except by an authority. You cannot pass through the regions of the heavens and the earth except by a `sultan`; and that `sultan` is the noble form (`haykal`) of the Imam, in which all the forms come together and are gathered. The departing soul passes the bounds of heaven and earth only by the authority of the Imam's form, where every form has its meeting.

### The Definition Resumed: A Divine Power Whose Origin Is the Intellect

The author now sets out the full definition of the Universal Soul as Amir al-Mu'minin gave it, completing the ascending series of the four definitions. The questioner asked, after the rational soul: `ma hiya ya mawlay al-nafs al-kulliyyah?` — what is the Universal Soul, my master? And he answered, peace be upon him: `hiya quwwatun ilahiyyah, jawharun basitun, 'alimatun bil-quwwati wa-al-fi'l, asluha al-'aql, minhu istamaddat, wa-ilayhi da'at` — it is a divine power, a simple [widespread] substance, knowing in potentiality and in actuality, whose origin is the Intellect, from which it takes its support and toward which it calls.

The contrast across the four souls is now complete and exact. The vegetative soul is a natural power (`quwwatun tabi'iyyah`); the sensory soul is a celestial power (`quwwatun falakiyyah`); the rational soul is an intellectual power (`quwwatun 'ilmiyyah`); and the Universal Soul is a divine power (`quwwatun ilahiyyah`) — and this is a great difference indeed, marking the Universal Soul as the highest of the four. It is a simple, widespread substance (`jawharun basitun`), knowing in potentiality and in actuality (`'alimatun bil-quwwati wa-al-fi'l`). Its origin is the Intellect (`asluha al-'aql`) — from the Intellect it takes its support (`minhu istamaddat`), and toward the Intellect it calls (`wa-ilayhi da'at`).

### Each Soul Returns to Its Origin

Here the whole doctrine of return is gathered into a single principle: every soul goes back to its origin, and the origin of each lower soul is the rank immediately above it. The vegetative soul returns whither? `Ila ma minhu bada'at` — to that from which it began, which is the Moon; from the Moon down to the Rock is its province, and to the Moon it returns. The sensory soul returns to that from which it began, which is the Sun, the sphere of the Sun. The rational soul returns to that from which it began, which is the world of life, the Pillar of Light. And the Universal Soul returns to that from which it began, which is the Intellect, the world of lights — the very circle of the Intellect out of which it came forth is the circle to which it must go. The origin of the rational soul is the Universal Soul; the origin of the Universal Soul is the Intellect; and in every case the higher rank is the origin of the lower, so that the whole ladder of souls is a ladder of returns, each rung ascending to the one above it.

### The Universal Soul as the Supreme Word and the Tree of Tuba

The Universal Soul, having taken its support from the world of Intellect and called toward it, is now named with the loftiest of names. The text reads: `wa-hiya kalimatullah al-'ulya, shajarat al-tuba, jannat al-ma'wa, 'inda sidrat al-muntaha` — and it is the supreme Word of Allah, the Tree of Tuba, the Garden of Abode, beside the Lote-tree of the Utmost Boundary. The Universal Soul is the supreme Word of Allah (`kalimatullah al-'ulya`); it is the Tree of Tuba (`shajarat al-tuba`), the tree in which all the trees are planted; it is the Garden of Abode (`jannat al-ma'wa`); and it stands beside the Lote-tree of the Utmost Boundary (`sidrat al-muntaha`), which is the First Intellect (`al-'aql al-awwal`). Near the First Intellect lies the Garden of Abode, the place of everyone's gathering.

And the consequence is set down with finality: `man 'arafaha, sabaqa wa-naja; wa-man jahilaha, dalla wa-ghawa` — whoever knows it precedes and is saved, and whoever is ignorant of it is misguided and goes astray. To know the Universal Soul is to be among the foremost and the saved; to be ignorant of it is to lose one's way.

### Every Imam Corresponds to the First Intellect

The author now expounds the correspondences between the world of religion and the world of the Intellect. The text reads: `li-anna kulla maqamin fi 'alam al-din huwa muqabilun lil-'aql al-awwal, mawsumun bi-sidrat al-muntaha` — because every station in the world of religion corresponds to the First Intellect, named the Lote-tree of the Utmost Boundary. Having first established the First Intellect, the author shows that in the world of religion every station — and in particular every Imam — stands in correspondence to the First Intellect, and bears its name, the Sidrat al-Muntaha. The First Intellect is the Sidrat al-Muntaha; if the Imam is regarded under this aspect, then the Imam is the Sidrat al-Muntaha. And the Garden of Abode lies near it, the destination toward which the believer travels — the Bab's paradise.

The text continues: `wa-babuhu babun muqabilun lil-muba'ath al-awwal, al-mukanna 'an martabatihi bi-jannat al-ma'wa, al-mukanna 'anhu 'inda dhalika bi-al-nafs al-kulliyyah` — and his Bab is a Bab corresponding to the First Emanated Being, whose rank is alluded to by the Garden of Abode, and which is itself alluded to, in that context, by the Universal Soul. The Bab of the Sidrat al-Muntaha corresponds to the First Emanated Being (`muba'ath al-awwal`), whose rank is named by the Garden of Abode (`jannat al-ma'wa`); and this First Emanated Being is alluded to by the Universal Soul. In the world of Intellect, the First Intellect may be called the Imam, and the First Emanated Being may be called the Bab.

Therefore, as the text concludes: `wa-kana Amir al-mu'minin salawatullah 'alayh qad ramaza 'an al-bab bi-al-nafs al-kulliyyah, wa-'an jannat al-ma'wa 'an maqamin muqabilin li-hadha al-muba'ath al-awwal` — and Amir al-Mu'minin, the prayers of Allah be upon him, alluded to the Bab by the Universal Soul, and to the Garden of Abode by a station corresponding to this First Emanated Being. By the Universal Soul, Ali alluded to the Bab; and by the Garden of Abode, he alluded to the rank corresponding to the First Emanated Being. `Wa-kadhalika kullu haddin min hudud al-din fa-huwa muqabilun li-'aqlin min 'uqul 'alam al-ibda'` — and likewise, every rank among the ranks of religion corresponds to an intellect among the intellects of the world of origination. `Wa-sayati 'alayhim al-qawl fi ma ba'd, in sha'a Allah ta'ala` — and the discourse upon these will come hereafter, if God the Exalted wills.

### The Flowing of the Pillar of Light Through the Hudud

The chapter now traces the course of the Pillar of Light as it descends through the ranks of the religious hierarchy. The text reads: `thumma inna dhalika al-'amud al-nurani jarin min al-bab ila al-hujjah, wa-min al-hujjah ila al-da'i, wa-min al-da'i ila al-ma'dhun, wa-min al-ma'dhun ila al-mu'min, wa-min al-mu'min ila al-mustajib` — then that luminous Pillar flows from the Bab to the Hujjah, and from the Hujjah to the Da'i, and from the Da'i to the Ma'dhun, and from the Ma'dhun to the believer, and from the believer to the respondent. The Pillar of Light flows down in order: from the Bab to the Hujjah, to the Da'i, to the Ma'dhun, to the believer (`mu'min`), and to the respondent (`mustajib`) — the one who takes the noble covenant — `kama sabaqa min al-qawl`, as has preceded in the discourse. By this graded flow the light of the Imam reaches even to the lowest who has answered the call.

### Grammar as a Mirror of the Luminous World

Before describing the soul's final transfer, the chapter pauses upon a remarkable teaching: that the rules of Arabic grammar mirror the order of the luminous world, and that Amir al-Mu'minin founded the science of grammar precisely to encode this correspondence. When Mawla Amir al-Mu'minin, peace and blessings be upon him, undertook to teach Abu'l-Aswad al-Du'ali the sciences of morphology (`sarf`) and syntax (`nahw`), he began by directing him to understand the letters. There are twenty-eight letters; the one who utters them produces a word. From these twenty-eight letters, some combinations form meaningful words and some do not: the meaningful are called `kalimah`, the meaningless are unintelligible. And meaningful words are of three kinds: the noun (`ism`), the verb (`fi'l`), and the particle (`harf`).

Ali expounded the inner sense of each. What is a noun? It signifies the luminous essences themselves. What is a verb? It signifies their intentions, their acts. And what is a particle? It is that which creates the relation between the two — between a noun and a verb. Thus the three parts of speech mirror the three realities of the luminous world: the essences, their intentions, and the relations among them. In this way the whole science of grammar was unfolded as a likeness of what is happening in the world of life and the world of lights.

The correspondence extends to the very rules of case. As the text gives them: `kullu fa'ilin marfu'un` — every subject is raised. The subject (`fa'il`) bears the `dammah`, the mark of elevation; and the inner meaning is that in the luminous world, the one who acts, who first forms an intention and advances foremost, is the one elevated and raised (`marfu'`), set at the forefront. `Kullu maf'ulin mansubun` — every object is established. The one upon whom an effect is wrought (`maf'ul`) bears the `fathah` and is established (`mansub`). And `kullu majrurin maksurun` — everything governed by a preposition is broken. The noun drawn by a preposition (`majrur`) bears the `kasrah` and is broken (`maksur`), being neither here nor there. The grammar of raising, establishing, and breaking is thus a figure of the ranks of the luminous world: the foremost raised, the acted-upon established, the dependent broken.

### Arabic, the Original Language

From this it follows that Arabic is no ordinary tongue but the original and still-living language. Other languages of the world have evolved and changed; but Arabic stands apart, for the foundation of `La ilaha illa Allah` itself rests upon its twenty-eight letters. Since the formula of divine unity is built upon these twenty-eight letters, it follows of necessity that the language encoding it must be one that still exists today. As the Quran declares, it was revealed `bi-lisanin 'arabiyyin mubin` — in a clear Arabic tongue. Allah named no other language — not English, not any of the tongues of the regions — but named the Arabic language; and so it is. All the numbers, the figures, the words came with `La ilaha illa Allah`, for within that formula all the laws are encoded; the whole word, the light of the word `La ilaha illa Allah`, contains everything within it.

### The Transfer of the Departing Soul

The chapter now arrives at the moment of the soul's departure from the world. The text reads: `fa-idha aanat nuqlatu 'aqlin min al-mustajibin aw ghayrihim, harraka al-'aql al-'ashir dhalika al-'amud al-nurani al-mawsum bil-jari` — and when the time of transfer of one of the respondents, or of others, draws near, the Tenth Intellect moves that luminous Pillar named "the flowing one." The verb `aanat`, a past-tense feminine verb from the root meaning to draw near, must be read with understanding, for one who reads heedlessly might mistake it for `anta` or `anti` or `antum`; but read with understanding it is `aanat` — it has come near, it approaches. When the transfer (`nuqlah`) of a respondent or another draws near, the Tenth Intellect (`al-'aql al-'ashir`) sets in motion the luminous Pillar. The Tenth Intellect is the governing rank of the world of nature, distinct from the Imam, yet its relation is toward the Imam; it receives the command and moves the Pillar. The Pillar so moved is named `al-jari`, the flowing one — for `jari` means flowing; the Tenth Intellect causes it to flow.

The text continues: `wa-ittasalat al-harakatu bi-imam dhalika al-zaman, fa-'alima anna tilka al-harakata li-naqlati dhalika al-muntaqil` — and the movement connects with the Imam of that time, and he knows that that movement is for the transfer of that departing one. The Tenth Intellect is above; below it is the Imam; the movement of the Pillar connects with the Imam of the time, and he knows by it that the movement signals the transfer of one who is departing — that the soul has been purified and its time of transfer has come. The movement is the sign read by the Imam.

Then: `wa-harraka dhalika al-'amud, wa-jazabahu ilayhi` — and he moves that Pillar, and draws it toward himself. The Imam of the time moves the Pillar of Light and attracts toward himself the one who is departing. The Imam draws the Pillar, and by it the departing soul, toward himself; only by the sign and act of the Imam of the time does this transfer occur. The Tenth Intellect receives the command, and through it the Imam of the time, peace and blessings be upon him, accomplishes the drawing.

### The Drawing of the Luminous Form and Its Neighbor

The text describes the attraction of the soul in two stages, the rational soul drawn first and the sensory soul drawn along with it. `Wa-anjazabat tilka al-surah bi-anjizabi dhalika al-'amud` — and that form is drawn by the drawing of that Pillar. The verb `anjazaba` is the intransitive form — it was drawn, attracted — as distinct from the transitive `jazaba`, he drew. The luminous form, the rational soul, is drawn by the attraction of the Pillar.

`Wa-jazabat ilayha tilka al-nafs allati kanat mujawiratan laha` — and there is drawn toward it that soul which had been its neighbor. The pronoun in `ilayha` refers back to the form; toward that form is drawn the soul that had been its neighbor — that is, the sensory soul (`nafs hissiyya`) which, as was stated before, abided as a neighbor (`mujawir`) of the rational soul. With the rational soul, the sensory soul too is drawn and comes along. The Pillar of Light draws only the rational soul directly; but with the rational soul, the sensory soul that neighbored it is pulled along as well.

A precise distinction governs this. The sensory soul was the neighbor (`mujawir`) of the rational soul, not mixed into it (`mumazij`); and the difference between neighboring and mixing is of the first importance. To neighbor (`mujawarah`) is to abide in the vicinity; to mix (`mumazajah`) is to become one fused thing. Throughout the person's life in the world the sensory soul remained the neighbor of the rational soul, not intermingled with it; for had they mixed, both would share a single fate, and the sensory soul would itself become rational and be drawn by the Pillar of Light directly, the very name "sensory soul" no longer applying to it. The Pillar of Light is related to the rational soul, or to whatever has become like the rational soul; that becoming-like happens upon departure. But in the world they remained neighbors. And so the question arises: how much of the sensory soul is drawn along? The answer is given by the measure of obedience: as much of the sensory soul as followed and obeyed the rational soul in the world is drawn along with it at departure. Whatever portion of the sensory soul submitted to the rule of the rational soul becomes, at the end, fit to be drawn with it; whatever did not, remains behind.

The point of light, meanwhile, is described as growing from the very graces that sustain it: `thumma inna tilka al-nuqtata tansha'u shay'an ba'da shay'in min tilka al-'inayat al-ilahiyyah wa-al-mahabbat al-khalisah wa-al-a'mal al-saliha, wa-takunu al-nafs al-hissiyyatu mujawiratan laha` — then that point grows, little by little, out of those divine providences, that pure love, and those righteous deeds, while the sensory soul remains its neighbor. The point of light increases from the divine emanations, from sincere love, and from righteous works; and throughout, the sensory soul abides as its neighbor, not mixed into it, until the moment of departure.

### Nothing Small or Great Is Left Behind

The chapter reaches its culmination in the gathering of the whole into a single luminous reality. The text reads: `fa-anjazabat ilayha 'ala hay'ati al-jism al-kulli, la yughadiru saghiratan minhu wa-la kabirah` — so it is drawn toward it in the form of the universal body, leaving behind nothing of it, small or great. The sensory soul is drawn toward the rational soul in the form of the universal body (`haykal al-jism al-kulli`), and in this drawing nothing is left behind, neither the small nor the great; the whole is gathered and taken up. So the long descent that scattered the luminous essence into souls and body and the kingdoms of nature is at last reversed in a complete ascent, in which not the least portion is lost.

The chapter closes with praise and benediction: `wa-al-hamdu lillahi 'ala ma an'ama 'alayna min ma'rifatihi, wa-ittisali 'ilmina bi-'ilmihi` — and all praise belongs to Allah for the favor He bestowed upon us of His gnosis, and the joining of our knowledge to His knowledge. `Wa-salla Allahu 'ala nabiyyihi Muhammad salatan min nabiyyin, wa-'ala wasiyyihi 'Aliyyin salatan min wasiyyin, wa-'ala a'immati al-tahirin, wa-'ala mawlana al-Imam al-Tayyib al-Qasim` — and may Allah bless His Prophet Muhammad with the blessing of a Prophet, and His legatee Ali with the blessing of a legatee, and the pure Imams, and our master the Imam al-Tayyib al-Qasim — upon them all be peace.

So the whole arc of the chapter is sealed: the descent of the luminous essence through the planetary governance of the womb and the three souls of the body, and its return — drawn by the Pillar of Light at the Imam's sign, gathering its purified neighbor and leaving nothing behind — to the Universal Soul, the supreme Word of Allah and the Garden of Abode beside the First Intellect. Whoever knows this precedes and is saved.

## The Soul's Ascent through the Ranks of Nature

### The Structure of the First Chapter and the Departing Soul

The first chapter of *Mabda' wa-Ma'ad* is built upon five principles. To these the author appends two further movements: the sixth is cast as a debate, a dialectical examination of the matter; and the seventh expounds and unfolds the reality of the fifth principle, drawing out what was stated there in compressed form. It is within this architecture — five principles, a debate, and a final exposition — that the discourse on the soul's passage out of the body and its ascent through the ranks of nature is set.

The chapter opens with a statement about what befalls the soul at the very moment of its extraction from the body: `thumma inna-l-nafsa 'inda intiza'iha min jismiha fa-tamtaziju bi-tilka-l-surati wa-tasirani shay'an wahidan, wa-dhatan wahidatan, wa-nuran sha'sha'aniyan, malakutiyan, jibriliyan` — "Then indeed the soul, at the time of its being drawn out of its body, mixes with that form, and the two become one single thing, one single essence, and a radiant, angelic, Gabrielic light." This is the soul in question — the soul that was bound to the body, the believer's soul, more precisely the sensory soul, the *nafs-e-hissiyah*. When it is drawn out of its bodily dwelling, the operative verb is *tamtaziju*: it mixes. It does not merely stand beside its spiritual form but merges with it, and through that merging it becomes joined to the rational soul, the *natiqah*. The Arabic continues, `wa-tasirani shay'an wahidan` — "and the two become one single thing." Soul and form, henceforth, are one and the same.

Here lies a distinction of the first importance, and it governs everything that follows. In the state of life, the soul and its form were *mujawir* — adjacent, neighbouring, lying alongside one another but remaining two. After death they become *mumazij* — intermixed, fused so completely that no boundary remains between them. The difference between adjacency in life and intermixture in death is not a minor refinement of vocabulary; it is a transformation of the soul's very mode of being. What was two becomes one essence, `wa-dhatan wahidatan` — one *dhat*, a single substance — and that single substance is described as `nuran sha'sha'aniyan malakutiyan jibriliyan`, a light that is radiant, of the angelic kingdom, and Gabrielic in nature.

The text then turns to the body that has been left behind: `wa-yabqa dhalika-l-jismu farighan muwatan` — "and that body remains empty and dead." It lies vacant of all that animated it, until the departed substance has become `dhalika-l-jawhara-l-nuraniya-l-hayya-l-qadira-l-'alima-l-natiqa-l-sami'a-l-basira-l-dha'iqa-l-shamm` — that luminous substance, the *jawhar-e-nurani*, which is living (*hayy*), powerful (*qadir*), knowing (*'alim*), speaking (*natiq*), hearing (*sami'*), seeing (*basir*), tasting (*dha'iq*), and smelling (*shamm*). These eight qualities — life, power, knowledge, speech, hearing, sight, taste, and smell — are precisely the qualities of the sensory soul. The *nafs-e-hissiyah* carries all of them, and when it departs the body it carries them with it. The substance that leaves is therefore not a faint residue but a complete, percipient, articulate being.

Why is this departing substance called *luminous* — a *jawhar-e-nurani*? Because it has become one with the rational soul, the *nafs-e-natiqah*. The qualities of the *natiqah* have entered into it, and it is from that union with the rational soul that its luminosity derives. The sensory soul, joined to the rational, is bathed in the rational soul's light, and so the whole that departs shines.

What, then, of the corpse? The text states that the soul and the two forms have become a single essence, while `baqiya-l-jismu dumlatan min-al-dumal, 'adima-l-idrak wa-l-shu'ur` — "the body remains a corpse among the corpses, devoid of perception and feeling." There is nothing left in the dead body; it is emptied of all things. And the reason is exact: every quality the body once possessed belonged to it only on account of the soul. It was through the soul that the body was luminous, living, powerful, knowing, and speaking. Once the soul has separated, every one of these powers ends with it, for they were never the body's own. The body was a vessel borrowing life; when the borrower departs, the loan is recalled in full.

### The Purified Soul's Union with Its Exalted Limit

The text advances to a further stage with the line: `thumma inna tilka-l-nafsa wa-l-surata-l-latayni qad sarata dhatan wahidatan yansalikhani bi-suratin a'la minha, fa-yumazijaniha wa-yasirani wa-iyyaha dhatan wahidatan wa-jumlatan wahidatan mujawarina li-nafsi sahib tilka-l-surati-l-lati qad yumazijatiha` — "Then that soul and that form, the two of which had become a single essence, slough off toward a form higher than themselves, and they intermix with it, and the two together with it become a single essence and a single whole, adjacent to the soul of the owner of that form with which it has intermixed."

There is a crucial qualification to grasp before unfolding this. The process just described does not apply to every soul indiscriminately. It applies to the soul and the form that have *become pure*. And whose soul and form are these? They belong to the one whose end was good — *khatima bil-khayr* — the one who departed upon the *Sirat al-Mustaqim*, the Straight Path. His end was good; his soul has therefore become pure, and his form was pure from the beginning. For such a one, both soul and form are purified, and only such a purified pair is fit for the ascent the text now describes.

The mechanism is this. That soul and that form, having become one essence and one whole — having merged into a single thing — do not remain at their own level. They rise and seek out a form higher than themselves: the luminous form of their *hadde aali*, their Exalted Limit. With this higher form they intermix; *fa-yumazijaniha* — "the two intermix with it" — they merge into it. And so soul, form, and the higher form of the Exalted Limit become *dhatan wahidatan*, one essence, *wa-jumlatan wahidatan*, one whole. The lower part — the believer's purified soul-and-form — does not remain a separate thing alongside its Limit; it is absorbed into the Limit's luminous form and becomes one essence with it.

Yet the text adds a precise reservation: this union is *mujawarina li-nafsi sahib tilka-l-surah* — they become adjacent to the soul of the owner of that higher form. The purified believer's whole merges with the *form* of its Exalted Limit, becoming one essence with that form, while remaining merely *adjacent* — *mujawir* — to the Exalted Limit's own sensory soul, its *nafs-e-hissiyah*. The believer's part fuses with the form but stands alongside the living sensory soul of the higher being. This is the very same distinction that governed the soul's own passage: fusion with the form, adjacency with the living soul.

And this raises the obvious question: does the Exalted Limit die? It does not. The *hadde aali* remains alive — this is the meaning of `li-nafsi sahib tilka-l-surah`, "to the soul of the owner of that form," for that owner is living. The believer's purified soul therefore does not attach itself to something dead and waiting; it attaches to a living higher being, fusing with the Limit's luminous form while remaining adjacent to the Limit's still-living sensory soul. The author is at pains here to clarify that he is describing the *process* of the believer's ascent during the Limit's lifetime; what becomes of the Exalted Limit when its own term ends — whether its own end too is good, and what follows — is reserved for a later place in the discourse. For now the teaching stands complete: the purified believer's soul-and-form ascends, merges with the luminous form of its living Exalted Limit, becomes one essence with that form, and remains adjacent to its living sensory soul.

### The Sun, the Moon, and the Aeolian Souls

From the destiny of the individual purified soul the discourse widens to the great cosmic machinery by which souls are refined among the spheres. It begins with a line of Arabic poetry concerning grammatical gender. In Arabic the sun, *shams*, is treated as feminine, and the moon, *qamar*, as masculine. One might imagine this implies some inferiority on the part of the sun. The poet answers that to name the sun feminine and the moon masculine in no way diminishes the sun's superiority, for it is the sun's rays that make the moon luminous in the first place. The moon has no light of its own; whatever brilliance it possesses is borrowed wholly from the sun. Grammatical gender is a convention of language, *zaban*; it carries no judgment about rank or power. The sun remains the giver, the moon the receiver, whatever the genders the language assigns them.

This image of borrowed light opens onto a deeper doctrine. The relevant text reads `wa-yajtami'u 'indahu shay'un` — "and there gathers with it [the moon] a thing." What gathers with the moon, little by little, are the aeolian souls — the *nufus-e-rihiyyah*, the windy or breath-borne souls carried within the sun's light. The moon collects these souls a little at a time, drawing them in as they are conveyed toward fullness, and the result of this accumulation is the brilliant, complete light of the moon on the fourteenth night. The famous full moon of the fourteenth, of which people speak with wonder, is in reality the gathered radiance of the aeolian souls that the moon has been collecting from the sun's light. The completion of that light comes `min jami'-il-afaq` — from all the horizons — so that the moon's brilliance is filled out from every quarter.

But these aeolian souls are not, at the outset, in a clean state. They carry impurities — *kudurat*. The moon's early shining derives precisely from these impurity-laden *rihiyyah* souls; the first dense glow of the waxing moon comes from souls that still bear their accumulated denseness. There is, moreover, a contrast of substance between the moon's own light and the souls it gathers: the moon's light is *kasif* — gross, dense — while the aeolian souls are *latif* — subtle. As the cycle proceeds, purification begins; the impurities are progressively cleansed, and as they are removed the luminosity of these souls increases. The light grows brighter not by addition of new matter but by the refinement of what is already there — the denseness burning away to leave subtlety behind.

It is in this connection that a ritual point is drawn out. The white nights — *ayyam al-biz* — are the thirteenth, fourteenth, and fifteenth of the lunar month, the nights of the moon's fullest brilliance, with the day before and the day after sometimes joined to them. Fasting upon these white nights is *mustahabb*, recommended. The recommendation of fasting on precisely these nights is woven into this cosmic account of the moon gathered to fullness by the purified aeolian souls.

### The Return of the Vapor and the Ordering of the Spheres

Having reached fullness, the moon does not retain its gathered light. It begins to return it. After the full moon, the moon deposits the subtle vapor — *bukhar-e-latif* — back toward the sun. The *bukhar-e-latif* and the *nufus-e-rihiyyah* are one and the same thing, named now by its subtlety: the aeolian souls considered as subtle vapor. This return is not direct and not sudden. It proceeds *shay'an ba'da shay'in* — bit by bit, a little after a little, not all at once — and it passes through intermediary stations: first to Mercury, *'utarid*, then to Venus, *zuhra*, and only then onward toward the sun.

This requires a clear picture of the celestial ordering, which the text supplies from lowest to highest. The moon is the lowest. Above the moon is Venus. Above Venus is Mercury. And above Mercury is the sun. The vapor, then, ascends: the moon hands it first to Mercury, then to Venus, and from there it reaches the sun — and as the souls are collected, the moon keeps sending them upward, station by station, until the month is complete.

What is it, exactly, that is being purified in this returning *rihiyyat*? It is not the sins that departed with the rational soul, nor the believer's own moral characteristics, which each person must cleanse for himself by his own striving. What is purified here is the *jismaniyyat* — the bodily corporeality — that the aeolian souls acquired during their burial with the body. When these souls were interred together with the corpse, they took on a grossness; they became *kal-jism*, like a body, and impurities developed within them. It is this acquired bodily denseness that the planetary stations now remove, refining the souls into subtle vapor. The image given is that of cleaning a house: a house is cleaned by carrying out the accumulated trash, and just so, the things of denseness, the accumulated grossness, are drawn out of the aeolian souls as they pass from station to station. The reader is cautioned not to confuse two purifications: the purification of one's moral self is a matter for the *dhat*, for one's own striving; the purification spoken of in the planets is the burning away of acquired corporeal denseness through heat and the stages of the spheres.

Concerning that heat, a striking proportion is offered. In place of the heat of hellfire, the souls undergoing this purification are given the heat of the sun. The sun has great heat indeed — yet the heat of hell is far greater, a thousand times greater. The souls that are refined among the spheres are spared the immeasurable fire of hell and given instead the lesser, sufficient heat of the sun for their cleansing. The mercy in this exchange is plain: a thousandfold lesser fire accomplishes the refining work.

When the moon has handed over to the sun, by way of Mercury and Venus, all the aeolian souls it had gathered, it is left empty of that light. Having delivered the full moon's radiance bit by bit and then completely, it becomes wholly empty, and then returns once more to receive what is renewed for it from that matter — `abadan sarmadan`, forever and ever, an unending cycle. This emptying explains an observed fact: in the last days of the lunar month, around the twenty-ninth and thirtieth, the moon's light is no longer visible. It is obscured because the *rihiyyah* are absent from it and its light has grown faint. Over the span of about three days this obscuration occurs and the meeting — the handing-over to the sun — is completed.

The lunar cycle is thus mapped onto a pattern of ascent and descent. From the first of the month to the twelfth is the period of ascending. The fifteenth is the zenith — the *intila'* — the height of the gathering. And from the sixteenth onward begins the period of coming down to the world: the moon delivers the vapors down to the world. The first half is gathering and rising; the second half is delivery and descent.

### The Sun's Refinement of the Souls

When the aeolian souls have reached the precinct — the *hayyiz* — of the sun, they arrive at a region of a particular nature. The sun's light is the quintessence — the *khulasa* — of the heat of the subtle instinctive bodies, the *ajsam-e-ghariziyyah*. In this light the very distillate of their heat is present. This sun's heat is a vehicle — *markab* — for the sensory life, *hayat-e-hissiyyah*, and a spouse — *zawj* — for it, and a companion — *qarin* — in the heart, such that it is the very center — *markaz* — of the instinctive heat, the *hararat-e-ghariziyyah*. The meaning is that within the precinct of the sun the souls encounter the concentrated essence of the heat of all the instinctive bodies: the sun's light *is* that quintessence, the carrier and consort of sensory life and the focal center of instinctive heat.

Within this precinct the sun performs its refining work, expressed by the verb *arraqat-ha* — "it makes it sweat." *'Araq* is sweat; the sun causes the souls to sweat, breaks them down, and ferments them. Through this triple action — sweating, breaking down, fermenting — the souls are processed until they are conveyed to a station suitable for the door of the totality of the subtle, spiritual, noble forms. The sun, in other words, takes the souls that have been delivered up to it and works upon them as one works upon raw matter to be perfected: drawing out their residue as sweat, dissolving their structure, and letting them ferment until they are fit to enter the assembly of subtle and noble forms.

Having been so refined, the whole is then delivered upward by the sun to higher stations. The sun delivers that whole to the level of Jupiter, *mushtari*, which is the Greater Fortune — *Sa'd al-Akbar* — and to the spiritualities of Venus, *zuhra*, which is the Lesser Fortune — *Sa'd al-Asghar* — while Mercury, *'utarid*, serves as the scribe of the heavens, *katib-e-falak*. The Greater Fortune is Jupiter; the Lesser Fortune is Venus; Mercury is the heavens' recording scribe. By this delivery the whole, after it has become pure and refined — a subtle, spiritual, luminous, radiant whole — becomes a pure vessel prepared, together with its station, for the command of the Imam. The souls, processed by the sun and elevated through the fortunate planets, are made into a purified instrument awaiting the Imam's command.

### The Descent into the World and the Food of the Imam

Now the purified spiritualities must be brought down into the world, and the text describes the manner of their descent. The rays of the two Fortunes — the Greater and the Lesser — deposit these purified spiritualities upon some fruit among the fruits that are eaten, or upon the waters that are drunk, like the dew. Settling upon fruit and water as dew settles, the refined spiritualities become available to be consumed.

It is here that the account turns to the birth of the Prophet, peace and blessings upon him, as an illustration of how these descended spiritualities enter the chain of generation. It is said that his birth came about from the apple of paradise, or from waters drunk like the dew. A narration is recalled: that while traveling, a voice from the unseen was heard, a spring appeared, and the command came, "Drink from this — from this the birth of the Seal of the Messengers, *khatim-ul-mursalin*, shall come about." The water was drunk, and thereafter, from Sayyidatuna Amina, the Prophet was born as the Seal of the Messengers. The descended dew, bearing the refined spiritualities, became by way of the drunk water the very matter from which the Prophet's coming was prepared.

This descended subtle whole is, the text says, the food of the Master of the Era and the *'asr* — that is, the Imam of the Time — and the food of the moon and the sun. Divine providence stands guardian over it, a trustee and supervisor watching over it, until it attains the Near Station — *maqam-e-qarib* — which is the master of the *nasut*, the corporeal world below. The Imam of the Time and his pure spouse take this descended subtle vapor as their food; it comes from above and falls upon their food, included within it. And from this food, received by the two of them together, the wife's purified quintessence is formed — the *sulalah*.

The term *sulalah* is then defined precisely. The *sulalah* denotes the purified parts of a human's emergence which are brought and placed in the father's pure loins and in the mother's womb. These are the *sulalah*. When the two combine — the part in the father and the part in the mother — a *nutfah*, a sperm-drop, is formed from their union. This is the stage of *sulalah*: the purified parental matter that, on combining, becomes the *nutfah*. The Imam's spouse is from the most virtuous of women, the most noble and the purest of them, and it is in her, joined to the Imam, that this purified quintessence is gathered.

### The Imam's Joy and the Gathering of the Pure Whole

When the Imam and his spouse partake together of that descended thing, a momentous event occurs at the level of knowledge. The Station — the Imam — knows at that very time the reality of that thing, which is from the decree of Allah, concerning the newborn who is to come. And he is gladdened by it: there enters upon the two of them, from joy and delight and happiness, what cannot be described or expressed in words. The conception is not a blind biological event but a knowing one; the Imam apprehends by Allah's decree the reality of the coming child and is filled with an indescribable joy.

At that time, too, the Imam gives good tidings of this matter to the deputy of the Station — the one who will succeed him and return to him. He announces the coming successor, the child who is to be born and who will inherit the rank, and he alludes to the child's matter, his hidden affair. The good news is passed from the Imam to his deputy, the future is foretold, and the deputies of the deputies are then gathered together with what has come about — with the purified matter present with the Imam and with his wife. Through this gathering the whole becomes a single thing, ascending through the ranks. From this single ascending whole the food of the fetus is made: the child in its mother's womb eats from it, is nourished by it, and grows by it.

The consequence is profound. The Imam's body in the womb is therefore not nourished, as other fetuses are, by menstrual blood; its food is this pure, revered, noble whole — the purified aeolian souls that have descended from the spiritualities and been placed upon the fruits. Where an ordinary child draws its nourishment in the womb from the menstrual blood, the body of the Imam draws its nourishment from this purified *rihiyyah* whole. And because of this it is free from the impurity — the *tams* — that affects other fetuses in their mothers' wombs. The menstrual blood does not come near it. The fetus of the Imam grows upon a pure and purified whole, untouched by the impurity that nourishes other children.

### The Ahl al-Bayt and Freedom from Impurity

This freedom from *tams* is anchored in scripture. As Allah, exalted is He, has said: `Innama yuridu-Llahu liyudhhiba 'ankumu-r-rijsa Ahl al-Bayt wa-yutahhirakum tathira` — "Indeed Allah desires only to remove all impurity from you, O People of the House, and to purify you with a thorough purification." This verse is the scriptural proof of the Ahl al-Bayt's freedom from impurity. The pure whole that nourishes the Imam's body, and the removal of *rijs* — impurity — that the verse proclaims, are two faces of the same truth: the People of the House are constituted, from the womb onward, in purity.

From this two conditions of membership in the Ahl al-Bayt are drawn. The first is freedom from the impurity of *tams* — that one's nourishment in the womb not be the menstrual blood, but the pure purified whole. That is one condition. The second condition is that the Ahl al-Bayt do not eat the wealth of *sadaqah* — they do not consume charity. These two together — purity of origin and abstention from charity — mark the true People of the House.

The second condition is illustrated by a famous incident concerning the young Imam Hasan, peace upon him. A date — given as charity — had been set down, and the child, being small, picked it up and was about to eat it. The Prophet took the date out of his mouth, and because the child's saliva was upon it he removed that too, and he declared: `sadaqatun la tahillu li-Ahl al-Bayt` — "charity is not lawful for the Ahl al-Bayt." Even from a small child, even a single date of charity, the Prophet removed, to make the prohibition unmistakable.

From this prohibition an argument is then pressed. The wives of the Prophet claimed to be of the Ahl al-Bayt. Yet they consumed the wealth of *sadaqah* — they ate charity upon charity. So much so that when charity did not arrive, when wealth from the public treasury was not forthcoming, 'Aisha would raise a commotion, riding out upon a camel to demand it. But the sadaqah criterion is decisive: the true Ahl al-Bayt do not eat charity. By that very measure, then, the claim of the wives cannot be reconciled with genuine membership of the Ahl al-Bayt. They consumed what the Ahl al-Bayt may not consume, and even sought treasury wealth besides; therefore, by the criterion the Prophet himself established with the date in Imam Hasan's mouth, they fall outside the People of the House the verse purifies.

### The Convergence of the Believer's Three Faculties

The discourse now returns to the believer, tracing how the believer's own purified faculties enter into the formation of the Imam's son. The believer's purified growth-soul — the *nafs-e-namiyyah* — and his sensory-soul — the *nafs-e-hissiyyah* — having been refined and brought up through the spheres, go into the Imam. There they meet the Imam's rational soul, his *natiqah*, and his growth-soul and his sensory-soul. The believer's part becomes the spirit — the *ruh* — and the *nasut*, the corporeal substrate from which the body of the Imam's son is formed. The Imam's own being and soul are a *command*, the *ruh*; and from the believer's contributed part the body is built. Those parts are therefore no longer the believer's own; they have not stayed with him. In the *lahut* — the higher world of forms and souls — the believer's *nafs* is present, the *nafs-e-hissiyyah* abiding there; and in the *nasut*, the new body that comes, the believer's *namiyyah* is present. So the believer's faculties pass out of him and into the formation of the Imam's son.

The text describes the moment of convergence. At the begetting of the Imam's son, three streams come together. The believer's growth-soul had earlier become connected with his form, his *surah*, and his sensory-soul; the two had merged. And in the *lahut*, when the connection with the *nasut* is made, three streams arrive and gather: the believer's growth-soul, his sensory-soul, and his form all come and collect together. They congregate in one ocean — the ocean of the Imam's son — and connect with the *nasut*. When the Imam begets his son, the father's entire collection, the *surah* and the rest, comes and connects with the *nasut*; and so all three — growth-soul, sensory-soul, and form — are gathered in one place. The author underscores the reason for their earlier separation: the three had been processed separately — one process for the rational soul, one for the spiritual form (the *surat-e-ruhani*), one for the sensory soul, and then, separately again, the process for the growth-soul, which came up only after its own purification. They were divided precisely so that they might be reunited; the separation was for the sake of the reunion. Upon the begetting of the Imam's son, the three congregate at last in one gathering.

There is, moreover, an order to this ascent of the believer's faculties, and the order reflects their relative perfection. The first and most superior to ascend was the rational soul, the *nafs-e-natiqah* — it reached the father first. After it came the sensory soul, the *nafs-e-hissiyyah*, which reached the father next. The growth-soul, the *nafs-e-namiyyah*, had within it some defect, some lack of completion; and because of this defect it did not ascend in the first rank but came up later, ascending through a further process of perfection. Thus the rational soul is first and highest, the sensory soul second, and the growth-soul last — its lateness being the consequence of the imperfection it had to make good before it could rise.

### The Forming of the Imam's Son's Body

The body of the Imam's son is unlike an ordinary human body in its very constituents. The parts of an ordinary human body are the four elements — fire, air, water, and earth. The body of the Imam's son, by contrast, is formed from the growth-souls, the *nufus-e-namiyyah*. For this whole there is a subtle part, drawn for it from the fermentation of the sun and the planets, the beneficent ones, together with a gaze from the divine providences. The Imam's body, the *nasut*, having been formed, now requires what any living body requires: a growth-soul and a sensory-soul to animate it. The text turns to explain how the growth-soul and sensory-soul of the Imam's son come to it — for this is not an ordinary fetus, and its faculties do not arise in the ordinary way but descend from the celestial bodies.

The growth-soul of the Imam's son is the single most subtle part to have ascended from the entire universe. Among the most subtle parts of the refined growth-souls — the *nufus-e-namiyyah* gathered and refined among the spheres — the very subtlest, the most superior, is drawn out, and it is this that becomes the growth-soul of the Imam's son. That most subtle part becomes a *nafs* for the whole — for the whole, that is, with which all the growth-souls have collected. The growth-soul that came from the celestial bodies and from the fermentation of the sun, this subtlest part of it, becomes the *nutfah*, attaining the station of the sperm-drop.

The timing is then specified. When the fourth month comes, the growth-soul — the *nafs-e-namiyyah*, the most subtle ascended part — is obtained from the sun and connects with the developing whole, becoming its *nafs*. On the fourth month, from the sun, the growth-soul comes down and joins the body, and thereby the body acquires its animating growth-soul. So the most subtle part that ascended from the whole universe, refined in the sun, descends in the fourth month and becomes the very growth-soul of the Imam's son.

When that whole has completed its term in its mother's womb, what appears is a complete, perfect, knowing, powerful, subtle, transparent man, equipped with the five senses — a noble disembodied substance, a *jawhar-e-latif*, brought to completion little by little and in the quickest of times. Being *mujarrad* — disembodied, abstracted from gross matter — all his powers are pure. His rays and his lights see; he sees with the eye of the beloved, with the eye of love. His faculties are of piety, purity, nobility, and loftiness beyond what any describer can describe or express. He is, in sum, a subtle and transparent man whose every faculty is pure, formed not of earth but of the refined growth-souls and animated by the subtlest soul the universe has yielded.

A final clarification is made concerning the state of these parts. The parts gathered in the Imam's *nasut* are not still undergoing purification once they have arrived; they have already been fully processed. In the celestial spheres the purification took place; the believer's body has already become his soul, refined among the planets. When these parts entered the Imam's *nasut*, the process of purification had ended there. In the *nasut*, in this world, there is no further purification — only the ordinary give-and-take of worldly life remains. In the sun there is no give-and-take; in the world there is. But the refining work itself is complete before ever the parts enter the Imam's corporeal nature; only the commerce of worldly existence continues thereafter.

### The Years of Upbringing and the Transfer of the Gate

The discourse, resuming with the fourth lesson, turns to what happens after the Imam's son is born and reaches a certain age. The governing line is: when the born one — the *mawlud* — reaches the years of upbringing, *sini al-tarbiyah*, which are four years from his birth, the gate — the *baab* — of his father transfers to him. This is the inner gate, the *baab-e-baatin*, of the father, and at the completion of four years it passes to the son.

A point of grammar is drawn out to fix the sense of the verb. When *iza* — "when" — precedes a past-tense verb, it gives the verb the meaning of the present tense; so the past-tense form here is to be read as "when the born one reaches," not "reached." A second grammatical observation concerns *sini*. The original of *sini* is *sinin*, and the original of *sinin* is *sanun*; the years, *saal*, are *sinun* in the nominative state. In the accusative or genitive — the states of *zabar* and *zer* — the form takes a *ya* and becomes *sinin*. The word is plural, not singular, and here it stands in the genitive. Why, then, is its final *nun* dropped, yielding *sini*? Because it is *mudaf ilayh* — the possessed term in a possessive construction, governed by *tarbiyah* — and the *nun* of a *mudaf* is dropped. Hence *sini al-tarbiyah*, "the years of upbringing," with the *nun* fallen away by the rule of the construct. So when the born one completes the years of upbringing, which are four years, when the Imam becomes four years old from his birth, the gate transfers.

The relations among the figures must be kept exact, for it is easy to confuse them. The *mawlud* — the born one, the son — is not himself the gate. The *baab* that transfers is the gate *of his father*. The Imam, the son's father, had appointed this *baab*. The son of the Imam, when he is born, is the *mawlud*; and when he reaches four years, the *baab* of his father — the inner gate that the father had appointed — transfers and becomes connected to him. The son himself has no *baab-ul-abwaab*, no supreme gate of his own; rather, the heart — the *qalb* — of the son becomes a temple, a *haykal*. The *baab* belongs to the father. The father has two gates: an outer gate, the *baab-e-zaahir*, and an inner gate, the *baab-e-baatin*. The outer gate is connected to the inner gate, and the inner gate is connected to the son of the Imam. So when the transfer occurs, the inner gate — with all that is gathered with it — connects to the son.

What is gathered with this transferring gate is described as the forms and souls, *suwar* and *nufus*, the *al-mustakhrajah* — the extracted ones, those that had been drawn out and gathered in a collective manner with the *baab-ul-abwaab*. The governing Arabic is `intaqala baabu abih` — "the gate of his father transferred" — and these extracted forms and souls `wa-ttasalat bi-nafsi dhalika al-mawlud` — "and they became connected to the soul of that born one." And it is on account of this that he is the Imam, `'alayhi afdal as-salam` — upon him be the best of salutations. The forms and souls that had been gathered with the supreme gate now reach the *mawlud* and connect with his soul, and the *mawlud* is thereby the Imam. This is stated as a general rule — a *kulliyah*: the transfer of the gate and the connection of the gathered, extracted forms and souls to the soul of the born one is the rule by which the born one is the Imam.

### Designation and the Imam from Birth

A question naturally arises: if the connection occurs at four years, does this mean the son is not the Imam until then? The answer holds two truths together. From birth, an Imam is an Imam — Imamate is his from the first. But the general rule, allowing for special circumstances and exceptions, is that *nass* — the designation — occurs upon the Imam's son at the age of four years. The designation may be of two kinds: it may be manifest, *jali*, openly proclaimed; or it may be hidden, *khafi*, concealed. Either kind may occur. Yet usually, at four years, the *nass* is made upon the son of the Imam. So the son is an Imam from birth in reality, while the public designation by which his Imamate is declared ordinarily comes at four — the moment, too, at which the gate transfers and the gathered forms and souls connect to his soul. The Arabic distinguishes the cases carefully: `bi-nafsi dhalika al-mawlud, alladhi huwa al-Imam` — "with the soul of that born one, who is the Imam" — for an Imam may have many sons, and the connection is made specifically with that born one who is the Imam.

And the father — when this transfer has occurred, does he cease to be the Imam? No; rather, his rank is elevated. The father is raised higher, and the *mawlud* becomes the Imam, the Imam of the Time — *Imam-e-Zamana* — and he begins, slowly and gradually, to take charge. Upon him descend *jadhb* (attraction), *fath* (opening), *khayal* (imagination), and all the *ishraqat* (illuminations); and bit by bit, through the years of upbringing, he assumes the office. The transfer is not a sudden seizure of all authority but a gradual taking-up of charge as the illuminations settle upon him.

This four-year connection is evidenced in the words of the Prophet, peace upon him: `Al-Hasanu wal-Husaynu imamaani qaama aw qa'ada` — "Al-Hasan and Al-Husayn are two Imams, whether they rise or sit." When was this said? Around the time of the Prophet's passing, in the third or fourth year of the Hijra, when Hasan and Husayn were young — about four years of age, and in the years following, around seven and eight. The saying belongs to the period when they were four. Its meaning, in light of the doctrine, is that the special forms and the gate that belonged to them had come and become connected to them — and if they were not yet fully connected, the forms were nonetheless with them, coming to be connected. The Prophet's declaration of their Imamate while they were of that age testifies to the four-year connection of their special forms and gates.

### The Thousandfold Luminous Temple and Its Spirit

The text now describes what the gathered forms and souls become once connected to the Imam's son: `wa-tasiru tilka as-suwaru wan-nufusu al-mushtama'ah fil-haykali al-alfi an-nurani al-insani ash-shan` — "and those forms and souls, gathered in the thousandfold, luminous, human, glorious structure, become..." Those forms and souls, the *suwar* and *nufus* gathered in the *haykal al-alfi* — the thousandfold luminous human glorious structure — are constituted into this temple. And `wa-yasiru tilka an-nafsu ruhan li-hadha al-haykal` — "and that soul becomes a spirit for this structure": the Imam's soul becomes the spirit, the *ruh*, of this *haykal*.

What is this *haykal*? The supreme gate, the *baab-ul-abwaab*, has the forms — the *suwarat* — that come to him; that gathering is what is called a *haykal*. It is also called the *lahut*. The *lahut* connects with the *nasut*: the *lahut* is the forms-and-souls, the *suwarat wa nufus*, and the *nasut* is the *mawlud*, the born body that came from above, from the moon, the *qamar*. So the Imam — the *mawlud*, the son of the Imam — has gathered at the *baab-ul-abwaab* all the *nasut*, all the souls of the believers, of the *hudud*, of the gates; and joined to these come the special forms and souls of that era. The lower parts of the believers' bodies were there below; the higher forms and souls came down and joined them in one gathering, until it becomes a single gathering — the *lahut* of forms and souls connecting with the *nasut* of the descended body.

This gathered structure takes the very shape of a body. The text reads `wa-hiya 'ala hay'at al-jism` — "and it is upon the form of the body" — fashioned, *mutashakkilatan*, in the likeness of a body. Its human form is constituted of the *ashbah* — the apparitions — which are luminous, *nurani*. The Arabic enumerates: `mutashakkilatan bi-jami'i a'da'ihi wa 'asabihi wa 'uruqihi wa-jami'i aalatihi wa ra'sihi wa 'aynihi wa udhunihi wa anfihi wa famihi wa yadihi wa rijlihi wa ghayri dhalik` — "formed with all of its limbs, its nerves, its veins, all its instruments, its head, its eye, its ear, its nose, its mouth, its hand, its foot, and other than that." The luminous human structure is complete in the shape of a body, with all its organs and members, its nerves and veins and instruments — and the text counts seven items: head, eye, ear, nose, hand, foot, and "other than that" — all of them made of luminous apparitions.

The relation of each form to its soul within the *haykal* is then stated: `wa-kullu suratin min hadhihi as-suwar as-sa'iratu fi hadhal-haykali bi-taraddudi at-tawliyah wa nafsuha al-jabizatu laha fa huma shay'un wahidun wa sinfun wahidun 'ala hay'at al-jism aydan, la yughadiru saghiratan wa la kabirah` — "and every form among these forms moving in this structure, and its soul that attracts it, the two are one single thing and one single kind upon the form of the body as well, leaving out neither small nor great." The forms in the *haykal* are the forms of the people — the believers, the *hudud*, the gates — and their soul is the sensory soul, the *nafs-e-hissiyah*. Each form moving in the *haykal* and the sensory soul that attracts that form are one and the same thing and one and the same kind, fashioned upon the form of the body; and nothing is left out of this constitution, neither small thing nor great. The luminous body is complete to its least detail, each member a form-and-soul united.

### The Magnification of the Souls and the Paradise of the Haykal

Of this gathered structure the text says: `wa huwa hayyun naatiqun qaadirun 'aalimun masrurun mughbitatun` — "and it is living, speaking, powerful, knowing, joyous, delighted." And then it states the great effect of the gathering: `lakinnahu li-ijtima'i basa'iri ikhwanihi fi dhalika al-majma' ash-sharif, alladhi huwa al-haykal an-nurani, takunu hayatuhu wa quwwatuhu wa qudratuhu wa 'ilmuhu wa hiddatuhu wa dhaka'uhu wa sama'uhu wa basarahu aksaru wa a'zamu wa adkhala wa ashrafu wa ambalu min kawnihi 'ala hiddatihi ad'afan muda'afah li-ijtima'i al-lata'ifi ba'dihim ba'da` — "but, on account of the gathering of the insights of his brethren in that noble assembly, which is the luminous structure, his life, his strength, his power, his knowledge, his sharpness, his intelligence, his hearing, and his sight become more, greater, more profound, more noble, and more exalted than his being in his singularity — many times multiplied — on account of the gathering of the subtle entities one with another."

The meaning is that each soul, considered alone, had a certain measure of glory; even at the *baab-ul-abwaab* its glory was of one kind. But now, gathered into the noble *haykal* of the Imam's son, its life and strength and power and knowledge and sharpness are raised far higher. This is because of the gathering of all the brethren — the *ikhwan* — in that assembly. Through the assembling of the insights, the *basa'ir*, of the brethren, and through the gathering of the subtle entities, the *lata'if*, one with another, each soul's faculties become many times — `ad'afan muda'afah`, multiplied manyfold — greater than they were in singularity. Life, strength, power, knowledge, sharpness, intelligence, hearing, and sight are all magnified by union. What a soul could never attain alone, it attains in the gathering, because the *lata'if* reinforce one another.

The one who makes the spirit flow through all of them is then named: `wa-majra dhalika ar-ruhi fi jami'iha, alladhi huwa nafsu dhalika al-Imam al-mansus 'alayhi` — "and the flowing of that spirit in all of them, which is the soul of that Imam upon whom designation has been made." The one who makes that spirit course through every member of the gathering is the soul of the designated Imam, the *mansus 'alayhi*. The Imam upon whom *nass* has been made becomes the soul and the spirit of the entire *haykal*; he is what animates all the gathered forms and souls. And of their attainment the text says: `bulughuhum tilka ar-rutbat al-'aliyah wal-manzilat al-janniyyat as-samiyah` — "their reaching that high rank and that paradisiacal, lofty station." Their arrival at this high rank and lofty station *is* the paradise — the *jannah* — of this world, the paradise attained within the *haykal*. Heaven, on this teaching, is not only a thing of the hereafter; it is reached here, in the noble luminous assembly, by the souls gathered into the Imam's structure.

### The Total Connection and the Four Years of the Four Ranks

The connection of the divine light with these souls is then declared total: `wa-ttisalu dhalika an-nuri al-ilahi bi-hadhihi an-nufus ittisalan kulliyan` — "and the connection of that divine light with these souls is a total connection." The divine light that is coming joins with these souls in a complete, *kulliyan*, connection; from the soul of the Imam, with the souls of all, the connection becomes total — entire and without remainder.

But the timing of this connection is bound to the four years. The text reads: `wa-innama kana ittisalu hadhihi al-lata'ifi bi-nafsi al-Imam, 'alayhi afdal as-salam, ba'da wafa'i arba'i sinin, li-kawni ad-dini da'amatan lil-qalb` — "and the connection of these subtle entities with the soul of the Imam, upon him be the best of salutations, is only after the completion of four years, because religion is a support for the heart." The connection of these *lata'if* with the soul of the Imam occurs only after four full years have passed, and the reason given is that religion is a support, a *da'amah*, for the heart — `ad-dini da'amatan lil-qalb`.

This four-year requirement is then grounded in the structure of knowledge itself. The text reads: `wa-kana al-mustadillu qa'iman la yusharruhu alladhi huwa al-Imam 'alayhi as-salam, wa la yufaariquhu, laysa bi-khaarijin 'anhu, illa ba'da ma'rifati al-naatiq wal-wasi wal-Imam al-'asr wal-hujjah` — and the meaning drawn from it is that the seeker of proof, the *mustadill*, is not called a *mustadill* except after knowing the *Naatiq* (the Speaker), the *Wasi* (the Legatee), the *Imam al-'Asr* (the Imam of the Age), and the *Hujjah* (the Proof). These are precisely the four things taught first in the religious sciences, the *haqaiq*: we mention the Prophet, we mention Ali, and we teach the name of the Imam of the Age and the Summoner — the *Da'i* — of the time. These four are necessary, and this too is stated to be after four years.

The argument is then made exact: `wa-kullu wahidin min ha'ula'i al-hudud al-arba'ah wa huwa mansubun li-sanatin ba'da ukhra min al-arba'i sinin` — "and each one of these four ranks is attributed to a year, one after another, of the four years." Each of the four ranks — the *hudud al-arba'ah* — is assigned to one of the four years, year following year. From this it follows necessarily: `fa-wajaba an la yakuna ittisalu tilka al-lata'ifi illa ba'da wafa'i arba'i sinin` — "so it became necessary that the connection of those subtle entities not occur except after the completion of four years." Because each of the four ranks corresponds to one year, the connection of the subtle entities cannot occur until all four years are complete; and this span is named the age of upbringing, *sinn al-tarbiyah*.

This is confirmed, the text notes, even by the exotericists. The people of the outward, the *Ahl al-Zahir*, do not pronounce any ruling upon the age of a newborn until at least four years have passed; only after four years do they say what the child's age will be, and they too name this span the years of upbringing — *sinn al-tarbiyah*. So the four-year period is acknowledged on both sides: the people of the inward bind to it the connection of the subtle entities with the Imam's soul, and the people of the outward bind to it their own reckoning of the child — both naming it the age of upbringing.

### Idris and the Origin of the Science of the Stars

The discourse turns to the science of the stars and its origin. It was our prophet Idris, peace upon him, who brought the science of the stars — *'ilm an-nujum* — and his establishing of it was a miracle, a *mu'jizah*, which he brought for the people of his time, the stars being of his time. The reason Idris devised this science was that he lived in a time of precautionary dissimulation and concealment — *taqiyyah* and *sitr*. In an age when the truth could not be openly proclaimed, Idris framed the science of the stars as the vehicle through which the hidden proof might be made known.

The occasion is then narrated. When the Imam of Idris's time passed away — having willed the Imamate to Idris — it was not accepted from him; the people did not receive Idris as the Imam designated by *nass*. So Idris said to the people of the *da'wah* of that Imam: `Hal yakunu fi 'adli Allah Ta'ala an yakhluwa ardahu min hujjatihi?` — "Can it be within the justice of God, exalted is He, that He leaves His earth empty of His proof?" They answered: `laisa dhalika fi 'adli Allah Ta'ala an yakuna dhalik` — "That is not within the justice of God, that such a thing should be." So Idris pressed further: `fa-nuridu an na'lama man hujjatu Allah 'alayna al-aan` — "Then we wish to know who is the proof of God upon us now." And they asked: `wa-kayfa as-sabilu ila dhalik?` — "And what is the way to that?"

Idris then established for them the science of the stars and taught it to them — `wa-wad'a lahum 'ilma an-nujum wa 'allamahum iyyahu`. When they had learned it, he said: `iqsimu al-arda arba'atan arba'an` — "Divide the earth into four quarters" — `fa-anzuru fi ayyi al-arba'ah nizratu Allah Ta'ala 'alaykum` — "and look in which of the quarters is the gaze of God, exalted is He, upon you." They did so, and they found it in the very quarter in which they themselves were: `fa-wajaduhu fir-rub'i alladhi hum fihi`. Then Idris said: `al-aana khaffafa Allahu 'ankum` — "Now God has lightened the burden for you" — `wa-zidu iqsimu hadha ar-rub' alladhi antum fihi arba'an wa-anzuru fi ayyi hadhihi huwa` — "and increase: divide this quarter in which you are into four, and look in which of these it is." They did so, and again `fa-wajaduhu fir-rub'i alladhi huwa fihi` — they found it in the quarter in which they were. Again he said: `zidu iqsimu arba'an` — "Increase, divide into four" — `wa-lam yazalu yaf'aluna dhalik hatta afdat al-qismatu ilayhi` — and they did not cease to do this until the division led at last to Idris himself. Then they said to him: `anta al-aana hujjatullahi fi khalqihi wa shahiduhu 'alayhim` — "You are now the proof of God in His creation and His witness over them." By the repeated quartering of the earth, each time finding God's gaze upon the quarter they were in, the division narrowed until it could rest upon no one but Idris, whom they then acknowledged as the Proof and witness of God. They answered: `na'am, nashhadu annaka la-huwa` — "Yes, we bear witness that you are indeed he." `Fa-'arafu haqqahu wa-iltazamuhu` — so they recognized his right and clung to him. `Fa-kana wad'uhu salamullahi 'alayhi li-dhalika ka-dhalik` — and his establishing of that science, peace of God upon him, was for this purpose.

To this is joined a narration of Amir al-Mu'minin Ali, which illustrates the same theme of the proof discovered by elimination. While Ali was in Kufa, seated upon the pulpit, having declared that any might ask him whatever they wished, a man came and stood and said: "Tell me — where is Jibra'il at this moment?" Ali said, "Let me look in the book of traditions and tell you." He looked here and there, looked toward every direction, and toward the sky, and then said: "You are Jibra'il." The man asked: "How did you recognize me as Jibra'il?" Ali answered that he had looked toward the earth and in all directions, behind and ahead, and had not found Jibra'il anywhere; he had looked toward the sky, and through the seven layers of the earth, and still had not found Jibra'il; and so he understood that Jibra'il could be none other than the questioner himself. Then the man cried out and ascended toward the sky. By searching every place and finding Jibra'il nowhere, Ali concluded that the one before him was Jibra'il — the proof reached, as with Idris, by the exhaustion of every other possibility.

### The Friends of God and the Sciences of Concealment

The chapter draws toward its close with an ascription of praise: `wal-hamdu lillahi alladhi khassa awliya'ahu bi-asrari khilafatihi fi 'ilmi al-Imam` — "and praise be to God, who singled out His friends with the secrets of His vicegerency in the knowledge of the Imam." God specified His friends — His *awliya* — with the secrets of His vicegerency, His *khilafah*, in the knowledge of the Imam, and He made them upholders — *quwwam* — of the gnosis of His oneness, the *ma'arif al-tawhid*, and treasurers — *khuzzan* — of the depths of His wisdom, `khuzzanan li-ghawamidi hikmatihi`. The grammar is noted in passing: *quwwam* is the plural of *qa'im*, the one who stands and upholds, and means one who performs the standing — the upholding — abundantly; and *khuzzanan* is the plural of *khazin*, the treasurer, the keeper of the storehouse, the one who guards and gathers the treasury. The friends of God are thus made the standing upholders of the knowledge of God's oneness and the treasurers who guard the deepest recesses of His wisdom.

A question is then raised about the nature of these sciences of the Imams. The sciences of the Imams are not like ordinary sciences; they operate through symbols — *rumuz* — and indications — *isharat*. They are understood by means of appointed symbols and indications: the one who speaks or writes appoints a symbol, and the one for whom it is intended understands it, while others do not. This is illustrated by the recorded conversations of Sayyidna Dawud, Sayyidna Musa, and Sayyidna Sulayman, in which certain things were written in symbol and indication, understood only by the speaker and the intended hearer, and closed to all others.

This connects to the place of number in the order of things. Just as everything is derived from "La ilaha illa Allah" by way of numbers, so too every thing is related to numbers. Allah created numbers and placed within numbers the arrangement of everything, so that there is nothing whose order is not bound up with number. The derivations of the *haqaiq* from the testimony of oneness through numerical relation are an instance of this universal truth: the arrangement of all things is laid up in number.

Finally, the meaning of the sciences of concealment is made plain. The sciences of *sitr* are those that were manifest in the time of unveiling — the time of *kashf* — but which, since the time of *Baqiyyat al-suwakh*, have been expressed in a hidden, *makhfi*, manner. The very sciences that once stood open in the age of unveiling have, in the age of concealment, been clothed in symbol and number and indication, hidden from the unworthy while remaining accessible to those for whom they are intended. The astronomy of Idris, the numerical sciences, the secrets of the Imam's knowledge — all of these are sciences of *sitr*, manifest once and now veiled.

The chapter closes with blessings: `wa-sallallahu 'ala sayyidina Muhammadin an-nabiyyi wa sayyidi al-wasiyyin wa 'ala aalihim al-ladhina hum a'immat at-tayyibin al-akramin` — "and may God's blessings be upon our master Muhammad the Prophet and master of the legatees, and upon their family, who are the pure and most noble Imams." Upon Muhammad the Prophet, master of the legatees, and upon his family — the *a'immat at-tayyibin al-akramin*, the pure and most noble Imams — the chapter rests its closing prayer.

## The Cycle of Unveiling and the House of Ibrahim

### The Ascent of the Souls and the Forming of the Imam's Progeny

We begin where the previous discourse left off: with the journey of the soul at the moment of death, and the way that journey furnishes the very substance from which the household of the Imam is constituted. The Nafse Namiyah — the growth-soul, the vegetative or growing soul of the believers — does not perish at death. When the believer dies, this Nafse Namiyah departs together with the sensory faculties, the hissiyat, and it travels in the form of rihyat: a subtle, fragrant exhalation, a perfumed essence. From the moon, the qamar, it rises toward the sun, the shams. From the qamar it moves upward to the shams, and from there it passes through the various stars and celestial spheres, the kawakib and the aflak. Having traversed those spheres, it descends — and where it descends is precisely upon those things that fire cannot touch and cannot consume. The image is exact: it settles upon what is beyond the reach of flame, as fire that rests upon water never burns the water. This descended subtlety is the rihyat of the entire world, the perfumed essence belonging to the whole realm of creation.

What becomes of this rihyat once it has descended? The Imam and the angelic household around him — the Imam's malak, his retinue — partake of it; they consume it, they drink it in. It is, in its inner reality, a salawat, a blessing offered up and received back. And from this descended rihyat the Imam's offspring are formed. Their very bodies, the takwin, the bodily formation, are constituted out of the rihyat — the gathered, perfumed essence of the believers who have died and ascended. But the body alone does not make the child of the Imam. The bodies take their shape from rihyat, and their Nafse Namiyah — the growth-soul that animates them — is formed from the most excellent subtleties of the sun, the lataif of the shams. So the child of the Imam is doubly noble: his frame is woven from the ascended essences of the faithful, and his living growth-soul is drawn from the finest subtleties of the solar realm. This is the takwin, the engendering, of the Imam's progeny.

### The Gathering at the Babul Abwab and the Making of the Haykal

When the Imam's child reaches four years of age, a momentous gathering takes place, and through it the very form of the cosmos is assembled into a single human shape. With the upbringing and spiritual training, the tarbiyat, of the Imam's child over those four years, the hudud of all the believers — the entire community of the faithful — undergo a separation: their surat, their form, and their nafs, their soul, are drawn apart from one another. Both, form and soul, separate, and they ascend in proper order until they reach the Babul Abwab, the Gate of Gates. They arrive at the Babul Abwab arranged in rank, each at its station. There their forms, taking the shape of a human being, are held in that human configuration.

This continues until the Imam's child has completed his fourth year. At that point the connection is made. Upon the Imam — through the agency of the Babul Abwab — all the gathered forms and souls come and connect with the nafs of the Imam's child. They join to that nafs. And upon that nafs the Imam's own form exerts its effect. The forms and souls connect with the nafs of the child, while the Imam's own form stands distinct from that nafs, for the Imam's form has become a ruh, a spirit; it now occupies the station, the maqam, of the ruh. Through this conjunction the assembled whole becomes a haykal — a luminous temple, a complete human structure. The haykal is the human form thus composed. And the Babul Abwab, which presided over the gathering, becomes the qalbe haykal, the heart of the temple. Within this constituted human form every rank is preserved at its proper place: just as in the living body the hair, the nails, and each part keep their station, so here whatever rank a believer held is retained at its corresponding station within the cosmic human form. In this manner the form of the entire universe is gathered with the Imam into one complete shape.

It is worth drawing a careful distinction here, for the term haykal is not to be used loosely. With the nutaqa', the Speaker-prophets, a gathering does indeed take place, and a great gathering at that — but no haykal is formed in that case. The method of the nutaqa' is not the same as the method of the Imams. With the Imams, the haykal is a thing of its own order. With the nutaqa', what occurs in their assembly is a gathering of forms, an ijtima' of suwar, and nothing more; the haykal as such is not constituted there. When, then, do we properly speak of a haykal? Only when the forms come to the Babul Abwab. That alone is called a haykal — the assembly of all the forms into the complete human form, the form of the alif, the upright human figure. Consider the image of a great mirror: if you shatter it into pieces, each small fragment remains a mirror, and a form can still be seen within each piece. But the original, unbroken mirror — the one in which the complete human form is reflected — corresponds to what gathers at the Babul Abwab, and it is there, and there alone, that the haykal is described. With the nutaqa', then, the people of their eras gather as a gathering of forms, but the form of the haykal is not present; theirs is a gathering, an assembly, but not a haykal. The word haykal has been reserved deliberately for the place where the human form — the form of the alif — is established, which is the Babul Abwab.

### The Grand Gathering at the Coming of the Qaimul Qiyam

What unfolds with each Imam unfolds on an immeasurably vaster scale when the Qaimul Qiyam comes — the Imam of the Imams, the Resurrector of resurrections. With him too a gathering of the same order occurs, but magnified beyond reckoning. There as well a human form is composed, and into that form are gathered all the believers — all who have passed away across the ages are present in it. In this way are formed qawaim of qawaim, resurrectors upon resurrectors, oceans upon oceans: a gathering so great and so grand that it embraces the whole succession of the faithful from the beginning. This is the supreme assembly toward which the entire economy of unveiling has been tending. Bismillah. Allahumma salli ala Sayyidina Muhammadin wa ala aali Sayyidina Muhammad wa sallim — O Allah, send blessings upon our master Muhammad and upon the family of our master Muhammad and grant them peace.

### The Extraction of Each Imam and the Perpetual Da'wat

We now take up the text itself: `Wa istikhraju kulli imamin min da'wati abih li-anna kulla imamin yuqimu da'watahu wa ya'malu fiha ila an yujad min da'watihi man yakhlufuhu fi makanihi ala hadhal-minhaj abadan sarmadan fi Daur al-Kashf wa Daur as-Satr` — "And the extraction of every Imam from the da'wat of his father, because every Imam establishes his da'wat and works within it until there is brought forth from his da'wat one who will succeed him in his place, upon this same path, forever and perpetually, in the Daur al-Kashf and the Daur as-Satr."

The word istikhraj means "to draw out," "to extract"; some have rendered it as akhraja, "he brought forth." The teaching it carries is this: every Imam is drawn out from the da'wat — the summons, the spiritual mission — of his father. This is how it comes to pass. Every Imam establishes his own da'wat and labors within it, working in it and acting upon it, until from that very da'wat there is brought forth the one who will be his successor and stand in his place. And so the process continues, without end and without interruption, forever and always, in the same manner, throughout both the Daur al-Kashf, the Era of Unveiling, and the Daur as-Satr, the Era of Concealment. This extraction of successor from predecessor is the unbroken thread that runs through both great cycles of sacred history; it never ceases.

### The Daur al-Kashf: The Open Reading of the Realities

The text continues: `Wa fi Daur al-Kashf yazharu fihil-ulumul-batina al-haqiqa tuqra' fil-mahafil wa ala ru'usil-manabir wa la 'amala fihi bi-shay'in minal-awda'iz-zahirah` — "And in the Daur al-Kashf there appear within it the esoteric sciences, the realities; they are read aloud in the gatherings and from the heads of the pulpits, and there is no acting within it upon anything of the apparent ordinances."

In the Era of Unveiling, then, the inner sciences come into the open. The batin, the esoteric, and the haqa'iq, the realities or true sciences, manifest plainly. These are read aloud in the assemblies and proclaimed from the pulpits, openly and without concealment. And in that era there is no enforcement of the zahir, the outward ordinances; none of the apparent ritual prescriptions is performed as binding law. The realities themselves take the place that the outward forms held in the era of concealment. The text has already indicated which things will be abrogated in such a time and which will remain.

Here a subtle but indispensable principle must be insisted upon: without Nasut there can be no manifestation of Lahut. The divine realm of Lahut cannot appear, cannot manifest itself, except through the human realm of Nasut. There is no Lahut made manifest without a Nasut to bear it. And this brings us to the Nasut-e-tabi'i, the "natural" or "constitutive" Nasut, whose mention now arises and whose characteristics have been explained. The Nasut-e-tabi'i is that order in which interpersonal relationships subsist — relationships among persons, this connection and that — yet in which the five senses are not present. There the senses do not operate, but relationships nonetheless exist. The khuddam, the servants of the divine economy, and the Imam himself stand in such relationships; and for the relationships of the khuddam and of the Imam to be manifested, the manifestation of the Nasut-e-tabi'i is necessary. Without this constitutive Nasut, in which relation persists though sensation is absent, the whole web of ranks and offices could not be displayed at all. These matters have already been set out in detail.

### The Piety of the People of Unveiling

We arrive at a remarkable claim concerning the people of the Daur al-Kashf, and it is worth dwelling upon, for it overturns any suspicion that the lifting of obligatory law signifies a slackening of devotion. The text moves to the Daur as-Satr — `Wa Daur as-Satr, ad-da'watu fihi ala darbayn`, "And as for the Daur as-Satr, the da'wat within it is of two kinds" — but before passing on, the teacher pauses on the people of unveiling. Although the injunctions of the Shari'ah are not enforced in the Daur al-Kashf, the people of that era are so deeply pious that the supererogatory acts they perform of their own free will — the tatawwu'at — exceed our obligatory duties, our fara'id. Of their own accord, though nothing of it is obligatory upon them, they multiply voluntary worship so abundantly that it surpasses what is incumbent upon us.

Consider the particulars. They offer the prayer of thanksgiving, the shukranah, voluntarily. They fast — and whereas our obligatory fasting is confined to the single month of Ramadan, they keep supererogatory fasts beyond all obligation, and with these they worship. As for zakat: the obligatory system of zakat that governs us will not be in force among them; yet they will give so abundantly in charity, in sadaqat, that their giving far surpasses ours. This is the very quality of their station. And all the disciplines of cleanliness and purity they preserve in full measure, equally and without diminishment. The practice of circumcision, the khatna, they maintain — indeed it is said of some among them that the foreskin removed in circumcision does not even appear upon them, so refined is their constitution. The point stands clearly: the absence of compulsory law in the Era of Unveiling does not lower the people of that age beneath us; on the contrary, their freely chosen devotion towers above our prescribed minimum.

### The Two Da'wats of the Era of Concealment

Now we may take up the Daur as-Satr in earnest. In the Era of Concealment the da'wat is of two kinds: one is an esoteric da'wat that is hidden — da'watun batinatun khafiyyah — and one is a manifest da'wat that is clear — da'watun zahiratun jaliyyah. The text defines the first: `Fad-da'watul-batinatul-khafiyyah qismul-maqamatil-ilahiyyah wal-hayakilin-nuraniyyah wa abwabihim wa hujajihim wa du'atihim wa ma'dhunihim` — "So the esoteric, hidden da'wat is the division of the divine stations, and the luminous structures, and their gates, and their proofs, and their summoners, and their authorized ones."

The hidden, esoteric da'wat, then, is the whole ordered division of the maqamat ilahiyyah, the divine stations, together with the hayakil nuraniyyah, the luminous structures, and their abwab, their gates; their hujaj, their proofs; their du'at, their summoners; and their ma'dhun, those granted licence to call. This is the inner hierarchy of the spiritual world, ranged in its concealed order — the architecture of divine offices that subsists behind the veil throughout the era of concealment.

### Concealment in the Era of Adam: Qabil and Habil

The text then turns to the condition of this hidden da'wat in the earliest era: `Wa kanu fi Daur Adam, salamullahi alayh, fi ghayatin minal-istitari wat-taghatti khawfal-addad wal-fara'inata wa ahlil-'inad li-qillatil-munasabati baynahum wa bu'dihim 'anhum` — "And they were, in the era of Adam, peace of Allah be upon him, at the extreme of concealment and covering, out of fear of the adversaries and the pharaohs and the people of obstinacy, on account of the scarcity of affinity between them and their distance from them."

In the era of Adam, peace be upon him, the people of the esoteric and hidden da'wat were in the utmost degree of istitar — concealment — and taghatti — covering, the drawing of a veil over themselves. These were the people of the era of Imam Hunaid, peace be upon him, and his followers; they reached the very limit of concealment, withdrawn behind the curtain of fear. And the cause of this extreme concealment was fear of opponents — the addad, the adversaries; the fara'in, the pharaohs and tyrants; and the ahl al-'inad, the people of stubborn opposition. Behind this lies a narrative: the progeny of Qabil gained the upper hand and seized dominance, while the progeny of Habil went into concealment. And along with the descendants of Habil, the followers of Imam Hunaid likewise withdrew into hiding. All of this came about because of the scarcity of munasabah — the lack of affinity — between them and the world around them, and because of the great distance that separated them. The distance is a distance of stations: the gulf between the lofty rank of these hidden ones and the world that surrounded them was so wide, and the affinity so slight, that nothing remained for them but to vanish from view.

### The Manifest Summoners and Their Outward Call

The manifest da'wat operates on an opposite principle, and the text describes it: `Ammad-da'watuj-jaliyyah, fa-hum qismu du'atihimuz-zahirah fa-hum mutazahiruna bi-dhalika da'una ilayh... wa dhalika li-qurbil-munasabati baynahum wa bayna sa'iril-'alam` — "As for the clear da'wat, they are the division of their manifest summoners, and they are manifest through it, calling toward it... and that is on account of the closeness of affinity between them and the rest of the world."

The clear and open da'wat is the division of the manifest du'at, the summoners who operate in the open. They make themselves manifest through this da'wat; by it they are named and by it they are known; and they call toward it — da'una ilayh — summoning to the Imam of the time. And the reason they can act openly is precisely the inverse of what drove the others into hiding: there is a closeness of affinity, a qurb al-munasabah, between them and the rest of the world. Because their affinity with the surrounding world is near, they can stand forth in the open and call.

And what is the purpose of their open calling? `Fa-hum yastakhrijuna man anasu rushduhum min ahlid-da'watiz-zahirah ila da'wati mawalihimul-batinah` — "So they extract those in whom they sense sound guidance, from the people of the manifest da'wat, toward the esoteric da'wat of their masters." The manifest du'at draw out, from among the people of the open da'wat, those in whom they perceive rushd, sound and well-directed guidance — those with whom they find a true affinity in their right-mindedness. And they extract such persons and direct them onward toward the hidden, esoteric da'wat of their mawali, their masters. The manifest summoners thus serve as a bridge: standing where affinity with the world is near, they gather the well-guided and convey them inward, toward the concealed da'wat of the masters that the world at large could never reach.

Here a luminous symmetry comes into view, and it is the key to the whole structure. From the text it appears that in the Daur as-Satr the affinity among the momineen — the believers among themselves — is greater, while their affinity with the rest of the world is what governs the work of the manifest du'at. The manifest summoners possess affinity with the realm of the world, and that nearness is what allows them to call openly. The people of the esoteric, by contrast, have no such affinity with the world below; their affinity does not lie here. Theirs is the qillat al-munasabah, the scarcity of affinity, that holds them apart and hidden. The relationship with the Imam does not persist for the common people; they fall away, and what remains is the relationship of the few. In the Daur al-Kashf this inner relationship comes into the open and is manifested; in the Daur as-Satr it must be carried inward, soul by soul, through the bridge of the manifest du'at.

### The Du'at as Instruments of the Imams

The text now describes the inner disposition of these summoners: `Li-annahum li-amrihim mu'tamirun wa 'anhu akhizun wa li-qawlihim qa'ilun wa bi-'uluwwi maqamatihim 'arifun 'alimun wa hum abadan 'ala dhalika fi jami'il-adwar mustamirrun` — "Because they act under their command, and take from them, and speak by their word, and are knowing and aware of the loftiness of their stations; and they are forever, in all the eras, continuing upon this."

The du'at, that is to say, are entirely instruments of the Imams. They act under the command, the amr, of the Imams — having received their orders from them. From the Imams they take the responsibility for every task — 'anhu akhizun — receiving their charge from them. By the word of the Imams they speak — li-qawlihim qa'ilun — uttering nothing of their own but only what their masters give them to utter. And they are knowing and aware of the loftiness of the Imams' stations, recognizing and acknowledging the exalted rank of those they serve. And so they remain, forever, throughout all the eras, the adwar, continuing in this same way without deviation, passing on in this single posture of obedience from age to age.

This complete self-effacement of the summoner before the Imam was given perfect expression by Sayyidna Ali — cited here as Sayyidna Ali bin al-Husayn, al-Jamali — who declared in a khutbah, a sermon, delivered when he came to visit: "I am nothing but like a pen in the hand of the Imam." He is, he says, as a pen in the hand of a scribe — moving only as the writer moves it, forming only the letters the writer intends, possessing of itself no motion and no meaning. So the du'at stand in the hand of the Imams: the qalam in the grasp of the katib.

### The Succession of the Natiqs: From Adam to Nuh to Ibrahim

We now follow the chain of the Speaker-prophets, the nutaqa', as one era completes and another begins. `Wa kadhalika lamma tamma Daur Adam 'alayhis-salam bi-akhiri mutimmihi, qama ba'dahu Nuh 'alayhis-salam natiqan wa wasiyyuhu, thumma a'immatu dawrihi 'alayhis-salam wa du'atuhum bid-da'wataynil-zahirah wal-batinah` — "And likewise, when the era of Adam, peace be upon him, was completed by its last completer, Nuh, peace be upon him, arose after him as a Natiq, and his wasi, then the Imams of his era, peace be upon him, and their summoners with the two da'wats, the manifest and the esoteric."

When the era of Adam, peace be upon him, came to its completion through its last completer, its akhir mutimm, then Nuh, peace be upon him, arose after him as a Natiq, a Speaker. After Nuh, his wasi, his successor, arose; and the wasi of Sayyidna Nuh is Sam, Sayyidna Sam, peace be upon him. Then came the Imams of Nuh's era, and their du'at carrying both da'wats, the manifest and the esoteric, just as in every era. `Ila an tamma Daur Nuh 'alayhis-salam bi-akhiri mutimmihi` — "until the era of Nuh, peace be upon him, was completed by its last completer." And when the era of Nuh too reached its completion through its last completer, `Wa qama ba'dahu Ibrahim 'alayhis-salam natiqan` — "and after him arose Ibrahim, peace be upon him, as a Natiq." So after Nuh, Ibrahim, peace be upon him, arose as a Natiq.

### Ibrahim the Confluence of the Two Preceding Eras

Now we reach the distinguishing mark of Ibrahim, peace be upon him, which sets him apart from the two Natiqs who came before him. `Wa kana majma'an li-suwari ahlid-dawraynis-sabiqayn 'alayh alladhina huma Dawr Adam wa Nuh 'alayhimas-salam` — "And he was a confluence of the forms of the people of the two preceding eras, which are the era of Adam and the era of Nuh, peace be upon them both."

Ibrahim, peace be upon him, was a majma' — a confluence, a place of gathering and convergence — of the suwar, the forms, of the people of the two eras that had gone before him: the era of Adam and the era of Nuh. He was their confluence; in him the forms of both prior eras flowed together and met. (A point of method is worth noting on the word majma': although one might be tempted to derive it from the root jama'a in some other inflection, the rule is that where a clear and simple meaning is available, one should not chase after meanings drawn from secondary derivatives — that is a special principle of sound interpretation, and the plain sense of majma', "confluence," is the meaning intended.)

This is precisely what distinguishes Ibrahim from Adam and from Nuh. Concerning Adam and Nuh, the quality of being a majma', a confluence, is not predicated. They were not themselves the confluence; rather, as the text explains, `Li-annahuma kana da'iyayn wa-stahaqqa martabatan-natiq wa kana Ibrahim bi-khilafihima li-annahu-jtama'a 'indahu suwaru jami'i ahlid-dawrayn wa huwa haykalun nuraniyyun` — "because the two of them were summoners and each deserved the rank of Natiq; and Ibrahim was contrary to them, because there were gathered with him the forms of all the people of the two eras, and he is a luminous structure." Both Adam and Nuh were da'i — they were callers, summoners, toward that confluence which Ibrahim would be; and by virtue of their office each deserved and held the rank of Natiq. But Ibrahim stands in contrast to them both: with him were gathered the forms of all the people of the two eras, and he himself is a haykal nurani, a luminous structure. Adam and Nuh summoned toward the gathering; Ibrahim was the gathering. They were da'i for the confluence; he was the confluence itself.

### The Changing of the Shari'ah and the Constancy of Worship

A question presses itself here, and it must be answered, for it touches the deepest puzzle of sacred law. When a Natiq abrogates his Shari'ah and a new one supersedes it — from the standpoint of the realities, the haqa'iq, what is actually happening? In the visible world one law is repealed and another is enacted; all manner of changes occur. Why should this be?

The answer is that in each such era the emergence of the fudala' — the excellent ones, the superior substances and souls that come forth in that age — makes it necessary that some laws of the Shari'ah be altered. The things that are manifesting from below in a given era manifest in a particular, special manner proper to that era, as has been explained in Zahr al-Ma'ani. An analogy makes the principle plain. Every physician, when he comes, prescribes his own remedy. One goes to a doctor and he gives a certain medicine; though the illness be the same, when one goes to another doctor he will make some changes or offer his own new medicine. The disease is one; the prescriptions differ with the practitioner and the moment. So too with the Shari'ah: the underlying condition is constant, but the prescriptions are adjusted to the age and to the fudala' who have emerged within it.

A crucial line must be drawn, however, between two domains. The changes belong to the sphere of halal and haram — what may be eaten and what may not, what may be done and what may not; the things of the past are abrogated and new things established in their place. But the worship of Allah and the commands of Allah remain everywhere in their place; these cannot be changed. The ground of this is the Quranic word: `Innad-dina 'indallahil-Islam` — "Indeed, the religion in the sight of Allah is Islam." That is the religion, fixed and unchangeable. Yet within it, on account of the emergence of certain fudala', some adjustments must be made, and these adjustments are made through the Shari'ah. In each era, the fudala' that have emerged for that Natiq necessitate that some changes be made in the ordinances of the Shari'ah; and so the changes are made — without ever touching the immovable core, which is the worship and the command of Allah, the din that in His sight is Islam.

### The Three Ranks Gathered in Ibrahim

The text now situates Ibrahim with respect to the two who preceded him in terms of the three great offices of revelation, interpretation, and reality. `Fa-Adam yaqumu maqamat-tanzil wa Nuh yaqumu muqabilat-ta'wil wa Ibrahim muqabilul-haqa'iq wa li-dhalika qala fihil-kitabul-karim, wa Ibrahimalladhi waffa` — "So Adam stands in the station of tanzil, and Nuh stands opposite ta'wil, and Ibrahim opposite the haqa'iq; and for this reason the Noble Book said concerning him, 'And of Ibrahim, who fulfilled.'"

Adam, peace be upon him, stands in the station of tanzil, of revelation in its outward descent. Nuh stands opposite ta'wil, in the place of interpretation, for the sake of ta'wil. And Ibrahim stands opposite the haqa'iq, the realities. And it is for this reason that the Noble Book declared of him, `wa Ibrahimalladhi waffa` — "and Ibrahim, who fulfilled." He fulfilled — meaning that he attained all three of these ranks at once: the rank of tanzil, the rank of ta'wil, and the rank of haqa'iq. The text glosses the verse: `ishratan ila hadhihith-thalathir-rutab allati huwa hiya wa huwa maqamun karim, dhul-haykalin-nuraniyyil-'azim` — this is "a pointing toward these three ranks, of which he is the very possessor; and he is a noble station, the possessor of the grand luminous structure." The verse points toward these three ranks, and Ibrahim is the one who possesses them all; his is a noble station, maqamun karim, and he is the bearer of the grand and luminous haykal, the haykal nurani al-'azim. Because the forms of the two eras were gathered in him and he is a luminous haykal raised above all the stations, he fulfilled — and the Book named him so.

### Ibrahim the Son of Salih, in Whom Four Ranks Convened

The text proceeds to Ibrahim's lineage and the convergence of offices within him. `Wa huwa waladul-imamis-sabiqi 'alayh al-mujidu al-mushiru ilayh, fa-jtama'at 'indahun-nubuwwah war-risalah wal-wasayah wal-imamah` — "And he is the son of the preceding Imam, the one who brought him into being and the one who pointed toward him; so there were gathered with him prophethood, messengership, successorship, and imamate."

Ibrahim is the son of the preceding Imam — and that Imam is Salih. Ibrahim is the son of Salih: the one who brought him into being, al-mujid, and the one who pointed toward him and designated him, al-mushir ilayh. And in Ibrahim there were gathered four ranks together: nubuwwah, prophethood; risalah, messengership; wasayah, successorship; and imamah, imamate. These four offices, which in others are dispersed, convened all at once in the single luminous person of Ibrahim. He is at once the prophet, the messenger, the wasi, and the Imam — the confluence of the eras made also the confluence of the offices.

### The Daur al-Fitrah and the Reckoning of the Eras

We must now situate all of this within the larger architecture of the ages, and clarify a point about the Daur al-Fitrah, the Era of Nature, which is easily misunderstood. Adam belongs to the Daur al-Fitrah, the era of nature. In that era two kinds of people emerged. One kind were the bearers of the sciences of the realities, the haqa'iq — upon which everything is founded. The other kind were the bearers of the apparent sciences, the zahir, and toward those outward sciences they inclined. The bearers of the true and real knowledge — those who were the priests in the highest sense, the carriers of the genuine sciences — had their gathering with Imam Hunaid, peace be upon him; while Sayyidna Adam, peace be upon him, had the gathering of the people of the apparent. Adam's assembly was the assembly of the zahir; Imam Hunaid's was the assembly of the haqa'iq.

Now a careful correction must be registered concerning the placement of these three thousand years, for a misconception had taken root. It had been supposed that the three thousand years of the Daur al-Fitrah stand separate from and additional to the Daur al-Kashf. The conception current in some quarters — and it had lodged itself in the minds in India — was that there are fifty thousand years of the Daur al-Kashf, three thousand years of the Daur al-Fitrah on top of them, and seven thousand years of the Daur as-Satr. But this is not correct. When the matter was examined book by book, the truth emerged otherwise: the last three thousand years within the fifty thousand years of the Daur al-Kashf are themselves the Daur al-Fitrah. They are not a separate addition but the closing portion of the Daur al-Kashf. In those last three thousand years two kinds of qualities manifested: some inclined toward the knowledge of reality, and some toward the knowledge of the apparent. Those final three thousand years are what is called the Daur al-Fitrah.

The reckoning confirms it, and the count must come out exactly. The total is fifty thousand plus seven thousand, which is fifty-seven thousand years. This figure is then multiplied by seven: seven times fifty-seven thousand yields three hundred and ninety-nine thousand years, and with a further thousand the round figure of four hundred thousand years is completed. In this way the four hundred thousand years are fully made up. Were one instead to count the three thousand years of the Daur al-Fitrah as a separate addition, the reckoning would be thrown off and would land somewhere else entirely, with a great discrepancy. So the Daur al-Fitrah must be understood as the last three thousand years of the fifty thousand of the Daur al-Kashf, and the present age is the last part of this first great dawr of four hundred thousand years.

Within that Daur al-Fitrah, the three-thousand-year era of priesthood and astrology stood at its very peak, and the era of sorcery as well. The priesthood, the reading of the stars, and the magical arts flourished to their fullest extent during those three thousand years. And it was Adam, peace be upon him, who began the Daur as-Satr, the Era of Concealment, after the Daur al-Fitrah. Adam is the one who inaugurated the Daur as-Satr. Nor was the Daur al-Fitrah void of prophets: prophets were present within it. During the long span of the Daur al-Kashf proper — the forty-seven thousand years before the final three thousand — there were only those who held to the sciences of the realities, and two factions formed. After those forty-seven thousand years were completed, in the closing three thousand years, two distinct types of people emerged: one type bore the earlier thought of the Daur al-Kashf, the sciences of reality; and the other gave precedence to the developing science of priesthood and the apparent sciences. As their intentions inclined, so their actions followed. Thus the parting of the two ways took place in those final years.

### Imam Hunaid, the Qaim of the Daur al-Kashf and the Highest Veil

A natural question arises: who is the Qaim of the Daur al-Kashf — the Resurrector who presides over the Era of Unveiling, the Qaim of the full fifty thousand years? The one who gathered the people of the Daur al-Kashf, those who bore the realities and the forms, was Imam Hunaid, peace be upon him. And when the question was pressed — where, then, is the station of the Qaim of the Daur al-Kashf, once the three thousand years whose bearers were the people of realities and forms had run their course — the answer given was this: he became the Hijab-e-A'la, the Highest Veil. Imam Hunaid, the Qaim of the Daur al-Kashf, became the Hijab-e-A'la; and one may take out the books and verify it there. He is the Highest Veil. The gatherer of the Daur al-Kashf was Imam Hunaid; the gatherer of the Daur as-Satr was Sayyidna Adam. The last people of the Daur al-Kashf were those who went forward into the Daur as-Satr; and among them, it was the people of the apparent sciences who carried over and advanced into the era of concealment.

### The Ten Ranks and the Station of Divine Speech

To grasp where the nutaqa' stand, we must lay out the ladder of ranks, for the rank of divine speech lies above even the imamate, and this is easily overlooked. The nutaqa' under discussion are not masters of Lahut and Nasut in the manner of the Imams; rather they are bearers of nutq-e-ilahi, divine speech, and the rank of divine speech stands above the imamate. The very word "imamate" does not reach so high. Let us see the ranks in order. There are ten ranks, and they ascend thus. The first, taken at the 'Ahd-e-Sharif, the noble covenant, is the Mustajib, the responder. Then the Momin, the believer. Then the Mukasir. Then the Ma'dhun, the licensed one — first mukasir, then ma'dhun. Then the Da'i, the summoner. Then the Hujjat, the proof. Then the Bab, the gate. And the eighth rank is that of the Imam, peace be upon him.

The rank of the Imam, the eighth, is described in terms of the Nasut-e-tabi'i: the Imam is the one possessed of Nasut-e-tabi'i who has attained the haykal through the Babul Abwab. When the Imam has received the imamate — when, that is, the bearer of Nasut-e-tabi'i has attained the haykal by way of the Babul Abwab — this rank is, in a sense, completed, and his further ranks now begin. For above the imamate there are higher offices still. After imamate comes wasayat, successorship; and above that is nutq-e-ilahi, divine speech. The ranks rise very high. When the imamate has its haykal, then divine speech must have its station above it; the gatherings of the Imams possess divine speech.

And who are the bearers of this divine speech? They are the three Ha's — three figures whose names begin with the letter Ha: Hunaid, Ibrahim, and Hashim. Sayyidna Imam Hunaid, peace be upon him; Sayyidna Ibrahim, peace be upon him; and Sayyidna Hashim. These three Ha's are the bearers of the nutq-e-ilahi. And the Prophet, peace be upon him, stands in the second rank in this regard. This compels a vital distinction: one must differentiate between divine speech, nutq-e-ilahi, and the Natiq as such. There is a Natiq, and there is a divine Natiq, and there is a difference between the two. Ibrahim belongs to the higher order: because the forms of the two eras were gathered in him, and because he is a luminous haykal raised above all the stations, he stands among the bearers of divine speech — the three Ha's — and is more than a Natiq merely; he is a divine Natiq.

### The Creation of the Intellects and the Three Gifts

Now we descend to the very root of the matter — the beginning, the mabda', which is the special opening subject of Mabda' wa Ma'ad. Allah Most High is the Creator, and from non-existence He brought things into existence. What did He first create? Not forms. He created the intellects, the uqul; the luminous specters, the ashbah nuraniyah; and the essences, the zawat. The word used here is zawat, essences — and ashbah nuraniyah, luminous specters; and uqul, intellects. The word nafs, soul, is not yet used at this stage at all. Intellects, luminous specters, essences — these were what He created.

And what did He then do? He bestowed three things upon these created intellects. First, He gave them life — hayat — that is, existence itself. Then, the knowledge, the ilm, of what powers reside within that life: first existence, then the knowledge of the faculties contained in it. Then, third, free will — ikhtiyar — the power of choice, the power to decide whether or not to make use of those faculties. These three things — life, knowledge, and free will — no one can take away. They are a gift from God: neither does the Creator take them back, nor can anyone else strip them away. They are present in every human being. It may be that for a time they lie dormant, unmanifested — that is a separate matter — but these three are within every person without exception: life, knowledge, and the freedom to use them or not.

### The Tenth Intellect, Its Delay, and Its Return

What happened next divides all of subsequent reality. Once endowed with free will, some of the intellects made use of it; and when they used it, they ascended and departed. But some intellects delayed in the use of their free will; they did not employ it. And who was the one who delayed? The tenth — the ashir. In the tenth intellect there arose a corrupt imagination, a wahm-e-fasid, and on account of it he delayed. By delaying, he underwent a transformation: he changed into the form of a nafs, a soul. From intellect he descended into soul. This is anchored in the Quranic address: `Ya ayyatuhan-nafsul-mutma'innah... irji'i` — "O tranquil soul... return." He changed from the form of intellect into the form of a nafs — a descent, a falling, a passage downward from one condition to another. In the realm of origination, the 'alam-e-ibda', no other attained this condition; the rest did not take on the form of a nafs at all. Indeed, the word nufus, souls, is not used for the others. From where, then, did the word nafs first come? From the ashir, the tenth — for it was he who, after his delay and his corrupt imagination, left the form of an intellect.

But the descent was not his final state. When the ashir later left the form of a nafs and re-entered the intellect, he became the 'Aql-e-Kulli, the Universal Intellect — the luminous intellect. He entered the realm and attained perfection: his own perfection, a perfection in which there is no flaw, after which there is no further descent and no further fall. That is finished. The word nafs, and the plural nufus, thus originated solely from the tenth intellect, the ashir. After this, the many forms of the nafs arise: one accepted the call late, another very late, another very late indeed, and according to the degree of their delay the various forms of the nafs were brought into being.

### The Nafse Kulli and the Souls of the Imams

Among these forms of soul, one is the Nafse Kulli, and the Nafse Kulli is the ashir himself — the Nafse Mutma'innah, the tranquil soul. As soon as the ashir realized his error, he immediately turned and changed; he is therefore called the Nafse Kulli, the Nafse Mutma'innah. And in like manner the souls of the Imams are also called Nufuse Kulli. The reason is this: when the call of the ashir went forth in that realm — and with the ashir were his deputies, so that the call of the two, the call of the qawaim, was raised — the souls of the Imams immediately accepted it. Because they answered at once, without delay, a station was established for them there, in the realm of life, the alam-e-hayat. Having gained a station in the realm of life through their immediate acceptance, they too are called Nafse Kulli.

### The Centers of the Three Souls and the Realm Without Dimension

From the Nafse Kulli a further unfolding proceeds, and the three souls must be located in their proper centers. From the Nafse Kulli there comes a soul, the Nafse Hissiyah, the sensory soul — but in the realm of life there are no dimensions; the Nafse Hissiyah does not exist there as such. The dimensions come afterward. It is here, in the dimensional world, that the Nafse Hissiyah is born — and it is born from the sun. For those who are under the sun, the term Nafse Hissiyah is applied; the center of the Nafse Hissiyah is the sun. The center of the Nafse Namiyah is the moon: the moon is the center of the growth-soul. And the Nafse Kulli — where is it? The Nafse Kulli is wholly in the realm of life, which is not in dimensions at all. The sun came into dimensions, entered the dimensional order; but the realm of life is dimensionless. So the Nafse Kulli is entirely in the realm of life: it encompasses all, and its flow and its effects reach down into the dimensional world here below. The Nufuse Kulli are all in the realm of life; here in the dimensional world are the Nufuse Hissiyah, the sensory souls; and below the moon are the Nufuse Namiyah, the growth-souls. So the three are ranged: the Kulli in the dimensionless realm of life, encompassing all and pouring its effects downward; the Hissiyah centered in the sun; the Namiyah centered in the moon.

### The Descent into Body and the Distances Within the One

When the nafs changes its form yet further and descends still more, falling lower, then the body, the jism, is born. And what was the body before? It was a nafs. And what was the nafs before? It was an intellect. The chain of descent has traveled a long way: intellect became soul, and soul became body. Consider the body as it now is, and how vast are the distances within this single thing. Your heart is a physical body; your feet are physical; the urinary and excretory tracts are physical too — all of them parts of the one body. Yet what an immense difference there is among them. On one side is the heart, on another the liver, on another this organ and that. In one and the same body all these things are present, and yet they are separated by an enormous gulf in dignity and station. Just so is the difference between the Nufuse Hissiyah and the Nufuse Kulli: as the parts of one body differ vastly though they belong to one frame, so the sensory souls and the universal souls differ vastly though all derive from the one descent.

### The Nafse Natiqa: The Believer's Own Acquisition

The Nafse Natiqa, the speaking or rational soul, also came from the ashir, but it must be understood with particular care, for it is the believer's own acquisition. The Nafse Natiqa is the spiritual form of knowledge and action. The essence, the zat, that you were given acquired knowledge and action — and this acquired form of knowledge and action is what is called the Nafse Natiqa. Here is the heart of it: the form of knowledge and action — who made this form? Your own essence made it. And whose creation is the essence? It is Allah's creation. But whose creation is the form? It is yours. And so one may say a remarkable thing: the thing made by us has surpassed the thing given to us by Allah. This is no impiety — it is precisely the point of the gift of free will. Allah gave you free will for exactly this reason: that you should create something by your own effort, and that the thing you create by your own effort should be, for you, the real thing, your genuine possession. Who gave the free will? Allah, the Creator. Who gave the underlying life, knowledge, and capacity for action? Allah, the Creator. But the form of knowledge and action raised upon that foundation is your own work, and it is yours.

And the diversity of bodies into which the descent issues is itself an ocean without measure: there is the human body, then the animal body, then those that lack vegetative growth, the nafs-e-numu, and on through countless other kinds of bodies — an ocean, beyond counting.

### The Non-Believer as a Fallen Form

From the order thus laid out, one may draw a sobering inference concerning the non-believer, the ghair-e-momin. A non-believer is a fallen physical form of a believer's body. He is one who did not attain the Nafse Natiqa. He possesses a soul — a nafs he has — but he does not possess a Nafse Natiqa. The meaning of Nafse Natiqa is the form of knowledge and action that is established through the 'Ahd-e-Sharif, the noble covenant, whose very starting-point is the 'Ahd-e-Sharif. That form is not present in the non-believer; lacking it, he is a non-believer — a fallen physical form, possessed of soul but bereft of the speaking soul that the covenant alone establishes.

### The Essence as Pure Intellect and the Reverse Order of Return

We may now answer a question that gathers the whole teaching together: what, in the end, is the essence — is it intellect, soul, or body? It is all intellect. The soul and the body are nothing but changed forms of the intellect. And from this it follows that the return journey reverses the order of descent. Just as the descent fell in sequence — the intellect came into being, then became soul, then became body — so the ascent must climb in the same sequence reversed. Because the body came last in the descent, the body goes first in the return; then, after the body, the soul departs; and so the ascent proceeds, unwinding the descent in reverse. The one who obtained his station first, who went up first, is the hero, the foremost — the one who departed first. He is the one who made the arrangement first and then left this realm; after him the others remained. There is a difference of precedence and succession among them, and nothing more than that: the one who set the order in place departed first, leaving this realm, and the rest followed in their turn.

### The Khameer-e-Nizamat and the Seed Planted a Thousand Years Before

This same principle of precedence and succession illuminates the meaning of the 'Ahd-e-Sharif and the khameer-e-nizamat, the leaven of the spiritual order. At the time of the 'Ahd-e-Sharif one receives the Nafse Natiqa — and this is, in truth, the receiving of one's own form. The khameer-e-nizamat that you yourself performed before is the very thing obtained by you at the moment of the 'Ahd-e-Sharif. The body of yours that appeared a thousand years ago planted a seed at that time; the one who came before has gone, has departed, but in departing he planted a seed — and you are now the one obtaining that very seed. At the noble covenant, the predecessor's leaven, sown a thousand years earlier, is at last gathered in by the successor who stands in his place. The seed planted by the one who left is harvested by the one who comes. This is the inner workmanship of the 'Ahd-e-Sharif. Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim. Allahumma salli ala Sayyidina Muhammad.

### The Four Ranks Convened in Ibrahim and the Bringing Forth of Isma'il

We resume with the text that opens the next chapter: `Al-fasl al-thalith min al-bab al-awwal min al-suradiq al-thalith. Fa-lamma ijtama'at al-arba' al-rutab fi Ibrahim, wa kana 'ala al-halat al-madhkurah min al-fadl wa al-sharaf, aqama da'watahu ila an istakhraja minha waladahu Isma'il 'alayhim al-salam. Wa qad kana wulida lahu Ishaq qabla mawlid Isma'il 'alayhima al-salam` — "And when the four ranks were gathered in Ibrahim, and he was in the aforementioned state of virtue and honor, he established his da'wat until he brought forth from it his son Isma'il, peace be upon them. And Ishaq had been born to him before the birth of Isma'il, peace be upon them both."

When the four ranks — nubuwwah, risalah, wasayah, and imamah — were gathered in Ibrahim, and he stood in that mentioned state of virtue and honor, of fadl and sharaf, he established his da'wat and labored within it until he extracted from it his son Isma'il, peace be upon them both. And it was the case that his son Ishaq had already been born to him before the birth of Isma'il. The text takes care to mark this: though Isma'il is the one drawn out as successor, Ishaq was the elder, born before him.

We may pause to make the meaning of "gathering the eras" concrete. Adam, peace be upon him, was the Natiq of his era; Nuh was the Natiq of his era; and when Ibrahim came, what did he do? He gathered the era of Adam and the era of Nuh together. To gather the eras means this: all the individuals who had been assembled in the era of Adam and the era of Nuh — all who had entered the haykal of the Natiq of their time — Ibrahim gathered all of them into himself. The forms of both eras converged in him.

### The Two Ibrahims

A clarification of names must be entered here to prevent confusion, for the Kalam-e-Pak speaks of more than one Ibrahim. There are, in fact, two Ibrahims. One Ibrahim is the Natiq, the one of whom it is said alladhi waffa, "who fulfilled his covenants." And there is another Ibrahim, concerning whom it is said `kana rajulun yuqalu lahu Ibrahim` — "there was a man called Ibrahim." This second Ibrahim was a prophet, a Nabi, the Nabi of his own time, but he is not the Natiq Ibrahim who fulfilled. The Ibrahim of our discourse, upon whom the four ranks were gathered and who gathered all the forms of the eras of Adam and Nuh, is the Ibrahim alladhi waffa, Ibrahim who fulfilled.

### Ibrahim's Rank Among the Khilafats and the Mutimmin

Within the world of the khilafats — the successorships further detailed in the text — Ibrahim holds a special rank. He stands among the three, the thalathaha: Maulana Hashim, Maulana Hunayn, and Maulana Ibrahim. This is a special khilafah, and among the khilafats of the Qa'im, peace be upon him, these hold a distinguished station. The wazir of the Qa'im is 'Ayn 'Azim; and those who take the khilafah of 'Ayn 'Azimah, the Mim, are Janab 'Abd al-Muttalib, then Janab Abu Talib, then the line of 'Abdullah, with conjunctions, qiran, arising among them. After them come the mutimmin, the completers, who each hold a special rank — the mutimmin of the Dawr-e-Muhammadi, the era of Muhammad, peace be upon him.

Particular distinction attaches to the seventh in each cycle, every seventh one. Who are these sevenths? Janab Maulana Muhammad bin Isma'il; then after him Janab Ma'add al-Mu'izz; and Imam al-Tayyib, peace be upon him; then the twenty-eighth, Maulana Ma'add ibn Husayn; then the twenty-second, Maulana Ahmad; then the forty-second, Maulana 'Ali; and the forty-ninth, Abu al-Qa'im, peace be upon him. The rank of these seven is very high. Yet Hazrat Nasrullah Sahib, may his secret be sanctified, used to teach that although the rank of these seven is high, the rank of the atimmah, the completers, of the dawr of the Prophet rose so high that a conjunction, a qiran, occurred between them and the sevenths — the two became equal, sometimes the one taking the khilafah, sometimes the other. The people of the first week, the first haftah, are of so lofty a rank that a qiran was produced between them and the seventh.

Among the atimmah, the highest rank is that of Maulana Muhammad bin Isma'il, with whom a personality of the order of Imam Husayn is gathered. His rank is very high, and for this reason he does not fall into the ordinary category; his rank is established in the kashf, in the dawr-e-kashf — a very high rank indeed. Concerning these, the words are spoken: `Qul huwa Allahu ahad, Allahu s-samad, lam yalid wa lam yulad` — "Say: He is Allah, the One; Allah, the Eternal Refuge; He neither begets nor is born." Among the seven, four are greater: Maulana Muhammad bin Isma'il, Imam al-Tayyib, Maulana Ahmad, and the coming Abu al-Qa'im. These four are the greatest. Each of the latter three gathered two ranks: Imam al-Tayyib gathered two, Maulana Ahmad gathered two, and the coming Abu al-Qa'im will likewise gather two; whereas Maulana Muhammad bin Isma'il gathered but one rank — and yet even so his rank is the most special among them, the rank of the master of the era of unveiling, sahib-e-dawr-e-kashf. The rank of the dawr-e-kashf is manifest in him. He bears the nutq-e-ilahi and holds the station of the seventh; in name he is Abu 'Ali, and it is by the name of our Qa'im, 'Ali, that he is so called, Abu 'Ali.

A point of doctrine on the ordering of the four ranks deserves mention. In most books the offices are listed as Nabuwwah, Risalah, Imamah, and Wisayah. But the author of al-Anwar — and al-Anwar is a foundational book, from which most of the du'at draw their proofs — has placed Wisayah before Imamah, bringing wasayah first and imamate after. And the question of which rank stands higher is settled accordingly: Wisayah is the higher. Wisayah is higher than Imamah.

### Isma'il the Wasi, Ishaq the Manifest Hujjah and Veil

We return to the text and follow the disposition Ibrahim made of his sons. When the four ranks were gathered in Ibrahim, and he was in that state of virtue and honor, he established his da'wat until he brought forth from it his son Isma'il, peace be upon them both — Ishaq having already been born before Isma'il. Ibrahim then established Maulana Isma'il as Wasi: he entrusted to him the rank of Wisayah and of Imamah, by the command of Allah, the Exalted — both wisayah and imamah, by Allah's command — because Ibrahim himself is a maqam ilahi, a divine station, and a haykal nurani, a luminous temple.

To his son Ishaq, by contrast, Ibrahim entrusted the ranks of Nabuwwah and Risalah, prophethood and messengership. And he made Ishaq a khadim, a servant, in the service of his brother Isma'il, peace be upon them all; he made Ishaq a hijab, a veil, over Isma'il; and he made him a da'i, a summoner, calling toward Isma'il. Moreover, Ibrahim enjoined upon Ishaq that he should charge his own son to know the station of Isma'il and of all his progeny — that the preceding should enjoin the succeeding, one generation charging the next, until the time of the rising of their Qa'im.

Then Ibrahim passed away to the abode of Allah's generosity. And his son Isma'il stood after him as a Wasi in his station, but concealed, mustatiran; while Ishaq stood as his manifest hujjah, his zahir proof, and as a veil, until the passing of Isma'il drew near. Then Isma'il entrusted to his son Qaydar the Imamah and the Wisayah — both ranks together — and this continued in succession in the progeny of Qaydar, transmitted from father to son. And Ishaq entrusted to his son Ya'qub his own rank, the rank of the du'at; and this rank continued to be transferred among them until the Qa'im of the progeny of Isma'il at last received it.

### Why Lut Is Not Mentioned: The Two Lines of Istiqrar and Istida'

A pointed question must be addressed: why is Lut not mentioned in this chain? The answer lies in the fundamental division between two lines. Isma'il, peace be upon him, and his progeny are maqamat ilahiyyah, divine stations, possessors of luminous temples, dhu hayakil nuraniyyah, because they are ahl al-istiqrar, the people of permanence, of settled establishment. Ishaq and his progeny, on the other hand, are hujaj and du'at zahirun — proofs and manifest summoners — manifest by way of Isma'il and his progeny, and veils, hujub, upon them, made manifest according to what the confrontations necessitated and what the times and the periods decreed.

So the first of the du'at and the veils was Ishaq, and after him Ya'qub, and after him Yusuf; and the line of entrustment passes through Lawa, and Yahuda ibn Lawa, and Ayyub ibn 'Amus, and Zariq, and Yunus ibn Ayyub, and Shu'ayb ibn Safwan, and Musa ibn 'Imran, and Harun, and Yusha' ibn Nun, and Finhas ibn Harun, and Ilyas, and al-Yasa' ibn Akhtub, and Shamwil ibn Hali, and Dawud ibn 'Asa, and Sulayman, and Ash'iya ibn Anzaya, and 'Uzayr ibn Subaya, and Zakariyya, and his son Yahya, and Zinayal, and Janab 'Isa, peace be upon him. And after 'Isa it passes to Sham'un, and 'Abd al-Masih, and Marwat al-Rahib, and Sarjis — who is Bahira al-Rahib. These are the ahl al-istida', the people of entrustment, who are the du'at and the hujaj; and though they are of various lineages and relationships, all of them trace their lineage back to Ishaq, peace be upon them all — for every mustawda', every one entrusted, is traced back to Ishaq.

This line of nubuwwah ran through them: in the era of 'Isa it had reached Bahira the monk, and then again, after the Prophet Muhammad al-Mustafa, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, was made manifest, this rank of nubuwwah became submerged within the Prophet himself. Before that, nubuwwah was with them; when the Prophet was manifested, a rank of nubuwwah was then taken up by Ubayy ibn Ka'b.

### The Manifestation of the Prophet and the Two Lines United

`Al-fasl al-rabi' min al-bab al-awwal.` The Prophet, peace be upon him, was manifested from Janab 'Abd al-Muttalib, who was the bearer of the ranks of Imamah and Wisayah. From him the Prophet was made manifest. The distinction of the Prophet is great. Janab Hashim — Hashim al-sarid, "he who crumbled bread for the poor" — gathered the ranks and bestowed them in full upon Janab 'Abd al-Muttalib; he gave him all four ranks completely. And what did 'Abd al-Muttalib do? He divided the gathered offices into two parts: one part he gave to Janab 'Abdullah, and the second part to Janab Abu Talib. To Janab 'Abdullah came Nabuwwah, Risalah, Imamah, and Wisayah; and when 'Abdullah passed away, all those ranks were gathered in Janab Abu Talib. When the four ranks were gathered in Abu Talib, he too divided them: he gave the part of Nabuwwah and Risalah to the Prophet, peace be upon him, and the part of Imamah and Wisayah to Janab 'Ali. But because they were young, all the ranks of Imamah and Wisayah were also gathered, in effect, in the Prophet — through the kafalat-e-'uzma, the great guardianship. The blessing of the great guardianship was obtained, and its effect was so powerful that all the mustawda'in, and even the mustaqarrin who were with 'Ali, were overshadowed by him. For at the point where Hashim's da'wat was accepted, when the da'wat of 'Ayn occurred, the very first to affirm it were the Mim; and it is recounted, in a vivid and dramatic manner, how the Mim displayed khuzu' and khushu', humility and submissiveness, at that moment.

The two lines now meet. The chain of the Ambiya and the Rusul runs toward Ishaq. The text says: `hadhihi al-rutbah allati hiya rutbat al-du'at ila an tasallamaha qa'im walad Isma'il` — "this rank, which is the rank of the du'at, until the Qa'im of the progeny of Isma'il received it." The Qa'im of the progeny of Isma'il is Janab 'Ali, and the Qa'im of the progeny of Ishaq is the Prophet. The succession of the du'at, the line of Ishaq, came to rest with the Prophet; and when the Prophet is gathered with 'Ayn 'Azima, then he will gather them all.

### The Chain of the Divine Stations from Isma'il to Musa

`Al-fasl al-rabi' min al-bab al-awwal min al-suradiq al-thalith.` The first of the A'immah, the divine stations, the maqamat ilahiyyah, after Ibrahim, peace be upon him, was Isma'il ibn Ibrahim. Ibrahim entrusted it to his son Qaydar, and Qaydar to his son Nabat, and so the entrustment descends through a long chain of the divine stations — Sa'i, Ma'dhar, Mahshar, Kumayr, Nazal, Hizan, Dariz, 'Amamah, Badwan, Ra'ib, Mahmud, al-Dada', 'Akhut (said to be 'Unuq), Qaswar, Tamah (said to be Tabikh), Mat'am, Isma'il dhi al-'Awaj, Barat, Isma'il dhi al-Mutani', and on to his son Ibrahim who is Jami' al-Shaml, the gatherer of the assembly. This Ibrahim is yet a third, distinct from the two named before — an Ibrahim who appears among the A'immah, named Jami' al-Shaml. The chain continues through 'Antar, Zurayb, Shahdud, Muhsin, 'Illah, 'Aqba, Ra'imah, Muhtamil (said to be Mutahalhil), 'Awwam, Mahlam, Shahib, Amin, and 'Udad — until it reaches 'Udad ibn 'Udad, who is famous, and whose mutimm, whose completer, is Janab Musa, peace be upon him; the muqim, the one who establishes, is Musa. Between Ibrahim and Musa, then, there are a great many A'immah.

When the time became binding for the manifestation of a Natiq, Janab 'Udad established from among his du'at a Natiq — and that Natiq is Musa, peace be upon him. Then he entrusted it to his son 'Adnan, and 'Adnan to Ma'add, and Ma'add to his son Nizar, and Nizar to Mudar, and Mudar to his son Ilyas, and Ilyas to his son Mudrikah, and Mudrikah to his son Khuzaymah. And again the time became binding for the manifestation of a Natiq, so the Imam commanded his da'i to establish 'Isa, peace be upon him — for the manifestation of 'Isa.

A word should be entered here on the muqims, the establishers, of the great Natiqs, for these are the Imams who stand behind each Speaker. The muqim of Janab Adam is Maulana Hunayn. The muqim of Janab Nuh is Maulana Hud, peace be upon him — and from Hud the lineage runs, as it is said, `Min Hud Muhammadun`, "From Hud is Muhammad." The muqim of Janab Ibrahim is Maulana Salih. The muqim of Janab Musa is Janab 'Udad ibn 'Udad. And the muqim of Janab 'Isa is Janab Khuzaymah. These muqims, Hud and the rest, are themselves A'immah, divine stations standing behind their Natiqs.

Then Khuzaymah entrusted it to his son Kinanah, and Kinanah to Nadr, and Nadr to Malik, and Malik to Fihr, and Fihr to Ghalib, and Ghalib to Lu'ayy, and Lu'ayy to Ka'b, and Ka'b to Murrah, and Murrah to Kilab, and Kilab to Qusayy, and Qusayy to 'Abd Manaf, and 'Abd Manaf to Hashim, and Hashim to his son Maulana 'Abd al-Muttalib. And with 'Abd al-Muttalib the four ranks were gathered — the same four that had been gathered with his ancestor Ibrahim: Wisayah, Imamah, Nabuwwah, and Risalah. The ranks below these belong to the maqamat ilahi and are a different matter; the present account is clarifying the khilafats, the line of the nutq-e-ilahi.

### The Reserved Portions and the Conjunction of the Sevenths with the Qa'im

`Al-fasl al-khamis min al-bab al-awwal min al-suradiq.` It is stated here that for all the Imams of the dawr-e-kashf there is a Qa'im who manifests after a thousand years — every thousand years, on the seventh, one manifests who is equal to one Imam; and after seven thousand years one manifests who gathers all the thousands. The Qa'im, the Imam who manifests after seven thousand years, and the thalathaha sukra, the three intoxicated ones, are in qiran, in conjunction — meaning that the ranks of the dawr-e-sidq are exceedingly high. This conjunction takes place when the coming Qa'im of the era will also gather the dawr-e-kashf. First he will gather his own dawr-e-sidq; then he will gather the dawr-e-kashf as well, the era of unveiling that came before; and when he gathers the dawr-e-kashf, then in that gathering, in his haykal, the two will be peers — the seventh and the Qa'im of the seven thousand years standing as equals.

The text records the disposition of the four ranks among the lines of Isma'il and Ishaq: `Wa lamma ijtama'at hadhihi al-rutub al-arba' 'inda 'Abd al-Muttalib, kanat al-shintan minhunna mudkharatayn li-qa'im walad Isma'il, wa huma al-wisayah wa al-imamah. Wa al-shintan li-qa'im walad Ishaq, wa huma al-nabuwwah wa al-risalah` — "And when these four ranks were gathered with 'Abd al-Muttalib, two portions of them were reserved for the Qa'im of the progeny of Isma'il, and they are the Wisayah and the Imamah; and two portions were for the Qa'im of the progeny of Ishaq, and they are the Nabuwwah and the Risalah."

When the four ranks were gathered with Maulana 'Abd al-Muttalib, two of them were reserved — mudkharatayn, stored up and held in joined safekeeping — for the Qa'im of the progeny of Isma'il, and those two are Wisayah and Imamah. This carries a doctrine of its own: Imamah and Wisayah will under no circumstances be separated; they remain together, never parted in any place. The remaining two, Nabuwwah and Risalah, were reserved for the Qa'im of the progeny of Ishaq.

### The Luminous Temple and the Condition of the Father

`Fa-kana min 'adl Allahi ta'ala wa fadlihi 'ala Muhammadin wa sharafihi, salawat Allahi 'alayhi, an yakuna maqaman ilahiyyan wa haykalan nuraniyyan. Wa lan yakuna dhalika illa li-man yakunu abuhu kadhalika haykalan nuraniyyan. Wa illa fa-la sabil ila dhalik` — "So it was from the justice of Allah the Exalted and His grace upon Muhammad, peace be upon him, and his honor, the blessings of Allah be upon him, that he should be a divine station and a luminous temple. And that will not be except for one whose father is likewise a luminous temple. Otherwise, there is no way to it."

From the justice of Allah, and from His grace and honoring upon Muhammad, peace be upon him, it was decreed that the Prophet should become a maqam ilahi, a divine station, and a haykal nurani, a luminous temple. And this could not be except for one whose father is likewise a haykal nurani; if it were not so, there would be no path to it at all. The meaning is precise: Maulana Muhammad is a haykal nurani for the very reason that Maulana 'Abdullah is a haykal nurani. By the ordinary reckoning, it was necessary for the Prophet to gather only the ranks of Nabuwwah and Risalah. But because of the great guardianship, the kafalah, that Janab 'Abd al-Muttalib entrusted to him, the Prophet — though the bearer of Nabuwwah and Risalah — achieved superiority over all the ranks that had been gathered with Janab 'Ali in the spiritual world. He became the deputy of 'Ayn; after 'Ayn his rank stood established. And why did he attain that rank? Because he was a haykal nurani — his father being Janab 'Abdullah, and 'Abdullah's father Janab 'Abd al-Muttalib.

This discloses a new perspective, one not set out in this way in other books: that the condition of being a haykal nurani must hold for the father, and indeed for the grandfather. Yet a qualification must be added at once. This condition does not entail that only one son of a father can be an Imam; two children of a single father can both be Imams, both haykal nurani — as from Maulana 'Abd al-Muttalib two children became Imams, luminous temples. In the mustawda' line there is one Imam, and in the mustaqarr line another. After 'Abd al-Muttalib, it is not required of any Imam that only one of his sons be an Imam; both sons may be Imams. And so it follows that Maulana 'Abdullah is himself a haykal nurani — for only then can Maulana Muhammad be called a haykal nurani — and along with Maulana 'Abdullah, Maulana Abu Talib too is a haykal nurani.

The special quality of 'Ayn 'Azima is what crowns this. When the hijab of 'Ayn 'Azima was manifested, 'Ali said: `ana 'abdun min 'abid Muhammad` — "I am a slave from among the slaves of Muhammad." So lofty is the station of the awaited one, the one for whom all wait, that on account of it the Prophet not only obtained all the ranks of Nabuwwah and Risalah and entered upon their ten ranks, but in addition gathered to himself the Imamah and all the Imams and all the Wasis who were gathered with Janab 'Ali — every rank save the rank of 'Ali himself. No rank of the Qa'im remained outside him, save three: `illa thalathah sukra`, except the three intoxicated ones. And even these three intoxicated ones have gone to the Qa'im, with a connection still subsisting — `qaba qawsayn aw adna`, "at a distance of two bow-lengths or nearer." Who is the adna, the nearer? Janab 'Abdullah. And the qaba qawsayn, the two bow-lengths? Janab 'Abd al-Muttalib and Janab Abu Talib, with 'Abdullah as the adna.

This is a new point, not mentioned in other books — that the Prophet, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, cannot be a haykal nurani unless his father is a haykal nurani. The reason is that here there is no istida'iyyat, no entrustment-relation; here there is fawqiyyat, superiority, over all the Imams — and this by virtue of the kafalah, the guardianship. Were it a matter of mere entrustment, the case would differ. It is a condition set within the madarij, the spiritual degrees: if Janab 'Abdullah is an Imam and a haykal nurani, then the rank of Muhammad cannot be conferred upon any son who is not himself the son of a haykal nurani; it cannot be given to anyone else. The imamates differ in their kind and their rank — as with Hasan and Husayn, and as with Dawud and Sulayman, where among the prophets there is also imamate.

### The Two Gates of 'Abd al-Muttalib and the Order of the Bab al-Abwab

`Fa-kana min 'Abd al-Muttalib, salamu Allahi 'alayhi, an nasaba lahu babayn yatasallamani al-suwar al-muntaqilah al-murtaqiyah min ahl da'watihi, wa ja'ala ahadahuma a'la min al-akhar. Fa-kana al-'ali mujma'an li-suwar al-mu'minin al-bulagha' wa al-hudud al-fudala'. Wa al-thani mujma'an li-suwar al-mustajibin alladhina hadduhum akhdh al-'ahd wa al-wila, al-mustaqimin 'alayhi la ghayr` — "So it was from 'Abd al-Muttalib, peace of Allah be upon him, that he set up for himself two gates to receive the transferred, ascending forms from the people of his da'wat, and he made one of them higher than the other. So the higher was a congregation for the forms of the eloquent believers and the virtuous Hudud, and the second was a congregation for the forms of the respondents whose limit was the taking of the covenant and allegiance, remaining steadfast upon it and nothing more."

Maulana 'Abd al-Muttalib set up for himself two gates, two abwab, to receive the transferred and ascending forms from the people of his da'wat, making one of the two higher than the other. The higher gate was a gathering-place for the forms of the eloquent, accomplished believers and the virtuous hudud; the second was a gathering-place for the forms of the respondents, the mustajibin, whose station went no further than the taking of the covenant and the allegiance, and remaining steadfast upon it.

The arrangement of the bab al-abwab through the generations is precise, and it must be traced with care, distinguishing always the bab al-abwab from the qalbe haykal. For Janab Hashim, the bab al-abwab was his wife, Maulana Fatimah bint 'Amr; the gathered forms became submerged in his son, Janab 'Abd al-Muttalib. Janab 'Abd al-Muttalib then established a bab — his own wife, Fatimah bint 'Amr, as bab al-abwab — and the forms became submerged in his son Maulana Abu Talib. The second bab he established was 'Abdullah ibn Saba; through him the hearing, the istima', took place, and it gathered in Janab 'Abdullah. Janab Abu Talib appointed as his bab al-abwab his wife, Janab Fatimah bint Asad, and she became submerged in the qalbe haykal of Janab 'Ali. Janab 'Ali appointed as his bab al-abwab Janab Salman-e-Farsi, who would become the qalbe haykal for Imam Husayn, peace be upon him.

Here a distinction must be held firmly in mind: the bab al-abwab of the Imam and the qalbe haykal are not the same, and there is a difference between the two that should never be confused. The Prophet, peace be upon him, appointed as bab al-abwab for Janab Fatimah none but Janab Khadijah, who became submerged in Janab Fatimah. And Janab 'Abdullah appointed as bab al-abwab Janab Aminah, who became submerged in Janab Rasulullah, may Allah bless him and grant him peace. This must be understood: the Imam has a bab al-abwab who, by the gathering of the forms, becomes the qalbe haykal for the son who will be the next successor. To become qalbe haykal is one thing; to be bab al-abwab is another. The Imam appoints his wife as bab al-abwab, and she becomes submerged in his son; but through the gathering of the hearing she becomes the qalbe haykal.

### The Four Fatimahs of the Ya Fateru Prayer

This order of the four gates is mirrored, and made an object of devotion, in the Ya Fateru prayer that we recite. There are four invocations of Ya Fateru — four Fateru. One is in the sajdah, the prostration, a hundred times. Then upon the right cheek, a hundred times. Then upon the left cheek, a hundred times. Then again in the sajdah, a hundred and ten times. The first sajdah is for Janab Fatimah al-Zahra. The Prophet has said, "The believer is closest to Allah the Exalted when he is in sajdah." When the haykal is prepared together with the bab al-abwab, at that moment the spiritual realities stand closest to Allah within the da'wat; the worshipper is in sajdah. So the first sajdah belongs to Janab Fatimah al-Zahra, peace be upon her.

The sajdah-station upon the right cheek belongs to Janab Fatimah bint Asad, who was the mother of Maulana 'Ali and the bab al-abwab of Janab Abu Talib — the hujjah of 'Ayn 'Azima, the bab whose submersion is in the Mim; this is the right cheek. The third, upon the left, is Fatimah bint 'Amr, the wife of Janab 'Abd al-Muttalib and the mother of Janab Abu Talib. And the fourth sajdah, whose rank is given as a hundred and ten, belongs to the Umm al-Qa'im, the mother of the Qa'im — also the wife of the Qa'im, the Zawjat al-Qa'im, peace be upon him, whose name too is Fatimah. Her invocation is a hundred and ten times, indicating that she will be superior to the ten ranks. She will be superior to these three Fatimahs of the cheeks — yet Janab Fatimah al-Zahra is superior to all.

### The Seven Limbs of the Sajdah and the Completion of the Order

A final teaching crowns the account, drawn from the discipline of the prostration itself. In the sajdah, seven parts of the body must touch the ground. What are these seven parts? The forehead and the nose, counted together as one face; the two hands; the two knees; and the toes of the two feet. Taking the face as one, the seven are: the face, the two hands, the two knees, and the two feet. If a man is very old and cannot bring all seven to the ground, then the seven do not touch — and the rank is diminished accordingly.

The inner meaning is this. Where all seven limbs touch the ground in the sajdah, there the hudud — the ranks of the da'wat belonging to Janab Fatimah and to this other Fatimah, to Fatimah 'Ayn Ula and Fatimah 'Ayn Ukhra — are complete; the whole order of the da'wat is fulfilled, with seven of their jawarih, their limbs, present and prostrate. But upon the cheek, where only the cheek is touching and not the full seven, the rank is less. The full sajdah, with its seven limbs, marks the complete and highest order; the prostration of the cheek alone marks a lesser station. So the gradations of the four Fatimahs, and through them the whole architecture of the gates and the gathering of forms, are inscribed in the very members of the body bowed in prayer — the completed sajdah crowning the order, the cheek marking the rank that falls below it.
