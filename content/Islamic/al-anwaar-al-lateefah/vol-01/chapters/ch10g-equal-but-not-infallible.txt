# Equal but Not Infallible

*Episode ten of Al-Anwaar al-Lateefah, Volume One — The Oneness. The teaching draws out what follows from the absence of any obstacle in the abode of origination: the whole order came into being not in time but in a single instant, like sunlight flooding the air or a fire filling a dark room. It shows that the potential and the actual intellects existed together, with no interval of time between the first perfection and the second. Then it explains the crucial qualification — that the forms, though created perfectly equal, were not yet infallible, and that "error" means precisely the failure to attain the second perfection. Finally it narrates the pivotal moment: one of the luminous forms looked at its own essence, recognized an Originator unlike itself, and became the first to declare the Oneness of Allah — reaching toward Him through the means of His own greatness.*

## Where this episode picks up

If every being was created perfectly equal, with nothing whatsoever to separate the first from the last, how did anything ever go wrong? If there was no difference among them at all — no preference, no inclination, no disparity in great or small — then where did error enter, and how did some rise while others fell? The teaching has established that the Originator brought the luminous forms into being from nothing, in perfect equality, as an act of justice. That very equality now raises a sharp problem, because the whole of the volume has been about ranks and ascents and declines, about some who rise toward the source and some who sink below the beasts. If all began equal, that ordered hierarchy must have emerged from somewhere. This episode shows how: the equal forms were not yet infallible, and from that single fact the whole drama of ascent, error, and the first declaration of Oneness unfolds.

## No obstacle, a single instant

The teaching takes up a citation from Sayyidna Hamiduddin to explain why the whole originated order could come into being at once. In the abode of origination and the abode of emanation there is no obstacle, because these abodes are free of the obstructing matters — the kinds of matter that would impede are simply not present there. The forms are abstracted from such matter; they are pure forms attached to no matter, with no matter in them. Matter is what obstructs: wherever matter is present, there hindrances and impediments arise. But here there is no matter at all to halt the forms from action. There is power and ability present, and these can show their effect freely; no obstruction has been set in their way that would prevent the second action or impede the attainment of the second perfection. This is the point Sayyidna Hamiduddin — Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani, may Allah sanctify his soul — fixed in his great work on the repose of the intellect: that in the abode of origination there is no obstacle, precisely because the forms there are abstracted from the matter that obstructs (Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani, *Rahat al-'Aql*; on this work see Paul E. Walker, *Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani: Ismaili Thought in the Age of al-Hakim*).

And since there is no obstacle, the existence of the existents of these abodes came to be not in time but in a single instant — all at once, in one moment. Two images make this vivid. The first is the spreading of light through the expanse of the air from the light of the sun: when the sun is present, the whole air is illuminated at once, not piece by piece in time. The second is the lighting of a dark house by a fire: the moment the fire is kindled, the entire room is filled with light at once. So too the whole world of origination came into existence in a single instant, like air flooded with sunlight, like a dark house suddenly illumined. We naturally imagine creation as a sequence — first this, then that, the way a builder lays one course of bricks after another. But sequence belongs to the world of matter, where one thing must be moved aside before another can take its place. Where there is no matter, there is no such delay; the cause is present and the whole effect is there at once, the way light does not travel across a lit room but simply fills it.

Scripture speaks of the divine act in just this register of instantaneity. When the Qur'an measures the coming of the Hour — the most total of all events — it reaches for the swiftest image the eye knows:

> And unto God belongs the Unseen of the heavens and the earth. And the Command of the Hour is but as the twinkling of an eye, or nearer still. Truly God is Powerful over all things. — Quran, chapter 16, verse 77 (Surat (صورة) al-Nahl), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

If even the Hour, when it comes, comes as swiftly as the eye blinks or faster, then the origination of the world — where there is no matter at all to slow it — is the more reason that no interval of time divides the command from the existence it calls forth.

## Potential and actual together

The teaching draws out the implication of this simultaneity. In that abode, no thing's existence preceded another's. The existence of the intellects subsisting in actuality did not come before the existence of the intellects subsisting in potentiality. Rather, the existence of all was together. The "intellects in potentiality" are, in effect, the powers that were given to the intellects, which were afterward actualized and established; their existence was not preceded by some prior existing thing. Both the intellect subsisting potentially and the intellect subsisting actually existed at one and the same time.

And this carries a momentous consequence: there was no interval of time separating the first perfection from the second perfection. The two perfections were not divided by any passage of time; the potential and the actual co-existed from the outset, so that no temporal gap stood between the initial state of equality and the higher state to be attained. The teaching grants that some span of time seems necessary for our understanding to follow the matter — even the kindling of a fire seems, to us, to take some moment before the room fills with light. But the light in question here is not like ordinary light that spreads gradually; it is such that the instant it is lit, the whole is filled at once, and this is the character of the first perfection. The distinction between potential and actual is therefore not a distinction in time, as though the forms first existed merely possible and only later became real. It is a distinction in order, not in time — and holding that apart from our time-bound imagination is the key to everything the rest of the episode says about error, which is likewise not a matter of one thing happening after another but of a failure to move from one perfection to the perfection that was given alongside it from the first.

## Equal but not infallible

The teaching now explains, following Sayyidna Mohyuddin, what the state of first perfection does and does not include. In the state of their first existence and their first perfection, the beings are all equal, as already established — and yet, along with that equality, in the beginning of their collective existence, in action and deed, they are not infallible from error. This is the crucial qualification. The state of first perfection, for all its equality, is not the perfect, infallible state. The perfect state is the second perfection; the first perfection is the lower, initial condition, and in it the beings, though equal, are not protected from error. Error can proceed from them.

And what is this "error"? The teaching is precise: the error consists in failing to attain the second perfection. That is the error — a delay in responding to the invitation, a refusal to come when called, and, beyond that, the misleading of others, the setting of others upon the wrong path. All of this can issue from beings who are in the state of first perfection, for in that state they are not infallible. The first perfection was given to them as a test: go straight, and use rightly the powers given to you. The state is not yet the state of return-perfected; it is the trial-state of equality from which one must journey, by the right use of one's powers, to the second perfection. This answers the problem the episode opened with. The equal forms did not start out ranked, but they started out free — given powers and the freedom to use them, and not yet sealed against error. The hierarchy of the whole volume, the ascents and declines, the Mukathir (مُكَثِّر) who rises and the man who falls below the cattle, all trace back to this: from a single equal beginning, some used their powers to attain the second perfection and some failed to, and the difference between attaining and failing is the difference that opened up the ranks.

The Qur'an frames the human soul in exactly this double light — fashioned equal, then given the knowledge of both roads, and left to the use it makes of itself:

> By the soul and the One Who fashioned it, and inspired it as to what makes it iniquitous or reverent! Indeed, he prospers who purifies it, and he fails who corrupts it. — Quran, chapter 91, verses 7–10 (Surat (صورة) al-Shams), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

The soul is not made iniquitous or reverent; it is fashioned and then "inspired as to" both, taught the one road and the other. Prospering and failing are not stamped into it from the first; they are what becomes of it according to whether it purifies or corrupts what it was equally given. That is the first perfection precisely as the teaching describes it — equality that is not yet infallibility, a being set free to attain the second perfection or to fall short of it.

It is worth pausing on how this preserves the justice the teaching has insisted upon. The equality of the first perfection was an act of justice — the Originator gave to none more and to none less. Had the ranks been stamped into the forms from the start, that justice would be hollow, for some would have been made high and some low by no doing of their own. Instead the forms were made equal and then set free, so that the order which later emerged among them was the fruit of their own use or misuse of the powers entrusted to them. The hierarchy is thus not a violation of the original equality but its consequence: equal beings, freely exercising equal powers, arrived at unequal stations precisely because the powers could be used well or ill. The justice of the beginning and the order of the end are not at odds; the second grows out of the first through the freedom that lay between them.

## Slipping and being made to slip

The teaching then notes Sayyidna Mohyuddin's prayer at this point — that Allah protect us from slipping, or from being made to slip, by the right of Muhammad and his pure progeny, the blessings of Allah upon them all — and pauses over its grammar to drive home the meaning of "error." The grammatical contrast is itself instructive. One form of the verb means "he slipped"; another form means "he made another slip" — just as one form means "he cut" and another "he had another cut," or one means "he prostrated" and another "he made another prostrate." So the prayer asks protection both against our own slipping and against our being made to slip, or our making others slip. Likewise the teaching notes that one verb can carry the sense of going astray, and a related form the sense of leading another astray.

The Qur'an turns on this very causative form at the first catastrophe of the human story. Of the fall it does not say merely that Adam and his wife slipped; it names a slipper who made them slip:

> Then Satan made them slip therefrom, and expelled them from that wherein they were. — Quran, chapter 2, verse 36 (Surat (صورة) al-Baqarah), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

The verb is the causative the teaching dwells upon — not "they slipped" but "he made them slip" — and it carries the whole weight the morphology was meant to bear: error has one who suffers it and, sometimes, one who inflicts it upon another.

The point of dwelling on the grammar is to fix the two faces of the single "error." Error is not only what a being does to itself; it is also what a being does to others. To fail to attain the second perfection is one face — the form's own slipping, its own failure to rise when called. To mislead others, to set them on the crooked path so that they too fail to rise, is the other face — making others slip. Mohyuddin's prayer asks protection from both at once, because both are forms of the same failure that the first perfection leaves possible: that we ourselves fall short of the second perfection, or that we become the cause of another's falling short. The morphology of the verbs, with its steady rule that one form means "he did" and another means "he had it done," carries the whole moral weight: in the trial-state of equality, a being can ruin itself, and a being can ruin others, and the seeker prays to be kept from both.

## The first to declare oneness

The teaching now narrates the pivotal moment in which one of the originated forms became the first to perform the act of declaring Oneness. One of those imaginal forms looked, with his essence, at his own essence and at the others of his kind; and so he knew that they had an Originator unlike them; and so he negated divinity from himself and from the others of his kind, and affirmed it for their Originator, the Exalted, and bore witness to His oneness. This is the heart of the matter. He looked, by means of his own essence, at his own essence, and then at his kind, the children of his own genus. He saw himself, and he saw all his fellows. And from this looking he knew that there was, for himself and for them, an Originator unlike them — one different from them all, who had brought them into being.

Having known this, he negated divinity from himself and from the others of his kind: he could not claim, "I am the god," nor could any of his kind. And he affirmed divinity for their Originator, the Exalted, and bore witness to His oneness. In this single act he performed all three branches at once — the declaring of Oneness, the divesting of names from the Originator, and the negating of divinity from the ranks — and that very form became the Possessor of Tawhid (توحيد), the first to declare the divine unity. By looking at his own essence and his kind and recognizing therein an Originator unlike them, by negating divinity from all that was originated and affirming it for the Originator alone, he enacted the whole of Tawhid (توحيد) in its threefold completeness. Notice that this is the same act the seeker performed in climbing the chain of bewilderment — to negate divinity from every rank and affirm it for the Originator alone — but now traced back to its very first occurrence in the originated world. What the believer does when he declares the Oneness of Allah, the first luminous form did at the dawn of existence; the believer's Tawhid (توحيد) is a repetition, in his own horizon, of that primordial recognition.

The Commander of the Faithful gave this same movement — knowing the Originator, then stripping every likeness from Him — its most exact formulation. The Father of Imams, Ali ibn Abi Talib (peace be upon him) makes sincerity in the divine unity consist in negation itself:

> The foremost in religion is the acknowledgement of Him ... and the perfection of His purity is to deny Him attributes, because every attribute is a proof that it is different from that to which it is attributed, and everything to which something is attributed is different from the attribute. — the Father of Imams, Ali ibn Abi Talib.

This is the structure of the first form's act. To know the Originator and in the same breath to negate from Him every likeness to the originated is to do with a single recognizing glance what the Imam later set down as the perfection of sincerity: to affirm the Originator by denying that anything originated could be what He is.

## Seeking the means

What did this first form do after his act of recognition? He acknowledged Lordship for the Originator, and glorified Him, and was reverent, and humbled himself, and submitted, and sought means through His greatness toward Him, and supplicated. He acknowledged the Originator's Lordship, glorified Him, was reverent, humbled himself, and submitted. And then, most importantly, he sought means through the Originator's own greatness toward Him, and supplicated.

This is the special point on which the teaching dwells: the first form took the very greatness of the Originator as the means by which to reach the Originator. The greatness of the Originator cannot itself be reached directly; therefore the form took that greatness as his means of approach. Through the means of that greatness, he gave testimony to the Originator's Lordship and divinity, confessing his own lowliness. He did not presume to approach directly, for direct approach is impossible; rather, he made the Originator's greatness itself the intermediary by which he turned toward Him. This is the meaning of seeking the means: to take the greatness of the Originator as the means to the Originator, when the Originator Himself cannot be reached.

Seeking the means is no later refinement laid over the Qur'an; it is the Qur'an's own imperative. The believers are commanded not merely to revere God but to seek a means of approach to Him:

> O you who believe! Reverence God, and seek the means of approach unto Him, and strive in His way, that haply you may prosper. — Quran, chapter 5, verse 35 (Surat (صورة) al-Ma'idah), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

The command would be empty if the means were superfluous; that the believer is told to seek a means is itself the confirmation that the approach to the Originator runs through what He has appointed, and not through a bare, unaided turning of the self.

There is a beautiful inversion in this. The Originator's greatness is exactly what places Him beyond reach — the loftiness of His state is why no thought can rise to Him and no imagination attain Him. And yet that same greatness, which is the obstacle, becomes the road: the form cannot reach the Originator because of His greatness, and so the form reaches toward Him by way of that very greatness, making the unreachable height itself the ladder. What barred the approach becomes the means of approach.

This first act of seeking the means is the primordial model of everything the volume teaches about approach to the divine. The believer cannot reach the Originator directly; he reaches Him through the ranks, through the wali, through the appointed guide — and this is no makeshift arrangement forced upon a later, fallen world, but the very pattern set by the first form at the dawn of existence. From the beginning, the way to the One who cannot be reached has been to take what belongs to Him — His greatness, His appointed means — as the road toward Him. The seeker who binds himself to a guide and approaches Allah through the hierarchy is therefore not settling for an indirect route in place of a direct one; he is walking the only route there has ever been, the route the first Possessor of Tawhid (توحيد) walked when he made the Originator's own greatness his means of approach.

The Sufi path reached the same conclusion about the necessity of a means and gave it the homeliest possible form — that the seeker must bind himself to a living guide:

> Take a Pir, for without a Pir this journey is exceedingly full of woe and fear and danger. — Jalal al-Din Rumi, *Mathnawi*, Book I, trans. Reynold A. Nicholson.

Rumi's Pir and the teaching's appointed guide name one necessity: the One who cannot be reached directly is reached by way of an appointed means, and to refuse the means in the name of a more direct approach is not a higher piety but the very error this chapter has been tracing — a failure to take the only road there is.

## What this teaching lands

The drama of the cosmology has now turned on its hinge. Because the abode of origination is free of the matter that obstructs, the whole order came into being not in time but in a single instant — sunlight flooding the air, fire filling a dark room — with the potential and the actual intellects existing together and no interval of time between the first perfection and the second. The equal forms, though created without the slightest disparity, were not yet infallible; and error, the teaching makes precise, is exactly the failure to attain the second perfection — whether by one's own slipping or by making others slip, the two faces of the single fault that Mohyuddin's prayer names. From that trial-state of free equality, one form looked at its own essence, recognized an Originator unlike it and its kind, and negated, affirmed, and bore witness in a single act — becoming the first Possessor of Tawhid (توحيد), and reaching toward the Originator through the means of His own unreachable greatness. The hinge has turned: from a beginning of perfect equality, free and not yet infallible, the whole order of ascent and decline was set in motion, and the first act of that order was a creature's recognition that it was not God, and its turning — by way of God's own greatness — toward the God it could not reach.
