# Origination From Nothing

*Episode nine of Al-Anwaar al-Lateefah, Volume One — The Oneness. The teaching now turns from the gnosis of the ranks to the act that produced them, drawing on a long exposition of Sayyidna Mohyuddin, of which it presents the summary. It opens with the distinction that governs everything to follow — between origination, which brings being out of nothing, and ordinary making, which only rearranges what already exists — and shows that the Originator made the world out of no prior matter, no concurrent matter, and no underlying place. It explains why He originated at all: not from any need, but from pure favor and generosity. And it describes what He brought forth — luminous imaginal forms, of which the human body is only a fallen copy — created perfectly equal in their first perfection as an act of divine justice.*

## Where this episode picks up

Before anything at all existed, where was the world that was about to be made? The answer the teaching gives is stranger than "nowhere": there was no "where" at all, no place, no prior stuff, no canvas waiting to be painted. The earlier episodes followed the seeker up the ranks of the spiritual hierarchy to the First Intellect and the Originator beyond it, and traced how a soul declares the Oneness of Allah by negating divinity from every created rank. Now the teaching turns to the other half of the matter — not how the ranks are known, but how they, and everything else, came to be. And it begins by guarding against the most natural misunderstanding of the word "create," which we always picture as a making-of-something out of some material at hand. The origination of the world was not that. It was the bringing of being out of sheer nothing, and the whole cosmology depends on holding that distinction firm.

## The world of origination

The teaching opens with a foundational statement: that Allah the Exalted, toward whom the passing thoughts dare not turn and toward whom the imaginations and the inmost selves cannot rise, originated the world of origination in a single instant — not from any thing preceding it, nor with any thing accompanying it, nor upon any thing bearing it. Allah is such a Being that our thought and our inner being alike fall back, unable to ascend to Him. This is the Being who originated the world of origination. And this originated world is alluded to by a cluster of names: the world of command, the world of intellect, the world of sanctity, the world of purity, the world of subtlety, and the spiritual world — several names for the single originated realm.

Three negations are pronounced to make the matter unmistakable. First, the world was originated not from any thing that preceded it — there was no prior substance to serve as its prime matter, its first leaven. Second, it was originated not with any accompanying thing — there was no co-present matter alongside it from which it was shaped, nothing it could resemble or take after. Third, it was originated not upon any thing bearing it — there was no pre-existing substrate to serve it as a place, a first location upon which it rested. No prior matter, no concurrent matter, no underlying place: the world of origination was brought forth in a single instant, from nothing whatsoever. Each negation closes off one of the ways our minds smuggle a "something" back into the picture — a material the thing was made from, a mould it was made alongside, a floor it was set upon. The teaching removes all three at once, leaving the act of origination standing utterly alone, with nothing before it, beside it, or beneath it.

The three negations are not redundant; each guards against a distinct reflex of the imagination. When we try to picture creation, we first reach for a raw material — clay for the pot, dough for the bread — and the first negation strips that away: there was no prior substance to be worked. Foiled there, the imagination next supplies a companion-stuff, some matter present alongside the act, on the model of which the new thing might be shaped; the second negation strips that away too: there was nothing co-present for the originated world to resemble or take after. And still the imagination clutches at a place, a bare location, an empty space in which the new thing could at least appear; the third negation removes even that: there was no substrate, no first "where." Only when all three props are knocked out does the mind finally confront what origination means — being brought forth with no material, no model, and no place — and feel how unlike it is to anything we have ever called "making."

## Origination, not making

To fix the point, the teaching marks a fundamental distinction that governs everything to follow: the distinction between origination and ordinary making. Making, or creation in the common sense, is what occurs when the arrangement of already-existing things is changed — when a pre-existent thing is broken or reshaped and a new composite form is brought forth from it, the form altered while the underlying matter is presupposed. This is the only kind of "creating" we ever witness. A potter makes a pot, but the clay was already there; a builder raises a house, but the stone and timber existed before him. In every case we call "making," something is on hand to be worked, and the maker only gives it a new shape.

Origination is something else entirely. It is the bringing of being out of nothing, where no prior thing exists at all. The world of origination was brought into being by origination in this strict sense — and this is exactly why the three negations were needed. If even a trace of prior matter, or concurrent matter, or an underlying place had been involved, the act would have collapsed back into mere making: a reshaping of something already there. The teaching insists on the difference because the whole account of how the ranks came to be rests upon it. The Originator is not a craftsman working pre-existent stuff into form; He is the One who brings the very stuff, and the form, and everything, out of an absolute nothing. Nothing in our ordinary experience of making is an image of this; it is a unique act belonging to the Originator alone, and the word "origination" is reserved for it precisely to keep it from being confused with the making we know.

## Pure favor, not need

Why did the Originator originate at all? The teaching answers that He originated the existents into a world of existence, drawing them from a nothingness that was not existent, as a favor from Him, and generosity, and bestowal. And what is this nothingness from which they were drawn? It is not some world or realm; it is simply the absence of being. The teaching recalls how, in Sindh, a teacher once asked his students: when all this was taking place, where were we? — the point being precisely that there was no "where," for there was nothing but non-existence from which we were drawn. The question has no answer because it assumes a place and a presence that did not yet exist; we were not anywhere, because we were not at all.

And the cause of the origination was no need or compulsion, but pure favor. It is of the very nature of the Generous and the Powerful to bestow favor, and of the very disposition of the Mighty and the Great to be generous, to give and to bestow. He brought the existents into being not because He needed them — the Giver had no need of them whatsoever — nor as idle sport, not playing with them or creating them to no purpose, for He is exalted and transcendent above any such thing. On the question of why the Originator created, the teaching notes that Sayyidna Mohyuddin wrote chapter after chapter, negating every possible purpose and every possible need, refuting one supposed motive after another, until he arrived at the single conclusion that this favor was itself the only "purpose": that He originated out of sheer favor, generosity, and bestowal, and nothing else.

The method is itself instructive. Mohyuddin does not begin by asserting that the motive was favor; he begins by eliminating, one after another, every motive that would imply a lack in the Originator. Did He create to gain something? Then He lacked it before. Did He create to be served, or known, or praised? Then He needed the service, the knowledge, the praise. Did He create in play, for diversion? Then He was a being who could be diverted, who had idle time to fill. Each supposed purpose, examined, turns out to attribute to the Originator some deficiency that a true God cannot have. When every such motive has been struck down, what remains is the only "purpose" compatible with absolute self-sufficiency: not a purpose in the sense of an end He needed to reach, but the sheer overflow of a generous nature that gives because giving is what generosity does. The favor is not a means to anything; it is the whole of the matter. This matters for everything that follows. A god who created out of need would be a god lacking something, and so a god with a nourisher of his own — exactly the mark of the created gods the earlier episode set aside. The true Originator creates out of fullness, not lack: the origination flows from the bestowing nature of the Generous and Mighty, free of need and free of frivolity.

## The luminous forms

In what manner were the originated beings brought forth? The teaching answers that He brought them into existence as forms — purely imaginal, pure, and luminous — possessing thousand-fold statures like the human corporeal stature, distinct from their material souls. He brought them into being as forms, and these forms were purely imaginal, pure, and luminous. They possessed statures of a thousand-fold measure, like the human bodily stature.

But the teaching is careful to set the direction of the likeness rightly. It is said that the originated forms were like the human bodily stature only to make the matter intelligible to us; in reality it is the other way around. The human bodily stature is patterned upon those originated forms, not they upon it. When the Originator created, He created in such a state that the human bodies which later came into the world were fashioned on the pattern of those primordial luminous forms. So although we are taught that the originated forms resembled the human stature, the truth is that the human stature was made on the model of the originated forms. And these luminous forms are distinct from the material souls that came later. Those souls did not yet exist; the souls are things of a later stage. The forms that were originated were luminous imaginal forms; the souls, when they were formed, were a fallen, degraded condition derived from these; and the bodies of flesh and blood, when they were formed, were a still more fallen condition. The forms were luminous subtleties; the bodies are dark densities. The soul is a fallen form of the luminous original, and the body a yet more fallen form. We are accustomed to think of the body as the original and the spiritual form as a faint abstraction of it; the teaching reverses this entirely. The luminous form is the original and the body its dim, heavy shadow — which is why, throughout this volume, the movement of salvation is a movement of ascent and refinement, a return from the dark density toward the luminous subtlety that was first.

This reversal recasts the whole human predicament. If the body were the original and the luminous form a pale copy, then to be embodied would be to be at home, and the spiritual life would be a flight from our native condition into something thinner and less real. But the teaching says the opposite: the luminous form is the real and original thing, the soul a fallen condition derived from it, and the body of flesh a still-further fall. To be lodged in dark, dense flesh is therefore not to be at home but to be at the furthest remove from one's own origin. This is exactly why the earlier chapter could call this lower world a place of exile and salvation a homecoming. The refinement of the Mukathir, whose flesh was made subtle though it looked unchanged, is the first stirring of that return — the dense shadow beginning to recover the luminosity of the form it was copied from.

## Created equal

The teaching now sets out the doctrine of the first perfection: that He brought those luminous forms into existence equal in the first perfection — which is existence, life, strength, and ability — with no disagreement in them, no preference, no distinction, and no inclination in what was subtle or great, much or little, as an act of justice from Him, the Mighty and Majestic. He brought the luminous forms into existence equal in the first perfection. This first perfection is the original state in which Allah created them, and it consists of four things: existence, life, strength, and ability — which the teaching glosses as knowledge and ability together. In this first perfection the forms were created perfectly equal: there was no disagreement among them, no preference of one over another, no distinction, and no inclination — neither in what is subtle nor in what is great, neither in much nor in little. The Originator created the first exactly as He created the last; there is not the slightest difference among the originated beings in the state of first perfection.

And this equality was an act of justice from Him, mighty and majestic, for the justice of the Just is equal in its distribution, and the grace of the Gracious is universal in its reach; therefore the originated forms were created in perfect equality. This equality is anchored in scripture, in the saying of the High, the Great: And if it had been from other than Allah, they would have found in it much contradiction (chapter 4, verse 82). If the originated order had proceeded from any other than Allah, much contradiction would be found within it — for only the perfect justice of Allah could produce a creation without disparity. Because Allah created it, He showed His creation this kindness: He gave to none more and to none less; what He gave, He gave equally. This is the very verse that answers the question of whether the equal creation of all beings is attested in the Holy Qur'an: it is attested here. And it is the same reality affirmed by Sayyidna Hamiduddin, may Allah sanctify his soul, in his great work on the repose of the intellect — that in the abode of origination there is no obstacle, because the forms there are free of the matter that obstructs. That freedom from obstruction, and what follows from it, is where the next episode begins.

## What this episode lands

The cosmology has now been founded. The Originator brought the world of origination into being in a single instant, from no prior matter, no concurrent matter, and no underlying place — an act of origination utterly unlike the making we know, which only reshapes what already exists. He originated not from any need, for a god who needed his creation would be a god in lack; He originated from pure favor, generosity, and bestowal, drawing the existents out of a nothingness that was no place at all. What He brought forth were luminous imaginal forms of thousand-fold stature, upon which the human body was later patterned — the soul a fallen copy of the luminous form, the body a fallen copy of the soul, so that salvation is a return from dark density toward luminous subtlety. And He created all the forms perfectly equal in their first perfection — existence, life, strength, and ability — as an act of the justice that gives to none more and to none less, a justice the Qur'an itself attests. The next episode takes up what follows from the absence of any obstacle in that abode: how the whole order came to exist in a single instant, why the equal forms were nonetheless not yet infallible, and how one of them became the very first to declare the Oneness of Allah. The cosmology, in other words, is only half-told: we have heard how the world was originated and in what perfect equality it began, but not yet how, from that equality, the ranks and the first act of Tawhid emerged.
