## Where this episode picks up

What if the highest praise the highest creature can offer its Creator is, by its very nature, a kind of failure?

The previous episode left us with a paradox in waiting: we are compelled to name a God whom no name can reach. This episode follows that paradox down to its root. It begins with the First Intellect's own first words — the act in which it denied divinity to itself and affirmed it for Allah alone — and from there it works out why we name the Unnameable at all, why the existence Allah gives is never taken back, and how naming Him turns out to be a sin that is already joined to the seeking of forgiveness. It closes with two short codas the book attaches here: the ladder of spiritual ranks rising toward the bare "He," and the hidden meanings of three forms of prayer. The strange resolution we are heading toward is that the same act can be a fault and an act of worship at once.

## Negation and affirmation

The book now recounts the primal act of the First Intellect — the act in which divinity was first denied and then affirmed. Contemplating the Originator, the First Intellect first performed a negation. He said, in effect, "I am not the creator of myself." He pronounced the denial — there is no god — negating divinity from himself: I have no power to create myself, nor does my genus, the whole order of beings like me, have any such power. He stripped divinity, al-ilahiyyah, from himself and from his entire kind, refusing it for the highest created thing and for everything beneath it. And then, in the same breath, he affirmed that divinity for Allah alone — except He. The movement is two-part and inseparable: first a clearing-away of every false claimant, including the noblest, and then a single affirmation that leaves only the Originator standing.

This is the very pattern the Noble Book records in the words "Allah testifies that there is no god but He" (chapter 3, verse 18). And the deep sense the book draws out of that verse is striking. The "Allah" who testifies in it is the First Intellect. It is the First Intellect that bears witness that there is no god but He, the Originator Most High. The verse, read this way, is not God describing Himself from outside; it is the first and highest of His creations confessing, on behalf of all created being, that it cannot account for itself and must point beyond itself. The First Intellect, and with it the whole order of creation, first confessed that it could not create itself nor its own genus, and then pointed past itself — He alone — to the Originator, indicated only by the bare pronoun "He."

And nothing of that, the text adds, falls upon Him save, perhaps, the saying "He"; the reality of even that the book promises to clarify later, as if to warn us that the last word we have is itself not quite adequate. So the Intellect expressed Him only through what belonged to the attributes of His origination and His act — saying, in effect, "He created us thus, He fashioned us thus." We cannot praise the Creator as He deserves, because we do not know Him; but from the fact of our own existence, from the manner in which He made us, we can speak. He gave us life; He gave us knowledge; He gave us power. That much we may say, because that much we possess and know first-hand. As for true glorification, the tamjid, the highest praise that would actually befit Him, that is beyond us entirely. The very highest thing we are able to say is "Allah," and above that, "He." Beyond those two words we have nothing at all with which to interpret Him — and even they, the book has just hinted, reach Him only barely.

## Why we name at all

Why, then, do we name Him at all with these admittedly inadequate names? The text explains that we let the name fall upon the exalted Ipseity because of our inadequacy, the narrowness of our essence, our confinement, and our deficiency. We let the name fall upon the exalted huwiyyah on account of our 'ijz, our inadequacy, and the narrowness of our jawhar — the essence of speech and articulation itself is too small for the task. We simply do not possess the essential capacity to comprehend Him, and so we have named Him with the last word that comes to our minds, the word beyond which there is nothing further to reach for. Our sense is confined, and we are deficient and constrained; out of that very confinement and deficiency the naming arises. The name, in other words, is not a measure taken of Him; it is a measure of our own limits, the mark left by a reach that cannot extend any further.

This points to a precise distinction in what the created intellects were granted, and the distinction is the key to the whole paradox. They were given total knowledge, ilm al-kulli — but not knowledge of the divine essence. As Sayyidna Mu'ayyad explains, the knowledge they were given is the knowledge of life, 'ilm al-hayat: the knowledge of what powers and faculties reside in life itself — life, knowledge, power, and choice. Sayyidna Mu'ayyad expounds at length what each of these is in turn: what life is, what knowledge is, what power is, what choice is. The created intellects, in other words, were handed a complete understanding of the structure of existence, the full architecture of what it is to be alive and aware and capable. But nothing beyond this was given them. They were not given the knowledge by which to know Allah; that one knowledge was deliberately withheld, for it cannot even be comprehended. Hold the two halves together and the shape of the gift becomes exact: total knowledge of everything that can be known, joined to no knowledge whatever of the One who gave it. The intellects know the whole of life and remain sealed off from its Source — and that seal is not a defect in the gift but the very condition under which any creature exists at all.

## The gift not withdrawn

A further teaching of Sayyidna Mu'ayyad concerns the permanence of what Allah bestows, and it adds a note of mercy to a chapter otherwise full of distance. After Allah has given existence, He does not take that existence back — just as a generous human being who gives a thing to another does not afterward snatch it away from him; so too Allah, having granted existence, does not withdraw it. The gift, once given, stands. What He brings into being He sustains in being; the very fact that we exist at all is therefore not a fragile loan that might be recalled at any moment, but a settled bestowal.

This bears on a subtle question about the reach of divine power, and the book illustrates it with a debate. There was once a dispute between an Arya Samaji and the Muslims, and the Arya Samaji posed a sharp challenge. "Your Allah can do everything?" The Muslims answered, "Yes." "Then can He do injustice, zulm? If He can do everything, He can do injustice too — and if you say He cannot, then you are limiting His power." It is a clever trap, designed to force a choice between an unjust God and a limited one. The Muslims replied with a counter-question of their own. "Can you eat everything? You have the power to eat — so eat this filth set before you." The man, of course, could not bring himself to it; and the point lands. Possessing the capacity to eat does not oblige one to consume what is vile, and the refusal is no failure of the capacity. In just the same way, Allah can do everything, yet He does not do injustice. His not doing wrong is no limitation upon His power; it is the perfection of His justice. Power that could do anything is simply not the same as a will that would do anything, and the whole distance between the two is exactly where His justice lives. And it is in that same spirit that He does not take back the existence He has given — not because He could not, but because it would be a wrong, and wrong is what He does not do.

## A sin paired with pardon

The text now adduces a prayer of Sayyidna Mu'ayyad — Hibatullah ibn Musa al-Shirazi, author of the Mabda' wa Ma'ad — that draws out the paradox at the very root of all naming and finally resolves it. The prayer begins by confessing the truth of what Allah declared in His wise remembrance: that they have not appraised Allah with His true appraisal. Our confession is rendered to the truth of that word — we have not been able to measure the worth of Allah as He truly deserves, and we cannot, because we do not know Him. We are able only to say that, judging from our own existence, He has created so good and so excellent a thing; from the gift we infer the generosity of the Giver, and there our appraisal must stop.

Then the prayer asks for pardon, and the request itself is precise. It asks Allah for forbearance — musamahah, from the root meaning to forgive — toward one who is caught in the snare of servitude, hemmed in by the narrow straits of confinement and constraint, who, when he sets himself to undertake His remembrance, is compelled to do so by means unworthy of Him, for He is worthy only of what we cannot comprehend. We are bound slaves, the prayer says in effect, with limits set upon us beyond which we cannot pass; we say as much as we are able, as much as our power permits; and where we fall short and cannot say what truly befits Him, we ask His pardon. And the reason this pardon is needed is given exactly: to undertake His remembrance by that which He is not worthy of, under the rule of compulsion, is only a sin coupled with the seeking of forgiveness. We are compelled; out of necessity, idtirar, we have no other word to say, and so we say what we can. Therefore it is a sin coupled with the seeking of forgiveness — and this is a technical term established by the Du'at, a sin that is mashfu', paired, with istighfar. It is a sin, and yet a sin already joined to the asking of pardon, and because it arises from compulsion rather than from defiance it is accepted, for compulsion is excusable and the deepest humility is shown in the very asking.

This teaching, the text is careful to note, applies not to the physical world alone but to all the worlds equally, and this is where the paradox sharpens before it resolves. The istighfar required here is for all; the First Intellect itself is included along with everyone else. This means the "sin" cannot be a sin in the ordinary moral sense, for the First Intellect committed no transgression at all. Rather, even its pure act of bearing witness — its saying that He is the Originator Most High, its pointing to Him by the bare pronoun "He," since there is simply nothing beyond that to say — carries within it, in a manner of speaking, the character of a sin, because it falls short of His true majesty; and so even that act is coupled with istighfar. From the first intellect down to the tenth, none can do anything beyond this; all are compelled, and all are perfectly equal before the Originator, whom none can recognize. The "sin," then, is just the unavoidable shortfall built into any creature's speech about God — and its remedy is built in alongside it.

The principle of compulsion is illustrated, finally, by a case judged in the time of Umar. A woman was brought before him who was with child, yet she said she had no husband. Asked how this had come to be, she explained: she had been traveling through the desert, perishing of thirst for three days, when she met a shepherd who said he would give her water only on the condition that she give herself to him. Driven by the extremity of her thirst, near to death, she yielded for the sake of the water, and afterward bore a child. Umar said, "Stone her." But the Mawla of all creation, Imam Ali, arrived and asked what the matter was, and when it was told to him he said: "She is compelled, helpless. Let her go, for this act occurred under compulsion." Where there is true helplessness, the act does not bear the full weight of a sin. So it is, the text intimates, with the soul that in its helplessness names the Unnameable: it sins, in a sense, by speaking inadequately of Him, yet because it is genuinely compelled, its sin is paired with the asking of forgiveness, and it is excused. Even in the pure world of spirits, where all is illumined save His essence, this shadow of a "sin" can fall — and even there it is answered, in the same instant, by the istighfar that is already coupled to it.

## The ten ascending ranks

The first section closes with two short codas, and the first is an enumeration of the spiritual ranks through which the ascent toward the divine is ordered — the maratib, the ranks rising toward the highest. The series begins low, with the Imamate of spirituality, and from there the ranks rise in fixed order: Hujjiyyah, then Hijabiyyah, then Khayaliyyah, then Fathiyyah, then Jiddiyyah, then Anza'iyyah, then Qa'imiyyah, then Jussiyyah, then Ahadiyyah, then Samadiyyah — culminating in Ilahiyyah and finally Huwiyyah. For the last and highest rank is Huwiyyah, the rank of the bare "He." The ladder set out here maps the great series of spiritual stations by which the soul mounts step by step toward its origin. And it is no accident that the ladder ends precisely where the whole doctrine of these episodes has been pointing all along — at the Ipseity, the "He" beyond every name. The highest reach of the ascent is the same bare pronoun that was the highest word in our mouths; the structure of the climb and the limit of our speech meet at the top.

## Shafa', witr, and julus

The second coda is drawn out from the word for "paired" in the phrase about a sin coupled with forgiveness. That word, shafa', means pairing, and among the Du'at it carries an esoteric referent, as do its two companion terms, witr and julus, all three taken from the language of prayer. The prayer that is shafa' is the paired one of two units, the two rak'at; witr is the single, odd unit; and julus is the prayer of two units performed sitting. Each of these has an inner meaning, a ta'weel, beneath its outward form, so that the very vocabulary of ritual prayer turns out to be carrying a hidden teaching about the household of the Prophet.

The referent of shafa', the paired prayer, is the Prophet — peace and blessings be upon him — together with Lady Fatima, for they are the noble pair. The referent of witr, the single prayer, is Imam Ali, peace be upon him, the odd and singular one. And the referent of julus, the sitting prayer of two units, is Imam Hasan and Imam Husayn together. It is told that Imam Husayn himself at Karbala would name these correspondences aloud — that the referent of shafa' is the Prophet, that the referent of witr was his own father, and so forth — and the text affirms that these together form one sign. So the very terminology by which the Du'at express the "coupled sin" of inadequate naming opens, in its turn, onto the hidden ranks of the Prophet and his pure household. The language of our helplessness before God turns out, at its root, to be the language of those through whom God is approached. The chapter that began with the limits of our speech ends by showing that even those limits are inscribed with the names of the chosen ones.

## What this episode lands

The episode has resolved the paradox it opened with. The First Intellect's first act was to deny divinity to itself and affirm it for Allah — and even that pure act of witness counts, in a manner of speaking, as a "sin," because it falls short of His true majesty. We name the Unnameable not out of presumption but out of compulsion: our essence is too narrow to comprehend Him, and so we reach for the last word we have. That naming is a fault and a faithfulness at once — a sin already coupled with the asking of pardon, excused because it is forced, just as the helpless woman before Imam Ali was excused. And the chapter's two closing ladders, the rising ranks and the three prayers, both point the same way: every approach to the unknowable Originator runs through the chosen ones who stand nearest to Him.

## Closing

We have reached the floor and the ceiling of human speech about God in a single chapter — the bare "He" at the top of the ladder, and the coupled sin-and-pardon of every word beneath it. Both, in the end, point toward the household of the Prophet as the way the unknowable is approached. The next stretch of the book leaves behind the question of the Originator's essence and turns to what the Oneness, the tawhid, actually asks of the believer — not a bare statement that "God is one," but the gnosis of the ranks through which He is known.
