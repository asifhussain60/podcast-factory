# Naming the Unnameable

*Episode three of Al-Anwaar al-Lateefah, Volume One — The Oneness. The previous lesson closed on a paradox: the First Intellect cannot know the Originator, yet it is the First Intellect that first speaks of Him. This episode follows that speaking to its strange conclusion. It opens with the primal act in which the First Intellect negated divinity from itself and affirmed it for Allah alone — the act the Qur'an records as "Allah testifies that there is no god but He." It shows why we name Him only out of our own inadequacy, having been given knowledge of life but never knowledge of His essence; why the existence He grants is never taken back, so that His refusal to do injustice is the perfection of His power and not a limit upon it; and why naming the Unnameable is, in the end, a sin that comes already joined to the asking of forgiveness — a sin excused because it is compelled. It closes with the ten ascending ranks of the spiritual ladder and the hidden meanings folded into the postures of prayer.*

## Where this episode picks up

If you cannot know a thing at all, by what right do you give it a name — and what does the naming cost you? The previous lesson left that paradox unresolved. We say "Allah," yet we have just been told that the word lands not on the Originator but on the first thing He made, and that the bewilderment behind it is the common condition of everything created. Now the teaching presses the paradox to its root. It asks how the First Intellect, which cannot comprehend its Maker, nonetheless became the first to speak of Him — and it discovers that this first speech is at once the noblest act in all creation and, in a precise and startling sense, a sin. This episode follows that thread: the primal negation and affirmation, the reason we name Him so poorly, the justice that never reclaims its gifts, the sin that arrives already forgiven, and the ladder of ranks on which the whole drama is staged.

## The First Intellect's first act

Before anything could be said about the Originator, the First Intellect performed an act — and the teaching presents it as the first and founding act of the whole created order. Contemplating the One who had brought it forth, the First Intellect first negated. It said, in effect, "I am not the creator of myself." It pronounced the great denial — there is no god — stripping divinity away from itself: I have no power to create myself, nor does my kind, the order of beings like me, have any such power. It cleared divinity from itself and from its entire genus. And having negated, it affirmed — affirmed that divinity for Allah alone, for Him and no other.

This, the teaching says, is the very pattern the Qur'an records in the verse "Allah testifies that there is no god but He" (chapter 3, verse 18). And the deep sense is startling: the "Allah" who testifies in that verse is the First Intellect. It is the First Intellect that bears witness there is no god but He — the Originator, indicated by nothing more than the bare pronoun "He." The whole order of created being, through its first member, first confessed that it could not create itself or its own kind, and then pointed beyond itself with that single word — He alone — toward the One it could not name.

And nothing of that testimony, the teaching adds, falls upon the Originator save, perhaps, the saying "He." Even the First Intellect, in bearing witness, could express Him only through what belonged to its own origination — through the manner in which it had been made. It could say, in effect, "He created us thus, He fashioned us so; He gave us life, He gave us knowledge, He gave us power." That much it possessed and so could speak. But the true glorification, the praise that would actually befit Him, lay beyond it. The highest word available was "Allah," and beyond that, "He" — and past those two words there was nothing left with which to interpret Him at all.

## Naming from inadequacy

Why, then, do we name Him with such inadequate words instead of keeping a reverent silence? Because, the teaching answers, we are driven to it by our own poverty. We let the name fall upon the exalted "He" on account of our inadequacy and the sheer narrowness of our essence — the essence of speech and articulation that is all we have to work with. We simply do not possess the inner capacity to comprehend Him, and so we name Him with the last word that comes to mind, the word beyond which the mind finds nothing further. Our perception is hemmed in; we are deficient and constrained; and out of that confinement the naming arises, not as a triumph of language but as its breaking point.

This points to a precise distinction in what the created intellects were actually given. They were granted total knowledge — but not knowledge of the divine essence. As Sayyidna Mu'ayyad explains, the knowledge they received is the knowledge of life: the knowledge of what powers and faculties reside in life itself. He sets them out one by one — life, knowledge, power, and choice — and expounds at length what each of them is: what it means to live, to know, to have power, to choose. The created intellect understands the whole structure of life in its fullness; nothing of that order is hidden from it.

But nothing beyond this was given. The intellects were not granted the knowledge by which to know Allah, for that knowledge cannot even be comprehended; it was withheld because it is, by its very nature, unreceivable. So the most exalted creature knows everything about the gift of life and nothing about the Giver. This is the exact shape of our predicament when we name Him. We are not lazy or careless namers who could do better with a little more effort. We are namers who have been handed a complete knowledge of one thing — life and its faculties — and no knowledge whatever of the One we are trying to name. We name Him with the vocabulary of life because it is the only vocabulary we were ever given.

## The gift not withdrawn

A further teaching of Sayyidna Mu'ayyad turns on the permanence of what Allah bestows. Once Allah has given existence, He does not take it back. The teaching draws the human comparison: a person who gives a gift to another does not afterward snatch it away; so too Allah, having granted existence, does not withdraw it. What He gives, He gives.

This bears on a subtle challenge about the reach of divine power. There was once a debate between a follower of the Arya Samaj and the Muslims, and the challenger set a trap. "Your Allah can do everything?" "Yes." "Then can He do injustice? If He can do all things, He can do wrong as well — and if you say He cannot, then you have set a limit on His power." It is the old snare, made to force omnipotence to swallow its own coherence.

The Muslims answered with a question of their own. "Can you eat anything? You have the power to eat — so eat this filth set before you." The man, of course, could not bring himself to it. Possessing the capacity to eat does not oblige a man to consume what is vile; the power is altogether real, and its non-use is not the absence of the power. Just so with the Originator. Allah is capable of all things, and yet He does not do injustice — and His not doing it is no limitation upon His power but the very perfection of His justice. A capacity is not a compulsion. That He could and does not is precisely the point, and it is the same point as His never reclaiming the existence He has given: power held in perfect rectitude, never turned to wrong.

This is why the gift of existence is secure. The same justice that refuses to do injustice refuses also to betray a gift already given. Our being is not on loan, to be recalled at a whim; it rests on the steadiness of the One who, able to do anything, does only what is right.

## A sin coupled with forgiveness

Now the teaching reaches its most paradoxical point, and it does so through a prayer of Sayyidna Mu'ayyad — Hibatullah ibn Musa al-Shirazi, the spiritual heir of Mawlana Sayyidna Mu'ayyad al-Din, author of the Mabda' wa Ma'ad. In it he confesses, on behalf of all who name God, the truth of the Qur'anic word that they have not appraised Allah with His true appraisal. We have not measured His worth as it deserves, the prayer admits, and we cannot, because we do not know Him; we can only say that, judging from our own existence, He has made something good.

Then the prayer asks for pardon — forbearance for the one caught in the noose of servitude, in the narrow straits of confinement, who sets himself to remember God by means unworthy of Him. We are bound servants, hemmed in by limits we cannot pass; and when we undertake His remembrance, we are forced to do it with words that fall short of Him, because He is worthy only of what we cannot supply. We say what we are able, and where we fall short — which is everywhere — we ask His forgiveness.

The reason this pardon is needed is stated exactly: to take up His remembrance by what is unworthy of Him, under the rule of compulsion, is a sin coupled with the seeking of forgiveness. The phrase is a technical term established by the Du'at — a sin that is paired with the asking of pardon. It is a sin, and yet a sin joined at the root to its own asking of forgiveness; and because it arises from compulsion, it is accepted, for what is compelled is excused.

And the teaching is careful to extend this to every world, not the physical one alone. The forgiveness is needed by all — the First Intellect itself included. This shows that the "sin" is no sin in the ordinary moral sense, for the First Intellect transgressed nothing. Even its luminous act — bearing witness that there is no god but He, pointing to Him with the bare "He" because there is nothing beyond it — even that falls short of His true majesty, and so it too comes coupled with the asking of pardon. From the first intellect down to the tenth, none can do more than this; all are compelled, and all are equal before the Originator whom none can recognize.

The principle of compulsion is sealed by a case judged in the time of Umar. A woman with child was brought before him who said she had no husband. Asked how this had come about, she told of traveling through the desert, dying of thirst across three days, until she met a shepherd who would give her water only if she gave herself to him; near death, she yielded for the water's sake, and afterward bore the child. "Stone her," said Umar. But the Father of Imams arrived, asked what the matter was, and when he had heard it said: "She is compelled, helpless. Let her go, for this happened under compulsion." Where there is true helplessness, the act does not carry the full weight of a sin. So it is, the teaching intimates, with the soul that in its helplessness names the Unnameable: it sins, in a manner of speaking, by speaking inadequately of Him — yet because it is compelled, the sin comes paired with the asking of forgiveness, and it is excused.

## The ten ascending ranks

Having shown that every rank, from the first intellect to the tenth, is equal in its incapacity before the Originator, the teaching closes by naming the ranks themselves — the ladder of spiritual stations by which the soul mounts toward its origin. There are ten of them, set out in ascending order, and they map the great hierarchy of spirituality from its lowest named station to its highest.

The lowest of those named is the Imamate of spirituality, the rank of Ilahiyyah at the foot of its ascending series. From there the ranks rise in order, each a step above the last — Hujjiyyah, then Hijabiyyah, then Khayaliyyah, then Fathiyyah, then Jiddiyyah, then Anza'iyyah, then Qa'imiyyah, then Jussiyyah, then Ahadiyyah, then Samadiyyah — mounting upward and culminating at the summit in Huwiyyah, the rank of the bare "He." Ten ranks in all are set out, ascending from the Imamate of spirituality to the Ipseity, mapping the great ladder by which the soul climbs toward its origin.

There is a quiet rhyme in where the ladder ends. It begins far down, in the stations a soul can name and climb toward, and it ends at the very word in which all naming gives out. The highest rung is not a fuller name but the emptiest one — "He" — the same bare pronoun that was all the First Intellect could find with which to point at the Originator. So the ascent of knowledge arrives, at its summit, back at the confession with which the whole doctrine began: that the One toward whom all this rises cannot be reached by any name richer than a pronoun. The ladder is real, its rungs are ordered, and a soul truly mounts them; but its top is the threshold of the unknowable, not its conquest.

## The hidden pairs

One last matter belongs to this discussion, and it is drawn out of a single word. The "sin coupled with forgiveness" used a term that means, in its root, "paired" — and that word for pairing carries, among the Du'at, a hidden meaning, as do its two companion terms, all three borrowed from the language of prayer. The paired prayer is the one of two units of bowing; the odd prayer is the single, unpaired unit; and the sitting prayer is the one of two units performed seated. Each of these, the teaching says, has an inner referent.

The referent of the paired prayer is the Prophet — peace and blessings be upon him — together with Lady Fatima, for they are the noble pair. The referent of the odd, single prayer is the Father of Imams, the singular one. And the referent of the sitting prayer of two units is Imam Hasan and Imam Husayn together. It is related that Imam Husayn himself, at Karbala, would name these correspondences — that the referent of the paired prayer is the Prophet, that the referent of the odd prayer was his own father, and so onward — and the teaching affirms that these together form a single sign.

So the chapter ends where it would least be expected to end. A discussion that began with the bare pronoun "He" and the impossibility of naming the Originator closes by opening the postures of prayer onto the hidden ranks of the Prophet and his pure household. The same teaching that emptied our names of any grip on the divine essence fills the simplest acts of worship with concealed meaning. What we cannot say of the Originator directly, the tradition says obliquely — folding the Prophet, Lady Fatima, the Father of Imams, and the two grandsons into the very pairing and oddness of the prayers a believer performs each day.

## What this episode lands

The paradox has been carried to its resolution. The First Intellect's first act was to negate divinity from itself and affirm it for Allah alone, and the Qur'an's own testimony of oneness is, at its root, that act. We name the Originator only out of inadequacy, having been given a complete knowledge of life and no knowledge at all of its Giver; and because that naming is compelled, the sin of speaking unworthily of Him comes already joined to the asking of forgiveness, exactly as true helplessness excuses the one who acts under it. Above us rises a ladder of ten ranks whose summit is the bare "He," and even the daily prayers conceal the ranks of the Prophet and his household within their pairings.

With this the opening movement of the volume is complete. We have been given the trust and its method, the path of allegiance and action, the unknowable Originator, and the First Intellect that names Him in its bewilderment. What we have not yet been given is the thing the whole book is named for. We have heard the word "Tawhid" used throughout — the Oneness toward which the path leads — but never once defined. The next movement takes that up directly, and its first move is a shock: that the most natural thing to say about the Oneness of Allah, the thing nearly everyone says, is not Tawhid at all.
