## Where this episode opens

What if the answer almost everyone gives to the most basic question in the faith — what is Tawhid? — were not merely incomplete, but the very error it imagines itself to be refuting?

This episode opens the fourth chapter of the first gate, where the book turns from the unknowable Originator to the ranks through which He is approached. As established earlier, in the teaching on the book's fivefold architecture of canopies, gates, and chapters, the order of the chapters mirrors the ascending order of the realities they unveil; we have now climbed to the chapter where the ranks themselves become the subject. And the chapter begins by administering a correction to our most ingrained notion of divine unity — a correction so sharp that, taken seriously, it remakes what we thought we were affirming every time we said the word. From that single correction an entire science unfolds. We trace it in six moves: what Tawhid actually is; why its object is boundless; its three branches; the two kinds of gnosis by which a rank is known; the thresholds a seeker must cross to reach the first rank; and what it costs to enter that rank at all. Each move turns the familiar word "Tawhid" further from arithmetic and closer to gnosis.

## The gnosis of the ranks

When we are asked what Tawhid is, we answer almost reflexively that it means to consider Allah as one. The book stops us at exactly this reflex and shows that the answer cannot be right. To count Allah as one is already to subject Him to number, and whatever can be numbered as one can in principle be numbered as two or three, or counted as a part of one. To declare Allah one in this sense is therefore not Tawhid at all but its opposite, the gravest of errors, because it imposes a definition and a measure upon the One who admits of no definition and no measure. He cannot be called one, He cannot be called two, He cannot be called three, He cannot be called a part of one. No number applies to Him, and no boundary can be drawn around Him. The very arithmetic by which we thought we were honoring His unity turns out to drag Him down among the countable things.

What, then, is Tawhid? The book reports the saying of the hudud, the ranks of the spiritual hierarchy, peace be upon them: verily Tawhid is the gnosis of the ranks, and tanzih is the negation of divinity from them, and tajrid is the divestment of the names and attributes from Allah the Exalted. The cornerstone is the first clause — Tawhid is the gnosis of the ranks. To affirm the divine unity is not to declare Allah unique, but to recognize each rank, each had of the hierarchy, as utterly unique in its own place: to know that every rank is singular and irreplaceable in its proper station, and to attain the gnosis of where precisely that rank stands, at what level, in what relation to those above and below it. The uniqueness being affirmed is the uniqueness of each rank in its degree; the gnosis of that ordered hierarchy of distinct, singular ranks is what Tawhid actually means. This, the book says, is the greatest thing that can be known.

The ranks did not take their positions arbitrarily. Allah created the hudud and conferred upon each of them authority, saying in effect: use your powers. Each rank, according to its own nature, was given a share of power and the freedom to exercise it, and from the differences that consequently arose among them the ordered arrangement of the hierarchy was established. To know this arrangement — to understand by what gradation one rank stands above or below another — is Tawhid. The hierarchy is not imposed from without; it is the very expression of how each rank used the authority entrusted to it. And because comprehending the whole of this at once is beyond most, Tawhid itself admits of levels. As a person's own station rises, so does the depth of his Tawhid. The Noble Prophet himself indicated as much when he said that none had recognized him except himself and Allah. There are many stations of Tawhid, each answering to the elevation of the knower, which is why this science is named the highest of all knowledge.

## Innumerable beings, innumerable gnosis

Why should the gnosis of the ranks be the highest knowledge of all? Because its object has no measure. Take the human body as an analogy. Within it are some three hundred and sixty parts, and each organ has its own quality and its own degree of excellence, one greater than another; to have full gnosis of the body would mean knowing every one of these parts in its proper rank and worth. Now extend that to the whole order of existence. All the beings that Allah created — and they are innumerable — passed through their stages until they attained human perfection. The true gnosis, the real Tawhid, is to know each of these beings: when and how it reached its perfection, at what station it now stands, how it relates to all the rest. Since the beings are innumerable, the gnosis answering to them is likewise innumerable, and the intellect is left bewildered before the sheer immensity of what might be known. As much as one is able to attain, one attains, and no more.

From this immensity follows a sober and entirely practical consequence. For salvation, the question is precisely which of the ranks' gnosis a person manages to acquire, and how much. As much gnosis as you are able to attain within your lifetime, accordingly will your station be established; and according to that station, the corresponding hierarchy will be set in place within your spiritual form. It is through knowledge and action together — through what you come to know and what you enact — that this inner arrangement is fixed. A person's eventual rank in the spiritual world is not arbitrary; it is the precise measure of the gnosis he attained and lived. And what, in the end, is salvation? It is a good end, to finish well, to close one's life upon the good — and that good end is itself the fruit of the gnosis and action gathered along the way.

This raises a delicate question about agency. Do we attain gnosis by our own doing, or is it made to happen to us? The teaching holds both together without flinching. The effort you yourself expend — by that effort you do genuinely attain gnosis; you strive and you acquire. But upon that effort there also descends the grace of those who stand above you, so that gnosis is also bestowed upon you from above. Neither alone suffices: there is the labor that is yours to perform, and there is the grace given from a higher hand, and gnosis is the meeting of the two. We should recall, too, what the very word for the ranks signifies — a limit. Every rank has a boundary it cannot pass, and to know that final point to which a given rank ascends is itself part of the gnosis. So the limit of the Prophet's ascent is the station of being a veil; the limit of Janab-e Fatimah is the station of the Hujjah; the final point of Janab-e Abd al-Muttalib is the station of the Jadd — and that station is not a single point but comprises ten degrees, so one must further know at which of those ten a given soul is found. To attain the gnosis of these limits and their internal gradations is a very great science indeed. This is Tawhid.

## Tawhid, tanzih, and tajrid

The opening saying named three things, and Tawhid in its full statement has three branches, each of which must be taken in turn. The first is Tawhid itself, already established: the gnosis of the ranks.

The second is tanzih, defined as the negation of divinity from the ranks. To perform tanzih is to declare transcendent, to declare pure, and specifically to deny that divinity resides in the hudud. We must be exact about what "divinity" means here, for the word carries more than one sense. What is intended is the power of origination — the power to bring a being into existence from nothing. Tanzih negates this power from the ranks: not one of them possesses the power to originate a being. Not even the First Intellect, the highest of all created things, possesses it. All of them, without exception, are themselves created, and the created cannot originate. There is a second sense in which a higher rank becomes a "god" for the one bewildered before it, but that sense belongs to a later episode; for now it is enough to fix the meaning relevant to tanzih — the creating power belongs to none of the ranks, and so divinity, in the sense of origination, must be negated from them all.

The third branch is tajrid, the divestment of names and attributes from Allah. The root of the word is the stripping of bark from a tree; here it means to strip away from Allah every name and every attribute, for no name can truly be predicated of Him. Even the name "Allah" cannot strictly be His, for it too is a name, and He is beyond all names. Out of sheer necessity we are reduced to calling Him "He" — and even in saying it we confess our helplessness, for we have no word adequate to call upon Him. This is the predicament established earlier: the very act of naming Him is, as it were, a sin already coupled with the asking of forgiveness, a fault that comes accompanied by its own pardon, because man is helpless and must call upon his Lord by some name or else deny the Existent altogether. So tajrid is the negation, from Allah, of all the names and attributes whatsoever that might be ascribed to Him. The book then notes of the whole three-branched saying that it is a summary — an encompassing statement that stands in need of detailed exposition. And the rest of the chapter is that exposition.

## Outer gnosis and inner gnosis

The exposition begins by dividing the gnosis of the ranks itself into two kinds. There is an outer gnosis, by which the person of a rank and his station are recognized — the knowledge by which one knows who a given rank is and where he stands. Its distinguishing feature is striking: in this outer gnosis the friend and the adversary stand on perfectly equal footing. The devoted disciple, the bitter enemy, and the indifferent stranger all perceive the same outward person; all of them see how tall he is, the shape of his face, whether he wears a beard, what his hands and feet are like. In this respect there is no difference between the lover and the foe. The outer gnosis is by no means a trivial knowledge — it too is difficult to attain in its fullness — but its mark is precisely that it makes friend and enemy equal, because it concerns only the outward person and station.

The second and far higher kind is the inner gnosis, and this is specific to the people of gnosis from among the people of truth. Its object is the very beginning of the existence of a rank. When did the existence of a given rank begin? Where was it created, and from whom? Here the book opens, in compressed form, the long and difficult account of the origin of the ranks: how the hudud were brought into being, how the first call was issued and not answered, how a second call was made, and a third, and a fourth, until in each room they were called in turn and one must know in which room each rank finally made its confession. This is a very long story whose full telling belongs elsewhere; but the point is that the gnosis of this beginning — of where and from whom and through how many summons a rank came into being — is the inner gnosis, and such knowledge belongs to the Imam al-Zaman, peace be upon him.

The inner gnosis does not concern the beginning alone. It comprises also the rank's end, and where its return and final terminus lie. To know the inner reality of a rank is to know the whole arc of its essence: at what point that essence was established, and at what level a person stood when he passed from this world. Was he in the ninth degree of the believer, or the eighth, or the seventh? Did he ascend from one rank to the next, and the next? At precisely what station did his existence come to rest, and to what return is it destined? This is a gnosis of extreme difficulty, reachable only by the disciplined and the verifiers — those who have purified themselves and proven the truth within themselves. And then comes a statement of the utmost severity: for one whose gnosis of this inner reality is deficient, there is no Tawhid at all. Without the inner knowledge of the beginning, the level, and the return of a rank's essence, a man may possess no true Tawhid whatever. It is at this point that the author announces his intention — to clarify something of this matter, as a help for his brethren in the gnosis of the Oneness of Allah.

## The thresholds and the closest rank

The exposition proper begins with a foundational principle: the closest of the ranks to us is the Mukathir, and whatever is established of the lower ranks is established also of the higher. Two things are set down here at once. First, the rank nearest to ordinary humanity, the one through which we make our first true contact with the spiritual hierarchy, is the Mukathir. Second, there is a rule of proportion that will govern much of what follows: whatever holds for the lower rank holds, raised to its degree, for the higher. What is said of the Mukathir applies in its measure to every rank above him, and it works in both directions, for a higher rank contains the lower within himself — a higher rank was once, in his own place, a Mukathir, before he was anything more.

But before reaching the Mukathir, one must pass through two preliminary levels that stand below the ranks proper. The first is the Mustajib, the responder, the one who accepts. The Mustajib is the one who takes the noble pledge, who declares with his tongue, "I accept the noble pledge," and binds himself by that word — whether or not his heart and his deeds have yet caught up with his tongue. His defining mark is precisely this profession with the tongue: that he affirms the Imam of the time and commits himself to that affirmation. The Mustajib has ten levels, graded by the intensity with which the pledge is taken — the sharpness, the force, the keenness of the one taking it. Some come to the pledge as casually as one comes to a marriage; others take it with burning intensity; according to the intensity of the one pledging, his level is established, and so there are ten degrees of the noble pledge.

The second preliminary level is the Mu'min, the believer, whose classical definition the book gives directly: profession with the tongue, affirmation with the heart, and action with the limbs. The believer is the one in whom all three are joined — he professes outwardly, he affirms inwardly, and he acts in his deeds. The believer, too, has ten levels of his own. And it must be emphasized that neither the responder nor the believer falls within the ranks proper. They are below the hierarchy, the necessary thresholds across which one approaches it, but not yet of it. It is only after these — only with the Mukathir — that one truly enters the ranks at all.

## Breaking the self

With the Mukathir we cross the threshold into the ranks themselves. He is the first rank of the hierarchy, the rank the Prophet's transmitted saying placed closest to us, and the meaning of his name is illuminating. The word derives from breaking, for the Mukathir is the one who breaks with his proofs. But whom does he break first? Not his opponents — he must first break his own self. This breaking of the self is the indispensable beginning. To perform it is to subject one's every word, every action, and every intention to a single relentless examination: is this statement I am uttering in accordance with the command of Allah and the Prophet? Is this deed I am performing in accordance with their command? Is this very thought passing through my mind in accordance with it? To bind one's self entirely to that measure — to make oneself captive to the command in speech, deed, and thought alike — is what it means to be a Mukathir.

The logic is exacting. Only when a man has broken his own self can he break others. If he has set himself in order upon this matter, then he is able, with his proofs, to break the one who stands before him. But if he could not break his own self — if he indulged it, telling it that this is permissible and that is permissible, granting himself license — then by what standing could he break another? He has none. And from this follows a strict requirement: for the Mukathir, the acquisition of the sciences is indispensable. Without knowledge no Mukathir can be formed, for he must first know the command of Allah and the Prophet in order to test himself against it. Only once he knows the command can he recognize that some word of his fell short, that some deed diverged, that some thought strayed. Having first judged and broken himself by this knowledge, he is then able to break the one before him with his proofs. And by the rule already established — what is fixed for the lower is fixed for the higher — this same description holds, in its proper degree, for every rank of the hierarchy above him.

## What this episode lands

This episode has overturned the question it began with. Tawhid is not the counting of Allah as one, which would subject Him to number and so betray Him; it is the gnosis of the ranks, the recognition that each rank is singular in its own place and the knowledge of exactly where it stands. Because the beings are innumerable, this gnosis is the highest of all knowledge, and a person's eternal station is the precise measure of how much of it he attains and enacts — through his own effort meeting the grace that descends from above. The full saying carries three branches: Tawhid as the gnosis of the ranks, tanzih as the negation of divinity from them, and tajrid as the stripping of names and attributes from Allah. The gnosis of the ranks divides into an outer knowledge, in which friend and enemy are equal, and an inner knowledge of a rank's beginning, level, and return, without which there is no Tawhid at all. And the road into the ranks runs through two thresholds, the responder and the believer, to the first rank proper, the Mukathir — who becomes what he is only by first breaking his own self against the command of Allah and the Prophet.

## Closing

We have entered the ranks at their lowest rung and learned the discipline that admits a man to it. But the book has only hinted at something stranger: that the Mukathir and the ordinary man may be made of the very same flesh and blood and bone, stand on the very same ground, and yet be separated by the whole distance between ascent and ruin. The next episode takes up that paradox — how one body can become a house fit for the descent of revelation while another, identically composed, declines below the beasts — and reads the difference out of a single act: the establishing, or the abandoning, of prayer.
