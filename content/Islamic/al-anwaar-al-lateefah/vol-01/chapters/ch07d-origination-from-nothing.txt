## Where this episode picks up

What was there before anything was there — and how can a whole world come from a "where" that does not exist?

The previous episode climbed the ladder of bewilderment up to the First Intellect and found it, too, perplexed before the One who originated it. This episode turns from the gnosis of the ranks to the act that produced them: origination itself. Drawing now on a long exposition by Sayyidna Mohyuddin, the book asks the most radical question available to thought — how does anything at all come to be from nothing whatsoever? — and answers it across six teachings: the difference between origination and ordinary making; why the Originator originated at all; the luminous forms He brought forth; the perfect equality in which He made them; how a whole world could exist in a single instant; and how the potential and the actual came into being together, with no interval between them. The thread is the strangeness of a beginning that rests on nothing, owes nothing, and waits for nothing.

## Origination, not making

The book first fixes a distinction that governs everything to follow: the distinction between origination and ordinary creation. Ordinary creation is what occurs when the arrangement of already-existing things is changed — when a pre-existent thing is broken or reshaped and a new composite form is brought forth from it, the form altered while the underlying matter is presupposed. Origination is something else entirely: it is bringing-forth from nothing, where no prior thing exists at all. This is the act by which the world of origination came to be.

The book describes that world with a citation: Allah the Exalted, toward whom the thoughts dare not turn and toward whom imaginations and inmost selves cannot rise, originated the world of origination in a single instant — not from any thing preceding it that would be a prior matter for it, nor with any thing accompanying it that would be similar and like to it, nor upon any thing bearing it that would be a first place for it. The Being who did this is such that the passing thoughts dare not turn toward Him, and the imaginations and the inmost selves cannot rise or grow lofty toward Him; our thought and our inner being alike fall back, unable to ascend to Him. And the world He originated is alluded to by a whole cluster of names — the world of command, the world of intellect, the world of sanctity, the world of purity, the world of subtlety, and the spiritual world — seven names in all for the single originated realm. To make the bringing-forth-from-nothing unmistakable, three negations are pronounced. First, it was originated not from any thing that preceded it — there was no prior substance to serve as its prime matter. Second, not with any accompanying thing — there was no co-present matter alongside it from which it was shaped, nothing it could resemble. Third, not upon any thing bearing it — there was no pre-existing substrate to serve it as a place. No prior matter, no concurrent matter, no underlying place: the world of origination was brought forth in a single instant, from nothing whatsoever.

## Pure favor, not need

Why did the Originator originate at all? The book gives the answer: He originated the existents as a world — existence drawn from a non-existent nothingness — as a favor from Him, and generosity, and bestowal. He brought them into a world of existence out of a nothingness that was not itself existent. And what is this nothingness? It is not some world or realm; it is simply the absence of being. The book recalls how, in Sindh, a teacher once asked his students: when all this was taking place, where were we? — the point being precisely that there was no "where," for there was nothing but non-existence from which we were drawn. The question has no answer because it has no subject; we were not anywhere, because we, and every place, were not yet at all.

And the cause of the origination was no need and no compulsion, but pure favor. The book grounds this in Mohyuddin's words: since it is of the nature of the Generous and the Powerful to bestow favor, and of the nature of the Mighty and the Great to be generous — not needing them, nor playing with them, since He is exalted and transcendent above that. It is of the very nature of the Generous and the Powerful to bestow; it is of the very disposition of the Mighty and the Great to give. The point turns on the word "nature." A generous one gives not because the recipient has earned it or because the giver gains by it, but because giving is simply what generosity does; the favor flows from the giver's own character, not from any lack in him that the gift would fill. He brought the existents into being not because He needed them — the Giver had no need of them whatsoever — nor as idle sport, not toying with them or creating them to no purpose, for He is exalted far above any such thing.

The book is careful to close off both of the wrong answers at once. To say He created out of need would make Him dependent on His creation, which contradicts His transcendence; to say He created for amusement would make the creation purposeless, which contradicts His wisdom. On the question of why the Originator created, the book notes, Sayyidna Mohyuddin wrote chapter after chapter, negating every possible purpose and refuting one supposed motive after another, until he arrived at the single conclusion that this favor was itself the only "purpose": that He originated out of sheer favor, generosity, and bestowal, and nothing else. The origination flowed from the bestowing nature of the Generous and Mighty, free of need and free of frivolity — a gift given for no reason beyond the goodness of the Giver.

## The luminous forms

The book next describes the manner in which the originated beings were brought forth. He brought them into existence as forms — purely imaginal, pure, and luminous — possessing thousand-fold statures like the human, corporeal stature, distinct from their material souls. They were forms, and these forms were purely imaginal, pure, and luminous; they possessed statures of a thousand-fold measure, like the human bodily stature.

But the book is careful to set the direction of the likeness rightly, and the correction is important. It is said that the originated forms were like the human bodily stature only to make the matter intelligible to us; in reality it is the other way around. The human bodily stature is patterned upon those originated forms, not they upon it. When the Originator created, He created in such a state that the human statures which later came into the world were fashioned on the pattern of those primordial luminous forms. So although we are taught that the originated forms resembled the human stature, the truth is that the human stature was made on the model of the originated forms. And these luminous forms are distinct from the material souls that came later; those souls did not yet exist, for the souls belong to a later stage. The forms that were originated were luminous imaginal forms; the souls, when they were formed, were a fallen, degraded condition derived from these; and the bodies of flesh and blood, when they were formed, were a still more fallen condition. The forms were luminous subtleties; the bodies are dark densities. The soul is a fallen form of the luminous original, and the body a yet more fallen form — so that the order of our experience, body then soul then spirit, exactly reverses the order of reality, which descends from luminous form to soul to body. We meet ourselves at the bottom of a staircase and must climb back up; what feels to us like the foundation, the solid body, is in truth the farthest-fallen rung, and the luminous form we can scarcely imagine is the original from which we came.

## Created equal as justice

The book now sets out the doctrine of the first perfection. He brought those luminous forms into existence equal in the first perfection — which is existence, life, strength, and ability — with no disagreement in them, no preference, no distinction, and no inclination in what was subtle or great, much or little, as an act of justice from Him, the Mighty and Majestic. He made the luminous forms equal in this first perfection. The first perfection is the original state in which Allah created them, and it consists of four things: existence, life, strength, and ability — which the book glosses as knowledge and power together. In this first perfection the forms were created perfectly equal: no disagreement among them, no preference of one over another, no distinction, and no inclination — neither in what is subtle nor in what is great, neither in much nor in little. The Originator created the first exactly as He created the last; there is not the slightest difference among the originated beings in the state of first perfection. And this equality was an act of justice from Him.

The book grounds the principle in Mohyuddin's words: since the justice of the Just is equal, and the grace of the Gracious is universal. The justice of the Just is equal in its distribution, and the grace of the Gracious is universal in its reach; therefore the originated forms were created in perfect equality. This is anchored in scripture, in the verse, "And if it had been from other than Allah, they would have found in it much contradiction" (chapter 4, verse 82). Had the originated order proceeded from any other than Allah, much contradiction would be found within it, for only the perfect justice of Allah could produce a creation without disparity. The word for "contradiction" carries the sense of difference or discrepancy, and the verse declares that had the matter come from another, such discrepancy would abound. But because Allah created it, He showed His creation this kindness: He gave to none more and to none less; what He gave, He gave equally. This, the book notes, is the very verse that answers the question raised earlier — whether the equal creation of all beings is attested in the Qur'an — and it is the very reality affirmed by Sayyidna Hamiduddin in the seventh path of the fourth form of his Rahat al-'Aql, who taught that in the abode of origination and the abode of emanation there is no obstacle, because they are free of the matters that obstruct, being pure forms attached to no matter that would prevent them from action.

## Existence in a single instant

That last citation from Hamiduddin furnishes the reason the whole originated order could come into being at once. In the abode of origination and the abode of emanation, there is no obstacle. And why is there no obstacle? Because these abodes are free of the obstructing matters — the kinds of matter that would impede are simply not present there. The forms are abstracted from such matter; they are pure forms attached to no matter, with no matter in them. Matter is what obstructs: wherever matter is present, hindrances and impediments arise. But here there is no matter at all to halt the forms from action. There is power and ability present, and these can show their effect freely; no obstruction has been set in their way that would prevent the second action or impede the attainment of the second perfection.

From this the conclusion follows. Since there is no obstacle, the existence of the existents of these abodes came to be not in time but in a single instant — all at once, in one moment, like the illumination of the expanse of the air by the light of the sun, not in time; and like the lighting of a dark house by a fire in a single instant, not in time. Two images make this vivid, and both are chosen precisely because light, unlike a built thing, does not assemble itself part by part. The first is the spreading of light through the expanse of the air from the sun: when the sun is present, the whole air is illuminated at once, not piece by piece in time. The light does not travel across the room like a workman laying bricks; it is simply there, everywhere, the moment its source is. The second is the lighting of a dark house by a fire: the moment the fire is kindled, the entire room is filled with light at once, not corner after corner. So too the whole world of origination came into existence in a single instant — like air flooded with sunlight, like a dark house suddenly illumined. There was no sequence, no first part finished before a second began; the act and its whole effect were one. This is the deepest reason origination differs from ordinary making: a craftsman works in time because his matter resists him and must be shaped step by step, but where there is no matter to resist, there is nothing for time to measure.

## No interval between perfections

The book draws out the implication of this simultaneity. In that abode, no thing's existence preceded another's. The existence of the intellects subsisting in actuality did not come before the existence of the intellects subsisting in potentiality; rather, the existence of all was together. The "intellects in potentiality" are, in effect, the powers that were given to the intellects, which were afterward actualized and established; their existence was not preceded by any prior existing thing. The existence of all was together, at one and the same time: both the intellect subsisting potentially and the intellect subsisting actually existed simultaneously.

And this carries a momentous consequence: there was no interval of time separating the first perfection from the second perfection. The two perfections were not divided by any passage of time; the potential and the actual co-existed from the outset, so that no temporal gap stood between the initial state of equality and the higher state to be attained. The book grants that some span of time is needed for our understanding to follow the matter — even the kindling of a fire seems, to us, to take some time before the room fills with light. Our minds are creatures of sequence, and we cannot help laying out, one after another, what in reality happened at once. But the light in question here is not like ordinary light that spreads gradually; it is such that the instant it is lit, the whole is filled at once, and this is the character of the first perfection. What lies between the first perfection and the second, then, is not time at all but something else — a difference that the next stage of the teaching will have to name, since it cannot be a difference of when. If the two perfections do not stand apart in time, and yet they truly differ, then the difference between them must be of another order altogether than before and after.

## What this episode lands

This episode has set out the act behind everything the book has taught so far. Origination is not ordinary making: ordinary making reshapes pre-existing matter, but origination brings forth from nothing — not from a prior thing, not with an accompanying thing, not upon any underlying place. The Originator did this not from need or for sport, but from sheer favor and generosity, the only "purpose" being the giving itself. What He brought forth were luminous imaginal forms, of which the human body is the far-fallen copy, not the model. He made those forms perfectly equal in the first perfection — existence, life, strength, and ability — as an act of justice, attested by the verse that only a creation from Allah could be free of discrepancy. And because the abode of origination holds no obstructing matter, the whole order came to be not in time but in a single instant, like air flooded with sunlight; the potential and the actual existed together, with no interval of time between the first perfection and the second.

## Closing

We have watched a whole world flash into being, equal and luminous, with no gap between its first state and its destined second. But equality, the book has hinted, is not yet perfection — and a state that is equal can also be a state that goes wrong. The next episode asks what "error" could mean for beings created equal and faultless, and then tells of the one among them who looked at himself, recognized his Maker, and became the very first to declare that there is no god but God.
