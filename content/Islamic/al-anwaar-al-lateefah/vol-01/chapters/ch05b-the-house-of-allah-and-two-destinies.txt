## Where this episode picks up

How can two men of identical flesh and blood, set upon one and the same platform, end at opposite poles of the universe — one a house fit for the descent of revelation, the other driven below the cattle?

The previous episode left us at the entrance to the ranks, with the first rank, the Mukathir, defined by the breaking of his own self against the command of Allah and the Prophet. This episode follows what that breaking does to a man — not only inwardly but, astonishingly, even in his outward substance — and then sets him beside his opposite to draw the contrast that gives the whole teaching its moral urgency. We move through six teachings: how the Mukathir's very composition is transformed though it looks unchanged; how he becomes a house of Allah and a place where revelation alights; the hidden inner meanings folded inside the pillars of the religion; the two destinies of two men on one platform; the same pattern proved out of the lawful and forbidden animals; and finally how the Mukathir's very soul is transmuted. The thread running through all six is a single insistence: sameness of substance is no guarantee of sameness of fate.

## Same composition, different reality

A profound question opens the second gate. The Mukathir and the ordinary human being are gathered together within the same bodily limits and the same regions; both are composed of flesh, both of blood, both of bones and nerves and veins and all the rest. To outward inspection there is no difference between them: the ordinary man is of flesh and bone, and the Mukathir is of flesh and bone. And yet, the book insists, they are not the same. Despite the identity of their outward composition, an immense difference separates them — and the book refuses to let us locate that difference only on the inside.

The reason is that the Mukathir's temperament has become refined and his essence has become pure, and this through ascending many stations. The difference reaches even the outward. His flesh is no longer simply common flesh, his blood no longer common blood; everything of his has been transformed — and this even outwardly, though others did not perceive it and the ignorant could not discern it. The transformation came about because he ascended through numerous stations that others never attained. Through that refinement and ascent he drew near to the first principle — near, that is, to the very source from which his creation proceeded, so that he is now close to returning there. He reached the highest stations of humanity, and these highest human stations are themselves the first of the spiritual ranks. The summit of the human is the threshold of the spiritual world; the Mukathir stands precisely at that joining-point, where the topmost rung of the human order is the bottommost rung of the angelic. He looks like any other man, and he is no longer like any other man.

## The House of Allah

From this elevation flows the most exalted of the descriptions given to the Mukathir: he is a place of descent for the revelation of Allah, and a house from among His houses. He is the very locus upon which revelation alights, and the book explains exactly why. It is because in his every thought he kept the command of Allah and the Prophet before him; in his every word he kept that command before him; in his every deed he kept it before him. Having made that command the standing presence of his thought, speech, and action, he became thereby a house from the houses of Allah, a place fit for the descent of revelation. The transformation of the previous teaching now bears its fruit: the refined essence is exactly what makes a man habitable by the divine word.

The book anchors this in the Qur'an, in the verse, "In houses which Allah has permitted to be exalted and His name to be remembered therein; in them He is glorified in the mornings and the evenings" (chapter 24, verse 36). Then it establishes that the "houses" of the verse are, in their inner meaning, the ranks — the hierarchy of which the Mukathir is the first. The verse continues, "Men whom neither commerce nor sale diverts from the remembrance of Allah and the establishing of prayer and the giving of alms" (chapter 24, verse 37), and these "men" are these same ranks. What is meant by their "establishing of prayer" is, inwardly, the establishing of the divine summons, the da'wah. The verse's outward language of commerce is illuminated by the parallel passage: "O you who believe, when the call is proclaimed for the prayer on the day of congregation, hasten to the remembrance of Allah and leave off trade. That is better for you, if you only knew. And when the prayer is concluded, disperse in the land and seek of the bounty of Allah, and remember Allah much, that you may be successful" (chapter 62, verses 9 to 10). Those whom the buying and selling of the world does not divert from the remembrance of Allah, from the establishing of prayer, and from the giving of alms — they are the ones who, in the inner sense, establish the da'wah.

## The inner meaning of the pillars

This identification of "establishing prayer" with "establishing the da'wah" opens onto a broader teaching: that each pillar and obligation of the religion carries an esoteric, inner meaning beneath its outward form. The book sets these out in turn, and the cumulative effect is to reveal the whole law as a coded interior discipline.

Wilayat, in its inner sense, is to repent in the world of motes — to turn in repentance in that primordial world of the smallest particles of being. Purity is to cleanse oneself from the filth of Iblis through the knowledge of the Tenth. Prayer is to act upon the commands of the Imam in whose sphere one has arrived and whose summons has reached one — to enact what he has commanded; and this is why, when the prayer is established, it is announced that "the prayer has stood," for the standing of the prayer is the standing-up to enact the command. Alms is to give a portion of the knowledge that has been given to you: when what you have received reaches a certain measure, a small portion of it becomes obligatory to dispense — and just as material alms come in two kinds, the fixed alms owed by every person and the alms that fall due only when wealth accumulates, so some sciences are owed by nature to everyone and must be explained to all, while others are owed only once one's store of knowledge has grown to the threshold that makes a portion obligatory.

The inner meanings continue. Fasting is to conceal knowledge — not to disclose it to the undeserving, to keep it hidden from those unfit to receive it. The fifth is to give a fifth part of knowledge that comes to you unexpectedly; just as, in the outward law, a fifth of an unlooked-for windfall must be given — as Imam Husayn, peace be upon him, used each month to set aside the fifth of his very salary, reasoning that he had not expected such money to come — so likewise, if knowledge descends upon you unexpectedly from the saints of Allah, its fifth part must be given out, propagated, made clear, while the remaining four parts are yours by right. Pilgrimage is the intending toward the House of Allah — and the House of Allah, inwardly, is the Imam al-Zaman, peace be upon him, who is the place of Allah, the inner gate; pilgrimage is to prepare one's whole bearing, with a true heart, to set out toward that inner gate. And struggle in the path of Allah is to take one's own self — rather, to sacrifice it, to slay it — when the command comes; for the greatest struggle is the slaying of the commanding self, so that the true hero, the lion of the battle, is the one who has killed within himself that great beast which is his own soul. The book then seals the point: what Allah intends by the verse is the establishing of the da'wah, which is the very object the outward prayer serves and supports — and the da'wah itself has no standing except through prayer. The outer and the inner are bound together, the one upholding the other.

## Two men on one platform

Now comes the contrast that gives the teaching its moral weight. The book sets the Mukathir beside the man who does not establish prayer, and finds them, at the start, indistinguishable. The "other than him" is the man who is not a Mukathir — the ordinary person, or the opponent. Outwardly he stands in the very same state, on the very same platform: both are of flesh and blood, both inhabit the same human form, both have come into the same world. Yet their trajectories run exactly opposite. The Mukathir is going up; this other is going down. The one draws near to the first principle; the other moves away from it. They are gathered together on a single platform, and from there the one goes this way and the other that — as the book vividly puts it, one sets out for the cinema and one for the mosque, though both started from the same point.

The decisive difference is the establishing of prayer. Where it does not occur — where the man establishes neither the inward prayer nor even the outward — he proceeds away from the first principle, declining toward the last of the human ranks and pushed back beneath even the lowest of them. The book presses the descent to its terminus: such a man is driven below the thousand-fold form, so that he becomes more astray in his path than the cattle, turned onto the crooked path, ending at last in the isthmus of fear. He proceeds, step by step, toward that dreadful terminus. And here the author breaks off into prayer: may Allah keep us, and all our brethren, far from such a matter, for the sake of the master of the prophets and his pure progeny, the blessings of Allah upon them all. The lesson stands stark: two men of identical outward composition, set upon one platform, one establishing the inward prayer and so becoming a Mukathir who ascends, the other establishing neither inward nor even outward prayer, whose temperament and deeds have hardened him so that he descends. The same flesh, the same blood, the same world — and yet opposite ends, because of the establishing or the abandoning of prayer.

## The lawful and the forbidden animals

The book then finds the very same pattern mirrored in the animal kingdom, and draws from it a proof, noting that in the animal forms there is a lesson for one who takes a lesson and insight for one who reflects and contemplates. The argument is simple and devastating. The lawful animals — the cow, the sheep, the camel, and their kind — are identical in outward creation to the forbidden predatory animals. Both are of flesh, blood, bones, nerves, veins, skin, hide, and fur; in their physical composition there is no difference between them. Yet they are not the same, and the proof of their difference lies in their relation to man.

The lawful animals have become food for the human being — for that human who is the caliph of Allah upon His earth. They take intimacy with man, draw near to him, become his food, and thereby, by becoming part of the human, proceed toward ascent and progress upon the straight path. And what is the straight path here? It is the human person himself — the human form, which is close to the first principle that is our origin and our destination. The lawful animal, eaten and assimilated into the human who is the caliph of Allah, ascends through him toward the source. The predatory animals, by contrast, are averse to man, hostile to him; they do not draw near to be his food and so do not ascend through him, but proceed in decline, moving ever farther from the first principle. Thus, though the two kinds are of one and the same created stuff, they are not equal. And just so it is with men: the lawful animal that ascends through intimacy with man is like the Mukathir who rises toward the first principle; the forbidden animal that flees man and declines is like the man who falls. So among human beings the obedient and the disobedient — though they too share one platform, one composition, one world — are separated by a distance greater even than that which divides the two kinds of beast. One rises and one descends, and between them lies the whole interval between the first principle and the furthest distance from it.

## The Mukathir's transmuted soul

The book closes the movement by returning to the inside of the Mukathir, to the soul itself and what flowed into it. What is in question is the sensory soul of the Mukathir. In the ordinary person and in the Mukathir alike there is a sensory soul, yet the two are not the same; the Mukathir's has itself become refined, because of all that he acquired in his ascent. He passed through the ten levels of the responder, and the ten levels of the believer, and then through the degrees of the Mukathir to whatever station he reached — and from all this his very soul took on a subtlety. Then, upon this refined soul, there connected the divine sciences and the spiritual secrets, which perfected it with a total purity, refined it with a spiritual refinement, dyed it with a divine dye, and made it shine, clarified and transparent. He became, in effect, a man possessed of a holy spirit, his sensory soul itself transmuted.

A subtle point of discernment follows. Does it then follow that whenever a soul appears refined, the person before us must be a Mukathir? Not necessarily, for a person's stations may be hidden, known to Allah alone; outwardly it may appear that he is a Mukathir, and his soul indeed be refined, yet the formal designation belongs to Allah. The book resolves the matter from the side of justice: the demand of justice is that if a soul has genuinely become pure, then that person must in fact have become a Mukathir — he will have passed the ten levels of the believer and reached the station of the Mukathir, and his soul's subtlety is the consequence of that ascent. The subtlety need not be outwardly manifest, and the public designation is not necessary; the station rests with Allah. But where the purity of the soul is real, the rank is real, even if it is concealed from all but Allah.

## What this episode lands

This episode has driven home one claim from every angle: identical substance does not mean identical destiny. The Mukathir is made of the same flesh as any man, yet his temperament has been refined and his essence purified by his ascent, until even his outward substance is transformed and he becomes a house of Allah, a place where revelation alights — because he held the command of Allah and the Prophet present in his every thought, word, and deed. The same inward principle governs the whole law: every pillar carries a hidden meaning, and what looks like outward worship is, inwardly, the establishing of the da'wah, which the outward prayer exists to support. Set against the Mukathir, the man who establishes neither inward nor outward prayer shares his platform and his flesh but takes the opposite road, declining below the cattle to the isthmus of fear. The lawful and forbidden animals prove the same logic out of one created stuff — and among men the gap between the obedient and the disobedient is wider still. And inwardly, the Mukathir's very sensory soul is transmuted, dyed with a divine dye, so that where the purity is real the rank is real, though it may be hidden from all but Allah.

## Closing

We have seen one man become a center of light and another decline into ruin, separated by what they did with the command. But the book has not yet told us what happens when a seeker meets such a refined man face to face — when the disciple stands before a teacher who pours out a knowledge his ears had never so much as suspected. The next episode follows that encounter into its strangest turn: the moment the teacher becomes, for the bewildered disciple, a kind of god — and how that very bewilderment, climbed rank by rank, becomes the ladder to the one true God.
