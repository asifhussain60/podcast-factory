# The Unknowable Originator

*Episode two of Al-Anwaar al-Lateefah, Volume One — The Oneness. The opening lesson laid the road home; this one turns to ask what waits at its end — and finds that the One toward whom the whole return is directed cannot be reached by any pointing, any thought, or any name we possess. The teaching draws the hardest line in the whole doctrine: no indication falls upon the Originator, no thought can bridge the gap to Him, and the names and attributes we use so freely slide off Him entirely, because a created thing can never characterize its own creator. Where, then, do those names land? Upon the first thing He originated — the First Intellect — and the incapacity to know Him turns out to be universal, shared by the highest created being and the lowest alike. The episode ends at the strangest place of all: our very word "Allah" is not a description but a cry of bewilderment.*

## Where this episode picks up

How do you point at something that no pointing can ever reach? The previous lesson left us at a doorway — the road of allegiance and action leads the exiled soul back to its origin, and the origin is the One who made it. But the moment we turn to face that One directly, the doctrine does something disorienting. It tells us that everything we would normally use to grasp Him — a gesture, a thought, a name — fails at the threshold. He is not far away in the sense that a distant mountain is far; He is beyond the very faculties by which nearness and distance are measured at all. This episode walks that hard line carefully: why no indication and no thought can fall upon the Originator, why even the names we give Him do not actually touch Him, what they land upon instead, and why, in the end, the word "Allah" on our lips is less a description than the sound of a mind that has come to the end of itself.

## The Originator beyond reach

There is no path even to the mention of these realities, the teaching insists, except by first mentioning Allah; without His mention, nothing can be opened at all. By the mention of Allah every door is unlocked, and by His most beautiful names a means is sought to Him in every request, and success is granted. So the discourse about the Originator begins, as everything begins, with His name. But no sooner has the name been spoken than the teaching states the cornerstone of right belief about the One who bears it — and the cornerstone turns out to be a wall.

He is the One glorified, never the glorifier. It is we who perform the glorification; the act of praise rises from us toward Him and never the reverse. And He is exalted on high — exalted so far that no indication of any indicator can fall upon Him so as to point Him out. This, the teaching says, is the very foundation of correct doctrine: that no indication reaches Him. Point as you like; the pointing finds nothing to land on, because He is not a thing among things that a gesture could single out.

Nor can thought do what the gesture cannot. The image the teaching reaches for is a bridge that cannot be crossed. The thought of the heart sets out toward Him and finds no span wide enough to carry it across the gap; neither imagination nor reasoning ever reaches the far side. We may imagine, and we may reason, but the objective is never attained, because the objective is not the kind of thing that imagining or reasoning can close upon.

What, then, can we honestly claim to know of Him? Exactly this much, and no more: that since we were created, there must be One who created us. The bare fact of our own existence forces the affirmation of an Originator. But what He is in Himself — what His nature is, how He is to be conceived — of this we know nothing whatever. This is where the book's declared method meets its hardest test. Its method is to state pure reality directly, with no veiling interpretation and no symbol — to give us the thing itself rather than a code for it. And the thing itself, when the thing is the Originator, turns out to be precisely what no statement can deliver. The pure reality of God is that He exceeds every reality we are able to state.

## Seen only by insight

This truth — that He cannot be seen or pointed to — was put to the test in a famous exchange from the time of the Commander of the Faithful. A man named Ibn al-Kawwa came to him meaning to trap him with a question he could not answer. "Have you seen your God?" he asked. "Have you seen Allah?" It was meant as a snare with no safe exit: say yes, and you have made Him a visible object like any other; say no, and what kind of worshipper serves a God he has never seen?

The Commander of the Faithful did not flinch. "Woe to you," he answered. "Could I worship a God I had not seen? How could I worship a God without having seen Him?" The man, thinking the trap had now sprung the other way, pressed on: "How, then, did you see Him?" And the answer came: "I did not see Him with the eyes. I saw Him with insight — with the basirah."

It is a mighty saying, and it threads the needle the previous teaching had drawn so tight. The physical eye cannot fall upon Him, any more than a pointing finger or a traveling thought can; on that the doctrine is absolute, and the answer does not retreat from it for an instant. But there is a seeing that is not the eye's. The basirah, the inner sight of the heart, perceives Him — not by grasping His essence, which remains sealed, but by an affirmation so certain that it deserves the name of vision. The worshipper does not behold a form; he sees, with a clarity beyond the senses, that He is.

So the two halves of the teaching hold together without contradiction. No eye sees Him and no indication reaches Him — and yet He is not worshipped blindly, as one might serve a rumor or a guess. He is seen by the only faculty fit for the task, the insight that affirms without circumscribing. The denial of sight and the affirmation of vision are spoken in a single breath, and the seam between them is nothing other than the difference between the outward eye and the eye of the heart. The whole of right worship lives in that distinction.

## Names fall only on their like

A consequence now follows that unsettles the most ordinary religious speech. When we express something about Him — when we call Him "Allah," or "the Originator," or refer to Him by the bare, exalted "He" — none of these names, and no attribute, actually falls upon Him. Neither the name nor the quality lights upon Him at all. The reason is stated with the precision of a rule: names and attributes fall only upon their like. A name applies only to something of its own kind, something already resembling it.

Consider what we are really doing when we name Him. We say "Allah" because we wish to recognize Him, to know Him, and so we assign Him a name — and we assign it from our own tongue. But we are ourselves the makers of that tongue and of the names it forms. The very instrument by which we would interpret the Originator is a thing of our own manufacture. As the Commander of the Faithful said, names and attributes are indicators that point to the named — and an indicator points only to its like. We once observed mercy in some creature and so coined the word "Merciful"; the word fell upon its like, upon a kind of knowledge and arrangement we could actually perceive.

But these names and attributes are themselves originated things — brought into being, and brought into being specifically by human beings. A human pronounces a name; a human assigns it. You named the child; you are the one who gave the name. And here is the crux: the originated thing cannot fall upon its originator as an equal. The made cannot characterize its maker as though the two were of one kind. As Sayyidna Mu'ayyad al-Din observed, since the very letters of the alphabet are originated things, they cannot serve as a true and verifying indicator of the Originator who made them. Words built of created letters cannot reach back to characterize the One who is their source.

An objection rises here, and the teaching meets it squarely. One might protest: it is well enough to say we create our words, but there is a limit to our making — countless things proceed by His command before any word issues from us, so are we truly the makers of anything at all? The reply holds to the precise point at issue. The words I am speaking now — these I am genuinely creating; of these I am the maker. And it is exactly with this handiwork of ours, the tongue and the names it forms, that we are attempting to characterize our own Creator. That is what cannot be done. Our power of choice is real, but it does not extend to recognizing Him; He dwells in an unseen whose curtains we have no power to lift.

## Where the names land

If the names and attributes do not fall upon Allah, the question presses with new force: upon what, then, do they fall? They cannot simply hang in the air. The teaching gives a definite answer. Since the matter stands as it does — since name and attribute are negated from Allah the Exalted — these names and attributes fall instead upon His origination. And what is that origination? It is the First Intellect.

This First Intellect is the first thing He brought forth, and the teaching names it in several ways at once: it is the First Intellect, and it is also the first existent — the first thing that came to be — and the first emanation, the first to issue forth from the originating act. Whatever names and attributes we possess, they are stripped away from Allah and they descend upon this first origination. When we speak, we are in truth speaking of what appeared from His act in His creation, not of any quality belonging to Him directly. All such direct predication is severed from Him; it comes to rest on what He made.

And even here the teaching presses a subtle and sobering point. If the names and attributes do not fall even fully upon His origination — if they sit uneasily even on the First Intellect, the highest and first of all created things — then how could they ever be imagined to fall upon Allah, the Originator Himself? The distance between our words and the First Intellect is already a kind of shortfall. The distance between our words and the Originator beyond it is not a longer distance of the same kind but a gap of another order altogether.

This relocation is one of the quiet hinges of the whole doctrine. It does not leave our religious language meaningless — the names still land somewhere, still do real work, still point truly to the first of created realities. But it moves the whole apparatus of naming off the divine essence and onto the created order. What we describe when we describe "God" is, strictly speaking, the highest thing He made. The Originator Himself stands behind even that, affirmed but never described, indicated but never reached.

## An incapacity that spares none

The teaching now widens its hardest claim until it covers everything that exists. The incapacity to know the Originator is not a peculiar weakness of human beings, to be cured by sharper minds or longer study. It is comprehensive. It embraces the entire world of intellect and the entire world of sense together — the realm of pure intelligences above and the realm of bodies below, with nothing left standing outside its reach.

This means the First Intellect itself, the loftiest of all created things, shares the incapacity fully. It cannot know the Originator either. And if the first and highest creature cannot, then plainly neither can we, far down the order; we are simply equal to it in this one respect. The teaching states the equality without softening it: in the matter of knowing the Originator, the powerful and the overpowered are level, the sensed and the intelligible are level, the world of intellect and the world of body stand on perfectly even ground. The mightiest intelligence and the dullest lump of matter are exactly as far from comprehending Him — which is to say, infinitely far.

There is a strange consolation buried in this severity. The inability to grasp the Originator is no mark of our particular failure, no deficiency we might have avoided with more diligence. It is the condition of being a creature as such. To be made is to be unable to comprehend the Maker; the limit is built into the very structure of created existence, not into our share of it alone. The First Intellect, flooded with every light a creature can hold, reaches the very same wall we reach with our far dimmer portion.

That leveling matters for everything that is to come. The doctrine is about to build an immense hierarchy of ranks, an ordered ladder of spiritual stations rising one above another. But before a single rung is laid, the teaching plants a floor beneath the whole structure: with respect to the Originator, every rung is equal, because every rung is created, and no created thing of any height can close the distance to the One who originated it. The ladder measures the heights creatures reach toward each other and toward Him; it never measures any nearer approach to His unknowable essence.

## The cry of bewilderment

What, then, is happening when we say the word "Allah"? The teaching gives an answer of remarkable depth. Our saying "Allah" — our expressing Him by this name — arises from the bewilderment of the First Intellect before the Originator. The Intellect, contemplating the One who made it and finding no name adequate to Him, is overcome; and the word "Allah" issues out of that bewilderment. It is not a label fastened onto a known object. It is the sound a mind makes when it has reached the end of what it can know and still must speak.

In this, once more, all are equal. The Imam is equal in it; the ardent lover is equal in it; whoever stands bewildered before Him reaches for the same word. "Allah" is the common cry of every creature that has come up against the unknowable and confessed it. The most exalted knower and the most fervent seeker are united in having, at the very last, only this one word left to them.

The teaching unfolds this through the image of a circle. Picture the Originator as the center, the fixed point about which a circle turns. Why does the circle turn at all? Because it is bewildered about its center — it cannot rest in a comprehension it can never reach, and so it revolves. One circle wheels around its center in bewilderment; then another circle comes, bewildered about its own center; every circle turns, and the reason is always the same. And here is the heart of the image: the very energy by which the circle revolves is its bewilderment. The motion is not something added to the perplexity; the motion is the perplexity itself, made visible as a turning.

So the whole created order circles eternally about a center it cannot comprehend, and the circling is nothing other than its unceasing wonder before the Originator. Our word "Allah" is one note of that turning. We do not name Him as we name a thing we have caught and examined; we name Him as the circle names its center — by wheeling around a point we cannot touch, and calling out, from the motion itself, the one word that confesses we cannot stop.

## What this episode lands

The hard line is now fully drawn. No indication falls upon the Originator, no thought bridges the gap to Him, and the names and attributes we use slide off Him entirely, because what is made can never characterize its maker. Those names come to rest instead upon the First Intellect, the first thing He brought forth — and even there they sit uneasily, so that they never reach the Originator at all. The incapacity to know Him is not ours alone but the common condition of everything created, the highest intelligence as much as the lowest body. And our very word "Allah" is, at bottom, the cry of a bewildered creation circling a center it cannot reach.

But this raises a question the next episode must take up directly. If the First Intellect cannot know the Originator, and yet it is the First Intellect that first speaks of Him — that first negates divinity from itself and affirms it for Allah alone — then by what right does it name Him at all, and what does that naming cost? The next lesson turns to the primal act of the First Intellect, to the strange teaching that to name the Unnameable is itself a kind of sin, and to the mercy by which that unavoidable sin comes already joined to its own forgiveness.
