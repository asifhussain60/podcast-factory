# What Tawhid Really Is

*Episode four of Al-Anwaar al-Lateefah, Volume One — The Oneness. We now enter the fourth chapter of the first gate, and the teaching opens by overturning the most ordinary answer to the most basic question. Asked what it means to affirm the Oneness of Allah, almost everyone replies that it means to consider Him one. The hudud — the ranks of the spiritual hierarchy — teach otherwise, and indeed they must, for to count Allah as one is already to subject Him to number. This episode lays out what Tawhid actually is: the gnosis of the ranks, a knowledge so vast its object is innumerable, by which a soul's very station is established. It then opens the threefold saying — Tawhid, tanzih, and tajrid — that governs the whole treatise.*

## Where this episode picks up

Ask almost anyone what it means to declare that Allah is one, and the answer comes back without hesitation: it means He is a single being, not two and not three. This chapter says that answer is not merely incomplete — taken as a definition of Tawhid, it is the gravest error a person can commit. To count Allah as one is to make Him a thing that can be counted, and whatever can be counted as one can in principle be counted as two, or reckoned a part of some larger whole. The instinctive answer, then, smuggles number and measure into the very place where number and measure can never reach. So the chapter begins by clearing the ground, and the question it leaves standing is the one the whole volume has been building toward: if Tawhid is not the counting of Allah as one, then what on earth is it? The answer, when it comes, relocates the entire enterprise — away from the divine essence, which cannot be grasped, and onto the ordered ranks of the spiritual world, which can.

## What Tawhid is not

The chapter opens by citing a saying transmitted from the hudud, the ranks of the spiritual hierarchy: that Tawhid is the gnosis of the ranks, that tanzih is the negation of divinity from them, and that tajrid is the divestment of names and attributes from Allah the Exalted — adding at once that this is a summary statement still in need of unfolding. But before any unfolding, a correction is administered to our most ingrained habit of thought.

When we are asked what Tawhid is, we answer almost reflexively that it means to consider Allah as one. The teaching denies this flatly. To count Allah as one is already to subject Him to number, and whatever can be numbered as one can in principle be numbered as two or three, or counted as a part of one. To declare Allah one in this sense is therefore not Tawhid at all but shirk, the gravest of errors, because it imposes a definition and a measure upon the One who admits of no definition and no measure. He cannot be called one; He cannot be called two; He cannot be called three; He cannot be called a part of one. No number applies to Him, and no boundary can be drawn around Him.

The Father of Imams pressed exactly this danger in his great sermon on the divine unity, where each step of describing God is shown to drag Him downward toward limit and number:

> Whoever attaches attributes to Him has limited Him, and whoever limits Him has numbered Him, and whoever numbers Him has denied His eternity. — Ali ibn Abi Talib, the Father of Imams, *Nahj al-Balagha* (compiled by al-Sharif al-Radi), Sermon 1, trans. Sayyid Ali Reza.

To number Him is to deny His eternity — for whatever is counted is bounded, and whatever is bounded had an edge at which it began. The reflexive "Allah is one," taken as a tally, commits precisely this denial: it slips a beginning and a boundary into the One who has neither.

This is a hard saying, and it is meant to be. The most pious-sounding formula in the believer's mouth — "Allah is one" — turns out, if it is taken to mean a numerical unit, to do precisely what the believer most wants to avoid: it makes the Creator into a member of a class, an item that could be tallied alongside others. The whole difficulty of speaking about an Originator who exceeds all speech, which the earlier chapters confronted, returns here in a sharper form. If even the word "one" misfires, then the affirmation of unity must mean something other than what it appears to mean. The chapter now supplies that other meaning.

## The gnosis of the ranks

What, then, is Tawhid? It is the gnosis of the ranks — ma'rifat al-hudud. This is the cornerstone teaching of the entire treatise. To affirm the divine unity is to recognize each had, each rank of the spiritual hierarchy, as utterly unique in its own place. It is not Allah who is here being declared unique; rather, every rank is unique in its proper station, and the gnosis of where precisely that rank stands — at what level, in what relation to those above and below it — is what Tawhid actually means. To know that ordered hierarchy of distinct ranks, each singular and irreplaceable in its degree, is the greatest thing that can be known.

The ranks did not assume their positions arbitrarily. Allah created the hudud and conferred upon each of them authority, saying, in effect: use your powers. Each rank, according to its own nature, was given a share of power and the freedom to exercise it, and from the differences that consequently arose among them the ordered arrangement of the hierarchy was established. The hierarchy is not imposed from without; it is the very expression of how each rank used the authority entrusted to it. To know that arrangement — to understand by what gradation one rank stands above or below another — is Tawhid.

It follows that Tawhid admits of levels, for not every person can comprehend the whole of it at once. The Noble Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) himself indicated as much when he said that none had recognized him except himself and Allah. As a person's own station rises, so too does the depth of his Tawhid; there are many stations of Tawhid, each answering to the elevation of the knower. And since this is so, the supreme rank of this science is established: the knowledge of Tawhid is the highest of all knowledge. To recognize the station of each and every rank, to know where each one stands, is the most exalted of all knowing.

That this knowing is the very road of salvation, and no mere ornament of the learned, the Prophet made plain in a saying the tradition has never let go of:

> Whoever treads a path in search of knowledge, God makes easy for him thereby a path to Paradise. — Prophet Muhammad, *Sahih Muslim*, Kitab al-Dhikr wa al-Du'a, no. 2699.

The path of gnosis and the path to the homeland are not two roads but one: to set out into the knowledge of the ranks is already to be walking home. This is why the science can be called the highest — not because it is the most admired, but because it is the one that carries the soul across.

This relocation of Tawhid deserves to be felt for the shift it is. The reflexive answer placed the whole weight of the affirmation upon the divine essence — it asked the believer to make a statement about Allah Himself, namely that He is numerically one. The teaching moves that weight entirely onto the ranks. The believer is no longer asked to pronounce upon the essence, which cannot be grasped and which the earlier chapters showed to lie beyond all indication and thought; he is asked instead to come to know the ordered hierarchy of created realities. Tawhid thus becomes something a creature can actually do — for the ranks can be known, gradually and truly — whereas a verdict upon the unknowable essence is something no creature can deliver at all. The affirmation of unity is not abolished; it is redirected to the place where a human knower can honestly stand.

## Innumerable beings, innumerable gnosis

The reason Tawhid is the highest of knowledge follows from the boundlessness of its object. Consider the human body as an analogy. Within it there are some three hundred and sixty parts, and each organ, each member, has its own quality and its own degree of excellence, one greater than another. To have full gnosis of the body would mean knowing each of these parts in its proper rank and worth. Now extend this to the whole order of existence. All the beings that Allah created — and they are innumerable — passed through their stages until they attained human perfection. The true gnosis, the real Tawhid, is to know each of these beings: when and how it attained its human perfection, at what station it stands, how it relates to all the others.

This is the very limit of gnosis, and it is a limit that recedes endlessly. Since Allah created innumerable beings, the gnosis answering to them is likewise innumerable, and the intellect is left bewildered before the sheer immensity of what may be known. As much as one is able to attain, one attains, and no more. There is no completing this knowledge; there is only the journey deeper into it. The very vastness that makes Tawhid the highest science also makes it a science no creature exhausts.

It is worth pausing on how decisively this reframes the believer's task. The reflexive formula — "Allah is one" — could be uttered in a moment and considered finished. But Tawhid as the gnosis of the ranks is the work of a lifetime and beyond, an ocean whose far shore no one reaches. The believer is not asked to pronounce a verdict on the divine essence; he is set walking through a hierarchy of realities, learning one rank and then the next, with the horizon always opening further ahead.

## Salvation by rank

From this immensity follows a sober and practical consequence for every seeker. For salvation, the question is precisely which of the ranks' gnosis a person manages to acquire, and how much. As much gnosis as you are able to attain within your lifetime, accordingly will your station be established; and according to that station, the corresponding hierarchy will be set in place within your haykal, your spiritual form. It is through knowledge and action together that the arrangement within the haykal is fixed. A person's eventual rank in the spiritual world is not arbitrary; it is the precise measure of the gnosis he attained and enacted in this life. And what, in the end, is salvation itself? It is a good end — to finish well, to close one's life upon the good. That good end is itself the fruit of the gnosis and the action accumulated along the way.

This raises a delicate question about the agency by which gnosis comes. Do we attain gnosis by our own doing, or is it made to happen to us? The teaching holds both together. Scripture fixes the reality of the seeker's own labor in the plainest terms:

> And that man shall have nothing but what he strived for. — Quran 53:39 (al-Najm), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

Nothing is had but by striving; the gnosis a soul carries is the gnosis it worked for. The effort you yourself expend — by that effort you do attain gnosis; you genuinely strive and you genuinely acquire. But upon that effort there also descends the grace of those who stand above you, so that gnosis is also given to you from above. Neither alone suffices. There is the labor that is yours to perform, and there is the grace that is bestowed, and gnosis is the meeting of the two. A man who only waits for grace acquires nothing; a man who only strives, cut off from the grace of those above him, also falls short. The seeker must work as though everything depends on his effort, and receive as though everything depends on the gift.

This twin balance also guards the doctrine against two opposite distortions. If gnosis came by effort alone, salvation would be a purely human achievement, earned and owed, and the ranks above would be superfluous — a man could climb to the homeland by his own exertion, owing nothing to anyone. If gnosis came by grace alone, effort would be pointless, and the seeker could only wait, passive, for what might or might not descend. The teaching closes off both. The grace is real and indispensable, so that no one ascends by his own power apart from those above him; and the effort is real and indispensable, so that no one ascends without striving. The station a soul attains is therefore neither a wage it has earned outright nor a gift dropped upon an idle recipient, but the precise fruit of a labor that was met, at every step, by a grace from above.

## The limits and their stations

We should also recall what the word hudud itself signifies. A had is a limit. Every rank has its limit, a boundary it cannot pass beyond, and to know that limit — the final point to which a given rank ascends — is itself part of the gnosis of the ranks. The science is not only a matter of placing each rank above or below another; it is a matter of knowing exactly how high each one reaches.

The chapter gives concrete examples to fix the point. The limit of ascent of the Prophet is the station of being a veil. The limit of Janab-e Fatimah, the Mother of the Imams, is the station of the Hujjah. The final point of Janab-e Abd al-Muttalib is the station of the Jadd — and that station is not a single point but comprises ten degrees, so that one must further know at which of those ten a given individual is found, the seventh being lower and the higher being other than it. To attain the gnosis of these limits, and of their internal gradations, is a very great science indeed. This is Tawhid: not a single pronouncement about God, but a detailed map of where each rank stands and how far it rises.

The examples also quietly correct a tempting simplification. One might suppose that knowing the order of the ranks meant only memorizing a list, top to bottom. But each station itself opens into further stations — the Jadd alone holding ten degrees — so that the map is not flat but layered, and the work of Tawhid is to know not merely the rungs of the ladder but the gradations within each rung. The closer one looks, the more there is to know, which is exactly why this knowledge has no end.

## Tanzih and tajrid

The opening saying named three things, and Tawhid in its full statement has three branches. The first is Tawhid itself, which we have now established: the gnosis of the ranks. The second is tanzih, which the saying defines as the negation of divinity from the ranks. To perform tanzih is to deny that divinity resides in the hudud. We must be precise about what "divinity" means here, for the word carries more than one sense. What is intended in this context is the power to bring a being into existence from nothing — origination. Tanzih negates this power from the ranks: not one of them possesses the power to originate a being. Not even the First Intellect possesses it. All of them, without exception, are themselves created, and the created cannot originate. There is a second sense in which a higher rank becomes a "god" for the one bewildered before it, and the chapter will unfold that sense in due course; but for tanzih, the meaning is fixed — the creating power belongs to none of the ranks, and so divinity, in the sense of origination, must be negated from them all.

The third branch is tajrid, the divestment of names and attributes from Allah the Exalted. The root of the word is the stripping of bark from a tree; here it means to strip away from Allah every name and every attribute, for no name can truly be predicated of Him. The Qur'an itself sets the bar that every name then fails to clear:

> There is naught like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing. — Quran 42:11 (al-Shura), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

If nothing whatever is like Him, then no word drawn from the world of likeness — and every word we own is drawn from precisely that world — can land upon Him. Even the name "Allah" cannot truly be His, in the strict sense, for it too is a name, and He is beyond all names. Out of sheer necessity we are reduced to calling Him "Hu" — He — and even in saying "Hu" we confess our helplessness: we have no word adequate to call upon Him. This predicament is a sin coupled with a plea for forgiveness. The very act of naming Him is a kind of fault, yet it is a fault that comes already accompanied by its own forgiveness, because Allah understands the helplessness of His servant — that the servant has no word fit to call upon Him, and that by whatever name he calls, the calling will be accepted and the plea for forgiveness granted along with it. So tajrid is the negation, from Allah, of all the names and attributes whatsoever that might be ascribed to Him.

The three branches, taken together, form a single coherent posture before the Real. Tanzih and tajrid clear away two opposite errors. Tanzih forbids us to push divinity downward, onto the ranks, by imagining that some created being possesses the power to bring things into existence; tajrid forbids us to drag the Originator down into our speech, by imagining that our names and attributes can truly land upon Him. The one denies that any creature is a god; the other denies that the one God can be captured in any word. And between these two negations stands the single great affirmation that is Tawhid itself — the positive knowledge of the ranks in their order. The believer does not merely empty his speech of false attributions and stop there; having denied origination to the ranks and names to Allah, he fills the space that is left with the patient, lifelong knowing of where each rank stands. Negation clears the ground, and gnosis builds upon it.

## What this teaching lands

The ground is now cleared and the foundation laid. Tawhid is not the counting of Allah as one — that, taken as a definition, is the very shirk it means to escape. Tawhid is the gnosis of the ranks: the recognition that each had is unique in its place, and the knowledge of where it stands and how high it rises. Because the beings are innumerable, this gnosis is the highest and most inexhaustible of all knowledge, and a soul's own station is established by exactly how much of it he attains — through his own effort meeting the grace given from above. And the full saying has three branches: Tawhid, the gnosis of the ranks; tanzih, the denial that any rank possesses the power to originate; and tajrid, the stripping of every name and attribute from Allah. What remains for the seeker is to walk the road these three branches open — to deny origination to the ranks, to deny names to the Originator, and then to spend a lifetime coming to know, rank by rank, the ordered hierarchy in which each created reality stands. That patient knowing, met at every step by grace from above, is the whole of the work, and its far shore is the good end toward which the soul is travelling home.
