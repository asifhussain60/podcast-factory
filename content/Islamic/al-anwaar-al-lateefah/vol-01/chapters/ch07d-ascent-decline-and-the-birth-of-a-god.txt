# Ascent, Decline, and a God

*Episode seven of Al-Anwaar al-Lateefah, Volume One — The Oneness. The teaching now draws the contrast that gives the whole doctrine its moral urgency. The Mukathir and the man who is not a Mukathir stand on a single platform, made of the same flesh — yet one ascends toward the source of his being and the other declines below the level of the beasts, and the decisive difference is the establishing of prayer. The same pattern is then found in the animal kingdom, where lawful and forbidden creatures of identical make part ways by their nearness to man. The episode turns inward to the Mukathir's transmuted soul, and then reaches the heart of the chapter: the moment a bewildered seeker, overwhelmed by his teacher's knowledge, finds that the teacher has become for him a "god" — in one precise and carefully limited sense.*

## Where this episode picks up

Two men stand on the same platform, made of the same flesh and blood, born into the same world — and one of them is rising toward Allah while the other is sinking below the level of cattle. What separates them? The teaching has just shown how the Mukathir's very substance is refined though he looks like any other man, and how he becomes a house in which revelation alights. Now the teaching sets that refined man beside his exact outward double — the ordinary man, or the opponent — and shows that identical composition is no guarantee of identical destiny. From the same starting point the two set out in opposite directions, and the whole difference turns on a single thing. The episode follows that contrast through the world of men and the world of animals alike, and then carries the seeker into the bewilderment that is the beginning of his ascent.

## Two men, one platform

The teaching draws the contrast in a long passage about the man who is not a Mukathir: that this other person, even though he is in the very same state in which the Mukathir is, has a temperament that is unrefined and an essence that is impure, and is proceeding in decline, in distance from the first principle, toward the last of the human ranks, pushed back to what is below it. The "other than him" is the man who is not a Mukathir — the ordinary person, or the opponent. Outwardly he stands in the very same state, on the very same platform, as the Mukathir: both are of flesh and blood, both inhabit the same human form, both have come into the same world.

Yet their trajectories are opposite. The Mukathir is going up; this other is going down. The one is drawing near to the first principle; the other is moving away from it. They are gathered together on a single platform, and from there the one goes this way and the other that way — as the teaching vividly puts it, one is going to the cinema and one is going to the mosque, yet they set out together from the same point. The image is almost comic in its ordinariness, and that is its force: the parting of two eternal destinies looks, from the outside, like nothing more than two men leaving the same doorstep in different directions. Nothing in the platform itself, nothing in the shared flesh, tells you which man will rise and which will fall. The difference lies not in what they are made of but in which way they have turned.

This is the moral counterpart of the previous metaphysical lesson. There we learned that the Mukathir and the common man share an identical outward composition while belonging to different orders of being; here that same shared composition becomes the stage for a moral drama. The identity of the platform is not a comfort but a summons: precisely because the two men begin from the same point, with the same materials and the same opportunity, each is fully answerable for the direction he takes. No one can plead that he was made of baser stuff, or set upon a lower platform, than the man who rose. The starting line is common to all; what is not common is the turning, and it is the turning, not the stuff, that decides where a man ends.

## The parting of prayer

The decisive difference is the establishing of prayer. Where the establishing of prayer does not occur — where the man does not establish the inward prayer, and indeed in such a case does not even establish the outward prayer — he proceeds away from the first principle, declining toward the last of the human ranks and being pushed back beneath even the lowest of them. The Mukathir, who establishes the inward prayer that is the standing-up to enact the command, ascends; the other, who establishes neither the inward prayer nor even its outward form, descends. The hinge of two destinies is this single act, taken in its full inner sense.

That prayer should be the very hinge on which a destiny swings is not a claim peculiar to this teaching; the Prophetic tradition makes the abandonment of prayer the boundary line of faith itself:

> Between a man and disbelief and idolatry stands the abandonment of prayer. — Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, narrated by Jabir ibn Abdullah, *Sahih Muslim*, Kitab al-Iman, no. 82.

The hadith draws the same line the teaching draws, only from the outward side: to let prayer fall is to step across the frontier that separates a believer from one who has lost his faith. What the teaching adds is the inner depth of that frontier — that the prayer whose abandonment is so grave is, in its reality, the standing-up to enact the command, and that the man who lets it fall does not merely lose a ritual but loses the very motion by which a human being ascends.

The teaching presses the descent to its terminus. The man who fails to establish prayer is pushed below the thousand-fold form, so that he becomes more astray in his path than the cattle, turned onto the crooked path, ending at last in the isthmus of fear. This is the very judgment the Qur'an passes on the heedless, and in almost the same image:

> They have hearts with which they understand not; they have eyes with which they see not; and they have ears with which they hear not. Such are like cattle. Nay, they are even further astray. It is they who are heedless. — Quran 7:179 (the chapter of the Heights), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

The verse and the teaching arrive at one place: the man who has the outward equipment of a human being — heart, eyes, ears — and yet does not use them to understand, to see, to hear the command, sinks below the beasts, for the beast at least fulfils what it was made for. To be more astray than cattle is precisely the terminus toward which the abandonment of prayer drives a man. He proceeds, step by step, toward that dreadful terminus — and the master breaks off into prayer that Allah keep us, and all our brethren, far from such a matter, for the sake of the master of the prophets, upon him and his pure progeny be peace and blessings. The lesson stands stark. Two men of identical outward composition, set upon a single platform, one establishing the inward prayer and so becoming a Mukathir who ascends, the other establishing neither inward nor even outward prayer, whose temperament and deeds have hardened him so that he descends below the beasts. The same flesh, the same blood, the same world — and yet opposite ends, because of the establishing or the abandoning of prayer. To be made of human stuff is not yet to be safe; the human form is a platform from which one may climb to the spiritual world or fall beneath the animals, and the establishing of prayer is what decides the direction.

## The lesson of the animals

The teaching next finds the same pattern of ascent and decline mirrored in the animal kingdom, and draws from it a proof: that in the animal forms there is a lesson for one who takes a lesson, and insight for one who reflects and contemplates. This echoes the Qur'an's own habit of pointing to the cattle as a sign to be read:

> And truly in the cattle there is a lesson for you. — Quran 16:66 (the chapter of the Bee), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

The Qur'an bids the reflective eye find an ibrah — a crossing-over lesson — in the very beasts that feed us; the teaching takes up that invitation and reads the lesson all the way down. The argument unfolds with care. The lawful animals — the cow, the sheep, the camel, and their kind — are identical in outward creation to the forbidden predatory animals. Both are of flesh, blood, bones, nerves, veins, skin, hide, and fur; in their physical composition there is no difference between them. Yet they are not the same, and the proof of their difference lies in their relation to man.

The lawful animals have become food for the human being — for that human who is the caliph of Allah upon His earth. They take intimacy with man; they draw near to him, become his food, and thereby, by becoming part of the human, proceed toward ascent and progress upon the straight path. And what is the straight path here? It is the human person himself — the human form, which is close to the first principle that is our origin and our destination. The lawful animal, eaten and assimilated into the human who is the caliph of Allah, ascends through him toward the source. The predatory animals, by contrast, are averse to man, hostile to him; they do not draw near to be his food and so do not ascend through him, but proceed in decline, moving ever farther from the first principle. Thus, although the two kinds of animal are of one and the same created stuff, they are not equal — and just so it is with men. The lawful animal that ascends through intimacy with man is like the Mukathir, the pure human who ascends toward the first principle; the forbidden predatory animal that flees man and declines is like the man who falls. So among human beings the obedient and the disobedient — though they too share one platform, one composition, one world — are separated by a distance greater even than that which divides the two kinds of beast. One rises and one descends, and between them lies the whole interval between the first principle and the furthest distance from it. With this the fifth chapter of the first gate is brought to its close.

## The transmuted soul

The second gate now opens with a closer examination of the soul of the Mukathir — how it became transformed and what flowed into it as a result. The teaching speaks of the sensory soul, the soul of perception that the ordinary person and the Mukathir alike possess. Yet the two are not the same, for the Mukathir's sensory soul has itself become refined, because of all that he acquired in his ascent to that rank. He passed through the ten stations of the Mustajib, and through the ten of the Mu'min, and then through the degrees of the Mukathir to whatever level he reached — and from all this his very soul took on a subtlety.

Then, upon this refined soul, there connected the divine sciences and the spiritual secrets, which perfected it with a total purity, refined it with a spiritual refinement, dyed it with a divine dye, and made it shine, clarified and transparent. He became, in effect, a man possessed of a holy spirit, his sensory soul itself transmuted. This is the inward face of the transformation whose outward face the teaching has already described. There the Mukathir's flesh was refined though it looked unchanged; here it is his very soul of perception that is dyed and made luminous by the divine sciences flowing into it. The two go together: a man who has climbed through all those stations and held the command before him at every step does not merely acquire knowledge as an external possession — the knowledge enters and remakes the soul that receives it, until the soul itself shines with the light of what it knows.

The Father of Imams named knowledge in just this way — not as an ornament laid upon the soul but as its very life and light:

> Knowledge is the life of the hearts and the light of the sight. — Ali ibn Abi Talib, the Father of Imams, *Ghurar al-Hikam wa Durar al-Kalim* (compiled by al-Amidi), among the maxims on knowledge.

If knowledge is the life of the heart, then the soul into which the divine sciences flow is not merely informed but quickened — brought to a higher life and lit from within. That is exactly the transmutation the teaching describes: the sensory soul dyed luminous by what has entered it, so that to receive the divine sciences is not to be furnished with a possession but to be made newly alive.

## Where purity is real

A subtle point of discernment follows. Does it then follow that whenever a soul appears refined, the person before us must be a Mukathir? Not necessarily, for a person's stations may be hidden and known to Allah alone. Outwardly it may appear that he is a Mukathir, and his soul indeed be refined, yet the formal designation belongs to Allah. We cannot read off a man's rank from the surface and pin a public title upon him; that judgment is not ours to make.

And yet the teaching resolves the matter from the side of justice. The demand of justice is that if a soul has genuinely become pure, then that person must in fact have become a Mukathir — he will have passed the ten stations of the Mu'min and reached the station of the Mukathir, and his soul's subtlety is the consequence of that ascent. The subtlety need not be outwardly manifest, and the public designation is not necessary; the station rests with Allah. But where the purity of the soul is real, the rank is real, even if it is concealed from all but Allah. This holds together two things that might seem to pull apart. On the one hand, we must not go about assigning ranks by appearances, for the station belongs to Allah's knowledge. On the other hand, the connection between a purified soul and a true rank is not loose or accidental: a genuinely pure soul is the genuine fruit of a real ascent, so that the rank is truly there wherever the purity is truly there — present and real, though hidden from every eye but the eye of Allah.

The discernment matters because it keeps the doctrine honest in both directions. It refuses the credulity that would crown every impressive soul with a public title, for the outward signs can be misread and the station is not ours to confer. But it equally refuses the cynicism that would treat the ranks as bare names with no reality answering to them, as though "Mukathir" were merely an honorific bestowed by men. The teaching insists that the rank is a real fact about a real soul, grounded in a real ascent — only that the fact is hidden, lodged in the knowledge of Allah rather than displayed for our verdict. So the seeker is taught neither to appoint ranks nor to doubt that ranks exist, but to trust that where a soul has genuinely been purified, a genuine station stands behind the purity, whether or not any human being is permitted to name it.

## The birth of a god

We now reach the heart of the chapter — the precise sense in which a higher rank becomes a "god" for the one beneath him. The scene is that of teacher and disciple. When a seeker comes to the Mukathir with sincerity, soundness of intention, and purity of nature, and converses with him, the Mukathir bestows upon him the spiritual speech and the divine knowledge — knowledge so sublime that it had never before crossed the seeker's heart, nor had his ears or the ears of his likes ever so much as suspected it. Confronted with such elevated realities, the disciple's intellect is bewildered and he is astounded: he cannot fathom how his teacher attained this station and these sublime matters. In this bewilderment the Mukathir becomes for him a god, a master of his neck, and he recognizes the teacher's guidance and his great precedence.

It is essential to grasp precisely the sense in which the Mukathir is here called a "god," for this is the second meaning of the word that was deferred in the opening of the chapter, where divinity in the sense of origination was denied to every rank. He is a god in the sense that he is the center of the circle. Around the center the circle revolves, and that revolving is the very motion of bewilderment; the disciple revolves around his teacher as a circle revolves around its center, drawn into orbit by his astonishment. It is in this sense alone — as the center around which the bewildered seeker circles — that the Mukathir is a "god" for him. It is emphatically not in the sense that he is the creator of beings; the power of origination was negated from all the ranks at the very outset. He is "god" only as the center of bewildered orbit, and the master who holds the seeker's neck for its release — the one whose guidance, greatness, and precedence the seeker has come to recognize. The word, so carefully qualified, is not a lapse into associating partners with Allah; it is the name for a real relation between a dazzled student and the teacher whose knowledge has overwhelmed him, and it is the first rung of a ladder that ascends, rank by rank, toward the One.

That a seeker should need such a master — should be held by the neck and drawn into orbit rather than left to find the way alone — is the settled wisdom of the path. Rumi put the necessity of the guide as starkly as it can be put:

> Whoever travels without a guide needs two hundred years for a two-day journey. — Rumi, *Mathnawi*, Book III, trans. Reynold A. Nicholson.

The bewilderment that fixes the disciple upon his teacher is, read this way, not a snare but a mercy: it binds the dazzled seeker to the one who can carry him across in two days the distance he would otherwise wander for two centuries. The danger is only that he mistake the guide for the goal — and against that danger the teaching has already set its guard, fixing the precise and limited sense in which the teacher is a "god" and reserving origination to Allah alone.

It is striking that the teaching should be willing to use so dangerous a word at all. To call any creature a "god" is, on its face, the very thing the whole doctrine of tanzih forbade — the placing of divinity upon a rank. But the teaching does not flinch from the word; it disciplines it. By fixing its meaning precisely — center of a turning circle, master of the seeker's neck, object of his bewildered orbit, and emphatically not the creator of his being — it shows that the bewilderment which makes a teacher into a "god" is not an error to be stamped out but an experience to be understood and then surpassed. The seeker really does experience his teacher this way, and pretending otherwise would falsify the path. The remedy is not to deny the bewilderment but to follow it upward until it discloses the One before whom even the highest teacher is himself bewildered.

## What this teaching lands

The doctrine of the ranks has now been given its moral edge. Two men of identical flesh stand on one platform, and one ascends toward the first principle while the other is driven below the level of the cattle — the difference being nothing in their composition but only the establishing of prayer in its inner sense. The same logic is written into the animal kingdom, where lawful and forbidden creatures of one created stuff part ways by their nearness or aversion to man, the obedient and the disobedient among men separated by a distance greater still. The Mukathir's sensory soul, meanwhile, has been dyed luminous by the divine sciences flowing into it; and where such purity is genuinely present, a true rank is genuinely present, though it rests hidden with Allah. And at the heart of it all stands the bewildered disciple, for whom his overwhelming teacher has become a "god" — not a creator, but the center of the circle around which his astonishment turns. From here that bewilderment is to be followed upward, rank by rank, until it reaches the First Intellect bewildered before its Originator — and the seeker, by negating divinity from every rank and affirming it for Allah alone, performs the true act of Tawhid.
