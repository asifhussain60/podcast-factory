## Where this episode picks up

If a being is created perfect and faultless, what could it possibly mean for it to commit an error?

The previous episode left the luminous forms standing equal in the first perfection, with no interval of time between that state and the higher perfection awaiting them. This episode asks what could go wrong in such a state — and discovers that "error," here, has a meaning unlike any we are used to. Then it follows the pivotal moment of the whole cosmology: the instant one of those forms looked at itself, recognized its Maker, and became the very first to perform Tawhid. We move through five teachings: the meaning of error in the first perfection; the form that performed Tawhid; how it reached toward its Maker through His own greatness; the descent of the divine Word into it; and how, through that Word, the First Intellect came to encompass all knowledge — but only after first negating itself. The thread running through all five is a single, almost paradoxical law: the highest gift is given only to the one who first confesses that he is not the giver, and the knowledge of everything comes only to the one who first admits he is nothing of himself.

## Error in the first perfection

The book, following Mohyuddin, now explains what the state of first perfection does and does not include. By it is meant the state of the beings' first existence and first perfection, in which they are all equal, as already established — and yet, along with that equality, in the beginning of their collective existence, in action and deed, they are not infallible from error. This is the crucial qualification. The state of first perfection, for all its perfect equality, is not the perfect, protected state. The truly perfect state is the second perfection; the first perfection is the lower, initial condition, and in it the beings, though equal, are not preserved from error — error can proceed from them.

And what is this "error"? The book is precise: the error consists in failing to attain the second perfection. That is the error — a delay in responding to the invitation, a refusal to come when called, and, beyond that, the misleading of others, the setting of others upon the wrong path. All of this can issue from beings who are in the state of first perfection, for in that state they are not infallible. We must hold this carefully against everything the previous teaching established. The forms were created perfectly equal, with no preference and no disparity, as an act of justice; one might suppose that such equality was already the summit. But the book insists it was not. Equality is the starting line, not the finish. The first perfection was given as a test: go straight, and use rightly the powers given to you. The state is not yet the state of return-perfected; it is the trial-state of equality from which one must journey, by the right use of one's powers, to the second perfection. Justice required that all set out from the same point; what each does from that point is no longer a matter of justice but of effort, and it is there that error becomes possible.

The book notes Mohyuddin's prayer at this juncture — may Allah protect us from slipping ourselves or from being made to slip — and pauses over its grammar to drive the meaning home. There is a difference between "he slipped" and "he made another slip," as there is between "he cut" and "he had another cut," or "he prostrated" and "he made another prostrate"; likewise between going astray oneself and leading another astray. The forms of the words shift in a fixed way: one form means to do a thing, the other to cause another to do it. The prayer therefore asks protection from both faces of the one "error" that is failure to attain the second perfection: that we ourselves fall short of it, or that we cause others to fall short. So error here is not a sin against a law, but a failure to complete a journey — and a failure that can be either one's own or one inflicted on another. With this prayer the book moves to the next section, where the journey is at last completed by one who does not slip at all.

## The form that performed Tawhid

The book now narrates the pivotal moment. One of those imaginal forms looked, with his essence, at his essence and at the children of his kind; so he knew that they had an Originator unlike them; so he negated divinity from himself and from the children of his kind, and affirmed it for their Originator, the Exalted, and bore witness to His oneness. This is the heart of the matter. One of the luminous forms looked — with his own essence — at his own essence, and then at his kind, the children of his own genus. He saw himself, and he saw all his fellows.

And from this looking he knew that there was, for himself and for them, an Originator unlike them — one different from them all, who had brought them into being. The logic is exact: a being that finds itself among equals, none of whom can account for the existence of the rest, is driven to recognize a Maker who is none of them. It is worth dwelling on how the recognition arises, for the book makes the looking itself the engine of the discovery. He did not receive a report that he had a Maker; he reasoned his way to the Maker by examining what he was. Looking at his own essence, he found a being that did not give itself existence; looking at his kind, he found the same lack in every one of them. And from the universal lack he inferred the One who supplies it — an Originator unlike them, since none like them could be its source.

Having known this, he negated divinity from himself and from the children of his kind; he could not claim, "I am the god," nor could any of his kind make that claim. And he affirmed divinity for their Originator, the Exalted, and bore witness to His oneness. In this single act he performed all three branches at once — Tawhid, tajrid, and tanzih together — and that very form became the Possessor of Tawhid, the first to declare the divine unity. By looking at his own essence and his kind, by recognizing therein an Originator unlike them, by negating divinity from all that was originated and affirming it for the Originator alone, he enacted the whole of Tawhid in its threefold completeness. The Tawhid that the seeker in the earlier episode climbed toward, rank by rank, was first performed here, at the dawn of existence, by a single form looking honestly at what it was. The whole later ladder, with its bewildered circles, is a re-enactment, in time and through teachers, of this one timeless act of honest self-examination.

## Seeking the means of His greatness

What did this first form do after his act of recognition? He acknowledged for the Originator His Lordship, glorified Him, was reverent, humbled himself, submitted, sought means through His greatness toward Him, and supplicated. He acknowledged the Originator's Lordship, glorified Him, was reverent, humbled himself, and submitted. And then, most importantly, he sought means through the Originator's own greatness toward Him, and supplicated.

This is the special point on which the book dwells. The first form took the very greatness of the Originator as the means by which to reach the Originator. The greatness of the Originator cannot itself be reached directly; therefore the form took that greatness as his means of approach. There is a fine paradox here that the book wants us to feel. The same greatness that makes the Originator unreachable is what the form uses to draw near; the obstacle becomes the road. One cannot scale the height, but one can lean upon it. Through the means of that greatness he gave testimony to the Originator's Lordship and divinity, confessing his own lowliness. He did not presume to approach directly, for direct approach is impossible; rather, he made the Originator's greatness itself the intermediary by which he turned toward Him. This is what it is to seek the means: to take the greatness of the Originator as the means to the Originator, when the Originator Himself cannot be reached. The recognition of an unreachable Maker does not end in paralysis; it turns into a movement of approach that uses the only thing available — the Maker's own greatness — as its road. And this, the book has already shown, is the seed of the whole later doctrine of seeking the means through the chosen ones: the pattern by which a creature reaches the unreachable through a greatness not its own is set here, at the very first act of worship in existence.

## The descent of the Word

The consequence of this seeking of means was a great descent of confirmation upon the first form. There reached him from his Originator the divine supports and the confirmations of oneness, and there connected with him the flowing knowledge and the spreading light, which is the Word of Allah, the Exalted. There reached him from his Originator the divine supports and the confirmations of oneness; and there connected with him the flowing knowledge and the spreading light — and this flowing knowledge and spreading light is the Word of Allah. And what is that Word? It is "there is no god but Allah." This is the essence of the whole matter: through the first form's act of negation, recognition, and seeking-of-means, there flowed into him the flowing knowledge and spreading light which is that Word — the Word standing between the Originator and the originated, through which knowledge and light flow.

The book gives the Word a precise station: it is said that the Word is above the intellect and below the Originator, and it is also named the command. The Word "there is no god but Allah" occupies exactly this intermediate place — beneath the Originator and above the intellect — and the flowing light came from the Originator down to it, and through it to the intellect. This middle position matters. The Word is not the Originator, for nothing is on His level; and it is not the intellect, for the intellect receives it rather than producing it. It hangs between them as the channel down which everything passes, the one rung of the ladder that touches both what is above and what is below. The Word is thus a continuous link, in which the Originator's will and the declaration of His oneness are manifested together. And the same Word is also given the name "the command," for all the commands are placed within it; every directive that will ever govern the creation is folded into this single declaration. It is no momentary thing; it is a link established for as long as creation endures. As long as the creation remains, the Word remains, and the flowing light remains. The book observes, too, that this is why the world of origination ended at seven: the First Intellect came to be, and the Originator made seven, the tenth being the last; the world ended at seven because the will of the Originator was that it should end at seven, for He had placed everything within the Word and willed that it terminate there.

## The First Intellect encompasses all

Through the Word the First Intellect attained a knowledge that encompassed all things. Through it he encompassed all things in knowledge, and knew what was and what will be. For the Word "there is no god but Allah" contains everything — all that has happened and all that will happen. But there is a crucial sequence here that the book underscores, and it overturns any picture of knowledge as a simple deposit. The complete knowledge had, in a sense, already been given with the first perfection; yet the First Intellect did not recognize it, did not know how to use it. What the light of the Word supplied was the understanding of what he had been given and the way to use the powers he possessed. Knowledge he had; what he lacked was the knowledge of his own knowledge, and the key to its use. The treasure was already in the house, but the lamp by which to find it, and the hand by which to spend it, came only with the Word. This is why the powers given in the first perfection are not enough on their own: a being may possess every faculty and still remain inert, until it learns both what it holds and how to wield it.

And before he could obtain the Word and its light, he had first to negate himself — to affirm that he was not his own creator. Just as the first form had said, in effect, "I am not my creator; our creator is someone else," so the First Intellect had first to confess that he and all his kind were not their own creators, but that their Creator was another. The moment he said this — the moment of self-negation — he obtained the Word, and in the light of the Word all things were obtained by him. Self-negation precedes the gift: one must first own one's not-being-the-creator before the Word and its all-encompassing light are bestowed. This is the inner reason the negation comes first in the Word itself: "there is no god" must be uttered before "but Allah," because the creature must empty itself of every false claim before the true affirmation can be lodged in it. The order of the syllables is the order of the soul's ascent.

And the book records what the First Intellect then found within his own essence: such joy and delight and happiness and protection and praise and greatness and pride that no pen could write its description and no tongue could translate its account. From the light of the Word he knew that after death there would be salvation, and he found within himself a joy beyond all writing and all speech — the overflow of a creature that has at last understood both what it is and what it is not. The same act that looks, from the outside, like the bleakest of confessions — I am nothing, I made nothing — turns out, from the inside, to be the doorway into a happiness no language can hold. The creature that lets go of the claim to be its own source receives, in exchange, the knowledge of everything and a delight beyond all telling.

## What this episode lands

This episode has named the strangest term in the book's cosmology and then shown the act it made possible. "Error," in the first perfection, is not the breaking of a law but the failure to complete a journey — falling short of the second perfection, whether by one's own delay or by leading others astray. Against that possibility stands the first triumph: one luminous form looked at itself and its equals, recognized an Originator unlike them all, and in one act negated divinity from everything originated and affirmed it for the Originator alone, becoming the first Possessor of Tawhid. Unable to reach his Maker directly, he reached Him through His own greatness, and in answer there descended upon him the divine supports and the flowing light that is the Word, "there is no god but Allah" — the link that stands below the Originator and above the intellect, also called the command. Through that Word the First Intellect came to encompass all that was and will be — but only after first negating himself, confessing he was not his own creator, which is the condition on which the whole gift, and a joy beyond all telling, was poured into him.

## Closing

We have heard the Word descend and seen the First Intellect filled with all knowledge and unspeakable joy. But the book is not finished with that Word: it will show that its very letters are numbered, that the same numbers run through the opening formula of every chapter of the Qur'an, and that the more the First Intellect knew, the more he trembled. The next episode reads the hidden arithmetic of the Word, the fear that belongs only to the learned, and the supreme station to which the First Intellect was finally raised — veil, gate, and Greatest Name — closing on the one circle no creature has ever entered.
