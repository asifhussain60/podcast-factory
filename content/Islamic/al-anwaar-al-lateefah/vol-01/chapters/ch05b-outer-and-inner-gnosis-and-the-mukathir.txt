# The Two Gnoses and the Mukathir

*Episode five of Al-Anwaar al-Lateefah, Volume One — The Oneness. Having established that Tawhid is the gnosis of the ranks, the teaching now divides that gnosis in two. There is an outer knowing, by which a rank's person and station are recognized — a knowing in which the devoted friend and the bitter enemy stand on equal footing. And there is an inner knowing, reserved for the people of truth, which reaches to the very beginning, the level, and the return of a rank's essence — and without which, the teaching warns, a man may have no Tawhid at all. From there the episode names the two thresholds a seeker crosses before the ranks proper — the Mustajib and the Mu'min — and the first rank within the hierarchy, the Mukathir, entry to which begins with the breaking of one's own self.*

## Where this episode picks up

How can a man's most devoted disciple and his sworn enemy know him equally well? It sounds impossible, and yet the teaching insists that in one whole register of knowledge the lover and the hater of a saint stand on exactly the same footing — they see the same face, the same stature, the same outward bearing, and neither has the least advantage over the other. The teaching has established that to affirm the Oneness of Allah is to know the ranks of the spiritual hierarchy in their order. But that knowing, it now turns out, is not all of one kind. There is a knowing that an adversary can possess as fully as a friend, and there is a knowing that no adversary can touch at all — a knowing so deep that its absence empties a man's Tawhid of all reality. This episode draws that line, and then walks the seeker up to the threshold of the ranks themselves.

## Outer gnosis of the ranks

The teaching cites that the gnosis of the ranks is of two kinds. The first is the outer gnosis, by which the person of a rank and his station are recognized — the knowledge by which one knows who a given rank is and where he stands. Its distinguishing feature is that in this outer gnosis the friend and the adversary stand on equal footing. The lover, the enemy, and the indifferent alike perceive the same outward person: how tall he is, whether his face is round, whether he wears a beard, what his hands and his feet are like. The devoted disciple sees this, and the bitter enemy sees this, and in this respect there is no difference between them.

This is no trivial knowledge. The outer gnosis is itself difficult to attain in its fullness; to know a rank truly even in his outward person and station is a real accomplishment. Its mark is not that it is easy but that it is shared — friend and foe are made equal in it, because it concerns only the outward person and the outward station, the things that any pair of eyes can register. A man may know all of this with great precision and still stand entirely outside the deeper knowledge, just as an enemy who studies a saint closely may describe his every feature and yet be his enemy still. The outer gnosis tells you who a rank is and where he stands; it does not yet tell you what he is.

## Inner gnosis of the ranks

The second and far higher kind is the inner gnosis, and this is specific to the people of gnosis from among the people of truth. What is its object? It is the very beginning of the existence of the rank. When did the existence of a given rank begin? The ranks that have been created — where were they created, and from whom? Here the teaching opens, in compressed form, the long and difficult account of the origin of the ranks: that the hudud were brought into being where those whose sins were few had been, and were then summoned. A first call was issued, and in that first call they did not confess; a second call was made, and a third, and a fourth, in each room in turn, and one must know in which room each rank finally made its confession. This is a very long story whose full unfolding belongs elsewhere; but the point stands — the gnosis of this beginning, of where and from whom and through how many summons a rank came into being, is precisely the inner gnosis, and the gnosis of such a thing belongs to the Imam al-Zaman, upon him be peace.

What makes this knowledge inner is exactly what made the outer knowledge shared: it does not lie open to any pair of eyes. No enemy can study his way into it, no indifferent observer can stumble upon it, because it concerns not the visible person but the hidden origin from which that person came. It is reserved for those who stand within the truth, because only from within can the beginning of a rank be known at all. The friend and the foe see the same face; only the friend, and only the friend who has been admitted to the people of truth, sees where that face began.

Scripture draws this very line between the surface of a revealed thing and the hidden sense that only the few may reach. Of the verses whose inner meaning is sealed, the Qur'an says:

> And none know its interpretation save God and those firmly rooted in knowledge. — Quran 3:7 (Al 'Imran), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

The interpretation here is the ta'wil — the tracing of a thing back to the origin from which it came — and that is exactly the inner gnosis the teaching describes: not the outward letter any reader can recite, but the hidden beginning that opens only to those whom knowledge has rooted within the truth. The enemy may read every word; only the firmly rooted know from where it came.

It is worth noticing how this maps the difficulty of Tawhid onto two registers at once. The outer gnosis is hard in the way that any demanding observation is hard — it takes attention and care to know a rank truly even in his outward features and station. But its difficulty is the difficulty of effort, and effort is available to anyone, friend or foe alike. The inner gnosis is hard in an entirely different way: it is hard because it is hidden, sealed off from all but those whom the truth has admitted, and no amount of effort by an outsider can open it. The first difficulty is a wall any climber might scale; the second is a door that opens only from within. This is why the same body of knowledge — the gnosis of the ranks — can be at once something an enemy may partly possess and something an enemy can never possess at all.

## Beginning, level, and return

The inner gnosis does not concern the beginning alone. It comprises also the rank's end — where its return and its final terminus lie. To know the inner reality of a rank is to know the whole arc of its essence: at what point that essence was established, and at what level a person stood when he passed from this world. Was he in the ninth degree of the believer, or the eighth, or the seventh? Did he ascend from believer to become a Mukathir, and from Mukathir to a higher rank still? At precisely what station did his existence come to rest, and to what return is it destined? This is the knowledge of the soul's beginning, its level, and its return — the full trajectory of a created essence from its origin to its homecoming.

That homecoming is no peculiarity of the ranks alone; it is the law of every created thing, which the Qur'an fixes in the words a believer says in the face of affliction:

> Truly we are God's, and unto Him we return. — Quran 2:156 (al-Baqara), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

Every essence that was originated is bound, in the end, to return to the source that originated it; and the inner gnosis is precisely the knowledge of that arc in a particular rank — where its return lies, and at what level it stood when it set out toward it.

This is a gnosis of extreme difficulty, and the teaching states plainly its limits and its stakes. It cannot be reached except by the disciplined ones and the verifiers — those who have purified themselves and proven the truth within themselves. And then comes a statement of the utmost severity: for one whose gnosis of this inner reality is deficient, there is no Tawhid at all. Without the inner knowing of the beginning, the level, and the return of a rank's essence, a man may possess no true Tawhid whatever. This is why the outer gnosis, for all its real difficulty, cannot be the whole of Tawhid: a man could master every outward feature of every rank and still, lacking the inner knowing, have nothing of Tawhid in him. It is at this point that the master announces his own intention — to clarify something of this matter, so that it may serve as a help to his brethren in the gnosis of the Oneness of Allah. What follows in the rest of the teaching is the keeping of that promise.

## The closest rank

The exposition proper begins with a foundational principle, set down with an invocation of the help of Allah and the glances of His Wali upon His earth: that the closest of the ranks to us is the Mukathir, and that whatever is established of the saying regarding the lower of the ranks is established for the higher.

Two principles are fixed here at once. The first is that the closest rank to us — the rank nearest to ordinary humanity, the one through which we make our first true contact with the spiritual hierarchy — is the Mukathir. The second is a rule of proportion that will govern much of what follows: whatever is established for the lower of the ranks is likewise established, raised to its degree, for the higher. What is said of the Mukathir holds, in its measure, of the Ma'dhun, the Da'i Mutlaq, the Da'i Mahsur, and every rank above. And the rule works in both directions, for a higher rank contains the lower within himself: a Ma'dhun is also a Mukathir in his own place, and was a Mukathir before he was a Ma'dhun. This is why the teaching can afford to dwell on the Mukathir at length. He is not merely one rank among many; he is the pattern, the case from which the higher cases can be read upward, and the door through which the listener — who stands below all the ranks — first enters the discussion at all.

The rule of proportion is more than a convenience of exposition; it tells us something about the shape of the hierarchy itself. Because what is established for the lower is established for the higher, the ranks are not a heap of unrelated dignities but a single ordered series, each degree a fuller measure of the same reality that appears, in smaller measure, in the degree below. And because the higher contains the lower within himself — having been that lower rank before he rose — the series is not merely ordered but cumulative: nothing is left behind in the ascent. The Ma'dhun does not cease to be a Mukathir when he becomes a Ma'dhun; he carries the Mukathir within him, as a man carries his earlier self. So to understand the closest rank truly is already to hold the key to all the ranks above it, read upward in their proper measure — which is precisely why the teaching begins its detailed exposition here, at the nearest door, rather than at some lofty rank the listener could not yet reach.

## The two thresholds

Before reaching the Mukathir, however, one must pass through two preliminary levels that stand below the hudud proper. The first is the Mustajib, the responder, literally the one who accepts. The Mustajib is the one who takes the noble pledge, who declares with his tongue, "I accept the noble pledge," whether or not his heart and his deeds have yet caught up with his tongue. The defining mark of the Mustajib is precisely this profession with the tongue — that he affirms the Imam of the time and binds himself by that word. The Mustajib has ten levels, and these are graded by the intensity with which the pledge is taken: the sharpness, the force, the keenness of the one taking it. Some come to the pledge as casually as one comes to a marriage; others take it with the burning intensity of a Sayyidna Ja'far; the Imam himself takes the pledge. According to the intensity of the one pledging, his level is established, and so there are ten degrees of the noble pledge.

The second preliminary level is the Mu'min, the believer, whose classical definition the teaching gives as profession with the tongue, affirmation with the heart, and action with the limbs. The Mu'min is the one in whom all three are joined: he professes with the tongue, he affirms inwardly with the heart, and he acts outwardly with his limbs. The Mu'min, like the Mustajib, has ten levels of his own. And it must be emphasized that neither the Mustajib nor the Mu'min falls within the hudud. They are below the ranks proper — the necessary thresholds across which one approaches the hierarchy, but not yet of it. It is only after these, only with the Mukathir, that one truly enters the ranks. The believer, for all the seriousness of his belief, still stands outside the door; the Mukathir is the first to stand within it.

## Breaking the self

With the Mukathir, then, we cross the threshold into the ranks themselves. The Mukathir is the first rank within the hudud, the first degree of the spiritual hierarchy, and it is this rank that the transmitted saying placed closest to us. The meaning of the word is illuminating. Mukathir derives from breaking, for the Mukathir is the one who breaks with his proofs. But whom does he break first? Not his opponents — he must first break his own self. This breaking of the self is the indispensable beginning. To break one's self is to subject every word, every action, and every intention to a single relentless examination: is this statement I am uttering in accordance with the command of Allah and the Prophet ﷺ? Is this deed I am performing in accordance with their command? Is this very thought passing through my mind in accordance with their command? To bind one's self entirely to that measure — to make oneself captive to the command in speech, deed, and thought alike — is what it means to be a Mukathir.

This captivity to the command is itself a kind of striving, and the Prophet redirected the very name of the warrior toward it, away from the outward fight:

> The one who truly strives is he who strives against his own self in obedience to God. — Prophet Muhammad, narrated by Fadalah ibn Ubayd, *Jami' al-Tirmidhi*, Kitab Fada'il al-Jihad, no. 1621.

To be a Mukathir is to take up exactly this striving: to wage against one's own speech, deed, and thought the war that the enemy of a saint never thinks to wage against himself at all.

The logic is exacting. Only when a man has broken his own self can he break others. If he has set himself in order upon this matter, then he is able with his proofs to break the one who stands before him. But if he could not break his own self — if he indulged it, telling it that this is permissible and that is permissible, granting himself license — then how shall he break another? He has no standing to do so. The Father of Imams fixed this same order — the reform of the self before the reform of others — as the first duty of anyone who would lead:

> Whoever sets himself up as a leader of the people, let him begin with the teaching of his own self before the teaching of others; and let his teaching be by his conduct before his tongue. — Ali ibn Abi Talib, the Father of Imams, *Nahj al-Balagha* (compiled by al-Sharif al-Radi), Saying 73, trans. Sayyid Ali Reza.

To teach by conduct before the tongue is precisely to have broken the self first; only then are the proofs in a man's mouth backed by the proof of his life. And from this follows a strict requirement: for the Mukathir, the acquisition of the sciences is indispensable. Without knowledge no Mukathir can be formed, for he must first know the command of Allah and the Prophet in order to test himself against it. Only when he knows the command can he recognize that some word of his fell short of it, that some deed of his diverged from it, that some thought of his strayed. Having first judged and broken himself by this knowledge, he is then able to break the one before him with his proofs. And by the rule already established — what is fixed for the lower is fixed for the higher — this same description holds, in its proper degree, of every rank of the hudud above.

There is a quiet severity in making the breaking of the self the very door into the ranks. The seeker does not enter the hierarchy by acquiring a status, a title, or even a body of knowledge held as a possession; he enters by turning the full force of the command against his own speech, deed, and thought, and submitting to its verdict without exception. Knowledge is indispensable here, but not as ornament — it is the measuring-rod against which the self is broken, and a man who gathered knowledge while sparing himself its judgment would not be a Mukathir at all. This is why the first rank is at once the closest to us and the hardest to reach: closest, because every believer stands just below it; hardest, because the price of crossing is the surrender of the one thing a man is most reluctant to surrender, which is his own license over himself.

## What this teaching lands

The gnosis of the ranks has now been divided at its joint. There is the outer knowing of a rank's person and station, in which the devoted friend and the sworn enemy are made equal, because it concerns only what any eye can see. And there is the inner knowing — of the beginning, the level, and the return of a rank's essence — reserved for the disciplined and the verifying among the people of truth, and so decisive that without it a man has no Tawhid at all. The seeker climbs toward the ranks across two thresholds that are not yet ranks: the Mustajib, who binds himself by the tongue, and the Mu'min, who joins tongue, heart, and limb — each with ten levels of its own. And he enters the hierarchy proper only at the Mukathir, the closest rank, by first breaking his own self against the command of Allah and the Prophet, which is why knowledge is indispensable to him. The door into the ranks, then, is not a title conferred from without but a self subdued from within — the seeker made captive to the command in speech, deed, and thought, his proofs backed by his conduct, his knowing turned first upon himself. What stands closest to us is also what costs the most to reach: the breaking of one's own license, which is the price of the first rank and, by the rule of proportion, the seed of every rank above it.
