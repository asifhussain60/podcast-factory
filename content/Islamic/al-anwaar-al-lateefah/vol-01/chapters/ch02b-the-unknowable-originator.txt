## Where this episode picks up

Can a word you invented ever reach the One who invented you?

The previous episode ended on a promise: the soul's whole journey is a return to the Originator, but we had not yet asked what that Originator is in Himself. This episode takes up exactly that question and gives the book's most uncompromising answer. The One toward whom all worship is directed is so exalted that no indication can point to Him, no thought can cross toward Him, and — most radically of all — none of the names and attributes we use, not even the name "Allah," actually fall upon Him. We will trace that argument in four moves: His transcendence beyond all pointing and thinking; the negation of names and attributes from Him; where those names land if not on Him; and what our very utterance of "Allah" really is. Each move pushes the divine essence one step further out of reach, until the only honest posture left is bewilderment.

## Exalted beyond all indication

The book first establishes a gateway condition: there is no path even to the mention of these realities except by the mention of Allah. Without His mention nothing can be opened. The image is of unlocking — by the mention of Allah every thing is unlocked, and the opening is sought. And by His most beautiful names, the asma' al-husna, a means is taken to Him in every request, so that success is attained. We begin, then, by invoking Him and by reaching for Him through His names. But no sooner has the book granted us this beginning than it turns and warns us how little that invocation can claim to grasp. The names open the door; they do not deliver the One behind it.

Here is the cornerstone of right belief concerning the divine nature, and the book treats it as a foundation on which everything else stands. He is glorified and exalted far above that any indicator's indication should fall upon Him, or that the thought of any thinker should dare toward Him by imagination or by contemplation. He is the One whose glorification is performed — we are the glorifiers, never He; the tasbih is ours, the One glorified is He. And He is exalted on high, raised by His very nature, so exalted that no indication, no ishara, can possibly fall upon Him so as to point toward Him. This, the text insists, is the true statement of the matter and the very basis upon which correct doctrine stands: that no indication can reach Him. Nor can any thought dare to cross over toward Him. The verb the text chooses comes from the word for a bridge, and the image is of a bridge that cannot be crossed: the thought of the heart cannot bridge the gap toward Him, neither by imagination, tawahhum, nor by contemplation, tafkir. No imagining and no reasoning achieves the objective. We can know only this much — that, since we ourselves were created, there must be One who created us. But what He is in Himself, what His nature is, of this we know nothing whatever.

This truth is illuminated by a celebrated exchange from the time of Imam Ali, and the book brings it in precisely to keep the doctrine from drifting into denial. A man named Ibn al-Kawwa', intending to corner the Commander of the Faithful with a question he thought could not be answered, came and asked: "Have you seen your God? Have you seen Allah?" Imam Ali replied, "Woe to you — could I worship a God I had not seen? How could I worship a God without having seen Him?" The man then pressed his advantage: "How, then, did you see Him?" And the Imam answered: "I did not see Him with the eyes; I saw Him with the basirah — with insight." It is a mighty saying of our Mawla, and it draws the exact line the book needs. The vision of Allah is not the vision of the physical eye, which cannot fall upon Him any more than indication or thought can. It is the vision of insight, the inner perception of the heart that affirms Him with certainty without grasping His essence. He is unseen to the eye and to the mind, and yet not unknown to the heart — affirmed, never comprehended.

Notice how carefully the Imam's answer is balanced, because the doctrine of transcendence is easy to push too far in either direction. Deny that He can be seen at all and you risk denying that He can be known, which would make worship empty. Claim that He is seen as things are seen and you drag Him down among the objects of the eye, which is exactly what the book forbids. The Imam's reply walks the narrow line between these errors: the eye sees nothing of Him, and the heart, by its basirah, holds Him fast. The worshipper neither stares at a void nor points at an image; he stands before a certainty he cannot picture. That is the posture the whole rest of the chapter will ask us to keep — affirming without describing, certain without comprehending.

## Names negated from Him

From this transcendence a profound consequence follows concerning the divine names and attributes, and it is the most startling claim in the chapter. When we express something about Him by calling Him "Allah, the Exalted," or by calling Him the Originator, the Mubdi', or by referring to Him by the exalted Ipseity — the huwiyyah, the bare "He" — none of these names, and no attribute, actually falls upon Him. Neither name nor attribute lights upon Him. The reason is stated with rigor and turns on a single principle: names and attributes do not fall except upon their like. A name or an attribute can be applied only to something of its own kind, something that resembles it; and nothing resembles Him.

Consider what we are actually doing when we name Him, because the book wants us to catch ourselves in the act. We say "Allah" in order to recognize Him, wishing to know Him, and so we assign Him a name — and we do so from our own tongue. But you are yourself the creator of that tongue and of those names; the very instrument by which you try to interpret Allah is a thing of your own making. As the Commander of the Faithful said, names and attributes are indicators pointing to the named, and they point only to their like. Think of how any attribute actually arises in us: we observed mercy in some creature and so applied the word "Merciful" to that one; the attribute fell upon its like, upon a knowledge and an arrangement of the kind we could perceive. The word lands where it belongs — on something within our experience — and the Originator, by definition, is not within our experience.

The distinction the text insists on must be held precisely, because the whole argument rests on it. These names and attributes are themselves created, originated things — they are muhdathah. There is the one who originates, the muhdith, and there is the thing originated, the muhdath; the names and attributes belong firmly to the second category. They are created, and they are created specifically by human beings: it is a human who pronounces a name, a human who assigns it. You named the child; you are the one who gave the name; the names are our handiwork. And here is the crux: the originated thing does not fall upon its originator as its like. A created thing cannot characterize its creator as though they were equals; the made cannot describe its maker as one of its own kind. The book reinforces this with the word of Sayyidna Mu'ayyad al-Din — who lived in the time of Mawlana Mustansir Billah and held the rank of Bab al-Abwab — that since the very letters of the alphabet are originated things, they cannot serve as a true and verifying indicator of the very Originator who made them, an indication that would hold good upon His like. A natural objection arises here, and the text meets it head on. One might protest that there is a limit to our making as well — that many things proceed by His command before any word issues from us, so are we truly the creators of these things? The answer holds to the precise point at issue: the words I am speaking, these I am indeed creating; of these spoken words I am the maker. With my own creation — the tongue and the names that are my handiwork — I am attempting to characterize my own Creator, and that is exactly what cannot be done. The choice we have is real, but it does not extend to recognizing Him. He dwells in such an unseen, such a ghayb, that we cannot lift its curtains; we can say only that, to establish our own existence, there must be an Originator for us, and there the road stops.

## The First Intellect as locus

If the names and attributes do not fall upon Allah, the question presses hard: upon what, then, do they fall? The book's answer reorganizes the whole picture. Since the matter is as described, these names and attributes are negated, manfiyyah, from Allah, the Exalted — they are not predicated of Him at all. Instead they fall upon His origination, His ibda'. And what is that origination? It is the First Intellect, the 'aql al-awwal. It is named also the first existent, the mawjud al-awwal, and the first emanation, the sadir al-awwal — three names for the one reality that issued first from the Originator. Whatever names and attributes there are, they are negated from Allah and they descend upon this first origination, the First Intellect that is at once the first existent and the first to issue forth. The names do not vanish; they simply find their true resting place one level down from God.

The text presses the point further, and the further it presses the higher the essence recedes. What we can speak of is only what appeared from His act in His creation, not any name or attribute applied to Him directly, for all such direct predication is stripped away from Him. The names and attributes we apply fall upon that which He created, upon His ibda'. And even there the matter is subtle and the book makes us feel it: if these names and attributes do not even fall fully upon His origination — if they sit imperfectly even on the First Intellect — how then could they ever fall upon Allah, the Originator Most High? The argument is a kind of receding horizon. Each time we think we have located where our language belongs, the book shows that even that location is too high for it, and the essence withdraws another step.

The teaching now reaches its widest sweep, embracing the whole of creation in a single shared incapacity. Inadequacy — 'ijz, the inability to know the Originator — is comprehensive; it leaves nothing out. It embraces the world of intellect, the 'alam al-'aql, and the world of sense, the 'alam al-hiss, together. The First Intellect itself, the highest of all created things, shares in this incapacity, and so do we, the lowest; we are all equal in it. They are equal in this one respect, the text says — the powerful and the overpowered, the sensed and the intelligible, from both worlds together. This is a remarkable leveling. The powerful and the powerless, the sensed and the intelligible, the world of intellect and the world of body — all stand on perfectly level ground before the unknowability of the Originator. There is a real hierarchy of creation, and the First Intellect stands at its summit; but in this one matter the summit and the dust know exactly the same amount, which is to say nothing at all of His essence. Rank does not buy a single degree of access here.

## Allah, the cry of bewilderment

What, then, is our very utterance of the name "Allah"? The text gives an answer of remarkable depth, one that turns the name inside out. Our saying "Allah" — our expressing Him by this name — arises from the bewilderment of the First Intellect concerning the Originator. The First Intellect is bewildered before Him, and the word "Allah" issues out of that bewilderment. In this, again, all are equal: the Imam is equal in it, the ardent lover, the 'ashiq, is equal in it; whoever stands bewildered before Him uses the name "Allah." The name is not a grasp of Him and not a definition of Him; it is the sound the creature makes when it stands before what it cannot comprehend. The most exalted word in our mouths is, at its root, an admission of defeat — a defeat shared by the highest intellect and the humblest lover alike.

This is unfolded through the image of the circle, and the image rewards a slow look. Allah is the center of the circle — the markaz, the central point — around which the bewildered circle revolves. The circle turns; and why does it turn? Because it is bewildered about its center. One circle revolves about its center in bewilderment; then another circle comes, bewildered about its own center; every circle revolves around its center, and the reason for its revolving is always the same — bewilderment. The very energy by which the circle turns is, in truth, nothing other than its bewilderment. The created order circles eternally about a center it cannot comprehend, and the motion of that circling is the unceasing perplexity of the creature before the Originator made visible as movement. The turning is not idle; it is the perplexity itself, taking the form of a path.

So the name "Allah" and the turning of the circle are the same fact seen twice. To say "Allah" is to confess that we revolve around a center we cannot reach; to revolve is to be saying "Allah" with our whole being. Neither the word nor the motion is a possession of Him or a description of Him. They are the form our incapacity takes — the highest sound and the steadiest motion a creature can manage in the presence of the One who made it and remains, in His essence, sealed against it. We do not name Him from above, having understood Him; we name Him from within the circle, still turning, still unable to arrive.

There is a strange comfort folded into this bleak-sounding picture, and it is worth drawing out before the episode closes. If the name "Allah" were a claim to have understood Him, then every worshipper would be a kind of impostor, and the most learned would be the worst, claiming the most. But the book has shown the opposite: the name is an honest confession of bewilderment, and so the Imam and the ardent lover and the simplest believer all stand on the same ground when they say it. None of them is pretending to a knowledge he lacks. Each is doing the only thing a creature can do before its Originator — naming Him with the last word it has, and circling. The leveling that looked at first like a humiliation turns out to be a mercy: no one is shut out of this naming for lack of learning, because learning buys no entry here. The circle has no inner ring reserved for the clever; everyone turns at the same distance from the center.

## What this episode lands

The episode has driven a single claim as far as it will go: the Originator is unknowable, and our language knows it. He cannot be pointed to, He cannot be thought, and the names and attributes we use do not fall upon Him, because a created word cannot characterize its uncreated maker. Those words land instead on the First Intellect, the first of His creations — and even there they do not fully fit. From the summit of the intellectual world down to the world of the senses, every creature is equal in one thing: total inability to know His essence. And our very name for Him, "Allah," turns out to be not a definition but a cry — the sound of a bewildered circle turning around a center it can never reach.

## Closing

If even the First Intellect cannot name Him, and if to name Him at all is to fall short, a hard question remains. Why do we name Him? And what was the First Intellect actually doing in that first act of naming — was it praise, or was it, in some strange sense, a fault? The next episode follows the First Intellect's own first words, the negation and the affirmation by which it pointed beyond itself, and arrives at the book's most paradoxical teaching: that to name the Unnameable is a sin, and yet a sin already coupled with the asking of forgiveness.
