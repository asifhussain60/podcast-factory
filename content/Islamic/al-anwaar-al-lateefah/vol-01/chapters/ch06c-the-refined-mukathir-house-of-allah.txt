# The Mukathir, House of Allah

*Episode six of Al-Anwaar al-Lateefah, Volume One — The Oneness. The teaching now looks closely at the first rank within the hierarchy. The Mukathir and the ordinary man are made of the very same flesh and blood, gathered within the same bodily limits — and yet they are not the same kind of being at all. This episode traces the invisible transformation: how the Mukathir's temperament is refined and his essence made pure through ascent, until the summit of the human becomes the threshold of the spiritual world; how he becomes a house in which the revelation of Allah alights, anchored in the verses of the Qur'an on the houses Allah permits to be exalted; and how each outward pillar of the religion carries an inner meaning, with "establishing prayer" meaning, in truth, the establishing of the divine summons.*

## Where this episode picks up

What if the holiest man you ever met looked, to the eye, exactly like everyone else — same flesh, same bone, the same five feet and odd inches, the same face and hands — and the entire difference between him and his neighbor lay in something no inspection could ever detect? The teaching has just brought the seeker across the threshold of the ranks to the Mukathir, the first and closest rank, entered by breaking one's own self against the command of Allah and the Prophet. Now a profound question presses. If the Mukathir is outwardly indistinguishable from any other human being — flesh, blood, bone, nerve, and vein, like everyone else — then in what does his rank actually consist? The teaching answers that an immense transformation has taken place, one that reaches even into his outward composition, though no eye perceives it; and from that transformation flows the most exalted of the descriptions given to him.

## Same flesh, different reality

The teaching meets the question directly. The Mukathir and the ordinary human being are gathered together within the same limits and the same regions — the same boundaries of body and place encompass them both. Both are composed of flesh, both of blood, both of bones, of nerves, of veins, and of all the rest. To outward inspection there is no difference between them: the ordinary man is of flesh and bone, and the Mukathir is of flesh and bone. And yet, the teaching insists, they are not the same. Despite the complete identity of their outward composition, an immense difference separates them.

This is a deliberately unsettling claim, and it is meant to dislodge a natural assumption — that what a thing is must show in what it looks like. We expect that a being of a higher order would betray its rank somehow, in some visible mark. The teaching refuses that expectation. Two men can share every measurable feature, bone for bone and vein for vein, and belong to wholly different orders of being. The difference is real, but it is not on the surface, and the chapter spends its next breaths explaining where the difference actually lies and how it came to be. The mistake the listener must surrender is the mistake of the eye, which knows only outward sameness and is blind to the reality beneath it.

The claim also carries forward the warning the whole volume has been issuing about the inadequacy of the outward. We have already learned that the outer gnosis, in which friend and foe are equal, knows a rank's person but not his reality; here the same lesson appears in the flesh. If even the Mukathir — the first and lowest rank within the hierarchy — is outwardly indistinguishable from any common man, then the eye is no instrument at all for measuring rank. A person could stand before the holiest of his contemporaries and, judging by sight alone, see nothing but an ordinary human being. The hierarchy of the spiritual world is therefore not a hierarchy our senses can read; it must be known by the inner gnosis or not known at all, and the very invisibility of the Mukathir's transformation is the first proof of this.

## The refined essence

The teaching resolves the paradox: this rank, which is the Mukathir, has had his temperament refined and his essence made pure, and this through ascending many stations. The difference, then, is not merely inward but reaches even the outward. The Mukathir's flesh is no longer simply common flesh, his blood no longer common blood; everything of his has been transformed — and this even outwardly, though others did not perceive it and the ignorant could not discern it. The transformation came about because he ascended through numerous stations that others never attained. He passed through the ten levels of the Mustajib, the ten of the Mu'min, and then the degrees of the Mukathir, and from all this his very substance took on a subtlety.

The Prophetic tradition names this very transformation, and names it as the fruit of exactly the slow drawing-near that ascent through the stations is:

> My servant draws near to Me with nothing more beloved to Me than what I have made obligatory upon him; and My servant continues to draw near to Me through supererogatory works until I love him. And when I love him, I become his hearing with which he hears, his sight with which he sees, his hand with which he grasps, and his foot with which he walks. — Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, hadith qudsi narrated by Abu Hurayra, *Sahih al-Bukhari*, Kitab al-Riqaq, no. 6502.

What the teaching describes as the refining of the Mukathir's very flesh and blood, the hadith describes from the divine side: the servant who ascends toward his Lord through devotion is drawn so near that his own faculties — his hearing, his sight, his hand, his foot — are no longer merely his own. The outward man is unchanged to the eye; the reality that animates his every sense and limb has been wholly transformed.

Through this refinement and ascent the Mukathir has drawn near to the first principle — near, that is, to the very source from which his creation proceeded, so that he is now close to returning there. He has reached the highest stations of humanity, and these highest human stations are themselves the first of the spiritual ranks. Here is the hinge of the whole matter: the summit of the human is the threshold of the malakut, the spiritual world. The Mukathir stands precisely at that joining-point, where the topmost rung of the human order is the bottommost rung of the angelic. This is why his composition can be refined while his appearance is unchanged — he has not ceased to be human; he has reached the highest reach of the human, which is already the doorway into something higher. The ascent was not a flight out of the body but a purification carried through it, until the human stuff itself, without altering its look, became the first stuff of the spiritual world.

## A house of Allah

From this elevation flows the most exalted of the descriptions given to the Mukathir: that he is a place of descent for the revelation of Allah, and a house from among His houses. He is the very locus upon which revelation alights. And the reason he is so is exact: in his every thought he kept the command of Allah and the Prophet before him; in his every word he kept that command before him; and in his every deed he kept it before him. Having made the command of Allah and the Prophet the standing presence of his thought, speech, and action, he became thereby a house from the houses of Allah, a place fit for the descent of revelation. The refinement and the house are the same fact seen from two sides: a man who holds the command before him in all that he thinks and says and does is, by that very holding, made into a dwelling for what descends from above.

The teaching anchors this in the Qur'an, in the verses of Surat al-Nur that speak of the houses Allah has permitted to be raised:

> In houses God has permitted to be raised up and wherein His Name is mentioned, He is glorified therein, morning and evening, by men whom neither commerce nor trade diverts from the remembrance of God, the performance of prayer, and the giving of alms. — Quran 24:36–37 (Surat al-Nur), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

And it establishes that the "houses" of the verse are, in their inner meaning, the ranks of the hierarchy — of whom the Mukathir is the first. The outward word names a building of stone; the inner reality names a rank of the spiritual order. The men described in the same verse — those whom neither commerce nor trade diverts from the remembrance of Allah, from the establishing of prayer, and from the giving of alms — are these same ranks. The house and its inhabitant turn out to be the same reality: the rank that has been made a dwelling for revelation is itself the man whom no trade can pull away from the remembrance of his Lord.

There is a precise logic to why holding the command before oneself should make a man into a house of Allah. A house is defined by what it is built to receive and to shelter; and the Mukathir, having made the command of Allah and the Prophet the constant presence of his thought, his speech, and his deed, has built his whole inner life around that single content. He has emptied himself of every rival preoccupation and furnished himself entirely with the command — so that there is, as it were, room in him for nothing but what comes from above. A vessel so prepared is exactly what revelation requires for its descent: not a mind crowded with its own concerns, but one cleared and consecrated to receive. This is why the same man who is a house is also the man undistracted by commerce. The two descriptions name one discipline from two sides — the clearing-out of worldly distraction and the furnishing with the divine command — and the result of that discipline is a human being fit to be a place upon which the word of Allah alights.

## Establishing the da'wah

What, then, is intended by these men's "establishing of prayer"? In its inner sense it is the establishing of the da'wah, the divine summons. The verse's outward language of commerce and trade is illuminated by the parallel passage of Surat al-Jumu'a:

> O you who believe! When the call to prayer is made on the day of congregation, hasten to the remembrance of God and leave off trade. That is better for you, if you but knew. Then when the prayer is completed, disperse throughout the land and seek the Bounty of God, and remember God much, that haply you may prosper. — Quran 62:9–10 (Surat al-Jumu'a), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

Those whom the buying and selling of the world do not divert from the remembrance of Allah, from the establishing of prayer, and from the giving of alms — they are the ones who, in the inner sense, establish the da'wah.

The teaching then returns to seal the point: what Allah intends by the verse is the establishing of the da'wah, which is the very object that the outward prayer serves and supports; and the da'wah itself has no standing except through prayer. The outer prayer and the inner summons are thus bound together, each upholding the other. This is a careful balance, and it is easy to mishear. The teaching is not setting the inner meaning against the outer and discarding the outer; it is showing that the outward act of prayer exists for the sake of the inward reality it serves, and that the inward reality cannot stand without the outward act that carries it. The man who establishes prayer is establishing, through that very act, the summons to Allah; and the summons, in turn, is kept standing only by the prayer that supports it.

This binding-together of the outer and inner answers an objection that the doctrine of inner meanings always invites. If the establishing of prayer "really" means the establishing of the divine summons, one might conclude that the outward prayer is a mere husk to be discarded once its inner kernel is understood. The teaching forecloses exactly that conclusion. It does not say that the inner summons replaces the outer prayer; it says that the summons has no standing except through the prayer. The outward act is not a dispensable shell but the very support on which the inward reality rests, so that to abandon the prayer would be to topple the summons it upholds. The Mukathir who has grasped the inner meaning is therefore not freed from the outward observance; he is bound to it more tightly than before, because he now sees the great inner reality that the small outward act is carrying.

## The inner meaning of the pillars

This identification of "establishing prayer" with "establishing the da'wah" opens onto a broader teaching: that each of the pillars and obligations of the religion carries an inner meaning beneath its outward form. This inner reading of the pillars is no improvisation of the moment; the classical Ismaili tradition devoted an entire treatise to precisely it — Qadi al-Nu'man's *Ta'wil al-Da'a'im*, the interpretation of the pillars of Islam — so that what the teaching now sets out in brief stands within a long and deliberate line of inner exegesis. The teaching sets these out in turn. Loyal allegiance, in its inner sense, is to repent in the world of motes — to turn in repentance in that primordial world of the smallest particles of being. Purity is to cleanse oneself from the filth of Iblis through the knowledge of the Tenth. Prayer is to act upon the commands of the Tenth, or of whichever Imam in whose sphere one has arrived and whose summons has reached one — to enact the commands he has given; and this is why, when the prayer is established, it is announced that the prayer has stood, since the standing of the prayer is the standing-up to enact the command.

Alms, in the inner sense, is to give a portion of the knowledge that has been given to you: when the knowledge you have received reaches a certain measure, a small portion of it becomes obligatory to give out. And just as there are two kinds of material alms — the alms of breaking the fast, owed by every person, and the alms of wealth, which falls due only when wealth accumulates — so too with the alms of knowledge: some sciences are owed by nature to every person and must be explained to all, while others are owed only when one's store of knowledge has grown to the threshold that makes a portion of it obligatory to dispense. Fasting is to conceal knowledge — not to disclose it to the undeserving, to keep it hidden from those unfit to receive it. The fifth is to give a fifth part of knowledge that comes to you unexpectedly: just as, in the outward law, a fifth of an unexpected windfall must be given — as Hazrat Imam Husayn, upon him be peace, used each month to set aside the fifth of his very salary, reasoning that he had not expected such money to come — so likewise, if some knowledge descends upon you unexpectedly from the saints of Allah, its fifth part must be given out. Pilgrimage is the intending toward the House of Allah — and the House of Allah, in the inner sense, is the Imam al-Zaman, who is the place of Allah; the "house" is the inner gate, and pilgrimage is to prepare one's form and bearing, with a true heart, to set out toward that inner gate. And struggle in the way of Allah is to take one's own self — rather, not merely to take it but to sacrifice it, to slay it — when the command comes from the Imam al-Zaman; and the greatest struggle is the slaying of the commanding self, so that the true hero, the lion of the battle, is the one who has killed within himself that great beast which is his own commanding soul.

What unifies all of these inner meanings is a single movement: each outward pillar, in its depth, is an act performed upon knowledge and upon the self in relation to the appointed guide. Purity is a cleansing by knowledge; prayer is the enacting of the guide's command; alms and the fifth are the dispensing of knowledge received; fasting is the guarding of knowledge from the unworthy; pilgrimage is the turning of the whole self toward the inner gate; and struggle is the slaying of the self that resists. It is worth pausing on why the dispensing of knowledge should be reckoned a form of alms at all, since alms ordinarily names a parting-with that leaves the giver poorer. The Father of Imams drew exactly the distinction that resolves it:

> Knowledge is better than wealth. Knowledge guards you, while you have to guard wealth. Wealth decreases by spending, while knowledge multiplies by spending. — Ali ibn Abi Talib, the Father of Imams, *Nahj al-Balagha* (compiled by al-Sharif al-Radi), Saying 147, trans. Sayyid Ali Reza.

The alms of knowledge, then, is an alms of a peculiar kind: unlike the alms of wealth, which diminishes the store from which it is drawn, the giving-out of knowledge to its rightful recipients enlarges the very store it is drawn from. To dispense knowledge is not to lose it but to increase it — which is why the guarding of it from the unworthy and the giving of it to the worthy are, in the end, the same care for a single trust. The law, read this way, is not a set of disconnected observances but one continuous training of a soul in its knowledge and its allegiance — the very wilayah and action by which, the volume has insisted from the start, a soul reaches the Oneness of Allah. The Mukathir who has become a house of Allah is simply the man in whom this whole inner law has been lived through.

## What this teaching lands

The first rank has now been opened to view. The Mukathir is made of the same flesh and blood as any other man, gathered within the same bodily bounds — and yet he is not the same, for his temperament has been refined and his essence purified through an ascent that reaches even into his outward composition, though no eye can see it. Standing at the summit of the human, which is the threshold of the spiritual world, and holding the command of Allah and the Prophet before him in his every thought, word, and deed, he has become a house from the houses of Allah, a place upon which revelation descends — and the Qur'an's houses, and the men whom no commerce diverts, are these very ranks. Their "establishing of prayer" is, in its depth, the establishing of the divine summons, the outer act and the inner reality each upholding the other; and beneath every pillar of the religion — allegiance, purity, prayer, alms, fasting, the fifth, pilgrimage, and struggle — there lies just such an inner meaning. The first rank, then, is no mere title on the lowest rung of the hierarchy: the Mukathir is a human being whose every thought, word, and deed has been bound so wholly to the command that he himself becomes a dwelling upon which the revelation of Allah alights, and in whom the entire inner law of the religion has been lived out from within.
