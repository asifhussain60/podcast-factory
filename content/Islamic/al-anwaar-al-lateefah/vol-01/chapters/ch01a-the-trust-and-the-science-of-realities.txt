# Trust and the Science of Realities

*Episode one of Al-Anwaar al-Lateefah, Volume One — The Oneness. This opening lesson sets out what kind of knowledge the book carries — an inherited wisdom drawn not from speculation but from the sciences of the prophets and the Friends of Allah — then names the symmetrical five-fold architecture of the book and states its method: to set down pure reality, with no interpretation and no symbol. From there the doctrine itself opens: that access to the Oneness of Allah is won by two things only, loving allegiance and lawful action, and that this is the road by which the exiled soul returns to the homeland from which it first came.*

## The inherited science

The lessons gathered in the book *Anwaar al-Latifah* are composed of the lofty contents of the sciences of realities. They are not drawn from speculation, nor from the ordinary disciplines of exoteric learning. Everything in them has been interpreted and unfolded entirely upon the foundation of the sciences of the prophets and of the saints. What is offered here is therefore a transmission of inherited wisdom — knowledge whose source lies in the teaching of those whom Allah has chosen as the bearers of His most intimate truths. Every realization expounded in these pages traces itself back to that prophetic and saintly fountainhead, and it is in that light that the listener must approach what follows.

That such knowledge passes to those fit to carry it is old in this tradition. Ali ibn Abi Talib, the Father of Imams, made it vivid when he took the hand of his disciple Kumayl ibn Ziyad, led him out into the open country, and confided what the heart can and cannot hold:

> These hearts are vessels, and the best of them is the one that retains [its contents] most.... Here — and he pointed to his breast — is abundant knowledge, could I but find bearers for it. — Ali ibn Abi Talib, the Father of Imams.

The difficulty, in that telling, was never a scarcity of knowledge; it was the scarcity of those fit to carry it. The realities are entrusted to those prepared to hold them.

That a weighty thing should be received as a trust — with real consequences for whether it is kept — is the Quran's own framing of the human condition:

> Truly We offered the Trust unto the heavens and the earth and the mountains, but they refused to carry it, and were wary of it — yet man carried it; truly he has proved himself an ignorant wrongdoer. — Quran, the chapter of the Confederates (chapter 33, verse 72), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

To be human, in this telling, is already to be the bearer of a trust that the very mountains declined: a thing accepted is a thing one becomes answerable for.

## The five-fold architecture

The book itself is structured according to a deliberate and symmetrical design, and the very name of its parts conveys something of its character. Each major division is called a Suradiq, and the work opens with the First Suradiq from the Book of Lights. The word Suradiq carries the sense of a curtain, and beyond that of a palace, a grand encampment, a great tent — a canopy or a royal pavilion within which the matters of the book unfold. It is the image of an enclosing pavilion, and inside it the realities are set out in order.

The architecture is built upon the number five throughout. The whole book comprises five Suradiqs. Each Suradiq, as the text states, has five chapters — five abwab. And each chapter in turn gathers within it five sections, five fusul. Five Suradiqs, each of five chapters, each chapter of five sections: by this symmetry the whole work amounts to one hundred and twenty-five sections in all. The design is not decorative. It tells the listener that what follows is a single ordered structure rather than a loose collection of talks, and that every section has a fixed place within a larger architecture of fives. With this structure fixed in mind, the book turns to the very first section of the first chapter of the first Suradiq, where the purpose of the whole enterprise is announced.

## The method of pure reality

The opening section states the book's method before it states any doctrine, and the method is striking. The author declares that what is intended here concerns the noble affections — the mawaddah — that lead the soul toward the noble obligations, and that he will treat them as a pure reality, taken whole and unmixed. In that reality, he says, there is no interpretation and no symbol. The phrase carries great weight. The book does not propose to expound the outer exoteric meaning, nor to ascend into ta'wil (تأويل), the esoteric interpretation. It proposes rather to set down the pure, unmixed reality of these matters directly.

The negations that follow are emphatic, and the grammar itself underscores them. When the author says there is "no interpretation" in it, the construction is what the grammarians call the negation of the genus — an absolute, categorical denial. To say merely that there is "no particular interpretation" would leave room for some interpretation somewhere; but to negate the genus is to say there is no interpretation whatsoever, none of the kind at all. The same holds for what follows: no symbol from it, and no allusion. The intention is pure reality, stripped of veiling interpretation, of symbol, and of allusion alike.

This matters for how the listener should hear everything that comes after. Much of the Ismaili tradition is famous for ta'wil (تأويل) — for reading an inner meaning beneath the surface of scripture and law. Here the author signals that he is doing something different. He is not lifting a veil to show the secret beneath the letter; he is stating the realities as realities, directly, as far as they can be stated at all. The whole difficulty of the book — the difficulty of speaking about an Originator who exceeds all speech — flows from this declared method. The author has bound himself to give us the thing itself, not a code for it, and the strain of keeping that promise about a subject that resists all naming is precisely the drama of the chapters to come.

## The twin path to tawhid (توحيد)

With the method fixed, the discourse widens to embrace the whole of existence. The book proposes to address all the existing things that appeared before our own existence, and it gives its reason at once. It is through the gnosis of those things — the المعرفة, the true knowing and acquaintance with all that came into being before us — that the soul is set upon the road. And it is through obedience to the Friends of Allah, and love of them, and drawing near to Allah by means of them, that the road is actually traveled. Crucial to this is adherence to their hudud (حُدُود) — the ranks and bounds appointed for the believer — for these hudud (حُدُود) are appointed and assigned from the direction of the Riser himself. And it is through lawful action (عَمَل, action), laid down in the religious law, that the road is completed. By the whole of this — gnosis, obedience, love, nearness, adherence to the appointed hudud (حُدُود), and lawful action — guidance is given toward the tawhid (توحيد) of Allah, the Most High. This, the text insists, is the only path by which one may reach the Oneness of Allah.

It is worth distilling the two great pillars upon which this access rests, because they recur throughout the doctrine. Access to the tawhid (توحيد) of Allah is attained through two things and two things only: loyal allegiance (ولاية) and lawful action — allegiance and action. Allegiance is named also as love; it is the bond of devotion to the Friends of Allah and submission to the ranks they appoint. And action is the deed laid down by the law. By these two together — by loving allegiance and by righteous action — one attains access to the Oneness of Allah. There is no other way through. Knowledge alone does not arrive; devotion alone does not arrive; the two must be joined.

That the road should run through allegiance to the chosen household is not the author's own innovation; it rests on the Prophet's final counsel to his community. In the tradition of the two weighty things, the Prophet Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him) is reported to have said:

> Verily I am leaving among you two weighty things: the Book of Allah and my progeny, the people of my household. So long as you hold fast to them both, you will never go astray after me. — narrated from Zayd ibn Arqam; *Sunan al-Tirmidhi* 3788 (the hadith al-thaqalayn); cf. *Sahih Muslim*, Kitab Fada'il al-Sahaba, 2408.

Scripture and the appointed ranks — action and allegiance — are handed down together, in a single breath; to grasp one while letting the other fall is to lose the very guidance the Prophet entrusted. The book's twin pillars are an unfolding of this one bequest.

And the fruit of this attainment is twofold, as the text declares. By treading this path the ranks of the afterlife are raised up for the soul, and salvation is achieved from this lower world of being and corruption, this realm of generation and decay in which we are presently confined. The path does not merely improve a life here; it lifts the soul's standing in the world to come and frees it from the very order of coming-to-be and passing-away. That double fruit — elevation in the afterlife and release from the world of corruption — is what the twin pillars of allegiance and action are for.

## The return to the origin

This salvation is, in its deepest meaning, a homecoming. The text speaks of returning to the origin from which the noble beginnings first came, and of dwelling with the Friends of Allah in the Abode of Sanctity. Here the doctrine of return is set forth. The movement is a leading-back toward the origin — back to that source from which the noble beginnings first issued. For the soul does not belong to this lower world; this is not its native country. Our original homeland is the world of spirits, the alam al-ruh (عالم الروح). That is the land from which we came, and to which the whole movement of the religious life is directed as a return. The goal is to make our way back to that origin, and there to dwell together with the Friends of Allah in the Abode of Sanctity. As the text prays, may Allah cause us to reach them, and aid us in the ascent and the arrival, by the right of the Prophet and his pure progeny.

The image of the soul as an exile aching for a homeland it half-remembers is one the mystics of Islam returned to again and again. Jalal al-Din Rumi opened his *Mathnawi* with precisely this complaint, putting it in the mouth of a reed-flute cut from the reed-bed:

> Listen to the reed how it tells a tale, complaining of separations — saying, "Ever since I was parted from the reed-bed, my lament hath caused man and woman to moan.... Every one who is left far from his source wishes back the time when he was united with it." — Rumi, *Mathnawi*, Book I, lines 1–4, trans. Reynold A. Nicholson.

The reed cut from the bed and the spirit cut from the alam al-ruh (عالم الروح) are the same figure: the cry is the homesickness of something that knows, however dimly, that it does not belong here.

And the origin the soul strains back toward is, in the Quran, a covenant it once made and then forgot — the primordial assembly in which every spirit testified to its Lord before it ever entered this world:

> When thy Lord drew forth from the Children of Adam — from their loins — their descendants, and made them testify concerning themselves, [saying]: "Am I not your Lord?" — they said: "Yea! We do testify!" — Quran, the chapter of the Heights (chapter 7, verse 172), trans. Abdullah Yusuf Ali.

The knowledge of the origin, then, is in one sense a recollection: a turning back toward a Lord the soul once acknowledged with its own tongue and then lost sight of amid the world of generation and decay.

The text then characterizes this knowledge of the origin with a striking image. This, it says, is the knowledge around which all creatures revolve and toward which they incline. Every person, every creature, longs to move toward it and to attain it; it is the knowledge of the root, the knowledge of the origin — the answer to the question of where we came from and what our beginning was. All the creatures are circling about this knowledge, making their rounds around it, yearning to know where their origin lies. And yet, for all their circling, they cannot reach it. The text states it without softening: there is no path for any of them to it. For the generality of creatures there is no path at all to this knowledge; they revolve forever about a center they cannot attain.

There is, however, one exception, and it is decisive. Except for a few — for those who held fast to the Imams from the progeny of Muhammad, the peace of Allah be upon them, and who bound themselves to the Imams' blessed hudud (حُدُود). The few who attain to the knowledge of the origin are precisely those who held fast to the Imams from the household of Muhammad and committed themselves to the true and blessed ranks those Imams appoint. Only these reach that side; only these break out of the circle. The rest revolve endlessly around a goal they cannot reach. The lesson is plain, and it carries the whole weight of the chapter: the knowledge of the origin is not available to bare seeking, however intense. It is granted only through fast adherence to the Imams and submission to their appointed ranks — which is to say, through exactly the allegiance and action the path was built upon.

## The foundation laid

The foundation is now in place. These lessons are drawn from the sciences of the prophets and saints. The book that holds them is a single ordered structure of fives, and its declared method is to state pure reality directly, with no interpretation and no symbol. And the doctrine itself has its spine: the soul reaches the Oneness of Allah only by loving allegiance to the Friends of Allah and by lawful action, and by that road it returns from this world of corruption to the homeland of spirits from which it came. The creatures circle a center they cannot reach; only those who hold fast to the Imams break the circle. The hardest question this whole path raises still stands unanswered — the nature of the One toward whom the entire return is directed, and why no indication, no thought, and no name can reach Him at all. But the ground for even asking it has now been laid: an inherited science, an ordered book, a method of pure reality, and a road of allegiance and action that leads the exiled soul home.
