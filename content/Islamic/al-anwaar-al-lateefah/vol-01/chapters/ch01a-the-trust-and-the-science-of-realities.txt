# Trust and the Science of Realities

*Episode one of Al-Anwaar al-Lateefah, Volume One — The Oneness. Before a single doctrine of tawhid is taught, the recorder of these lessons stops to tell us what they are and how they reached us: an inherited wisdom drawn not from speculation but from the sciences of the prophets and the Friends of Allah, recorded by explicit permission and deposited in the treasury of the sacred mission. He then binds the listener with a covenant of safekeeping, names the symmetrical five-fold architecture of the book, and states its method — to set down pure reality, with no interpretation and no symbol. From there the doctrine itself opens: that access to the Oneness of Allah is won by two things only, loving allegiance and lawful action, and that this is the road by which the exiled soul returns to the homeland from which it first came.*

## A guarded invitation

What kind of teaching arrives with a warning that to copy it is a sin, and that those who fail to guard it face a punishment of cold iron? Most books want to be spread as widely as possible; the wider the audience, the better. The lessons gathered here begin from the opposite instinct. Before they will teach you anything about the Oneness of Allah, they ask you to understand that what you are about to receive is a guarded trust — knowledge meant for those prepared to carry it, not for indiscriminate circulation. That single inversion sets the tone for everything that follows. This is not information to be acquired; it is an inheritance to be protected. And the reason it must be protected is the same reason it is worth having: it claims to be the only road by which the human soul finds its way back home. This episode lays the foundation — where these lessons come from, the covenant that governs them, how the book is built, and the doctrine of the path itself.

## A trust bequeathed

The lessons gathered in the book *Anwaar al-Latifah* are composed of the lofty contents of the sciences of realities — the uloom-e-haqaiq. They are not drawn from speculation, nor from the ordinary disciplines of exoteric learning. Everything in them has been interpreted and unfolded entirely upon the foundation of the sciences of the prophets, the anbiya, and of the saints, the awliya. What is offered here is therefore a transmission of inherited wisdom — knowledge whose source lies in the teaching of those whom Allah has chosen as the bearers of His most intimate truths. Every realization expounded in these pages traces itself back to that prophetic and saintly fountainhead, and it is in that light that the listener must approach what follows.

The instinct to guard such knowledge rather than broadcast it is old in this tradition. Ali ibn Abi Talib, the Father of Imams, made it vivid when he took the hand of his disciple Kumayl ibn Ziyad, led him out into the open country, and confided what the heart can and cannot hold:

> These hearts are vessels, and the best of them is the one that retains [its contents] most.... Here — and he pointed to his breast — is abundant knowledge, could I but find bearers for it. — Ali ibn Abi Talib, the Father of Imams, in *Nahj al-Balagha* (compiled by al-Sharif al-Radi), Hikam (Saying) 147.

The difficulty, in that telling, was never a scarcity of knowledge; it was the scarcity of those fit to carry it. The realities are therefore entrusted, not published — and the present book inherits exactly that caution.

The recorder is equally explicit about how the lessons reached us, because their provenance is part of their authority. They were not recorded by private initiative, nor were they ever intended to be common property. He recorded them with the explicit permission — the ijazat — of the noble and esteemed one to whom this knowledge is ascribed, Hadrat Abdullah Sahib, with the prayer that Allah prolong his life and make us his ransom. The original purpose was a modest and intimate one: he undertook the recording for the benefit of his father and the other members of his family, that they might partake of these realities. Afterward, by the express command of his Aaqa and Mawla, the recording was deposited in the treasury of the Da'wat Sharif — entrusted, in other words, to the safekeeping of the sacred mission itself.

The authority behind these lessons is thus twofold and unambiguous. There is the permission of Hadrat Abdullah Sahib, which authorized the recording in the first place, and there is the command of the Aaqa, which consigned that recording to the treasury of the Da'wat Sharif. Two sanctions stand behind the words: one that allowed them to be set down, and one that placed them where they now belong. This is what the recorder means when he insists that these are a sanctioned trust and not a casual production. The listener is not overhearing a private lecture; he is being admitted, under conditions, to something that was authorized, gathered, and formally entrusted.

## The covenant of safekeeping

Because these lessons are a trust, their custody carries obligations that must be stated plainly at the very outset. The recorder makes a most humble request — an istida — that you do not email these lessons, nor exchange them in any manner upon the internet, nor store them upon your computer. The reasoning is not arbitrary caution. However secure an exchange may appear, once a thing has been placed upon the web there remains always the possibility that it becomes accessible to everyone, and that these lessons fall thereby into the hands of those who do not deserve them — the undeserving, the ghayr-mustahiqqin. The sciences of realities are not fit for indiscriminate circulation. They are meant for those prepared to receive them, and the careless dispersal of them does violence to their very nature.

That a weighty thing should be received as a trust — with real consequences for whether it is kept — is the Quran's own framing of the human condition:

> Truly We offered the Trust unto the heavens and the earth and the mountains, but they refused to carry it, and were wary of it — yet man carried it; truly he has proved himself an ignorant wrongdoer. — Quran 33:72 (al-Ahzab), trans. Seyyed Hossein Nasr et al., *The Study Quran*.

To be human, in this telling, is already to be the bearer of a trust that the very mountains declined. The covenant the recorder now asks of his listener is a small, deliberate echo of that primordial one: a thing accepted is a thing one becomes answerable for.

The consequence of disregarding this is to be understood with full seriousness. If anyone, despite this request, should commit such an act — copying these lessons, listening to them and passing them onward, transmitting them, handing them to another, modifying them, or performing any such deed with them without permission — then this is a sin in the sight of Allah and His Messenger. On the Day of Judgment, before Allah and His Messenger, the one who does this will stand solely responsible for it, and the recorder will be free of any responsibility for the act. The burden falls upon the doer alone. The chain of accountability rests on the hand that breaks the trust, not on the one who faithfully delivered it.

Nor is this a warning issued lightly or for mere effect. The Friends of Allah themselves have given news of a most severe punishment reserved for those who fail to safeguard these lofty sciences. They have specified it as the punishment of cold iron, which is described as the most severe and terrible of chastisements — may Allah protect us from it. The safeguarding of this knowledge is therefore not a matter of etiquette but of consequence reaching into the next world. The recorder closes this charge as he opened it, with a prayer: that Allah prolong the shadow of Sayyidna al-Da'i and of the noble dignitaries over us, and make their lights — their anwaar — flow continually upon us, by the right of our master and his pure family. The covenant and the blessing are spoken in one breath, because the same knowledge that can condemn the one who scatters it can illumine the one who guards it.

## The five-fold architecture

The book itself is structured according to a deliberate and symmetrical design, and the very name of its parts conveys something of its character. Each major division is called a Suradiq, and the work opens with the First Suradiq from the Book of Lights. The word Suradiq carries the sense of a curtain, and beyond that of a palace, a grand encampment, a great tent — a canopy or a royal pavilion within which the matters of the book unfold. It is the image of an enclosing pavilion, and inside it the realities are set out in order.

The architecture is built upon the number five throughout. The whole book comprises five Suradiqs. Each Suradiq, as the text states, has five chapters — five abwab. And each chapter in turn gathers within it five sections, five fusul. Five Suradiqs, each of five chapters, each chapter of five sections: by this symmetry the whole work amounts to one hundred and twenty-five sections in all. The design is not decorative. It tells the listener that what follows is a single ordered structure rather than a loose collection of talks, and that every section has a fixed place within a larger architecture of fives. With this structure fixed in mind, the book turns to the very first section of the first chapter of the first Suradiq, where the purpose of the whole enterprise is announced.

## The method of pure reality

The opening section states the book's method before it states any doctrine, and the method is striking. The author declares that what is intended here concerns the noble affections — the mawaddah — that lead the soul toward the noble obligations, and that he will treat them as a pure reality, taken whole and unmixed. In that reality, he says, there is no interpretation and no symbol. The phrase carries great weight. The book does not propose to expound the zahir, the outer exoteric meaning, nor to ascend into ta'wil, the esoteric interpretation. It proposes rather to set down the pure, unmixed reality of these matters directly.

The negations that follow are emphatic, and the grammar itself underscores them. When the author says there is "no interpretation" in it, the construction is what the grammarians call the negation of the genus — an absolute, categorical denial. To say merely that there is "no particular interpretation" would leave room for some interpretation somewhere; but to negate the genus is to say there is no interpretation whatsoever, none of the kind at all. The same holds for what follows: no symbol from it, and no allusion. The intention is pure reality, stripped of veiling interpretation, of symbol, and of allusion alike.

This matters for how the listener should hear everything that comes after. Much of the Ismaili tradition is famous for ta'wil — for reading an inner meaning beneath the surface of scripture and law. Here the author signals that he is doing something different. He is not lifting a veil to show the secret beneath the letter; he is stating the realities as realities, directly, as far as they can be stated at all. The whole difficulty of the book — the difficulty of speaking about an Originator who exceeds all speech — flows from this declared method. The author has bound himself to give us the thing itself, not a code for it, and the strain of keeping that promise about a subject that resists all naming is precisely the drama of the chapters to come.

## The twin path to tawhid

With the method fixed, the discourse widens to embrace the whole of existence. The book proposes to address all the existing things that appeared before our own existence, and it gives its reason at once. It is through the gnosis of those things — the marifah, the true knowing and acquaintance with all that came into being before us — that the soul is set upon the road. And it is through obedience to the Friends of Allah, and love of them, and drawing near to Allah by means of them, that the road is actually traveled. Crucial to this is adherence to their hudud — the ranks and bounds appointed for the believer — for these hudud are appointed and assigned from the direction of the Qa'im himself. And it is through the action, the amal, laid down in the shari'a, the religious law, that the road is completed. By the whole of this — gnosis, obedience, love, nearness, adherence to the appointed hudud, and lawful action — guidance is given toward the tawhid of Allah, the Most High. This, the text insists, is the only path by which one may reach the Oneness of Allah.

It is worth distilling the two great pillars upon which this access rests, because they recur throughout the doctrine. Access to the tawhid of Allah is attained through two things and two things only: wilayah and amal — allegiance and action. Wilayah is named also as muwalat, loyal allegiance, and as muhabbah, love; it is the bond of devotion to the Friends of Allah and submission to the ranks they appoint. And amal is the deed, the action laid down by the law. By these two together — by loving allegiance and by righteous action — one attains access to the Oneness of Allah. There is no other way through. Knowledge alone does not arrive; devotion alone does not arrive; the two must be joined.

That the road should run through allegiance to the chosen household is not the author's own innovation; it rests on the Prophet's final counsel to his community. In the tradition of the two weighty things, the Prophet Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him) is reported to have said:

> Verily I am leaving among you two weighty things: the Book of Allah and my progeny, the people of my household. So long as you hold fast to them both, you will never go astray after me. — narrated from Zayd ibn Arqam; *Sunan al-Tirmidhi* 3788 (the hadith al-thaqalayn); cf. *Sahih Muslim*, Kitab Fada'il al-Sahaba, 2408.

Scripture and the appointed ranks — amal and wilayah — are handed down together, in a single breath; to grasp one while letting the other fall is to lose the very guidance the Prophet entrusted. The book's twin pillars are an unfolding of this one bequest.

And the fruit of this attainment is twofold, as the text declares. By treading this path the ranks of the afterlife — the maad — are raised up for the soul, and salvation is achieved from this lower world of being and corruption, this realm of generation and decay in which we are presently confined. The path does not merely improve a life here; it lifts the soul's standing in the world to come and frees it from the very order of coming-to-be and passing-away. That double fruit — elevation in the afterlife and release from the world of corruption — is what the twin pillars of allegiance and action are for.

## The return to the origin

This salvation is, in its deepest meaning, a homecoming. The text speaks of returning to the origin from which the noble beginnings first came, and of dwelling with the Friends of Allah in the Abode of Sanctity, the dar al-quds. Here the doctrine of return is set forth. The movement is a leading-back toward the origin, the mabda — back to that source from which the noble beginnings first issued. For the soul does not belong to this lower world; this is not its native country. Our original homeland — our watan-e-asli — is the world of spirits, the alam al-ruh. That is the land from which we came, and to which the whole movement of the religious life is directed as a return. The goal is to make our way back to that origin, and there to dwell together with the Friends of Allah in the Abode of Sanctity. As the text prays, may Allah cause us to reach them, and aid us in the ascent and the arrival, by the right of the Prophet and his pure progeny.

The image of the soul as an exile aching for a homeland it half-remembers is one the mystics of Islam returned to again and again. Jalal al-Din Rumi opened his *Mathnawi* with precisely this complaint, putting it in the mouth of a reed-flute cut from the reed-bed:

> Listen to the reed how it tells a tale, complaining of separations — saying, "Ever since I was parted from the reed-bed, my lament hath caused man and woman to moan.... Every one who is left far from his source wishes back the time when he was united with it." — Rumi, *Mathnawi*, Book I, lines 1–4, trans. Reynold A. Nicholson.

The reed cut from the bed and the spirit cut from the alam al-ruh are the same figure: the cry is the homesickness of something that knows, however dimly, that it does not belong here.

And the origin the soul strains back toward is, in the Quran, a covenant it once made and then forgot — the primordial assembly in which every spirit testified to its Lord before it ever entered this world:

> When thy Lord drew forth from the Children of Adam — from their loins — their descendants, and made them testify concerning themselves, [saying]: "Am I not your Lord?" — they said: "Yea! We do testify!" — Quran 7:172 (al-A'raf), trans. Abdullah Yusuf Ali.

The knowledge of the origin, then, is in one sense a recollection: a turning back toward a Lord the soul once acknowledged with its own tongue and then lost sight of amid the world of generation and decay.

The text then characterizes this knowledge of the origin with a striking image. This, it says, is the knowledge around which all creatures revolve and toward which they incline. Every person, every creature, longs to move toward it and to attain it; it is the knowledge of the root, the knowledge of the origin — the answer to the question of where we came from and what our beginning was. All the creatures are circling about this knowledge, making their rounds around it, yearning to know where their origin lies. And yet, for all their circling, they cannot reach it. The text states it without softening: there is no path for any of them to it. For the generality of creatures there is no path at all to this knowledge; they revolve forever about a center they cannot attain.

There is, however, one exception, and it is decisive. Except for a few — for those who held fast to the Imams from the progeny of Muhammad, the peace of Allah be upon them, and who bound themselves to the Imams' blessed hudud. The few who attain to the knowledge of the origin are precisely those who held fast to the Imams from the household of Muhammad and committed themselves to the true and blessed ranks those Imams appoint. Only these reach that side; only these break out of the circle. The rest revolve endlessly around a goal they cannot reach. The lesson is plain, and it carries the whole weight of the chapter: the knowledge of the origin is not available to bare seeking, however intense. It is granted only through fast adherence to the Imams and submission to their appointed ranks — which is to say, through exactly the wilayah and amal the path was built upon.

## The foundation laid

The foundation is now in place. These lessons are a sanctioned trust, drawn from the sciences of the prophets and saints, recorded by permission and deposited in the treasury of the sacred mission, and bound by a covenant of careful safekeeping. The book that holds them is a single ordered structure of fives, and its declared method is to state pure reality directly, with no interpretation and no symbol. And the doctrine itself has its spine: the soul reaches the Oneness of Allah only by loving allegiance to the Friends of Allah and by lawful action, and by that road it returns from this world of corruption to the homeland of spirits from which it came. The creatures circle a center they cannot reach; only those who hold fast to the Imams break the circle. The hardest question this whole path raises still stands unanswered — the nature of the One toward whom the entire return is directed, and why no indication, no thought, and no name can reach Him at all. But the ground for even asking it has now been laid: a guarded trust, an ordered book, a method of pure reality, and a road of allegiance and action that leads the exiled soul home.
