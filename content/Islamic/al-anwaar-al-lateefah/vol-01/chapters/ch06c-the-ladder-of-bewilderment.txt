## Where this episode picks up

Could the very moment a teacher becomes a kind of "god" to his bewildered student be the first rung of the ladder that leads to the one true God?

The previous episode showed a man so refined by obedience that he becomes a house of Allah. This episode follows what happens when a seeker stands before such a man — and discovers that the doctrine of Tawhid is not a formula to be recited but a climb to be made. We will trace six teachings: how a higher rank becomes a "god" for the one bewildered before him, in a sense that has nothing to do with creating; how that bewilderment, verified rank by rank, becomes a ladder up to the First Intellect and beyond; how the linked circles of the hierarchy came to be, and how the very letters of the word "god" encode their structure; the sharp line between the created gods and the one true God; the tranquil soul that, having climbed, is called to return; and finally where all of this is written in the opening chapter of the Qur'an. The circle and its center were established earlier as the image of bewilderment before the unreachable; here that image is set in motion and climbed.

## The birth of a "god"

The book brings us to the heart of the chapter: the scene of teacher and disciple. When a seeker comes to the Mukathir with sincerity, soundness of intention, and purity of nature, and converses with him, the Mukathir bestows upon him spiritual speech and divine knowledge — knowledge so sublime that it had never before crossed the seeker's heart, nor had his ears, or the ears of his likes, ever so much as suspected it. Confronted with such elevated realities, the disciple's intellect is bewildered and he is astounded; he cannot fathom how his teacher attained this station and these sublime matters. And in that bewilderment the Mukathir becomes for him a god, a master of his neck, one whose guidance and great precedence he has come to recognize.

Everything depends on grasping precisely the sense in which the teacher is here called a "god," for this is the second meaning that was deferred earlier. He is a god in the sense that he is the center of the circle. As was established before, a bewildered circle revolves around its center, and the revolving is the very motion of the bewilderment; here the disciple revolves around his teacher as a circle revolves around its center, drawn into orbit by his astonishment. It is in this sense alone — as the center around which the bewildered seeker circles — that the Mukathir is a "god" for him. It is emphatically not in the sense that he is the creator of beings; the power of origination was negated from all the ranks at the very outset. He is a "god" only as the center of bewildered orbit, the master who holds the seeker's neck for its release, the one whose guidance, greatness, and precedence the seeker now acknowledges. The word that sounds like blasphemy turns out to name a precise relation: not maker to made, but center to circle.

## The ladder of bewilderment

The decisive step lifts that bewilderment out of idolatry and turns it into Tawhid. The believing seeker comes to verify that the very Mukathir before whom he stands bewildered is himself bewildered before the rank above him. The seeker sees that he is bewildered by the Mukathir — but that the Mukathir, in turn, is poor, needy, and bewildered before his own bestower, the one above him. And the one above the Mukathir is in turn bewildered before the one above him, and so the chain continues, rank upon rank, in the same manner through the physical ranks, until it reaches the Imam, peace be upon him. From the Imam it ascends further, toward the abstract intellects — those that are not in the physical world at all, the realities of the higher realms. And the ascent ends, at the summit, at the First Intellect, which was itself bewildered before all the originated beings, bewildered at how it attained its precedence, even though all were brought into existence together at one time. The seeker then verifies the bewilderment of the First Intellect itself before its Originator — the Originator of itself and of all its kinds — and its perplexity before Him.

The whole chain of bewilderment is thus laid bare, from the disciple before his teacher up to the First Intellect before the Originator. And a deep lesson is embedded in this universal bewilderment: the first step in acquiring knowledge is humility, the confession that one does not know. One must first become, as it were, ignorant — must own one's not-knowing — before one can acquire. The station of bewilderment is therefore necessary for every seeker, as the circle revolves around its center; that very revolving, that perplexity, is the precondition of gnosis. Having traced the bewilderment to its source, the seeker performs the act of Tawhid. He negates divinity from the rank in his own horizon — from the Mukathir — and from every rank above him, physical and spiritual, all the way to the end of the chain. He declares: none of these is a god who gives me my being; each is bewildered before another above him, the First Intellect itself bewildered before the Originator. The very condition the First Intellect holds toward the Originator — its bewilderment — the seeker now recognizes reproduced at every level, including in his own relation to his teacher. So he affirms that this Mukathir, for all his greatness and the majesty of his station, is not his god; above him is one, and above that one another, up to the summit, and all of them are bewildered, and the truly Real is the Originator alone. He affirms divinity for Allah alone and proclaims His greatness — He besides whom there is no god.

## The circles and the word "god"

How did these linked circles come to be? The book answers by recounting the formation of the da'wah in the spiritual world. When the summons was given there, those who joined and repented with the repentance of the caller ascended along with him; those who did not join were summoned anew. The pattern works like this: a circle has at its center a forerunner; he calls, and those who answer and repent with his repentance ascend with him; the final individual left behind, who has not ascended, is given the command, "Our work is finished; now you do the work" — and he becomes the center of a new circle. Around him gather those who repent with his repentance, and again the last one left is given the command in his turn. In this way circle was generated from circle, each formed upon the pattern of the first, which was established there in the spiritual world. And the bewilderment that orders each circle is this: the center of a given circle, around whom others revolve, must himself revolve around the center of the circle above his own. He too is in orbit; the structure of bewildered revolution is reproduced at every level. All of this is contained in the words "there is no god but Allah."

This brings the book to a remarkable reading of the very word for "god," which it finds to encode the three conditions present in every one of these circles. The word is composed of three letters. The first shows that there is precedence without an inspirer — that is, the First Intellect, who preceded all the rest by his own act, with none above him to prompt or inspire him. The second shows the condition of the one who advances not by independent precedence but by seeing another and repenting with his repentance — the station of the one emanated from the First, who attained his rank by following. And the third shows the giving of the summons, which was performed by the seven intellects; the tenth did not give it. These three conditions will always be present in every circle, for in every circle there is everything; the entire story of the life of the universe is contained in this composition. Whose station is the first letter? The First Intellect. Whose station is the second? The one emanated from the First. And the third is the summons given by the seven. So the very letters of the word "god" trace the structure of forerunner, follower, and summons that constitutes every circle of the hierarchy.

## The created gods and the one true God

The book now draws out fully the contrast between the created "gods" and the one true God. The seeker affirms divinity for Allah and proclaims His greatness, declaring that there is no god from among these "deities" — these gods that are created, invented, and originated. And here is their distinguishing mark: each one of these created gods carries within himself the name of the nourisher who stands above him, and the need that calls him upward toward that nourisher. A created god is recognizable precisely as a created god by this — that he is sustained by one above him and impelled by a need toward one above him; he is never self-sufficient. The only being who is not such a created god, who has no nourisher above him and no upward-calling need, is the one before whom the bewilderment of high and low alike is occasioned by the sheer loftiness of His state — the one above whom there is no higher, whose every subordinate is bewildered before Him while He is bewildered before none. So there is no god besides Him.

The book then completes the threefold movement at the highest level. The seeker declares the one God transcendent above the physical ranks, which are limited, and above the spiritual ranks, which are unlimited — for the true God, the nourisher of all, is beyond both the bounded corporeal hierarchy and the unbounded spiritual hierarchy alike. This is the fullness of tanzih. Then comes tajrid: the seeker strips from the Originator all the names and attributes and descriptions and relations that he had affirmed for the ranks, for those belong to the physical and spiritual ranks, while the Originator is beyond them, God in the absolute sense, divested of all that can be predicated. And finally the seeker returns to his own rank. The believer is bewildered before his teacher; above the teacher are ranks, and above them ranks, up to the First Intellect, which is itself bewildered before another; and having traced all this, the seeker returns and says, in effect: each must do his own work, and I will do mine. He returns to his own station, to his bewilderment within it, and to his obedience and commitment to his teacher. At that moment he becomes the one who performs true Tawhid, true tanzih, and true tajrid — with gnosis, with religion, with knowledge, and with certainty.

## The tranquil soul returns

To such a one Allah speaks the words of the Qur'an: "O tranquil soul, return to your Lord, well-pleased and well-pleasing" (chapter 89, verses 27 to 28). The crucial word is "return," for the soul ascends to the very top and then comes back. This is the movement: it goes up the linked circles, and then it descends to its own station. As established earlier, the journey of the creature is a return to its origin; here that return is named precisely. "Return to your Lord" means return to your bestower, the one who poured knowledge into you. And "well-pleased and well-pleasing" means both pleased together: you yourself well-pleased, and your bestower well-pleased with you. The soul's tranquility lies in what it has come to know — its serenity is the fruit of its gnosis — and its pleasure lies in the command of its Lord, the Lord to whom it is returned. And it is well-pleasing in His sight, and in the sight of all the ranks to whose gnosis the soul has reached and arrived. The movement closes in grateful praise: "Praise be to Allah who guided us to this; we would not have been guided had Allah not guided us. Indeed the messengers of our Lord came with the truth" (chapter 7, verse 43). The tranquil soul, having reached the end of the gnosis of its bestower, is summoned home, both pleased and pleasing.

## The Originator in the Fatiha

A question naturally arises: this Originator who created — is there mention of Him, and of the world of souls, anywhere in the Qur'an? The book affirms that there is. Different phrases at different places convey the matter — as in the verse that we were created from a single soul. But the summary of all that the Qur'an teaches about the Originator is the opening chapter, the chapter of praise, and its details are founded upon its first verse, "Praise be to Allah, Lord of the worlds," for He is the Lord of all the worlds, and from this the qualities of Allah are unfolded. The book then shows how that opening chapter encodes the three classes of people. The teaching is that Allah made three kinds: the repentant, those who incur wrath, and the astray. Where in the opening chapter are they found? In the verse, "Guide us upon the straight path, the path of those upon whom You have bestowed favor" (chapter 1, verses 6 to 7). Those upon whom favor was bestowed are the repentant — the favor given them was that they should return. Then the verse continues, "not of those upon whom is wrath": these are the insistent ones, who persist in their error and upon whom wrath has therefore descended. And the third, "nor of the astray": these are the bewildered, lost in a perplexity through which they never found their way. So the path that must be crossed is set before three classes — the repentant who are favored to return, the insistent who incur wrath, and the lost who go astray in bewilderment.

But the reality of the Originator Himself cannot be grasped, and this too the Qur'an establishes, declaring Him glorified above whatever attributes are ascribed to Him. Whatever attributes you describe for Him, He is pure of them, beyond them all — and this is the very meaning of tanzih: that whatever the bewildered seekers, incapable of His complete gnosis, ascribe to Him, He transcends. Even the First Intellect, their own bestower, was incapable of the gnosis of the Originator; it did not possess that gnosis but was bewildered before Him, and it was on account of this bewilderment that the First Intellect became a god for those below. And what word did they finally use of the Originator? "He" — and even that under compulsion, for they had no other; as established earlier, the naming is a fault that comes already coupled with the asking of forgiveness, uttered out of necessity, because to affirm our own existence we must affirm the Existent, and to affirm Him we must address Him by some word, and the only word that does not falsely circumscribe Him is "He."

## What this episode lands

This episode has turned the doctrine of Tawhid into a climb. When a seeker stands bewildered before his teacher, the teacher becomes for him a "god" — not as a maker of beings, but only as the center around which his bewildered circle turns. The climb begins the moment the seeker verifies that his teacher is himself bewildered before the rank above him, and that one before the next, rank upon rank, up to the First Intellect, which is bewildered before the Originator. Tracing that ladder, the seeker negates divinity from every rank and affirms it for Allah alone, completing tanzih and tajrid at the summit before returning to his own station. The linked circles were born in the spiritual world from a single repeating pattern of forerunner, follower, and summons — the very pattern the three letters of the word "god" encode. A created god always carries the name of a nourisher above him and a need that calls him upward; the one true God has neither, and so there is no god besides Him. The soul that completes this climb is the tranquil soul, called to return to its Lord well-pleased and well-pleasing — and the whole teaching, the book shows, is written into the opening chapter of the Qur'an and its three classes of men.

## Closing

We have climbed the ladder of bewilderment to the First Intellect and found even it bewildered before the One who made it. But the book now turns from the gnosis of the ranks to the act that produced them — the origination itself. How does something come from nothing at all? The next episode follows the Originator's first act: the bringing-forth of an entire world out of sheer non-existence, in a single instant, as nothing but a gift.
