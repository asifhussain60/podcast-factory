## The Opening Covenant and the Science of Realities

### A Trust Bequeathed: The Nature and Provenance of These Lessons

`A'udhu Billahi min al-Shaytan al-Rajim. Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim. Wa sallallahu 'ala rasulihi wa alihi al-tayyibin al-tahirin wa sallama 'alayhim ajma'in.` (I seek refuge in Allah from the accursed Satan. In the name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful. And may Allah's blessings be upon His Messenger and his pure and purified family, and may peace be upon them all.)

Believing brothers and sisters, the lessons gathered in the book *Anwaar al-Latifah* are composed of the lofty contents of the sciences of realities — the *'uloom-e-haqa'iq*. These are not lessons drawn from speculation or the ordinary disciplines of exoteric learning. They have been interpreted and unfolded entirely upon the foundation of the sciences of the prophets, the *anbiya'*, and of the saints, the *awliya'*. What is offered here, therefore, is a transmission of inherited wisdom — knowledge whose source lies in the teaching of those whom Allah has chosen as the bearers of His most intimate truths. Every realization expounded in these pages traces itself back to that prophetic and saintly fountainhead, and it is in that light that the reader must approach what follows.

These lessons were not recorded by private initiative, nor were they ever intended to be common property. I recorded them with the explicit permission — the *ijazat* — of Maulaya, the noble and esteemed one to whom this knowledge is ascribed, `'awliy al-ne'mati al-mansub al-karim wal-faqeem`, Hadrat 'Abdullah Sahib, `'atala Allahu baqa'ahu wa ja'alna fida'` (may Allah prolong his life and make us his ransom). I undertook this recording originally for the benefit of my father and the other members of my family, that they might partake of these realities. Afterward, by the express command of my Aaqa and Maula, this recording has been deposited in the treasury of the *Da'wat Sharief* — entrusted, in other words, to the safekeeping of the sacred mission itself. The authority behind these lessons is thus twofold and unambiguous: the permission of Hadrat 'Abdullah Sahib that authorized their recording, and the command of the Aaqa that consigned them to the *Da'wat Sharief*'s treasury. They are a sanctioned trust, not a casual production.

### The Covenant of Safekeeping and Its Sanctions

Because these lessons are a trust, their custody carries obligations that must be stated plainly at the outset. I most humbly request — *istida'* — that you do not email these lessons, nor attempt to exchange them in any manner upon the internet, nor store them upon your computer. The reasoning is not arbitrary caution. However secure an exchange may appear, once a thing has been placed upon the web there remains always the possibility that it becomes accessible to everyone, and that these lessons fall thereby into the hands of those who do not deserve them — the *ghair-mustahiqqeen*, the undeserving. The sciences of realities are not fit for indiscriminate circulation; they are meant for those prepared to receive them, and the careless dispersal of them does violence to their very nature.

Let the consequence of disregarding this be understood with full seriousness. If anyone, despite this request, should commit such an act — copying these lessons, listening to them and passing them onward, transmitting them, handing them to another, modifying them, or performing any such deed (*fe'l*) with them without permission — then know that this is a sin (*gunah*) in the sight of Allah and His Messenger. On the Day of Judgment, *baroz-e-qiyamat*, before Allah and His Messenger, the one who does this will stand solely responsible for it, and I shall be free of any responsibility for the act. The burden falls upon the doer alone; the chain of accountability rests on the hand that breaks the trust, not on the one who faithfully delivered it.

Nor is this a warning issued lightly or for mere effect. The *Awliya'* of Allah themselves have given news of a most severe punishment — *'azab* — reserved for those who fail to safeguard these lofty sciences, these *'uloom-e-'aliya*. They have specified it as the punishment of cold iron, which is described as the most severe and terrible of chastisements. `A'adhana Allah` — may Allah protect us from it. The safeguarding of this knowledge is therefore not a matter of etiquette but of consequence reaching into the next world. May Allah prolong the shadow of Sayyidna al-Da'i and of the Manasib-e-Kiram over us, and make their lights — their *anwaar* — flow continually upon us. Ameen. `Bi-haqqi Sayyidina Maulana Muhammadin wa 'ala aali Sayyidina Maulana Muhammadin wa barik wa sallim.` (By the right of our master, Maulana Muhammad, and upon the family of our master, Maulana Muhammad, and grant blessings and peace.)

### The Architecture of the Book and Its Stated Purpose

The book itself is structured according to a deliberate and symmetrical design, and the very title of its parts conveys something of its character. Each major division is called a *Suradiq* — and we open now with `Al-Suradiq al-Awwal`, the First Suradiq, from *Kitab al-Anwar*. The word *Suradiq* (also rendered *Sardaq*) carries the sense of a curtain, and beyond that of a palace, a grand encampment, a great tent — a *shamiyana* or *khaima*. It is the image of an enclosing canopy or a royal pavilion, within which the matters of the book unfold. The architecture is built upon the number five throughout. The whole book comprises five Suradiqs. Each Suradiq, as the text states, has `khamsatu abwab` — five chapters, five *babs*. And each *bab* in turn `yujma'u khamsata fusul` — it gathers within it five sections, five *fasls*. Five Suradiqs, each of five chapters, each chapter of five sections: by this symmetry the whole work amounts to one hundred and twenty-five sections in all. With this structure fixed in mind, we turn to `Al-faslu al-awwalu minha` — the very first section of the first chapter of the first Suradiq.

The opening section announces the purpose of the entire enterprise. The text begins: `Naqulu wa lamma kana ma huwa al-maqsudu wa al-kalamu 'ala al-mawaddati al-sharifati al-mu'addati ila al-wajibat al-sharifah haqiqatan mahdatan` (We say: and since that which is intended, and the discourse here, concerns the noble affections that lead to the noble obligations, taken as a pure reality), `la ta'weela fiha wa la ramza minha` (in which there is no interpretation and no symbol). The author's aim is declared at once: to treat of the noble affections — the *mawaddah* — that conduct the soul toward the noble obligations, and to treat of them as a pure reality, *haqiqatan mahdatan*. This phrase carries great weight. The book does not propose to expound the *zahir*, the outer exoteric meaning, nor to ascend into *ta'weel*, the esoteric interpretation; it proposes rather to set down the pure, unmixed reality of these matters directly.

The negations that follow are emphatic, and the grammar itself underscores them. `La ta'weela` is read with what the grammarians call *nafi al-jins*, the negation of the genus — that is, an absolute, categorical denial. Were one to say merely `la ta'weelun fihi`, it would mean only "there is no particular interpretation in it"; but `la ta'weela` means there is no interpretation whatsoever in it, none of the genus at all. So too with what follows: `wa la ramza minha` — and no symbol (*ramz*) from it — and no *ishara*, no allusion. The intention is therefore pure reality, stripped of veiling interpretation, symbol, and allusion alike. This is the declared method of the book.

### The Path to Tawhid: Gnosis, Love, and Action

The discourse next widens to embrace the whole of existence. The text continues: `Thumma al-kalamu 'ala kaffati al-mawjudat` — then the discourse is upon all existing things. The book proposes to address all the existents that have appeared before our own existence, to take them as its subject. And the reason it does so is given immediately: `idh bi-ma'rifati dhalika wa al-ittila'i 'alayhi wa ta'ati awliya' Allahi wa muhabbatihim wa al-taqarrubi ila Allahi bihim wa bi-luzumi hududihim al-mansubina min qibali al-qa'im al-'ilmi wa al-'amali bima warada min al-shara'i' allati la yutada ila tawhidi Allahi ta'ala wa takabbar` (for by the gnosis of that, and the acquaintance with it, and obedience to the Friends of Allah and love for them, and drawing near to Allah through them, and adhering to their appointed Hudud from the direction of the Qa'im, with the knowledge and the action that have come through the religious laws — by all this one is guided to the Oneness of Allah, the Exalted and the Supreme).

The teaching here is the heart of the matter, and it must be grasped in its full articulation. It is through the *ma'rifah*, the gnosis, of all those existing things which came into being before us — through truly knowing them and gaining acquaintance with them — that the soul is set upon the road. And it is through obedience to the *Awliya' Allah*, the Friends of Allah, and through love of them, and through drawing near to Allah by means of them, that the road is traveled. Crucial to this is adherence to their *hudud* — the ranks and bounds appointed for the believer — for these *hudud* are *mansubin*, that is, appointed and assigned, from the direction of the Qa'im himself. And it is through the action, the *'amal*, that is laid down in the *shari'ah*, the religious law, that the road is completed. By the whole of this — gnosis, obedience, love, nearness, adherence to the appointed *hudud*, and lawful action — guidance is given toward the *tawhid* of Allah, the Most High. This is the only path by which one may reach the *tawhid* of Allah.

It is worth distilling the two great pillars upon which this access rests, for they recur throughout the doctrine. Access to the *tawhid* of Allah is attained through two things and two things only: *wilayah* and *'amal* — allegiance and action. *Wilayah* is here named also as *muwalat* (loyal allegiance) and *muhabbah* (love); it is the bond of devotion to the Friends of Allah. And *'amal* is the deed, the action laid down by the law. By these two together — by loving allegiance and by righteous action — one attains access to the *tawhid* of Allah. There is no other way through. And the fruit of this attainment is twofold, as the text declares: `wa bi-dhalika ta'lu darajatu al-ma'ad` (and by that, the ranks of the afterlife are elevated), `wa yakunu al-khalasu min hadha al-'alami al-kawni wa al-fasad` (and salvation comes from this world of being and corruption). By treading this path the ranks of the *ma'ad*, the afterlife, are raised up for the soul, and salvation is achieved from this lower world of being and corruption — this realm of generation and decay in which we are presently confined.

### The Original Homeland and the Return to the Origin

This salvation is, in its deepest meaning, a homecoming. The text continues: `wa yumkinu al-qawdu ila al-mabda'i alladhi minhu al-ibtida'atu al-sharifah` (and it becomes possible to return to the origin from which the noble beginnings came), `wa al-kawnu ma'ahum fi dar al-quds` (and to dwell with them in the Abode of Sanctity). Here the doctrine of return is set forth. *Qawd* signifies the return, the leading-back, toward the *mabda'*, the origin — back to that source from which the noble beginnings (*ibtida'at*) first issued. The soul does not belong to this lower world; this is not its native country. Our original homeland — our *watan-e-asli* — is the world of spirits, the *'alam al-ruh*. That is the land from which we came, and to which the whole movement of the religious life is directed as a return. The goal is to make our way back to that origin, and there `wa al-kawnu ma'ahum fi dar al-quds` — to dwell together with the Friends of Allah in *dar al-quds*, the Abode of Sanctity. As the text prays: `ballaghana Allahu ilayhim` (may Allah cause us to reach them), `wa a'anana 'ala al-irtiqai wa al-wusuli ilayhim bi-haqqi Muhammadin wa alihi al-tahirin, salawatullahi 'alayhim ajma'in` (and may He aid us in the ascent and the arrival to them, by the right of Muhammad and his pure progeny, the blessings of Allah be upon them all).

The text then characterizes this knowledge of the origin in a striking image: `wa hadha huwa al-'ilmu alladhi yaduruna 'alayhi wa yajuruna ladayhi` (and this is that knowledge around which they revolve and toward which they incline). This is the knowledge that all creatures circle around. Every person, every creature, longs to move toward it and to attain it; it is the knowledge of the *asl*, the root, the knowledge of the *mabda'*, the origin — the answer to the question of where we came from and what our beginning was. All the creatures, the *khala'iq*, are circling about this knowledge, making their rounds around it, yearning to know where their origin lies. And yet, for all their circling, they cannot reach it. The text states it without softening: `wa la sabila li-ahadin minhum ilayhi` (and there is no path for any of them to it). For the generality of creatures there is no path at all to this knowledge; they revolve forever about a center they cannot attain.

There is, however, one exception, and it is decisive. `Illa al-qalil` — except for a few. `Mimman i'tasama bi-al-a'immati min al-i Muhammad, salamu Allahi 'alayhim ajma'in, wa iltazama hududahum al-mayamin` (from among those who held fast to the Imams from the progeny of Muhammad, the peace of Allah be upon them all, and bound themselves to their blessed Hudud). The few who attain to the knowledge of the origin are precisely those who held fast to the *a'imma*, the Imams from the household of Muhammad, and who committed themselves to the true and blessed *hudud* of those Imams. Only these attain that side; only these break out of the circle. The rest revolve endlessly around a goal they cannot reach. The lesson is plain: the knowledge of the origin is not available to bare seeking, however intense; it is granted only through fast adherence to the Imams and submission to their appointed ranks.

### The Transcendence of the Originator Beyond Indication and Thought

We move now to the second section of the first chapter of the first Suradiq — `al-faslu al-thani min al-babi al-awwali min al-suradiqi al-awwal` — which turns to the nature of the One toward whom all this is directed. The text opens: `wa kana la dalila 'ala dhikri dhalika illa bi-dhikri Allahi ta'ala wa takabbar alladhi bi-dhikrihi fi kulli shay'in yustaftahu, wa bi-asma'ihi al-husna yutawassalu ilayhi fi kulli matlubin wa yustanjahu` (and there was no guide to the mention of that except by the mention of Allah, the Exalted and Supreme, by whose mention every thing is opened, and by whose most beautiful names a means is sought to Him in every request, and success is attained). There is no path to the mention of these realities except by first mentioning Allah; without His mention nothing can be opened. The verb `yustaftahu` — "it is opened," from the root *fataha* — belongs to the form of *istif'al*, the form of seeking; *istiftah*, also called *iftitah*, means to seek the opening, to make the request that a thing be begun. By the mention of Allah, every thing is unlocked and the opening is sought. And `wa bi-asma'ihi al-husna` — by His most beautiful names, the *asma' al-husna* — `yutawassalu ilayhi`, a means (*tawassul*) is taken to Him, from the root *wasala*; `fi kulli matlubin`, in every object of seeking (*matlub*, that which is sought, as against *talib*, the one who seeks); `wa yustanjahu`, and success (from *najaha*, *istinjah*) is attained. Through His names, in every request, success is granted.

The text now states the cornerstone of right belief concerning the divine nature: `wa kana musabbahan muta'aliyan 'an an yaqa'a 'alayhi isharatu mushirin wa yatajasaru nahwahu khatiru khatirin bi-tawahhumin aw tafkirin` (and He is the One glorified, exalted far above that any indicator's indication should fall upon Him, or that the thought of any thinker should dare toward Him by imagination or by contemplation). He is *musabbah* — the One whose glorification (*tasbih*) is performed; not *musabbih*, for it is we who are the glorifiers, never He. And He is *muta'ali*, the One exalted on high, from the root *'uluww*. He is so exalted that no *ishara* — no indication of any indicator — can possibly fall upon Him so as to point toward Him. This, the text insists, is the true statement of the matter; it is the very foundation of beliefs, the basis upon which correct doctrine stands: that no indication can reach Him. Nor can any thought dare to cross over toward Him. The verb *yatajasaru*, from *jisr* (a bridge), conveys the image of a bridge that cannot be crossed: the *khatir*, the thought of the heart, cannot bridge the gap toward Him, neither by *tawahhum* (imagination) nor by *tafkir* (contemplation). No imagining and no reasoning achieves the objective. We can know only that, since we were created, there is One who created us; but what He is in Himself, what His nature is, of this we know nothing whatever.

This truth is illuminated by a celebrated exchange from the time of Imam Ali. A man named Ibn al-Kawwa', intending to corner the Commander of the Faithful with a question he could not answer, came and asked him: `Hal ra'ayta Allah?` — "Have you seen your God? Have you seen Allah?" Imam Ali replied, "Woe to you — could I worship a God I had not seen? How could I worship a God without having seen Him?" Then the man pressed: "How, then, did you see Him?" And the Imam answered: "I did not see Him with the eyes; I saw Him with the *basirah* — with insight." It is a mighty saying of our Mawla. The vision of Allah is not the vision of the physical eye, which cannot fall upon Him any more than indication or thought can; it is the vision of insight, the inner perception of the heart that affirms Him without grasping His essence.

### Names and Attributes Negated from the Originator

From this transcendence a profound consequence follows concerning the divine names and attributes. The text declares: `wa kana al-i'rabu 'anhu bi-Allahi ta'ala wa bi-al-mubdi'i bi-al-huwiyyati al-muta'aliyah ghayra waqi'in 'alayhi hadhani al-ismani wa al-sifatu` (and the expression about Him by "Allah, the Exalted," and by "the Originator," with the exalted Ipseity — these two names and the attribute do not fall upon Him). When we express something about Him by calling Him "Allah Ta'ala," or by calling Him *Mubdi'*, the Originator, or by referring to Him by the exalted *huwiyyah* — the Ipseity, the bare "He" (*huwa*) — none of these names, and no attribute, actually falls upon Him. Neither name nor attribute lights upon Him.

The reason is stated with rigor: `idh al-asma'u wa al-sifatu la taqa'u illa 'ala misliha` (for names and attributes do not fall except upon their like). A name or an attribute can be applied only to something of its own kind, something like itself. Consider what we do when we name Him: we say "Allah" in order to recognize Him, wishing to know Him, and so we assign Him a name — and we do so from our own tongue. But you are yourself the creator of that tongue and of those names; the very instrument by which you interpret Allah is a thing of your own making. As the Commander of the Faithful said, `al-asma'u wa al-sifatu dalalatun 'ala al-musamma` — names and attributes are indicators pointing to the named, and they point only to their like. We observed mercy in some creature and so applied "Merciful" to that one; the attribute fell upon its like, upon a knowledge and an arrangement (`'ala al-'ilmi wa al-tadbir`) of the kind we could perceive.

But these names and attributes are themselves *muhdathah* — created, originated things. The distinction must be held precisely: *muhdith* is the one who originates, while *muhdath* is the thing originated. The names and attributes are created; they are not their own creators. And they are created specifically by human beings: it is a human who pronounces a name, a human who assigns it. You named the child; you are the one who gave the name. They are, then, our given names — *muhdathah*, brought into being by us. And here is the crux: `wa al-muhdathu la yaqa'u 'ala al-muhdithi misluhu` (and the originated thing does not fall upon its originator as its like). A created thing cannot fall upon its creator in a manner befitting an equal; the made cannot characterize its maker as though they were of one kind. As Sayyidna Mu'ayyad al-Din said — and he lived in the time of Mawlana Mustansir Billah, holding the rank of *Bab al-Abwab* — `wa lamma kanati al-hurufu al-mu'jamu muhdathatan` (and since the letters of the alphabet are originated things), they cannot serve as a true indicator of the very Originator who made them. `Lam takun tadullu 'ala al-mabda'i al-sani'i laha dhalalatan muhaqqiqatan musaddiqatan 'ala misliha` (they could not indicate the originating Maker that made them with a true and verifying indication upon His like).

A natural objection arises here, and the text meets it. One might protest: it is a fine argument that we are the creators of our words, yet there is a limitation in us as well; many things proceed by His command before any word issues from us, so are we truly the creators of these things? The answer holds firmly to the point at issue. The words I am speaking — these I am indeed creating; of these spoken words I am the maker. The argument rests on the faculty of choice and creation that we do possess: with our own creation — the tongue and the names that are our handiwork — we are attempting to characterize our own Creator, and that is precisely what cannot be done. The choice we have is real, but it does not extend to recognizing Him; we have no power of choice by which to know Him. He dwells in such a *ghayb*, such an unseen, that we cannot lift its curtains. We may say only this much: in order to establish our own existence, we affirm that there must be an Originator for us. Who created us, what He is — this remains beyond.

### The First Intellect as the Locus of Names and Attributes

If the names and attributes do not fall upon Allah, the question presses: upon what do they fall? The text answers: `wa idh kanat al-qaulun kadhalika kanat hadhihi al-asma'u wa al-sifatu manfiyyatan 'an Allahi ta'ala wa takabbara wa kanat waqi'atan 'ala ibda'ihi alladhi huwa al-'aqlu al-awwal` (and since the matter is thus, these names and attributes are negated from Allah, the Exalted and Supreme, and they fall instead upon His origination, which is the First Intellect). All these names and attributes are negated, *manfiyyah*, from Allah Ta'ala. They are not predicated of Him at all. Rather they fall upon His *ibda'*, His origination — and what is that origination? It is the *'aql al-awwal*, the First Intellect. It is also named the *mawjud al-awwal*, the first existent, and the *sadir al-awwal*, the first emanation. Whatever names and attributes there are, they are negated from Allah and they descend upon this first origination, this First Intellect that is at once the first existent and the first to issue forth.

The text presses the point further: `wa ma zahara min fi'lihi fi ikhtira'ihi, la min jihati ismin wa la sifatin yakhtari'ani 'alayhi, li-anna dhalika maslubun 'anhu, waqi'un 'ala ibda'ihi` (and what appeared from His act in His creation — not from the aspect of any name or attribute that would be applied specifically to Him — for that is stripped from Him, and falls rather upon His origination). What we can speak of is only what appeared from His act in His creation, not any name or attribute applied to Him directly; for all such predication is *maslub*, stripped away, severed from Him. The names and attributes we apply fall upon that which He created — His *ibda'* — and even there the matter is subtle, for if these names and attributes do not even fall fully upon His origination, how then could they ever fall upon Allah, the *Mubdi'* Most High?

The teaching now reaches its widest sweep, embracing the whole of creation in a common incapacity: `wa al-'ijzu shamilun li-jami'i 'alam al-'aql wa 'alam al-hiss` (and inadequacy is comprehensive over the entire world of intellect and the world of sense). *'Ijz* — inadequacy, the inability to know the Originator — is comprehensive; it covers all. It embraces the world of intellect, the *'alam al-'aql*, and the world of sense, the *'alam al-hiss*, together. The First Intellect itself, the highest of created things, shares in this incapacity, and so do we; we are all equal in it. As the text states: `yatasawa fi dhalika al-qadiru wa al-maqdur wa al-mahsusu wa al-ma'qul min al-'alamayni jami'an` (in that they are equal — the powerful and the overpowered, the sensed and the intelligible, from both worlds together). The verb *yatasawa*, from *sawa* (equal), in the form that carries the sense of mutuality, declares them all equal to one another in this single respect. The powerful and the powerless, the sensed and the intelligible, the world of intellect and the world of body — all stand on perfectly level ground before the unknowability of the Originator.

### "Allah" as the Cry of Bewilderment

What, then, is our very utterance of the name "Allah"? The text gives an answer of remarkable depth: `wa qawluna Allahu i'rabun bi-hadha al-ismi li-ilhani al-'aqli al-awwal` (and our saying "Allah" is an expression by this name on account of the bewilderment of the First Intellect). Our saying "Allah" — our expressing Him by this name — arises from *ilhan*, the bewilderment, the *walah*, of the First Intellect concerning the Originator. The Intellect is bewildered before Him, and the word "Allah" issues from that bewilderment. In this, again, all are equal: the Imam is equal in it, the ardent lover (*'ashiq*) is equal in it; whoever is bewildered before Him uses the name "Allah."

This is unfolded through the image of the circle. Allah is the center of the circle — the *markaz*, the central point — around which the bewildered circle revolves. The circle (*da'ira*) turns, and why does it turn? Because it is bewildered about its center. One circle revolves about its center in bewilderment; then another circle comes, bewildered about its own center; every circle revolves around its center, and the reason for its revolving is always the same — bewilderment. The very energy by which the circle revolves is, in truth, its bewilderment. The created order circles eternally about a center it cannot comprehend, and the motion of that circling is nothing other than the unceasing perplexity of the creature before the Originator.

### The First Intellect's Negation and Affirmation

The text now recounts the primal act of the First Intellect, the act in which divinity was first negated and then affirmed: `wa qawluna huwiyyatu al-muta'aliyah lamma nafa 'anhu abna'a jinsihi al-ilahiyyah wa athbataha lahu ta'ala wa takabbar kama akhbara 'anhu al-kitabu al-karim bi-qawlihi: shahida Allahu annahu la ilaha illa Hu` (and our saying "the exalted Ipseity" — when he negated from himself and the members of his genus the divinity, and affirmed it for Him, the Exalted and Supreme, as the Noble Book informed concerning him in His saying: "Allah testifies that there is no god but He"). The First Intellect, contemplating the Originator, first performed a negation. He said, in effect, "I am not the creator of myself." He pronounced `la ilaha` — there is no god — negating divinity from himself: I have no power to create myself, nor does my genus, the order of beings like me, have the power to create. He stripped *al-ilahiyyah*, divinity, from himself and his kind. And then he affirmed that divinity for Allah Ta'ala alone — `illa Hu`, except He.

This is the very pattern the Noble Book records in the verse `shahida Allahu annahu la ilaha illa Hu` (Allah testifies that there is no god but He). And the deep sense is this: the "Allah" who testifies here is the First Intellect — it is the First Intellect that bears witness `annahu la ilaha illa Hu`, that there is no god but He, the *Mubdi'* Most High. The First Intellect, and with it the whole order of created being, first confessed that it could not create itself nor its own genus, and then pointed beyond itself with `illa Hu` — He alone — to the Originator, indicated only by the bare pronoun "He."

The text continues: `wa laysa yaqa'u 'alayhi shay'un min dhalika illa kabiran min qawlihi huwa, wa sa-nuzihu haqiqata dhalika in sha' Allahu ta'ala` (and nothing of that falls upon Him save, perhaps, His saying "He," and we shall clarify the reality of that matter, if Allah the Exalted wills). `Fa a'raba 'anhu bi-ma kana min sifati ibda'ihi wa fi'lihi` (so he expressed about Him by what belonged to the attributes of His origination and His act). The Intellect expressed Him only through what was manifest in the origination — saying, in effect, "He created us thus, He fashioned us thus." We cannot praise the Creator as He deserves, for we do not know Him; but from the fact of our own existence, from the manner in which He made us, we can speak: He gave us life, He gave us knowledge, He gave us power. That much we may say, because that much we possess and know. As for true *tamjid*, true glorification — the highest praise befitting Him — that is beyond us. The very highest thing we are able to say is "Allah," and above that, "Huwa," He. Beyond these two words we have nothing with which to interpret Him.

### The Reason for Naming: Our Inadequacy and the Total Knowledge Given to the Intellects

Why, then, do we name Him at all with these inadequate names? The text explains: `fa-waqa'a li-nuqi'a 'ala al-huwiyyati al-muta'aliyah li-'ijzina wa diqi jawharina wa hisrina wa qusurina` (and so it came about that we should let it fall upon the exalted Ipseity — because of our inadequacy, the narrowness of our essence, our confinement, and our deficiency). We let the name fall upon the exalted Ipseity (*huwiyyah*) on account of our *'ijz*, our inadequacy, and the *diq*, the narrowness, of our *jawhar*, our essence — the essence of speech and articulation (*nutq*). We simply do not possess the essential capacity to comprehend Him, and so we have named Him with the last word that comes to our minds, beyond which there is nothing further. Our sense (*hiss*) is confined, and we are deficient and constrained; out of this confinement and deficiency the naming arises.

This points to a precise distinction in what the created intellects were granted. They were given *ilm al-kulli* — total knowledge — but not knowledge of the divine essence. As Sayyidna Mu'ayyad explains, the knowledge they were given is *'ilm al-hayat*, the knowledge of life: the knowledge of what powers and faculties reside in life itself — `hayat` (life), `'ilm` (knowledge), `quwwah` (power), and `ikhtiyar` (choice). Sayyidna Mu'ayyad expounds at length what each of these is: what life is, what knowledge is, what power is, what choice is. But nothing beyond this was given them. They were not given the knowledge by which to know Allah; that knowledge was withheld, for it cannot even be comprehended. The created intellects know the structure of life in its fullness, but the essence of the One who gave that life remains sealed against them.

### The Gift That Is Not Withdrawn, and the Question of Injustice

A further teaching of Sayyidna Mu'ayyad concerns the permanence of what Allah bestows. After Allah has given existence, He does not take that existence back — just as a human being who gives a thing to another does not afterward snatch it away from him; so too Allah, having granted existence, does not withdraw it. This bears upon a subtle question of divine power. There was once a debate between an Arya Samaji and the Muslims, and the Arya Samaji posed a challenge: "Your Allah can do everything?" The Muslims answered, "Yes." "Then can He do *zulm*, injustice? If He can do everything, He can do injustice too — and if you say He cannot, you are limiting His power." The Muslims replied with a counter-question: "Can you eat everything? You have the power to eat — so eat this filth set before you." The man, of course, could not; possessing the capacity to eat does not oblige one to consume what is vile. In the same way, Allah Ta'ala can do everything, yet He does not do injustice. His not doing *zulm* is no limitation upon His power; it is the perfection of His justice. Though He is capable of all things, He does not do wrong, and He does not take back the existence He has given.

### Naming as a Sin Coupled with Seeking Forgiveness

The text now adduces a prayer of Sayyidna Mu'ayyad that draws out a paradox at the very root of all naming. Sayyidna Mu'ayyad — Hibatullah ibn Musa al-Shirazi, the spiritual son of Mawlana Sayyidna Mu'ayyad al-Din, author of the *Mabda' wa Ma'ad* — said in some of his speech: `Allahumma ya man waqa'a i'tirafuna bi-sidqi ma qalahu fi hakimi dhikrihi, idh yaqulu wa qawluhu al-haqq: wa ma qadaru Allaha haqqa qadrihi` (O Allah, O You for whom our confession has come about, attesting the truth of what He said in His wise remembrance, when He says — and His word is the truth — "And they have not appraised Allah with His true appraisal"). Our confession is rendered to the truth of what He declared in His wise remembrance: that they have not measured the worth of Allah with His true worth. We have not been able to appraise Allah Ta'ala as He truly deserves, and we cannot, because we do not know Him; we are able only to say that, judging from our own existence, He has created so good a thing.

The prayer then asks for pardon: `inna nas'aluka al-musamahata liman huwa min rubqi al-'ubudiyyati fi madiqi al-hisari wa al-tadkhiri, idha aradna wala dhikrika bi-ghayri ma anta ahluhu` (indeed we ask You for forbearance toward one who is in the noose of servitude, in the narrow straits of confinement and constraint, when we set ourselves to undertake Your remembrance by that which You are not worthy of). We ask for *musamahah* — forbearance, pardon, from the root *samaha*, to forgive — for the one who is caught in the *rubq*, the snare, of servitude (*'ubudiyyah*). We are bound slaves, hemmed in by the narrow straits of confinement (*hisar*); there are limits set upon us beyond which we cannot pass. And when we set out to undertake Your remembrance, we are compelled to do so by means unworthy of You — for You are worthy only of that which we cannot comprehend. We say as much as we are able, as much as our power permits; and where we fall short and cannot say what befits You, we ask Your pardon.

The reason this pardon is needed is given precisely: `li-anna tanawula wala dhikrika bi-ghayri ma anta ahluhu bi-hukmi al-idtirari fa-innama huwa dhanbun mashfu'un bi-al-istighfar` (because to undertake Your remembrance by that which You are not worthy of, under the rule of compulsion, is only a sin coupled with seeking forgiveness). To take up His remembrance by inadequate means is done `bi-hukmi al-idtirar` — by the rule of compulsion, of necessity. We are compelled; out of *idtirar* we have no other word to say, and so we say what we can. And therefore it is `dhanbun mashfu'un bi-al-istighfar` — a sin coupled with the seeking of forgiveness. This is a technical term established by the Du'at: a sin that is *mashfu'*, paired or coupled (from *shafa'a*, to pair), with *istighfar*. It is a sin and yet a sin joined to the asking of pardon, and because it arises from compulsion it is accepted, for compulsion is excusable. The utmost humility is shown in it.

This profound teaching applies, the text is careful to note, not to the physical world alone but to all the worlds equally. The *istighfar* required here is for all; the First Intellect, the *Mubda' al-Awwal* itself, is included along with everyone else. This means the "sin" in question is not a sin in the ordinary moral sense, for the First Intellect committed no transgression. Rather, even the First Intellect's act — its saying that "You are the *Mubdi'* Most High," its bearing witness `shahida Allahu annahu la ilaha illa Hu`, its indicating Him only by `illa Hu` with the bare pronoun, since there is nothing beyond that — even this carries within it, in a manner of speaking, the character of a sin, because it falls short of His true majesty; and so it too is coupled with *istighfar*. From the First Intellect down to the tenth intellect, none can do anything beyond this; all are compelled, and all are equal before the Creator, the Originator, whom none can recognize.

The principle of compulsion is illustrated by a case judged in the time of Umar. A woman was brought before him who was with child, yet she said she had no husband. Asked how this had come to be, she explained: she had been traveling through the desert, perishing of thirst for three days, when she met a shepherd who said, "I will give you water, but only on the condition that you give yourself to me." Driven by the extremity of her thirst, near to death, she yielded for the sake of the water, and afterward bore a child. Umar said, "Stone her" — a child without marriage, without a husband. But the Mawla of all creation, Imam Ali, arrived and asked what the matter was, and when it was told to him he said: "She is *mustarr* — compelled, helpless. Let her go, for this act occurred under *idtirar*, under compulsion." *Idtirar* means helplessness; and where there is true helplessness, the act does not bear the full weight of a sin. So it is, the text intimates, with the soul that in its helplessness names the Unnameable: it sins, in a sense, by speaking inadequately of Him, yet because it is compelled, its sin is paired with the asking of forgiveness, and it is excused. A subtle paradox remains — that even in the pure world of spirits, where all is illumined save His essence, there should be a possibility of sin at all — but it is resolved in this coupling: nothing beyond this can be done; it is compulsion; and so neither the first intellect nor the tenth can do otherwise, and in the matter of the Creator and Originator everyone is equal, for none can recognize Him.

### The Ten Ascending Ranks of Spirituality

This first section closes with an enumeration of the spiritual ranks through which the ascent toward the divine is ordered — the *maratib*, the ten ranks rising to the highest. The lowest of those named is the Imamate of spirituality, the rank of *Rutbat al-Ilahiyyah* in its ascending series; and from there the ranks rise in order: *Hujjiyyah*, then *Hijabiyyah*, then *Khayaliyyah*, then *Fathiyyah*, then *Jiddiyyah*, then *Anza'iyyah*, then *Qa'imiyyah*, then *Jussiyyah*, then *Ahadiyyah*, then *Samadiyyah* — and culminating in *Ilahiyyah* and finally *Huwiyyah*, for the last and highest rank is *Huwiyyah*, the rank of the bare "He." Ten ranks in all are thus set out, ascending from the Imamate of spirituality up to the Ipseity, mapping the great ladder of spiritual stations by which the soul mounts toward its origin.

### The Esoteric Referents of Shafa', Witr, and Julus

One further matter belongs to this discussion, drawn out from the term *mashfu'* in the phrase *dhanbun mashfu'un bi-al-istighfar* — a "paired" or "coupled" sin. The word *shafa'* itself, which means "pairing," carries among the Du'at an esoteric referent, as do its companion terms *witr* and *julus*, all three taken from the language of prayer. The prayer that is *shafa'* is the paired one of two *rak'at*; *witr* is the single, odd *rak'at*; and *julus* is the prayer of two *rak'at* performed sitting. Each of these has an inner meaning, a *ta'weel*. The referent of *shafa'*, the paired prayer, is the Prophet — peace and blessings be upon him — together with Lady Fatima, for they are the noble pair. The referent of *witr*, the single prayer, is Imam Ali, peace be upon him, the odd and singular one. And the referent of *julus*, the sitting prayer of two *rak'at*, is Imam Hasan and Imam Husayn together. It is told that Imam Husayn himself at Karbala would name these correspondences — that the referent of *shafa'* is the Prophet, that the referent of *witr* was his own father, and so forth — and the text affirms `wa tilka 'ayatun wahidah`, that these together form one sign. Thus the very terminology by which the Du'at express the "coupled sin" of inadequate naming opens, in its turn, onto the hidden ranks of the Prophet and his pure household.

## Tawhid, Tanzih, and the Knowledge of the Hudud

### The Architecture of the Book and the True Meaning of Tawhid

We have now arrived at the fourth chapter (fasl) of the first gate (bab) of the first canopy (suradiq). It is worth pausing over that word, suradiq, before we enter into the substance of the chapter, because the very architecture of this book is encoded in it. A suradiq is a great pavilion, an immense tent or marquee — what is called a shamiyana, a khaima, a vast encampment of curtained enclosures. The verbal root sardaqa means precisely this: to raise a curtain, to pitch a tent, to set up a canopy. The author has built his book in the image of such an encampment. There are five canopies in all. Within each canopy there are five gates. Within each gate there are five chapters. And each of these chapters is itself like a curtained room within the larger tent, partitioned off by hangings the way rooms are improvised within a great pavilion. We are presently in the first suradiq, the first canopy, and within it the first gate, and within that gate this fourth fasl. Holding this structure in mind is no idle exercise, for the order of the chapters mirrors the ascending order of the realities they unveil.

The chapter opens with a saying transmitted from the hudud, the ranks of the spiritual hierarchy, upon whom be peace. The author cites it thus:

`Wa mimma warada fi dhalika qawl al-hudud 'alayhim al-salawatu, inna al-tawhida huwa ma'rifat al-hudud wa al-tanzihu nafyi al-ilahiyyat 'anhum wa al-tajrida salb al-asma'i wa al-sifati 'an Allah Ta'ala. Wa hadha qawlun mujmalun.`

(And among what is related in that is the saying of the hudud, peace be upon them: verily Tawhid is the gnosis of the hudud, and tanzih is the negation of divinity from them, and tajrid is the divestment of the names and attributes from Allah the Exalted. And this is a summarized statement.)

Here, at the very foundation of all things, a correction is administered to the most common and most ingrained of our notions. When we are asked what Tawhid is, we answer almost reflexively that it means to consider Allah as one. But that is not what the hudud teach, and indeed it cannot be, for to count Allah as one is already to subject Him to number, and whatever can be numbered as one can in principle be numbered as two or three or counted as a part of one. To declare Allah one in this sense is therefore not Tawhid at all but shirk, the gravest of errors, because it imposes a definition and a measure upon the One who admits of no definition and no measure. He cannot be called one, He cannot be called two, He cannot be called three, He cannot be called a part of one. No number applies to Him, and no boundary can be drawn around Him.

What, then, is Tawhid? It is ma'rifat al-hudud, the gnosis of the ranks. This is the cornerstone teaching of the entire treatise. To affirm the divine unity is to recognize each had, each rank of the spiritual hierarchy, as utterly unique (yekta) in its own place — not to declare Allah unique, but to know that every had is unique in its proper station, and to attain the gnosis of where precisely that had stands, at what level, at what station, in what relation to those above and below it. Allah is not the one who is here being declared unique; rather, every had is unique in its own place, and the gnosis of that uniqueness, of that ordered hierarchy of distinct ranks, is what Tawhid actually means. This is the greatest thing that can be known: the Tawhid of the hudud, the recognition that every rank is singular and irreplaceable in its own degree.

The author is careful to add that the ranks did not assume their positions arbitrarily. Allah created the hudud and conferred upon each of them authority (ikhtiyar), saying in effect: use your powers. Each had, according to its own nature and similitude, was given a share of power and the freedom to exercise it. From the differences that consequently arose among them, the ordered arrangement (tartib) of the hierarchy was established. To know this arrangement — to understand by what gradation one rank stands above or below another — is Tawhid. The hierarchy is not imposed from without but is the very expression of how each rank used the authority entrusted to it.

It follows, too, that Tawhid admits of levels, for not every person can comprehend the whole of it at once. The Noble Prophet himself indicated as much when he said that none had recognized him except himself and Allah. As a person's own station (martaba) rises, so too does the depth of his Tawhid; there are many stations of Tawhid, each corresponding to the elevation of the knower. And since this is so, we may ask how many beings' Tawhid a single person is able to perform within the span of a lifetime. The answer establishes the supreme rank of this science: the knowledge of Tawhid is the highest knowledge of all — ajall al-'ilm. To recognize the station of each and every rank, to know where each one stands, is the most exalted of all knowing.

### Innumerable Beings, Innumerable Gnosis, and the Establishment of One's Rank

The reason Tawhid is the highest of knowledge follows from the boundlessness of its object. Consider the human body as an analogy: within it there are some three hundred and sixty parts, and each organ, each member, has its own quality and its own degree of excellence, one greater than another. To have full gnosis of the body would mean knowing each of these parts in its proper rank and worth. Now extend this to the whole order of existence. All the beings (zawat) that Allah Ta'ala created — and they are innumerable — passed through their stages until they attained kamal-e insani, human perfection. The real ma'rifat, the true Tawhid, is to know each of these beings: when and how it attained its human perfection, at what station it stands, how it relates to all the others. This is the very limit of gnosis. Since Allah created innumerable beings, the gnosis answering to them is likewise innumerable, and the intellect is left bewildered before the sheer immensity of what may be known — as much as one is able to attain, one attains, and no more.

From this immensity follows a sober and practical consequence for every seeker. For salvation (najat), the question is precisely which of the ranks' gnosis a person manages to acquire, and how much. As much gnosis as you are able to attain within your lifetime, accordingly will your station (martaba) be established. And according to that station, the corresponding hierarchy will be established within your haykal, your spiritual form. It is through knowledge ('ilm) and action ('amal) together that the arrangement within the haykal is set in place. A person's eventual rank in the spiritual world is not arbitrary; it is the precise measure of the gnosis he attained and enacted in this life. And what, in the end, is salvation itself? It is khatima bil-khair, a good end. To finish well, to close one's life upon the good, is salvation — and that good end is itself the fruit of the gnosis and action accumulated along the way.

This raises a delicate question about the agency by which gnosis comes. Do we attain gnosis by our own doing, or is it made to happen to us? The teaching holds both together. The effort (juhd) you yourself expend — by that effort you do attain gnosis; you genuinely strive and genuinely acquire. But upon that effort there also descends the grace (fazl) of those who stand above you, so that gnosis is also made to happen to you through their bestowal. Neither alone suffices: there is the labor that is yours to perform, and there is the grace that is given from above, and gnosis is the meeting of the two.

We should also recall what the word hudud itself signifies. A had is a limit. Every rank has its limit, a boundary it cannot pass beyond, and to know that limit — the final point (nuqta-e irtifa') to which a given rank ascends — is itself part of the gnosis of the ranks. So, for example, the limit of ascent of the Prophet, upon him be peace and blessings, is Hijabiyyat, the station of being a veil. The limit of Janab-e Fatimah is Hujjiyyat, the station of the Hujjah. The final point of Janab-e Abd al-Muttalib is Jaddiyyat, the station of the Jadd; and Jaddiyyat itself is not a single point but comprises ten stations (maratib), so that one must further know at which of those ten degrees a given individual is found, the seventh being lower and the higher being other than it. To attain the gnosis of these limits and their internal gradations is a very great science indeed. This is Tawhid.

### The Three Branches: Tawhid, Tanzih, and Tajrid

The opening saying named three things, and we must now take each in turn, for Tawhid in its full statement has three branches. The first is Tawhid itself, which we have already established: ma'rifat al-hudud, the gnosis of the ranks.

The second is tanzih. The saying defines it as nafyi al-ilahiyyat 'anhum — the negation of divinity from the ranks. To perform tanzih is to declare transcendent, to declare pure, and specifically to deny that divinity resides in the hudud. There is no divinity in the ranks; the attribute of creating, of bringing beings into existence, is not theirs. We must be precise about what ilahiyyat, divinity, means in this context, for the word ilah carries more than one sense. Here what is intended by ilahiyyat is ibda' al-zawat, the origination of beings — that is, the power to bring a being into existence from nothing. Tanzih negates this power from the ranks: not one of them, the saying insists, possesses the power of ibda'-e zawat, the origination of a being. Not even the First Intellect, the Aql al-Awwal, possesses it. All of them, without exception, are themselves created (paida kiye hue), and the created cannot originate. We will return to the second meaning of ilah — the sense in which a higher rank becomes, for the one bewildered before it, a "god" — when the author unfolds it later in this very chapter; for now it is enough to fix the meaning relevant to tanzih: the creating power belongs to none of the ranks, and so divinity, in the sense of origination, must be negated from them all.

The third branch is tajrid, defined as salb al-asma'i wa al-sifati 'an Allah Ta'ala, the divestment of names and attributes from Allah the Exalted. The root of tajrid is the stripping of bark from a tree; here it means to strip away from Allah every name and every attribute, for no name can truly be predicated of Him. Even the name "Allah" cannot truly be His, in the strict sense, for it too is a name and He is beyond all names. Out of sheer necessity, therefore, we are reduced to calling Him "Hu" — He — and even in saying "Hu" we confess our helplessness: O Allah, we have no words; to affirm our own existence we are forced to address You somehow, but by what name shall we call You? This predicament is captured in the phrase zanbun mashfu'un bil-istighfar — a sin coupled with a plea for forgiveness. The very act of naming Him is a kind of sin, yet it is a sin that comes already accompanied by its own forgiveness. For man is helpless: he must call upon Allah by some name, because if he did not, he would in effect deny the existence of the Existent altogether. To turn toward the existence of Allah he must address Him; to address Him he must name Him; and in naming Him he commits the fault. But the istighfar is built into the act, because Allah Ta'ala understands the helplessness of His servant — that the servant possesses no word adequate to call upon Him, and that by whatever name he calls, that calling will be accepted and the plea for forgiveness granted along with it. So tajrid is the negation, from Allah Ta'ala, of all the names and attributes whatsoever that might be ascribed to Him.

The author then observes of the whole three-branched saying that it is qawlun mujmalun — a summary, an encompassing statement that stands in need of detailed exposition. And it is to that exposition that the remainder of the chapter is devoted.

### Outer and Inner Gnosis of the Ranks

The unpacking begins with a fundamental division within the gnosis of the ranks itself. The author cites:

`Li-anna al-ma'rifata bil-hududi ma'rifatan. Ma'rifatun zahiratun yu'rafu biha shakhs al-had wa maqamuhu wa hadhihi al-ma'rifah yatasawi fiha al-wali wa ghayruhu min al-azdad wa al-tughat. Wa ma'rifatun batina yakhtassu biha ahl al-ma'arif min ahl al-haqq. Wa huwa ibtida' wujud al-had.`

(Because the gnosis of the hudud is of two kinds of gnosis. An outer gnosis, by which the person of the had and his station are known, and in which the friend and others from among the adversaries and tyrants are equal. And an inner gnosis, specific to the people of gnosis from the people of truth. And it is the beginning of the existence of the had.)

The gnosis of the ranks, then, is of two kinds. The first is the outer gnosis (ma'rifah zahirah), by which the person of a had and his station are recognized. This is the knowledge by which one knows who a given rank is and where he stands. Its distinguishing feature is that in this outer gnosis the friend (wali) and the adversary stand on equal footing — the lover, the enemy, and the indifferent alike perceive the same outward person. All of them see how tall he is, how many feet in height; whether his face is round, whether he wears a beard; what his hands are like, what his feet are like. The devoted disciple sees this and the bitter enemy sees this, and in this respect there is no difference between them. The outer gnosis is by no means a trivial knowledge — it too is difficult to attain in its fullness — but its mark is that in it friend and foe are made equal, for it concerns only the outward person and station.

The second and far higher kind is the inner gnosis (ma'rifah batinah), and this is specific (yakhtassu biha) to the people of gnosis (ahl al-ma'arif) from among the people of truth (ahl al-haqq). What is its object? It is ibtida' wujud al-had — the very beginning of the existence of the rank. When did the existence of a given had begin? The ranks that have been created — where were they created, and from whom? Here the author opens, in compressed form, the long and difficult account of the origin of the ranks. The hudud were brought into being where the fatahun were — those whose sins were few — and then they were called. The first call was issued, and in that first call they did not confess; a second call was made, and a third, and a fourth; in each room they were called in turn, and one must know in which room each rank finally made its confession. This, the author says, is a very long story, and its full unfolding belongs elsewhere; but the point for us is that the gnosis of this beginning — of where and from whom and through how many summons a rank came into being — is precisely the inner gnosis, and the gnosis of such a thing belongs to the Imam al-Zaman, upon him be peace.

The inner gnosis does not concern the beginning alone. The citation continues that this gnosis comprises also ghayatuhu, the rank's end, and ila ayna ma'aduhu wa nihayatuhu — where its return (ma'ad) and its final terminus lie. To know the inner reality of a had is to know the whole arc of its essence (khamira): at what point its essence was established, and at what level a person stood when he passed from this world. Was he in the ninth degree of the Mu'min, or the eighth, or the seventh? Did he ascend from Mu'min to become a Mukathir, and from Mukathir to become a Ma'dhun? At precisely what station did his existence come to rest, and to what return is it destined? This is a gnosis of extreme difficulty, and the author states plainly its limits and its stakes: it cannot be reached except by the murtazin, the disciplined ones, and the muhiqqin, the verifiers — those who have purified themselves and proven the truth within themselves. And then comes a statement of the utmost severity: for one whose gnosis of this inner reality is deficient, there is no Tawhid at all. Without the inner knowledge of the beginning, the level, and the return of a rank's essence, a man may possess no true Tawhid whatever. It is at this point that the author announces his own intention: he will clarify something of this matter, so that it may serve as a help for his brethren in the gnosis of the Tawhid of Allah Ta'ala.

### The Closest Rank: The Mukathir and the Levels Below the Hudud

The exposition proper begins with the author's invocation and a foundational principle:

`Fa-aqulu bi-'awn Allah subhanahu wa lahazati waliyyihi fi ardihi, salamullahi 'alayhi, inna aqrab al-hudud ilayna huwa al-mukathiru wa ma thabata min al-qawl fi al-dani min al-hudud thabata fil-'ali.`

(So I say, with the help of Allah, glory be to Him, and the glances of His Wali upon His earth, peace of Allah be upon him: verily the closest of the ranks to us is the Mukathir, and what is established of the saying regarding the lower of the ranks is established for the higher.)

Two principles are set down here at once. The first is that the closest had to us — the rank nearest to ordinary humanity, the one through which we make our first true contact with the spiritual hierarchy — is the Mukathir. The second is a rule of proportion that will govern much of what follows: ma thabata fi al-dani thabata fil-'ali — whatever is established for the lower of the ranks is likewise established for the higher. What is said of the Mukathir holds, in its measure, of the Ma'dhun, the Da'i Mutlaq, the Da'i Mahsur, and every rank above; the same saying that applies to the lower applies, raised to its degree, to the higher. And it works in both directions, for a higher rank contains the lower within himself: a Ma'dhun is also a Mukathir in his own place, and a Mukathir before he is a Ma'dhun.

Before reaching the Mukathir, however, one must pass through two preliminary levels that stand below the hudud proper. The first is the Mustajib, the responder — literally, the one who accepts. The Mustajib is the one who takes the noble pledge ('ahd sharif), who declares with his tongue, "I accept the noble pledge," whether or not his heart and his deeds have yet caught up with his tongue. The defining mark of the Mustajib is precisely this profession with the tongue: that he affirms, "the Imam of the time is such," and binds himself by that word. The Mustajib has ten levels, and these are graded by the intensity (hiddat) with which the pledge is taken — the sharpness, the force, the keenness of gaze of the one taking the pledge. Some come to the pledge as casually as one comes to a marriage; others take it with the burning intensity of a Sayyidna Ja'far; the Imam himself takes the pledge. According to the hiddat of the one pledging, his level is established, and so there are ten degrees of the noble pledge.

The second preliminary level is the Mu'min, the believer, and the author gives its classical definition:

`Al-qawl bil-lisan wa al-tasdiq bil-jinan wa al-'amal bil-arkan.`

(Profession with the tongue, affirmation with the heart, and action with the limbs.)

The Mu'min is the one in whom all three are joined: he professes with the tongue, he affirms inwardly with the heart, and he acts outwardly with his limbs. The Mu'min, like the Mustajib, has ten levels of his own. And it must be emphasized: neither the Mustajib nor the Mu'min falls within the hudud. They are below the ranks proper, the necessary thresholds across which one approaches the hierarchy, but not yet of it. It is only after these — only with the Mukathir — that one truly enters the hudud.

### The Mukathir: Breaking the Self and Entering the Ranks

With the Mukathir, then, we cross the threshold into the ranks themselves. The Mukathir is the first rank within the hudud, the first degree of the spiritual hierarchy, and it is this rank that the Prophet's transmitted saying placed closest to us. The meaning of the word is illuminating. Mukathir derives from kasr, breaking — for the Mukathir is the one who breaks with his proofs (hujjat). But whom does he break first? Not his opponents; he must first break his own self. This is kasr-e nafs, the breaking of the self, and it is the indispensable beginning. To perform kasr-e nafs is to subject one's every word, every action, and every intention to a single relentless examination: is this statement I am uttering in accordance with the command of Allah and the Prophet? Is this deed I am performing in accordance with their command? Is this very thought passing through my mind in accordance with the command of Allah and the Prophet? To bind one's self entirely to that measure — to make oneself captive to the command in speech, deed, and thought alike — is what it means to be a Mukathir.

The logic is exacting: only when a man has broken his own self can he break others. If he has set himself in order upon this matter, then he is able with his proofs to break the one who stands before him. But if he could not break his own self — if he has indulged his own self, telling it that this is permissible and that is permissible, granting himself license — then how shall he break another? He has no standing to do so. And from this follows a strict requirement: for the Mukathir, the acquisition of the sciences ('ulum) is indispensable. Without knowledge, no Mukathir can be formed, for he must first know the command of Allah and the Prophet in order to test himself against it. Only when he knows the command can he recognize that some word of his fell short of it, that some deed of his diverged from it, that some thought of his strayed from it. Having first judged and broken himself by this knowledge, he is then able to break the one before him with his proofs. And by the rule already established — what is fixed for the lower is fixed for the higher — this same description holds, in its proper degree, for the Mukathir, the Ma'dhun, the Da'i Mutlaq, the Da'i Mahsur, and every rank of the hudud above.

### Same Composition, Different Reality: The Refinement of the Mukathir

A profound question now arises, and the author meets it directly with a further citation:

`Wa dhalika annahu wa in kana yajma'uhu wa ghayruhu min al-ashkhas al-insaniyya al-hudud wa al-aqtar wa kawnuhuma jami'an min lahmin wa damin wa 'izamin wa 'asabin wa 'uruqin wa ghayri dhalika fa-laysa bi-sawa'in.`

(And that is because, although he and others from among the human persons are gathered by the limits and the regions, and although both of them are entirely of flesh and blood and bones and nerves and veins and other than that, yet they are not the same.)

The Mukathir and the ordinary human being are gathered together within the same limits (hudud) and the same regions (aqtar) — the same boundaries of body and place encompass them both. Both are composed of flesh, both of blood, both of bones, of nerves, of veins, and of all the rest. To outward inspection there is no difference between them: the ordinary man is of flesh and bone, and the Mukathir is of flesh and bone. And yet, the author insists, they are not the same. Despite the identity of their outward composition, an immense difference separates them. The author resolves the apparent paradox with the next line:

`Li-anna hadha al-had alladhi huwa al-mukathir qad talattafa mizajuhu wa safa jawharuhu wa hadha su'udan maratiban jumma.`

(Because this rank, which is the Mukathir, his temperament has become refined and his essence has become pure, and this through ascending many stations.)

The difference, then, is not merely inward but reaches even the outward. The Mukathir's temperament has become refined (talattafa mizajuhu) and his essence has become pure (safa jawharuhu). His flesh is no longer simply common flesh, his blood no longer common blood; everything of his has been transformed — and this even outwardly, though others did not perceive it and the ignorant could not discern it. This transformation came about because he ascended through numerous stations that others never attained. The text continues:

`Wa taqarraba biha fa-taqarraba biha min al-mabda' al-awwal hatta sara fi a'la maratib al-insaniyya allati hiya awwalu rutab al-malakutiyya.`

(And he drew near through that, drawing near through it to the first principle, until he reached the highest stations of humanity, which are the first of the spiritual ranks.)

Through this refinement and ascent the Mukathir has drawn close to the first principle (al-mabda' al-awwal) — close, that is, to the very source from which his creation proceeded, so that he is now near to returning there. He has reached the highest stations of humanity, and these highest human stations are themselves the first of the spiritual ranks (awwalu rutab al-malakutiyya). The summit of the human is the threshold of the malakut, the spiritual world; the Mukathir stands precisely at that joining-point, where the topmost rung of the human order is the bottommost rung of the angelic.

### The House of Allah and the Place of Descent of Revelation

From this elevation flows the most exalted of the descriptions given to the Mukathir:

`Wa huwa mahbat li-wahyi Allah wa baytun min buyutihi.`

(And he is a place of descent for the revelation of Allah, and a house from His houses.)

The Mukathir is the place of descent (mahbat) for the revelation (wahy) of Allah — the very locus upon which revelation alights — and he is a house (bayt) from among the houses of Allah. And the author explains exactly why he is so. It is because in his every thought he kept the command of Allah and the Prophet before him; in his every word he kept that command before him; and in his every deed he kept it before him. Having made the command of Allah and the Prophet the standing presence of his thought, speech, and action, he became thereby a house from the houses of Allah, a place fit for the descent of revelation. The author then anchors this in the Qur'an:

`Kama ramada bihi Ta'ala fi kitabihi al-karim wa qala: fi buyutin adhin Allahu an turfa' wa yudhkara fiha ismuhu yusabbihu lahu fiha bil-ghuduwwi wa al-asal.`

(As Allah the Exalted has alluded to it in His noble book, saying: "In houses which Allah has permitted to be exalted and His name to be remembered therein; in them He is glorified in the mornings and the evenings.")

`Thumma haqqaqa fil-ayah anna al-buyuta hum al-hudud.`

(Then He established in the verse that the "houses" are the ranks.)

The "houses" of the Qur'anic verse, which Allah permitted to be exalted and within which His name is remembered and glorified morning and evening, are, in their inner meaning, the hudud — the ranks of the hierarchy, of whom the Mukathir is the first. The verse continues, in the author's citation:

`Fa-qala Ta'ala: rijalun la tulhihim tijaratun wa la bay'un 'an dhikri Allah wa iqam al-salati wa ita' al-zakati. Yuridu iqamat al-da'wah.`

(So He said, the Exalted: "Men whom neither commerce nor sale diverts from the remembrance of Allah and the establishing of prayer and the giving of zakat." By which He intends the establishing of the da'wah.)

The "men whom neither commerce nor sale diverts from the remembrance of Allah, the establishing of prayer, and the giving of zakat" are these same ranks. And what is intended by their "establishing of prayer" here is, in its inner sense, iqamat al-da'wah — the establishing of the divine summons, the da'wah. The verse's outward language of commerce and trade is illuminated by the parallel passage of Surah al-Jumu'ah:

`Ya ayyuha alladhina amanu idha nudiya li-s-salati min yawm al-jumu'ati fas'aw ila dhikri Allah wa dharu al-bay'a. Dhalikum khayrun lakum in kuntum ta'lamun. Fa-idha qudiyat al-salatu fantashiru fil-ardi wabtaghu min fadli Allah wadhkuru Allah kathiran la'allakum tuflihun.`

(O you who believe, when the call is proclaimed for the prayer on the day of Jumu'ah, hasten to the remembrance of Allah and leave off trade. That is better for you, if you only knew. And when the prayer is concluded, disperse in the land and seek of the bounty of Allah, and remember Allah much, that you may be successful.)

Those whom commerce and the buying and selling of the world do not divert from the remembrance of Allah, from the establishing of prayer, and from the giving of zakat — they are the ones who, in the inner sense, establish the da'wah.

### The Esoteric Meanings of the Pillars of Religion

This identification of "establishing prayer" with "establishing the da'wah" opens onto a broader teaching: that each of the pillars and obligations of the religion carries an esoteric, inner meaning (ta'weel) beneath its outward form. The author sets these out in turn.

Wilayat, in its inner sense, is to repent in the world of motes — to turn in repentance in that primordial world of the smallest particles of being. Taharat, purity, is to purify oneself from the filth of Iblis through the knowledge of the Tenth. Salat, prayer, is to act upon the commands of the Tenth, or of whichever Imam in whose sphere one has arrived and whose da'wah has reached one — to enact the commands he has given; and this is why, when the prayer is established, it is announced that "the prayer has stood" (qad qamat al-salah), since qiyam means to stand, and the standing of the prayer is the standing-up to enact the command. Zakat is to give a portion of the knowledge that has been given to you: when the knowledge you have received reaches a certain measure, a small portion of it becomes obligatory to give out. And just as there are kinds of material zakat — the zakat al-fitra, obligatory upon every person, and the zakat al-mal, which falls due only when wealth accumulates — so too with the zakat of knowledge: some sciences are owed by nature to every person and must be explained to all, like the fitra, while others, like the zakat of accumulated wealth, are owed only when one's store of knowledge has grown to the threshold that makes a portion of it obligatory to dispense.

The inner meanings continue. Sawm, fasting, is to conceal knowledge — not to disclose it to the undeserving, to keep it hidden from those unfit to receive it. Khums, the fifth, is to give a fifth part of knowledge that comes to you unexpectedly. Just as, in the outward law, if some windfall or booty comes to a person which he was not expecting, a fifth of it must be given — as Hazrat Imam Husayn, upon him be peace, used every month to set aside the khums of his very salary, reasoning that he had not expected such money to come to him, and so dispensing its fifth — so likewise with knowledge: if some knowledge descends upon you unexpectedly, from the saints of Allah, its fifth part must be given out. When knowledge and virtues descended upon Imam Husayn, what did he do with their fifth? He propagated it, he clarified the matter, he gave the fifth out; the remaining four parts were his own by right, but the fifth he dispensed. Hajj is al-qasdu ila bayt Allah, the intending toward the House of Allah — and the House of Allah, in the inner sense, is the Imam al-Zaman, upon him be peace, who is the place of Allah; the "house" is the bab al-batin, the inner gate, and hajj is to prepare one's form and bearing, with a true heart, to set out toward that inner gate. And jihad is to take one's own self — rather, not merely to take it but to sacrifice it, to slay it — in the way of Allah: when the command comes from the Imam al-Zaman, to offer up the self; and the greatest jihad is the slaying of the commanding self (nafs), so that the true hero, the lion of the battle, is the one who has killed within himself that great beast which is his own commanding soul.

The author then returns to the verse to seal the point:

`Yuridu iqamat al-da'wati allati hiya mansur al-salati al-zahira wa la qiwama laha illa biha.`

(He intends the establishing of the da'wah, which is the object supported by the outward prayer, and the da'wah has no standing except through it.)

What Allah intends by the verse is the establishing of the da'wah, which is the very object that the outward prayer serves and supports; and the da'wah itself is not strengthened, has no standing, except through prayer. The outer prayer and the inner da'wah are thus bound together, the one upholding the other.

### Two Men on One Platform: Ascent and Decline

The author now draws the contrast that gives the whole teaching its moral urgency, citing the long passage that sets the Mukathir against the man who does not establish prayer:

`Wa ghayruhu min hadhihi al-ashkhas al-insaniyya wa in kana 'ala al-halati allati huwa 'alayha fa-inna mizajahu ghayr mutalattif wa jawharahu ghayr safin wa shakhsuhu sa'irun fil-inhidar wa al-bu'di min al-mabda' al-awwal sa'irun fi akhiri rutab al-insaniyya muqahqaran ila ma dunahu min al-surat al-'alfiyya.`

(And other than him, of these human persons — even though he is in the same state in which the Mukathir is — his temperament is unrefined and his essence impure, and his person is proceeding in decline and in distance from the first principle, proceeding toward the last of the human ranks, pushed back to what is below it of the thousand-fold forms.)

The "other than him" here is the man who is not a Mukathir — the ordinary person, or the opponent. Outwardly he stands in the very same state, on the very same platform, as the Mukathir: both are of flesh and blood, both inhabit the same human form, both have come into the same world. Yet their trajectories are opposite. The Mukathir is going up; this other is going down. The one is drawing near to the first principle; the other is moving away from it. They are gathered together on a single platform, and from there the one goes this way and the other that way — as the author vividly puts it, one is going to the cinema and one is going to the mosque, yet they set out together from the same point. The decisive difference is the establishing of prayer. Where iqamat al-salah does not occur — where the man does not establish the inward prayer, and indeed in such a case does not even establish the outward prayer — he proceeds away from the first principle, declining toward the last of the human ranks and being pushed back (muqahqaran) beneath even the lowest of them.

The text presses the descent to its terminus:

`Allati hiya adallu sabilan min al-an'am ila al-sirat al-mu'awwaj muntahiyan fihi ila barzakh al-khawf.`

The man who fails to establish prayer is pushed below the thousand-fold form (surat al-alfiyya), so that he becomes more astray in his path than the cattle, turned onto the crooked path, ending at last in the barzakh al-khawf, the isthmus of fear. He proceeds, step by step, toward that dreadful terminus. And the author breaks off into prayer: may Allah keep us, and all our brethren, far from such a matter, for the sake of the master of the prophets, upon him and his pure progeny be peace and blessings, the blessings of Allah upon them all. The lesson stands stark: two men of identical outward composition, set upon a single platform, one establishing the inward prayer and so becoming a Mukathir who ascends, the other establishing neither inward nor even outward prayer, whose temperament and deeds have hardened him so that he descends. The same flesh, the same blood, the same world — and yet opposite ends, because of the establishing or the abandoning of prayer.

### The Lesson of the Lawful and the Forbidden Animals

The author next finds the same pattern of ascent and decline mirrored in the animal kingdom, and draws from it a proof. He cites:

`Wa fil-ashkhas al-haywaniyya 'ibratun li-man i'tabara wa basiratun li-man tabassara wa tadabbara.`

(And in the animal forms there is a lesson for one who takes a lesson, and insight for one who reflects and contemplates.)

In the animal figures — and "person" here means figure, not human person — there is a lesson for whoever will take a lesson, and insight for whoever will reflect and contemplate. The argument then unfolds:

`Wa dhalika anna al-baha'im al-muhallalah min al-baqari wa al-ghanami wa al-ibili wa ghayri dhalika mutamathilatun fil-khalqi hiya wa al-siba' al-muharramah li-kawnihim jami'an min lahmin wa damin wa 'izamin wa 'asabin wa 'uruqin wa basharin wa jildin wa wabarin ghayra anna al-baha'im qad sarat ghidha'an lil-insan.`

(And that is because the lawful animals — of cattle and sheep and camels and the like — are similar in creation to the forbidden predatory animals, both being of flesh and blood and bones and nerves and veins and skin and hide and fur; except that the lawful animals have become food for the human being.)

The lawful animals — the cow (baqar), the sheep, the camel, and their kind — are identical in outward creation to the forbidden predatory animals (siba'). Both are of flesh, blood, bones, nerves, veins, skin, hide, and fur; in their physical composition there is no difference between them. Yet they are not the same, and the proof of their difference lies in their relation to man. The lawful animals have become food for the human being — for that human who is the caliph of Allah upon His earth. The text continues:

`Musta'nisatan ilayhi sa'iratan ila al-su'ud wa al-taraqqi fi al-sirat al-sawiyy alladhi huwa al-shakhs al-bashari qaribatan min al-mabda' al-awwal.`

(Taking intimacy with him, proceeding toward ascent and progress upon the straight path, which is the human person, near to the first principle.)

The lawful animals take intimacy with man; they draw near to him, become his food, and thereby, by becoming part of the human, proceed toward ascent and progress upon the straight path (sirat al-sawiyy). And what is the straight path here? It is the human person himself — sirat al-mustaqim is the bashari, the human form, which is close to the first principle that is our origin and our destination. The lawful animal, eaten and assimilated into the human who is the caliph of Allah, ascends through him toward the source. The predatory animals, by contrast:

`Wa al-siba'u nafiratun 'an al-insan mu'adiyatun lahu sa'iratan fil-inhidar sa'iratan ila al-bu'd al-ab'ad min al-mabda' al-awwal.`

(And the predatory animals are averse to the human, hostile to him, proceeding in decline, proceeding to the furthest distance from the first principle.)

The forbidden predatory animals are averse to man, hostile to him; they do not draw near to be his food and so do not ascend through him, but proceed in decline, moving ever farther from the first principle. Thus, although the two kinds of animal are of one and the same created stuff, the author concludes:

`Fa-laysu bi-sawa'in fi hadhihi al-dawahir wa al-khalq al-mushtarak wa kadhalika al-ta'i'una wa al-'usatu wa al-ba'idu ab'adu min dhalika.`

(So they are not equal in these outward appearances and shared creation; and likewise the obedient and the disobedient — and the distance is greater still.)

They are not equal despite their shared outward appearance and common creation. And just so it is with men: the lawful animal that ascends through intimacy with man is like the Mukathir, the pure human who ascends toward the first principle; the forbidden predatory animal that flees man and declines is like the man who falls. So among human beings the obedient (muti', ta'i') and the disobedient ('asi) — though they too share one platform, one composition, one world — are separated by a distance greater even than that which divides the two kinds of beast. One rises and one descends, and between them lies the whole interval between the first principle and the furthest distance from it. With this the fifth and final chapter of the first gate of the first canopy is reached and concluded.

### The Refinement of the Mukathir's Soul

The second gate now opens, and with it a closer examination of the soul (nafs) of the Mukathir — how it became transformed and what flowed into it as a result. The author cites:

`Wa idha kana dhalika kadhalika fa-inna nafs al-had alladhi huwa al-mukathir qad talattafat wa safat wa 'alat bi-irtifa' al-sifati al-mawdu'atiha wa 'uluwwihi thumma ittasala biha min al-'ulum al-ilahiyya wa al-asrar al-malakutiyya ma ahkamaha sifa'an kulliyan wa altahaha taltifan malakutiyyan wa sabaghaha sibghatan ilahiyyan wa ashraqaha wa jawharaha wa ashfaha.`

(And since the matter is so, then the soul of the rank which is the Mukathir has become refined and pure and elevated through the elevation of its established attributes and its loftiness; then there connected with it, of the divine sciences and spiritual secrets, that which perfected it with a universal purity, and refined it with a spiritual refinement, and dyed it with a divine dye, and made it shine and clarified its essence and made it transparent.)

What is here in question is the sensory soul (nafs hissi) of the Mukathir. In the ordinary person and in the Mukathir alike there is a nafs hissi, yet the two are not the same; the Mukathir's sensory soul has itself become refined, because of all that he acquired in his ascent to that rank. He passed through the ten stations of the Mustajib, and through the ten stations of the Mu'min, and then through the degrees of the Mukathir to whatever level he has reached — and from all this his very soul has taken on a subtlety. Then, upon this refined soul, there connected the divine sciences ('ulum ilahiyya) and the spiritual secrets (asrar malakutiyya), which perfected it with a total purity, refined it with a spiritual refinement, dyed it with a divine dye, and made it shine, clarified, and transparent. He became, in effect, a man possessed of a holy spirit, his sensory soul itself transmuted.

A subtle point of discernment follows. Does it then follow that whenever a soul appears refined, the person before us must be a Mukathir? Not necessarily, for a person's stations may be hidden and known to Allah alone; outwardly it may appear that he is a Mukathir, and his soul indeed be refined, yet the formal designation belongs to Allah. The author resolves the matter from the side of justice: the demand of justice is that if a soul has genuinely become pure, then that person must in fact have become a Mukathir — he will have passed the ten stations of the Mu'min and reached the station of the Mukathir, and his soul's subtlety is the consequence of that ascent. The subtlety need not be outwardly manifest, and the public designation is not necessary; the station rests with Allah. But where the purity of the soul is real, the rank is real, even if it is concealed from all but Allah.

### The Disciple's Bewilderment and the Birth of a "God"

We now reach the heart of the chapter — the precise sense in which a higher rank becomes a "god" for the one beneath him, and how this becomes the very ladder of true Tawhid. The author cites:

`Fa-idha qabalahu al-mustafidu bil-sidqi wa sihhati al-niyyati wa safa'i al-tabi'ati wa takallama ma'ahu wa afadahu min al-kalami al-malakuti wa al-'ilmi al-ilahi alladhi lam yakun qad khatara qabla dhalika bi-balihi wa la zanna bi-masami'ihi qattu wa masami'i amthalihi hara fihi lubbuhu wa walaha fa-sara lahu ilahan malikan li-riqqihi 'arifan bi-dalalatihi wa 'azimi sabaqatihi.`

(So when the seeker receives him with sincerity and soundness of intention and purity of nature, and speaks with him, and the Mukathir bestows upon him of the spiritual speech and the divine knowledge which had never before crossed his heart nor ever been suspected by his hearing or the hearing of his likes — his intellect is bewildered in it, and he is astounded, and the Mukathir becomes for him a god, a master of his neck, knowing of his guidance and his great precedence.)

The scene is that of teacher and disciple. When a seeker (mustafid) comes to the Mukathir with sincerity, soundness of intention, and purity of nature, and converses with him, the Mukathir bestows upon him the spiritual speech (al-kalam al-malakuti) and the divine knowledge — knowledge so sublime that it had never before crossed the seeker's heart, nor had his ears or the ears of his likes ever so much as suspected it. Confronted with such elevated realities, the disciple's intellect (lubb) is bewildered (hara) and he is astounded (walaha): he cannot fathom how his teacher attained this station and these sublime matters. In this bewilderment the Mukathir becomes for him a god (ilah), a master of his neck (malik li-riqqihi), and he recognizes the Mukathir's guidance and his great precedence.

It is essential to grasp precisely the sense in which the Mukathir is here called a "god," for this is the second meaning of ilah that was deferred earlier. He is a god in the sense that he is the center of the circle (markaz). Around the center the circle revolves, and that revolving is the very motion of bewilderment; the disciple revolves around his teacher as a circle revolves around its center, drawn into orbit by his astonishment. It is in this sense alone — as the center around which the bewildered seeker circles — that the Mukathir is a "god" for him. It is emphatically not in the sense that he is the creator of beings; the power of ibda' was negated from all the ranks at the outset. He is "god" only as the center of bewildered orbit and the master who holds the seeker's neck for its release, the one whose guidance, greatness, and precedence the seeker has come to recognize.

### Ascending the Ranks to the First Intellect and Negating Divinity From All

The teaching now turns from the seeker's first bewilderment to the verification that lifts that bewilderment into Tawhid. The author cites:

`Fa-idha tahakkaqa iftiqarahu ila man fawqahu min al-hudud wa hajatahu ilayhi wa iftiqar alladhi fawqahu... mutanahiyan dhalika ila al-imam... wa minhu ila man fawqahu min al-'uqul al-mujarradat mutanahiyan dhalika.`

(And when he verifies the Mukathir's need for the one above him among the ranks, and his poverty toward him, and the need of the one above him in turn... ending in that at the Imam... and from him toward the one above him among the abstract intellects, ending in that.)

The decisive step is this: the believing seeker (mu'min) comes to verify that the very Mukathir before whom he stands bewildered is himself bewildered before the rank above him. The seeker sees that he is bewildered by the Mukathir, but that the Mukathir in turn is poor, needy, and bewildered before his own mufid, the one who bestows upon him. And the one above the Mukathir is in turn bewildered before the one above him, and so the chain continues, rank upon rank, in the same manner through the physical ranks (the hudud al-jismaniyya), until it reaches the Imam, upon him be peace. And from the Imam it ascends further, toward the abstract intellects (al-'uqul al-mujarradat) — those intellects that are not in the physical world at all, the angelic realities of the higher realms. The ascent ends, at the summit of the abstract intellects, at the First Intellect (Aql al-Awwal), which was itself bewildered before all the originated and created beings: bewildered at how it attained its precedence, even though all were brought into existence together at one time. The text continues:

`Thumma tahakkaqa wulhuhu fi mubdi'ihi wa mubdi'i ajna'ihi wa hayratihi fihi.`

(Then he verifies its bewilderment at its Originator, and at the Originator of its kinds, and its perplexity in Him.)

The seeker then verifies the bewilderment of the First Intellect itself before its Originator (mubdi') — the Originator of itself and of all its kinds — and its perplexity before Him. The whole chain of bewilderment, from the disciple before the Mukathir up to the First Intellect before the Originator, is thus laid bare. And there is a deep lesson embedded in this universal bewilderment: the first step in acquiring knowledge is humility, the confession that one does not have knowledge. One must first become, as it were, ignorant — must own one's not-knowing — before one can acquire. The station of bewilderment is therefore necessary for the da'i and for every seeker: as the circle revolves around its center, so the seeker revolves in bewilderment, and that very revolving, that perplexity, is the precondition of gnosis.

Having traced the bewilderment to its source, the seeker performs the act of Tawhid. The author cites:

`Nafa 'an haddihi alladhi huwa fi ufuqihi... wa athbataha lillah Ta'ala wa takabbara, alladhi la ilaha illa hu.`

(He negates it from his rank, which is in his horizon... and affirms it for Allah the Exalted, and proclaims His greatness — He besides whom there is no god.)

The seeker negates divinity from the had in his own horizon — that is, from the Mukathir — and from every rank above the Mukathir, physical and spiritual, all the way to the very end of the chain. He declares: none of these is a god who gives me my being; each of them is bewildered before another above him, the First Intellect itself bewildered before the Originator. The very condition that the First Intellect held toward the Originator — its bewilderment — the seeker now recognizes reproduced at every level, including in his own relation to the Mukathir. So he affirms: this Mukathir, for all his greatness and the majesty of his station, which I have truly recognized, is not my god; above him is one, and above that one another, up to the summit, and all of them are bewildered, and the truly Real is the Originator alone, mubdi'-e ta'ala. And so the seeker affirms divinity for Allah Ta'ala alone and proclaims His greatness — alladhi la ilaha illa Hu, He besides whom there is no god. He negates divinity from the Mukathir and from all above him, and affirms it for the one true Ilah who is Allah.

The author pauses to make the structure of the circles explicit. The Mukathir has his circle; above him is the circle of his mufid; above that, another circle; and all these circles are linked one to another, ascending until the final circle, which is that of the First Intellect. The believer's path of Tawhid is precisely this: first he confesses that the Mukathir has become a god for him, in the sense of the center of his bewildered orbit; then he verifies that the Mukathir is himself bewildered before another, and so he denies that the Mukathir is his god in the absolute sense; and so on up the linked circles until he reaches the true God beyond them all.

### The Circles of the Da'wah and the Letters of "Ilah"

The author now explains how these linked circles came to be, by recounting the formation of the da'wah in the spiritual world. When the da'wahs were given in that world, those who joined and repented with the repentance of the caller — tawabu bi-tawbatihi — ascended along with him; they went up with him. Those who did not join were given the da'wah anew. The pattern works thus: a circle has at its center a forerunner (sabiq); he calls, and those who answer and repent with his repentance ascend with him; the final individual left behind, who has not ascended, is given the command, "Our work is finished; now you do the work" — and he becomes the center of a new circle. Around him gather those who repent with his repentance, and again the final individual left is given the command in his turn. In this way circle was generated from circle: the circle of the initial person, then the circle of the Imam, then of the Bab, then of the Hujjat, and so on, each formed upon the pattern of the first. The first pattern was established there, in the spiritual world, and every subsequent circle — the circle of the 'ashir, of Adam the summoner, of the Qa'ims, of the Da'is, the Hujjats, the Imams, the circle of 'ain, the circle of mim — was established upon it, one after another. The bewilderment that orders each circle is this: the center of a given circle, around whom others revolve, must himself revolve around the center of the circle above his own; he too is in orbit, and so the structure of bewildered revolution is reproduced at every level. As the caller says to the one he commands, in effect: "You have been formed by seeing us; now you correct those below you." All of this is contained in La ilaha illa Allah.

This brings the author to a remarkable reading of the very word ilah, which he finds to encode the three conditions present in every one of these circles. The word ilah is composed of three letters: alif, lam, ha. The alif shows that there is precedence without an inspirer (sabqat without a mulhim) — that is, the First Intellect, the Aql al-Awwal, who preceded all the rest by his own act, with none above him to prompt or inspire him. The lam shows the condition of the second: advancing not by independent precedence but by seeing another and repenting with his repentance — tawabu bi-tawbatihi — the station of the one who is munba'ith min al-awwal, emanated from the First, who attained his rank as the second emanation attained his, by following. And the ha shows the third condition: the giving of the da'wah, which was performed by the seven intellects ('uqul) — the 'ashir, the tenth, did not give it. These three conditions, alif, lam, ha, will always be present in every circle, for in every circle there is everything; the entire story of the life of the universe is contained in this composition. Whose station is the alif? The First Intellect. Whose station is the lam? The one emanated from the First, the munba'ith min al-awwal. And the ha is the da'wah given by the seven intellects. So the very letters of "ilah" trace the structure of forerunner, follower, and summons that constitutes every circle of the hierarchy.

### The Created Gods and the One True God

The author returns to the act of affirmation and now draws out fully the contrast between the created "gods" and the one true God. He cites:

`Wa athbataha lillah Ta'ala wa takabbara, alladhi la ilaha illa hu min hadhihi al-aliha al-makhluqa wa al-mukhtara'a wa al-mubtada'a al-shamil li-kulli wahidin minhum ism al-murabbi alladhi fawqahu wa al-hajatu al-da'iyatu lahu ilayhi.`

(And he affirmed it for Allah the Exalted and proclaimed His greatness — He besides whom there is no god from among these created, invented, and originated deities, each one of whom carries the name of the nourisher who is above him and the need that calls him toward him.)

He affirms divinity for Allah and proclaims His greatness, declaring that there is no god from among the "deities" (aliha) — these gods that are created (makhluqa), invented (mukhtara'a), and originated (mubtada'a). And here is their distinguishing mark: each one of these created gods carries within himself the name of the nourisher (murabbi) who stands above him, and the need that calls him upward toward that nourisher. A created god is recognizable precisely as a created god by this — that he is sustained by one above him and impelled by a need toward one above him; he is never self-sufficient. The text continues:

`Illa man tawwahamat hayratu 'alihi wa danihi 'ala 'uluwwi halatihi.`

The only being who is not such a created god, who has no nourisher above him and no upward-calling need, is the one before whom the bewilderment of high and low alike is occasioned by the loftiness of His state — the one above whom there is no higher, whose every subordinate is bewildered before Him while He is bewildered before none. So the author concludes:

`Fa-la ilaha siwahu. Laysa juzuriyyatun minhum. La yajidu 'anha muhasan.`

(So there is no god besides Him. It is not a compulsion upon them; he finds no escape from it.)

There is no god besides that one divine Being. And the seeker's affirmation of Him is not a mere helpless compulsion — or rather, though the naming of Him is indeed forced upon the helpless servant, who finds no escape (muhas) from having to address Him somehow, the affirmation of His sole divinity is nonetheless the truth that the whole ascent has demonstrated. The author then completes the threefold movement of Tawhid, tanzih, and tajrid at the highest level:

`Thumma nazzaha Ta'ala wa takabbara 'an al-hudud al-jismaniyya al-mahduda wa 'an al-hudud al-ruhaniyya al-ghayr mahduda.`

(Then he declared Him transcendent and proclaimed His greatness above the limited physical ranks and above the unlimited spiritual ranks.)

This is tanzih in its fullness: the seeker declares the one God transcendent above the physical ranks, which are limited (mahduda), and above the spiritual ranks, which are unlimited (ghayr mahduda) — for the true God, the nourisher of all, is beyond both the bounded corporeal hierarchy and the unbounded spiritual hierarchy alike. Then comes tajrid:

`Thumma salabahu Ta'ala ma athbatahu lahum min al-asma'i wa al-sifati wa al-nu'ut wa al-idafat.`

(Then he stripped from Him the names and attributes and descriptions and relations that he had affirmed for them.)

The seeker then strips from the Originator, mubdi'-e ta'ala, all the names, attributes, descriptions (nu'ut), and relations (idafat) that he had affirmed for the ranks: these names and attributes belong to the physical and spiritual ranks, but the Originator is beyond them, for He is God in the absolute sense, divested of all that can be predicated. Finally, the seeker returns:

`Wa 'ada ila haddihi wa wulhihi fihi wa ta'atuhu.`

(And he returns to his own rank, and to his bewilderment in it, and to his obedience.)

After the whole movement of affirmation and negation, the seeker returns to his own rank. The mu'min is bewildered before the Mukathir; above the Mukathir are ranks, and above them ranks, up to the First Intellect, which is itself bewildered before another; and having traced all this, the seeker returns and says, in effect: each must do his own work, and I will do mine. He returns to his own station, to his bewilderment within it, and to his obedience and commitment to his Mukathir. At that moment he becomes the one who performs true Tawhid, true tanzih, and true tajrid — with gnosis (ma'rifat), with religion (din), with knowledge ('ilm), and with certainty (yaqin).

### The Tranquil Soul That Returns to Its Lord

To such a one Allah Ta'ala speaks the words of the Qur'an, which the author cites:

`Ya ayyuha al-nafsu al-mutma'inna irji'i ila rabbiki radiyatan mardiyya.`

(O tranquil soul, return to your Lord, well-pleased and well-pleasing.)

The crucial word is irji'i, "return," for the soul ascends to the very top and then returns. This is the movement: it goes up, and then it comes back down. "Return to your Lord" means return to your mufid, the one who bestowed upon you. And radiyatan mardiyya means: you yourself well-pleased, and your mufid well-pleased with you — both pleased together. The author glosses the verse further with its own continuation:

`Itmi'nanuha bi-ma 'arafat... wa mardatuha bi-amri rabbiha alladhi ilayhi yurja'u, mardiyyatan 'indahu... alladhina ila ma'rifatihim intaha wa balagha.`

The soul's tranquility (itmi'nan) lies in what it has come to know — its serenity is the fruit of its gnosis — and its pleasure lies in the command of its Lord, the Lord to whom it is returned. And it is well-pleasing (mardiyya) in His sight, and in the sight of all the ranks to whose gnosis the soul has reached and arrived. The author closes this movement with words of grateful praise:

`Wa alhamdulillah alladhi hadana li-hadha wa ma kunna li-nahtadiya lawla an hadana Allah. Laqad ja'at rusulu rabbina bil-haqq.`

(And praise be to Allah who guided us to this; we would not have been guided had Allah not guided us. Indeed the messengers of our Lord came with the truth.)

The tranquil soul, having reached the end of the gnosis of its mufid, is summoned to return to its Lord, both pleased and pleasing; and the praise belongs to Allah, who guided to this gnosis a soul that could not have been guided of itself, and whose messengers came bearing the truth.

### The Originator in the Qur'an: Surah al-Fatiha and the Three Classes

A question naturally arises: this Originator who created — is there mention of Him, and of the world of souls, in the Holy Qur'an, in its verses or in our supplications? The author affirms that there is. Different phrases at different places convey the matter — as in the verse that we were created min nafsin wahidatin, from a single soul. But the summary of all that the Qur'an teaches about the Originator is Surah al-Fatiha, Surah al-Hamd; and its details, as Sayyidna has explained, are founded upon the opening verse, Alhamdulillah Rabb al-'Alamin — Praise be to Allah, Lord of the worlds — for He is Rabb al-'Alamin, the Lord of all the worlds, and the qualities of Allah are unfolded from this. All the details are based upon this verse in the book Kitab al-'Alim wa al-Ghulam; whoever reads it will understand. The matter is thus mentioned both in supplication and in the Qur'an, its summary being Surah al-Fatiha, within which the du'at have explained the whole teaching.

The author then shows how the Fatiha encodes the three classes of people. The da'i teaches that Allah made three kinds: the repentant (nadimin), then those who incur wrath, then the astray. Where in the Fatiha are these found? In the verse Ihdina al-sirat al-mustaqim, sirat alladhina an'amta 'alayhim — "Guide us upon the straight path, the path of those upon whom You have bestowed favor." Those upon whom favor was bestowed (an'amta 'alayhim) are the nadimin, the repentant: the favor given to them was that they should return. Then the verse says ghayr al-maghdubi 'alayhim — "not of those upon whom is wrath." Who are the maghdub, those who have incurred wrath? They are the insistent ones, the musirrin, who persist in their error, and upon whom wrath has therefore descended. And the third, wa la al-dallin — "nor of the astray" — these are the bewildered, the mutahayyirin, the ones lost in perplexity who have not found their way through. So the sirat, the path that must be crossed, is set before three classes: the nadimin who are favored to return, the musirrin who incur wrath by their insistence, and the mutahayyirin who go astray in bewilderment. The Originator's creation of beings, and the details of that creation in the world of the physical body, have indeed come down in His pure speech revealed to the people of the world.

But the reality of the Originator Himself cannot be grasped, and this too the Qur'an establishes. The author cites:

`Subhan alladhi... Subhan Allah 'amma yasifun.`

(Glory be to Him... Glory be to Allah, above what they describe.)

Allah is pure above whatever attributes are ascribed to Him; His reality cannot be understood, He Himself cannot be comprehended. This is stated in many places in the Holy Qur'an, and from it is proven that we cannot understand the reality of Allah Ta'ala. 'Amma yasifun — "above what they describe": whatever attributes you describe for Him, He is pure (tanzih) from them, He is beyond them all. This is the very meaning of tanzih: that whatever the bewildered seekers, incapable as they are of His complete gnosis, ascribe to Him, He transcends. Even the First Intellect, their own mufid, was incapable (ajiz) of the gnosis of the Originator; the First Intellect did not possess the gnosis of mubdi'-e ta'ala but was bewildered before Him, and it was on account of this bewilderment that the First Intellect became a god for those below. And what word did they finally use of the Originator? "Hu" — He — and even that under compulsion, for they had no other word; the naming is a zanbun mashfu'un bil-istighfar, a sin coupled with forgiveness, uttered out of necessity, because to prove our own existence we must affirm the existence of the Existent, and to affirm Him we must address Him by some word, and the only word that does not falsely circumscribe Him is "Hu."

### Origination From Nothing: The World of Ibda' and the Distinction of Ibda' From Khalq

With the fifth lecture the author turns from the gnosis of the ranks to the very act of origination by which all things, ranks included, came to be — drawing now upon a long exposition of Sayyidna Mohyuddin, of which he presents the summary. He opens with the invocation and then the foundational citation:

`Inna Allaha ta'ala alladhi la tatajasaru nahwahu-l-khawatir wa la tasmu ilayhi-l-awham wa-l-zama'ir, abda'a 'alam al-ibda'i al-mukanna 'anhu bi-'alam al-amri wa 'alam al-'aqli wa 'alam al-qudsi wa 'alam al-safa'i wa 'alam al-latafati wa bi-l-'alam al-ruhani daf'atan wahidatan min ghayri shay'in taqaddamahum fa-yakuna lahum hayulan ula wa la ma'a shay'in sahabahum fa-yakuna mumathilan lahum wa mushakilan wa la 'ala shay'in aqallahum fa-yakuna lahum makanan awwalan.`

(Indeed Allah the Exalted, toward whom the thoughts dare not turn, and toward whom imaginations and inner selves cannot rise, originated the world of origination — which is alluded to as the world of command, the world of intellect, the world of sanctity, the world of purity, the world of subtlety, and the spiritual world — in a single instant: not from any thing preceding them that would be a prior matter for them, nor with any thing accompanying them that would be similar and like to them, nor upon any thing bearing them that would be a first place for them.)

Allah Ta'ala is such a Being that the passing thoughts (khawatir) dare not turn toward Him, and the imaginations (awham) and the inmost selves (zama'ir) cannot rise or grow lofty toward Him; our thought and our inner being alike fall back, unable to ascend to Him. This is the Being who originated the world of origination ('alam al-ibda'). And this originated world is alluded to by a cluster of names — the world of command ('alam al-amr), the world of intellect ('alam al-'aql), the world of sanctity ('alam al-quds), the world of purity ('alam al-safa), the world of subtlety ('alam al-latafah), and the spiritual world ('alam al-ruhani) — seven names in all for the single originated realm.

Before continuing, the author marks a fundamental distinction that governs everything to follow: the distinction between ibda' and khalq. Khalq, creation, is what occurs when the arrangement of already-existing things is changed — when a pre-existent thing is broken or reshaped and a new composite form is brought forth from it, the form altered while the underlying matter is presupposed. Ibda', origination, is something else entirely: it is creation from nothing, where no prior thing exists at all. The 'alam al-ibda', the world of origination, is created by ibda' in this strict sense, and three negations are pronounced to make the point unmistakable. First, it was originated not from any thing that preceded it — there was no prior substance to serve as its hayula, its prime matter or first leaven. Second, it was originated not with any accompanying thing — there was no co-present matter alongside it from which it was shaped, nothing it could resemble or take after. Third, it was originated not upon any thing bearing it — there was no pre-existing substrate to serve it as a place (makan), a first location upon which it rested. No prior matter, no concurrent matter, no underlying place: the world of origination was brought forth in a single instant (daf'atan wahidatan), from nothing whatsoever.

### Origination as Pure Favor, Not From Need

Why did the Originator originate at all? The author cites the answer:

`Wa-khtara'ahum 'alaman, wujudun min 'adamin ghayri mawjudin, tafaddulan minhu wa judan wa tatawwulan minhu.`

(And He originated them as a world — existence from a non-existent nothingness — as a favor from Him, and generosity, and bestowal from Him.)

Allah Ta'ala originated (ikhtara'a) the existents into a world of existence, drawing them from a nothingness ('adam) that was not existent. And what is this 'adam, this nothingness? It is not some world or realm; it is simply the absence of being. The author recalls how, in Sindh, a teacher once asked his students: when all this was taking place, where were we? — the point being precisely that there was no "where," for there was nothing but non-existence from which we were drawn. And the cause of the origination was no need or compulsion, but pure favor: tafaddulan minhu, a favor from Him; wa judan, and generosity, a gift; wa tatawwulan minhu, and a bestowal, a boon from Him. On the question of why the Originator created, the author notes, Sayyidna Mohyuddin has written chapter after chapter, negating every possible purpose and every possible need, refuting one supposed motive after another, until he arrives at the single conclusion that this favor was itself the only "purpose": that He originated out of sheer favor, generosity, and bestowal, and nothing else. The author cites Mohyuddin's grounding of this:

`Iz min shimati-l-karim al-qadir al-tafaddul, wa min sajiyyati-l-'aziz al-'azim al-jud, la muhtajan ilayhim wa la 'abithan bihim, iz huwa muta'alin mutakabbirun 'an dhalika.`

(Since it is of the nature of the Generous, the Powerful, to bestow favor, and of the nature of the Mighty, the Great, to be generous — not needing them nor playing with them, since He is exalted and transcendent above that.)

It is of the very nature (shimah) of the Generous (karim) and the Powerful (qadir) to bestow favor (fadl); it is of the very disposition (sajiyyah) of the Mighty ('aziz) and the Great ('azim) to be generous, to give and to bestow. He brought the existents into being not because He needed them — the Giver had no need of them whatsoever — nor as idle sport, not playing with them or creating them to no purpose, for He is exalted and transcendent above any such thing. The origination flowed from the bestowing nature of the Generous and Mighty, free of need and free of frivolity.

### The Luminous Forms and the Thousand-Fold Statures

The author next describes the manner in which the originated beings were brought forth, citing:

`Awjadahum ashbahan suriyyatan mahdatan nuraniyatan, dhawati qamatin alfiyah ka-l-qamat al-insaniyah al-bashariyah, mubayinatan li-anfusihim al-hayulaniyah.`

(He brought them into existence as forms, purely imaginal and luminous, possessing thousand-fold statures like the human, corporeal statures, distinct from their material souls.)

He brought them into existence as forms (ashbah) — and these forms were purely imaginal (suriyyah), pure (mahdah), and luminous (nuraniyya). They possessed statures (qamat) of a thousand-fold measure (alfiyya), like the human corporeal stature (al-qamat al-insaniyya al-bashariyya). The author is careful to set the direction of the likeness rightly: it is said that the originated forms were like the human corporeal stature only to make the matter intelligible to us, but in reality it is the other way around. The human corporeal stature is patterned upon those originated forms, not they upon it. When the Originator created, He created in such a state that the human bodily statures that later came into the world were fashioned on the pattern of those primordial luminous forms. So although we are taught that the originated forms resembled the human stature, the truth is that the human stature was made on the model of the originated forms. And these luminous forms are distinct (mubayinah) from the material souls (al-anfus al-hayulaniyya) that came later. Those souls did not yet exist; the souls are things of a later stage. The forms that were originated were luminous imaginal forms (ashbah suriyyah nuraniyya); the souls, when they were formed, were a fallen, degraded condition derived from these; and the bodies of flesh and blood, when they were formed, were a still more fallen condition. The forms were luminous subtleties (lata'if nuraniyya); the bodies are dark densities (katha'if zulmaniyya). The soul is a fallen form of the luminous original, and the body a yet more fallen form.

### The First Perfection: Created Equal as an Act of Justice

The author now sets out the doctrine of the first perfection, citing:

`Fa-awjada tilka-l-ashbah al-nuraniyah mutasawiyatan fi-l-kamal al-awwali alladhi huwa-l-wujud wa-l-hayat wa-l-quwwah wa-l-qudrah, la takhalufa fiha wa la tafadula wa la tabayuna baynaha wa la tamayula fi-ma daqqa wa jalla wa kathura wa qalla, 'adlan minhu 'azza wa jall.`

(So He brought those luminous forms into existence, equal in the first perfection — which is existence, life, strength, and ability — with no disagreement in them, no preference, no distinction among them, and no inclination in what was subtle or great, much or little, as an act of justice from Him, the Mighty and Majestic.)

He brought the luminous forms into existence equal (mutasawiyah) in the first perfection (kamal al-awwal). This first perfection is the original state in which Allah created them, and it consists of four things: existence (wujud), life (hayat), strength (quwwah), and ability — which the author glosses as knowledge and ability together ('ilm and qudrah). In this first perfection the forms were created perfectly equal: there was no disagreement among them (la takhaluf), no preference of one over another (la tafadul), no distinction (la tabayun), and no inclination (la tamayul) — neither in what is subtle nor in what is great, neither in much nor in little. The Originator created the first exactly as He created the last; there is not the slightest difference among the originated beings in the state of first perfection. And this equality was an act of justice ('adl) from Him, mighty and majestic. The author cites Mohyuddin's grounding of the principle:

`Iz al-'adilu yatasawa 'adluhu wa-l-fadil ya'ummu fadluhu.`

(Since the justice of the Just is equal, and the grace of the Gracious is universal.)

The justice of the Just is equal in its distribution, and the grace of the Gracious is universal in its reach; therefore the originated forms were created in perfect equality. This equality is anchored in scripture, which the author cites:

`Kama akhbara-l-kitab al-karim min qawli-l-'ali al-'azim: wa law kana min 'indi ghayrillahi la-wajadu fihi ikhtilafan kathira.`

(As the Noble Book has informed, from the saying of the High, the Great: "And if it had been from other than Allah, they would have found in it much contradiction.")

If the originated order had proceeded from any other than Allah, much contradiction (ikhtilaf) would be found within it — for only the perfect justice of Allah could produce a creation without disparity. The word ikhtilaf derives from khalf and comes in the form bab al-ifti'al; its sense is difference or discrepancy, and the verse declares that had the matter come from other than Allah, such discrepancy would abound. But because Allah created it, He showed His creation this kindness: He gave to none more and to none less; what He gave, He gave equally. This, the author notes, is the very verse that answers the question raised earlier — whether the equal creation of all beings is attested in the Holy Qur'an: it is attested here, in wa law kana min 'indi ghayrillahi la-wajadu fihi ikhtilafan kathira. And the author observes that this is the very reality affirmed by Sayyidna Hamiduddin, may Allah sanctify his soul, in the seventh path (mashra') of the fourth form (suwar) of Rahat al-'Aql. He cites Hamiduddin's words:

`Wa dar al-ibda'i wa-l-inbi'athi la 'a'iqa fiha li-khuluwwihima min al-mawad allati ta'uqu wa tajarruduha minha wa kawnaha suwaran mahdatan la tata'allaqu bi-maddatin wa la biha maddah li-tahjuzaha 'an al-fi'l.`

(And in the abode of origination and the abode of emanation there is no obstacle, because of their freedom from the matters that obstruct, and their being abstracted from them, and their being pure forms attached to no matter, with no matter in them that would prevent them from action.)

### No Obstacle in the Abode of Origination: Existence in a Single Instant

This citation from Hamiduddin furnishes the reason why the whole originated order could come into being at once. In the abode of origination (dar al-ibda') and the abode of emanation (dar al-inbi'ath), there is no obstacle (la 'a'iqa). And why is there no obstacle? Because these abodes are free (khuluww) of the obstructing matters — the kinds of matter that would impede are simply not present there. The forms are abstracted (tajarrud) from such matter; they are pure forms (suwar mahdah) attached to no matter, with no matter in them. Matter is what obstructs: wherever matter is present, there hindrances and impediments arise. But here there is no matter at all to halt the forms from action (fi'l). There is power (quwwah) and ability (qudrah) present, and these can show their effect freely; no obstruction has been set in their way that would prevent the second action or impede the attainment of the second perfection. The author cites the conclusion that Hamiduddin draws:

`Wa idha kana la 'a'iqa fiha fa-wujud mawjudatiha la bi-zamanin bal daf'atan wahidatan, mithla wujudi ishraqi basit al-hawa' 'an daw' al-shamsi, la bi-zamanin... wa id'atu-l-nar al-bayt al-muzlima daf'atan wahidatan, la bi-zamanin.`

(And since there is no obstacle in them, the existence of their existents is not in time but in a single instant — like the illumination of the expanse of the air by the light of the sun, not in time... and like the lighting of a dark house by a fire in a single instant, not in time.)

Since there is no obstacle, the existence of the existents of these abodes came to be not in time (la bi-zaman) but in a single instant (daf'atan wahidatan) — all at once, in one moment. Two images make this vivid. The first is the spreading of light through the expanse of the air from the light of the sun: when the sun is present, the whole air is illuminated at once, not piece by piece in time. The second is the lighting of a dark house by a fire: the moment the fire is kindled, the entire room is filled with light at once. So too the whole world of origination came into existence in a single instant, like air flooded with sunlight, like a dark house suddenly illumined.

### Actual and Potential Intellects Existing Together: No Interval Between the Two Perfections

The author draws out the implication of this simultaneity, citing the continuation of Hamiduddin's passage:

`'Ala anna al-shay'a fi tilka-l-dar lam yataqaddam wujuduhu 'ala shay'in min al-'uqul al-qa'imati bi-l-fi'li wa-l-'uqul al-qa'imati bi-l-quwwah. Bal wujud al-kulli ma'an.`

(That a thing in that abode — its existence did not precede anything of the intellects existing in actuality and the intellects existing in potentiality. Rather, the existence of all was together.)

In that abode, no thing's existence preceded another's. The existence of the intellects subsisting in actuality (bi-l-fi'l) did not come before the existence of the intellects subsisting in potentiality (bi-l-quwwah). The "intellects in potentiality" are, in effect, the powers that were given to the intellects, which were afterward actualized and established; their existence was not preceded by some prior existing thing. Rather, the existence of all was together (wujud al-kull ma'an), at one and the same time: both the intellect subsisting potentially and the intellect subsisting actually existed simultaneously. And this carries a momentous consequence: there was no interval of time separating the first perfection (kamal al-awwal) from the second perfection (kamal al-thani). The two perfections were not divided by any passage of time; the potential and the actual co-existed from the outset, so that no temporal gap stood between the initial state of equality and the higher state to be attained. The author notes that some span of time is needed for our understanding to follow the matter — even the kindling of a fire seems, to us, to take some time before the room fills with light — but the light in question here is not like ordinary light that spreads gradually; it is such that the instant it is lit, the whole is filled at once, and this is the character of the first perfection.

### Equal but Not Infallible: The Meaning of Error in the First Perfection

The author now explains, following Mohyuddin, what the state of first perfection does and does not include. He cites:

`Yuridu bi-dhalika a'la Allahu qudsahu fi hali wujudihim al-awwali wa kamalihim al-awwali alladhina hum fihi mutasawun, kama sabaqa min qawlihi, wa hum ma'a dhalika fi bad'i wujudihim al-jami'i fi-l-fi'li wa-l-'amali ghayru ma'sumin min al-khata'.`

(By that, may Allah sanctify his soul, he means: in the state of their first existence and their first perfection, in which they are all equal — as has preceded from his saying — and yet, along with that, in the beginning of their collective existence, in action and deed, they are not infallible from error.)

By this, Mohyuddin means the state of the beings' first existence and first perfection, in which they are all equal, as already established. And yet — and this is the crucial qualification — along with that equality, in the beginning of their collective existence, in action (fi'l) and deed ('amal), they are not infallible (ghayru ma'sumin) from error (khata). The state of first perfection, for all its equality, is not the perfect, infallible state. The perfect state is the second perfection (kamal al-thani); the first perfection is the lower, initial condition, and in it the beings, though equal, are not protected from error; error can proceed from them. And what is this "error"? The author is precise: the error consists in failing to attain the second perfection. That is the error — a delay in responding to the invitation, a refusal to come when called, and, beyond that, the misleading of others, the setting of others upon the wrong path. All of this can issue from beings who are in the state of first perfection, for in that state they are ghayr ma'sum. The first perfection was given to them as a test: go straight, and use rightly the powers given to you. The state is not yet the state of return-perfected; it is the trial-state of equality from which one must journey, by the right use of one's powers, to the second perfection.

The author then notes Mohyuddin's prayer at this point, and pauses over its grammar to drive home the meaning of "error":

`Asmana Allahu min an nazilla aw nuzilla, bi-haqqi Muhammadin wa alihi-l-tahirin salawatullahi 'alayhim ajma'in.`

(May Allah protect us from slipping or being made to slip, by the right of Muhammad and his pure progeny, the blessings of Allah upon them all.)

May Allah protect us from this matter — that we should slip (an nazilla) or be made to slip (nuzilla). The grammatical contrast is itself instructive. Zalla means "he slipped"; azalla means "he made another slip" — just as qata'a means "he cut" and aqta'a means "he had another cut," or sajada means "he prostrated" and asjada means "he made another prostrate." So nazilla is "that we should slip," and nuzilla is "that we should be made to slip" or "that we should make others slip." Likewise, the author notes, zalla can carry the sense of going astray, and adalla of leading another astray: zalla, "he went astray," adalla, "he led astray." The rules of grammar and morphology hold constant — fa'ala, "he did," and af'ala, "he had it done." The prayer thus asks protection both from slipping ourselves and from being the cause of another's slipping — precisely the two faces of the "error" that is failure to attain the second perfection: that we ourselves fall short of it, or that we mislead others and cause them to fall short. With this prayer the author moves to the second section of the second gate.

### The Form That Performed Tawhid: Sahib al-Tawhid

The author now narrates the pivotal moment in which one of the originated forms became the first to perform Tawhid. He cites:

`Thumma inna wahidan min tilka-l-ashbah al-suwariyah nazara bi-dhatihi ila dhatihi wa ila abna'i jinsihi, fa-'alima anna lahum mubdi'an bi-khilafihim, fa-nafa 'anhu wa 'an abna'i jinsihi al-ilahiyata wa athbataha li-mubdi'ihim ta'ala wa shahida lahu bi-l-wahdaniyah.`

(Then indeed one of those imaginal forms looked, with his essence, at his essence and at the children of his kind; so he knew that they had an Originator unlike them; so he negated divinity from himself and from the children of his kind, and affirmed it for their Originator, the Exalted, and bore witness to His oneness.)

One of those imaginal forms looked — nazara bi-dhatihi, with his essence — at his own essence (ila dhatihi). This is the heart of the matter: he looked, by means of his own essence, at his own essence, and then at his kind, the children of his own genus. He saw himself, and he saw all his fellows. And from this looking he knew (fa-'alima) that there was for himself and for them an Originator (mubdi') unlike them (bi-khilafihim) — one different from them all, who had brought them into being. Having known this, he negated (fa-nafa) divinity (ilahiyya) from himself and from the children of his kind: he could not claim, "I am the god," nor could any of his kind. And he affirmed (athbata) divinity for their Originator, the Exalted, and bore witness to His oneness (wahdaniyya). In this single act he performed all three branches at once — Tawhid, tajrid, and tanzih together — and that very form became Sahib al-Tawhid, the Possessor of Tawhid, the first to declare the divine unity. By looking at his own essence and his kind and recognizing therein an Originator unlike them, by negating divinity from all that was originated and affirming it for the Originator alone, he enacted the whole of Tawhid in its threefold completeness.

### Seeking the Means of His Greatness: Tawassul

The author continues with what this first form did after his act of recognition, citing:

`Wa i'tarafa lahu bi-l-rububiyah wa sabbaha lahu wa khasha'a wa tazallala wa khada'a wa tawassala bi-'azmatihi ilayhi wa dara'a.`

(And he acknowledged for Him Lordship, and glorified Him, and was reverent, and humbled himself, and submitted, and sought means through His greatness toward Him, and supplicated.)

He acknowledged Lordship (rububiyya) for the Originator, glorified Him (sabbaha), was reverent (khasha'a), humbled himself (tazallala), and submitted (khada'a). And then, most importantly, he sought means (tawassala) through the Originator's own greatness ('azmah) toward Him, and supplicated (dara'a). This is the special point upon which the author dwells: the first form took the very greatness of the Originator as the means by which to reach the Originator. The greatness of the Originator cannot itself be reached directly; therefore the form took that greatness as his wasilah, his means of approach. Through the means of that greatness, he gave testimony to the Originator's Lordship and divinity, confessing his own lowliness. He did not presume to approach directly, for direct approach is impossible; rather, he made the Originator's greatness itself the intermediary by which he turned toward Him. This is tawassul: to take the greatness of the Originator as the means to the Originator, when the Originator Himself cannot be reached.

### The Descent of Divine Supports and the Word of Allah: La ilaha illa Allah

The consequence of this seeking of means was a great descent of confirmation upon the first form. The author cites:

`Fa-tadaraka min mubdi'ihi al-mawad al-ilahiyah wa-l-ta'yidat al-wahdaniyah, wa-ttasala bihi 'ilm al-jari wa-l-nur al-sari alladhi huwa kalimatullahi ta'ala.`

(So there reached him from his Originator the divine supports and the confirmations of oneness, and there connected with him the flowing knowledge and the spreading light, which is the Word of Allah, the Exalted.)

There reached him from his Originator the divine supports (al-mawad al-ilahiyya) and the confirmations of oneness (al-ta'yidat al-wahdaniyya). And there connected with him the flowing knowledge ('ilm al-jari) and the spreading light (al-nur al-sari) — and this flowing knowledge and spreading light is the Word of Allah Ta'ala (kalimatullah). And what is that Word, that kalimah? It is La ilaha illa Allah. This is the essence of the whole matter: through this first form's act of negation, recognition, and tawassul, there flowed into him the flowing knowledge and spreading light which is the kalimah, La ilaha illa Allah, the Word standing between the Originator (mubdi') and the originated (mubda'), and through which knowledge and light flow. The author cites the further teaching about this kalimah:

`Wa lidhalika qila inna-l-kalimata fawqa-l-'aqli wa dun al-mubdi'. Wa yusamma aydan bi-l-amr.`

(And for that reason it is said that the kalimah is above the intellect and below the Originator. And it is also named the command.)

For this reason it is said that the kalimah is above the intellect (fawqa al-'aql) and below the Originator (dun al-mubdi'). The kalimah La ilaha illa Allah occupies precisely this intermediate station: it stands beneath the Originator and above the intellect, and the flowing light came from the Originator down to it and through it to the intellect. The kalimah is thus a continuous link, in which the Originator's will and La ilaha illa Allah are manifested together. And the same kalimah is also given the name al-amr, the command, for all the commands are placed within it. The kalimah is no momentary thing; it is a link established for as long as creation endures. As long as the creation remains, the kalimah remains, and the flowing light remains. The author observes, too, that this is why the world of origination ended at seven: the First Intellect came to be, and the Originator made seven, and the tenth is the 'ashir; the world ended at seven because the will of the Originator was that it should end at seven, for He had placed everything within the kalimah and willed that it terminate at seven.

### The First Intellect Encompasses All Knowledge

Through the kalimah the First Intellect attained a knowledge that encompassed all things. The author cites:

`Wa bihi ahata bi-kulli shay'in 'ilman wa 'alima ma kana wa ma sayakun.`

(And through it he encompassed all things in knowledge, and knew what was and what will be.)

Through the kalimah the First Intellect encompassed every thing with knowledge ('ilm), and knew what had been and what would be (ma kana wa ma sayakun) — for La ilaha illa Allah contains everything, all that has happened and all that will happen in the future. But there is a crucial sequence here that the author underscores. The complete knowledge had, in a sense, already been given with the first perfection; yet the First Intellect did not recognize it, did not know how to use it. What the light of the kalimah supplied was the understanding of what he had been given and the way to use the powers he possessed. And before he could obtain the kalimah and its light, he had first to negate himself — to affirm that he was not his own creator. Just as the first form had said, "I am not my creator; our creator is someone else," so the First Intellect had first to confess that he and all his kind were not their own creators but that their Creator was another. The moment he said this — the moment of self-negation — he obtained the kalimah, and in the light of the kalimah all things were obtained by him. Self-negation precedes the gift: one must first own one's not-being-the-creator before the kalimah and its all-encompassing light are bestowed.

The author then turns to the inner composition of the kalimah, which the First Intellect found within his own essence. He cites:

`Wa wajada fi dhatihi min al-sururi wa-l-jazali wa-l-huburi wa-l-'ismati wa-l-thana'i wa-l-'azmati wa-l-kibriya'i ma la yaqdiru an yastura sifatahu bananun wa la yutarjimu 'anhu lisanun.`

(And he found in his essence, of joy and delight and happiness and protection and praise and greatness and pride, that whose description no fingertip could write nor any tongue translate.)

The First Intellect found within his essence such joy (surur), delight (jazal), happiness (hubur), protection ('ismah), praise (thana'), greatness ('azmah), and pride (kibriya') that no fingertip (banan) — no pen — could write its description, and no tongue (lisan) could translate its account. From the light of the kalimah he knew that after death there would be salvation, and he found within himself a joy beyond all writing and all speech.

### The Numbered Composition of the Kalimah and Bismillah

The author now expounds the numerical composition of La ilaha illa Allah, which he reads as encoding the whole structure of creation. The kalimah is composed in layers. First, it consists of negation (nafi) and affirmation (ithbat) — two: the negative and the positive, La ilaha (negation) and illa Allah (affirmation). Second, it contains three letters in the word ilah: alif, lam, ha — three. Then there are the jewels of the letters — five. Then four words: La, ilaha, illa, Allah — four. After this the count of one comes to its end at seven. And there are seven muqatta'at, the disjoined letters at which the pen, or the tongue, stops — for the author teaches that the world ended at seven luminous forms precisely because of these seven muqatta'at, the seven places where the pen halts. In the luminous world the matter ended at seven luminous forms, and it ended there because La ilaha illa Allah, by the will of the Originator, was to end at seven; the Originator placed everything within the kalimah and willed that it terminate at seven, and so it was. After the seven come twelve, and the twelve are the ascending spirits (ruh-i-sa'idah). This composition works everywhere — in every Imam, in every barzakh, in every believer, among all existents: the composition of two, three, four, seven, and twelve operates throughout. And seven plus twelve make nineteen.

Where are these nineteen found? In Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim there are nineteen. The author cites the formula and reads its structure:

`Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim.`

Within Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim the numbers reappear. Where are the two? In Bismillah and al-Rahman — in the two kinds of grammatical construction: the additive construction (tarkib-i-idafi) and the descriptive construction (tarkib-i-tawsifi). The author teaches that of these two, the additive construction (tarkib-i-idafi) is the more excellent, and the descriptive construction (tarkib-i-tawsifi) is the lesser. He notes, too, a striking feature of La ilaha illa Allah itself: its order is, as it were, inverted, for affirmation precedes negation in the deeper reading — the flaw, the reversal, has come upside down — and of this it is said: "Let my affirmation be established by your negation; let me be negated such that only You remain." In Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim the additive construction is a most exalted construction, and the descriptive lesser than it.

The author then cites the explanation of Sayyidna Muhammad Taher, may Allah sanctify his soul, regarding the structure of Bismillah. Bismillah — seven letters — is the week of the seven Imams. And with each Imam there are twelve of his deputies, his naqibs, twelve in number, and these twelve are al-Rahman al-Rahim. So Bismillah encodes the week of the seven Imams, each Imam with his Sath, Imam, and Ma'dhun, the whole built upon the pattern of the seven Imams' week. And the twelve naqibs are, in reality, with every Imam; when the seventh Imam appears, the twelve who appear with him are of a very high rank, higher than the twelve who appeared before, for he is a mursal, a messenger. Thus the additive and descriptive constructions of Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim, in Muhammad Taher's reading, encode the seven Imams and their twelve naqibs, and the additive construction is the more excellent.

### Increase in Reverence and the Fear of the Learned

Having obtained the kalimah, the First Intellect did not grow proud but grew more humble. The author cites:

`Thumma izdada li-dhalika li-mubdi'ihi ta'ala tazallulan wa khudu'an wa hayratan fi 'azmatihi wa khushu'an.`

(Then, because of that, he increased toward his Originator the Exalted in humility and submission and bewilderment at His greatness and reverence.)

When the First Intellect obtained the kalimah, he increased even more in humility (tazallul) and submission (khudu') toward his Originator, in bewilderment (hayrah) before His greatness, and in reverence (khushu'). The more he came to know, the more humble and reverent he became. The author grounds this in the Qur'an:

`Wa lidhalika nataqa-l-kitab al-karim bi-qawlihi ta'ala: in-nama yakhsha Allaha min 'ibadihi al-'ulama'.`

(And for that reason the Noble Book spoke with His saying, the Exalted: "Indeed, only those of knowledge among His servants fear Allah.")

For this reason the Noble Book declared: innama yakhsha Allaha min 'ibadihi al-'ulama' — indeed, only the learned ('ulama') among His servants fear Allah. The author dwells on the grammar: the ma in innama is not the negating ma here but rather lends the force of "indeed," with the ma being superfluous (za'idah), so that the sense is "indeed it is only the learned among His servants who fear Allah" — the ma carrying the force of illa, "except." Every knower ('alim) of the secret of creation and the causes of nature is, among all people, the one who fears Allah most. And this fear is not the fear of dread; it is the acknowledgment of His greatness. The more knowledge increases, the more the awe in the divine presence increases — not as terror, but as the recognition of His majesty. The author recalls how Amir al-Din al-Sadiq, upon him be peace, when the color of someone's face would change in his presence, would say, "Do you not know in whose court you are standing?" — for fear, rightly understood, is the acknowledgment of the greatness of the One before whom one stands.

### The First Intellect as Veil, Gate, and Greatest Name

The author now sets out the supreme station to which the First Intellect was raised. He cites:

`Thumma inna Allaha ta'ala ittakhadhahu lahu hijaban wa ja'alahu lahu baban la yutawassalu ilayhi illa bihi wa la yunaja illa min janabihi wa la yuwahhadu illa min asbabihi, fa-sara ismuhu al-a'zamu wa maqamuhu al-ashrafu al-akramu.`

(Then indeed Allah the Exalted took him as a veil for Himself and made him a gate for Himself, through whom alone one may seek means to Him, and from whose presence alone one may hold intimate discourse, and through whose means alone He is declared One; so he became His Greatest Name and His most noble, most honorable station.)

Allah Ta'ala took the First Intellect as a veil (hijab) for Himself and made him a gate (bab) for Himself — an access gate to the divine. And three exclusive functions were vested in him. One cannot seek means (tawassul) to Allah except through him (la yutawassalu ilayhi illa bihi): the means to the Originator can be sought only through the First Intellect. One cannot hold intimate discourse (munajah) with Allah except from his presence (illa min janabihi). And Allah is not declared One (yuwahhad) except through his means (illa min asbabihi): true Tawhid cannot be performed except through the First Intellect. Until one acknowledges the First Intellect — until one acknowledges the wali — one cannot attain the second perfection, cannot seek the means, cannot hold the intimate discourse, cannot rightly declare the divine oneness. By this exclusive mediation the First Intellect became the Greatest Name of Allah (ismuhu al-a'zam) and the most noble and honorable station (maqamuhu al-ashraf al-akram).

### The Second Emanation and the Delusion of Equality

The author here introduces the contrasting case of the second emanation, who failed where the first succeeded. The First Intellect was taken as veil and gate; but the second emanation (munba'ith al-thani) was deceived by a corrupt notion. He too acknowledged the First Intellect — he conceded the First Intellect's superiority and recognized that both their existences were real. But what undid him was the notion of equality (wahm-e masawat): he imagined that his own vision was equal to the vision of the First Intellect, the first emanation. And because of this delusion of equality, he could not attain the second perfection. The principle is exact: in the state of first perfection, all are indeed equal; but in the state of second perfection, no one can be equal. In the second perfection none is equal to another; each is unique, and the ranks are fixed in their before-and-after order. The second emanation's error was to carry the equality proper to the first perfection into the second, claiming for his vision a parity with the First Intellect's that the second perfection does not permit. He acknowledged the First Intellect's superiority and yet claimed equality with the first emanation — and in that contradiction he forfeited the second perfection. To acknowledge the rank is necessary, but to imagine oneself its equal is the very error that bars the way upward.

### The Circle of Oneness and the Final Station of the First Intellect

The author closes the section with the doctrine of the circle of oneness, which belongs to the Originator alone and which the First Intellect uniquely guards. The First Intellect is the first originated being, and the circle of the first Originator (mubdi') stands above and apart: no one has reached it, and no one can reach it, for it is the circle of oneness (da'irat al-wahdat). The First Intellect is the possessor of the veil (hijab); unless one has his means, one cannot approach. The circle of the first Originator is the circle of oneness, and the author records that it was asked of Sayyidna Sadiq how "oneness" can be spoken of as a "circle." He replied that "oneness" here is a special honorific (a term of special honor), and that quds, sanctity, is likewise called a circle — a circle that the First Intellect has encompassed. The author recalls the sequence by which the First Intellect rose to this station: first his state was that of the first perfection; then, when he looked with his essence at his essence, he took the means of the greatness of God Ta'ala — all these things happening one after another, with their before and after, yet all unfolding within the circle of oneness, into which no other being entered. This is the uniqueness (wahdat) of every circle: that each must be within its own measure — and yet the matter is "not in time," but in the before-and-after of origination, for time itself comes only from the sun, and there, in the abode of origination, there is only the priority and posteriority (qabliyyat and ba'diyyat) that precede time. And the kalimah La ilaha illa Allah is the essence of it all: appointed such that after seven it is broken, the pen halting at the seventh — for it is not a circle of time, and even a single point is its own circle. With this the second gate of the first canopy is brought to its close, in the name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.
