This is the first volume of a work called *Al-Anwaar al-Lateefah*, "The Subtle Lights," and its subject is stated in its subtitle: tawhid, the oneness of God. What you are about to read is an exposition — a sustained piece of religious teaching that moves through eight thematic chapters rather than telling a story or staging a debate. It argues its way toward a single claim: that God is one in a sense stricter than the word usually carries, one beyond every image, name, and act of pointing the mind can perform. The chapter titles trace that argument — from a soul sealed in a lamp finding its road home, through the God no gesture can reach and the sin of naming what cannot be named, to what oneness actually means and, at the last, the first being ever to declare it.

The material presented here gives no author, no date, and no place; the front matter of this edition does not record an attribution, and none should be inferred from the prose. What the volume does declare about itself is how it speaks. It is narrated in an external voice: an expositor stands outside the teaching and lays it out. Where a first person appears — an "I" or a "we" — it belongs to that narrator explaining, not to a character within a scene. There are no dialogue partners here to mistake for the voice.

The figures who populate the book are largely offices rather than named individuals. A vocabulary of a graded spiritual hierarchy runs through it: the Imam al-Zaman, the Imam of the Age, at its summit; below him the ranks collectively called the hudud, among them the da'i or summoner, the hujjah or proof, and the ma'dhun or licensed one, together making up the Du'at. It is worth knowing before you begin that most of these words are titles attached to a rank, not proper names — so the same honorific, and the plain word "teacher," may fall on different persons at different points. The book's sixth chapter turns exactly on this: every teacher is a threshold to be crossed, never a god to be halted at.

Around this sits a second cluster of terms doing the heavy conceptual work. Tanzih (declaring God beyond all likeness) and tajrid (stripping every attribute away) drive the argument for transcendence, while ta'wil, the drawing-out of inner meaning, is its method. A further set of abstract forms — Ahadiyyah, Samadiyyah, Huwiyyah and their kin — name modes or degrees of the divine itself. Let the glossary carry their precise weight; read them, at first, as the technical machinery by which the book says what oneness is.
