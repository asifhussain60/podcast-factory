This is a treatise on the oneness of God — tawhid — and the first volume of a longer work. It does not argue by dry definition; it moves, chapter by chapter, as a road the soul is meant to walk: from a sealed lamp and the journey home toward a God whom no pointing can reach and no name can enclose.

Its central claim is that the oneness of God is not a number but a purity. To speak of Him at all, the book holds, one must strip away every attribute that would liken Him to a created thing — for to name the unnameable is itself a kind of transgression. From there it turns to what oneness actually means, and to the ranks by which a person climbs toward it: the breaking of the self at the nearest rank, and the recognition that every teacher is a threshold to be crossed, not a god to be worshipped in place of God.

The later chapters treat of creation out of nothing and of a justice in which all stand equal, closing with the first who declared that there is no god but He.

No author is named within the volume itself. It speaks throughout not in one man's voice but as a transmitted report, handing down what it teaches rather than claiming it as its own.
