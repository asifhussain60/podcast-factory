## Retribution, Inheritance, and the Word of Unity

### The Scale of Justice Established Over Every Servant

This portion of the discourse, drawn from the fourth chapter (*bab al-rabi'*) of the seventh pavilion (*surad*) and continuing into the fourth pavilion, resumes a theme that had reached its natural conclusion in the preceding lectures: the doctrine of *mabda' wa-ma'ad*, the beginning and the end, considered with respect to three classes of human being — the *wali*, the friend of God; the *zahiri*, the merely exoteric believer who lives at the surface of religion; and the *didd*, the opponent who stands against the truth. The thread that binds these three together, and which now becomes the governing principle of the entire section, is the scale of justice — *mizan al-'adl* — which Allah, exalted is He, has established and set in motion over all His servants without exception.

The foundational statement is unambiguous. The scale of justice that Allah has erected over His creatures `lā yughādiru ṣaghīratan wa-lā kabīratan illā aḥṣāhā` — it leaves behind neither a small deed nor a great one without having enumerated it, counted it, and entered it into the reckoning. Nothing escapes this scale. Whether a person is a friend of God, an exoteric believer, or an outright adversary, the justice of Allah encompasses him entirely; to each is given precisely in the measure of what he intends and what he does. This is not a justice administered by some arbitrary or shifting official who differs from age to age. It is an appointed, established law of Allah — and whoever is appointed to administer it administers exactly this same unvarying decree. The law is implemented; the scale is upright and standing.

The principle of recompense built into this scale is stated in the Arabic of the text: `wa-jāzā ʿalayhā, in khayran fa-khayrun, wa-in sharran fa-sharrun` — and He recompenses according to it: if there be good, then good shall be the reward; and if there be evil, then evil shall be the requital. Whoever performs anything, however small or however large, will receive its recompense in kind. Do a good deed, and good returns to you; commit an evil, and the evil returns upon you. There is here no escape and no exception, for the operation is, in a sense, automatic: the one who does good reaps its benefit of itself, and the one who does evil suffers its loss of itself.

This is the doctrine the section labors to expound at every level, from the cosmic to the most minute, and it is worth dwelling on its full reach before turning to its difficult consequences. The justice of Allah is operative over everything. As the soul performs, so the soul bears. There is no act of good or evil, however hidden, however trivial it may appear in the eyes of men, that the scale does not register and to which it does not assign its exact and proportionate return.

### The Friends of God Are Not Exempt From Retribution

The most arresting application of this principle — and the one that most tests the believer's understanding — is that even the very highest among God's chosen are not removed from its operation. The discourse recalls a matter already mentioned in earlier lectures: the passing of the *natiq*, the speaking-prophet, who departed this world poisoned, and the passing of his *wasi*, his testamentary heir, Amir al-Mu'minin, who was killed. The Arabic preserves the formulation: `wa-dhakarnā intiqāl al-nāṭiq dawr masmūman wa-naqlat waṣiyyihi bil-mawt... maqtūlan` — we have mentioned the passing of the speaking-prophet of the cycle in a state of being poisoned, and the passing of his heir by death... slain. And likewise, the text continues, `wa-kadhalika mā wālānā al-Ḥasan wal-Ḥusayn wal-Mustanṣir wal-ʿĀmir, ṣalawāt Allāh ʿalayhim ajmaʿīn, alladhīna minhum man intaqala masmūman wa-minhum man intaqala maqtūlan` — and likewise our masters al-Hasan, al-Husayn, al-Mustansir, and al-'Amir, the blessings of Allah be upon them all, among whom were those who passed away poisoned and those who passed away slain.

These are not random tragedies, nor merely the cruelty of wicked men allowed to run its course. The text insists upon their deeper cause: `wa-inna dhālika jarā ʿalayhim bi-mūjib al-ḥikmah wa-qaḍiyyat al-ʿadl qiṣāṣan` — and indeed that befell them by the requirement of wisdom and the demand of justice, as retribution (*qisas*). The teacher pauses here over a fine point of the Arabic, distinguishing `inna`, which means "indeed," from `idhā`, which means "if" — the reading must be "and indeed that befell them," for it is an affirmation of a settled reality, not a conditional possibility. What befell these exalted figures came upon them as *qisas*, as the exacting of a recompense, `ʿammā kāna min kulli wāḥidin min ahl dawrihi` — for that which proceeded from every single one of the people of their cycle.

The doctrine is then stated in its most uncompromising form: `wa-dhāka anna al-qiṣāṣ wāqiʿun ʿalayhim, salām Allāh ʿalayhim, ṣafwat Allāh min khalqihi wa-umanāʾahu ʿalā waḥyihi wa-khulafāʾahu fī arḍihi wa-shuhadāʾahu ʿalā ʿibādihi, fa-lā suqūṭa lahu ʿan shayʾin min ʿālam al-ṭabīʿah jumlatun, wa-lā manāṣa li-ṣaghīrihi wa-lā kabīrihi wa-qalīlihi wa-kathīrihi` — and that is because retribution falls upon them, the peace of Allah be upon them, who are the chosen elect of Allah from His creation, His trustees over His revelation, His vicegerents in His land, and His witnesses over His servants; yet there is no falling away of it from anything whatsoever in the world of nature, in its entirety, and there is no escape from it for its small or its great, its little or its much. The very fact that they are God's elect, His *umana'* over revelation, His *khulafa'* upon the earth, His *shuhada'* over His servants — this exalted rank does not lift them out of the scale; the scale operates upon them precisely as it operates upon the world of nature in its entirety.

To drive home how absolute this is, the text reasons from the greater to the lesser: `idh law kānat al-musāmaḥah jāʾizatan li-aḥadin min dhālika aw shayʾin minhu, la-kāna Awliyāʾ Allāh ʿalayhim al-salām aḥaqqu wa-awlā bil-musāmaḥah min jamīʿ al-khalqah` — for if pardon (*musamaha*), the overlooking and remission of the debt, were permissible for anyone in any of this or any part of it, then surely the Friends of Allah, peace be upon them, would have been the most deserving and the most worthy of pardon of all creation. The argument is decisive. If the law admitted of any waiver, the Friends of God would have been the first to receive it; they would have stood at the head of the line for mercy. That they did not — that retribution was exacted from them in full — proves that no waiver exists. The very same law that governs all creation governs the Friends of God as well, and they too were held to account in the matter of *qisas*, whether the debt arose from their own actions or from the people of their cycle who stood with them in their age.

### Deeds Reach Across the People of an Era

This last point opens onto a further and subtler teaching: that a person's deeds are not sealed within his own person but extend to and are accounted against the people of his era. The retribution due from one age may therefore be exacted in another. The discourse illustrates this with the supreme example of Imam Husayn, upon him be peace. It was said of him — and the teaching is preserved as a settled doctrine — that Mawla Ali had fought certain battles, and in those battles a number of men were killed, men from among the forebears of those who later confronted Husayn. The recompense for those slayings was exacted not from Ali himself but from Imam Husayn, upon him be peace, in his own later age.

The principle is general: a person's actions are not confined to himself; they touch the people of his era, and the people of his era are held to account in turn. If they themselves committed the deed, the recompense returns to them directly; if it was committed by the people of their era, then the people of the era bear it; and a relationship is established between an event and those gathered around it through which the reckoning is completed. So it is said that in the time of Imam Husayn a veil, a concealment, descended — and the cause of that veil reached back to events of the time of Mawla Ali, the same matter manifesting again in a later cycle to bring its account to settlement. Nor does this transfer pertain to evil deeds alone; the giving and taking across the eras is of good and of evil alike. A good deed, too, can come to a man's aid across the span of an age; one's good deed comes in handy, and the scale credits it no less faithfully than it debits the evil. The justice of Allah is operative over everything, and `fa-lā suqūṭa lahu ʿan shayʾin min ʿālam al-ṭabīʿah` — there is no exemption from it for anything whatsoever in the world of nature. Nothing in the world of nature can slip beyond its grasp. As you do, so shall you bear.

The text gathers up this universal sweep in a single sentence: `wa-idh kāna al-qawl kadhālika, fa-lā manāṣa li-aḥadin min al-khalāʾiq ʿan dhālika, sharīfihim wa-mashrūfihim wa-ʿazīzihim wa-dhalīlihim wa-rafīʿihim wa-waḍīʿihim` — and since the matter is so, there is no escape for anyone among created beings from this, neither for their noble nor their lowly, neither for their mighty nor their humble, neither for their high-ranking nor their base. The scale spares none on account of rank or station. And the reason is given immediately: `idh mīzān al-ʿadl qāʾimun, wa-laysa lillāhi Taʿālā wa-lā li-aḥadin min ʿibādihi muḥābātun fī fiʿlin yafʿaluhu, khayran kāna aw sharran` — for the scale of justice is standing and upright, and there is no favoritism (*muhabat*) belonging to Allah, exalted is He, nor to any of His servants, in any act that he performs, whether it be good or evil. The word *muhabat* means precisely this favoritism, this showing of partiality — and it is excluded absolutely. Neither Allah nor any servant of His shows favor in the matter of good and evil; the deed and its recompense are bound together by a justice that cannot be bent.

This is sealed by the word of the Quran: `kamā qāla Taʿālā fī muḥkam kitābihi: fa-man yaʿmal mithqāla dharratin khayran yarah, wa-man yaʿmal mithqāla dharratin sharran yarah` — as the Exalted has said in the decisive verses of His Book: "So whoever does an atom's weight of good shall see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil shall see it." Even the weight of a mote of dust, of good or of evil, is registered and returned. The text affirms that this verse falls upon all creation together — `wa-hādhihi al-āyah wāqiʿatun ʿalā al-khalq ajmaʿīn` — and that everything a person does of good or evil returns upon him as its recompense.

### The Difference Between Evil and Sin, and the Tenfold Reward

A careful question arises here, and the discourse takes it up directly. The verse speaks of an atom's weight of *khayr* (good) and an atom's weight of *sharr* (evil); it does not speak of *gunah* (sin). Are evil and sin then the same thing? The answer drawn out is that they are not. *Sharr*, evil, and *gunah*, sin, are distinct: sin is born from evil. As good is the soil from which good deeds grow, so evil is the soil from which sin springs; sin issues from evil as a particular fruit issues from a tree.

And the rates of recompense differ between the two columns of the scale. A good deed earns a reward multiplied tenfold; an evil deed earns a single recompense, one for one. The reward for good is tenfold, the requital for evil is onefold — yet, and this must not be lost, there is most certainly a recompense for both. Neither column goes unpaid. The difference lies in the severity and the multiplication of the return, not in whether a return is rendered at all. Both good and evil are infallibly recompensed; good is simply rewarded with a generous abundance that evil's bare single measure does not receive.

### Two Kinds of Sin: Against God and Against His Servants

This leads to one of the central rulings of the section, repeated and emphasized because so much turns upon it. There are two distinct kinds of sin, and they are forgiven in entirely different ways. The first is a sin against Allah Himself — a transgression of a command that Allah has given to be obeyed. This kind of sin Allah forgives through the servant's seeking of forgiveness (*istighfar*); repentance washes it away, and it is done. We say as much in our prayers, asking forgiveness of God for what we have failed Him in.

But the second kind is a sin against the servants of God — a wrong done to another human being, a right of His servants violated, a debt of retribution incurred toward a fellow creature. This kind Allah does not forgive through the wrongdoer's *istighfar* alone. It remains owing until one of two things occurs: either the wronged servant himself forgives the debt, or Allah, exalted is He, arranges some means by which the recompense reaches the wronged party in another way, through which the account may be discharged and the sinner's burden lifted. And if neither the servant's pardon nor a divinely arranged means brings the dues to their owner, then *qisas* — retribution — must take place. This is the very meaning of retribution as it operates between the servants: the rights owed among them cannot simply be cancelled by repentance to God, for they are not God's to remit; they belong to the wronged, and they must reach the wronged.

There is one explicit limit upon this. Acts done under the rulings of the sacred law (*ahkam al-shari'a*) do not carry this debt. If a man is lawfully commanded to be killed — sentenced by a valid order — and is then killed in execution of that order, no retribution becomes obligatory upon the one who carries it out. The shari'a's making the act lawful removes the debt that would otherwise attach to a killing. Where the law has declared an act permissible and it is performed accordingly, *qisas* does not follow.

### What the Lawful Transaction Removes From the Scale

The text now states the general rule of which this is an instance: `wa-kullu mā yafʿaluhu al-insān min khayrin aw sharrin ʿāʾidun ʿalayhi mukāfātan bihi illā mā aḥallathu al-sharīʿah wa-sallama mā ahlāluhā fī qiymatihi ʿan tarāḍin bihi wa-inqiyādin lahu, ka-mithl al-nikāḥ al-jārī ʿalā kitāb Allāh wa-sunnat rasūlihi, ṣalawāt Allāh ʿalayhi, fa-lā qiṣāṣa fīhi` — and everything that a person does of good or evil returns upon him as its recompense, except for that which the shari'a has made lawful and whose price it has caused to be delivered, by mutual consent (*tarad*) and submission (*inqiyad*) — like marriage conducted upon the Book of Allah and the practice of His Messenger, the blessings of Allah be upon him, in which there is no retribution.

The principle here is that a transaction freed of debt requires three things together: the act must be made lawful by the shari'a; its consideration or price must actually be delivered; and there must be genuine mutual consent and submission between the parties. Where all three hold, no *qisas* attaches. The teacher draws the comparison with ordinary lawful trade. When goods, houses, lands, garments, and the like change hands by mutual consent, through buying and selling for a known and agreed price, no retribution is reckoned in it — for it was done by willing agreement, not by theft and not by stealth, given and taken in open consent and obedience to the terms. Yet the lawfulness of the shari'a must accompany the mutual consent; consent alone is not enough. Adultery (*zina*), the teacher notes pointedly, can also occur by mutual consent — and yet retribution is most certainly reckoned in it, precisely because the shari'a has not made it lawful. The lawful marriage and the unlawful coupling may both involve consent; what distinguishes them, and what removes or imposes the debt of *qisas*, is whether the shari'a has sanctioned the act and whether its proper consideration has been rendered.

### Why the Imams' Lawful Actions Still Incurred Retribution

A profound difficulty now confronts the believer, and the discourse states it as the objection of a hypothetical questioner: `wa-laʿalla qāʾilan yaqūlu: fa-inna qatl al-maqāmāt li-man qatalūhu, salām Allāh ʿalayhim, wa-jald man jaladūhu wa-ḥasb man ḥasabūhu, bi-mā amara Allāh Taʿālā bihi` — and perhaps a sayer may say: the killing by the *maqamat*, the Imams, of those whom they killed, the peace of Allah be upon them, and their flogging of those whom they flogged, and their stoning of those whom they stoned, by that which Allah, exalted is He, commanded — these were acts the Imams performed under divine command. The objection presses: these actions were pronounced lawful by Allah's noble Book in many of its verses, were issued from His Messenger, and were enjoined by all the Imams of His religion — `man naṭaqa bihi kitābuhu al-karīm fī kathīrin min āyātihi, wa-ṣudūr anābiya min rasūlihi, wa-kāffat aʾimmat dīnihi` — so why, then, was retribution made obligatory in these acts, when it was dropped entirely from the case of marriage — `fa-lima wajaba fīhi al-qiṣāṣ, wa-usqiṭa ʿan al-nikāḥ` — given that He, exalted is He, leaves behind neither a small thing nor a great thing without enumerating it?

In marriage, the questioner reasons, the companionship that takes place occurs with mutual consent, and so no *qisas* attaches. Why should the lawfully commanded killings, floggings, and stonings of the Imams not be similarly exempt, since they too were lawful by divine command?

Before resolving the objection, the discourse pauses on the example of stoning for adultery, for it sharpens the same puzzle from another angle. The punishment of *zina* is that the offender is stoned — yet the wrong, if it was a wrong of two persons, was committed by two; how is it that the whole community stones the one offender? The answer given is that the stoning is carried out by the command of Allah, and it is commanded collectively because *zina* is an act from which collective harms are bred. Adultery is an act of just such a kind: from it nothing arises but shamelessness and a corruption that spreads through the whole body of the community. To remove those collective harms — so that such evils do not take root and multiply among the people — Allah has established this command, that the offense of two be answered by the hand of all. Although it is an act of two individuals, its punishment is made collective in order to lift the collective harm. And this, the text adds, was not a command peculiar to Islam: in every earlier religion — among the Jews, among the Christians, among all of them — stoning for adultery was likewise the command of Allah.

The discourse then turns to the deeper resolution of the objection, and it does so by way of a story preserved from Mawla Ali, upon him be peace, in the Battle of the Camel (*Jamal*). As Amir al-Mu'minin's armies were advancing toward the field, there was a pregnant woman sitting at her doorway. When the armies arrived and passed, the trampling and stamping of the horses' hooves so alarmed her that she went into labor and gave birth, and the child died. Afterward her tribe came to Amir al-Mu'minin demanding the blood-money (*diya*) for her loss. The matter was presented: this had occurred in the press of a confrontation — who, then, was to pay the blood-money? Amir al-Mu'minin ruled that it should be paid from the public treasury (*bayt al-mal*); the *diya* would be discharged from the common funds of the Muslims, since it was the galloping of the Muslims' horses that had caused the death, in which the woman herself bore no fault. Yet before settling it, he asked a question that seemed, on its face, beside the point: "Tell me first — did the mother die before the child, or the child before the mother?" The reason for this question belongs to the law of inheritance, and the discourse returns to it below; here it suffices to mark that Ali's lawful conduct of a just war nonetheless produced a death that had to be answered, and that even a perfectly lawful military advance generated a debt the just ruler took pains to discharge.

The resolution of the great objection is this. Retribution remained obligatory upon the Imams' lawful actions — the killing, the flogging, the stoning — precisely because, in those actions, there was no consent from the party against whom they were carried out. The Arabic states it directly: `wa-amara Allāh Taʿālā bihi fī hādhihi al-ashyāʾ allatī dhakarnāhā min qatlin wa-jaldin wa-ḥasbin, lammā lam yaqaʿ bihā riḍan min al-maqtūl wa-lā sullima lahu qīmatuhu fī dhālik, maʿa iḥlāl al-sharīʿah lahā` — and Allah, exalted is He, commanded it in these things we have mentioned, of killing and flogging and stoning, when there occurred in them no consent from the one killed, and his price was not delivered to him in that matter, together with the shari'a's making the act lawful. The criminal who is being stoned does not consent to his stoning; the man who is being killed has not agreed to his death and has not been paid the value of his life. The shari'a has indeed made the act lawful — and so the agent who performs it is righteous in performing it — yet because the one acted upon neither consented nor received any consideration, the debt of retribution is not extinguished. One cannot say to the offender, "I shall give you the value, and you let me stone you"; that exchange does not occur. The lawfulness of the act protects the doer from sin in doing it, but the absence of consent on the part of the one who suffers it means that the account between them remains open and must be settled — either it was already owed from before, or it falls due in time to come.

This is why the text concludes: `wa-li-dhālika waqaʿa ʿalayhim al-qiṣāṣ bi-qiṣāṣ al-maqāmāt` — and for this reason retribution fell upon them, by the retribution of the *maqamat*, the Imams. The Imams themselves did not escape this law; how then could an ordinary person hope to escape it? The discourse marks how delicate and exact this whole relation of *qisas* is, so fine that even the blink of an eyelid, the slightest involuntary motion, is itself a tiny settlement of an account. And what of the twig whose prick draws blood, or the obstacle that trips a foot? That twig, too, was a soul — a soul that had taken the form of a twig, and that had its own giving and taking to discharge. So minute and so universal is the reach of the scale.

### Afflictions as Pending Settlements

This brings the discourse to a saying of the Messenger of Allah, the blessings of Allah be upon him, which casts the smallest mishaps of daily life in the light of the scale: `mā yuṣību aḥadakum khadshat ʿūdin wa-lā ikhtilāj ʿirqin wa-lā nakbat qadamin illā bi-dhanbin, wa-mā yaʿfū Allāh ʿanhu akthar` — no scratch of a twig afflicts any one of you, nor the throbbing of a vein, nor the stumbling of a foot, except by a sin (*dhanb*); and what Allah forgives is far more than that.

The smallest hurts — the prick of a twig, the involuntary twitching of a vein, the stumble of a step — none of these befalls a person except in connection with some sin. There is a hidden bond between the injury and the transgression; the twig, the vein, the stumbling foot are the very instruments through which a prior debt is being quietly exacted. These afflictions, then, are nothing other than pending retributions being applied, the scale collecting in small coin what is owed. And yet the Prophet's saying ends with a vast mercy: what Allah forgives is far more. The sins that are committed and then forgiven outnumber by far the ones that are exacted through such small afflictions; the discharge through suffering is the exception, the forgiveness the rule.

### Unanswered Prayers and Unfulfilled Sacrifices Stored for the Hereafter

The question whether a debt incurred in the age of concealment is repaid within that age or only in the age of manifestation prompts the teacher to relate a treasured teaching on the fate of unanswered supplications, which belongs to the same economy of the scale: nothing offered to Allah is lost; what is not requited in this world is stored and multiplied for the next.

A human being, throughout his life, makes a great many supplications (*du'a*), and many of them are not answered in this world. On the Day of Judgment, when such a person is brought to his reckoning and good deeds are found placed in his scale that he does not recall performing, he will protest: "I did not do these good deeds — have they been entered in my scale by mistake?" And he will be told: "These are the supplications you made that were not answered in the world; this reward is the recompense for them." Hearing this, the man will wish that none of his supplications had ever been answered in the world at all — so that the entire reward for them might have been laid up for him on the Day of Judgment. The unanswered prayer, far from being lost, is the most profitable of all, its full reward preserved untouched until the day it is most needed.

The teacher recalls the high esteem in which this teaching was held by the blessed master Sayyidna, who so cherished the saying that he directed it be recorded in writing among the virtues of supplication, drawing upon the book of Sayyidna Ghulam Husayn Sahib. He remembers the master in his last days — the prayer-rug laid out, the call to prayer given, the cotton pillow set by his side — directing that the book be read to him, listening to four or five lines, and correcting a passage with his own pen, saying, "It is good that you have come." The same principle was applied to the sacrifice (*qurbani*): if a person offers a sacrifice with the intention that a sick person be healed, and that sick person nonetheless passes away, does the benefit of the sacrifice come to nothing? The answer recorded is that he most certainly receives its benefit; and if that benefit has not reached him in this cycle, then in the cycle to come he will be granted its reward multiplied. What is intended for God's sake is never wasted; if its requital does not arrive in this turn of the wheel, it arrives, and arrives increased, in the next.

### The Lawful Marriage and the Rights It Settles or Leaves Owing

Returning to the case of marriage, the text states precisely what a man's lawful discharge of his obligations accomplishes: `fa-li-yaʿlam anna taslīm al-rajul mā yuslimu lil-marʾah min ḥāḍirin wa-mahrin wa-qabḍin wa-tarāḍin baynahumā maʿa iḥlāl al-sharīʿah lahu, law qad amāta ʿanhu ḥaqq al-qiṣāṣ fī dhālik` — so let it be known that a man's delivering to the woman what he delivers of the present gift (*hadir*) and the dower (*mahr*), with the taking of possession (*qabd*) and the mutual consent between them, together with the shari'a's making it lawful for him, has indeed put to death and nullified the right of retribution in that matter. When the union is contracted with consent, with the lawful sanction of the shari'a, and with the actual delivery of the present gift and the dower into the woman's possession, the question of *qisas* simply does not arise; the right has been satisfied at the source, exactly as in a lawful sale concluded for a known price.

But the text is equally precise about the opposite case: `wa-law sallama alladhī sallama min ḥāḍirin wa-mahrin bi-ghayr iḥlāl al-sharīʿah lahu, la-lazima fīhi al-qiṣāṣ` — and were he to deliver what he delivers of the present gift and dower without the shari'a's making it lawful for him, retribution would be obligatory in it. The mere transfer of goods, absent the shari'a's sanction, does not purchase exemption. And further still: `wa-law waṭiʾahā maʿa taḥrīm al-sharīʿah min ghayr dhālika bi-ghayr an yusallima lahā shayʾan min ḥāḍirin wa-mahrin, la-wajaba fīhi al-qiṣāṣ` — and were he to have intercourse with her, with the shari'a's prohibition standing and without delivering to her anything of a present gift or dower, retribution would be obligatory in it. The senior elders state this plainly: there must be both the shari'a's permission and the actual delivery of the dower; where either is missing, the debt of *qisas* falls upon him. The teacher notes, with a touch of irony toward a lax custom, that some people in their final days have the dower forgiven by the wife — she says, "I have remitted the dower" — so that the obligation is discharged by her pardon rather than by payment; but the principle stands that an undelivered dower, absent her remission, leaves the right owing.

### The Master Who Served His Slave

It is in this context that the discourse states the doctrine that the same retribution which befell the Imams in their lawful actions may be exacted within the very same cycle or carried over from a previous one. The Arabic frames it: `kamā wajaba al-qiṣāṣ ʿalā al-maqāmāt fī afʿālihim hādhihi al-madhkūrah` — just as retribution became obligatory upon the *maqamat* in these aforementioned actions of theirs. It is not necessary that the deeds for which the Imams were recompensed were performed in the same cycle in which the recompense fell; the deed may belong to an earlier cycle and the *qisas* be taken in this one — as in the case of Mawla Ali's battles, whose recompense, it is related, Imam Husayn had to render. So the settlement may occur in the same age or reach back across the ages.

And here, lest it be thought that this giving and taking concerns only debts of evil, the teacher unfolds a luminous example of the same law operating in the register of good — the maxim `khadam al-mawlā ʿabdahu`, "the master served his slave." He recalls an account preserved of Sayyidna Badruddin, blessed and sanctified: one night a certain gardener had served the master, and when the master rose for the dawn prayer and found the gardener asleep, he himself began to press the man's feet. The gardener woke in alarm — "Master, you are pressing my feet!" — and the master answered, "You served me by night; I serve you by day." This is the outward shell of the maxim. Its inner chapter is this: the prophets of old served the Imams. The Imams were hidden, veiled in concealment, while the prophets and messengers served them — bearing hardships in their cause, flogged, slain, given poison, while the Imams remained hidden. Then, when the appointed time came — the time of the Prophet, upon him be peace — all the prophets were gathered there with him. And who then served, who stood as the deputy and requiter of the hidden ones? It was Mawla Ali Murtada, upon him be peace. He served the Prophet, and through him all the outstanding accounts were settled. These were recompenses long owed; for retribution, as the teacher insists, does not occur only in matters of evil — the giving and taking runs through good and evil alike, and Ali discharged every one of those debts. When the prophets, in their hour of difficulty, called out, it was upon Ali that they called.

So too the lawful violence of the Imams generates the same kind of unconsented debt that must be settled. The shari'a has made it lawful for them — and yet, in a battle, does the sword stay its edge? Sometimes the horse bolts and tramples a bystanding woman; one cannot always halt it. And so its recompense must be rendered, exactly as Amir al-Mu'minin rendered the blood-money from the public treasury for the woman who died at the door. The teacher adds a candid aside: the questioner who raised this whole difficulty was assured by Sayyidna that every such question is already answered in the books; it is only because we have not read them that the answers do not reach us and do not encompass us. With the matter resolved, the section on retribution closes upon the recitation of `Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm`.

### The Numerical Structure of the Word of Unity

The discourse now turns from the law of recompense to the inner architecture of the word of confession itself — `lā ilāha illallāh`, "there is no god but Allah" — and to the way its very letters and their numerical values encode the deepest realities. The word is examined under the categories of the negative and the positive, of letters that bear a dot (*nuqta*) and letters that bear none, and of the numbers three, four, seven, and twelve that emerge from its composition.

The dotted letters in question are identified: the `yā`, which carries dots; and the contrast between `ʿayn` and `ghayn`, of which one is dotted and one bare. A fine master, the teacher recalls, composed a verse upon the open mouth of the letter `ʿayn`:

`Wajh-i is ʿayn ki munh khulne ki tujh se keh dun, kufr-i kafir ko yeh chahta hai ke ek dam niglun` — "Let me tell you the reason for this `ʿayn`'s open mouth: it desires to swallow the disbelief of the disbeliever in a single gulp." The open shape of the `ʿayn` is read as a mouth poised to devour unbelief whole.

The numerical reckoning is then unfolded. The three are the three letters `ʿayn`, `lām`, `yā`. The four arise from `lām` and `yā` together: `lām` has the value thirty and `yā` the value ten, which sum to forty. The seven belongs to the `ʿayn` alone, whose value is seventy. And the twelve arises from a composition of the letters' own constituent letters — the `yā` and `nūn` of the spelled-out `ʿayn`, the `alif` and `mīm` of the spelled-out `lām`, and the `alif` of the spelled-out `yā`: five elements, which together with the seven yield twelve. So the single confession, read through its letters and their values, opens into the structured numbers three, forty, seventy, and twelve, combined in several ways.

### The Seventy Special Characteristics Gathered in Ali

The mention of the seventy associated with the `ʿayn` carries the discourse to a teaching preserved in the *majalis* — the assemblies of the masters, of which the teacher notes that the collection in hand begins at the seventy-sixth and seventy-seventh assembly, the earlier ones being sought from Yemen for publication. In one of these assemblies it is related that in the person of Janab-i Ali there were gathered seventy special characteristics (*khususiyat*) such as could not be combined in any other human being.

A special characteristic, in the precise sense intended, is a distinction to which both Allah and His Messenger have borne testimony; only such a witnessed distinction counts as a *khususiyya*. When the men of knowledge surveyed and tallied these characteristics, they found that in no single person could more than ten of them be combined — one man might possess two, another three, but ten was the utmost any one person could gather. In Ali, however, seventy were combined together. The matter was enumerated, and examples of these characteristics were given in proof.

Among the examples adduced is a celebrated sermon. People had come to Ali, and by chance they had been disputing among themselves which letter occurs most frequently in the Arabic language. All agreed it was the *alif*; and from this they concluded that no oration could be composed without the *alif*. Then the Master of Creation arrived and delivered, on the spot, a sermon of roughly three pages in which the letter *alif* does not occur even once. This was done extempore — and the teacher fixes the term: extempore is *fil-badih*, which in the Arabic of the science is called *murtajjal*, the unpremeditated improvisation of a finished oration. The sermon is recorded in the foremost book, and likewise in the *Nahj al-Balagha*, and in the present book as well. And there is a second of its kind: a sermon of roughly thirteen pages, also delivered extempore, in which not a single dotted letter appears.

To these is joined the testimony of Umar, who said, `Lawlā ʿAlī lahalaka ʿUmar` — "Were it not for Ali, Umar would have perished." When the occasions of this saying were compiled, it was found that he uttered it seventy times. Thus the number seventy, which the letter `ʿayn` carries in the word of unity, finds its echo in the seventy characteristics gathered in the bearer of that name and in the seventy confessions of his indispensability upon the tongue of Umar.

### The Reward That Returns to the Luminous Structures While the Retribution Remains

The discourse now opens the third section of the fourth chapter of the fourth pavilion, which returns to the actions of the Imams and the *hudud* with a crucial addition: that even as the debt of *qisas* stands, a great reward for those same actions accrues to them. The Arabic states it: `inna afʿāl al-maqāmāt wa-awliyāʾihim ʿalayhim al-salām allatī faʿalūhā min mujāhadat al-azdād wa-qatli man qutila minhum wa-ḥaddi man ḥaddawhu min al-umam wa-ḥasbi man wajaba ʿalayhim al-ḥasab... ʿāʾidun ajruhu ʿalā hayākilihim al-nūrāniyya wa-ʿalā nufūsihim` — indeed the actions of the Imams and their friends, peace be upon them, which they performed — of striving against the opposites (*azdad*), and killing of them whom they killed, and applying the *hadd* upon those of the communities upon whom they applied it, and stoning those upon whom stoning became obligatory — the reward thereof returns to their luminous structures (*hayakil nuraniyya*) and to their souls, and to the souls of those whom they sent forward for that from among their friends, polishing their essences (*mujawhirin li-dhawatihim*), elevating their degrees and their ranks in the Abode of Purity (*dar al-safa*) and the elevated dwelling.

The teaching to be grasped is twofold and held in a fine balance: the *qisas* remains fixed in its place, and yet the reward is also given. For acting upon the command of Allah, the command of the Messenger, and the command of those in authority over them (*Uli'l-amr*) — for the slayings they performed, the striving they undertook, the *hudud* they applied, the stonings they carried out at command — a reward is earned that does not lapse. The teacher recalls the wisdom of the command that, when an offender is to be put to death for fornication, a company of Muslims must be present to witness it: this was enjoined so that, seeing the punishment, no person in the future would commit the like. The one who carries out the killing kills, but he does not kill out of personal enmity (*tarad*); he kills by command — by the command of Allah, who created the man and who is the giver of his life. Both realities therefore stand together: the reward of the deed returns to him, and the retribution of the deed remains upon him. How delicate, the teacher marvels, is this relation of *qisas* — that the very same act, performed in obedience to the sacred law, is at once a meritorious deed whose reward returns (`ʿāʾidun ajruhu`) and a deed whose *qisas* is yet owed, whether to the people of that age or, in time to come, by a fresh reckoning before the *Mudabbir*, the Governor. The reward is immense precisely because the agent is executing a divine command and striving to purge impurity from the world; and the grammatical pronoun in `ʿāʾidun ajruhu` returns, the teacher is careful to specify, to the reward of the one who performed the deed — that is, to the Imam — its merit returning to his luminous structures and to the souls of the friends he sent forward in the work.

The principle that killing is no good thing in itself, and yet may be rewarded, is then made explicit: `li-anna tilka al-afʿāl allatī faʿalūhā ṭalaban li-thawāb Allāh Taʿālā wa-amrihi bi-dhālika tabāraka wa-taʿālā wa-iḥlāl al-sharīʿa lahu lam yakun ajruhu bi-ḍāʾiʿin bal ʿalayhi al-qiṣāṣ al-awfā` — because those deeds which they performed, seeking the reward of Allah, exalted is He, and by His command for that, blessed and exalted, and to establish the shari'a — their reward is not wasted; rather, upon them is the fullest requital (*al-qisas al-awfa*). To take a life is not in itself a good; yet when it is done in self-defense (*dafa'-i hifazat*), in the protection of one's own life — when a man comes to kill you and you kill him in defense of your soul — or when it is done by the command of Allah and His Messenger or those in authority, then its reward stands. And its *qisas* stands also. The two are not at war; the seeking of God's reward in the act preserves the reward from being lost, while the fullest requital is exacted all the same.

### Upon Whose Body the Retribution Falls, and Why

The text now specifies upon what, in the case of the opposites, the retribution falls: `wa-kāna qiṣāṣuhum ʿalā kathāʾifihim salām Allāh ʿalayhim bi-amr Allāh Taʿālā bihi wa-iḥlāl al-sharīʿa lahu li-karāhiyat nufūs al-azdād bimā fuʿila bihā wa-qillat raḍāʾihim bihi` — and their retribution falls upon their dense bodies (*katha'if*), by the command of Allah, exalted is He, and the shari'a's making it lawful for them, on account of the aversion (*karahiya*) of the souls of the opposites to what was done to them and the scarcity of their pleasure in it. The *qisas* settles upon the dense bodies of the opposites — upon the gross forms in which their progress has been arrested — and it does so by Allah's command and the shari'a's sanction. The ground assigned for this is the aversion of the opposites' very souls to what was done to them: they did not suffer it with consent or pleasure. They were attacked, they were driven out, an assault was made upon them — and so what is done in legitimate defense (*mudafa'at*) stands on one footing, while what is done by those who themselves muster an army and unleash bloodshed and strife stands on quite another. The distinction is between the one who repels aggression and the one who initiates it; the retribution upon the dense bodies of the opposites is bound up with the involuntary, unwilling character of what they endured.

### The Question of Inheritance and the Order of Death

The text grounds even this in the universality of the scale: `li-anna mīzān al-ʿadl qāʾimun min Allāh Taʿālā fī al-qiṭmīri wa al-naqīr` — because the scale of justice is established from Allah, exalted is He, even in the *qitmir* and the *naqir*. The *qitmir* is the thin membrane upon the date-stone; the *naqir* is the small groove or hollow in the stone. The scale's reach extends to the most minute matters imaginable, down to the membrane and the groove of a single date-stone — there is no recess of creation, however small, in which justice is not erected and operative.

It is in connection with the incident of the trampled woman, narrated above, that the discourse now unfolds the law of inheritance, for it was to settle the inheritance that Amir al-Mu'minin had asked whether the mother or the child died first. The first principle is that the law of inheritance does not arise during a person's lifetime; what a person gives while living is a gift (*hiba*), not an inheritance, and the question of inheritance arises only at the moment of death. Connected to this is the ruling on a gift made in terminal illness (*marad al-mawt*): such a gift has no legal consideration. Whatever a person wishes to give as a gift he must give before the onset of his final illness; a gift made during *marad al-mawt* is not given effect. And even a valid *hiba* carries a strict condition: possession (*qabd*) must actually be transferred to the recipient. If possession is not given — if the thing remains in the donor's own hand and he has merely written that it is a gift — then the gift is not accepted in law.

The order of heirs is then set out. The first heirs of a person are his parents, if they are living. Each parent receives a one-sixth share (*sudus*) — the mother a sixth, the father a sixth. After the parents come the husband and the wife, each with a right in the other's estate. The wife's share has two aspects: an eighth part (*thumun*) where there are children, and a fourth part (*rub'*) where there are none. The husband's share, correspondingly, is a fourth where there are children and a half — the larger portion — where there are none, the shares being adjusted according to the presence or absence of offspring.

Upon the wife's eighth-share the teacher recalls the famous retort of Janab Abdullah ibn Abbas: `Laka al-thumunu min al-ʿushri wa li-l-kulli tamallakti` — "Yours is an eighth of a tenth, yet of the whole you have taken possession." The word *thumun* means an eighth part. The occasion was the dispute over the burial of Imam Hasan, upon him be peace, beside the Messenger of Allah, when the Banu Umayya, at the instigation and command of Hakam ibn al-As, shot arrows to prevent the burial and declared that they would not permit Imam Hasan to be laid to rest beside the Prophet. To those who claimed the Prophet's house as their own, Ibn Abbas replied that the Prophet had at his passing ten wives, and the share of all ten wives together in the house was but a *thumun*, an eighth — "yours is an eighth of a tenth" — while the remainder was the right of Janab-i Fatima, passing to the children; "yet of the whole you have taken possession," he charged, "you have seized it by force."

The wise point that Mawla Ali drew from the order of death in inheritance is then unfolded in full, for it shows how everything turns upon the sequence. Consider the mother and her child. If the mother dies first, the child becomes her heir — and then, when the question turns to the child's own heirs, his first heirs are his parents. But the mother has already died during the child's lifetime, so she takes nothing of the child's estate; and the father, if he is present, inherits, though it is not always known whether the father survives. After the parents, the child's heirs are his brothers and sisters — and if the child had no children of his own and no wife, the whole of his estate passes to his brothers and sisters, and then to the paternal uncle and the paternal uncle's children. Reverse the order, and the result is wholly different: if the mother is living and the child dies first, the child takes nothing from the mother, and the mother's own relatives inherit — the mother's father and mother, and her husband if she has one, and her other children if she has any. So great a difference is wrought by the mere sequence of the two deaths; and it was precisely to determine this that Amir al-Mu'minin asked, in the case of the trampled woman, which of the two — mother or child — had died first.

### The Imams' Actions as Retribution for Past Cycles

A further question is now posed in the text: `wa-li-qāʾilin an yaqūl, fa-qad ʿalimnā anna kathīran min al-maqāmāt wa al-ḥudūd ʿalayhim al-salām jāhadū al-azdād wa-qatalū minhum man qatalū wa-ḥaddū man ḥaddū wa-ḥasabū man ḥasabū thumma intaqalū ilā dār karāmat Allāh Taʿālā min ghayri qatlin wa-lā sammin` — and a sayer may say: we have known that many of the Imams and the *hudud*, peace be upon them, strove against the opposites, killed of them whom they killed, applied the *hadd* upon whom they applied it, and stoned whom they stoned — and then passed away to the abode of Allah's generosity without being killed and without poison. If retribution attaches to such actions, why did these die peacefully, in their beds, untouched by the violent death their deeds seemed to require?

The answer is given: `fa-l-yaʿlam anna afʿāl tilka ṣalawāt Allāh ʿalayhim fī azdādihim wa-ʿalā adwārihim ikhtiṣāṣun minhum bi-ḥasbi mā taqaddama min afʿālihim fīhim fī adwār salafat wa-ʿuṣūr taqaddamat` — so let him know that the actions of those, the blessings of Allah be upon them, against their opposites and within their cycles, are a taking of retribution from those opposites according to what had preceded of the opposites' own actions against them in cycles that had passed and ages gone by. The Imams' actions against their opposites in the present cycle are themselves the exacting of *qisas* for deeds the opposites had committed in earlier cycles and former times. The reckoning runs backward across the ages: the opposites are now being requited for what they did long before.

The teacher pauses over the word *qisas* itself, tracing it to its root `qaṣṣa` — *qaf*, *sad*, *sad* — from which arise *iqtisas*, the taking of retribution; the verb *aqassa*, "he took retribution"; and, through the form *bab mufa'ala*, *muqasasa*, which denotes the mutual, reciprocal taking of retribution between two parties. The seventeen augmented forms (*abwab-i mazid*) of the Arabic verb each shift the meaning, turning a simple taking into a giving, a mutual exchange, a reciprocity; and *qisas*, in its fullest sense, is just such a mutual settling of accounts.

The reason this reckoning is gathered into the Imams' time is then stated: `fa-awjabat ʿināyat al-ilāhiyya ijtimāʿahum fī zamān hādhihi al-maqāmāt wa-al-ḥudūd li-yakūna minhum lahum al-qiṣāṣ ʿammā salafa ʿadlan min Allāh Taʿālā wa-iqāmatan li-mā faʿalūhu min al-maʿāṣī` — so the divine providence (*'inaya ilahiyya*) necessitated their gathering together in the time of these Imams and *hudud*, so that there might be exacted from them, for them, the retribution for what had passed — as justice from Allah, exalted is He, and as a requital establishing the account for the sins they had committed. The teacher notes the precise sense of *ijtima'*, gathering: from the root that gives *ajma'a*, "he gathered," and *ijma'*, the consensus, and the passive *ijtumi'a*, "they were gathered." Divine providence so arranges matters that the opposites of every age are gathered together into the very time of the Imams against whom their past offenses were committed, so that the long-deferred retribution may be exacted from them there — a perfect justice from Allah, and an establishment of the reckoning for the sins of cycles past.

This is sealed by a verse of the Holy Quran: `wa-subḥāna man lā yaʿzubu ʿanhu mithqālu dharratin illā aḥṣāhā, wa-wajadū mā ʿamilū ḥāḍiran, wa-lā yaẓlimu rabbuka aḥadā` — and glory be to Him from whom not an atom's weight is hidden but that He has enumerated it; and they found present what they had done; and your Lord wrongs no one. Not the weight of a mote escapes His enumeration; on the day of reckoning, men find laid before them, present and undeniable, the very deeds they performed; and in all of it the Lord wrongs no one, for the whole is exact justice.

### The Grammar of Ali and the Ranks of the Soul

The closing words of that verse furnish the occasion for one of the section's most luminous teachings, drawn again from Mawla Amir al-Mu'minin. The verse ends `wa-lā yaẓlimu rabbuka aḥadā` — "and your Lord wrongs no one" — and the teacher asks why *ahad* here takes the accusative form *ahada*, the *zabar*, while *rabbuka* takes the nominative, the *pesh*. The answer lies in a grammatical maxim that Mawla Ali himself composed, a maxim that is at once a rule of Arabic syntax and a parable of the soul's ascent.

`Kullu fāʿilin marfūʿun` — every doer (*fa'il*) is in the nominative case (*marfu'*), raised and elevated. In the verse, *rabbuka*, "your Lord," is the doer, the one who does not wrong; and being the doer, He is raised. `Kullu mafʿūlin manṣūbun` — every object (*maf'ul*) is in the accusative case (*mansub*); and *ahada*, the one wronged, is the object, and so it is given the accusative *nasb*, the *zabar*. `Wa-kullu majrūrin maksūrun` — and every object of a preposition (*majrur*), every word drawn along by a particle of pulling, is in the genitive case (*maksur*), broken, bearing the *kasra*.

The esoteric reading of this triple maxim is the heart of the teaching. *Kullu fa'ilin marfu'un* — the one who moves forward, who acts, who is the very first to act, is the one who is elevated and raised up. *Kullu maf'ulin mansubun* — the one who, on seeing the doer, strives to become like him, who imitates and follows the one who acts, is the object: he is *mansub*, supported, given a support upon him. *Wa-kullu majrurin maksurun* — the one who must be pulled, for whose sake effort must be expended to draw him toward the good, is the one who is *maksur*, broken, given a break. Thus the three cases of the grammarian become three ranks of souls: the one who acts of himself and is raised; the one who imitates the actor and is supported; and the one who must be dragged toward the good and is broken in the drawing. So the verse, read through Ali's grammar, both declares that the Lord wrongs no one and pictures the hierarchy of souls in their ascent. The lecture closes upon the verse once more — `wa-wajadū mā ʿamilū ḥāḍiran`, they found present what they had done — and upon the recitation of the Fatiha.

### Passing the Hand Over the Face and the Five Facing Five

The discourse opens the fourth section of the fourth chapter of the fourth path with a question of practice that carries the deepest cosmology: why do we pass our hand over our face when we recite `Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm`? The answer is found in the doctrine of connection (*ittisal*).

In the human body there are five higher limbs (*a'la*) and five lower limbs (*adna*). Among the lower, the right hand is the highest of all; and with this, the highest of the lower limbs, we make a connection (*ittisal*) with the highest part of the upper body — the face. Passing the right hand over the face joins the loftiest of the lower five to the loftiest region of the upper five, and so unites the body's two pentads into one. From this bodily gesture a meaning is drawn that reaches into the unseen: just as the five lower limbs are joined to the five higher in the body, so the five luminous essences (*zawat*) of the Luminous Realm (*'alam-e-nurani*) are perpetually joined to the five essences of the world below. The five of the Luminous Realm, named in the Quran's symbolism, are *'aql* (Intellect), *nafs* (Soul), *jadd* (Fortune), *fatah* (Opening), and *khayal* (Imagination); the five of this world are the Imam, the Bab, the Hujjat, the Da'i, and the Ma'dhun. The passing of the hand over the face at the Bismillah is a sign and enactment of the unbroken connection between these two ranks of five.

But why, the questioner presses, do we make this gesture only at the Bismillah and not at every utterance? The answer leads to a comparison of the two highest words. The word `Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm` is the highest of summaries, the very essence of all that is gathered into a brief formula. Yet higher still than the Bismillah is a word — `lā ilāha illallāh`, "there is no god but Allah," which is wholly without dots in the deepest sense intended. This word comprises twenty-eight letters, of which fifteen are dotted and thirteen are undotted; the dotted ones, when counted out, are `bā`, `tā`, `thā`, `jīm`, `khā`, `dhāl`, `zā`, `shīn`, `ḍād`, `ẓā`, `ghayn`, `fā`, `qāf`, `nūn`, and `yā` — fifteen in all, leaving thirteen without dots.

Within the Bismillah itself a further structure is disclosed: `khamsatun bi-izāʾi khamsah` — five set facing five. Five repeated letters stand in correspondence with five non-repeated letters, and the non-repeated are the more excellent. The non-repeated letters of the Bismillah — the *bā* of *Bism*, the *sīn* of *Bism*, the *hā* of *Allāh*, and the *nūn* of *al-Raḥmān*, with the *yā* of *al-Raḥīm* — each occurs but once, a single letter of distinction in its word, while the *alif*, the *lām*, the *mīm*, and the *nūn* recur many times across the formula. The five non-repeated letters correspond to the five individuals of the Luminous Realm — *'aql*, *nafs*, *jadd*, *fatah*, *khayal* — those who do not need to make an appearance (*zuhur*) in the world; and the five repeated letters correspond to the five who must appear in the world again and again. And just as in the Bismillah the repeated and the non-repeated letters are joined together to make one single word, one *kalima*, so the five of the Luminous Realm and the five of the physical realm are perpetually connected and form one single *kalima*.

The teaching is then gathered into a threefold correspondence. Just as in `Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm` five repeated and five non-repeated letters combine into one word; and just as in the human body five higher and five lower limbs combine into one body; so in the Spiritual Realm (*'alam-e-ruhani*) the luminous and the worldly individuals combine — all of them becoming one in that realm, one single *kalima*. The word of the Bismillah, the human body, and the Spiritual Realm thus stand in exact correspondence, three mirrors of a single pattern. It is for this reason, the teacher notes, that the esoteric interpretation (*ta'wil*) we possess begins with `lā ilāha illallāh` and only thereafter takes up `Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm` — as in the treatise of Sayyidna Mu'ayyad, whose *ta'wil* opens with the word of unity, because everything is contained and present within it.

### La ilaha illallah as the Light of the Word

This brings the discourse to the supreme origin of the revealed word. `Lā ilāha illallāh`, the teacher affirms, is from God Almighty Himself; it is the light of the word (*nur-e-kalima*). The Bismillah, by contrast, and all the verses of the noble Quran, descended from the Circle of the First Intellect (*da'irat 'aql-e-awwal*), the Circle of Origination (*da'irat ibda'*); from there they were revealed. But the entire source and wellspring of all of them is `lā ilāha illallāh`. The word of unity is not one revealed verse among others; it is the light from which the Bismillah and every Quranic verse take their descent, the first principle to which the whole of revelation traces back.

### Deeds Unsanctioned by Law That Darken the Soul

The fifth movement of the discourse returns from the heights of the word of unity to the dark consequence of unlawful action, and here the doctrine of the descending forms is unfolded. The text states: `wa-ammā sāʾir al-ashyāʾ allatī bihi tafʿal min ghayri an tuḥillahā al-sharīʿah, aw yaʾmura bihā aḥad al-maqāmāt aw baʿḍ ḥudūdihi, wa-mithla qatli mayit al-insān min abnāʾi jinsihi, wa-nahbi man yanhubuhu, wa-ghaḍbi man yaghḍibuhu, wa-akhdhi man yaʾkhudhuhu ẓulman, wa-yabtaghu ghashman, fa-inna jawhara nafsihi yuẓlimu muʿajjalan` — as for all the other things that a person does which the shari'a has not made lawful, nor has one of the Imams or any of his *hudud* commanded — such as the killing of a human being from among his own kind, and the looting (*nahb*) of whomever he loots, and the usurping (*ghadb*) of whomever he usurps, and the taking by oppression (*zulm*) of whatever he takes, and the seeking of gain through treachery (*ghashm*) — then the essence of his soul (*jawhar al-nafs*) is darkened swiftly.

These are deeds that no command of the Imam sanctioned and no ruling of the shari'a made lawful: a man kills one of his own kind, or plunders, or seizes by force, or takes by oppression, or wins by treachery. When a person commits such an act — neither obeying a station of authority nor following the law — the essence of his soul grows dark, and grows dark quickly. And the text adds: `wa-yakūnu tilka al-afʿāl ḥājizatan bayna nafsihi wa-bayna sabīl al-rushd` — and those actions become a barrier between his soul and the path of guidance (*sabil al-rushd*). The unlawful deed does not merely stain the soul; it erects a wall between the soul and the road of right guidance, so that the man no longer finds the opportunity to come to the path. The barrier shuts him out.

A question is raised here: when the Imam imposes a prescribed punishment (*hadd*) upon a man — for instance, putting him to death for a killing — is the man's entire sin thereby erased, or does some portion of it remain? The answer is that this depends upon the retribution of the *Mudabbir*, the Governor, and upon the states of the cycles (*adwar*) and the returns (*karrat*) — in which return, and in what manner, the man committed his deed. For in a single great cycle each Imam has three hundred and sixty returns (*karrat*); and what a man did in each return, and how his account stands with the *Mudabbir*, determines whether the sin is wholly erased or not. It may be erased; it may not. The matter is too fine to be settled by the outward *hadd* alone.

And here the saying of the Prophet, upon him be peace, is brought to bear: `mā aṣarra man istaghfara, wa-law kāna sabʿīna marrah` — he has not persisted in sin who seeks forgiveness (*istighfar*), even were it seventy times. The one who, after sinning, turns and seeks forgiveness is not counted among those who persist in sin; even if his lapse and his repentance recur seventy times, he is not of the persistent. The number seventy is named, the teacher explains, because seventy marks the limit, the point at which the matter is gathered up and included among the seventy names. Yet a crucial qualification is restated: this forgiveness through *istighfar* avails for sins incurred toward Allah, not for sins incurred toward His servants. As many sins as a person commits in relation to Allah, He forgives them upon the seeking of forgiveness; but the wrongs done to His servants Allah does not forgive until that servant forgives, or until such means are arranged that the expiation reaches the wronged party — as by the method of *Bitaq*, by which Allah causes the recompense to reach whomever it must reach, so that the sinner's account may be cleansed.

### The Soul Driven Into the Paths of Error

The text now describes, in a chain of vivid participles, the full ruin that the darkening of the soul's essence works upon it: `mukassifatun lahā, sāʾiqatun lahā ilā ṭuruq al-ghawāya, mūriṭatun lahā fī arbāq al-maʿāṣī, mūbiqatun lahā fī qabāʾiḥ al-aʿmāl, dāʿiyatun lahā ilā manāfir al-ḥudūd, wa-muqṣiyatun lahā ʿan al-ḥaqq, muwaqqiʿatun lahā fī nāri jahannam, allatī lā tarjiʿu lahā raḥmatun, mukhalladatun bi-ʿadhābin alīm` — those deeds eclipse the soul and drive it into the paths of error (*turuq al-ghawaya*); they ensnare it in the snares of sins; they destroy it in the foulnesses of evil deeds; they call it toward the hatred of the *hudud*; they thrust it far from the truth; they cast it down into the fire of Hell, for which no mercy returns, abiding there in a painful torment. The darkened soul is hurried along the roads of error, caught in the nets of sin, made to loathe the divine limits and the people of truth, driven from the truth, and flung at last into the fire of Jahannam.

Yet at the very edge of this terror the discourse marks a hidden mercy, drawn from the precise wording of the Quran. Of the disbelievers it is said: `lam yakuni alladhīna kafarū min ahl al-kitābi wa-al-mushrikīna fī nāri jahannama khālidīna fīhā` — those who disbelieved among the People of the Book and the polytheists are in the fire of Hell, abiding therein — `ulāʾika hum sharr al-bariyya`, those are the worst of creatures. The word *khalidina*, abiding, is used of them — but the word *abada*, "forever," is not. Whereas of the believers it is said: `wa-alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti... ulāʾika hum khayr al-bariyya`, and those who believed and did righteous deeds — those are the best of creatures — `jazāʾuhum ʿinda rabbihim jannātu ʿadnin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru khālidīna fīhā abadā`, their reward with their Lord is gardens of perpetual residence beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein forever. For the righteous, paradise and within it eternity (*abada*); for the disbelievers, the fire — but without the word "forever." This withholding of *abada* from the abode of the fire, while it is granted to the gardens of the blessed, is a mercy from Allah, a token that the abiding of the damned does not bear the same seal of unending permanence as the eternity of the saved.

The passage closes upon the affirmation that the darkened soul has no escape from the reckoning: `wa-lā manāṣa lahā baʿda dhālika min qiṣāṣin ʿalayhā wa-lā ʿalā jismihā jamīʿan` — and there is no escape for that soul, after all this, from the retribution upon it and upon its body entirely. The teacher seals the section with a prayer: may Allah protect us and deliver us from the paths of destruction, by the right of Sayyidna Muhammad and his pure progeny, the blessings of Allah be upon them all.

### The Descending Garments of the Soul

The fifth and final chapter of the fourth section turns to the precise mechanism by which the retribution for grave human sins is exacted — the doctrine of the descending garments or forms (*qumus*) through which the unredeemed soul passes. The text opens: `wa-kadhālika afʿāluhā al-qabīḥa mithla al-zinā, wa-mā shākalahu, fa-lā budda min al-qiṣāṣi ʿalayhi maʿa ẓulam jawhar al-nafs wa-takassufihā wa-inhidārihā ʿalā mā sabaqa min al-qawl` — and likewise its vile acts, such as adultery (*zina*) and what resembles it, then there is no escape from the retribution upon it (*la budda min al-qisas*), together with the darkening of the soul's essence, and its eclipse (*takassuf*), and its descent (*inhidar*), as was stated before. The phrase *la budda* the teacher glosses with care: *budda* means a way out, an escape; *la budda* means there is no way out — the retribution will surely come, inescapably. For vile acts such as adultery, *qisas* is unavoidable, and it is accompanied by the darkening of the soul's essence, its eclipse, and its downward descent through the forms.

The descent begins in the human form itself: `ḥattā yakūnu al-qiṣāṣu muʿajjalan fī Qamīs al-Riss alladhī huwa qamīs al-bashar al-munhadira` — to the extent that the retribution is hastened in the Garment of the Riss (*Qamis al-Riss*), which is the garment of the descending human. The descending forms are named in sequence — Riss, then Waqs, then 'Aks, then Khars — and the first, the Qamis al-Riss, is the human form, the descending human shape; in this very first stage much of the *qisas* for major human sins (*kabair*) is completed. Beyond the human form lie the further stages: the second is the form resembling the animal, the monkey-like; then a tearing, sea-dwelling form; then the plant; then, when the soul is stripped away, the mineral — for the soul must pass through seven stages in all in its descent.

The text continues: `wa-rubbamā baqiya minhu shayʾun fī Qamīs al-Waqs, wa-huwa al-tarkīb al-māthil lil-bashar alladhī huwa al-qirdu wa-al-nasnāsu wa-al-adaru wa-al-ʿadabu wa-ashbāhu dhālik` — and sometimes a portion of it remains for the Garment of the Waqs (*Qamis al-Waqs*), which is the composition resembling the human — namely the monkey (*qird*), the *nasnas*, the *adar*, the *'adab*, and their like. The remaining retribution for adultery passes into the Qamis al-Waqs, the human-like animal composition, because of a particular reason given below. The teacher describes the *nasnas* — not the bear but a manlike species — as `nasnās jinsun min al-khalq fī ʿarabi al-shimāl`, a species of creation in the regions of the north; `lahum khalqun mughayyar`, they have an altered form; `yuqālu inna wujūhahum fī ṣudūrihim`, it is said that their faces are upon their chests; and `wa-yuqālu inna al-wāḥida minhum yuthbatu ʿalā rijlin wāḥidah`, it is said that one of them stands upon a single leg. And in the state of copulation in which such creatures are, the retributions for adultery are exacted upon them.

The text proceeds: `thumma fī Qamīs al-ʿAks, wa-ʿalā qadri kathrati dhālika al-fiʿl min qillatihi` — then in the Garment of the 'Aks, and according to the measure of the abundance or scarcity of that act. The recompense a soul receives in these descending realms is proportioned to how much of the deed it did by its own choice: the more adultery a man committed, the more retribution he receives; the less he did, the less. The particle *min* here is one of comparison, weighing abundance against scarcity.

### The Distribution of Retribution Across the Forms

A further refinement is given for the case in which one woman is sinned with by many men: `li-annahu rubbamā yaʿbathu bil-marʾati qawmun kathīrun, fa-yakūnu al-qiṣāṣu minhum mutafarriqan fī Qumus al-inhidār` — because sometimes a large group of men has intercourse with a single woman, so that the retribution from them is distributed across the garments of descent (*qumus al-inhidar*). The *qisas* is not gathered into a single stage but scattered through several: `minhā shayʾun fī Qumus al-Riss, wa-shayʾun fī Qumus al-Waqs, wa-aktharu dhālika yakūnu fī Qumus al-Waqs` — some of it in the garments of the Riss, and some in the garments of the Waqs, and most of it in the garments of the Waqs.

The reason most of the retribution falls in the Waqs, the monkey-like form, is stated: `li-anna al-munākaḥah bil-qurūd wa-ṣifātihā aktharu min al-munākaḥah bi-sāʾir al-ḥayawān` — because copulation among monkeys, and its qualities, is greater than copulation among all other animals. The lustful quality is more abundant in them than in other beasts; and so the surplus of a much-repeated human sin descends into the form whose own nature most answers to it. The text continues: `wa-kadhālika fa-inna munākaḥat ahl Qumus al-Riss aktharu min munākaḥat ahl al-Qumus al-Sawiyya, allatī hiya fī al-ḥaqīqati qumus al-bashariyya` — and likewise, the copulation of the people of the garments of the Riss is greater than the copulation of the people of the upright garments (*Qumus al-Sawiyya*), which are in reality the garments of humanity. Those who have already begun the descent into the Riss are more inclined to the act than those still in the upright human form; the descended state intensifies the very disposition that brought about the descent. And whatever retribution remained beyond this, `thumma mā baqiya min al-qiṣāṣi fī dhālika kāna fī Qumus al-ʿAks` — then what remained of the retribution in that regard was in the garments of the 'Aks — and likewise in the Khars (*al-khars*) and in all the descending animals. When the soul, in the human form, had the power of choice and performed such deeds, the effect of those deeds remains upon it through every one of the descending garments it enters.

### The Impermanence of the Garments and the Thousand-Year Stature

The teacher now sets all these descending forms against the one form that possesses a kind of permanence. These other forms, he explains, are temporary: a human soul enters into them, passes through the garments, and then returns; they are not its abiding state. Only the Thousand-Year Stature (*Qamat-e-Alfiyyah*) possesses a permanence — and even this is a contingent permanence, a blessing granted to every person for a limited time, after which the soul departs and returns to it, sometimes again, sometimes not, though return it must.

Why is this form called the Thousand-Year Stature, and in what sense is it primary? Because the luminous forms (*nurani zawat*) that were created in the unseen were created upon the pattern of the Qamat-e-Alfiyyah. The question is posed that at the time of their creation the Qamat-e-Alfiyyah did not yet exist — how, then, could they have been created upon it? The answer is that the luminous essences created in that realm were themselves upon the form of the Qamat-e-Alfiyyah, and that when Allah willed to create the Qamat-e-Alfiyyah in the world, He created it in accordance with that prior luminous form. So in truth the Qamat-e-Alfiyyah came first — first there in the realm of the luminous essences, and first here in the physical world as well. The degraded forms — the human, the animal, the mineral — are all a later matter, a subsequent thing. This whole world of density and atoms is the degraded world, and the descent through it proceeds by stripping away, one by one, the powers of the form: in the animal realm one faculty is taken; in the next, a second, a third, a fourth; in the plant stage only growth remains, sensation having been all but withdrawn; in the mineral, even that is finished; in the Khars, sensation is taken away. Stage by stage, the density and the atoms are removed and the powers stripped, until the form that was created whole upon the Thousand-Year Stature is reduced to the barest residue.

### Why Retribution Applies Despite Consent in the Descending Forms

A final and subtle difficulty is resolved. In the descending animal forms, the acts of copulation occur with consent and even with the payment of a price — so why should retribution still apply there? The text answers: `wa-innamā waqaʿa al-qiṣāṣu fī dhālika wa-al-ʿadhābu ʿalayhi maʿa mā annahu min marāḍāt wa-taslīm qīmatihi, li-kawnih waqaʿa bi-ghayr taḥlīl al-sharīʿa lahu, fa-kāna dhālika kadhālik` — and the retribution and the punishment occurred for that despite its being with mutual consent (*marada*) and with the delivery of its price (*taslim qima*), because it occurred without the shari'a's making it lawful; so the matter was thus.

The animals, the teacher explains, have consent in their unions — and the soul that has descended into them had, in its human life, performed the original act with consent and with payment, just as a man pays a prostitute and the act is done by mutual agreement. Yet because that original human act was performed without the shari'a's permission, the retribution and the punishment follow the soul into the descending forms regardless of the consent that attends the act there. The lawfulness that would have removed the debt was absent at the root; and so, exactly as in the human realm of choice the unsanctioned act incurred its penalty, in the animal realm the penalty is exacted still — though the form of the punishment becomes somewhat different in the descended state.

The chapter — and the section — closes with a final distinction concerning who descends into which order of lower states. The descending garments here described (the *darakat*) pertain to adultery and its likes — and the text is explicit that the doctrine of these descending forms has been expounded specifically with regard to *zina* and what resembles it: `al-zinā wa-mā shākalahu`, adultery and the acts similar to it, such as the usurping of another's wealth, theft, and robbery. The opponents of the Imam (*azdad*), however, do not descend into these descending garments (*darakat*) at all; they descend rather into the lower levels (*adrak*), a different and deeper order of degradation. Thus the *darakat* of the descending forms belong to the carnal and acquisitive sins of ordinary men, while the adversaries of the Imam are consigned to the *adrak* below them. With this the lecture closes upon the recitation of `Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm` and the prayer upon Muhammad and his progeny.
