## The Opening Covenant and the Science of Realities

### A Trust Bequeathed: The Nature and Provenance of These Lessons

`A'udhu Billahi min al-Shaytan al-Rajim. Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim. Wa sallallahu 'ala rasulihi wa alihi al-tayyibin al-tahirin wa sallama 'alayhim ajma'in.` (I seek refuge in Allah from the accursed Satan. In the name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful. And may Allah's blessings be upon His Messenger and his pure and purified family, and may peace be upon them all.)

Believing brothers and sisters, the lessons gathered in the book *Anwaar al-Latifah* are composed of the lofty contents of the sciences of realities — the *'uloom-e-haqa'iq*. These are not lessons drawn from speculation or the ordinary disciplines of exoteric learning. They have been interpreted and unfolded entirely upon the foundation of the sciences of the prophets, the *anbiya'*, and of the saints, the *awliya'*. What is offered here, therefore, is a transmission of inherited wisdom — knowledge whose source lies in the teaching of those whom Allah has chosen as the bearers of His most intimate truths. Every realization expounded in these pages traces itself back to that prophetic and saintly fountainhead, and it is in that light that the reader must approach what follows.

These lessons were not recorded by private initiative, nor were they ever intended to be common property. I recorded them with the explicit permission — the *ijazat* — of Maulaya, the noble and esteemed one to whom this knowledge is ascribed, `'awliy al-ne'mati al-mansub al-karim wal-faqeem`, Hadrat 'Abdullah Sahib, `'atala Allahu baqa'ahu wa ja'alna fida'` (may Allah prolong his life and make us his ransom). I undertook this recording originally for the benefit of my father and the other members of my family, that they might partake of these realities. Afterward, by the express command of my Aaqa and Maula, this recording has been deposited in the treasury of the *Da'wat Sharief* — entrusted, in other words, to the safekeeping of the sacred mission itself. The authority behind these lessons is thus twofold and unambiguous: the permission of Hadrat 'Abdullah Sahib that authorized their recording, and the command of the Aaqa that consigned them to the *Da'wat Sharief*'s treasury. They are a sanctioned trust, not a casual production.

### The Covenant of Safekeeping and Its Sanctions

Because these lessons are a trust, their custody carries obligations that must be stated plainly at the outset. I most humbly request — *istida'* — that you do not email these lessons, nor attempt to exchange them in any manner upon the internet, nor store them upon your computer. The reasoning is not arbitrary caution. However secure an exchange may appear, once a thing has been placed upon the web there remains always the possibility that it becomes accessible to everyone, and that these lessons fall thereby into the hands of those who do not deserve them — the *ghair-mustahiqqeen*, the undeserving. The sciences of realities are not fit for indiscriminate circulation; they are meant for those prepared to receive them, and the careless dispersal of them does violence to their very nature.

Let the consequence of disregarding this be understood with full seriousness. If anyone, despite this request, should commit such an act — copying these lessons, listening to them and passing them onward, transmitting them, handing them to another, modifying them, or performing any such deed (*fe'l*) with them without permission — then know that this is a sin (*gunah*) in the sight of Allah and His Messenger. On the Day of Judgment, *baroz-e-qiyamat*, before Allah and His Messenger, the one who does this will stand solely responsible for it, and I shall be free of any responsibility for the act. The burden falls upon the doer alone; the chain of accountability rests on the hand that breaks the trust, not on the one who faithfully delivered it.

Nor is this a warning issued lightly or for mere effect. The *Awliya'* of Allah themselves have given news of a most severe punishment — *'azab* — reserved for those who fail to safeguard these lofty sciences, these *'uloom-e-'aliya*. They have specified it as the punishment of cold iron, which is described as the most severe and terrible of chastisements. `A'adhana Allah` — may Allah protect us from it. The safeguarding of this knowledge is therefore not a matter of etiquette but of consequence reaching into the next world. May Allah prolong the shadow of Sayyidna al-Da'i and of the Manasib-e-Kiram over us, and make their lights — their *anwaar* — flow continually upon us. Ameen. `Bi-haqqi Sayyidina Maulana Muhammadin wa 'ala aali Sayyidina Maulana Muhammadin wa barik wa sallim.` (By the right of our master, Maulana Muhammad, and upon the family of our master, Maulana Muhammad, and grant blessings and peace.)

### The Architecture of the Book and Its Stated Purpose

The book itself is structured according to a deliberate and symmetrical design, and the very title of its parts conveys something of its character. Each major division is called a *Suradiq* — and we open now with `Al-Suradiq al-Awwal`, the First Suradiq, from *Kitab al-Anwar*. The word *Suradiq* (also rendered *Sardaq*) carries the sense of a curtain, and beyond that of a palace, a grand encampment, a great tent — a *shamiyana* or *khaima*. It is the image of an enclosing canopy or a royal pavilion, within which the matters of the book unfold. The architecture is built upon the number five throughout. The whole book comprises five Suradiqs. Each Suradiq, as the text states, has `khamsatu abwab` — five chapters, five *babs*. And each *bab* in turn `yujma'u khamsata fusul` — it gathers within it five sections, five *fasls*. Five Suradiqs, each of five chapters, each chapter of five sections: by this symmetry the whole work amounts to one hundred and twenty-five sections in all. With this structure fixed in mind, we turn to `Al-faslu al-awwalu minha` — the very first section of the first chapter of the first Suradiq.

The opening section announces the purpose of the entire enterprise. The text begins: `Naqulu wa lamma kana ma huwa al-maqsudu wa al-kalamu 'ala al-mawaddati al-sharifati al-mu'addati ila al-wajibat al-sharifah haqiqatan mahdatan` (We say: and since that which is intended, and the discourse here, concerns the noble affections that lead to the noble obligations, taken as a pure reality), `la ta'weela fiha wa la ramza minha` (in which there is no interpretation and no symbol). The author's aim is declared at once: to treat of the noble affections — the *mawaddah* — that conduct the soul toward the noble obligations, and to treat of them as a pure reality, *haqiqatan mahdatan*. This phrase carries great weight. The book does not propose to expound the *zahir*, the outer exoteric meaning, nor to ascend into *ta'weel*, the esoteric interpretation; it proposes rather to set down the pure, unmixed reality of these matters directly.

The negations that follow are emphatic, and the grammar itself underscores them. `La ta'weela` is read with what the grammarians call *nafi al-jins*, the negation of the genus — that is, an absolute, categorical denial. Were one to say merely `la ta'weelun fihi`, it would mean only "there is no particular interpretation in it"; but `la ta'weela` means there is no interpretation whatsoever in it, none of the genus at all. So too with what follows: `wa la ramza minha` — and no symbol (*ramz*) from it — and no *ishara*, no allusion. The intention is therefore pure reality, stripped of veiling interpretation, symbol, and allusion alike. This is the declared method of the book.

### The Path to Tawhid: Gnosis, Love, and Action

The discourse next widens to embrace the whole of existence. The text continues: `Thumma al-kalamu 'ala kaffati al-mawjudat` — then the discourse is upon all existing things. The book proposes to address all the existents that have appeared before our own existence, to take them as its subject. And the reason it does so is given immediately: `idh bi-ma'rifati dhalika wa al-ittila'i 'alayhi wa ta'ati awliya' Allahi wa muhabbatihim wa al-taqarrubi ila Allahi bihim wa bi-luzumi hududihim al-mansubina min qibali al-qa'im al-'ilmi wa al-'amali bima warada min al-shara'i' allati la yutada ila tawhidi Allahi ta'ala wa takabbar` (for by the gnosis of that, and the acquaintance with it, and obedience to the Friends of Allah and love for them, and drawing near to Allah through them, and adhering to their appointed Hudud from the direction of the Qa'im, with the knowledge and the action that have come through the religious laws — by all this one is guided to the Oneness of Allah, the Exalted and the Supreme).

The teaching here is the heart of the matter, and it must be grasped in its full articulation. It is through the *ma'rifah*, the gnosis, of all those existing things which came into being before us — through truly knowing them and gaining acquaintance with them — that the soul is set upon the road. And it is through obedience to the *Awliya' Allah*, the Friends of Allah, and through love of them, and through drawing near to Allah by means of them, that the road is traveled. Crucial to this is adherence to their *hudud* — the ranks and bounds appointed for the believer — for these *hudud* are *mansubin*, that is, appointed and assigned, from the direction of the Qa'im himself. And it is through the action, the *'amal*, that is laid down in the *shari'ah*, the religious law, that the road is completed. By the whole of this — gnosis, obedience, love, nearness, adherence to the appointed *hudud*, and lawful action — guidance is given toward the *tawhid* of Allah, the Most High. This is the only path by which one may reach the *tawhid* of Allah.

It is worth distilling the two great pillars upon which this access rests, for they recur throughout the doctrine. Access to the *tawhid* of Allah is attained through two things and two things only: *wilayah* and *'amal* — allegiance and action. *Wilayah* is here named also as *muwalat* (loyal allegiance) and *muhabbah* (love); it is the bond of devotion to the Friends of Allah. And *'amal* is the deed, the action laid down by the law. By these two together — by loving allegiance and by righteous action — one attains access to the *tawhid* of Allah. There is no other way through. And the fruit of this attainment is twofold, as the text declares: `wa bi-dhalika ta'lu darajatu al-ma'ad` (and by that, the ranks of the afterlife are elevated), `wa yakunu al-khalasu min hadha al-'alami al-kawni wa al-fasad` (and salvation comes from this world of being and corruption). By treading this path the ranks of the *ma'ad*, the afterlife, are raised up for the soul, and salvation is achieved from this lower world of being and corruption — this realm of generation and decay in which we are presently confined.

### The Original Homeland and the Return to the Origin

This salvation is, in its deepest meaning, a homecoming. The text continues: `wa yumkinu al-qawdu ila al-mabda'i alladhi minhu al-ibtida'atu al-sharifah` (and it becomes possible to return to the origin from which the noble beginnings came), `wa al-kawnu ma'ahum fi dar al-quds` (and to dwell with them in the Abode of Sanctity). Here the doctrine of return is set forth. *Qawd* signifies the return, the leading-back, toward the *mabda'*, the origin — back to that source from which the noble beginnings (*ibtida'at*) first issued. The soul does not belong to this lower world; this is not its native country. Our original homeland — our *watan-e-asli* — is the world of spirits, the *'alam al-ruh*. That is the land from which we came, and to which the whole movement of the religious life is directed as a return. The goal is to make our way back to that origin, and there `wa al-kawnu ma'ahum fi dar al-quds` — to dwell together with the Friends of Allah in *dar al-quds*, the Abode of Sanctity. As the text prays: `ballaghana Allahu ilayhim` (may Allah cause us to reach them), `wa a'anana 'ala al-irtiqai wa al-wusuli ilayhim bi-haqqi Muhammadin wa alihi al-tahirin, salawatullahi 'alayhim ajma'in` (and may He aid us in the ascent and the arrival to them, by the right of Muhammad and his pure progeny, the blessings of Allah be upon them all).

The text then characterizes this knowledge of the origin in a striking image: `wa hadha huwa al-'ilmu alladhi yaduruna 'alayhi wa yajuruna ladayhi` (and this is that knowledge around which they revolve and toward which they incline). This is the knowledge that all creatures circle around. Every person, every creature, longs to move toward it and to attain it; it is the knowledge of the *asl*, the root, the knowledge of the *mabda'*, the origin — the answer to the question of where we came from and what our beginning was. All the creatures, the *khala'iq*, are circling about this knowledge, making their rounds around it, yearning to know where their origin lies. And yet, for all their circling, they cannot reach it. The text states it without softening: `wa la sabila li-ahadin minhum ilayhi` (and there is no path for any of them to it). For the generality of creatures there is no path at all to this knowledge; they revolve forever about a center they cannot attain.

There is, however, one exception, and it is decisive. `Illa al-qalil` — except for a few. `Mimman i'tasama bi-al-a'immati min al-i Muhammad, salamu Allahi 'alayhim ajma'in, wa iltazama hududahum al-mayamin` (from among those who held fast to the Imams from the progeny of Muhammad, the peace of Allah be upon them all, and bound themselves to their blessed Hudud). The few who attain to the knowledge of the origin are precisely those who held fast to the *a'imma*, the Imams from the household of Muhammad, and who committed themselves to the true and blessed *hudud* of those Imams. Only these attain that side; only these break out of the circle. The rest revolve endlessly around a goal they cannot reach. The lesson is plain: the knowledge of the origin is not available to bare seeking, however intense; it is granted only through fast adherence to the Imams and submission to their appointed ranks.

### The Transcendence of the Originator Beyond Indication and Thought

We move now to the second section of the first chapter of the first Suradiq — `al-faslu al-thani min al-babi al-awwali min al-suradiqi al-awwal` — which turns to the nature of the One toward whom all this is directed. The text opens: `wa kana la dalila 'ala dhikri dhalika illa bi-dhikri Allahi ta'ala wa takabbar alladhi bi-dhikrihi fi kulli shay'in yustaftahu, wa bi-asma'ihi al-husna yutawassalu ilayhi fi kulli matlubin wa yustanjahu` (and there was no guide to the mention of that except by the mention of Allah, the Exalted and Supreme, by whose mention every thing is opened, and by whose most beautiful names a means is sought to Him in every request, and success is attained). There is no path to the mention of these realities except by first mentioning Allah; without His mention nothing can be opened. The verb `yustaftahu` — "it is opened," from the root *fataha* — belongs to the form of *istif'al*, the form of seeking; *istiftah*, also called *iftitah*, means to seek the opening, to make the request that a thing be begun. By the mention of Allah, every thing is unlocked and the opening is sought. And `wa bi-asma'ihi al-husna` — by His most beautiful names, the *asma' al-husna* — `yutawassalu ilayhi`, a means (*tawassul*) is taken to Him, from the root *wasala*; `fi kulli matlubin`, in every object of seeking (*matlub*, that which is sought, as against *talib*, the one who seeks); `wa yustanjahu`, and success (from *najaha*, *istinjah*) is attained. Through His names, in every request, success is granted.

The text now states the cornerstone of right belief concerning the divine nature: `wa kana musabbahan muta'aliyan 'an an yaqa'a 'alayhi isharatu mushirin wa yatajasaru nahwahu khatiru khatirin bi-tawahhumin aw tafkirin` (and He is the One glorified, exalted far above that any indicator's indication should fall upon Him, or that the thought of any thinker should dare toward Him by imagination or by contemplation). He is *musabbah* — the One whose glorification (*tasbih*) is performed; not *musabbih*, for it is we who are the glorifiers, never He. And He is *muta'ali*, the One exalted on high, from the root *'uluww*. He is so exalted that no *ishara* — no indication of any indicator — can possibly fall upon Him so as to point toward Him. This, the text insists, is the true statement of the matter; it is the very foundation of beliefs, the basis upon which correct doctrine stands: that no indication can reach Him. Nor can any thought dare to cross over toward Him. The verb *yatajasaru*, from *jisr* (a bridge), conveys the image of a bridge that cannot be crossed: the *khatir*, the thought of the heart, cannot bridge the gap toward Him, neither by *tawahhum* (imagination) nor by *tafkir* (contemplation). No imagining and no reasoning achieves the objective. We can know only that, since we were created, there is One who created us; but what He is in Himself, what His nature is, of this we know nothing whatever.

This truth is illuminated by a celebrated exchange from the time of Imam Ali. A man named Ibn al-Kawwa', intending to corner the Commander of the Faithful with a question he could not answer, came and asked him: `Hal ra'ayta Allah?` — "Have you seen your God? Have you seen Allah?" Imam Ali replied, "Woe to you — could I worship a God I had not seen? How could I worship a God without having seen Him?" Then the man pressed: "How, then, did you see Him?" And the Imam answered: "I did not see Him with the eyes; I saw Him with the *basirah* — with insight." It is a mighty saying of our Mawla. The vision of Allah is not the vision of the physical eye, which cannot fall upon Him any more than indication or thought can; it is the vision of insight, the inner perception of the heart that affirms Him without grasping His essence.

### Names and Attributes Negated from the Originator

From this transcendence a profound consequence follows concerning the divine names and attributes. The text declares: `wa kana al-i'rabu 'anhu bi-Allahi ta'ala wa bi-al-mubdi'i bi-al-huwiyyati al-muta'aliyah ghayra waqi'in 'alayhi hadhani al-ismani wa al-sifatu` (and the expression about Him by "Allah, the Exalted," and by "the Originator," with the exalted Ipseity — these two names and the attribute do not fall upon Him). When we express something about Him by calling Him "Allah Ta'ala," or by calling Him *Mubdi'*, the Originator, or by referring to Him by the exalted *huwiyyah* — the Ipseity, the bare "He" (*huwa*) — none of these names, and no attribute, actually falls upon Him. Neither name nor attribute lights upon Him.

The reason is stated with rigor: `idh al-asma'u wa al-sifatu la taqa'u illa 'ala misliha` (for names and attributes do not fall except upon their like). A name or an attribute can be applied only to something of its own kind, something like itself. Consider what we do when we name Him: we say "Allah" in order to recognize Him, wishing to know Him, and so we assign Him a name — and we do so from our own tongue. But you are yourself the creator of that tongue and of those names; the very instrument by which you interpret Allah is a thing of your own making. As the Commander of the Faithful said, `al-asma'u wa al-sifatu dalalatun 'ala al-musamma` — names and attributes are indicators pointing to the named, and they point only to their like. We observed mercy in some creature and so applied "Merciful" to that one; the attribute fell upon its like, upon a knowledge and an arrangement (`'ala al-'ilmi wa al-tadbir`) of the kind we could perceive.

But these names and attributes are themselves *muhdathah* — created, originated things. The distinction must be held precisely: *muhdith* is the one who originates, while *muhdath* is the thing originated. The names and attributes are created; they are not their own creators. And they are created specifically by human beings: it is a human who pronounces a name, a human who assigns it. You named the child; you are the one who gave the name. They are, then, our given names — *muhdathah*, brought into being by us. And here is the crux: `wa al-muhdathu la yaqa'u 'ala al-muhdithi misluhu` (and the originated thing does not fall upon its originator as its like). A created thing cannot fall upon its creator in a manner befitting an equal; the made cannot characterize its maker as though they were of one kind. As Sayyidna Mu'ayyad al-Din said — and he lived in the time of Mawlana Mustansir Billah, holding the rank of *Bab al-Abwab* — `wa lamma kanati al-hurufu al-mu'jamu muhdathatan` (and since the letters of the alphabet are originated things), they cannot serve as a true indicator of the very Originator who made them. `Lam takun tadullu 'ala al-mabda'i al-sani'i laha dhalalatan muhaqqiqatan musaddiqatan 'ala misliha` (they could not indicate the originating Maker that made them with a true and verifying indication upon His like).

A natural objection arises here, and the text meets it. One might protest: it is a fine argument that we are the creators of our words, yet there is a limitation in us as well; many things proceed by His command before any word issues from us, so are we truly the creators of these things? The answer holds firmly to the point at issue. The words I am speaking — these I am indeed creating; of these spoken words I am the maker. The argument rests on the faculty of choice and creation that we do possess: with our own creation — the tongue and the names that are our handiwork — we are attempting to characterize our own Creator, and that is precisely what cannot be done. The choice we have is real, but it does not extend to recognizing Him; we have no power of choice by which to know Him. He dwells in such a *ghayb*, such an unseen, that we cannot lift its curtains. We may say only this much: in order to establish our own existence, we affirm that there must be an Originator for us. Who created us, what He is — this remains beyond.

### The First Intellect as the Locus of Names and Attributes

If the names and attributes do not fall upon Allah, the question presses: upon what do they fall? The text answers: `wa idh kanat al-qaulun kadhalika kanat hadhihi al-asma'u wa al-sifatu manfiyyatan 'an Allahi ta'ala wa takabbara wa kanat waqi'atan 'ala ibda'ihi alladhi huwa al-'aqlu al-awwal` (and since the matter is thus, these names and attributes are negated from Allah, the Exalted and Supreme, and they fall instead upon His origination, which is the First Intellect). All these names and attributes are negated, *manfiyyah*, from Allah Ta'ala. They are not predicated of Him at all. Rather they fall upon His *ibda'*, His origination — and what is that origination? It is the *'aql al-awwal*, the First Intellect. It is also named the *mawjud al-awwal*, the first existent, and the *sadir al-awwal*, the first emanation. Whatever names and attributes there are, they are negated from Allah and they descend upon this first origination, this First Intellect that is at once the first existent and the first to issue forth.

The text presses the point further: `wa ma zahara min fi'lihi fi ikhtira'ihi, la min jihati ismin wa la sifatin yakhtari'ani 'alayhi, li-anna dhalika maslubun 'anhu, waqi'un 'ala ibda'ihi` (and what appeared from His act in His creation — not from the aspect of any name or attribute that would be applied specifically to Him — for that is stripped from Him, and falls rather upon His origination). What we can speak of is only what appeared from His act in His creation, not any name or attribute applied to Him directly; for all such predication is *maslub*, stripped away, severed from Him. The names and attributes we apply fall upon that which He created — His *ibda'* — and even there the matter is subtle, for if these names and attributes do not even fall fully upon His origination, how then could they ever fall upon Allah, the *Mubdi'* Most High?

The teaching now reaches its widest sweep, embracing the whole of creation in a common incapacity: `wa al-'ijzu shamilun li-jami'i 'alam al-'aql wa 'alam al-hiss` (and inadequacy is comprehensive over the entire world of intellect and the world of sense). *'Ijz* — inadequacy, the inability to know the Originator — is comprehensive; it covers all. It embraces the world of intellect, the *'alam al-'aql*, and the world of sense, the *'alam al-hiss*, together. The First Intellect itself, the highest of created things, shares in this incapacity, and so do we; we are all equal in it. As the text states: `yatasawa fi dhalika al-qadiru wa al-maqdur wa al-mahsusu wa al-ma'qul min al-'alamayni jami'an` (in that they are equal — the powerful and the overpowered, the sensed and the intelligible, from both worlds together). The verb *yatasawa*, from *sawa* (equal), in the form that carries the sense of mutuality, declares them all equal to one another in this single respect. The powerful and the powerless, the sensed and the intelligible, the world of intellect and the world of body — all stand on perfectly level ground before the unknowability of the Originator.

### "Allah" as the Cry of Bewilderment

What, then, is our very utterance of the name "Allah"? The text gives an answer of remarkable depth: `wa qawluna Allahu i'rabun bi-hadha al-ismi li-ilhani al-'aqli al-awwal` (and our saying "Allah" is an expression by this name on account of the bewilderment of the First Intellect). Our saying "Allah" — our expressing Him by this name — arises from *ilhan*, the bewilderment, the *walah*, of the First Intellect concerning the Originator. The Intellect is bewildered before Him, and the word "Allah" issues from that bewilderment. In this, again, all are equal: the Imam is equal in it, the ardent lover (*'ashiq*) is equal in it; whoever is bewildered before Him uses the name "Allah."

This is unfolded through the image of the circle. Allah is the center of the circle — the *markaz*, the central point — around which the bewildered circle revolves. The circle (*da'ira*) turns, and why does it turn? Because it is bewildered about its center. One circle revolves about its center in bewilderment; then another circle comes, bewildered about its own center; every circle revolves around its center, and the reason for its revolving is always the same — bewilderment. The very energy by which the circle revolves is, in truth, its bewilderment. The created order circles eternally about a center it cannot comprehend, and the motion of that circling is nothing other than the unceasing perplexity of the creature before the Originator.

### The First Intellect's Negation and Affirmation

The text now recounts the primal act of the First Intellect, the act in which divinity was first negated and then affirmed: `wa qawluna huwiyyatu al-muta'aliyah lamma nafa 'anhu abna'a jinsihi al-ilahiyyah wa athbataha lahu ta'ala wa takabbar kama akhbara 'anhu al-kitabu al-karim bi-qawlihi: shahida Allahu annahu la ilaha illa Hu` (and our saying "the exalted Ipseity" — when he negated from himself and the members of his genus the divinity, and affirmed it for Him, the Exalted and Supreme, as the Noble Book informed concerning him in His saying: "Allah testifies that there is no god but He"). The First Intellect, contemplating the Originator, first performed a negation. He said, in effect, "I am not the creator of myself." He pronounced `la ilaha` — there is no god — negating divinity from himself: I have no power to create myself, nor does my genus, the order of beings like me, have the power to create. He stripped *al-ilahiyyah*, divinity, from himself and his kind. And then he affirmed that divinity for Allah Ta'ala alone — `illa Hu`, except He.

This is the very pattern the Noble Book records in the verse `shahida Allahu annahu la ilaha illa Hu` (Allah testifies that there is no god but He). And the deep sense is this: the "Allah" who testifies here is the First Intellect — it is the First Intellect that bears witness `annahu la ilaha illa Hu`, that there is no god but He, the *Mubdi'* Most High. The First Intellect, and with it the whole order of created being, first confessed that it could not create itself nor its own genus, and then pointed beyond itself with `illa Hu` — He alone — to the Originator, indicated only by the bare pronoun "He."

The text continues: `wa laysa yaqa'u 'alayhi shay'un min dhalika illa kabiran min qawlihi huwa, wa sa-nuzihu haqiqata dhalika in sha' Allahu ta'ala` (and nothing of that falls upon Him save, perhaps, His saying "He," and we shall clarify the reality of that matter, if Allah the Exalted wills). `Fa a'raba 'anhu bi-ma kana min sifati ibda'ihi wa fi'lihi` (so he expressed about Him by what belonged to the attributes of His origination and His act). The Intellect expressed Him only through what was manifest in the origination — saying, in effect, "He created us thus, He fashioned us thus." We cannot praise the Creator as He deserves, for we do not know Him; but from the fact of our own existence, from the manner in which He made us, we can speak: He gave us life, He gave us knowledge, He gave us power. That much we may say, because that much we possess and know. As for true *tamjid*, true glorification — the highest praise befitting Him — that is beyond us. The very highest thing we are able to say is "Allah," and above that, "Huwa," He. Beyond these two words we have nothing with which to interpret Him.

### The Reason for Naming: Our Inadequacy and the Total Knowledge Given to the Intellects

Why, then, do we name Him at all with these inadequate names? The text explains: `fa-waqa'a li-nuqi'a 'ala al-huwiyyati al-muta'aliyah li-'ijzina wa diqi jawharina wa hisrina wa qusurina` (and so it came about that we should let it fall upon the exalted Ipseity — because of our inadequacy, the narrowness of our essence, our confinement, and our deficiency). We let the name fall upon the exalted Ipseity (*huwiyyah*) on account of our *'ijz*, our inadequacy, and the *diq*, the narrowness, of our *jawhar*, our essence — the essence of speech and articulation (*nutq*). We simply do not possess the essential capacity to comprehend Him, and so we have named Him with the last word that comes to our minds, beyond which there is nothing further. Our sense (*hiss*) is confined, and we are deficient and constrained; out of this confinement and deficiency the naming arises.

This points to a precise distinction in what the created intellects were granted. They were given *ilm al-kulli* — total knowledge — but not knowledge of the divine essence. As Sayyidna Mu'ayyad explains, the knowledge they were given is *'ilm al-hayat*, the knowledge of life: the knowledge of what powers and faculties reside in life itself — `hayat` (life), `'ilm` (knowledge), `quwwah` (power), and `ikhtiyar` (choice). Sayyidna Mu'ayyad expounds at length what each of these is: what life is, what knowledge is, what power is, what choice is. But nothing beyond this was given them. They were not given the knowledge by which to know Allah; that knowledge was withheld, for it cannot even be comprehended. The created intellects know the structure of life in its fullness, but the essence of the One who gave that life remains sealed against them.

### The Gift That Is Not Withdrawn, and the Question of Injustice

A further teaching of Sayyidna Mu'ayyad concerns the permanence of what Allah bestows. After Allah has given existence, He does not take that existence back — just as a human being who gives a thing to another does not afterward snatch it away from him; so too Allah, having granted existence, does not withdraw it. This bears upon a subtle question of divine power. There was once a debate between an Arya Samaji and the Muslims, and the Arya Samaji posed a challenge: "Your Allah can do everything?" The Muslims answered, "Yes." "Then can He do *zulm*, injustice? If He can do everything, He can do injustice too — and if you say He cannot, you are limiting His power." The Muslims replied with a counter-question: "Can you eat everything? You have the power to eat — so eat this filth set before you." The man, of course, could not; possessing the capacity to eat does not oblige one to consume what is vile. In the same way, Allah Ta'ala can do everything, yet He does not do injustice. His not doing *zulm* is no limitation upon His power; it is the perfection of His justice. Though He is capable of all things, He does not do wrong, and He does not take back the existence He has given.

### Naming as a Sin Coupled with Seeking Forgiveness

The text now adduces a prayer of Sayyidna Mu'ayyad that draws out a paradox at the very root of all naming. Sayyidna Mu'ayyad — Hibatullah ibn Musa al-Shirazi, the spiritual son of Mawlana Sayyidna Mu'ayyad al-Din, author of the *Mabda' wa Ma'ad* — said in some of his speech: `Allahumma ya man waqa'a i'tirafuna bi-sidqi ma qalahu fi hakimi dhikrihi, idh yaqulu wa qawluhu al-haqq: wa ma qadaru Allaha haqqa qadrihi` (O Allah, O You for whom our confession has come about, attesting the truth of what He said in His wise remembrance, when He says — and His word is the truth — "And they have not appraised Allah with His true appraisal"). Our confession is rendered to the truth of what He declared in His wise remembrance: that they have not measured the worth of Allah with His true worth. We have not been able to appraise Allah Ta'ala as He truly deserves, and we cannot, because we do not know Him; we are able only to say that, judging from our own existence, He has created so good a thing.

The prayer then asks for pardon: `inna nas'aluka al-musamahata liman huwa min rubqi al-'ubudiyyati fi madiqi al-hisari wa al-tadkhiri, idha aradna wala dhikrika bi-ghayri ma anta ahluhu` (indeed we ask You for forbearance toward one who is in the noose of servitude, in the narrow straits of confinement and constraint, when we set ourselves to undertake Your remembrance by that which You are not worthy of). We ask for *musamahah* — forbearance, pardon, from the root *samaha*, to forgive — for the one who is caught in the *rubq*, the snare, of servitude (*'ubudiyyah*). We are bound slaves, hemmed in by the narrow straits of confinement (*hisar*); there are limits set upon us beyond which we cannot pass. And when we set out to undertake Your remembrance, we are compelled to do so by means unworthy of You — for You are worthy only of that which we cannot comprehend. We say as much as we are able, as much as our power permits; and where we fall short and cannot say what befits You, we ask Your pardon.

The reason this pardon is needed is given precisely: `li-anna tanawula wala dhikrika bi-ghayri ma anta ahluhu bi-hukmi al-idtirari fa-innama huwa dhanbun mashfu'un bi-al-istighfar` (because to undertake Your remembrance by that which You are not worthy of, under the rule of compulsion, is only a sin coupled with seeking forgiveness). To take up His remembrance by inadequate means is done `bi-hukmi al-idtirar` — by the rule of compulsion, of necessity. We are compelled; out of *idtirar* we have no other word to say, and so we say what we can. And therefore it is `dhanbun mashfu'un bi-al-istighfar` — a sin coupled with the seeking of forgiveness. This is a technical term established by the Du'at: a sin that is *mashfu'*, paired or coupled (from *shafa'a*, to pair), with *istighfar*. It is a sin and yet a sin joined to the asking of pardon, and because it arises from compulsion it is accepted, for compulsion is excusable. The utmost humility is shown in it.

This profound teaching applies, the text is careful to note, not to the physical world alone but to all the worlds equally. The *istighfar* required here is for all; the First Intellect, the *Mubda' al-Awwal* itself, is included along with everyone else. This means the "sin" in question is not a sin in the ordinary moral sense, for the First Intellect committed no transgression. Rather, even the First Intellect's act — its saying that "You are the *Mubdi'* Most High," its bearing witness `shahida Allahu annahu la ilaha illa Hu`, its indicating Him only by `illa Hu` with the bare pronoun, since there is nothing beyond that — even this carries within it, in a manner of speaking, the character of a sin, because it falls short of His true majesty; and so it too is coupled with *istighfar*. From the First Intellect down to the tenth intellect, none can do anything beyond this; all are compelled, and all are equal before the Creator, the Originator, whom none can recognize.

The principle of compulsion is illustrated by a case judged in the time of Umar. A woman was brought before him who was with child, yet she said she had no husband. Asked how this had come to be, she explained: she had been traveling through the desert, perishing of thirst for three days, when she met a shepherd who said, "I will give you water, but only on the condition that you give yourself to me." Driven by the extremity of her thirst, near to death, she yielded for the sake of the water, and afterward bore a child. Umar said, "Stone her" — a child without marriage, without a husband. But the Mawla of all creation, Imam Ali, arrived and asked what the matter was, and when it was told to him he said: "She is *mustarr* — compelled, helpless. Let her go, for this act occurred under *idtirar*, under compulsion." *Idtirar* means helplessness; and where there is true helplessness, the act does not bear the full weight of a sin. So it is, the text intimates, with the soul that in its helplessness names the Unnameable: it sins, in a sense, by speaking inadequately of Him, yet because it is compelled, its sin is paired with the asking of forgiveness, and it is excused. A subtle paradox remains — that even in the pure world of spirits, where all is illumined save His essence, there should be a possibility of sin at all — but it is resolved in this coupling: nothing beyond this can be done; it is compulsion; and so neither the first intellect nor the tenth can do otherwise, and in the matter of the Creator and Originator everyone is equal, for none can recognize Him.

### The Ten Ascending Ranks of Spirituality

This first section closes with an enumeration of the spiritual ranks through which the ascent toward the divine is ordered — the *maratib*, the ten ranks rising to the highest. The lowest of those named is the Imamate of spirituality, the rank of *Rutbat al-Ilahiyyah* in its ascending series; and from there the ranks rise in order: *Hujjiyyah*, then *Hijabiyyah*, then *Khayaliyyah*, then *Fathiyyah*, then *Jiddiyyah*, then *Anza'iyyah*, then *Qa'imiyyah*, then *Jussiyyah*, then *Ahadiyyah*, then *Samadiyyah* — and culminating in *Ilahiyyah* and finally *Huwiyyah*, for the last and highest rank is *Huwiyyah*, the rank of the bare "He." Ten ranks in all are thus set out, ascending from the Imamate of spirituality up to the Ipseity, mapping the great ladder of spiritual stations by which the soul mounts toward its origin.

### The Esoteric Referents of Shafa', Witr, and Julus

One further matter belongs to this discussion, drawn out from the term *mashfu'* in the phrase *dhanbun mashfu'un bi-al-istighfar* — a "paired" or "coupled" sin. The word *shafa'* itself, which means "pairing," carries among the Du'at an esoteric referent, as do its companion terms *witr* and *julus*, all three taken from the language of prayer. The prayer that is *shafa'* is the paired one of two *rak'at*; *witr* is the single, odd *rak'at*; and *julus* is the prayer of two *rak'at* performed sitting. Each of these has an inner meaning, a *ta'weel*. The referent of *shafa'*, the paired prayer, is the Prophet — peace and blessings be upon him — together with Lady Fatima, for they are the noble pair. The referent of *witr*, the single prayer, is Imam Ali, peace be upon him, the odd and singular one. And the referent of *julus*, the sitting prayer of two *rak'at*, is Imam Hasan and Imam Husayn together. It is told that Imam Husayn himself at Karbala would name these correspondences — that the referent of *shafa'* is the Prophet, that the referent of *witr* was his own father, and so forth — and the text affirms `wa tilka 'ayatun wahidah`, that these together form one sign. Thus the very terminology by which the Du'at express the "coupled sin" of inadequate naming opens, in its turn, onto the hidden ranks of the Prophet and his pure household.
