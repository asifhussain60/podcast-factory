## The Prophetic Genealogy: from Abd al-Muttalib to the Qa'im

### Ibrahim as the Gathering-Point of the Eras

The lineage that runs down to the Prophet and the awaited Qa'im does not begin abruptly with the Quraysh of Makkah; it gathers up, in a single luminous channel, the whole inheritance of the cycles that preceded it. In Sayyidna Ibrahim, peace be upon him, the eras of Adam and of Nuh were drawn together. Every individual who had entered the haykal — the luminous temple — of the Natiq across those long ages was assembled in him, so that he became a confluence into which the accumulated spiritual forms of two complete cycles flowed. With that gathering of persons there gathered also the four ranks of the religious order: Nubuwwah (prophethood), Risalah (messengership), Wisayah (vicegerency), and Imamah (the imamate). All four were present and complete in Ibrahim, which is why his station stands as the archetype to which the later gathering in 'Abd al-Muttalib is repeatedly compared.

A point of careful discrimination must be set down here so that the reader does not confuse two figures who share a single name. There are, in the language of these realities, two Ibrahims. The first is Ibrahim the Natiq, the great speaking-prophet of his cycle, the one of whom the Quran says `alladhi waffa` — he who fulfilled, who paid in full what was asked of him. The second is a different man, also named Ibrahim, who was simply a Nabi of his own time and not the Natiq of a cycle; it is to this lesser Ibrahim that the Quranic phrase `kana rajulun yuqalu lahu Ibrahim` — "there was a man called Ibrahim" — refers. The honour, the gathering of the four ranks, and the role of cyclical confluence belong to the first; the name alone is shared by the second.

### The Highest Ranks of the Cycle: the Three and the Wazir of the Qa'im

Within the architecture of the Qa'im's order there are certain stations elevated above the ordinary chain of succession. Three figures in particular — Maulana Hashim, Maulana Hunayn, and Maulana Ibrahim, called together the thalathaha (the three) — hold a special khilafah and occupy a singular rank among the khilafats of the Qa'im. They are not merely links in the genealogical line; they are entrusted with a deputyship of a higher order, set apart from the ordinary transmission of the ranks.

Above even these stands the wazir of the Qa'im, who is named 'Ayn 'Azim — the great essence. His khilafah, his vicegerent succession, is taken up by Mim. Here Mim designates the line that passes through 'Abd al-Muttalib, then Abu Talib, then 'Abdullah: this is the deputyship of the 'Ayn carried in the persons of the Prophet's grandfather and his two sons. The pattern unfolds with two qiran — two conjunctions or equalities of rank — and then Mim Jadd al-Qa'im, the grandfather of the Qa'im, while Fatimah stands as the khalifah of Mim, the feminine vicegerent within this same noble succession. The whole arrangement is a hierarchy in which the great essence is served and deputized through this prophetic house.

### The Mutimmin and the Rank of the Sevenths

A further category of exalted persons must be set beside the three and the wazir: the mutimmin, the "completers" of a cycle. The mutimmin of the Dawr-e-Muhammadi — and, by the same principle, every seventh Imam — hold a very high rank. The seventh Imams named in this tradition are Muhammad bin Isma'il, Ma'add al-Mu'izz, Imam al-Tayyib, the twenty-eighth Maulana Ma'add, the twenty-second Maulana Ahmad, the forty-second Maulana 'Ali, and the forty-ninth Abu al-Qa'im. Each of these stands at the completing seventh of a heptad and therefore bears an authority above the ordinary Imams of the sequence.

Hazrat Nasrullah Sahib taught a subtle point about how this elevation operates. The atimmah — the completers — of the Prophet's own cycle reached so high a rank that a qiran, a genuine equality, occurred between them and the sevenths. The two categories, in other words, drew level: the completers of the Muhammadan cycle rose to such a degree that they became, at certain moments, equal to the seventh Imams in the taking of khilafah. The taking of the deputyship could therefore pass through either, since their stations had been brought into conjunction.

Among the atimmah themselves, one figure is exalted above the rest. Maulana Muhammad bin Isma'il holds the highest rank, for he is gathered together with a personality of the order of Imam Husayn, and his station is established in the dawr-e-kashf, the cycle of unveiling. Precisely because he belongs to the cycle of unveiling, he does not fall into the category of the seventh class at all; his rank transcends that classification rather than merely occupying its summit. Of the seven, four are the greater — Muhammad bin Isma'il, Imam al-Tayyib, Maulana Ahmad, and Abu al-Qa'im — and of these four, three each gather two ranks within themselves. But Muhammad bin Isma'il alone holds the special and unshared rank of the sahib-e-dawr-e-kashf, the master of the cycle of unveiling, a station anchored in Surat al-Ikhlas, in the verse `qul huwa Allahu ahad` — "Say: He is Allah, the One." His rank is fastened to the declaration of absolute unity itself.

### The Name of the Qa'im and the Precedence of Wisayah

The awaited one's name is 'Ali. Sayyidna, in the poetic responses he composed, referred to him as Abu 'Ali — "father of 'Ali" — a usage that both names the Qa'im and points to his generative station within the line. This identification of the Qa'im as 'Ali runs through the whole exposition that follows and must be held in mind whenever the Qa'im of Isma'il's progeny is spoken of.

A note on the very ordering of this book is warranted, for it explains a peculiarity the careful reader will notice. Because the Anwar is a foundational work — the book from which most of the Du'at draw their proofs — it deliberately places Wisayah before Imamah, contrary to the usual order followed in most other books. This is not an accident of arrangement but a doctrinal statement: Wisayah, the vicegerency, is the higher of the two ranks, and the Anwar gives it precedence in its listing to make that priority visible.

### Ibrahim's Two Sons: the Station of Isma'il and the Service of Ishaq

The great division upon which the entire later genealogy rests was made by Ibrahim between his two sons. Ibrahim established Isma'il as his Wasi, entrusting to him the ranks of Wisayah and Imamah by the direct command of Allah, because Isma'il is a maqam ilahi — a divine station — and a haykal nurani, a luminous temple. To Ishaq, by contrast, Ibrahim entrusted the ranks of Nubuwwah and Risalah, and made him a khadim (servant), a hijab (veil), and a da'i (summoner) to Isma'il. The higher, permanent ranks went to the one who was himself a divine station; the manifest, summoning ranks went to the one whose office is to serve and to call to the other.

This division opens into a doctrine of two whole lineages with two distinct natures. Isma'il and his progeny are the divine stations — maqamat ilahiyyah — and the possessors of the luminous temples; they are the ahl al-istiqrar, the people of permanence, those whose station is fixed and abiding. Ishaq and his progeny, on the other hand, are the proofs and the manifest summoners — the hujaj and the du'at zahirun — and they stand as veils placed upon the people of permanence. The one line is the settled, hidden reality; the other is the manifest, public office that both conceals and proclaims it.

From Ishaq, accordingly, ran the long chain of the Du'at and the hujub, the manifest summoners. It passed through Ya'qub, then Yusuf, and onward through the named prophets of that line down to 'Isa, peace be upon him; and after 'Isa it continued through Sham'un, then 'Abd al-Masih, and then Bahira al-Rahib, Bahira the monk. All of these are the ahl al-istida' — the people of summoning, those who hold the manifest office of calling — and all of them trace their lineage back to Ishaq, the father of the summoners. When at last the Prophet was manifested, the rank of Nabuwwah that had until then resided with Bahira the monk was taken up by Ubayy ibn Ka'b, so that the office did not lapse but passed forward into the Prophet's own community.

### The Imams of Isma'il's Line: the Chain Down to 'Adnan

If the line of Ishaq is the line of the summoners, the line of Isma'il is the line of the divine stations, the A'immah proper. The first of the A'immah, the divine stations, after Ibrahim was Isma'il ibn Ibrahim himself. From Isma'il the entrustment passed to Qaydar, and from Qaydar it descended through a long named succession of fathers to sons, generation after generation, down to 'Udad bin 'Udad — and the muqim, the establisher set up alongside this Imam, was Musa.

The principle that governs this chain is that when the appointed time bound the manifestation of a Natiq — when a new speaking-prophet was due to appear — the Imam of the age would establish a da'i to carry the manifest office. So 'Udad established Musa from among his Du'at to fill that role, and the genealogical chain itself continued forward through 'Adnan, Ma'add, Nizar, Mudar, Ilyas, Mudrikah, and Khuzaymah — and it was Khuzaymah's da'i who in turn was commanded to establish 'Isa. Thus the line of the Imams and the line of the summoners run in parallel, each Imam in his turn raising up the summoner appropriate to the unfolding of the cycles.

This pairing of each Natiq with his muqim, his establisher, can be set out plainly: the muqim of Adam is Maulana Hunayn; the muqim of Nuh is Maulana Hud; the muqim of Ibrahim is Maulana Salih; the muqim of Musa is 'Udad bin 'Udad; and the muqim of 'Isa is Khuzaymah. Each great speaking-prophet, that is, has standing with him an establisher who anchors and upholds his cycle, and these named pairings reveal the hidden scaffolding behind the apparent succession of prophets.

The chain then carries on from Khuzaymah, descending through Kinanah, Nadr, Malik, Fihr, Ghalib, Lu'ayy, Ka'b, Murrah, Kilab, Qusayy, 'Abd Manaf, and Hashim, until it arrives at 'Abd al-Muttalib. And with 'Abd al-Muttalib something occurs that had not occurred since Ibrahim: the four ranks — Wisayah, Imamah, Nabuwwah, and Risalah — gathered together in a single person exactly as they had once gathered in Ibrahim. The whole long descent was a movement toward this second great confluence.

### The Four Ranks Gather in 'Abd al-Muttalib

The fifth chapter opens precisely upon this gathering. As the text states it: `Wa lamma ijtama'at hadhihi al-rutab al-arba' 'ind Abd al-Muttalib, kanat ithnatan minhunna mudakhkharatayn li-l-qa'im min wuld Isma'il 'alayhis salam, wa huma al-wasayah wa-l-imamah, wa ithnatan li-l-qa'im min wuld Ishaq 'alayhis salam, wa huwa al-nubuwwah wa-l-risalah` — "And when these four ranks gathered with 'Abd al-Muttalib, two of them were reserved for the Qa'im from the progeny of Isma'il, peace be upon him, and they are the vicegerency and the imamate; and two were for the Qa'im from the progeny of Ishaq, peace be upon him, namely the prophethood and the messengership."

The doctrine compressed into this single line is the keystone of the chapter. When the four ranks assembled with Sayyidna 'Abd al-Muttalib, they did not simply rest in him; they were partitioned according to their two destinies. Two of them — Wisayah and Imamah — were held in reserve, stored up (mudakhkharatayn) for the Qa'im of the progeny of Isma'il. Two others — Nubuwwah and Risalah — were set aside for the Qa'im of the progeny of Ishaq. And the identities behind these two titles must be grasped firmly: the Qa'im from the progeny of Isma'il is 'Ayn-e-'Azimah, the great essence; and the Qa'im from the progeny of Ishaq is the Lord Prophet himself, Muhammad. The two streams that Ibrahim first separated between his two sons are thus carried, all the way down, to their two appointed culminations — and a governing law holds throughout: Imamah and Wisayah are never separated from one another, but always travel together as a single pair.

### The Necessity of the Luminous Father

The text proceeds to one of its most searching arguments, concerning the conditions under which a divine station can exist at all. `Wa kana fi 'adlillah fadlu Muhammadin wa sharafuhu salawatullahi alaih an yakuna maqaman ilahiyyan wa haykalan nuraniyyan` — "And it was in the justice of Allah the virtue and honour of Muhammad, blessings of Allah be upon him, that he should be a divine station and a luminous structure." It belonged to the very justice ('adl) and grace of Allah that Muhammad should be a maqam ilahi and a haykal nurani.

But this exaltation carries a precondition, and the text states it without softening: `wa lan yakuna dhalika illa li-man yakunu abuhu kadhalika haykalan nuraniyyan` — "and that will not be except for one whose father is likewise a luminous structure." A man cannot become a luminous temple unless his father is himself a luminous temple. For the gathering of the suwarat — the spiritual forms — that makes a haykal nurani what it is, there must already be in place a vessel of sufficient capacity and strength (quwwat) to receive and hold all those forms; and that receptive strength is not created in a man whose father was not himself such a temple. The principle is put with finality: `wa illa fa-la sabila ila dhalik` — "and if not, then there is no way to that." Absent the luminous father, there is simply no path to the luminous son.

To make the matter intelligible, the lecture draws an image from the natural world. The rays of the sun fall everywhere — over the whole earth, across the entire universe, indiscriminately. Yet only certain things respond to them in a particular way. A mirror, when the rays strike it, catches the light and casts it back; other bodies possess the quality of grasping the sun's rays with strength and holding them. The capacity to receive is not in the light but in the receiver. So too with the luminous forms: they may be present, but only a haykal nurani has the receptive strength to gather them, and that strength is itself inherited from a luminous father. This is why the station of Muhammad as a divine structure presupposes that 'Abdullah, his father, was likewise a haykal nurani — the luminosity of the son requires the luminosity of the father as its necessary ground.

### The Two Gates of 'Abd al-Muttalib

Having established the principle, the text turns to the mechanism by which the ascending forms are received and sorted. `Wa kana min Abd al-Muttalib an nasaba lahu babayn yatasallamani al-suwar al-muntaqilah al-murtaqiyah min a'dad da'watihi` — "And it was from 'Abd al-Muttalib that he established for himself two gates which would receive the transferring, ascending forms from the numbers of his da'wah." 'Abd al-Muttalib set up two gates (babayn) whose function was to receive the suwarat as they rose up and transferred from the ranks of his da'wah.

`Wa ja'ala ahadahuma a'la min al-akhar` — "and he made one of them higher than the other." The two gates were not equal: one was set above the other, and each gathered a different grade of form. `Fa-kana al-majma' al-'ali majma'an li-l-suwar al-mu'minin al-bulagha' wa-l-hudud al-fudala'` — "so the higher gathering was a place of assembly for the forms of the accomplished believers and the virtuous hudud." The higher gate — the majma'-e-'ali — was the assembling-place for the forms of the mu'minin bulagha', the believers who had reached completion, and of the hudud fudala', the virtuous ranks of the hierarchy. `Wa-l-thani majma'an li-l-suwar al-mustajibin alladhina ukhidhat 'alayhim 'uhud al-wila' al-mustaqimin 'alayhi la ghayr` — "and the second a place of assembly for the forms of the respondents from whom the covenants of allegiance were taken, who were steadfast upon it, and nothing else." The lower gate gathered the forms of the mustajibin, the simple respondents, from whom the covenant ('ahd) of allegiance (wila') had been taken and who stood steadfast upon that loyalty — wila' being, in its root, love and devotion.

The higher gate is then named: `fa-kanat tilka al-suwar al-mujtami'ah 'ind al-bab al-'ali Abu Talib 'alayhis salam` — "so those gathered forms were with the higher gate, Abu Talib, peace be upon him." The forms collected at the higher gate were gathered with Abu Talib; and the forms gathered at the lower gate, `wa-l-suwar allati 'ind al-bab al-dani 'Abdillah`, were the forms collected with 'Abdullah, the lower gate. The two sons thus served as the two receiving-gates of their father's da'wah, Abu Talib receiving the higher order of forms and 'Abdullah the lower.

### From Bab al-Abwab to Qalb-e-Haykal

The role of a gate, however, is not static. The text and its exposition unfold a deeper movement: the bab gathers all the forms of the world, and then, when the time comes to install them (nasb) in the Imam's son, the very gate that gathered them goes on to become the qalbe haykal — the heart of the structure — of that son, the next khalifah. The gate does not merely collect; it is itself transformed into the living core of the successor. And the decision as to which form is ready to be included in the haykal at any given moment does not rest with the gate but with the Imam, and ultimately with the Imam of Imams — 'Ayn-e-'Azimah, the great essence — from whom the indication comes that a particular form is now ready, at its appointed time, to be honoured with installation.

This principle is then traced concretely through the generations, each Imam's bab al-abwab being identified as his wife, who first gathers the forms and afterwards becomes the qalbe haykal of his son. Hashim's bab al-abwab was Salma, who was then submerged — became the qalbe haykal — in 'Abd al-Muttalib. 'Abd al-Muttalib's bab al-abwab was Fatimah bint 'Amr, who was submerged in Abu Talib. Abu Talib's bab al-abwab was Fatimah bint Asad, who became the qalbe haykal of 'Ali. And 'Ali's bab al-abwab was Salman-e-Farsi, who became the qalbe haykal of Imam Husayn. The same chain, expressed in the da'wah's own cryptic term `ummuhu, ummuhu, ummuha` — "his mother, his mother, her mother" — points to the qalbe haykal of the Prophet, of 'Ali, and of Fatimah respectively.

A careful distinction is drawn here between two offices that might be confused: being a bab al-abwab and becoming a qalbe haykal. The Prophet's bab al-abwab was Khadijah, who was submerged in Fatimah — that is, Khadijah became the qalbe haykal of Fatimah al-Zahra. And 'Abdullah's bab al-abwab was Aminah, who was submerged in the Prophet — Aminah became the qalbe haykal of Muhammad himself. So Aminah, called "his mother" (ummuhu), was the Prophet's qalbe haykal, not merely his gate, having served first as 'Abdullah's bab al-abwab; and Fatimah bint Asad, "his mother," was 'Ali's qalbe haykal; and Khadijah was the qalbe haykal of Fatimah. To be a gate is to gather; to be a heart of the structure is to be installed as the living centre of the next station — and the same woman passes from the first office to the second across a generation.

### The Four Fatimahs and the Recitations of Ya Fatir

The figure of Fatimah multiplies across this line, and the devotional practice of the community preserves the pattern. The four recitations of `Ya Fateru` — the four prostrations and tellings associated with that invocation — correspond to four Fatimahs. The first sajdah, recited one hundred times, belongs to Fatimah al-Zahra. The recitation made upon the right cheek, also one hundred times, belongs to Fatimah bint Asad. The third belongs to Fatimah bint 'Amr. And the fourth sajdah, recited one hundred and ten times, belongs to the Zawjat al-Qa'im, the wife of the Qa'im, who is likewise named Fatimah. Among all four, Fatimah al-Zahra is superior, her station above the rest.

The bodily form of the prostration itself carries a teaching. In the complete sajdah, seven parts of the body must touch the ground: the forehead or face, the two hands, the two knees, and the two feet — seven points of contact in all. This sevenfold touching signifies that the complete order of the da'wah requires seven limbs, seven hudud, for its full establishment. By contrast, the position in which only the cheek touches the ground signifies a lesser rank, an incompleteness of the order; the difference between the full prostration on seven members and the partial touching of the cheek is itself a sign of the difference between a complete and a diminished hierarchy.

### The Qa'imayn: 'Ayn-e-'Azimah and the Prophet

The fifth chapter restates, as a settled conclusion, the identity of the two Qa'ims. The Qa'im min wuld Isma'il is 'Ayn-e-'Azimah, the great essence, and the Qa'im min wuld Ishaq is the Prophet. And it reaffirms the law of the luminous structure: the haykal nurani requires a receptive strength (quwwat) capable of gathering all the forms, and such strength can be formed only if the father is himself a haykal nurani. The doctrine of the gate is likewise restated in its general form: the bab gathers all the world's forms, and when the time of installation (nasb) arrives, it becomes the qalbe haykal of the Imam's son; and it is the Imam — and above him the Imam of Imams, 'Ayn-e-'Azimah — who determines which form is ripe for inclusion.

### The Sacrifice of 'Abdullah

'Abd al-Muttalib's treatment of his son 'Abdullah is then explained in the light of these stations. `Wa shahara Abd al-Muttalib amrahu bi-l-dhabh` — "and 'Abd al-Muttalib made his matter famous through the sacrifice." 'Abd al-Muttalib had bound himself by a vow that, should he be granted a certain number of sons, he would offer one in sacrifice; and when his sons were born — ten or eleven of them — the lot for the sacrifice at length fell upon 'Abdullah, in a parallel to the trial of Ibrahim with his own son. In place of 'Abdullah, one hundred camels were sacrificed; and each time the lot returned, 'Abd al-Muttalib fulfilled it, until the redemption was complete. `Wa nawwaha bi-smihi li-ya'rifa ahl al-haqa'iq fadlahu` — "and he made his name renowned so that the people of realities might know his virtue."

The inner purpose of this whole episode was not the camels but the renown. By making 'Abdullah's near-sacrifice famous, 'Abd al-Muttalib made it known among the ahl al-haqa'iq, the people of realities, that 'Abdullah was being prepared for a particular station — the station for which Nubuwwah and Risalah were the appointed ranks. The dhabh was the public sign of an inward preparation, declaring to those who could read it that this son was the chosen vessel of the prophetic ranks. The lecture pauses on a verse of Iqbal that captures the hiddenness of such inner realities from outward reason: "What is intellect (khirad)? It is a lamp on the path (chiragh-e rahguzar), by which a person's sight is illumined; within the house (darun-e khana), what commotions there are — what does the lamp on the path know?" Intellect is the lamp set in the street to light the comings and goings of travellers; but of the affairs unfolding inside the house — the inner realities of these stations — the street-lamp of reason knows nothing.

### The Present Imam as Jadd-e-Qa'im

The pattern set in 'Abd al-Muttalib is not merely historical; it is the template of what is yet to come. The present Imam, Maulana Muhammad bin 'Ali, peace be upon him, holds the lofty station of jadd-e-qa'im — the grandfather of the Qa'im — exactly as 'Abd al-Muttalib held the station of jaddiyyat-e-'ayn, the essence-grandfather. This is a very high station, and it carries with it, by the custom (dastur) of these matters, a fixed sequel. Just as 'Abd al-Muttalib had two sons, so too will the present Imam, the jadd-e-qa'im, have two sons of necessity.

One of these sons will stand at the station of Sayyidna Abu Talib, and from him will come the Qa'im. The other will stand at the station of Sayyidna 'Abdullah, and he is Sayyidna Mahdi, peace be upon him. Sayyidna Mahdi will have a daughter whose name will be Fatimah — and here the four-fold pattern of the Fatimahs and the one hundred and ten recitations of the Zawjat al-Qa'im finds its fulfilment — and she will marry the Qa'im. From that union Hasan and Husayn will be born: those who stand at the stations of Hasan and Husayn will appear again. The same diagram (naqshah) that was traced in the house of 'Abd al-Muttalib will be traced once more in the house of the awaited one.

### The Division Between Abu Talib and 'Abdullah

The text now sets out exactly how 'Abd al-Muttalib partitioned the four ranks between his two sons. `Fa-sallama Abd al-Muttalib rutbat al-wasayah wa-l-imamah ila Abi Talib, wa rutbat al-nubuwwah wa-l-risalah ila 'Abdillah` — "so 'Abd al-Muttalib handed over the rank of vicegerency and imamate to Abu Talib, and the rank of prophethood and messengership to 'Abdullah." By this handing-over the two sons became two Imams, each bearing his portion of the inheritance.

Their natures, however, differed in kind. `Ahaduhuma imamun natiq` — "one of them is a speaking imam" — and this is Abu Talib, who is like Husayn bin 'Ali, an imam-e-natiq. `Wa-l-akhar samitun wa huwa 'Abdullah ka-l-Hasan bin 'Aliyy` — "and the other is silent, and he is 'Abdullah, like al-Hasan bin 'Ali." Abu Talib was the speaking Imam in the likeness of Husayn; 'Abdullah was the silent (samit) Imam in the likeness of al-Hasan. And from this division came the two great sons: `fa-awlada 'Abdullah Muhammadan wa awlada Abu Talib 'Aliyyan` — "so 'Abdullah fathered Muhammad and Abu Talib fathered 'Ali." The bearer of Nubuwwah and Risalah fathered the Prophet; the bearer of Wisayah and Imamah fathered 'Ali.

There is also a correspondence of these stations to a Quranic measure. `Qaba qawsayn aw adna` — "two bows' length, or nearer" — maps onto these persons: 'Abd al-Muttalib and Abu Talib occupy the station of `qaba qawsayn`, the two bows' length, while 'Abdullah occupies the station of `adna`, the nearer. The graded nearness of the Quranic ascent is thus read as a hierarchy of these three.

### The Transfers of the Ranks and the Great Guardianship

The succession of the ranks across the deaths of these figures is traced with precision. `Fa-lamma anat nuqlatu 'Abdillah, istawda'a abahu 'Abd al-Muttalib` — "so when the time of 'Abdullah's transfer came, he entrusted his father 'Abd al-Muttalib." The lecture pauses to insist on the correct reading: the word is `nuqlatu` — the transfer, the subject of the sentence — and the verb is `anat`, "came," not `anta`; a reader unacquainted with the Arabic might mangle the line, but its sense is exact. When the time of 'Abdullah's transfer (nuqlatu) came — that is, when he was to pass on — he entrusted to his own father, 'Abd al-Muttalib, the ranks of Nubuwwah and Risalah, depositing them with him on behalf of his son Muhammad, in the same pattern that occurred with Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq. Then `wa intaqala ila dar karamatillah` — "and he transferred to the abode of the honour of Allah" — 'Abdullah passed to the abode of honour.

The entrustment then moved one generation further. `Fa-lamma anat nuqlatu 'Abd al-Muttalib, istawda'a waladahu Aba Talib rutbat al-nubuwwah wa-l-risalah li-waladi waladihi alladhi huwa Muhammad salawatullahi alaih` — "and when the time of 'Abd al-Muttalib's transfer came, he entrusted his son Abu Talib with the rank of prophethood and messengership for his grandson, who is Muhammad, blessings of Allah be upon him" — `li-kawnihi fi hadd al-tufuliyyah`, "because of his being in the limit of childhood." Since Muhammad was still in childhood (tufuliyyah), 'Abd al-Muttalib, on his own transfer, deposited the ranks of Nubuwwah and Risalah with Abu Talib, to be held for the grandson. In this way two of the Prophet's ranks gathered with Abu Talib, who became the custodian (kafil) of Nubuwwah and Risalah. The distinction of degrees is precise: 'Abd al-Muttalib had been the owner of all four stations; Abu Talib was the owner of two and the custodian of the other two. Then 'Abd al-Muttalib too `intaqala ila dar karamatillah ta'ala`, passed to the abode of the honour of Allah the Exalted.

`Wa qama Abu Talib 'alayhis salam bi-l-rutab al-arba' ila an balagha Muhammad salawatullahi alaih ashuddahu` — "and Abu Talib, peace be upon him, upheld the four ranks until Muhammad, blessings of Allah be upon him, reached his maturity." Abu Talib upheld all four ranks until the Prophet attained the age of maturity; `fa-sallama ilayhi ma istudi'a lahu Abu Talib`, "then he handed over to him what had been entrusted to Abu Talib for him" — `wa hiya rutbatan, al-nubuwwah wa-l-risalah`, "and it was two ranks, prophethood and messengership." When Muhammad came of age, Abu Talib returned to him the deposited Nubuwwah and Risalah.

This sequence is the basis of one of the chapter's most striking conclusions. Because the Prophet received the great guardianship — the kafalat-e-'uzma — the overwhelming power of that guardianship made it so that, although Abu Talib had assigned Nubuwwah and Risalah to the Prophet and Imamah and Wisayah to 'Ali, all the ranks in fact gathered in the Prophet. Both Muhammad and 'Ali were young; and the strength of the kafalat-e-'uzma overshadowed all the mustawda'in and mustaqarrin — all the deposited and settled ranks — that had been gathered with 'Ali. So despite bearing in his own name only Nubuwwah and Risalah, the Prophet achieved superiority over every rank gathered with 'Ali, becoming the deputy of the 'Ayn. No rank of the Qa'im remained outside this gathering except the thalathah sukra — the three intoxicated ones — who alone stood apart from the Prophet's overarching guardianship.

A further ruling on the conditions of the imamate follows from this house. After Maulana 'Abd al-Muttalib, the haykal-nurani condition requires that an Imam's father and grandfather both be haykal nurani — the luminosity must be doubly secured in the immediate ancestry. Yet within this requirement a special possibility is opened: two sons of a single father may both be Imams, one occupying the mustawda' (the deposit) and the other the mustaqarr (the settled station), exactly as occurred with the two sons of 'Abd al-Muttalib. The condition of luminous ancestry does not forbid two simultaneous Imams from one father; it provides for them in the twin offices of deposit and permanence.

### The Three Courses of the Prophet–'Ali Relationship

The relationship between the Prophet and 'Ali is not single but threefold, and the text names the three courses as the salasa majari. The first course is expressed in 'Ali's own words, `ana 'abdun min 'abid Muhammad` — "I am a slave from the slaves of Muhammad" — and this is the majra-e-adna, the lowest course, in which 'Ali declares himself one of the Prophet's servants and so illustrates, by contrast, the loftiness of the awaited one's station. The second course is one of equality, masawat, expressed in the saying `ana wa 'Aliyyun ka-hatayn` — "I and 'Ali are like these two," spoken of two fingers held side by side; yet even this equality is a balanced one, the one on the right and the one on the left. The third course is the a'la, the higher: the elevation of the Hijab-e-'Ain that took place on the day of the conquest of Makkah, when the Prophet raised 'Ali up, and the veil that had stood lower or level was lifted to the higher station. Lower, equal, and higher — adna, masawat, a'la — these are the three courses through which the single relationship moves.

### The Completion of the Religion and the Final Ascent

A question of interpretation is settled here regarding the verse of completion. When the Quran declares, "This day I have perfected for you your religion," the meaning is the completion of the obligations of the din — the bringing to fullness of all its commands. The command of wilayah was announced last of all, after the command of Hajj had become the final ordinance; only then, after every command had been revealed, was the announcement of wilayah made and the religion declared complete. The verse therefore refers to the completion of the obligations, not to any change in the Prophet's own station or rank.

For the ascent of the Prophet through the ranks themselves is described in its own terms. `Wa dhalika anna Muhammadan salawatullahi alaih taraqqa fi rutbatay al-shay' wa-l-shay'an ila an balagha rutbat al-nutq al-ilahi, wa huwa qa'im wuld Ishaq 'alayhis salam alladhi sabaqa al-qawl 'alayhim` — "and that is because Muhammad, blessings of Allah be upon him, ascended through the two ranks, thing by thing, until he reached the rank of divine speech, and he is the Qa'im of the progeny of Ishaq, peace be upon him, about whom the saying has preceded." When at maturity Abu Talib handed over the entrusted Nubuwwah and Risalah, Muhammad ascended (taraqqa) step by step through the ranks until he reached the rank of nutq-e-ilahi, divine speech — the final and highest rank, which stands above Nubuwwah, Imamah, and Wisayah alike. These latter three are the lower-order ranks; above them the higher ranks begin, and there is found the nutq-e-ilahi and the station of the hujjab, the maqamun mahmudun, the praised station.

This praised station is not fixed in magnitude but varies according to the 'ayn it serves. Because an 'ayn does not appear in a single uniform form — one 'ayn being of a single cycle (dawr), another of seven cycles — the maqam-e-mahmud changes correspondingly: as the station of the 'ayn is, so will the praised station be. At the khutbat al-tasmeem, the sermon of designation, the station of the 'ayn itself changes into the form of the Qa'im. The text marks that the Prophet, having reached the rank of divine speech, is the Qa'im of the progeny of Ishaq about whom the foregoing saying had been made, and that `wa kana judullahi 'alayhi haykalan nuraniyyan wa maqaman ruhaniyyan` — "and the beneficence of Allah was upon him as a luminous structure and a spiritual station."

### The Gathering of All the Cycles in the Prophet

The chapter rises to its summit with the Prophet's own declaration: `wa li-dhalika kana yaqul, ana da'watu Abi Ibrahim` — "and for that reason he used to say, I am the prayer (da'wat) of my father Ibrahim." The Prophet identified himself as the answered supplication of Ibrahim, and in that identification he gathered into himself the whole inheritance of the cycles: `wa ijtima'at al-maqamat al-'aliyah wa-l-hayakil al-nuraniyah fi jami' al-adwar min awwal al-thalathah alaf sana` — "and the high stations and the luminous structures gathered in all the cycles from the first three thousand years." All the lofty stations and luminous temples of every cycle were assembled in his haykal.

The exposition specifies which forms these were. Within the cycle of unveiling, the dawr-e-kashf — understood here as a span of some fifty thousand years, of which the period of interval, the dawr-e-fatrah, is three thousand — the last three thousand years bore two kinds of forms. One kind carried the qualities of the dawr-e-sitir, the cycle of concealment; the other carried the characteristics of the dawr-e-kashf, the cycle of unveiling. The higher and superior kind is not subjected to the qualities of the period of interval; these are reckoned as the suwarat of the dawr-e-kashf. Whereas the lower (adna) forms of the three thousand years of the interval gathered with Imam Junayd, peace be upon him, the higher forms of the dawr-e-kashf are those that gathered in the Prophet. As the text continues, `wa kanat bi-jumlatiha haykalan nuraniyyan wa maqaman ruhaniyyan za nasutin ka-manasik al-a'immah 'alayhim al-salam` — "and in their entirety they were a luminous structure and a spiritual station, possessing a human form like the rites of the Imams, peace be upon them." These gathered forms, taken altogether, constituted a luminous temple and a spiritual station clothed in human form (nasut), after the manner of the rites of the Imams.

And these gathered forms had a name and an outcome: `wa kanat 'Aliyyan 'alayhis salam wa kana qa'im wuld Isma'il 'alayhis salam` — "and it was 'Ali, peace be upon him, and he was the Qa'im of the progeny of Isma'il, peace be upon him." The luminous structure assembled from all the high stations and temples of the cycles became 'Ali — and 'Ali is the Qa'im of the progeny of Isma'il. With the Prophet, then, the two streams that Ibrahim divided are joined again: the Qa'im of Isma'il's progeny is 'Ali and the Qa'im of Ishaq's progeny is the Prophet, but the succession occurred in the person of the Prophet, who gathers them both when he is joined with 'Ayn-e-'Azima. The Prophet, by his union with the great essence, becomes the point at which the whole genealogy converges.

### The Chain of Concealed Knowledge Among the Du'at

The chapter closes upon a note about the transmission of knowledge itself, in the supplication (du'a) of the da'i during the cycle of the Qa'im. Sayyidna sahib used to teach that the work of the Du'at was a work of progressive disclosure that nonetheless preserved a reserve of hidden knowledge at every stage. One da'i, Sayyidna Sulayman, wrote an exposition of the du'as that had come down, explaining them and performing a great service; yet in some of his books he kept certain pieces of new knowledge concealed. When Sayyidna Ja'far came after him, he explained those hidden matters — but he too, from his own side, kept hidden some of what he had received from the Imam. So it ran: one da'i after another would explain the du'as and unveil what his predecessor had withheld, while himself withholding something further. This series of concealment continues from da'i to da'i, an unbroken chain in which each summoner discloses and conceals in his turn.

The exposition ends with the closing benediction of the text: `wa-l-hamdu lillah 'ala bulugh al-ghayat wa-l-ittila' 'ala khawafi al-asrar al-khafiyyat. Wa salla Allahu 'ala Muhammadin khatim al-nabiyyin wa 'ala alihi al-tahirin al-tayyibin, wa 'ala al-qasimayn min huma al-qasim al-awwal 'Abdillah, 'ala khalqihi ajma'in` — "And praise be to Allah for reaching the goals and for being informed of the hidden secrets. And may Allah's blessings be upon Muhammad, the seal of the prophets, and upon his pure and goodly family, and upon the two Qasims, of whom the first Qasim is 'Abdullah, upon all of His creation." With praise for the attainment of the goal and for the unveiling of the hidden secrets, and with blessings upon the seal of the prophets, his pure family, and the two Qasims, the chapter of the prophetic genealogy is brought to its close.
