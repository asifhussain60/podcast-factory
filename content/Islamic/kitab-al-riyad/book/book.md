# Kitab al-Riyad: A Judgment Between the Two Reformers

## Introduction to the Book

This is a book of judgment. Its author, the Ismaili missionary Hamid al-Din Ahmad ibn 'Abd Allah al-Kirmani (اَلْكِرْمَانِيّ), known by the title Hujjat al-Iraqayn (حُجَّةُ الْعِرَاقَيْن), did not compose it to lay out a doctrine of his own. He composed it to weigh, within a garden already planted, the work of two who came before him — each a reformer in his turn. The first is al-Nasafi (اَلنَّسَفِيّ), author of a book called al-Islah; the second is Abu Yaqub al-Sijistani (أَبُو يَعْقُوبِ السِّجِسْتَانِي), author of al-Nusra. Between these two the author takes his stand, measuring what each advanced and rendering a verdict.

The setting is the world of Islam as the third century of the Hijra drew toward its close, a world stamped with philosophy and letters and unsettled by fresh calls to doctrine and disputes that branched in every direction. Against that background the book turns to the questions its two authors argued: the rank of the soul beside the intellect, the parts that compose the human being, the human person as a small image of the whole, decree and destiny, the origin of the sacred law in Adam and Noah, and the oneness of God together with the act of creation.

It is, then, a work of measured argument — a garden opened not to be planted anew, but to be judged.

## 1. The Freedom to Disagree

The Islamic world at the close of the third century of the Muhammadan Hijra bore, as I read it, the unmistakable stamp of its sciences, its philosophy, and its literature. New currents, tangled doctrinal disagreements, and an unending theological argument ran through its cultural life and its learned society, settling with no single party and coming to rest in no single place. Any researcher who wishes to understand the origins of those differences will find it worthwhile to weigh the opinions of the writers and scholars who lived in that distant age. They left behind scholarly traces and fresh modes of expression suited to their time, to their understanding of philosophical questions, and to the reach of their effort in interpreting texts and construing verses. From all of this, a careful reader can form a reasoned view that carries him toward reality and toward truth.

Those disputes more often than not spilled beyond the oral discussions that used to be raised in salons and learned centers, growing into long treatises that spread across the pages of books and pamphlets. Among the sects that contributed most generously to this field were the Ismailis. What strikes me as remarkable is that this group did not wait to conclude its theological argument with the other sects before turning inward and plunging into a dispute of an altogether new kind — a dispute among the very men who had summoned others to the Ismaili cause. In this there is clear evidence of an intellectual freedom that prevailed in their lives: a release from the constraints that hold conservative societies in check, binding them to the inherited order and permitting no irregularity and no departure from the old established plan.

The first argument — or, more precisely, the first scholarly discussion — that opened new horizons for the Ismailis, carried them into an atmosphere of far-reaching intellectual liberation, and made them a subject of conjecture and hearsay throughout the Islamic world, was the appearance of the book *al-Mahsul* (The Harvest). This book was put into circulation at the beginning of the fourth century AH — the tenth century AD — and is attributed to the great Syrian preacher

> محمد بن احمد النسفي

Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Nasafi, who was credited with turning the Samanid state toward Ismailism and who was put to death in the year 331 AH (942 AD), as recorded in

> اَلْفَرْقُ بَيْنَ الْفَرْقِ

*The Difference Between the Sects* by Abd al-Qahir al-Baghdadi. It strikes me as very strange that the Proof of the Two Iraqs, the movement's greatest preacher and supreme philosopher,

> حميد الدين احمد الكرماني

Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani, who examined the whole subject and went deeply into it, never once mentions the author of *al-Mahsul*. His discussion is directed instead at

> أَبِي حَاتِمٍ الرَّازِي ، وَأَبِي يَعْقُوبَ السِّجِسْتَانِي

Abu Hatim al-Razi and Abu Yaqub al-Sijistani. There must be substantial reasons behind this silence — reasons that justify it — and it may well be that the attribution of *al-Mahsul* to al-Nasafi is mistaken.

However that may be, the acclaim *al-Mahsul* won among Ismaili thinkers and the ranks of their scholars at that time spurred Abu Hatim al-Razi, the foremost preacher in northwestern Persia, to compose a book discussing what *al-Mahsul* contained. He named it *al-Islah* (The Correction). The opinions al-Razi advanced plainly provoked a reaction in another great preacher, Abu Yaqub al-Sijistani, who judged them contrary to truth and first principles. This led al-Sijistani to set about composing a book in which he challenged al-Razi's views. He named it *al-Nusra* (The Defense), and in it he upheld *al-Mahsul* and attacked Abu Hatim al-Razi without mercy. It appears that *al-Nusra* was among al-Sijistani's earliest works, as al-Kirmani hints in his own book. Not long afterward, al-Kirmani undertook a full survey of the whole subject and composed his precious treatise *al-Riyad* — a judgment between the two reformers, as he called them, that is, between the authors of

> الاصلاح والنصرة

*al-Islah* and *al-Nusra*. With surpassing skill he appraised the opinions advanced in all three books, subjecting them to rigorous scholarly criticism and measuring them against the

> قَانُونُ الدَّعْوَةِ الْهَادِيَةِ

Law of the Guiding Mission — or in other words, against the official philosophical and doctrinal positions of the Fatimid court of his day.

The scholarly ideas these works contain are also worth attending to. A philosophical body of this kind deserves to be studied, compared, and scrutinized deeply. By ill fortune, however, the two books —

> الْمَحْصُولُ وَالنُّصْرَةُ

*al-Mahsul* and *al-Nusra* — have been lost. *Al-Islah* by al-Razi and *al-Riyad* by al-Kirmani are still preserved.

## 2. The Two Rivals

To my mind, al-Kirmani's *al-Riyad* stands among the important works that give voice to the philosophical theories of Ismailism in the fourth century of the Hijra. In it he sits in judgment between the two rivals, the authors of الاصلاح والنصرة, weighing their opinions against the standard of the guiding mission, قَانُونُ الدَّعْوَةِ الْهَادِيَةِ. The controversy first grew out of al-Nasafi's book المحصول and al-Sijistani's reply to it. Those two books — الْمَحْصُولُ وَالنُّصْرَةُ — set the terms of the whole dispute, and it was al-Kirmani who finally brought that exchange into order. I have obtained two copies of *al-Riyad*, the first from India and the second from Tehran, and I was charged with editing the book, explaining it, and writing its footnotes and commentary. Before I take up its opinions, its arguments, and its statements in any detail, I must give a brief account of the lives of the three philosophers who took part in the discussion: Abu Hatim al-Razi, al-Sijistani, and al-Kirmani.

### Al-Razi

He is Abu Hatim Ahmad ibn Hamdan al-Laythi al-Warsanani al-Razi (ابو حاتم احمد بن حمدان الليفي الورسناني الرازي), whom the Ismailis name, in reverence, our master Abu Hatim (أَبُو حَاتِم). He was a preacher of the mission in the land of Rayy, then moved to Baghdad and made it his residence. The date of his birth is not known exactly. His activity in the mission fell during the imamate of Muhammad al-Mahdi Billah (مُحَمَّدُ الْمَهْدِيُّ بِاللَّهِ) (Ubayd Allah (عُبَيْدُ اللّٰه)), founder of the Fatimid state in the Maghrib. He was distinguished by the sweetness of his speech and by the eloquence for which he was famous. He won over al-Marwazi (الْمَرْوَزِي), the prince of Rayy — a strict Sunni — and pressed steadily on, persuading the princes of those regions to enter the mission. In time he became one of the pillars of both politics and religion, and a leading guide of the Islamic scholarly renaissance in the late third and early fourth centuries, having found his ground in the disciplines of theology and philosophy. The great orientalist Paul Kraus said of him:

> كان من كبار دعاة الاسماعيلية ولعب دوراً هاماً في الشؤون السياسية في طبرستان والديلم ولا سيما في الري واصفهان حتى استجاب له كبار رجال الدولة مثل اسفار بن شيرويه ومرداويخ القائد وغيرهما

He was a leading preacher of the Ismailis and played an important part in political affairs in Tabaristan and Daylam, and especially in Rayy and Isfahan, until the chief men of the state responded to him, men such as Asfar ibn Shirawayh (أَسْفَرُ بْنُ شِيرَوَيْهِ), Mardawij (مَرْدَاوِيجْ) the commander, and others.

In truth, Abu Hatim al-Razi was one of the pillars of the scholarly renaissance not within Ismailism alone but across the wider Islamic world. He helped to spread the philosophical teachings through all the eastern lands, and above all within the reach of the general Islamic culture. For all of this, he was not spared the persecution of his enemies in Daylam, and was driven into hiding in the last years of his life. He died in the year 322 of the Hijra. By then the Fatimid al-Qaim (الْقَائِم) had taken charge of the affairs of the Ismaili imamate in the Maghrib. Some historians say he lived sixty-two years.

He was a contemporary of Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi (أَبُو بَكْرٍ مُحَمَّدُ بْنُ زَكَرِيَّا الرَّازِيُّ), the famous physician, who held well-known philosophical views. Abu Bakr al-Razi (أَبُو بَكْرٍ الرَّازِي) broke with many of Aristotle's theories, both natural and metaphysical, denied any reconciliation between philosophy and religion, and held that philosophy was the only road to the reform of the individual and of society. Between the two men named al-Razi there were fierce and repeated disputations, attended by a number of scholars and political leaders. Abu Hatim set these debates down in his book *Alam al-Nubuwwa*, and though he never names the man he is refuting — calling him only "the atheist" — there is conclusive evidence that this atheist was none other than Abu Bakr al-Razi. The great philosopher al-Kirmani, Hujjat al-Iraqayn, records in his book *al-Aqwal al-Dhahabiyya* that debates over prophethood and the ritual duties of the Law took place between Abu Bakr al-Razi and Abu Hatim in the region of Rayy, in the days of Mardawij and in his presence. Abu Hatim, he writes, was able to humble Abu Bakr and to cast off his views in *Alam al-Nubuwwa* — in pages that overflow with charm and wonder, closing off against the arrogant and the stubborn every means of evasion and escape.

The most important of his works is the book *al-Zina*, on the Arabic letters, their derivations, the reasons behind their forms, and the meanings of words. It is a great work on both the native and borrowed Arabic vocabulary — the words through which the Quran was articulated, which the Muslims put to lasting use, and from which Islamic terminology took shape. This book runs to twelve hundred pages, and he dedicated it to the Fatimid imam who succeeded Muhammad al-Mahdi Billah (Ubayd Allah). To him belong also *Alam al-Nubuwwa*, *al-Islah*, *al-Jami*, and others.

### Al-Sijistani

Abu Yaqub Ishaq al-Sijistani (أَبُو يَعْقُوب اِسْحَاق اَلسِّجِسْتَانِي), also called al-Sijzi (اَلسِّجْزِي), stands at the forefront of those scholars who struggled, labored, and gave themselves wholly to establishing philosophical principles on cosmic and doctrinal grounds — principles built on solid intellectual foundations, pillars firm enough to hold against whatever crises might fall upon them. Indeed he is among those who sacrificed all they possessed to spread that teaching and to circulate it through other lands, until late in his life he was accused of unbelief and atheism by the conservative public, and was at last killed for the sake of his faith. Al-Sijistani played an important part in the field of philosophy and rendered the noblest intellectual services in the third century of the Hijra. His intellectual mark appeared in his disciple Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani (حَمِيد الدِّين أَحْمَد الْكِرْمَانِي), Hujjat al-Iraqayn, who walked in his path, called to his teachings, and drank from the overflow of his springs. Once we recognize that al-Kirmani studied philosophy in al-Sijistani's school of thought, we may place al-Sijistani in the first rank among the Muslim scholars, the thinkers, and the famous philosophers of the world.

He lived alongside the esoteric Ismaili mission in the Age of Manifestation — that is, during the flourishing of the Fatimid state and its emergence as an Islamic state with a body of civilization, of learning, of society, and of politics. Yet he lived in a country whose people held to a doctrine other than his own, and so he was compelled to take التَّقِيَّة as his cover, and to be most cautious in his movements and his preaching. For this reason his life has come down to us veiled in a certain obscurity, and we may not be blamed if we have not been able to reach a full knowledge of his biography or to lay hold of everything concerning it.

The same is true elsewhere. Of the great preacher al-Nasafi, al-Nakhshabi, author of *al-Mahsul*, only a little has reached us — what the authors mention of his efforts and his dealings with Nasr ibn Ahmad (نَصْرُ بْنُ أَحْمَدَ) the Samanid in Transoxiana, until that Samanid embraced the Ismaili mission. We know little, too, of Abu Hatim al-Razi, the linguist and author of the oldest work in the science of the Arabic language, the book *al-Zina*. And we know little of Hujjat al-Iraqayn Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani himself — this despite the survival of most of his writings, and despite the fact that al-Muayyad fil-Din Hibatallah al-Shirazi, the friend and disputant of Abu al-Ala al-Maarri (أَبُو الْعَلَاءِ الْمَعَرِّي), wrote his biography in his own hand. Of the senior men of the Ismaili mission — the حُجَّج and the preachers of the islands in Syria, Iran, Yemen, and elsewhere — we may say this: their lives are wrapped in the deepest obscurity, and the books in which they recorded their own biographies have been lost, with only a little remaining of them.

Al-Sijistani is traced to Sijistan (سِجِسْتَان), a province in the south of Khurasan, and to a Persian family. It is said that his was the family of the Persian hero Rustam (رُسْتَم), while others say he was of Iraqi origin, that his grandfather came from Kufa and settled in Sijistan. Some researchers who have studied his philosophy place his death in the year 331 of the Hijra, but this cannot be reconciled with the historical record. It is known that al-Sijistani was al-Kirmani's teacher, and al-Kirmani remained alive until the year 411. At what time, then, did al-Kirmani take from him the philosophical sciences of the mission? Al-Sijistani himself states in his book *al-Iftikhar* that he composed it in the year 360. Since *al-Iftikhar* is in turn cited in al-Kirmani's *al-Riyad*, al-Sijistani must have been an active preacher in the province of Bukhara during the caliphate of the imam al-Muizz li-Din Allah al-Fatimi (الْمُعِزُّ الدِّينِ الْفَاطِمِيُّ). This means he was a contemporary of the great preacher Jafar ibn Mansur al-Yamani (جَعْفَرُ بْنُ مَنْصُورِ الْيَمَنِيِّ), and of the learned jurist al-Numan ibn Muhammad ibn Hayyun al-Maghribi al-Tamimi (اَلنُّعْمَانُ بْنُ مُحَمَّدٍ بْنِ حَيُّونٍ اَلْمَغْرِبِيُّ اَلتَّمِيمِيُّ) (*al-Numan ibn Muhammad ibn Hayyun al-Maghribi al-Tamimi*) — the اَلْقَاضِي and chief judge of the Fatimid state — along with other great scholars and authors of the mission in that golden and radiant age of learning.

There is no better proof of al-Sijistani's scholarly worth than the books he left behind him. He left, as we have said, a rich body of scholarly writing reckoned among the deepest ever written in philosophy, numbering more than thirty titles. Perhaps the most famous of his books are *Ithbat al-Nubuwwat*, *Kashf al-Mahjub*, *Tuhfat al-Mustajibin*, and *al-Yanabi*. This last he divided into forty springs, and it came to rank among the books whose depth is rarely surpassed. He seems to have composed it for a particular class of readers — the preachers and those of the highest ranks in the mission, and those whose philosophical studies had reached their summit. His reason for dividing it into forty springs goes back to his wish that each spring should carry the interpretation belonging to one of the forty limits who make up the supreme council of the mission.

Among his other books, which the great traveler and famous Persian poet Nasir-i Khusraw (نَاصِرِي خُسْرَو) named in his *Zad al-Musafirin*, are these: *The Foundations of the Dawa*; *The Interpretation of the Laws*; *The Iris of Blessings*, also called *The Iris of Survival*; *The Brilliant Message*; *Kitab al-Iftikhar*; *The Scales*, which is divided into nineteen scales; *The Ladder of Salvation*; *al-Nusra*, *The Victory*; *The Hooks*; *Amusements of Sorrows*; *Secrets of the Return*; *Sermons on Ethics*; *The Stranger on the Meaning of the Elixir*; *Sociable of Hearts*; *Composition of Spirits*; *Security from Confusion*; *Closets of Evidence*; *The Proof*; and others besides.

### Al-Sijistani on the divine

In these lines I set down in brief al-Sijistani's views on the divine, as he expressed them across most of his writings. He holds that the Originator of all originated things is an Ancient Creator — Exalted and firmly rooted in the bringing forth of primacy — and that the world of beings and originated things is itself brought into being. Were it otherwise, something prior to the world would have had to bring it into being. Were the world ancient and prior to the Creator, it would be impossible for His might to attach itself to that which is co-eternal, or for His being to rest upon nothingness, and it would then require another to bring it into being.

He is exalted above the grasp of the attributes, so that He is not reached by any sense, does not fall under any gaze, is not perceived by sight, is not defined by any genus, does not enter into conjecture, and is not seen by the eyes. He is not described by the senses, nor grasped by analogy, nor likened to men. He is set apart from any contrary that would stand against Him, any equal that would match Him, any likeness of anything. He is exalted above the likeness of things that are bounded. The imaginations are bewildered before the character of His might, the understandings fall short of the description of His kingdom, and the eyes are dulled from grasping His greatness. He has no equal and no likeness; He is without rival and without opposite, for an opposite would be a contrary that stands against Him.

He has pointed to His own identity through His creation and His works, and to His names through His prophets. The intellect, therefore, has no field and no likeness by which to attain His heavens — for to place the created thing alongside its Originator is impossible. He is the cause of every existent, for He is the Originator of the originated and the Inventor of the invented, the cause of the being of all beings, the Lord of every thing, its Creator, its Perfecter, and the One who carries it to the best of its states. He is too exalted for any thought to bound Him or any measure to encompass Him.

He has no names, for names were coined for the things He brought into being. He has no attributes, for attributes belong to essences. And the letters of language cannot arrive at the pronouncing of His name, nor be applied to Him in any part, since they are all among the things He invented. All the names He created He made names for the things He originated.

He is Ancient, before pre-eternity, the Owner of the source of primacy in the order of being. From Him the first limit burst forth and the first existent overflowed, while He remains the Originator of the originated, the Cause of causes, the Maker of created things, the Perpetual Existent by His own singleness and His self-sufficiency, the Owner of the first act of bringing into being. He is the first Number, which He made the origin of numbers, just as He made the Intellect the origin of existing things and the Articulator the origin of the world of religion. To all of this is added: He is not reached by any attribute, He is neither a body nor contained within one, no reasoning can reason out His essence, and no sense can sense Him. He is not an image and not a matter, and there is nothing in the languages by which He may be expressed. He exists — yet it is not right to say that He exists after the manner of the existents that have come to be from Him. The proof of Him is drawn from the existence of other existents: there is no effect without a cause, and nothing exists except through what makes its existence necessary. The existents lean one upon another in their being, and that on which some of them lean is itself, among the existents, not fixed in being and not existent of itself.

### The order of the existents

After this al-Sijistani passes to the existents in their order and their sequence, and says:

The Originator brought nothing into being at the first of creation except the Intellect, and He bounded within its essence the forms of all the originated things. To the Intellect is given the name of the Pen, because by the pen the inscriptions of creation appear from the very beginning. It is called the Throne — that is, a seat for the One who is seated upon it — and by His being seated upon it His majesty is known, above whoever is beneath Him. It is called the First, meaning that among all created things, all that exists and all upon which a stamp is set, the Intellect is the earliest to receive the marks of wisdom before the remaining limits. This is by virtue of its nearness to that source and its union with it — knowledge and command taken in a single sense. It may therefore be said that the act of the Intellect precedes its power, an excellence found in no other existent, for all the limits below it have their powers preceding their acts. This excellence belongs to the Intellect alone, and by it the Intellect is whole and complete. The Intellect is called the Decree, in that by the Intellect the soul achieves the grasp of what is known and the attainment of what is sought. It may be said that the Intellect is the decree of God, mighty and majestic, among His creation. The Intellect is likewise called the اَلْهَيُولَى, prime matter, meaning that by the Intellect there is a subsisting of what wells up of the forms; and it is called the Sun, meaning that by the Intellect the realities are seen.

Then comes the Soul, which is the second creation, welling up out of the first. It is named "Soul" (نَفْس) only because it breathes (يَتَنَفَّس) continually to draw benefit, so that the steady succession of its breathing is the subsisting of creation. The Soul is called the Tablet, meaning that what is loosed from the Intellect — the lights of wisdom — is inscribed upon the Soul, and from the Soul it reaches, by its outflow, whatever pours forth from it, in the measure of its purity and refinement. The Soul is called the King, and the meaning of this is that the Soul is the property of the Intellect and its handmaid, for by the Soul the excellence of the Intellect appears, just as by the servant the excellence of the sovereign appears. The Soul is called the Follower, meaning that it is the Soul which follows the Intellect in receiving the marks of the Word. It may be said that the Soul, by its power, follows after the Intellect by its act. The Soul is called Destiny, meaning that what unites with the Soul of the benefits of the Intellect has measure and limit encompassing it. The Soul is called the Image, and the meaning of this is that the Soul was conceived from the very essence of the Intellect and its radiance — and that whenever it resolves to overtake the Intellect and descend into its station, its light is effaced. This is as the moon draws its light from the sun: when the two meet at one station, the moon's light is effaced. Of the Intellect and the Soul together it is said, in a single word, that they are the Two Principles (الْاَصْلَان).

These are some of al-Sijistani's philosophical views on the divine, which I have set out in brief and joined to what I have been able to recover of his biography. Perhaps they give compelling proof of his firm command of the sciences and of his deep skill in laying out their texts in a pleasing order and an exquisite arrangement.

### Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani

He is an extraordinary scholarly figure whose life-history is enveloped in obscurity — a great philosopher who lived in a radiant scholarly age, a noble preacher who traced across the pages of thought the soundest inquiries and the deepest lines, and who left to the generations a number of works of which the least that can be said is that they are a precious treasure and an enduring heritage. The philosophers of the Islamic world reckon him the greatest scholar the Ismaili school produced.

## 3. The Judge and His Cosmos

The great preacher and Yemeni historian Idris Imad al-Din mentioned him in his book, saying:

> "هُوَ أَسَاسُ الدَّعْوَةِ الَّتِي عَلَيْهِ عِمَادُهَا وَبِهِ عَلَا وَاسْتَقَامَ مَنَارُهَا وَبِهِ اسْتَبَانَتِ الْمُشْكِلَاتُ وَانْفَرَجَتِ الْمُعْضِلَاتُ"
>
> "He is the foundation of the دَعْوَة upon which it rests and by which it is raised up; through him its beacon was set straight, and by him problems were identified and dilemmas resolved."

The Syrian Ismaili preacher Nur al-Din Ahmad (نُورُ الدِّينِ اَحْمَد) described him in his book Chapters and Reports, saying:

> "لو ان الدعوة الاسماعيلية لم تنتج غير الكرماني لكفاها فخراً ومجداً ولكان ذلك كافياً"
>
> "If the Ismaili dawa had produced nothing but al-Kirmani, that alone would have been ample pride and glory, and that alone would have been enough."

His impact and stature came into full view during the reign of the Fatimid Caliph al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah (اَلْحَاكِمُ بِأَمْرِ اَللّٰه). His famous title was *Hujjat al-Iraqayn* — the حُجَّة of the Two Iraqs — meaning that he oversaw the affairs of the dawa's cultural centers throughout Persia and Iraq. In Cairo, he held the rank of حُجَّة, one of twelve hujjahs charged with managing the dawa's affairs and constituting the intellectual vanguard of the world. He was later appointed head of *Dar al-Hikma* — the House of Wisdom — in Cairo, the cultural institution we may rightly call the first university established in the world.

He traveled in a delegation to Cairo in 408 AH at the request of al-Sadiq al-Mamun (اَلصَّاْدِق اَلْمَأْمُوْن), then the foremost of the Fatimid state's preachers in the era of al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah. The moment was charged: religious battles were intensifying, and new movements were flooding the market of innovation with calls toward exaggeration and deviation from the foundations of the faith. He delivered talks and lectures at *Dar al-Hikma* and produced a great many researches and books. The most famous of these is the Preacher's Message, composed in reply to al-Hasan al-Farghani (الْحَسَنُ الْفَرْغَانِي), who held that the Ruler al-Hakim was divine by the command of God. Among his other works are the epistles known as the Good Tidings and the Lamps. In these he proved the Imamate as a necessary cosmic reality through reasoned, compelling arguments — arguments richly furnished with Hebrew, Syriac, and Persian expressions drawn from the books of the prophets of the heavenly utterance. He then took up the Platonic opinions as the foundation of his researches and deployed them with a skill no one before him had brought to bear. All of this served as a general call to consolidate the philosophical and intellectual system, to raise its level, and to erase every trace of doubt, controversy, suspicion, and dispute.

In these pages, I will not present the philosopher al-Kirmani merely as one of the Ismaili preachers who played an important role in the world of thought during the reign of al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah, nor merely as one of the philosophers who served the Fatimid دَعْوَة with clear intellectual services. I present him, rather, as a philosopher among the philosophers of the world — one who walked the stage of universal philosophy and gave everything he had to founding a philosophical school upon a new intellectual foundation. That foundation was drawn from the modern, innovative theories of his time and from the mental systems then in circulation. This applies especially to the theory of the unity of being, which the Second Teacher, al-Farabi (اَلْفَارَابِي), and the Chief Sheikh, Ibn Sina, both uphold. Al-Kirmani expanded it considerably in exposition, departing somewhat from the general curriculum laid down for the Ismaili preachers — whether those who came before him or those of his own age — who were bound to follow a well-defined general framework whose limits they could not cross.

Returning to al-Kirmani's writings and reading them with careful attention, I find that he resolved that there is contradiction, disharmony, and an attempt on the part of some existents to erase others — and yet that these existents persist in spite of this dissonance and contradiction. None of them is lost by the presence of an opposite; all are preserved under existence. This is identical to the Second Teacher's theory on creation, in which he says:

> "حفظ ادامة وجود الشيء الذي ليس وجوداً لذاته ادامة لا تتصل بشيء من العلل غير ذات المبدع"
>
> "The preservation of the continuance of the existence of a thing that does not possess, in itself, a permanence unrelated to any of the contingents, other than the Creator."

And Ibn Sina, in speaking of the possible and the necessary among the other existents, gives an account that clarifies the meaning of the continuity of existence among existents, saying:

> "أَمَّا كَوْنَ الْعِلَلِ مُمْكِنَ الْوُجُودِ فِي نَفْسِهِ وَاجِبَ الْوُجُودِ لِغَيْرِهِ فَلَيْسَ يُنَاقِضُ كَوْنَهُ دَائِمَ الْوُجُودِ بِغَيْرِهِ"
>
> "As for the fact that the contingents can exist in themselves, while not being obligated by the existence of others, this does not contradict their being always present in others."

What these three philosophers — the Second Teacher, the Chief Sheikh, and the حُجَّة of the Two Iraqs — arrived at in this regard closely approaches what the philosopher Descartes said in the modern era:

> "اذ يوجد من الفعل الذي يحفظ الله به العالم وبين الفعل الذي خلقه به"
>
> "There is, between the act by which God preserves the world and the act by which He created it, no real difference."

This is what is known in his philosophy as the theory of continuous creation.

Al-Kirmani shared certain things with the Muslim philosophers and was influenced by some of them, especially those who engaged with Greek philosophy. What distinguishes him from them, however, is that when he presented these philosophies, he stamped them with the marks of novelty, wit, and innovation, and proved to be the precursor of the modern European philosophers and the ancient Oriental renewers. I can therefore say that al-Kirmani left behind books and produced ideas that should be seen not only as Ismaili intellectual views, but as Islamic philosophical views of a high, public-facing order — looking into the essence of things and into the deep intellectual dimensions of existence in a steady, elaborated manner in which genius and brilliance are manifest.

Yes, al-Kirmani worked within the Neoplatonic systems and devoted all his efforts to applying some of their materials in his attractive philosophical and rhetorical style. He worked to prove Prophethood and the Imamate from both the philosophical and the religious sides. On this point I can say that he upheld the theory that adherence to the outward Shariah leads to scriptural worship, while the further reach of philosophical interpretation and discussion of the boundaries (al-hudud, الحدود) is reserved for those permitted ones who have attained a high level in philosophy and science. I find all these features when I look closely at *The Illuminating Message*, *The Lamps in Proving the Imamate*, *Alerting the Guide and the Guided*, and *Rest of the Mind*. Beyond this, al-Kirmani is a major asset in developing the intellectual system and expanding theoretical discourse. He added new elements, broadened certain topics, laid down the basic rules of interpretation, and addressed contradiction and similarity — especially on the subject of the Imamate, which had been the focus of unstable and shifting opinions and theories. For this reason his book *Rest of the Mind* defined the rules, origins, ranks, and status of the Imamate and set new foundations, systems, and order for it.

Al-Kirmani was prominent in the doctrine of the call to existence, in the negation of essence (al-aysiyya, الايسية) and substance (al-laysiyya, الليسية), in the doctrine that the divine attributes are subject to absolute negation, in the doctrine of the call to التَّوْحِيد, in the doctrine of the call concerning the two principles of origination, in the elaboration of emanation, and in the question of priority between the Imamate and the Prophethood — all treated in a purely scientific, logical manner. He did not stop there. At the outset he gathered the two first principles, the Active Intellect and the Universal Soul, together with the system of the ten Platonic intellects upheld by the Second Teacher, al-Farabi. He set the world of origination, the world of intellects, the spiritual world, and the corporeal celestial world — the world of stars and planets — alongside the physical world below the lunar orbit and the world of religion, the world of the boundaries of religion. He then drew geographical and astronomical diagrams for it, along with terrestrial and corporeal plans that came out highly artistic and creative, showing his ingenuity of expression and his eminence in philosophy.

On returning to *Rest of the Mind* and reviewing what it contains, I see that it is full of formulations and arguments concerning the apostolic statements about God and the negation of the divine attributes. Among the positions he stated and supported: that no attribute attaches to Allah; that He is not a body nor in a body; that He neither rationalizes Himself nor senses Himself; that He is not in a form or in a substance; that He has no opposite and no likeness; that no language can express Him; and that He has no rank among existents. This indicates the breadth of his knowledge across the various sciences, especially the rational ones. On this the Mutazila and the theologians (mutakallimun) agree, and he agrees with Ibn Rushd. On the argument for the existence of God, I see him agreeing with Descartes, even if they differ in the details and the method of expression. Examining his solutions and ideas a little further, I see that he folded into them some of the views of philosophers who preceded him and of his contemporaries — Plato, Aristotle, al-Kindi (اَلْكِنْدِي), al-Farabi, and Ibn Sina. I note from al-Baqillani (اَلْبَاقِلَّانِي), al-Baghdadi (اَلْبَغْدَادِيُّ), and al-Ghazali (اَلْغَزَالِي) that they ruled the Ismailis, by their denial of the attributes, to have fallen into invalidity (al-tatil, التعطيل). But al-Kirmani responded to this charge, saying that outright denial is to direct the particle of negation "لا" against the identity itself — as in saying "لَا هُو" (He is not) or "لا اله" (there is no god). This is not what the Ismailis say. Their negation is the negation of the attributes alone; the negative particle "لا" for them is directed toward the attributes, leaving the identity untouched.

However the matter stands, al-Kirmani gave special attention to this subject in *Rest of the Mind*. And in this book I see him departing from all philosophers, writers, historians, and scientists: instead of dividing his book into the usual books, chapters, and sections that other thinkers and authors employ, he likens his book to a city encircled by seven walls. Whoever enters it must pass through the seven walls, and to pass through them he must face seven gates (مَشَارِع) branching off from each wall — except the seventh wall. As for the seventh and last wall encircling the city, it has fourteen gates. I will not enter here into an exposition of what al-Kirmani meant by his walls and gates, for fear of going on at length; but this does not stop me from affirming his superiority in philosophy, the power of his thinking, and the depth of his grasp of the essence of things.

Al-Kirmani published a number of works, the most important of which include: *The Divine Treatise*, *The Treatise of the Systems*, *The Treatise of Light*, *The Illuminating Treatise*, *The Necessary Treatise*, *The Guiding Treatise*, *The Preacher's Treatise*, *The Sufficient Treatise*, *Alerting the Guide and the Guided*, *Wrists of the Guide*, *The Golden Sayings*, *Separating the Discourse and Distinguishing the Manifest Truth from Doubt*, *The Treatise of the Returner*, *The Treatise of the Index*, *Quantities and Facts*, *The Treatise on Tawhid in the Promised Land*, *The Crown of Intellects*, *The Balance of the Mind*, *The Book of Criticism and Obligation*, *Psychological Adequacy*, *The Book of Scales*, *The Baghdadi and Basran Sessions*, *al-Shaari's Treatise on the Elite*, *Rest of the Mind*, and *The Treatise of the Week of the Role of the Cover*.

Finally, I must say that al-Kirmani is among the philosophers who weigh heavily on our philosophical world. The study of his writings and his output is of great importance. It gives a clear picture of the impact of philosophy on the history of thought for those interested in Oriental Studies and Islamic Philosophy.

## 4. Why I Wrote This Book

Upon Him is my reliance.

> بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ

Praise be to Allah, Lord of fathers and mothers, Creator of animal and plant, to whom all that is in the earth and the heavens gives praise, and who in His bounty bestows His blessings upon His creation. Nothing that exists has existence except by His command, and nothing that is has being except by His decree and His measure. His decree is firm, so that minds cannot deny it; it endures without change. Glory be to Him: to His might all greatness and splendor return, by His command favors and bounties overflow, and by His leave the earth and the heavens stand. The misguided who liken Him to His creation are lost and have gone far astray.

I praise Him, I thank Him, I believe in Him and do not disbelieve in Him. I bear witness that there is no god but Allah, the true Object of worship, who originated all things in the difference of their essences — the simple, which possesses splendor and majesty, and the composed, which possesses order and perfection. Nothing among them resembles Him, and He is exalted above them in every respect. And I bear witness that the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful, the Gentle, the Generous, sent Muhammad — His chosen and pure servant, His righteous and God-fearing Messenger.

He sent him at a time when idols were worshipped in Allah's sanctuary, when the landmarks of the upright اَلْحَنِيْفِي religion had been laid low, when the fire of disbelief and atheism blazed and the edifices of polytheism and idolatry stood high, and when the people had sunk deep into the wastes of misguidance and plunged blindly into the dust of affliction. So he rose up for Allah, striving and contending on their behalf. He stripped away the confusion and darkness that had overshadowed them, shattered the idols and images they had been worshipping, and began to call them to the worship of the Most Merciful and to the rites of faith and Islam. He replaced the houses of fire with the Sacred House of Allah, abrogated the rule of the crosses by turning toward the Corner (al-Rukn (رُكْن)) and the Station (al-Maqam), guided the servants of Allah in His worship onto the straight path, saved them from the claws of the seducing devil, and brought them by his blessing to the most trustworthy handhold (اَلْعُرْوَةُ الْوُثْقَى). He led them to the courtyards of right guidance and God-fearing, until the word of التَّوْحِيد rose shining and the word of الشِّرْك was abased and darkened, until tongues spoke the clear verses, and the winds drove the ships of salvation across the seas of dispute. So the decree of Allah the Exalted came to pass in what He willed for the trial of the people of opinions and religions.

May Allah bless him with a blessing that reaches, through him, his followers in general and the company of the callers in particular, so that He may make easy for them the way of his intercession and fulfill for them the right of following him and obeying him, upon

> يَوْمَ لَا يَنْفَعُ مَالٌ وَلَا بَنُونَ ، إِلَّا مَنْ أَتَى اللَّهَ بِقَلْبٍ سَلِيمٍ

a day on which neither wealth nor children will be of any use, except for him who comes to Allah with a sound heart.

And may He bless the Axis of the Religion and the pillar of the believers — the receiver of his lights and the one who upholds the cause of his followers and his helpers, the restrainer of anger in fulfillment of the covenant of Allah the Exalted, the one patient under the humiliation of usurpation in seeking the face of Allah the Exalted: the Commander of the Believers, Ali bin Abi Talib (عليه السلام), whose deeds cannot be numbered and whose virtues cannot be counted. And may He bless his progeny, the blessed Imams, by whose lights souls are made to endure, by whose following calamities are lifted, by whose obedience hopes are attained, and by whose intercession perfection is reached. And may He bless the Imam who rules by the command of Allah, the Commander of the Believers, and his forefathers, the radiant and rightly guided, the family of Taha (طَه) and Yasin (يَاسِين) (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ).

To proceed: the most deserving matter to which one who affirms the divine unity should direct his care and resolve is a matter that leads, in the affirmation of the unity of Allah the Exalted and the knowledge of His limits, to a settled order of belief for those of certainty; that calls toward the removal of confusion and darkness from the souls of the believers; and that opens the way to affirming the unity of the Lord of the Worlds.

I saw that Shaykh Abu Hatim al-Razi, may Allah have mercy on him, had undertaken to correct in the book al-Mahsul what he held to have been corrupted, and that Shaykh Abu Yaqub al-Sijzi (أَبَا يَعْقُوبَ السِّجْزِي), may Allah have mercy on him, had come to the defense of that book's author and stood as a witness to the soundness of his statement. I recognized that what the two of them disputed was not among the branches in which disagreement is permissible while the soundness of the principles remains intact. I found that Shaykh Abu Yaqub al-Sijzi was truthful, in some of what he brought forward, in the aim he intended by his refutation, yet in some of it prejudiced against Shaykh Abu Hatim; and I found that some of the words of both of them were issued without proper order. Beyond all this, the book al-Mahsul contained — in the author's chapter on التَّوْحِيد, concerning the First Intellect in particular and apart from the branches — matters it was incumbent upon Shaykh Abu Hatim to correct and address, matters he was more entitled to treat than the long passages on branches with which he lengthened his book, and yet he left them and neglected them. All of this together weighed upon the people of the Guiding دَعْوَة, so that whenever they stood upon a point of difference they were drawn into disagreement in the paths of التَّوْحِيد and into disorder in the knowledge of the limits.

I therefore resolved to set down the sayings of each of the two: those of the first in the book al-Islah — that is, "The Reform" — and those of the second in the book al-Nusra — "The Support" — each by its own text. Then I would speak upon what necessarily follows from them, what is intended by them, and the aspect by which their meanings may rightly be attributed to their authors, so that the truth of the one may be distinguished from the other, the prejudiced and the transgressor between them made plain, and the meaning of what they differed over made clear, so that a man may hold his belief accordingly. Then I would speak upon what was neglected in the book al-Mahsul by way of correction among the foundations in which disagreement is not permissible, and I would make the truth plain, seeking reward.

I did this, and gathered all of it in this book, naming it the book al-Riyad — "The Gardens" — as a ruling between the two: the author of al-Islah and the author of al-Nusra. For in what it gathers of their sayings and in what it sets forth, it is a division between them, a clarification of the error they persisted in and of what they neglected to correct in the book al-Mahsul, and a discharge of what is due therein, out of what we have gained of the blessings of the أَوْلِيَاء of Allah (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ). I named it so because it is like gardens: a greenery pleasing to the eye, and a fruit that comes into the hands. In Allah we seek help. In bringing forth this matter I have sought to be kept from the marks of error, though we are not free of it.

The book comprises ten chapters, and together they contain one hundred and fifty-seven sub-chapters. And Allah is the Helper.

> وَهُوَ حَسْبُنَا وَنِعْمَ الْوَكِيلُ

He is sufficient for us, and the best Trustee.

## 5. The Perfect and Perfection

This is the first chapter, from the book *al-Riyad*, concerning the ruling between the two disputants, in the chapter on what was discussed regarding the soul, which is the first emanation.

### Section One

The author of *al-Islah* held that the soul is complete in itself, having been emitted from the First Intellect complete. It is a complete emanation from what is complete, for the Intellect is perfection.

The author of *al-Nusra* replied in refutation: the author of *al-Islah* does not recognize that perfection is better and more complete than the perfect. Perfection is an attribute, while the perfect is what is described. Perfection is what is borne, while the perfect is the bearer. What is described — the bearer — is better and more complete than the attribute and than the borne. For the perfect is described by perfection and bears it, while perfection is merely the attribute of the perfect, predicated upon it. This is what they say.

We say: when the author of *al-Islah* says that the soul is complete because it is emitted from perfection, he does not mean that perfection is an attribute or is not one, nor that the perfect is described or is not. What he means is that perfection stands as a cause from which the existence of the effect arises. He saw that perfection is the cause of the perfect and precedes it in rank, for without perfection the perfect would not exist. The proof is that if we remove perfection in the imagination, the perfect is removed with it — just as with the source-forms from which the past act, the future, the agent, and the object are all derived: remove those source-forms, and whatever is derived from them by conjugation is removed along with them. He therefore treated the First Intellect — first in existence, and the source from which all existents came to be — as the cause, and said: it is perfection. He treated the emanating soul as the effect, and said: it is the perfect. Since this was the author of *al-Islah*'s intention, his statement that the First Intellect is perfection is true. And since it is true, what the author of *al-Nusra* said in this manner was an overreach against the author of *al-Islah*.

### Section Two

The author of *al-Nusra* holds that the perfect is better than perfection. Yet the discourse here — about perfection and the perfect — concerns the First Principle, which is the First Effect and the First Emanation, not any other thing. His claim therefore entails that nobility belongs to the effect and not to its cause. The proof: the perfect is perfect only by means of perfection, and without perfection it would not be perfect. Whatever a thing's being depends on is its cause. Since the perfect's existence is by perfection, perfection is the cause of the perfect, and the perfect is the effect of perfection. If the perfect is the effect of perfection, and the author of *al-Nusra* assigns nobility and merit to the perfect over perfection, then by that assignment he has made it necessary that the effect be nobler than its cause — and that is impossible. For whatever comes to exist through the existence of something, such that when that something is removed it ceases to exist, is below that something in nobility and precedence. And since that is impossible, the author of *al-Nusra* in what he brought forward was overreaching against the author of *al-Islah*.

### Section Three

The author of *al-Nusra* said, secondly: perfection is an attribute and the perfect is what is described; perfection is what is borne and the perfect is the bearer. He offered this as proof of his claim that the perfect is nobler than perfection, and that the bearer is nobler than the borne.

We say: this does not hold without qualification. From the side of existing beings and the second emanation, we find instances in which what is borne is nobler than its bearer. The nutritive power is the proximate cause of the existence of the growth power and is its bearer, while the growth power is a form borne within it and its completion — and the growth power is nobler than the nutritive. The growth power is the proximate cause of the existence of the sensitive power and is its bearer, while the sensitive power is a form borne within it and its completion — and the sensitive is nobler than it. The sensitive power is the proximate cause of the existence of the speaking power and is its bearer, while the speaking power is a form borne within it and its completion. The nobility of the one is not like the nobility of the other.

Moreover, from the side of origination and the first emanation, it is impossible that the First Intellect be what is borne and the Second Intellect emanating from it be the bearer, or that the perfect — which is the First Intellect — be an attribute of the perfect that is the Second Intellect. This being so, the author of *al-Nusra*'s inference in his refutation was an overreach against the author of *al-Islah*. The meanings of his statement would hold true only if applied to natural things, which are far removed from what the author of *al-Islah* speaks of, namely the originative and emanative principles.

The statement of the author of *al-Islah* is correct with regard to the world of origination and emanation. The statement of the author of *al-Nusra* — though the author of *al-Islah* erred with respect to it — is correct with regard to the world of bodies and their accidents.

### Section Four

We say, reflecting on this: perfection is a name that applies to the First Thing, which is the First Cause; and it is also the First Perfect, which is the First Effect. Its perfection comes from the relation of its existence to that from which its existence came, and its perfection comes from its relation to that upon which its essence rests — and this is analogous to origination and the Originator in their proper order. Origination is not something other than the Originator; it is that by which the Originator is itself. What, then, is it? It is a single essence: considered in relation to that from which its existence came, it is origination; considered in relation to that upon which its essence rests, bearing the mark of temporal occurrence, it is originated. Thus the First Intellect is perfection, and it is also the perfect in truth. It deserves to be perfect only because existence of its own kind is precluded outside it. The perfect thing is that outside which there is nothing like it in its species — as with the existents in natural things, and all the more so in origination. The body too is a name applying to something perfect in its three dimensions, such that nothing outside it is like it; whatever is like it is from it, and is counted as part of it. The sun is likewise perfect: nothing exists outside it that is like it in its species.

In sum, the existence of what lies below the First Intellect, in relation to it, is a deficient existence. Yet that deficiency is not in its essence but in its rank — in relation to what is above it. All things are perfect in their essences, since their existence comes from the perfection that is the First Intellect, and there is no deficiency in them. Were they deficient in their essences, their existence from perfection would be impossible; and it would likewise be impossible for anything to come forth from what is deficient, since the deficient cannot even complete its own essence, let alone bring something else into existence from it. Deficiency attaches to things only when they are considered in relation to what is above them in rank, not in their essences. The essence, insofar as essences belong to a single thing, is like numbers: numbers are perfect in their essences, and their deficiency arises only from their relation to others by which their existence is. Likewise, all the existents of the world are perfect — with no deficiency in them insofar as they are bodies — and deficient only in respect of their ranks among forms, which differ when some forms are nobler than others. The human being is the noblest of all forms: perfect in his essence like all the others, and deficient only in relation to what is above him. And if the matter is thus in bodily things, then what is not a body follows the same order in respect of rank — like for like.

For this reason Allah, exalted, said:

> سَنُرِيهِمْ آيَتِنَا فِى ٱلأٓفَاقِ وَفِىٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَهُمْ أَنَّهُ ٱلْحَقُّ
>
> "We shall show them Our signs in the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth."

That is: We shall establish for them the Imams and the limits, in the manifest and the hidden, that they may be guided to what the state of the observable bodies is, and of the souls whose actions exist — upon what had been hidden from them of the knowledge of the limits of Allah, which are the truth — so that the truth of the form may become clear to them.

### Section Five

The author of *al-Islah* said: what stands in need of perfection is the act of the soul, not the essence of the soul, because the soul's act is completed only in time. The emanation of the soul is with time, just as the First Intellect and time are one, for the First Originator, origination, time, and perfection are one existent. The Creator originated all originated things at once. The soul's need to derive benefit from the First is not a deficiency in its essence — just as the First was not deficient though it stood in need of the command. Completion and deficiency appear in the act of the soul, not in its essence.

The author of *al-Nusra* said: he did not recognize that the Intellect does not stand in need of anything in the way the soul needs to derive benefit from the Intellect. The state of the Intellect and matter is not like the state of the soul and the Intellect, for matter, from the side of the Intellect, is nothing other than the Intellect itself — their identity is one. It is impossible to say that a thing stands in need of what it itself is; the need of a thing for another thing arises only when they are other than each other. This is what they say.

We say: the statement of the author of *al-Nusra* is a reply to what the author of *al-Islah* maintained regarding the Intellect's need for the command only. Since the discourse concerned the principles that come out of the natural world, the statement of the author of *al-Nusra* on this point is closer to the truth than that of the author of *al-Islah*. What may properly be said to stand in need of perfection — completed only in time, because it is an emanation from the natural world and not from outside it — is the souls that attain the degree of perfection, that is, the ascent to the degree of the second perfection, only in time, and whose existence is an emanative existence. If the statement of the author of *al-Islah* is understood in this light, it is correct.

### Section Six

The statement of the author of *al-Islah* — that the Intellect and time are one, because the Originator, origination, time, and perfection are one existent — is not correct. Time is the number of the motions of the degrees of the celestial sphere; it is the imagined duration between the motion of the first degree and the motion of the following degree to where the first had been. Origination, which is the Intellect, and perfection — their existence precedes the sphere by whose degrees' motions the existence of time comes to be. To attach the existence of time to the existence of the First Intellect, and to hold that belief, contradicts what the law of the guiding دَعْوَة rests upon. We have explained the coming-to-be of time, what precedes it in existence, and the indication of this from the ordinances of the prophetic Law in our book known as رَاحَةُ الْعَقْلِ (Peace of Mind), in terms sufficient for those who seek.

### Section Seven

The author of *al-Nusra* said: were it not for the Intellect's denial of divinity from itself, the Following one would not have been able to carry its knowledge beyond the rank of the Intellect to the Originator. What deficiency could be more evident than this — that it knows the Originator only by way of the First?

We say: that may not be believed of the Following one. The power flowing through all the noble existents in the world of unity gives them knowledge that they have an Originator. Were it not for that power, nothing would have existence or subsistence, and all would vanish away. For this reason every thing deserves the name of oneness: though it be multiple in its essence, its share of that power is unseen, while the justice in it is evident. It is impossible to conceive of a single one of the human kind not knowing that it has a Creator, or imagining that it has no Creator — let alone that this should be thought of that great and majestic Limit. We say rather: it apprehends that from which its existence came, knowing it as the True Object of worship, even if there be a god within it. If we then apply to the First Intellect what the author of *al-Nusra* mentioned, and to the Following one what he said of it, it would be obligatory that the Asas — whose standing is in the world of body — stand in the place of the Following one in the world of Intellect, since without the Speaker one would not know one has a god. But the matter is not so, for each of the two has a portion from Allah, and the Speaker precedes it in rank only by his nearness to the First and his coming after it. For this reason Allah said:

> لَا الشَّمْسُ يَنْبَغِي لَهَا أَنْ تُدْرِكَ الْقَمَرَ وَلَا اللَّيْلُ سَابِقُ النَّهَارِ وَكُلٌّ فِي فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ
>
> "It is not for the sun to overtake the moon, nor does the night outstrip the day; and each is swimming in an orbit."

All, from the side of movement, are one; and by ranks they differ.

### Section Eight

The author of *al-Nusra* said: and enough for you, as proof of deficiency, is that all the ancients placed the soul in the station of the form, and the Intellect in the station of prime matter and material, saying: forms ever yearn and long for materials, and materials have no subsistence without forms.

We say: his statement that the soul is a form is true. But his statement that the Intellect is in the station of prime matter and material — since the discourse was about the First Intellect — was an overreach against the author of *al-Islah*, addressing him with terms that do not apply to his subject. For "Intellect" is a shared name, and what the author of *al-Nusra* said does not hold for the separate originative and first emanative intellects; it is correct only concerning the other intellects that exist within the natural world — the intellect in potency and the intellect in act. Since the discourse was about what is separate, to apply to it what is said of natural things and to use that as a basis for contradiction was an overreach.

His statement that forms ever yearn and long for materials, and that materials have no subsistence without forms, is also incorrect. Forms do not long for materials, for their existence is not for the sake of materials and they are not what acquire materials. It is materials that yearn for forms, since their existence is for the sake of forms and it is they that acquire forms. Matter is considered in relation to the base form and to the nobler form.

This being so — and he has run the soul in the course of the form and the Intellect in the course of matter, and ruled that materials have no subsistence without forms — it is as though he has made it necessary that the existence of the Intellect be by the soul. Yet how could that belief hold for something whose existence was an originative existence? Or how could it be conceived that the Intellect be the cause of the existence of the Intellect and the soul, while the discourse is about what is separate and comes out of the world of nature? Never. For it is known that the existence of the Following one is by the Preceding one, not the Preceding one's existence by the Following one. That is a plain fallacy, arising only from carrying upon what is natural what is said about the world of origination. As for what is in the world of origination and the first emanation — the existents from the abode of nature, of the Intellect and the soul, which are of the category of the second emanation — the matter is only as he mentioned, and it is correct from that aspect.

## 6. Nothing Stands Beside the First

The author of al-Nusra said:

The Intellect is the first mean and the Soul is the second mean; and the first mean stands nearer to the Cause than the second. Nearness and rank can arise only from perfection and nobility, and remoteness only from deficiency and lack of nobility.

We say: this statement carries no true meaning when applied to the originative and emanative first Intellects. To be a mean requires two ends, and it is not admissible to call the First Intellect a mean, since that from which its existence comes is not of the same kind as it — there is no end on that side, no shared nature that could anchor a mean — and since the First Intellect stands alone in existence, of a kind that nothing precedes.

How could one even imagine that the First of ones is a mean, when nothing of its own kind of existence precedes it, that it might stand between that and what comes after? The supposition itself is impossible: the First Intellect has a first existence, and what has a first existence cannot be called a mean. A thing deserves the name "mean" only when something of its own kind of existence precedes it — establishing an affinity on one side — and something suited to it in certain respects follows it — establishing an affinity on the other. Those two are its ends, and it is a mean between them. But between the First Intellect and its Originator, exalted be His mention, there is no affinity whatsoever. The First Intellect is not of the kind of His existence, so it cannot be a mean between Him and what lies below it, and there is no occasion for it to be a mean between itself and its Originator. What stands between it and its Originator — the cause by which it exists — is other than it. And what is called the mediator between it and what lies below it is the very essence of origination, the Cause said to be at once originating and originated, as we have shown in *The Illuminated Epistle* and in our well-known book *Peace of Mind*.

Given all this, the author of al-Nusra's inference in support of his position carries no force — what has already been said about deficiency and nobility is sufficient. Deficiency attaches to the Following only in relation to the First Intellect, not in its own essence. Its essence is sound, with no deficiency in it; it possesses completeness. It is only in relation to what precedes it that it stands at a lesser rank. Consider the Speaker and the Foundation, the Sun and the Moon: the Foundation and the Moon are each perfect in their own essence, with no deficiency in them; yet set beside their counterparts — the Speaker and the Sun — each is deficient in rank. The author of al-Nusra binds the Following to deficiency by relating it to what precedes it, while the author of al-Islah binds its essence to perfection, since it emanates from the Perfection that is its Cause. Each is right on his own side — except that the author of al-Nusra wrongs the author of al-Islah by taking him in a direction he did not intend and that his purpose does not touch.

The author of al-Nusra said:

If he says — meaning the author of al-Islah — "I do not mean that the Intellect is like the Soul in the matter of perfection," we say to him: then it is either better than the Soul or more deficient than it. He will not claim that the Soul is superior to the Intellect, so it remains for him to say that the Soul is deficient.

We say: the author of al-Islah may well not say what the author of al-Nusra has undertaken to report on his behalf. Rather, he says that all things are perfect in their own essences, and that none of them merits deficiency except in relation to what stands at a higher degree — so that the higher possesses a rank this thing does not. Once this connection is understood, the nature of the wrong done to him is plain.

The author of al-Nusra said:

Among the clearest proofs of the Soul's deficiency and the Intellect's perfection is the saying of God, exalted be He:

> وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ اللَّيْلُ، وَالنَّهَارُ، وَالشَّمْسُ، وَالْقَمَرُ
>
> "And among His signs are the night, the day, the sun, and the moon."

The night and the day stand for the two Foundations, and the sun and the moon for the two Roots: the sun for the First, and the moon for the Following. There is no disagreement among the people of knowledge and wisdom that the moon is of less light than the sun, less in light and radiance, that it draws its light from the sun, and that the sun bestows light upon it. This is what he says.

We say: his own saying makes it clear — the moon is of less light than the sun, less in light and radiance, and draws its light from the sun. Its deficiency attaches to it only by relating it to the sun; in its own essence, there is no deficiency in it, for were it deficient in essence it could not have received light from the sun at all. Because its essence is perfect, it stood ready to receive from the sun what it is able to receive. The author of al-Islah speaks of the perfection of the essence; the author of al-Nusra speaks of its deficiency in its states and accidents, through which its completeness comes. These are two different statements about two different things. The author of al-Islah is wronged — taken in a direction that is not his purpose and not his intent.

The author of al-Nusra said:

Another indication of the Soul's deficiency is that the wise set the Intellect in the station of the spirit to the Soul, and set the Soul in the station of the body to the Intellect. They did this only because they recognized the Soul's deficiency and the Intellect's completeness, and so likened the Soul to the deficient thing and the Intellect to the complete thing.

We say: with this he has only added a further clarification of what we have already said — that the deficiency is not in the Soul's essence but in other things, its states. Even so, the meaning of his words holds true only for the beings of the world and the second emanation. The drawing of benefit belongs only to the souls in the world of nature, that they may reach their second completeness; in their own essence they are complete and perfect, though deficient in the intelligibles they do not yet possess — intelligibles that run to them as the spirit runs to the body. The second, which is the first emanation, follows a different way: its completeness and perfection were given at the beginning of its existence, not through time and the drawing of benefit, but only through a deficiency of rank below what precedes it, through precedence and posteriority.

The author of al-Nusra said:

Even if the Soul had no deficiency other than that nature lies upon its horizon, this alone would suffice. The Soul has two ends: one toward the Intellect and one toward nature. The Intellect is not so — its two ends are one toward the Word and the other toward the Soul, and nothing of it clings to dark nature, because of its perfection and completeness. That an end of the Soul clings to nature, because nature lies upon its horizon — this alone, for whoever contemplates it, is enough to establish the truth of the Soul's deficiency and the Intellect's perfection. This is what he says.

We say: this statement proceeds without order. That nature lies upon the horizon of the Following — which he calls the Soul — does not earn it any deficiency. On the contrary, it indicates a perfection belonging to it: nature moves toward the Following because nature is deficient in relation to it, while the Following, in relation to nature, is noble and perfect, distinguished by what nature is not distinguished by. And yet the essential reality of both — I mean the essence of the Following and the essence of nature — is perfect, since both emanate from Perfection.

As for his saying that the Intellect has two ends, one toward the Word and the other toward the Soul: the Intellect is not a thing other than the Word, nor is the Word a thing other than the Intellect. The essence is one and the entity is one; it is only by relation that it merits the various names assigned to it. What has already been said on this point, when the name "mean" was discussed, makes this plain.

As for his saying that nothing of the Intellect clings to dark nature because of its perfection and completeness, while an end of the Soul has clung to nature because nature lies upon its horizon: it is not the Following that has clung to nature and loved it and desired it. It is nature that has loved the Following; it is nature that moves toward it and seeks union with it. To hold otherwise — to picture an end of the Following as clinging to nature — is an error. The most charitable reading of his words is that he meant the Following pours forth upon nature, and has called that outpouring a clinging.

The author of al-Islah said:

The deficient is the act of the Soul, not the essence of the Soul; and it is its act that is perfected, not its essence.

The author of al-Nusra said:

This statement is self-contradictory. If the act is deficient, the deficiency must come from one of three places: a deficiency in the essence of the agent, a deficiency in the substance from which the agent acts, or a deficiency in both. If it comes from a deficiency in the essence of the agent, then he has affirmed the deficiency of the Soul, since the Soul is the agent. If it comes from a deficiency in the substance, then he has affirmed an eternal substance — which is a departure from the doctrine of religion and an inclination toward the doctrine of Abu Zakariyya al-Razi (أَبُو زَكَرِيَّا الرَّازِي) and those who hold the pre-eternity of matter. It has already been suspected of this book's author that he leaned in that direction, since he made time pre-eternal alongside the Intellect, which is the core of his doctrine. And if the deficiency of the act comes from both, he has embraced both positions at once: affirming the deficiency of the Soul as agent, and affirming the pre-eternity of matter. Whatever he says, he ends up affirming the deficiency of the Soul. This is what he says.

We say: the author of al-Islah's position and his purpose in what he said about deficiency and perfection have already been set out. As for the author of al-Nusra's argument here, it is sophistry, injustice, and a departure from the bounds of knowledge.

He argued first that if the deficiency of the act comes from the essence of the agent — and the agent is the Soul — then the Soul is deficient. Yet the author of al-Islah has made plain that deficiency does not attach to the essence of the Soul, which is the Following, but to its act in the sense of the thing acted upon. This frees him from this division entirely.

He argued secondly that if the deficiency comes from the substance, then he has affirmed an eternal substance. But Perfection and the Perfect — the First and the Second — have an eternity that is not a first eternity affirmed of them; how much more, then, the deficient? If that is so, deficiency belongs only to the substances that receive their perfection, not to that from which perfection is received. This is well known in natural science: deficiency arises from the side of the recipients, not from the side of that from which it is received. Consider the heat of the sun: bodies quick to be affected receive it — wax, for example, which softens under it — while other bodies refuse it, like stone and iron, which are not quickly affected. The source of the heat is one; the deficiency lies in the others.

He argued thirdly that the author of al-Islah inclined toward the doctrine of Abu Zakariyya in the way he described — and this too is a departure from the bounds of knowledge and a transgression. He did him no justice: neither in how he framed the division, nor in what followed it, when he charged him with holding both positions at once — deficiency and the pre-eternity of matter.

The author of al-Nusra said:

As for his claim that the Soul became perfect only because it emanated from the First Intellect, which is Perfection, and that what emanates from Perfection cannot but be perfect — this is not right. It does not follow that the Soul must be perfect simply because it emanates from the perfect Intellect. Were that necessary, then what emanates from the Soul — namely matter and form — would also be perfect, and what emanates from matter and form would likewise be perfect, and so on down to the single, indivisible individuals, so that all would be perfect. This saying entails that the whole world is perfect, with no deficiency anywhere in it.

We say: the gist of his saying is this — if the Soul were perfect by virtue of emanating from the First Intellect, which is Perfection, then matter and form, by their emanating from the Soul, would likewise be perfect, and so would everything emanating from matter and form, down to the individuals as he supposes, so that the whole world would be perfect with no deficiency in it. We have already shown what the author of al-Islah meant in saying that the Soul is perfect. The author of al-Nusra's argument in this chapter is not thoroughly examined.

Matter is not emanated from the Soul, and the individuals are not, as he supposed, indivisible — all individuals are divided into their parts. Matter is emanated together with the Soul from that which precedes it in existence, not from the Soul itself. The proof is this: the Soul, which is the Following, does not have a first existence. There is therefore no existence of its own kind found alongside it, nor does anything rise above it in rank as with the First, which is the First Intellect. Its existence is a second existence. And whatever has a second existence does not exist in isolation — with it there must be an existence of its own kind, such that the two share the same kind.

For the First Thing — from which this Soul, the Following, came to be — is that which nothing of its own kind of existence precedes, which is one in its existence. This was solely for the sake of the transcendence of God, exalted be He, who brought it into existence free of relations, additions, multiplicity, and all that by which a thing is described in its kinds. They have found — I mean the relations and the additions — that in the active causes these are a cause for what comes to be from them being more than one, since the existence of multiplicity in caused things corresponds to what their causes and grounds possess of relations.

And so, since God, exalted be He, is too prior for another to precede Him or to exist with Him, and too exalted to be diversified by attributes and relations — since He, exalted be He, is the Existentiator, the Originator, the Cause that has no cause — it was impossible, given His purity, His holiness, and His exaltation above multiplicity, that there should come from Him anything other than one. And this one, which is the First Intellect, stood in two relations: one its being a cause for the existence of what lies below it, and the other its being an effect, since it is originated and brought into being. It was therefore necessary that what comes from it should be two, corresponding to the two relations in which its essence stands. What came from the first relation is the second Intellect, and from the other relation came the matter of which the spheres are made, and the rest of the existents of the world of body. Then, when the existent passes beyond the one into coming-to-be, it is only two, since there is not

For beyond the rank of the one in existence there is nothing but two; and beyond the individual there is nothing but the pair, which is plurality. If the first thing in the order of existents is one, then what follows it — as the intellect conceives — is two: the pair, which is the multitude. And if what follows is two, then one of those two is what we call the Soul, and the other is what we call اَلْهَيُولَى, the matter from which the spheres, the stars, and all that lies beneath them come forth.

### The Sixteenth Section of the First Part

The foundation of religion and its ordinances rests upon a balancing of existents against their correspondences, so that their arrangement may point toward those existents among the higher limits that lie hidden from sight. The Book and the Sharia proceed from the side of the Speaker, and the establishment of the Foundation stands as their interpreter, likewise proceeding from the side of the Speaker. This is the clearest sign that what proceeds from the First Intellect — which stands in its own world as the Speaker stands in the world of body — is the Follower; and that Follower is the Soul and اَلْهَيُولَى together, just as the Book and the Foundation are the two that proceed from the side of the Speaker.

Witness to this is what the Prophet did when he joined the Book and the 'Itra — whose existence is from him — when he said:

> إِلَّا أَنِّي تَارِكٌ فِيكُمُ الثَّقَلَيْنِ كِتَابَ اللَّهِ وَعِتْرَتِي ، حبل ممدود من السماء الى الأرض ، طرف منه بيد الله وطرف منه بايديكم، فتمسكوا بها لن تضلوا ما ان تمسكتم بهما، وقد سألت ربي ان يردا عليّ الحوض كهاتين
>
> "Indeed, I leave among you the two weighty things: the Book of God and my 'Itra, a rope stretched from heaven to earth, one end of it in the hand of God and one end of it in your hands. Hold fast to them, and you will never go astray so long as you hold to them. I have asked my Lord that the two return to me at the basin like these two."

And he joined the two forefingers of both his hands together and said:

> ولا اقول كهاتين

"And I do not say like these two" — pointing with the forefinger and the middle finger of a single hand, one of them going before the other — and saying: whoever helps them helps me, and whoever forsakes them forsakes me.

In likening them, in their existence, to his two forefingers, he showed that neither precedes the other. He thus placed himself — in the abode of body — in the rank of the First Intellect in the abode of origination. And he placed what he brought of the Book, and the one he coupled with it from his 'Itra — who is the Foundation, together with the rest of the Imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ) — in the rank of what proceeds from the First Intellect as the Second Intellect and as اَلْهَيُولَى, according to what we mentioned in the book Rahat al-Aql. Thus اَلْهَيُولَى does not manifest its nobility — nor the beautiful form that lies within its potency — except through the power of the Soul, which is the Follower, and through the shining of light within both of them. Just as the Book and the Sharia do not announce themselves, nor disclose the wonders of wisdom that lie within and beneath them, except through the interpretation of the Foundation and whoever stands in his place. Thus اَلْهَيُولَى, in its existence, proceeds from the First Intellect together with the Second.

### The Seventeenth Section of the First Part

We say: We have stated that nothing of the kind of the First Intellect's own existence stands alongside it, because its existence — the existence by which it exists — is only a first existence, and the first cannot be more than one. Were it otherwise, the ways of professing divine unity would break down. For if the First Intellect had a counterpart in existence, then the existence of the two together would be caused — since their existence would not proceed from itself — and this would require something prior to both from which their existence comes; and that is forbidden and absurd.

For twoness, as an existent, is a being multiplied in essences; and multiplicity in essences does not derive its existence from another multiplicity in essences. Rather, its existence comes from what precedes it — from what is one in essence in one respect and multiple in meanings in another. And what is one in essence yet multiple in meanings does not have its existence through itself, but through another that is not multiplied, neither in meanings nor in essence.

It has become clear, then, that if the First Intellect had alongside it a counterpart of the kind of its own existence, the two together would be originated from a one that precedes them. The existence of that one would then depend on its being multiple in meanings — not through itself, but through what precedes it and makes it one. And since nothing stands above the Intellect, it is impossible for the Intellect to have a counterpart of the kind of its own existence. It is therefore established that it is one. And in establishing that the Intellect is one, we establish as well that what proceeds from it is two: the Soul and what is called اَلْهَيُولَى. And since the existence of the Soul and اَلْهَيُولَى is simultaneous, as we have shown, it becomes plain that the claim that اَلْهَيُولَى appears from the Soul is an error.

### The Eighteenth Section of the First Part

As for what the author of al-Nusra committed himself to when he spoke of the deficiency of اَلْهَيُولَى and the Image, and of the deficiency of the world and all it contains, we say:

اَلْهَيُولَى and the Image are both complete, and the world with all it contains is perfect and complete, and its parts in themselves are complete and perfect. The proof is this: the complete is that outside of which nothing of the kind of its own existence exists. The world is the sum of اَلْهَيُولَى and the Image together with all the parts of body. Body, in itself, is complete in its three dimensions — nothing that its definition entails is found outside it, but within it. It is therefore complete. And if it is complete, then the author of al-Nusra's denial of the world's completeness and perfection is impossible; and since it is impossible, his argument is mere overreach.

What suffices as an indication of the world's perfection is what the author of al-Nusra himself said in his book known as al-Iftikhar, in the chapter of the Command, where he said:

> وَصُورَةُ اسْتِغْنَاءِ الْعَالَمِ بِكُلِّيَّتِهِ عَنْ غَيْرِ سِوَاهُ عَدَمُ الْغَيْرِ ، وَاكْتِفَاءُ الْحِكْمَةِ بِمَا فِي كُلِّيَّةِ الْعَالَمِ مِنَ الْغِنْيَةِ وَالْكَمَالِ
>
> "The form of the world's self-sufficiency in its totality, needing nothing other than itself, is the nonexistence of any other, and wisdom's contentment with the richness and perfection that lie in the totality of the world."

I do not know how this escaped him; perhaps he composed the book al-Iftikhar after the book al-Nusra. As for the disparity found among the parts of the world — on account of which the author of al-Nusra charges it with deficiency — that disparity arises only from the addition of some parts to others in respect of their forms and states, and this is the first perfection that exists. For some parts of the world are given both their first and second perfection from the outset; others receive their second perfection only in the end, by acquisition. The discussion has therefore made plain that the deficiency the author of al-Nusra speaks of lies only in the states that supervene upon the natural essences — states that appear when some essences are added to others. The completeness of which the author of al-Islah speaks, meanwhile, belongs only to the essences whose existence is a first existence.

### The Nineteenth Section of the First Part

The author of al-Nusra said: If, because the Soul emanates from the Intellect — which is the Complete — the Soul must have stature, then likewise, because the Soul emanates from the Intellect — which is the first intermediary and the first originator — the Soul must be an intermediary like the First, and an originator, such that the Soul attains of the virtues what the Intellect attains, and likewise the whole world and its individuals. That is his statement.

We say: The demonstration that the Intellect cannot rightly be called the first intermediary has already been set forth; and in sum, what he has advanced here is a corrupt proposition. For when a thing's existence proceeds from the requirement of another thing — one being cause, the other effect — it does not follow that the effect shares the cause in all its states. If it did, the effect's very identity would be the cause's identity and its reality the cause's reality; nothing could then be said of it except what is said of that identity; it would no longer be a distinct existence, and it could not be said to proceed from the cause by being that very cause. Yet though the effect need not share the cause in all its states, it must share it in some; for if it shared it in none, it would not be an effect of it at all, and the cause would not have brought it into existence.

Since this is the way things exist in multiplicity, each must share with its cause in one respect and differ from it in another. It follows, then, that the Follower called Soul — which emanates from the First Intellect, the cause of its existence — must resemble it in one respect and differ from it in another. Since this is so, it is plain that when the Follower called Soul has its existence from the Intellect, its being need not resemble the Intellect in all its states; nor does it follow that what is said of the Intellect as cause must also be said of the Follower called Soul. This refutes what the author of al-Nusra said: that if it were necessary, it would be necessary.

### The Twentieth Section of the First Part

The author of al-Nusra said: This has many parallels in the sensible world, such as thought and the partial soul. For the partial soul that is in us is in the position of the Intellect, and thought is in the position of the Soul. Yet thought, even though it emanates from the soul, may not be said to be like the soul, because thought perceives what it perceives part by part, and time after time, whereas the soul perceives the whole of what it perceives without time. That is his statement.

We say: The soul — I mean the soul of the human being — has acts, and each of its acts has a name proper to it. When it seeks to grasp some thing, that seeking is called thought (al-fikr). When it regards the forms of the known things that have come to be within its essence, that regarding is called retention (al-hifz). When it imprints its essence with the marks it captures by way of inference or by way of the senses, that imprint is called conception (al-tasawwur). And when it knows that outside itself there are things it wishes to conceive, that will is called striving (al-nuzu) toward them.

Since the soul has acts, and thought is an act of it that does not pass beyond its own essence so as to have an existence apart from its existence, how can it be sound to liken thought to the Follower called Soul — which has an existence other than the existence of the Intellect? And how can it be said that thought perceives part by part, when perception belongs only to the essence of the soul, through its act called thought within itself? And how can it be sound that the soul — the soul of the human being — should perceive the whole without time, when its only path to knowledge is through the senses and inference, passing from particulars to universals in time?

Nor does it follow, because thought may not be said to resemble the soul, that the Second Intellect — called the Universal Soul — does not resemble the First Intellect. For thought as it proceeds from human souls does not run in the same course as the Second Intellect from the First Intellect. The Second Intellect is a second existence from origination, subsisting through itself, even though in existence it is one rank below the First. But thought has no existence separate from the soul; rather it is the very identity of the soul, and has no subsistence through itself. Every act of the soul has a cause that makes it necessary: the soul's being in the world of nature. When the soul departs from that world, these acts are annulled with their cause, and nothing remains subsisting except the essence conceived as pure Intellect.

He made the Second Intellect — called the Soul — proceed from the First Intellect in the manner that thought proceeds from the soul. But since the existence of thought is no existence other than the existence of the soul — which he likened to the Intellect — and since thought has no perception of the soul through its own essence, his argument amounts to a denial that the Second Intellect called the Soul has any existence other than the existence of the First Intellect, or any perception like the perception of the First. And that is impossible; for the Follower has an existence other than the existence of the First, and a perception like the perception of the First. It does not deserve to be called an originator, just as the First Existent does not deserve to be called emanated. Both are pure intellects; each has encompassed its own essence and preserved it by its own essence. They are alike in one respect and unlike in another, as the foregoing discussion has established.

Since this is so, there is no sense in the likenings he drew: no sense in making thought stand as what he likened it to, no sense in placing the human soul in the position of the First Intellect, and no sense in placing thought within it in the position of the Universal Soul. For the nobility of the human soul, and its passage from potency to act, lie entirely in acquisition; and acquisition comes only from the senses as deed and from conception as knowledge. Since the nobility of souls lies in acquisition — part of which is thought and inference as conception — and since thought belongs to souls only so that they may be ennobled by it, his argument is as though he had made the cause of the First Intellect's nobility to be the Second Intellect called the Soul. And that is an inverted proposition. For the nobility of the Follower and its rank come from what precedes it in existence — the Antecedent — while the nobility of the Antecedent and its rank come from what comes after it. The rule governing what has a second existence — in respect of perfection and completeness — is that it be the reverse of what has a first existence. Neither, therefore, runs in the course of the other in this respect, though they share from another aspect. Yet the author of al-Nusra has judged what has a first existence by what belongs to what has a second existence. And that is impossible.

## 7. The Soul That Forgets

Chapter Twenty-One of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said:

One of the clearest signs that the Soul is deficient and the First Intellect perfect is this: the First and the author of al-Islah all said that action belongs to the Second and attributed nothing to the First. Action is always a sign of deficiency and need; the absence of action is evidence of perfection and self-sufficiency. In other words, one acts only to draw a benefit or to repel a harm.

Abu Zakariyya said:

The Initiator, through manipulation and tampering, would already be the Soul — and the people of religion do not hold this. All that remains, then, is the drawing of benefit or the repelling of harm; and wherever either is found, there is deficiency. In this, the Soul seeks to draw the benefit that consists in reaching the end destined for it, and so it begins to act. The Intellect, with no rank above it, is not deficient and does not move to action at all. The Soul, however, finding a station above it, remained in motion, persisting in its quest. By this reasoning we say what we say about the deficiency of the Soul and the integrity of the Intellect. The statement is clear, and praise be to God.

The sum of what he says in this chapter is this: the Second is the agent; action serves only to draw benefit or repel harm; drawing benefit and repelling harm are marks of deficiency alone; the Intellect, being without deficiency, is still from action — its stillness arising from the fact that no rank above it remains to be sought; and the Soul, finding a rank above it, moved toward that rank to attain it.

We say: Had the author of al-Islah been speaking of the soul as it exists in human beings, the response of the author of al-Nusra would have been the more fitting one. But his words concerned the Following that bears the name "the Soul." To invoke the condition found in human souls — that they act only to draw benefit, to repel harm, or to tamper — in order to invalidate his statement and overturn his ruling that the Following is complete and perfect: this is a manifest prejudice. For it has already been established that what is affirmed of that whose existence is a first existence — in respect of completeness and perfection — is not affirmed of that whose existence is a second existence, nor of that whose existence was once a first existence.

The account of the author of al-Nusra on the nature of action and its divisions is not correct. Not every act aims at drawing a benefit, repelling a harm, or mere futility. An act may proceed from an agent in such a way that its benefit falls upon the object of the act rather than upon the agent from whom it issues.

The proof of this is that heating is an act of fire, yet the act does not return to the fire; neither benefit nor harm comes back to it. The benefit and the harm fall instead upon whatever the fire's heating reaches. The same holds for the act of the sun in illuminating the world — enabling human beings to order their affairs by their own will, allowing plants and animals and minerals to come into being through its heat. These benefits return to others, not to the sun itself; it neither gains from this act nor suffers by it. The same holds for the whales of the Sea of Oman and those others called the dolphin (الدُّلْفِين), whose nature is such that when a man is drowning in the sea, they help him escape and push him toward the shore. That act brings them no benefit and no harm; the benefit falls entirely upon the one they aid.

If this is so — if it is found among natural things that do not reason, even though they are deficient in their states and dependent on others in their action — then there is nothing to deny concerning the pure, abstract, complete intellects, which were granted their perfections at the very first moment of their existence. It is fitting that they should perform acts greater than anything that appears in natural things, acts that their own perfections require of them, in proportion to the nearness of their ranks and the height of their noble degrees. These are noble acts, not for the sake of drawing benefit to themselves or repelling harm from themselves, but for the sake of others. They are acts of wisdom — not acts of drawing benefit, not of repelling harm, not of futility. Indeed, the perfect power from which the perfect, well-ordered act proceeds exists only in the perfect.

Furthermore, the Following — which he called "the Soul" — is not in motion as he claimed, such that what befalls natural things would befall it. Motion of that kind belongs only to bodies, with their bodies and their natural forms. The Following stands higher in rank than natural things, though it is below the First in rank, as we made clear in the book Rahat al-Aql. By what we have set forward here, his prejudice and his argument — which do not hold for the one he is speaking of — have become plain. The statement of the author of al-Islah, that the Following is complete in completeness, stands, as we explained earlier.

It is not permissible to predicate motion and rest of what belongs to the abode of origination, namely the First and Second Intellects and the upper letters. We have already shown that motion and rest belong only to what is a body or possessed of a body. And in this there is sufficiency.

Chapter Twenty-Two of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said:

The upshot of what the author of al-Islah says is that the act of the Soul is perfected, not the essence of the Soul itself — even though with him the Soul is complete. On this view, the act of the Soul would become a soul, which is impossible with a manifest impossibility, for acts have never once been seen, in what is observed, to become agents. Consider the writer and his writing, the builder and his building: the writing does not become a writer, nor does the building become a builder. Rather, it is the man who is a writer in potency and a builder in potency who becomes a writer and a builder in actuality. We say the same of the Soul and the Intellect: the Intellect is complete in actuality; the Soul is deficient in actuality, subsisting in potency; and it comes to subsist in actuality when it gains completeness. That its act should become complete, however, is impossible.

We say:

The aim of the author of al-Islah, in saying that the act of the Soul is perfected rather than the essence of the Soul, is this: what attains the second perfection is that which comes from the Soul's side — that which proceeds from it in the manner of what the Soul pours forth upon, issuing from potency to the measure of the act. Since the aim of his saying — that the act of the Soul is perfected — is what issues from the Soul's overflowing, moving from potency into act and thereby attaining the second perfection; and since nothing can issue from the limit of potency into act except through what already subsists in actuality: it follows that when what subsisted in potency issues into act and attains the second perfection by acquisition, there is nothing to prevent it from resembling, in the perfection it has received, that which subsists in actuality and was granted perfection from the very first of its existence.

They share in that both subsist in actuality, even though each attained what they share not from the same side as the other. There is nothing impossible in their being alike in the respect they share; for it is well known that whatever is joined with another thing in some meaning is, in that meaning, its like.

The witness to this is what our argument yields: everything that passes from the limit of potency to the limit of act subsists in actuality; everything that subsists in actuality does so by the occurrence of the second perfection to it; and therefore everything that passes from the limit of potency to act has the second perfection occur to it.

If what subsists in actuality does so by the occurrence of the second perfection to it, and what subsisted in potency and afterward came to subsist in actuality does so likewise by the occurrence of the second perfection — then they share and are alike in both having attained the second perfection, even though they differ in other respects.

Since what we have set forward is neither impossible nor to be denied but rather necessary, the statement of the author of al-Nusra amounts to nothing but prejudice.

Chapter Twenty-Three of Part One

Then the author of al-Nusra said:

This is impossible with a manifest impossibility, for acts have never once been seen, in what is observed, to become agents — as shown by what he adduced from the case of the writer and the building: a proof that testifies to the very impossibility he claimed.

We say:

Acts are, in truth, agents. The writing, which he cited as the act of the writer, is itself what converses with its reader and addresses him. Were the writing — in its forms and its tracing — not, for all that it is an act belonging to another, itself an agent upon the very soul of its reader, speaking on behalf of its author, translating him, and impressing him in its forms, the reader would never know what is in the soul of the writer. The building, in the form the builder brought into being from what was in his soul, conveys its own form to the observer through its arrangement and disposition, so that he conceives it. Were the form of the building not subsisting in actuality — capable of impressing itself upon the soul of the observer who holds its forms within him — the observer would never conceive it.

Likewise, the act of one who strikes — the striking itself — is the agent that produces pain in the one struck, in the manner we described in our two well-known epistles, al-Rawdah and al-Mudiah (الْمُضِيْئَة). And the word of God, exalted is He, is the most solid proof that acts are themselves agents, where He says, exalted is the Speaker:

> وَإِن مِّن شَىْءٍ إِلَّا يُسَبِّحُ بِحَمْدِهِۦ

There is nothing but glorifies Him with His praise. The "thing" is the act of the Maker, exalted is He, and it, together with being an act of an agent, is itself its glorification. And His saying, exalted is He:

> وَأَرْسَلْنَا ٱلرِّيَحَ لَوَقِحَ

And We sent the winds as fecundators. The winds are a thing, and their fecundating is His act.

With this explanation in place, the objection of the author of al-Nusra against the author of al-Islah in this chapter fails.

What the author of al-Nusra truly means when he says that acts have never been seen to become agents is this: the acts themselves have not become identical with their agents in what is observed — just as the writing has not become identical with its writer, nor the building with its builder. This, as he stated it, is a correct observation. An act does not become one and the same as that from which it issued; rather it is an agent like its own agent in respect of the action proceeding from it, even though the ranks differ and the causes vary. But the author of al-Nusra wished to take the side of the one he defends, by prejudice, against the author of al-Islah — may God sanctify their souls — and many who are associated wrong one another.

Chapter Twenty-Four of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said:

He likened the Intellect and the Soul — in their completeness and their deficiency — to a man and to sperm, because the sperm is, in truth, the form of the man in potency and comes to stand upright like the man. Likewise the Soul is, in truth, the form of the Intellect and becomes complete like the Intellect when it receives from the Intellect the benefits destined for it. That is what he said.

We say:

His claim — that sperm is in truth the form of the man in potency, and likewise that the Soul is in truth the form of the Intellect and becomes complete — is not correct. Sperm is not the form of the man, nor is the Soul, while in potency, the form of the Intellect. Sperm is matter prepared for form, empty of forms, which then acquires them under given conditions. Likewise the Soul is only a natural readiness, empty of forms, whose nature is to acquire the forms of knowable things, so that its essence is perfected through them.

Had the Soul truly been the form of the Intellect, it would follow that the Intellect must have something of which it is the form — making the form one thing and that of which it is the form another. How can this apply to what has an originated existence, in which otherness exists only by relation while the essence is one? No. This discourse, and this analogy, holds only for natural things, not for what is separate and parted from bodies.

Chapter Twenty-Five of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said:

Likewise, the rank of the speaker (اَلنَّاطِق) in the corporeal world is like the rank of the Intellect in the spiritual world, and the rank of the foundation (أَسَاس) is like the rank of the Following. So if it is permissible to say that the Soul is complete like the Intellect, it must equally be permissible to say that the foundation is complete like the speaker. But that is not correct, for the rank of the foundation is below the rank of the speaker — as has been made clear in the meaning of the prayer. That is what he said.

We say:

The Second is like the First, even though in rank it is below it — just as the foundation is like the speaker, even though in rank he is below him. The Second is established in its existence, and no further degree remains for it to attain by which it might gain completeness. Likewise the foundation is complete and perfect in its own rank, with no degree left for it to ascend to that would bring it a completeness not already in its essence.

Sufficient witness to this is the act of the Prophet (صَلْعَم) and what it indicates, in that he made it obligatory to slaughter camels by nahr (النَّحْر) and to slaughter other animals by dhabh (اَلذَّبْح). The nahr is the delivering of the iron to the head of the heart (fuad) of the one slaughtered, so that whatever turbid blood is there may issue from him — corresponding to the degree of conveyance, the disclosure of secrets, and the departure of doubts and uncertainties through the ranks. The dhabh corresponds to the taking of the covenant and the release in the summons, while the one slaughtered by nahr corresponds to the foundation.

It is as though the Prophet (صَلْعَم), in making this act manifest, informed the community that followed him, on the one side, and the imams of his cycle: that he did not place the foundation in his own station within the summons to the inner call — which is the completion of the worship of God that leads to the affirmation of God's oneness and the knowledge of His bounds — until he had taught him what holds his perfection and disclosed to him what holds his completeness. Thus he raised the foundation's degree through conveyance, and the foundation's path to perfection and completion is unlike the path of any other member of the Prophet's community — the path by which each of them obtains from him the conveyance and the completeness.

The conveyance and the covenant were the last things I set down, and the prayer itself bears witness to the order in which they fall. The Prophet did not take the covenant from the sons of the call first and then disclose to them the degree of perfection out of any contradiction. The conveyance serves only to point and indicate: it shows that the natural souls, which stand below the Second, begin in potency and then emerge by acquisition into act at the last — which is their perfection and their completion — so that each may believe of it what is fitting to believe. Nor did the matter rest at this single indication. He indicated the Follower's completion from another side as well, in the two units of the dawn prayer, which stand upon the First and the Second. In the first unit, which stands upon the First, he made it obligatory to recite the glorification, the praise, and the sura. In the second unit he made obligatory the recitation of the Qunut in place of the glorification that belongs to the first, so that the second might stand in the same state as the first from the side of perfection. Their distinction lies only in rank within existence: the first is the beginning of existence, like the first unit, and the second is the second of existence, like the second unit.

The twenty-sixth chapter of Part One. And we say, in answer to his claim that if it were permitted to call the soul as perfect as the Intellect, it would equally be permitted to call the Foundation like the Speaker, and that this is not right: how could it not be right, when the Foundation is the one who stands in the place of the Prophet and his family? Were he to fall short of standing in that place, or were he not sufficient, complete, and perfect as the Prophet is, God would not have enjoined obedience to him. How would God the Exalted enjoin obedience to one who is like others in deficiency? Neither of these can stand. Nor does wisdom permit — given that the Messenger is a messenger to all who are of his time and all who are born after him, and a proof upon His creation — that the community be left without one who is like the Messenger, so that no one among them is as he is and the proof stands established against them. The Foundation, then, is like the Speaker who comes after him in one respect, and unlike the Speaker in another. It is proof enough that the disparity between them is in rank and not in essence that the author himself argues by rank alone, saying the rank of the Foundation is below the rank of the Speaker — for he could argue by nothing except rank. It is clear from what we have set out that the position of the author of al-Islah has withstood the objection of the author of al-Nusra, and that his saying that the Second is deficient — though it is the saying of the author of al-Nusra — holds only in the sense in which it must rightly be said.

Out of that deficiency, they desire nature's appetites and pleasures and turn away from their own world and its pleasures and beauty and splendor. The Intellect, by contrast, for its perfection and its beauty and its splendor and its completion, does not forget the pleasures of its own world, does not incline to nature or desire what nature holds of pleasures and appetites, and does not look toward it. When it does look toward nature, it looks only from the side of tenderness and mercy and disdain, informing it that what it holds is little set against what is in the Intellect's own world. Were the soul perfect like the Intellect, it would have held fast to what it saw in its own world and would not have desired what stands beneath it. This is his saying: that many speaking souls submit to nature, are led by it in what it commands them, and incline to it out of their deficiency.

And we say: the one that submits and inclines — that is led toward preferring nature and its pleasures, toward following natural appetites and abandoning the acts of worship — is the sensual soul, since it is of nature, and not the speaking soul that has become attached to it. The sensual soul is restrained and held back from base and vile things only when the traces of the Intellect work upon it by way of the bodily bounds, through teaching, so that it is held back from what would destroy it and so acts rightly. These souls are not the Follower that issues from the First Intellect, of whom the author of al-Islah speaks. What has already passed contains the point that the Follower is other than these souls, and these souls other than it, and that the Follower is of such completeness and perfection that nothing in his essence remains unattained by which he could be deficient — unlike these souls, which begin with a first perfection and attain a second perfection in time and by acquisition.

The twenty-ninth chapter of Part One. And we say, secondly, that the Follower — who is complete and perfect and who was given his perfection and completion at the very beginning of his existence, all at once — is an existent, and through him and through his overflow the deficient one attains acceptance of the degree of perfection. The proof of this is that the existents in the world of nature are in potency first and in act at the last, and there is no passing of what stands in potency into act except by the existence of what stands in act. And if there is no such passing except by the existence of what stands in act, and the thing that passes into act exists, then the existence of what stands in act is a necessary existence. And if that existence is necessary, then what stands in act is either the First Intellect or something other than it. That it should be the First Intellect is void, for the First Intellect's act does not come to exist through another, nor does he need alongside himself something existing outside him to act upon. The First Intellect is free, in his act, of any other than himself to act upon — he is the complete one whose existence is from the One from whom he came to be, the Exalted and the Magnified, a single existence with no like and no other, so that there should be something beside him for him to act upon. For if there were, the Worshipped, the Exalted, would no longer be the Worshipped. Enough has already been said of this, and more than we have set out here, in our book known as Rahat al-Aql. What stands at this station of oneness has nothing beside it to act upon, and so no multiplicity issues from it save by its acting within its own essence. And since its act is within its own essence, and its act is the act that brings out — by it and by its overflow into another — what stands in potency, it is ruled void that what brings the existents in potency out into act should be the First Intellect. And since it is not the First Intellect, it is established that it is other than it, and that it is the Second.

Witness to the soundness of our saying is that the Prophet and his family, for the greatness of his rank and his completeness, had no need of another among mankind to complete in his place his act, which is the revelation and the law. He stood alone by his essence, and from his essence there issued — according to what he drew from the One by whom he attained the degree of perfection — what he recited and what he laid down as law, in complete form. For this reason he ran his soul, in the abode of the body and in the revelation, along the course of the First Intellect in the very act of origination, so that his acts and his rank might lead whoever contemplates them to soundness of belief in the knowledge of what is hidden from the senses among the first principles, by origination and by procession, and so that the path of affirming God's oneness might be made whole. The Foundation, though he stands at the highest degree of perfection, did not have that rank by which he could stand alone in his act — in the ordering of the call and the interpretation and the teaching and the guidance — by his own essence. He was therefore not free, in his act, of what he found beside him, which is the revelation and the law. Neither did the Foundation dispense with these — the revelation and the law — nor was the Speaker one who dispensed, in the world of the body, with a thing existing alongside him by which to be aided in his act, as the Foundation is aided. For this reason he ran the Foundation along the course of the Follower, so that the manner in which his affair stands, in what we have mentioned, might serve as a proof for knowing the rank of the Second. The Prophet and his family run along the course of the First, who is completeness: he dispenses, in his act, with any other than himself, and is aided by nothing but his own essence. The Foundation runs along the course of the Second, whose completeness consists in this: that there is no act of his without the revelation and the law by which his act comes to be — just as the Follower was not free, in his act, of another by which his act comes to be. And the following community runs along the course of what is beneath the sphere, in the world of nature, in which the passing of its existents from potency into act comes about by the overflow of the Second; for the souls standing in potency need what will bring them to the degree of conveyance and perfection, and that comes from the side of the Foundation's call. Just as the Second is the one who confirms the one made ready for acceptance by his overflow upon him from the abode of nature, so too the Foundation — and whoever stands in his place in the call — is the one who brings the willing and obedient to the degree of conveyance and completion. The Speakers, being of the world of nature, did not attain completion and the lofty degree from the side of the First Intellect, but from the side of the Second Intellect first; just as none among our kind reaches the degree of conveyance from the side of the Speaker, but only from the side of the Foundation's call first. They run from those above them as we run from them,

as one sandal matches the other. The perfection of the Speakers and of those who stand in their place comes from those above them, and our perfection comes from them. May God gather us with them. This discourse has made clear that these souls are not the Follower.

The Soul That Forgets

> حَذْوَ النَّعْلِ بِالنَّعْلِ

Whatever exists under such conditions is itself proof of its own deficiency. The objection that the author of al-Nusra raises against the author of al-Islah is therefore an impossible one.

Chapter Thirty of Part One

He said: The Follower is deficient because it forgets its world and does not remember it.

But how can this be valid? Forgetting occurs only after knowledge. If these souls had existed beforehand in a state of knowing, it would have been impossible for alteration to overtake them through their descent into the abode of nature — not after they had dwelt in majesty and attained the perfection that is intellectual knowledge. The existence of whatever exists in the world of origination and emanation, among the intellects, is an existence resembling what is necessary and fixed. No alteration overtakes it, and for that reason it is called eternal. How, then, could the conditions overtake what is eternal, so that it possesses knowledge at one moment and is stripped of it at another? The abode in which it dwells is not the abode of alteration that he has asserted for it by his word "نسيّ" — that it forgot.

In sum, I say: if these souls were bestowed upon human persons from that world, coming in order to learn and to be trained in the sciences, then their ignorance at the very outset of their existence here must be rooted either in their essence or in an accident.

If they are ignorant in their essence, they are barred from knowing anything at all, and ignorance is intrinsic to them — but this is impossible, since we observe the soul coming to know after not knowing.

If the ignorance is an accident, that accident must have arisen either in that world or in this one. If it arose in that world — and that world is the abode of eternity and completeness, not the abode of alteration and deficiency — then it is impossible that any accident should befall them there to alter them. If it arose in this world, and their end is to come to know and to return to the majesty they were in at first before alteration overtook them, then this too is impossible in wisdom: remaining in the first state is more fitting for them than being altered from it and then returning to it.

It is void in every case, therefore, to hold that these souls receive any accident — whether ignorance or knowledge — in this world, given that their existence comes from another source. If a thing's existence is not from this lower world, then its receiving such an accident here is impossible: it is by its completeness that it is what it is, and what derives from it is the completeness that is the blessing of that world — "لِأَهْلِ الْعِلْمِ" — for the people of knowledge, not of this world. When that is established, and it is impossible that their ignorance lie in their essence or that an accident from that world befall them, it follows that their arrival was not from that world. And once it is established that their arrival was not from that world, it is void to say that they forgot their world.

Chapter Thirty-One of Part One

If its arrival is from the world of origination, then its existence is a first existence, on the side of origination and emanation from the most excellent rank. Whatever has this manner of existence stands in actuality. And whatever stands in actuality cannot receive any accident — for the reception of accidents belongs to things that stand in potentiality, and, in sum, to whatever hovers between potentiality and actuality in the abode of the body. That whose existence is in the abode of origination, however, was granted its perfection and completeness at the very beginning of its existence; it is eternal, and it stands in that state. It is therefore impossible that it should receive any accident, or that anything should overtake it by which it is altered from its existence. When that is established, then on the view of the author of al-Nusra — that it forgot — forgetting would be an impossible accident for it. It is void, then, to say of it that it forgot its world.

Chapter Thirty-Two of Part One

Then I say: forgetting befalls only those souls that are prepared, from the world of nature, to receive knowledge — prepared through the entry upon them of accidents from the causes to which their existence is owed. States then overtake them belonging to what they follow in their coming to be. These are the richest of souls, when they are trained by the rites of the Law, and when the things prior to them in existence, up to the First Principle, have been grasped by the intellect. We call this an intellect and a second emanation, just as the Follower is a first emanation, according to what we have explained in our well-known book (بِرَاحَةِ الْعَقْلِ), Peace of Mind. It is impossible that these souls should come from the world of the intellect, and there is no evidence whatever for that claim. What the author of al-Nusra himself set down — in the forty-fourth key of his well-known book, al-Maqalid — concerning the impossibility of these souls coming from the world of the intellect is sufficient proof refuting what he said elsewhere in his defense, and confirming what we have set down. I do not know how this escaped him. By my reckoning, al-Maqalid was composed after al-Nusra. May God gather its author with our pure masters in the Abode of Holiness, and seal our religion and our world with good, by His grace.

Chapter Thirty-Three of Part One

Then he said: The intellect, by reason of its completeness and perfection, does not forget its world, does not incline toward nature, and does not desire it.

This is a correct statement, if it is said of those souls that have acquired their perfection through knowledge and works, and have thereby become attached to the abode of nature and come to resemble the outgoing intellects in completeness — like the souls of the Speakers and the Foundations who stand in their place (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ). As for the outgoing intellects — which are the First, the Second, the Previous, the Follower, the Creative, and the Emanated — the First is higher and loftier than to hold any measure for the natural world, let alone to incline toward it; its overflow upon that world is continuous, completing through it whatever is not yet complete.

Then he said: Were the soul as complete as the intellect, it would have held fast to what it beheld in its world, and would not have desired what is with its effect, so as to entail that it had been altered from its first state; yet it did not hold fast to that state.

Enough has already been said on this meaning.

And he said: His words — that it did not desire what is with its effect — amount to affirming that nature is its effect. But how can it be true to say that nature is the effect of souls, and that its existence is by the existence of the persons who belong to the world of nature? Or how is one to conceive that the heavens, the planets, the natures, and the things generated should be from them, when they are confined within persons whom number encompasses? Or how is it true that the complete should desire, in its own states, what is beneath it? That is impossible; and if it is impossible, then it follows that the existence of the natural world is together with the First Emanator, and that the completeness of the existents within it is by his overflow. To expound this is a discourse that lays bare the partisanship of the author of al-Nusra against the author of al-Islah, even though the words of the author of al-Nusra are sound in the manner we have explained, not in the manner he himself intended in his rebuttal.

Chapter Thirty-Four of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said: The Wise One stated, in logic, that all things exist in four senses: either in the essences of things, or in thought, or in speech, or in writing. The things existing in their own essences are those the intellect has encompassed — none of which escapes its encompassing, since there exists nothing that has no essence; and were it not that whatever has an essence cannot elude the intellect, the intellect would not have encompassed all that has an essence. As for the soul, it has encompassed only what thought has first presented to its encompassing; and if among things there is that which has an essence which thought has not first presented for it to grasp, then the soul has not encompassed all that has an essence, and it remains deficient in relation to the rank of the intellect — since it has not encompassed all that has an essence, whereas the intellect has encompassed all that has an essence, and there is nothing that has no essence. This is among the clearest indications of the perfection of the intellect and the deficiency of the soul. This is what he said.

We say: This discourse is not well ordered. What the Wise One meant by his words — if indeed he said them — is to show that existents fall into four divisions.

He said: As for their being in the essences of things, he means that among things there is that whose existence is outside the soul, and that has a meaning in its essence by which it is what it is — like the body, whose essence exists outside the soul together with the meaning by which it is what it is in its essence, which is length, breadth, and depth.

He said: And as for their being in thought, he means that among things there is that which has no essence outside the soul, but whose existence is in the mind — like genus, matter, place, and the circles that are traced in the soul, by way of supposition, from the motion of the spheres, and the like.

He said: And as for their being in speech, he means that among things there is that whose existence is in speech, like propositions of affirmation and negation concerning essences that are not changed by them.

He said: And as for their being in writing, he means that among things there is that whose existence is in writing and whose existence takes its rule from it — like the divine matters overflowing from the world of the intellect upon the Speakers and the aided ones, which, if they are not fixed by composed letters and their forms not wholly deposited so that the soul may have a conception and an existence through them, their existence and the benefit of them come to nothing.

The Wise One had confined the existents to these four divisions, though under examination the divisions diminish and fall back to what is below them. It is therefore possible that the Wise One meant, by his words, to point out that writing stands, in exposition, in place of what exists in speech; that speech stands in place of the soul in what it wishes to make plain; and that the soul, in turn, stands in place of those things outside it, by conceiving them and taking their place through attaching itself to them — in the sense of becoming their like by acquiring the meanings by which they are what they are. There is nothing in the words of the Wise One that serves as evidence for what the author of al-Nusra wished to defend, namely the deficiency of the one spoken of from that great rank. But if the words of the author of al-Nusra are carried upon what is in the world of nature — upon the souls that have risen in rank and become intellects standing in actuality, which are called a second emanation, and which are still standing in potentiality below the First Emanator in the world of Holiness — then he is right.

Chapter Thirty-Five of Part One

Here the author of al-Nusra made it obligatory, in his words, to interpret the saying of the Wise One so that the deficient one has thought. But this is not sound, since his discourse concerns the Follower, of whom the author of al-Islah speaks. For thought, in the souls existing in the world of nature, is a power in them to acquire what they do not yet possess the image of; and the end of this power is that they may become independent of it. The proof is that the soul has no need of thought in what it has already encompassed, whose image is present with it and in which it stands in actuality; it needs thought only in the things it has not yet encompassed — things whose image, by which it stands in actuality and attains the degree of perfection and independence, it has not yet acquired. And since the soul at last becomes independent of thought,

I say: The Follower, from which the overflow and blessing come upon the existents of the world — I mean the things generated — was granted its perfection at the first, and, by what we have already said concerning the perfection it attained at the beginning of its existence, was made independent. When that is so, the partisanship of the author of al-Nusra against the author of al-Islah in what he discussed has become plain, and it has appeared that his statement is sound only in what is natural, not in what the Abode of Holiness contains.

Chapter Thirty-Six of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said: As for his saying that the deficiency is in the action of the soul, not in its essence, because the action of the soul is completed only through time — this is error and mistake, since it is not necessary that whatever is done in time be deficient. If the cause of the deficiency of things were time alone, then it would be necessary that whatever is done in a longer time be the more deficient, and whatever is done in a shorter time be the more complete; yet we see the contrary in what is observed, for among things that may be the more complete, the shorter the time, the more complete they are by that very fact. Were the matter as the author of al-Islah described, it would be necessary that it be the reverse. Time, then, is not the cause of the deficiency of things or of their perfection; rather, the deficiency of a thing comes from the deficiency of the agent, or from the deficiency of the matter, or from the deficiency of both together. This is what he said.

We say: The author of al-Islah made no such claim. He did not say that action in time is deficient. What he said is this: any meaning that unfolds within time and reaches its completion through duration is deficient until that completion arrives, at which point it is complete; and everything in the observable world has a completeness of this kind — once it receives that completeness, it is complete. As has already been said, when the author of al-Islah speaks of the deficiency being in the action of the soul and not in its essence, he means that the soul performs the act by which, from its own side, it attains the degree of perfection and acquires the form through which it becomes complete by way of overflow. His words — that the deficiency is in the action of the soul, not in its essence — point to nothing other than the deficiency in the receptive essence of the soul, upon which the Follower overflows, such that the soul cannot receive this overflow all at once but only through time: days, months, and years. Understood in this way, the words of the author of al-Islah, on the ground from which he speaks, are sound; and the words of the author of al-Nusra, if applied to natural things, are likewise sound — though he wished to be an advocate for his teacher. May God gather them and us with the masters.

Chapter Thirty-Seven of Part One

The author of al-Islah said: The Creator, exalted be He, originated the intellect and time together in a single instant.

The author of al-Nusra said: It is known that time is the measure of motion, and that motion belongs to the spheres. It follows from this that the spheres — and the time generated from their motions — were together with the intellect in a single instant, which would also make it permissible to say that whatever has place, and the corruptible beings, were together with the intellect in a single instant. And if it is permissible to say that the intellect and time were together in a single instant, then it follows by analogy that time receives from the excellences and lights overflowing upon the intellect the like of what the intellect receives, so that time too becomes complete — which is impossible. And if it is permissible to say that the intellect and time were together in a single instant, they must be either two things or one thing; and if they are one thing, the plural wording cannot apply to a single thing.

It remains, then, to weigh the claim itself and see where it holds and where it fails.

Chapter Thirty-Seven of Part One

The author of al-Islah said: The Most High Creator (اَلْبَارِئ) originated the intellect and time together, in a single instant.

The author of al-Nusra said: It is known that time is the measure of motion, and that motion belongs only to the heavens. It must follow from this that the heavens — and the time generated from their motions — were present with the intellect at once; and by the same reasoning one might say that whatever has place, and the corruptible beings, were present with the intellect at once. Yet if it is permitted to say that the intellect and time were together in a single instant, it follows by analogy that time receives from the virtues and lights overflowing upon the intellect the like of what the intellect receives, so that time too becomes complete — and that is impossible. Granting that the intellect and time were together in a single instant, they are not without being either two things or one thing. If they are one thing, the plural wording cannot apply to a single thing by way of its names, for it is impossible to say that the stone was together with the rock, or the blade together with the knife. And if they are two distinct things, then consider: when the intellect apprehended its own essence, did it do so without time or with time? If it apprehended its own essence without time, then it existed, and time did not. But if it apprehended its own essence only with time, then time is prior to it — and what is prior to the intellect is not time; it is eternity and pre-eternity.

We say: The substance of this argument — though it falls short in its proofs and its demonstration — is that the existence of time together with the intellect is impossible. This is sound in our doctrine, and enough has already been said in this book on the impossibility of anything existing together with the first intellect to make plain that the matter stands otherwise than the author of al-Islah set it down in this chapter. Yet the author of al-Nusra erred in saying that what precedes the intellect is not time but the eternal and the pre-eternal, for there is nothing at all prior to the first intellect, as we have made clear in what has already been said in this book and in the book Rahat al-Aql. What is sound — and upon which the rule of belief and of the doctrine concerning the eternal and the pre-eternal rests — is that these belong to the world of origination, as we have explained in the epistle known as al-Rawda; and what we set down there is enough to spare us any lengthening here.

Chapter Thirty-Eight of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said, invoking the saying of Banidh Qalis (بَانْذَقْلِيس) as an argument: the intellect became complete only because it submitted to nothing except the Creator, mighty and exalted, while the soul became deficient because it submitted to nature.

We say: The philosophers — even the foremost among them — however excellent they were in their own age, remained under the sway of error in much of what they discussed concerning intelligible things. Had they lived in our time and seen the springs of grace overflow from the House of Revelation with the realities of things as they truly are, in the manner of the Alawite call, then for all their personal striving they would have done themselves justice: they would have held back from much of what they believed about matters hidden from the senses, and would have rested content with the traces of the one for whom God has set a light in the obscurities of things. In their own times they followed their own opinions and their own intellects in all that they pursued, until each of them arrived, in his belief, only at what his own writings attest — just as the people of the outward do in the Hanafi community when they lean upon their own intellects in treading the paths of religion. Their opinions accordingly diverged, and there was disparity among them: some were more often right, and some were more often wrong. Our reliance is upon what the balance of religion bears witness to, which is the horizons and the souls; and in what has already been said on this, and in the demonstration that a belief holding the matter to be as they held it is false, there is enough to spare us all contrivance and lengthening. Banidh Qalis brought forward nothing beyond what the author of al-Nusra brought forward in his argument, and the saying of Banidh Qalis is more apt to be assailed.

We ask God to illumine our intellects, to seal our affairs with felicity, and to grant us success in good words and in righteous, sound deeds, by His bounty, His generosity, His gentleness, and His grace.

## 8. Motion, Stillness, and the Bodiless Intellect

This second part of Kitab al-Riyad takes up the adjudication between the two disputants over the question raised about the intellect, which is the first creation.

Chapter One of Part Two.

The author of al-Islah said:

We say: motion and stillness are two effects united in the first and the second. When the first became creative, it united with creativity, and that union produced two effects within it in potentiality. We speak of two effects because creativity and the creative are two distinct things: creativity itself, and the creative itself. Yet though they are two, they are understood as a single essence — the whole. These two effects exist in the first in potentiality. When the second is emitted from the first, that emission produces two effects in the intellect: the emission itself, and the union of the two origins within the matter. Of these two effects, motion is higher than the other, which is stillness — just as the first of the two origins is higher than the second. The motion of the first is its conveyance to the second of all the essences; stillness is the second's acceptance of the benefit received from the first, its taking in all those essences and holding them within itself. I do not say that motion and stillness are born of the soul by the power it learned from the intellect. I say, rather, that they are two effects of the soul — by the power it learned from the intellect — in the image and the form. And so the image and the form became the foundation of this world.

The sum of his statement in this section is as follows: motion and stillness are two effects united in the first and the second; they exist in the first in potentiality and in the second in actuality; motion is higher than stillness; motion is the benefit that the first conveys, and stillness is the second's acceptance of that benefit; the soul received them by the power it learned from the intellect, in the image and the form; and when the first became creative, it united with creativity, so that creativity and the creator, though they are one thing, are two.

The author of al-Nusra said:

This statement contains some confusion, for motion is only for the sake of seeking. The motion of bodies is a seeking of place: fire rises to seek its place, and earth descends to seek its own. The motion of souls is a seeking of benefit. Wherever there is seeking, there is need — for seeking arises only from need. It is impossible to say that the intellect needs anything for which it would move. Even if it were permissible to ascribe motion to the two origins, the soul is a more fitting subject for it than the intellect, since the soul needs to benefit from the intellect and moves toward it with the motion of longing — the motion of an effect toward its cause. If motion belongs to the soul, then stillness belongs to the intellect. The intellect is still within what it receives of the virtues and lights bestowed by its cause, seeking no increase — for its cause has already poured upon it such lights and radiance that the intellect knows no increase beyond what it has been given, and so has nothing to seek. Yet the intellect has not poured forth upon the soul all that was itself poured upon it — only a part. The soul dwells within what it has received, while the intellect withholds a part, so that the soul may move in seeking it.

The sum of his statement is this: motion arises only from need; the intellect is not in need and therefore does not move; it is more fitting to ascribe motion to the soul than to the intellect; motion belongs to the soul, and stillness to the intellect; and the intellect has poured forth upon the soul only a part of what it possesses, so as to be the cause of the soul's motion as it seeks the rest. This is the whole of what the two of them say.

We say: the author of al-Islah holds that motion and stillness are two effects united in the first and the second — existing in the first in potentiality and in the second in actuality. This amounts to saying that the first is at rest and in motion in potentiality, since both motion and stillness are in it in potentiality, and that the second is at rest and in motion in actuality. That is impossible, for several reasons. The author of al-Nusra's position on this point — that the intellect is not in need and therefore is not in motion — is the correct one. Motion, in all its kinds, is found only in natural bodies. The first and second intellects, belonging to the first existence, are not bodies, and so cannot be in motion or at rest.

The proof is as follows. A body requires matter and form together, each needing the other for its existence. A body's existence is therefore not from God — may He be glorified — as a first existence. Whatever has its existence from God as a first existence is an essence with no otherness in it: it is the very act that is the first existence from Him — may He be exalted and magnified — and the essence of that act is one essence, not two. If its existence were not a first existence, another would have to precede it in existence, and it would be something else. It follows from this argument that if the intellect were a body, something else would have to precede it in existence. But it is impossible that anything precede the first intellect in existence, for it is the essence of the act that is the cause and the creation — behind it is nothing but God the Creator, the Originator, may He be exalted, from whom its existence comes. Since nothing can precede the first intellect in existence, it cannot be a body. Since it is not a body, and motion belongs only to bodies, there can be no motion in the intellect. And since there is no motion in it, the claim of the author of al-Islah — that the intellect has motion in potentiality — is without foundation.

Chapter Two of Part Two.

The author of al-Islah said:

We spoke of two effects because creativity and the creative are two distinct things: creativity itself, and the creative itself. Creativity and the creative, though they are two, are nothing but a whole. This is a picture that may not be held as a literal belief, for the reality of creativity and the creative is contrary to what he mentioned — that they are two. Creativity is, in truth, the creative, and the creative is creativity; there is nothing there but one. When it is referred to in relation to that from which it has its existence, it is a creating — by virtue of being an act of Him and the very essence of the act. And when it is referred to itself, it is a created — by virtue of being the object of another, namely Him from whom its existence comes.

Chapter Three of Part Two.

We say, concerning the author of al-Islah's claim that motion and stillness are in the first in potentiality and in the second in actuality: whatever has a first existence can be nothing but pure act — the very reality of act. Were motion and stillness to exist in the first in potentiality, it would follow that the second is more noble than the first — for what subsists in actuality ranks higher in nobility than what subsists in potentiality. But it is impossible that the second precede the first in nobility. What al-Islah described belongs to the attributes of what is later in existence — things that need, in coming forth, an act and something already subsisting in actuality. The first intellect, however, is intellect in actuality — and so too is the second, and whatever follows them of the intellects separate from matter. It is not permissible to attribute to what is beyond nature, among the high limits, what necessarily belongs only to a being of the natural realm.

Chapter Four of Part Two.

As for the author of al-Islah's claim that motion and stillness are two effects of the soul — by the power learned from the intellect — in the image and the form: his meaning is that bodies, which are matter and form together, move by a natural power whose existence within them comes from outside them, from the first and second intellects — a motion of longing. To locate both effects — mover and moved — within the matter and the form is impossible. But to say that this power is learned from the intellect, in the sense that its existence derives from it, is sound. That is the best meaning that can be drawn from his words.

Chapter Five of Part Two.

As for the author of al-Nusra's claim that motion belongs to the soul and stillness to the intellect, it is not permissible to hold that as a belief concerning those transcendent limits. We say in summary: the world of origination — comprising both the preceding and the following — and the pure intellects, which are the high separate letters distinct from bodies, are too elevated for motion or stillness to be attributed to them even in imagination. Motion in a moving thing comes only from a mover within it that is other than it, and the existence of the thing's essence depends on both of them together. Whatever has the existence of its essence through differing things is an essence of deficiency and need. The following, which the author of al-Nusra named a soul, belongs to the world of origination — a world proper to completeness and perfection. Its essence is not an essence of deficiency or need. Since no difference can exist within it, there is nothing in it other than its own essence, and so it cannot be in motion, and motion cannot be in it.

Moreover, motion is that by which the thing in which it resides passes from its first state to another, whether in the essence or in its states. The following, which he named a soul, has an eternal existence — by virtue of arising, from the side of origination, in the second, and of being intellect. If it is eternal, it cannot pass from what it was at first, and so motion is impossible in it. The same reasoning that applies to motion applies equally to stillness: the first intellect has no stillness, and the following, which he named a soul, has no motion.

The author of al-Nusra, in what he said, intended only those souls and intellects that are in the abode of nature, emitted by a second emission. Such a being is moved, as we have explained in our book known as بِرَاحَةِ الْعَقْلِ — for motion and stillness belong to the world of the body and what resides in it, being among the causes of its perfection, and not to what is proper to the abode of origination. But he transferred the meaning, out of eagerness to satisfy the deficiency he inclined toward in support of his master. May God lodge them in the abode of His generosity, and gather them and us with our pure masters (صَلَوَاتُ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهِمْ اجمعين).

Chapter Six of Part Two.

The author of al-Nusra held that motion is for need, and that it is more fitting to the soul than to the intellect. It has already been made clear, from what preceded, that motion and stillness befit only what is a body or what is in a body, and that the first and second, of whom the discourse speaks, are not bodies — so the ruling of motion and stillness does not apply to them, as we have shown in our book known as بِرَاحَةِ الْعَقْلِ. His saying holds true for what is natural by way of the second emission, not for what is originated, whose existence is by the first emission.

Nor is motion in every thing a sign of need, as he claimed. Motion in a natural thing may be among the causes of its perfection — as with the motion of the spheres, whose movements are not driven by any need in their own essence to attain something they were not given from the beginning, but rather are a completion that belongs to them. The same is true of the motion of the speaking one — his striving in gathering the word upon worship, and his exertion in the affairs of governance. That is a completion incumbent upon him: something is required of him, something by which he is completed, after he has attained perfection and risen to the rank of the speaking one.

Since this is so, the author of al-Nusra's unqualified claim that motion is for need is not thorough. His ruling holds only in some natural things, where motion is inseparable from the nature of the thing, as we have described.

Chapter Seven of Part Two.

The author of al-Nusra said that the intellect did not pour forth upon the soul all that it has, but only a part. His meaning — that the first is distinguished by a rank of precedence and a loftiness of station through what is not proper to what lies below it, which he named a soul — is the best interpretation that can be drawn from his words.

Otherwise, if the matter were as he described — if each of the limits withheld from pouring forth some part of what it has — then it would follow that the blessings reaching the Prophet (صَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَآلِهِ) as an overflow from the world of holiness were such that he did not convey them in their fullness to the legatee and his followers, did not teach them to anyone, and that they came to nothing when their benefit ceased. This would leave the legatee (عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ) doubtful in his perfection, his worthiness, and his standing in the place of the Prophet (صَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَآلِهِ), and the obedience incumbent upon believers toward him would be uncertain in its obligation — held merely as opinion. That is not permissible to hold as a belief, neither of the speaking one, nor of the legatee, nor of any of the limits.

Rather, we say: the blessings are poured out and bestowed from the side of all the limits. The reason that the blessings by which perfection is attained do not reach a given being is not any withholding on the part of the limits, but the incapacity of the receivers to accept them. Each attains the blessings according to the strength of the receiver: some receive fully, others receive a part; and this is found among natural things, and among compassionate teachers and eager learners. It is not permissible for any of the limits to withhold benefit from one who is capable of receiving it, standing in his right, and willing — out of fear that he might rise to a higher rank. That would be before the spiritual birth.

And the speaking one (صَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَآلِهِ) recited what came to him from God, and said:

> ولا تقتلوا اولادكم خشية املاق ومن فعل ذلك فقد أثم وتعدى طوره واسقط نفسه عن مرتبة الفاضلين

Do not kill your children for fear of poverty; and whoever does that has sinned, and exceeded his proper stage, and cast himself down from the rank of the excellent. How, then, could it be permissible to lock away the spiritual wealth — the sciences and the luminous blessings — from its rightful owners, when the proof of their worthiness stands, and the Prophet has commanded its delivery, where He says, exalted is the Speaker:

> فَإِنْ آنَسْتُم مِّنْهُمْ رُشْدًۭا فَٱدْفَعُوٓا۟ إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَلَهُمْ وَلَا تَأْكُلُوهَآ إِسْرَافًۭا وَبِدَارًا

If you perceive in them sound judgment, then deliver to them their wealth, and do not consume it wastefully and in haste. No indeed: the blessings are poured out only that they may be conveyed to those who deserve them and settled in their proper place, as we have explained in our letters of interpretation and in our assemblies at Basra and Baghdad. May God make us, and a company of our brethren, among those who render the trust to its owners and fulfill its conditions. And perhaps the author of al-Nusra brought forward what he brought forward by way of analogy.

Chapter Eight of Part Two.

The author of al-Nusra said:

Moreover, action is the substance of encompassing — by it things are encompassed. This is not the state of what is in motion, for what is in motion does not encompass things. Rather, it needs something still — still at the utmost — whose stillness makes possible the encompassing of things. Were the intellect in motion, it would encompass nothing, and the soul would encompass nothing by benefiting from it. Since we find the intellect encompassing all things — so that nothing escapes it, and it encompasses whoever attains and acquires of its lights and virtues — we know that the intellect is at the utmost of stillness, and no motion may be imagined in it. The soul moves only to reach the inmost of the intellect, and then to become still. The soul does not move in order to encompass what it wishes to encompass through benefiting from the intellect. So long as the soul is in motion, it encompasses nothing and attains no virtues or lights — until it becomes still through benefiting from the intellect, and only then does it encompass things. The soul is thus in motion, a motion of subsistence, becoming still by benefiting from the one who is still. This is what he said.

We say: the question of the intellect's freedom from motion has already been treated at sufficient length to spare us repetition. Nor does it follow that, because the intellect is not in motion, it must be still in the manner of bodies — for bodies are never free of being either still or in motion. The intellect's existence is not like the existence of bodies, which are never free of that condition. In what preceded, there is sufficiency and evidence that motion and stillness do not befit it.

As for what he claimed to be necessary — that the soul, so long as it is in motion, encompasses nothing and attains no virtues — this is not sound, neither for what is separate from the second, as we have explained, nor for what is not separate. The souls in the abode of nature acquire only through motion in their bodies — through the use of the senses, through inference, and through benefiting. To maintain the contrary, when proof has been established against it, only confirms the persistence of the error.

Chapter Nine of Part Two.

His saying — that the soul is in motion, a motion of subsistence, becoming still by benefiting from the one who is still — is true when a meaning is assigned to it, and false when it is carried upon the surface of his words.

What makes it true is this: one takes "the soul" to mean the natural souls. The saying that the soul is in motion then means that the natural souls, which subsist in potentiality, are in motion for the sake of acquisition. The phrase a motion of subsistence means that their acquisition drives them toward subsistence. The phrase it becomes still by benefiting means that it passes from potentiality to actuality through acquiring, benefiting, and pursuing knowledge and virtues — in knowledge and in action. And from the one who is still means from the one who subsists in actuality.

What makes it false is taking his words at face value, for the second is not in motion, nor is the intellect still — since neither belongs to the abode of the body, where motion and stillness are necessarily found. Care must be given to knowing the ranks of the limits, lest the proper conception of things in their realities become disordered — and lead to the corruption of the substance of souls. May God illumine our intellects with their ink, and grant us the happiness of following the pure ones of the stages of the divine sciences and the treasuries of the holy lights (صَلَوَاتُ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهِمْ).

## 9. Light, Darkness, and the Matter Below

**Section One**

The author of al-Islah said:

As for what the author of al-Nusrah has established: we do not say that nature and the dark اَلْهَيُولَى are born from the Second as the Second is born from the First. That world is the world of honor and virtue; this world is the world of darkness and turbidity. The Second, though born from the First, resembles him; the state of اَلْهَيُولَى is not like that state.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

This is wrong. Were that world all light with no darkness in it, and this world all darkness with no light in it, no connection would fall between this world and that world. We say rather that the soul has two extremes. One faces the intellect, lying upon its horizon — this is the luminous, noble side. The other faces nature, which lies upon its horizon, and carries some darkness and turbidity, because nature falls upon that horizon. We say likewise that nature has two ends. One faces the partial soul, and in it there is some light, because it falls upon the soul's horizon. The other faces the elements — this is the dark and turbid side, in which there is no light. By these divisions we speak of the soul and of nature, of their darkness and their light. We do not say that that entire world is a world of light with no darkness, nor that this entire world is a world of darkness with no light. Were it as he describes, there would be between them neither neighboring nor pairing.

He said:

It stands established, then, that the dark اَلْهَيُولَى is born from the soul, though it does not resemble it, just as the soul is born from the First, though it does not resemble him. اَلْهَيُولَى fell dark by reason of its distance from the First Cause, on account of its being born from the soul. Likewise the diminution fell in the soul, because it is farther from the First Cause — by which I mean the Preceding One (السَّابِق).

This is his statement.

We say:

What the author of al-Islah says in his book is not what the author of al-Nusrah takes it to be. The heart of his argument — his answer to the claim that the Second is born from the First yet does not resemble him — is his own words: we do not say so. We say instead that the Second is born from the First, does not resemble him, yet is his counterpart and likeness, even though the First is higher in rank and nobler in station. This is like the male and the female in all genera and substances: the male, though higher and nobler than the female, is nonetheless the counterpart and likeness of his fellow.

Then we say:

The Second is too noble and elevated for its relation to what lies below it to mirror its relation to the First. The First and the Second are two counterparts, and they are the source of honor, perfection, and elevation — there is no honor above them. God made them the source of perfect honor and perfect light, of complete elevation and complete knowledge, with no ignorance, no turbidity, and no darkness whatsoever. They are the two foundations of the higher world, which is the source of purity, of sanctity (اَلْقُدْس), and of life. اَلْهَيُولَى, by contrast, is a trace of the Second — it is the substance of this world, a turbid and dark substance, not to be described by mercy or by power. The Second manifested it by act out of himself, by the power derived from the First, and then brought forth from it the form, which is nature, by act.

Then the author of al-Islah said:

It is not permissible to say that اَلْهَيُولَى is born from him. We say rather that it is a trace of him, and therefore does not resemble him. The Second is born from the First and does resemble him, even though the First is higher in rank.

This is his statement, and its substance is this: the Second issues from the First and is like him, though the First is higher in rank; and اَلْهَيُولَى is a trace of the Second, its substance dark and turbid, because the relation of اَلْهَيُولَى to the Second is not like the relation of the Second to the First. The First and the Second are counterparts, whereas اَلْهَيُولَى does not resemble the Second, being only a trace of him.

If this is the substance of his saying, then what the author of al-Nusrah offers in reply is no reply at all. The author of al-Islah did not assert in his statement that that entire world is light and radiance. He said only, in describing the state of the First and the Second, that God Most High made them the source of honor and elevation. This says nothing about whether things unlike them in state can exist. What has already been offered to indicate that اَلْهَيُولَى is not born from the Second is sufficient; there is no need to extend the discussion in ways that might cast suspicion upon an aim we do not hold in this section.

We say:

اَلْهَيُولَى does not resemble the Second, nor the First, because its existence, in its issuing from the First, is by the lower relation that we set forth in our earlier words and in our book known as Rahat al-Aql. In that is sufficiency.

**Section Two of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

This statement is wrong, because were that world all light with no darkness in it, and this world all darkness with no light in it, no connection would fall between this world and that world.

We say:

This statement, insofar as it charges the author of al-Islah with something he did not say, is an error; but insofar as it expresses his own view, it is sound. For in the world of origination and issuance there is that which is shared: it suits what is above it by being issued, and it suits what is below it by being a body. It possesses two aspects — it does not resemble the First from whom its existence comes; it resembles the Second in one respect and differs from him in another. The respect in which it resembles him is its being issued from him. The respect in which it resembles the body is its being a natural body. And that is nature. When it is joined to the world of origination and issuance, it is a life flowing from that world into what is below it, giving it what it is able to receive of succession and of conception. When it is joined to this world, it is a principle of motion and stillness in the thing it is in essentially, not by way of accident. So nature came to be, even though in its existence it is issued — an intermediary between the two issuings, the First and the Second.

**Section Three of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The soul — by which I mean the Second — has two extremes. One of them is toward the intellect, upon its horizon, and it is the luminous, noble side. The other is toward nature, upon its horizon, and it is the side in which there is some darkness.

We say:

That is not sound. Whatever has extremes has dimensions measurable by area. The Second — which he has called a soul in the way we have set forth — is not a body, and if it is not a body, it cannot have two extremes. By his words, moreover, he requires the Second to have a luminous extreme upon the horizon of the intellect, and an extreme carrying some darkness upon the horizon of nature — making it distinct from the intellect and distinct from the First by virtue of being upon the horizon of nature. Yet it is impossible that the Second should have any attribute by which it is distinct from the First, since its existence, in its issuing, derives from the nobler relation in the First — namely the First's being a cause. We have already established that the First, in its own essence, is not simultaneously cause and effect. It is cause by relation to what lies below it — this is the nobler relation in the First — and effect by relation to itself, which is the lower relation in the First. From its intellection of its own essence as cause there issues from it its like, for it is the nature of a cause to produce its effect from itself. From its intellection of its essence as effect there issues from it what is not its like, since this issuance derives from the lower relation in the First, namely its being an effect.

What we have said is confirmed by the condition of the Foundation (al-Asas), who is the living likeness of the Second in the knowledge of the body, and who is not distinct from the Speaker (al-Natiq). Everything that exists in the Foundation exists in the Speaker from whom the Foundation's existence comes, and thus the Foundation is not distinct from him. Yet not everything belonging to the Speaker exists in the Foundation, for the Speaker possesses something from which the Foundation is excluded — something the Foundation does not share. It is thus clear that the Foundation is not distinct from the Speaker: it shares with him everything that exists in it, and there is nothing in it that is not also in the Speaker. Since the Foundation is not distinct from the Speaker, and since it is the likeness through which — and through whose rank — the existence and rank of the Second are inferred, the Second is not distinct from the First. And since it is not distinct, its existence as he describes it — upon the horizon of nature — is impossible.

**Section Four of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

اَلْهَيُولَى fell dark only by reason of its distance from the First Cause, on account of its being born from the soul.

We say:

The unlikeness of اَلْهَيُولَى to the First, and the unlikeness of the Second, does not arise from distance from the First in the relation by which the First stands as effect, since the First is a gathering of two relations: its being cause, and its being effect. What comes to be from the relation of effect does not exist in the same way as what comes to be from the relation of cause, for the cause is always nobler than the effect and prior to it in rank and in nature. Of the First it is said that it is cause, and it is said that it is effect; yet it is a single essence, acquiring these relations through the additions.

**Section Five of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The diminution fell in the soul only because it is farther from the First Cause — by which I mean the Preceding One.

We say:

There is no diminution in the Second — which he calls a soul — as we have already established, nor is it remote from the First; on the contrary, it is his like. The diminution is a necessary attribute, not the very nature of the soul. The soul became deficient not from the side of its own essence, which is its perfection, but from the side of its second perfection, which it acquires by benefit.

**Section Six of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Nature's birth from the soul is no greater a step than the soul's birth from the intellect, for the soul, relative to the intellect, is farther removed than nature is relative to the soul. If the soul may be born from the intellect — despite the intellect's perfection, nobility, and nearness to the First Cause — then nature may equally be born from the soul, since nature resembles the soul more closely in all its states, acts, and powers than the soul resembles the intellect in its acts and powers. Among the ancients, nature is defined as the principle of motion; some called it the active power. From either of these definitions, the greater and fuller share belongs to nature. The soul, among the ancients, is the moving, abiding substance, and is characterized by intellect as well. Yet there is nothing in the intellect's definition that belongs to the soul's definition, nor is the soul defined by the definition of the intellect. The intellect is defined as the first of things: the Originated, the first effect, the first intermediary. None of these may be said of the soul — not that it is the first of things, not that it is originated, not that it is the first effect.

If, then, it is permissible that from the intellect — with its perfection, nobility, and nearness to the Word (الْكَلِمَة) — there should be born a likeness of the soul, which is not defined by the intellect's definition (and this cannot be denied), then it is equally permissible that from the soul there should be born a likeness of nature, which resembles the soul in its defining limits (and this too cannot be denied). Had one approached this matter with proper reflection, this insight would not have remained hidden. Had the author of al-Islah attended to the wonders and wisdom contained in nature, and to the splendor, nobility, goodness, and beauty in its acts — qualities by reason of which the partial soul desires to incline toward nature and follow it — he would have known with certainty that nature is born from the soul, though it does not resemble it. For causes always find the order of their effects beautiful, and it is for this reason that the partial soul desires to follow nature. All goodness, splendor, nobility, and adornment come from the higher noble world. Had nature not been born from the soul, it could never have borne witness to goodness, beauty, and adornment. Because it is born from the soul, it declares what it has beheld of beauty and nobility in the world of its cause — a deficient declaration, falling short of the soul's own. Mark this well.

This is his statement.

We say:

The sound clarification of what he has brought is contained in what has already passed in the discourse on the intellect, the soul, and nature, and on the existence of what precedes another in existence, and the rest. There is no need to extend it further by dwelling on the error that has persisted in conception. In that is sufficiency.

We may therefore take up the judgment between the two contenders directly: the question whether the soul is parts or traces, and the discussion that follows from it.

**The First Section**

The author of the Reform said:

According to what the author of al-Nusrah has established: the parts within us are traces of the first essence, not parts of it. Our union is by the traces that come from the first essence, not by its parts. The union of the First is not by its parts, because the First is united by the very Word. The parts within us, though they are traces of the First, are not parts of it, and the First is not united by them; rather, those parts are united by its traces, as we have shown in the union of its products with our traces within them. Nor is the union of the first essence with the Word of this kind, for the first essence is united with the very Word, until it and the Word became one thing. This is his statement.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

This statement holds confusion and manifest contradictions. He said that the parts within us are traces of the first essence — yet how can parts be traces, when they are essences subsisting in themselves? Traces have no subsistence in themselves. If he says that the parts within us are traces of the first essence, then, once the name "parts" has fallen upon them, he is obliged to show that they are parts of some essence. Were they not parts of the first essence, he would not have called them parts of any essence at all. And once it is established that they are parts of some essence, it is void that they should be traces, because the parts of a thing are not traces of another. This is his statement.

We say:

The author of al-Nusrah is right that souls are essences subsisting in themselves after acquisition, and that traces cannot subsist in themselves. Yet the author of the Reform refused to give the souls the name of parthood because he saw that the things which admit of division are the bodies, which are never free of the nine accidents and are grasped by the five senses. The soul is not a body, and that which overflows upon it is not a body either. So he did not see fit to give it the name "part," lest he set it in the course of bodies and misrepresent what the soul's own essence is and what it has disclosed of itself. Since he did not intend that, he said "traces," naming them as things that come to have essence and to endure. What he means is that these souls have their coming-to-essence and their endurance by the traces of those separate essences that reach them from the side of the limits, who are the intermediaries (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ). If this is so, then it appears that the intention of the author of the Reform, in saying "traces," is that which runs in the soul in the course of the form: that by which the essentiality of the souls is completed, and by which the soul endures when it receives it. This is what he made necessary where he says: we said only that honor and merit belong to these worlds because we received the trace of that noble essence in full, and were prepared to receive it. So it is established that by "trace" he intends that which is received, and that what the author of al-Nusrah intends by "part" is that which runs in the soul in the course of the اَلْهَيُولَى — which is that which receives. This is by way of likeness and approximation to the understandings.

**The Second Section**

As for the author of al-Nusrah's claim that the souls are parts of the Universal Soul, it rests upon no proof. For when something is divided, its whole is the sum of all those parts. If he grants this, he has made it necessary that the souls are parts of the Universal Soul. But concerning the souls in the abode of this world, the matter is settled by the proof already established, earlier and later: the Second — which he named the Soul — has its existence not in the abode of the body, which is the abode of this world. Since it is not in this world, it is void that the Second should be a whole for these souls. And once it is void that it be a whole for them, these souls are not parts of it.

**The Third Section**

His assertion that souls are parts of the Universal Soul does not hold together with his intention that the Universal Soul is the Second. For if the souls are parts of the Second, the matter is not free of one of these cases: either the whole of them, which is the Second, is in the abode of this world; or some of them are in it and some in the higher world; or the Second has nothing of parthood in this world at all.

It is void that the whole which is the Second should be in the entire abode of this world. What is in this world subsists by potentiality and has no passage into act except by that which subsists in act. The first Intellect is prevented from being that by which the souls subsisting in potentiality pass into act, because of what this would require of the precedence of that whose existence is impossible for God, glorified be He — upon which the discourse has already passed in a book other than this.

It is likewise void that some of them be in this world and some in the higher world. The part in the higher world, were it like this one in the world, subsisting by potentiality just as this one is, would stand in the very condition of this one. That is void by the proof already established: that the existence of the Second is an existence subsisting in act, not like the existents of this world's abode, which subsist by potentiality.

After the voidness of both divisions, it is established that the Second has nothing of parthood in this world, and it is void that the souls should be parts of it.

**The Fourth Section**

Beyond the matter of the parts, we note that a whole gives to all that is under it what it gives, in all its states, according to itself. So if these souls were parts of the Second, and we have found them neither knowing nor wise, then the whole whose parts they are would be of this same condition. To hold such a thing of that mighty limit is unbelief — we seek refuge in God — when it is known that the substances of the Messengers (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ) are from it, and that it is the proximate cause of the overflow upon the lower world. The souls, then, are not parts of the Second.

When it was established as void that these souls could be parts, we said: the souls are of the nature of those separate things whose existence is a creative existence. Their appearance and their existence are only from between the natural world and the world of emanation. The soul is a natural power that attains what it acquires from the side of the limits through obedience and the two worships. From its own self there is emitted an abiding, abstracted form, which resembles the abstracted things in the abode of the Hereafter from one aspect and differs from them from another.

Hence we say: it is the second emanation. Our epistles on the Return (al-Maad) contain the mention of that, God willing.

**The Fifth Section**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Likewise, our traces united with our products do not receive our traces after we have finished the work, nor do the products prepared to receive from us receive any further, except by the entry of corruption into them and their change from their states. They are not parts of us, nor are they essences subsisting in themselves, but rather vanishing accidents. This is his statement.

We say:

What is meant by this is that bodies reach a limit in what they can receive — a limit beyond which they receive nothing further. This is like iron heated in the fire: once it has reddened, that is its utmost, and it grows no hotter. Or like a black body, which grows no blacker, for it may reach in blackness a limit beyond which it is not further blackened. That meaning is more fitting to be held than what his own phrase requires. And it is not the traces that are the receiver; the receiver is other than they, namely the body, which is the substrate for the succession of the accidents and the traces upon it.

**The Sixth Section**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

But the speaking souls within us do not cease to acquire from the light of the higher, noble world, and to increase thereby in nobility within themselves, without any change occurring in themselves, and without the entry of corruption into them from the Soul when it wishes to benefit them. So it is established that they are parts and essences of the Soul, not traces of it.

We say:

That the soul is unbounded in receiving is, as he said, one of the proofs that the soul is not a body. But the matter of the soul being parts of the Second is not as he held, as the discourse above has already shown through what that claim entails and folds within it. The best that can be ascribed to his statement, for one who intends by "parts" this, is that these souls, which are the receiver, when they intellect their own essences as such, are then one thing with what they have intellected: it is they, and they are it, distinguished from it only by rank. So it is like the part of a thing in which is found what is found in the whole of it, their way in their own essence being one and the same from this aspect.

**The Seventh Section**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Also, to say that the speaking souls existing in this world are not parts of the Universal Soul nullifies prophethood, the imamate, the executorship, revelation, and the sacred laws. For it would then not be permitted for the soul to perceive that which influences it, nor to encompass it, nor to benefit from it. Yet the prophets claimed that they beheld the noble upper world, and stood upon the spiritual limits, and benefited from what they conveyed to those after them of light and exposition — from those above them, from the two roots and the three branches — and that they beheld the angels. Were their pure souls only traces of the Universal Soul, they would not have been able to comprehend what was revealed to them, nor to stand upon those spiritual realities that are parts of the Universal Soul.

We say:

The discourse has already established that the souls are neither parts nor traces, and that they are a second emanation. What the one who called them "traces" means is that by which the essentiality of the souls is completed and which earns them endurance. What the one who called them "parts" means is that the souls, by intellecting their own essence as such, are like those things that are their cause. Prophethood is not nullified by this, nor the imamate, nor anything else — for it is established that these ranks are not from this aspect. The prophets were able to perceive those things absent from them of the upper world only after they attained the rank of perfection and became thereby complete at its utmost. When they return to their own essences, it is as though they have perceived those transcendent essences by their very being — they and those essences being, from the aspect of completeness, one thing, with no difference save in rank. We have set out in our book known as Rahat al-Aql enough of that to suffice and to heal, that it may be known.

**The Eighth Section**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Rather, it is necessary to say that the Intellect is creative through the Word, the Soul is the image of the Intellect, nature is generated from the Soul, and the speaking souls are parts of the Universal Soul — so that by this order التَّوْحِيد, reward, punishment, prophethood, the imamate, the sacred laws, and revelation are made sound.

We say:

The soundness of التَّوْحِيد, of reward and punishment, of prophethood and the imamate, of the mission and the laws drawn from revelation — together with their establishment — is not from the aspect that the Intellect is creative through the Word and that the Soul is the image of the Intellect. Their establishment is from another aspect, which we have set out in our epistles and our writings concerning them. As for the Intellect, it may not be said that it is creative through the Word, for that would require the precedence of the Word over the Intellect, with the disorder this brings into the paths of التَّوْحِيد, according to what we have expounded in our discourse.

Then we say:

The Intellect is nothing but the very Word, and the Word is nothing but the very Intellect. This is confirmed by the statement of our master the Imam al-Muizz li-Din Allah (ع) in the book تَأْوِيل al-Sharia, where he speaks of the intelligibles: that the Word is an expression of the Intellect, and that it merits these names by relation. There has already passed, in our exposition and our discourse upon the Creator, the Intellect, the Word, and what is said of the world of creation and the first emanation — in the Illuminating Epistle and the other epistles — what suffices the seeker. As for the Soul and nature, they are two emanations from the first Intellect, each according to its own relation. For that reason one became more excellent than the other: the more excellent is the first emanation, the Soul, and the speaking souls are the second emanation. So what occurred in the first of them is emanated in the last of them. Our epistles alone on the Return (al-Maad) contain enough mention of that to make lengthening unnecessary, for the expansion of speech upon the like of this in every place is forbidden, and a gesture suffices the seeking student, while another discards it for the difficulty of its reach and the hardship of knowing it. God grants success to the believing knower for the knowledge of that according to his strength in sincerity to God and to His friends, and aids him when he is sincere to His face, exalted be He, and gives his allegiance to Him and to His friends. May God make us among the followers, the receivers, who fulfill His covenant; and may He grant us success in His praise, by His grace and His glory.

## 11. The Fruit of Two Worlds

The fifth book of the Riyad concerns the dispute between two thinkers over whether human beings are the fruit of the physical world. That is the question I now take up.

In the first section of this book, the author of al-Islah said:

Man is the fruit of this entire world, and it is for his sake that the world was created in all its parts — its origins and its foundations. The first of these is the اَلْهَيُولَى, which is the principle of the individuals born from it by motion and rest; then the individuals, which are the foundation of the vehicles; then the compounds, which are the very productions of this whole world; and the natures from which the human form is composed — a form prepared to receive the three souls: the vegetative, the sensitive, and the speaking.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

To say that human beings are the fruit of this world is the position of the materialists — those who did not affirm the soul's survival after the dissolution of bodies. For if human beings were the fruit of this world, this world would have held them fast and not allowed them to pass into another.

But we would say:

Human beings are the fruit of the highest world, the honorable and luminous world. That is why the prophets (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ) came with their warning: so that people might prepare themselves for the passage from the house of mortality to the house of survival, and from this world to the abode of the Return. The prophets knew that this world is no true home and that man is not its fruit. So said the author of al-Nusrah.

We say:

Each of the two is truthful in what he said from one side, and untruthful from another. The side on which they are both truthful is that each has said of human beings what truly belongs to him. The side on which they are both untruthful is that each denies the other its share — denies, that is, that man bears the title of fruit of both worlds together. For human beings issue from between the two worlds. From this world man receives his first perfection, and from that world his second.

The natural world fulfills its purpose by bestowing on its creature the first perfection: the bringing forth of the bodily form by way of the circling bodies, the luminous planets, and the proximate causes, until the creature arrives at the disposition by which it is ready to receive what belongs to it. And how could it not receive this, when it is that world's very fruit — the summit beyond which that world has well nigh nothing? The higher world fulfills its purpose by bestowing on what has been received from the existents of the natural world the second perfection: an effusion upon the spiritual image, that which is naturally disposed and intrinsic to it, by which it becomes substantial and endures. This second perfection is the knowledge of the Oneness of Allah Almighty by means of all the حُدُود, and obedience to them in the two modes of worship — in knowledge and in deed. That is man's fruit.

What confirms what we have said in this matter is the saying of Mawlana Amir al-Muminin al-Muizz li-Din Allah اَلْمَعَاد ibn Ismail (ع) in the book *The Interpretation of Sharia* (كِتَابُ تَأْوِيلِ الشَّرِيعَةِ): that man is the purpose of the second world and the end of the action of nature.

Now to the second section, concerning the objection of the author of al-Nusrah — that if human beings were the fruit of this world, this world would have held them fast and not allowed them to pass from it to another world. By this very objection he has handed his opponent the means to turn the matter back upon him.

For his opponent says:

If, as you have said, man is the fruit of the higher world, then that world would hold him fast and would not let him return to these persons, so that he should forget — which is impossible, according to your own writing and requirement. This is no argument that proves what he intends to demonstrate in the abstract. Neither is the sending of the prophets, whom he urges toward provision, a proof that human beings are the fruit of this world. For the human being who is in truth a human being is such by the conjoining of the effusion of the two worlds: from the natural world, that which runs in the channel of the اَلْهَيُولَى, and from the world of the Intellect, that which runs in the channel of the form. The one comes by intermediaries, and the other by intermediaries.

In the third section, the author of al-Islah said:

The Soul emanates from the First Intellect in perfection; the اَلْهَيُولَى emanates from the Soul in perfection; and the form emanates from the اَلْهَيُولَى in perfection. The whole, therefore, was perfect.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

If the matter is as he has said — that the perfection of the Soul is like that of the Intellect — then his statement about what is spread out upon the اَلْهَيُولَى, the form, and the compounds is void and self-contradicting. For it is not permissible to imagine that these fashioned things come to be except from the pairing of two: one perfect and self-sufficient, the other deficient and in need, so that a marriage may take place between them, as is found among things witnessed — such as the heaven and the earth. The heaven is as the male: perfect and self-sufficient in all its motions, its orbits being manifest. The earth is deficient, in the rank of the female, her need for him established. For this reason a marriage took place between them, and from between them appeared the various kinds of metals differing in their forms, and the plants, and the animals — not one of which appears actually in the heaven or in the earth or in the mothers, but which were in them in potency. When they were married, all of them appeared in actuality. Had their state not been as described — one deficient and the other perfect — nothing would have appeared from them. So said the author of al-Nusrah.

We say:

As for the statement of the author of al-Islah — that the Soul is the second, emanating from the First Intellect in perfection — it is correct, as has already been established above. As for his saying that the اَلْهَيُولَى emanates from the Soul: we have already shown that its existence is not from the Soul, which is the second, but from the First Intellect. Its existence and the second are together; neither precedes the other, and neither has existence apart from the other except in the soul by way of imagination. Outside the soul they exist only together.

As for the statement of the author of al-Nusrah — that it is not permissible to imagine that the fashioned things come to be except from the pairing of one perfect and self-sufficient with another deficient and in need, so that they may be coupled by what lies between them, and his likening of this to the heaven and the earth — he has refuted himself by the very words that follow. For he says that from between them appeared the various kinds of metals differing in their forms, and the plants, and the animals, not one of which appears actually in the heaven or in the earth or in the mothers, because they were in potency. And this is to say that if any of these things — I mean the generated beings — does not appear actually in the heaven and the earth because it is in potency, then the heaven and the earth are equal in respect of their being in potency, and no superiority of one over the other appears.

It is not the case that when existents come to be from between two things, one of the two must be perfect and the other deficient. We have already shown that deficiency is disclosed in things not in their selves but in their ranks, when some are set beside others, and in their actions, when they do not come to be except by something other than that which completes their existence — while in their selves they are perfect. For if their selves were not perfect in the existence that befits them, no action could proceed from them, neither by themselves nor by another. The condition in which the existents are actually found bears witness to this. The heaven, which he singled out for perfection and artistry, is deficient in its action, since its action is not completed except by that upon which it turns; and the earth, which he singled out for deficiency and need, has its deficiency not in its self but in its action, for its action is not completed except by that which turns upon it. So too with the animal and the newborn. And if this is so, then the saying of the author of al-Nusrah — that the statement of the author of al-Islah is a contradictory and prejudiced statement — is itself the contradictory and prejudiced statement.

In the fourth section, the author of al-Nusrah said:

He did not answer what was raised at the end of this section: that the name of perfection falls upon the Originator, glorified be He, because it falls upon perfect origination.

We say:

The author of al-Islah mentioned nothing of the sort. What he reported from the sages in the book of al-Islah — that they said the perfect Originator has perfect origination, and that the origination which is the perfection could be nothing but perfect — is a saying than which none could be more correct. By that saying he established that the perfect and the perfection are the origination and the originated, not the Originator, glorified be He. The name of perfection, by what he mentioned, does not fall upon the Originator, glorified be He, nor does his speech contain anything of that. The claim of the author of al-Nusrah here is prejudice against the author of al-Islah.

In the fifth section, the author of al-Nusrah said:

We all say that origination is the cause of the originated, and that it and the originated, from the side of the originated, are one thing, and it is perfect; and that what is originated by perfect origination could be nothing but perfect. Nor are we obliged to say that the Originator, glorified be He, is perfect, or that He is a cause, or that He, glorified and exalted, and the originated are one thing.

We say:

That is a statement correct in its meaning, save that we do not say that the originated by perfect origination could be nothing but perfect because origination is the perfection. Rather we say that the originated by the origination which is the perfection could be nothing but perfect; that the originated is the origination; and that it is the cause, the effect, the perfection, and the perfect. These names follow one upon another over it according to the relations, because there is a multiplicity there in the essences, so that the meanings of these names fall upon them all, while the thing there is one. In what we have set down upon this, in this book and in others, there is sufficiency without prolonging.

In the sixth section, the author of al-Nusrah said:

If it is established that this world is the act of the Soul, and that it is withal in the utmost order and arrangement, with perpetual circling motions, then we know and are certain that in her act she sought to attain the rank withheld from her before she began to act. Since this is so, and since there was in the world nothing more honorable than the human being, nor anything more disposed to receive the effects of the Soul from her and in which her benefits are more efficacious, we know and are certain that human beings are the fruit of the Soul's attainment of the end appointed for her from the side of her cause. So understand that.

We say:

It has already gone forth from our speech, in the matter of the perfection of the second — which in his account is the Universal Soul — what indicates that in her act she did not seek to attain a rank she had not gained. The Universal Soul, which is the second, effuses upon this world not to draw to herself what is not hers, for Allah enriched her, when He brought her into being, by perfection, so that she seeks nothing that was not given her. Rather her act is to establish wisdom in the bringing forth of what is in potency into act, and to make what is base excellent, and what is later in existence to follow upon what went before. And whoever considers it well finds that the benefit returns upon that which is brought forth into act, and upon him who is under the charge of striving toward perfection, not upon her who is neither charged nor in need. To believe otherwise of that mighty حَدّ is error and sin. And since this is so, and since the human being has two sides — one of them natural and one of them divine — he is neither the fruit of this world purely nor the fruit of that world purely, but the fruit of both worlds together. He is the last in the beings, united with eternity by what he has acquired in time. Therefore it has been said:

> مَا جَرَى فِي أَوَّلِهِمْ، جَرَى فِي آخِرِهِمْ

What came to pass in the first of them came to pass in the last of them.

And whoever has known from that the stations of the prophets, the guardians, the imams, and the righteous is disposed for deliverance. May Allah gather us with our pure masters in the House of Holiness.

As for the seventh section, and the saying of the author of al-Nusrah that every doer does what he does only in seeking to attain what he wills, and especially when the act proceeds from the doer with motion, order, and arrangement, and his holding that this is not binding upon the First Originator and upon origination, because the act there is by way of metaphor, not with motion, nor order, nor arrangement, but rather its first is its last, and its first and its middle are the utmost of its utmost, and its utmost is its middle, there being in it no order and no arrangement; but the order and the arrangement are in that which the second gains, from the lights of Him in whom there is no arrangement and no order, and He is the First Intellect; and the Soul comes to resemble the Intellect greatly, so that when she looks into it toward Him she apprehends the powers of things that are without order and without arrangement, apprehending things rather all at once, and this belongs to her only when she is united with the Intellect and has driven back the material things of dark nature:

We say:

How can what he said be sound, when order and arrangement are found only from the Intellect? And how can it be sound, when the things taken all at once are made excellent only by the Intellect, and the Soul, in which the effects of the Intellect leave no impress, is on a level with the beasts? It is for this reason that Allah has lifted knowledge from the Soul until the time when the effects of the Intellect leave their impress upon her. We have already said above that the proposition concerning doers is not universal, so that not every doer seeks by his act to attain a want or a purpose. What I reckon he intended by that saying is that the essence of the First Intellect is not composite, so as to have arrangement and order. But as for apprehension, the Intellect apprehends only by a true apprehension.

## 12. Body, Form, and the Two Letters of 'Be'

Here I speak of motion and stillness, of hyle and of form, and of how the two authors before me have reasoned about them.

**Section One**

The author of al-Islah said:

On the discourse concerning hyle and form: they stand as the spirit of motion and stillness, just as the body stands to them. The first hyle is the imagined motion within composite things, and the hyles, taken together, are three. Much of what has been said on this matter carries confusion, so I will state it briefly. Motion and stillness stand to hyle and form as the soul stands to the body, because hyle and form stand to motion and stillness as the body stands to them. The soul is subtler than the body, and the body denser than the soul; and in the same way, motion and stillness are subtler than hyle and form, while hyle and form are denser than motion and stillness. It is more fitting to represent the subtler by the soul and the denser by the body than the reverse. This is clear, and its proof lies with it.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The relationship of soul to body, and the measure taken between them, does not rest upon subtlety and density alone. Were it so, perceptible substances would have to count as bodies for the accidents, being denser than the accidents, and the accidents would have to count as souls for those substances, being subtler than them. We must instead look to the definition of body and the definition of soul, and then liken whatever resembles the soul's definition to the soul, and whatever resembles the body's definition to the body.

We find the body to be what receives the three dimensions: length, width, and depth. We find the soul to be what has parts that resemble one another — much as water, fire, and air are each alike in their parts. In the same way, we find that motion divides into three kinds, as the body divides: motion from the center, motion toward the center, and motion around the center. For this reason, motion must resemble the body in being divisible — splitting as the body splits into length, width, and depth. Hyle is not like this, for the parts of hyle resemble one another. Since it is more fitting to liken motion to the body, the two being alike in the matter of division, and to liken hyle to the soul, the parts being alike, rather than to liken motion to the soul: one of the two, namely divided motion, resembles the body, and the other is the body that is not divided; while hyle resembles the soul, being undivided, and the other is the body that is divided. This is what they said.

We say:

In this section both authors have gone far astray in their analogies and likenings. Both have built upon a corrupt foundation on which no proof can stand, so that neither the likeness nor the thing likened holds. The author of al-Islah has laid it down that motion and stillness stand to hyle and form as the soul stands to them — and this is impossible. Motion is the act of the mover, and the mover is what we call the soul, the self. Stillness, likewise, is the mover's leaving off the moving of what it moves. If the mover is the soul, then to represent motion — which is its act — by the soul is an impossible representation; and the same holds for stillness. When this is shown to be void for motion and stillness, it is shown void as well that motion and stillness should be hyle and form.

Moreover, the way of hyle and form is not the way of motion and stillness. Motion and stillness, though each is an act, are two contraries that cannot meet in a single place, a single state, or a single time. Hyle and form, by contrast, exist together, neither parting from the other — except that one is the agent, which is the form, and the other is what is acted upon within it, which is the hyle. Our well-known book The Rest of the Mind gathers the explanation of this.

**Section Two**

Then he was led to say that the soul is subtler than the body, on the ground that the soul is a body — and this is impossible. The soul cannot be a body to which density or subtlety could attach, for density and subtlety belong only to what is a body: air, being a body, is marked by subtlety, and earth, being a body, is marked by density. The soul is the self, and proof has been firmly established that it is not a body. If the self is not a body, it is impossible to argue that it is subtler or denser, or to represent it in such terms.

**Section Three**

The author of al-Nusrah came forward and undertook to set out the definition of body and the definition of soul. He defined the body by its receiving the three dimensions, and defined the soul by the likeness of its parts to one another — drawing it out in the manner of water, fire, and air, each of which is alike in its parts. This is impossible in light of what has already been said: the soul is not a body, and so this cannot attach to it. The soul and the self are two synonymous names for one thing, and that thing is not a body. Were it a body, the nine qualities and accidents that attach to a body would attach to it, and it could be grasped by a sense. But since it is not grasped by a sense, it cannot be perceived; the nine accidents are neither imagined in it nor present in it; and since bodily accidents do not inhere in it, it cannot be a body. Our well-known book The Lamps holds what heals concerning this.

**Section Four**

Then he made motion — by its division into three sections — resemble the body, in the way he described: that the body divides into length, width, and depth. This is impossible. Each of the three dimensions into which he says the body divides is not itself a body, nor is it called a body; whereas each section of motion is itself a motion, and is called a motion. How, then, can the body — which divides by form into nine sections, as we have shown in our well-known book Milestones of Religion — be likened to motion, which divides into three sections, or into six? That is far-fetched indeed. The discourse in this chapter, from the author of al-Mahsul, the author of al-Islah, and the author of al-Nusrah, is confused and without order.

We say:

Motion is that by which a thing is changed — whether in place, coming-to-be, passing-away, increase, decrease, or alteration — and it proceeds from the mover, which is the principle within the thing in which it resides, by nature and not by accident. Hyle and form are what emanates from the First by the lower relation within it, as the second emanates by the nobler relation within it. Hyle has no existence outside the soul at all except together with form; and from it come the heavens and the earth and what is between them. God, exalted, brought all of this into being from the First Intellect, which He originated from no thing. Exalted be God with a great exaltation.

**Section Five**

The author of al-Islah said:

The First Cause — which is origination, and which the learned name the word of the Maker, mighty and exalted — has for its form:

> كن
>
> "Be."

This is two letters: a كاف (kaf) in motion and a نون (nun) at rest. The كاف and the نون of the word occupy the place of the body, while motion and stillness occupy the place of the soul. On this pattern the likeness lies in motion and stillness as the two things effected by the word, and the First Cause — which is the word — is the hyle of the whole world. On this same pattern it must be said of motion and stillness in all substances, from hyle and form down to what is below them, throughout all the worlds. The sum of the discourse on the three hyles is this:

1. The first hyle is imaginary, and it is motion and stillness; it is the very substance of hyle and its form.
2. The second hyle comprises the individuals and the things generated from the first hyle.
3. The third hyle is the four composite mothers derived from the second hyle.

Then we say:

Hyle and its form are the act of the command, which is origination. Just as we have said that hyle is imaginary, so too origination is imaginary: its essence appears only in the First Originated.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

As for the ground the author of al-Islah took in كن — saying that the كاف is in motion and the نون at rest, and that this stands in the place of the body while motion and stillness stand for it in the place of the soul — this is not granted to him. These two letters are used to speak of the matter here only because there is, at that level, a كاف, a نون, a motion, or a stillness; for it cannot be said that above the First Effect there is motion or stillness or anything of this manner of speech. Rather, there is only pure origination and the complete cause. This is what they say.

We say:

The claim of the author of al-Islah — that the First Cause, which is origination, is a word of God, exalted, and that its form is these two letters — is a remote analogy in the way he intended it, and it scarcely holds. For our saying "form" requires something for which it is a form, and the word has nothing for which its form would serve as a form. When we say "form" meaning what is above nature, we mean the abstract form; and when we say "form" meaning what is in the natural world, we mean the form that inheres in matter. Had he said instead that origination — the word of God, exalted, and His command, as expressed by كن — is two letters, one in motion and the other at rest, and that this points to an essence gathering two attributes, he would have drawn from it a proof of that noble Essence and its attributes.

He said:

It gathers two states, just as the word كن gathers two states: motion and stillness. He then referred those two states to what belongs to that Essence of the two noble relations — one being its being a cause, the other its being an effect. He set the motion whose end is stillness against the relation that is the cause whose end is the effect, and the stillness whose end is motion against the relation that is the effect whose end is the cause — not because there is motion or stillness there, but by way of making the likeness understood. Had it been so, it would have been more fitting, and what he said would have met the very state of the thing spoken of, which is the thing likened by the likeness.

**Section Six**

Then he said:

The first hyle is imaginary — and he made it motion and stillness; then he made motion and stillness one with hyle, while form is not hyle. Rather, motion and stillness are hyle and form. For hyle is a substance emanating from the First, in the manner already discussed, and from it is the lower world. As for motion and stillness, their existence is in bodies according to the principles from which their appearing comes; and hyle and form together constitute the body, the body's being lying in hyle and form.

**Section Seven**

Then he said:

When he made the first hyle out of the matter which is origination — saying that the first hyle is imaginary, so that origination is imaginary, its essence appearing only in the First Originated — his saying entails that there existed, from eternity, together with the Maker (exalted be His majesty above that), something in which origination appeared, so that by origination it became originated. This is a grave error. The doctrine and belief concerning divine unity is that whatever is other than God, exalted, is originated and brought to be in time, having no reality in existence at all; and if that is so, origination is nothing other than the Originated. We have established the proof of this in what has preceded of our epistles, to a sufficient degree.

**Section Eight**

As for the saying of the author of al-Nusrah:

In what he stated, and in the way he opposed the author of al-Islah, he is correct — except that his saying that it should not be said that above the First Effect there is motion and stillness amounts to affirming them in the First Effect and in what is below it. Yet we have shown that the First Effect is not in motion, that motion does not befit it, nor does stillness, since it is other than a body.

**Section Nine**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Contradiction is in bodies, and there is no contradiction in souls.

We say:

Bodies, insofar as they are bodies, have no difference among them and no contradiction. Difference and disparity fall only in the forms that are within them — the forms that move them — not in respect of their being bodies.

And his saying:

That it is more fitting to liken that in which contradiction is found to what is likewise — the matter is as he said, if the very thing spoken of is such.

## 13. The Small World

Here I set myself to judge between the two, and I speak now, in the sections that follow, of the divisions of the world.

**The First Section**

The author of al-Islah said:

The whole world is composed of four jewels, four structures, and four classes of beings: three of them without humankind, the fourth being humankind. The knotted and the dissolved are all one essence. We do not say that the pair of humankind is the form of the horse; rather, the pair of the horse and its form is what belongs to the horse's own essence — just as gold is paired with silver, and ruby with emerald, so too the horse with its like. The horse's likeness lies among the animals: the elephant, the falcon, the lion, and the rest. Since all of these are animals, it is not permissible to say that the horse is the pair and form of humankind, for humankind alone possesses the rational soul and has no peer among the parts of the world and its generated beings. Humankind is rather the counterpart of the world itself, because it gathers within itself all the world's jewels. For this reason it is named the small world: it stands as a counterpart that collects all the world's parts.

We say:

His claim that humankind has no peer among the parts of the world, and that humankind is the counterpart of the whole world, follows from his prior argument: that the world is divided into four parts, that the horse does not stand in the form of humans, and that humans have no peer among the world's parts but are the equivalent of the whole world.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The state of pairing is not only the state of union; the state of pairing is also the state of acceptance.

We say:

This dense lower world is the pair of the subtle upper world, and if you look between the two you will find the distance between them greater than the distance between the horse and the human. The horse is not of the human race except insofar as it shares in the genus of life. He made this dense lower world the pair of the spiritual world by virtue of the connection between them in the matter of benefit and beneficence: the forces of the upper world are joined to this world, and this world receives those forces from the upper world — and for this he made this world the pair of that world. Likewise the human and the horse: he made the horse the human's pair because it accepts teaching from him more readily than the other animals do. This is the substance of his saying — that the horse was made a pair for humankind because it accepts teaching from him as no other animal does.

We say:

The two who investigated these matters did not rely upon sound research. The author of al-Islah divided the world into four jewels, four structures, and four classes including humankind — and that is impossible, for the divisions of the world are more numerous than what he set down. The word "world" names many things, from the center to the encompassing ocean: the sphere, the planet, fire, air, water, earth, and the generated beings. From the plain sense of his words it is clear that he means the generated beings of the world, not the world and its generated beings together. These are three genera, with no fourth, and under each of them fall many species. The first is the nearest of the four elements; its beginning is the minerals, which are everything whose existence is confined to one place in the earth rather than another, without its parts increasing — and this gathers together what melts and what does not, what can be beaten out and what cannot. The second is the plant, which is all that grows from the earth, and this gathers together the herb and the tree. The third is the animal, which is all that moves among those that have species, and this gathers together what flies and what does not. The generated beings of the world do not exceed these three, and the human — whom he named the fourth — is of the genus of animal, a species among its species, set apart by consciousness.

**The Second Section**

He said:

That humankind has no peer and that the horse has no pair. This is impossible from two angles. The first is that the human is a species within the genus of animal, and insofar as it is a species it is a pair to the other species of its genus, since all species are forms of one another in respect of their being species. If humankind is a species, then it is a pair to the other species. The second is that humankind, being similar in its structure, morals, actions, conditions, motions, and natures to all the beings that preceded it in existence, is for that very reason called the small world — and so must be a pair to all things according to the correspondence and likeness within it. If this is so, then humankind, when it is good, virtuous, wise, religious, knowing, and pure, is married to the angels by the correspondence between them; when it is courageous, brave, bold, and ready to help, its pair is the lions; when it is unjust, cruel, and murderous, its pair is the wolves; when it is deceitful, its pair is the foxes; when it is a thief and a traitor, its pair is the crows, the magpies, and the cats; when it possesses guile, cunning, and force, its pair is the leopard; and when it is given to fornication, immorality, amusement, and play, its pair is the monkeys. Upon this pattern, whatever a person trains himself to resemble he comes to resemble — whether by his actions, his morals, his customs, or in any manner of his ways — and that thing becomes a pair to him. If this likeness is established, then it is plain that his saying is not exhausted where he claimed that humankind has no peer, for humankind has forms and pairs from many sides.

**The Third Section**

As for the author of al-Nusrah, he spoke rightly regarding the meaning of the genus, yet he erred in his saying that the horse was made a pair for humankind because it accepts the teaching of humankind. For if it becomes a pair by way of acceptance and teaching, then the horse has no greater claim than any other animal to be its pair, since among the animals there are others besides the horse that accept teaching and discipline; and if it is by way of species, then again the horse has no greater claim than the rest of the animal species to be its pair. In that case humankind has many pairs, not one alone.

In sum we say:

The true pair of humankind can only be the angels. And what we mean by "humankind" here are those possessed of all the virtues — the prophets, the guardians, and the imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ), and those who follow in their footsteps, who uphold their obedience and worship Allah with both acts of worship. Not those beneath them among the people who bear the outward form of humankind while their souls are the souls of beasts and predators — like the monkeys, the donkeys, the foxes, the beasts of prey, and the wolves, as we have said and shown. Those who follow the traces of the Imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ), and above them the prophets and the guardians, are distinguished from the beasts by their striving in obedience through the two acts of worship and their holding fast to command and prohibition, though they resemble the beasts in form. In this way they come to resemble the guardians, and so they are saved in their company and among them and none other. This accords with the verse:

> وَمَنْ يَتَوَلَّهُمْ مِنْكُمْ فَإِنَّهُ مِنْهُمْ
>
> "And whoever takes them as guardian from among you, he is one of them."

May Allah place us among those whose gathering and raising is with the prophets, the guardians, and the pure Imams.

**The Fourth Section**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

He mentioned Bandhaqlis, who said:

The bestial soul returns to the rational soul through love; and the rational soul, when it deems good what returns to it from her, inclines toward her with benefit and mastery and finds her image beautiful. There is no image among the bestial souls more beautiful than the image of the horse, nor any more disposed to receive the traces of the rational soul. For this he made it a pair for it. This is plain.

We say:

Had the author of al-Nusrah drawn his evidence from the Book of Allah, Lord of the Worlds, and from the words of our masters the pure Imams, for what he intends concerning the matter of the soul by way of interpretation, what is found there would have freed him from clinging to the words of Bandhaqlis. For had Bandhaqlis and those like him among the ancients lived in our time, they would have needed to draw from the seas of the knowledge of the House of Revelation, overflowing from the treasuries of Allah in His earth, and from our pure masters; and they would have turned back from much of what they believed concerning what is hidden from the senses — of which they spoke: the intellects and their ranks, the soul and its principles — and believed otherwise. For what Bandhaqlis intended in his saying, once his statement is weighed rightly and read in the sound sense, is other than what the author of al-Nusrah took it to be — namely, that the bestial soul is the horse and the rational soul is humankind. Rather, since the sensory soul, which is the bestial one, accepts the ruling of the rational soul when no obstacle hinders it, and the rational soul, when it sees the other accepting its ruling, inclines toward it with benefit and sets it right, we say: by "the sensory soul" he means whoever bears the form of humankind, whose nature it is to accept teaching, discipline, and reason — and not the horse; and by "the rational soul" he means the teachers, who are the prophets, the guardians, the imams, and their followers, whose nature it is to incline toward whoever bears the form of humankind, to teach them their interests in their religion and their world, to benefit them in their right guidance, and to raise them in degrees according to their acceptance — they being in truth humankind, and not every one of the people. This accords with what is indicated by the interpretation of the saying of Allah Almighty:

> يَا أَيَّتُهَا النَّفْسُ الْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ ارْجِعِي إِلَى رَبِّكِ رَاضِيَةً مَرْضِيَّةً
>
> "O reassured soul, return to your Lord, pleased and pleasing" (the verse).

And to carry his statement upon the sound face is preferable to carrying it upon the unsound one. The horse is no more receptive than one who bears the form of humankind, nor is the horse's image finer; nor are the guardians, the imams, and their followers less compassionate toward those beneath them than men are in the sport and gentling of the horse — rather, they are far more so. Since this is so, and since there is nothing in the plain words of Bandhaqlis to establish that no image among the bestial souls is more beautiful than the image of the horse — nor is it the case, among the animal images that exist, that the horse's image surpasses all others, nor is the matter as he said — to single out the horse as the pair of humankind on the grounds of teaching and beauty of image, and to argue by it, is therefore impossible.

**The Fifth Section**

The author of al-Islah said:

Humankind is the counterpart of the whole world and is preferred to it by a noble essence that is not of this world. His body stands opposite all bodies in their genera — the solid and the dissolved — because in his body there is something corresponding to each of their genera: bone, flesh, nerve, veins, and cartilage, and all the flowing substances that issue from his body, such as blood, semen, and sweat, and what flows from the eye, the ear, and the mouth, and the rest of his interior. All of these are of the essence of bodies and of their kinds, and they correspond to the kinds of the essence that is one of the three essences of the world — the essence of bodies, which is not united with any of the three souls. His growth stands opposite the growing kinds among the species of trees in their varieties, and this is of the kinds of the second essence, united with the growing soul. His sense stands opposite the animal in its differing kinds, and this is of the kinds of the third essence, united with the sensory soul, the bestial soul. This is his correspondence to the three jewels of the world, and he stands alone in the fourth rank, so that he became the fourth essence — the essence of the rational soul — alone in this station. The substance of his saying is that humankind is the counterpart of the whole world: his body stands opposite all the bodies of the world in the difference of their forms, his growth opposite the plants, his sense opposite the animal, and he possesses that by which he surpasses all and stands alone, which is the rational soul that is his.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

As for his saying that the dissolved and the contracted are one essence, that is not to be rejected. The dissolved, the contracted, the growing, the sensory, and the rational are all one essence in respect of being essence; but in respect of their divisions and properties, the contracted essence is not the dissolved essence, and the dissolved is not the contracted. They are distinguished only in the locus where they are distinguished, not in the locus where they are not distinguished. He needed to distinguish between them only because he made the human a mediator between the dense bodily world and the subtle spiritual world. For in him is all that is in the two worlds, and so everything in the lower world is likened to him, until nothing of it is left out. He therefore set the contracted bodies opposite his body — his body likewise being contracted, as the bodies are — then set the dissolved bodies opposite his growth, on account of the dissolution within them, for dissolution occurs only in the motion of the jewels that dissolve, and likewise growth occurs only by motion; when the two resemble each other in respect of motion, each is likened to its companion. He set the growing bodies opposite his sense, and set the animal opposite his speech. So the remainder of the world's bodies — contracted, dissolved, growing, and sensory — is gathered in him, and he surpasses the rest of the jewels by speech. The substance of his saying is that humankind resembles the world until nothing of it is left out: his body stands opposite the contracted bodies, his growth opposite the dissolved bodies, his sense opposite the growing bodies, and his speech opposite the animal, and he surpasses all the rest of the jewels by speech.

We say:

The disagreement between the two in this section is this: the author of al-Islah set opposite the human's growth the plant of the greater world that grows and develops, while the author of al-Nusrah set opposite it the dissolved bodies; and the author of al-Islah set opposite the human's sense the animal of the greater world, while the author of al-Nusrah set opposite his sense the growing bodies; and the author of al-Islah made the rational soul the very thing by which the human is set apart, preferred above the world and made its master, while the author of al-Nusrah set that opposite the animal. The saying of the author of al-Islah is the closer, since what he likens is of the genus of that to which it is likened, and it has a conformity that the saying of the author of al-Nusrah lacks. In sum, the human is not the counterpart of this world only, but the counterpart of both worlds together — being the collector of all the things that preceded him in existence and, by what is in him, resembling both of them. The matter concerning him is upon this, and not upon what he said.

**The Sixth Section**

We said that the human, who is humankind, is the counterpart of the two worlds because he gathered the multiplicity existing between origination and the second emanation, then became one, so that in himself he became parallel to the world of origination and emanation, and by his body — the collector of its parts — parallel to the bodily world, and so parallel to both worlds. As for his resemblance to this world: the world is entirely body, of it the subtle, which is that which is high, and of it the dense, which is that which is low; and the human body is likewise subtle and dense, the subtle being that which is high and the dense that which is low. In the world there are planets and encompassing spheres; and in the human body likewise there is the skin encompassing the whole body and the rest that clothes the bone, and what runs in the course of the planets, such as the two ears, the two eyes, the nose, and the mouth. In the human there is likewise a mover that moves, which is the soul. In the world there are the four elements; and in the human likewise the four natures. In the world there are rivers, valleys, and waters of differing tastes, and mountains and minerals; and in the human likewise, as the author of al-Islah set forth: the bones and cartilage, the flesh and the blood, and what flows from him and what issues out. In the world there is what grows and develops without sensing; and in the human likewise there is what grows and develops without sensing, such as the hair and the nails, and his body grows and increases from its smallness to its greatness. In the world there is the sensing animal; and likewise in the human the sensory faculties. The whole world is structures and compositions, and by what exists in the human the resemblance to the world is complete — though all that we have set down here in summary is detailed in our book known as The Peace of Mind. As for his resemblance to the world of origination and the first emanation: that world is the world of intellect, the world of glorification, praise, and purification, the world of unity and deification, the world of virtues, the world of generosity, and the world of bounties. And the human, who is humankind, is likewise the collector of these virtues — in intellect and glorification, in unity and deification, and in bounty and generosity toward others and in himself. That world has marked him, and by his body he has resembled this world; and being thus, he came to be distinguished from those that preceded him in existence by rank alone. May Allah make us among those who follow the traces of His friends, and let us not be excluded from the intercession of His chosen ones, by His grace and His bounties.

**The Seventh Section**

And the saying of the author of al-Islah — that humankind is preferred over the world by the noble essence that is not of this world — is not to say that the essence which he denied to be of this world is of that world, I mean the high and noble upper world, according to what was disclosed to him. Rather, since it is neither of that world only nor of this world only, we said that it is of both worlds together. For his state in existence between the two worlds is like the state of the child in his existence between his two parents: it is not permissible to attribute him to one of them apart from the other, since it is by them both together, and from them, that he came to be. What may be attributed of him to this world is that which runs in him in the course of matter, which is the receptive; and what may be attributed of him to that world is that which runs in him in the course of form, which is the received — as with the child, who has from the side of his two parents, the higher and the nobler, his form, and from the other side, which is below that, the receptive matter. The proofs of this are many, found in our book known as The Peace of Mind. And Allah Almighty grants us success toward the abode of His religion, and provides us the intercession of Muhammad His prophet, and the Imams of his pure family, and his household.

## 14. Decree and Destiny

**Section One of Chapter Eight**

The author of al-Islah said:

Those who say decree comes first and destiny second are mistaken, for destiny precedes decree, and what is prior comes before what is posterior. Decree cannot be identified with what precedes the posterior, since decree itself comes after destiny. Decree and destiny are words whose meaning is plain in common speech: destiny is estimation, and decree is elaboration. There is no elaboration except after estimation, so the first precedes the second by the widest possible margin. The witnesses to this from the Book of God are His words:

> قضي الامر الذي به تستفتيان
>
> "The matter concerning which you two sought counsel has been decreed."

meaning that it has been separated out and brought to an end; and His words:

> فَإِذَا قُضِيَتِ ٱلصَّلَوٰةُ
>
> "When you have finished the prayer,"

meaning, when you have completed it. Decree is like a garment that the tailor first estimates: before he cuts it he estimates it, and he adds and subtracts, widens and narrows; but once he has cut it he has decreed it and made an end of it, and after that no increase in it is possible. That is the likeness of decree and destiny. The conclusion is this: decree cannot be predicated of what is prior, since it comes after destiny; and destiny cannot be predicated of what is posterior, since it comes before decree. Destiny is estimation and decree is elaboration, and so long as something remains within estimation it may be enlarged, reduced, or altered; but once it has passed into elaboration and been decreed, no increase or decrease in it is possible.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Decree is not elaboration. The act of elaboration within decree matters no less than the separated thing itself, for the separated thing is fixed upon its form by a decree that requires it — just as that same separated thing is established only by what has been decreed for it. As for destiny, it is estimation, as he says — but there is no estimation except through a judgment that demands that estimation. The witnesses he drew from the Book of God, showing decree to be a bringing to completion, cannot be dismissed. The sages have said: when God, exalted be He, originated the Intellect, He brought the two worlds to completion, since the Intellect holds the sum of the images of both worlds and nothing of those images is absent from it — it is pure completion. Yet the author of al-Islah has erred in likening destiny and decree to the garment that the tailor estimates. He assigned estimation to destiny and the cutting to decree, but the analogy does not hold. He should instead have matched the image of the sewing that lives within the tailor's soul to the decree, since sewing is spiritual; and matched the estimation by which the tailor measures that sewing to destiny, for estimation too is a spiritual thing that has not yet emerged to the level of intellect — this is what the image of the two origins signifies. Then the cutting he performs after estimation, upon the bodily boundaries that have emerged from the first and the second, corresponds to what appears in the body. If the matter is as described, then decree precedes destiny, just as the sewing precedes the estimation, for by that sewing the tailor is made ready to estimate. So decree is prior and destiny is posterior.

This is the upshot of his saying:

Decree is ahead of destiny, and there is no estimation except by a judgment, so decree is prior and destiny posterior.

We say:

The interpretation each contender gives of decree and destiny — however each assigns them to the prior and the posterior — is impossible, even granting the meanings of the two words as we have established them. The author of al-Islah, by saying that destiny is estimation and decree is elaboration, and by the analogy of the garment and the sewing, has committed himself to the position that destiny is what stands in potency — what could yet be — and that decree is what stands in act, having already passed out of possibility into actuality. But if this were the meaning of destiny and decree, then neither could be predicated of the prior or the posterior at all, since neither the prior nor the posterior stands in potency, nor passes from potency into act — as the proofs we have already set down make clear.

**Section Two of Chapter Eight**

The author of al-Nusrah, by saying that there is no estimation except through a judgment that demands it, and by representing decree with the craft of sewing from which estimation proceeds, has committed himself to placing destiny posterior — while conceding that the meaning of destiny is, as the author of al-Islah established, the existence of an existent in potency. Yet this cannot be predicated of the posterior, for we have shown that the posterior already stands in act; and if it could be so predicated, then destiny would not be posterior. And if destiny is not posterior, then decree has still less claim to be prior — it is even further from being predicated of the prior — since the posterior was never in potency and never passed into act in the manner its own word implies of the prior's condition. It is equally impossible that decree and destiny be predicated of the prior and the posterior. Since this is so, we say that both men are correct only in the sense we shall now explain — not in the sense they pursued — for decree participates in meaning with several things, and those things all belong to the world of body, not the world of origination; and so too with destiny.

**Section Three of Chapter Eight**

As for decree, it points — first — to the Speaker who was in potency and then emerged into act by the support of God, exalted be He, and to those who take his place (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ), who have reached in their attainment the rank parallel to the first upsurge. All of these — in their standing in act, in their command, their prohibition, and their decree within the summons that is already established in act, having been in potency — answer to the meaning conveyed by the word "decree." This is what the author of al-Nusrah intends, and on this basis his position and his placing of decree first are sound.

**Section Four of Chapter Eight**

Decree points, second, to what is drawn out by interpretation from the potency of the revelation and the Law — from the sciences that reside within them in potency rather than in explicit form — into exposition and act, by which souls are led from the limit of potency to the limit of act. This is what the author of al-Islah intends by his interpretation of the word "decree."

**Section Five of Chapter Eight**

Decree points, third, to what emerges into act and to the latent unfolding of events and transformations appointed to arise in the world — through the movement of moving things and the stillness of still things — those that admit of no reversal. For this reason, when some matter comes to pass and is realized in act, it is said that there is no repelling the decree of God. These are the things of which decree is predicated.

**Section Six of Chapter Eight**

As for destiny, it points — first — to the promised الْقَائِم, foretold and heralded from the time of Adam (ع), and to his summons, whose safekeeping and upholding are borne by the Imams and the Messengers (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ) through their summoners in the period of concealment. Its force persists in the fact that he is appointed to come forth from estimation into act and existence. His summons, though established, stands — from the side of the ranks of the cycle of the Prophet ﷺ and his family — in a concealment that runs in the manner of what stands in potency, not in the manner of what stands in act and is manifest. For this reason the inner meaning of the Night of Determination, Laylat al-Qadr (ع), is he, and to him point the allusions of the shaykhs in their saying:

كوني قدر
"Be, O destiny."

**Section Seven of Chapter Eight**

Destiny points, second, to what the Prophet ﷺ and his family brought from the revelation, the Law, the rulings, and the parables that hold within their potency the sciences and knowledge the soul needs in its passage to maturity and act — cast in a form that gathers all of this together. No complete science is found on the outer face of these except through interpretation drawn out of them, and this is the meaning of destiny to which the author of al-Islah points.

**Section Eight of Chapter Eight**

Destiny points, third, to the world in all its parts, which has within its power to come forth into being and act through the passage of days, months, years, events, and the processes of coming-to-be whose existence proceeds from it by the perpetual movement of the movers over what lies beneath them — of necessity, since these are appointed within it. For this reason it is said: what God has appointed will be, for its coming-to-be is by necessity of its existence — and though the appointed time be long, it stands as something already existing in act. So God, exalted be He, said:

> إِنَّهُمْ يَرَوْنَهُۥ بَعِيدًۭا وَنَرَاهُ قَرِيبًا
>
> "They see it as far off, but We see it as near."

Whatever was in estimation and appointed to exist becomes decree once it exists, for the word "decree" signals what was in potency and then came out into act. These are the meanings of decree and destiny.

If one ponders the saying of the author of al-Islah — that destiny precedes decree — its meaning is sound only in respect of what the Prophet ﷺ and his family brought of the revelation and the Law, which precedes the decree that is interpretation, just as he himself explained and interpreted. And the saying of the author of al-Nusrah — that decree precedes destiny — is sound only in respect of the Speaker (ع) who stands in act, who is the decree that precedes what he brought of the revelation and the Law, which is destiny.

**Section Nine of Chapter Eight**

The author of al-Nusrah said, reporting from the learned sages:

When God, exalted be He, originated the Intellect, He brought the two worlds to completion, because it is the gathering place of the images of the worlds.

We say:

The Intellect that is the gathering place of the images of the worlds is not the First originated Intellect, nor the second Intellect, which is the second upsurge. It is rather the Intellect that ascends from the world of nature by acquisition — in deed and in knowledge — to the parallel of the first upsurge, so that it surges in a second surging and thereby becomes a compendium of the images of the worlds in all their multitude, its very essence imprinted with the forms of all existents, as is the case with the Enunciators, the Executors, and the Imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ). The First Intellect is too exalted to be multiplied in this way, for its existence is originative, and originative existence is multiplied only according to what it intellects of its own essence — namely, that it is a cause and an effect, and that its existence derives entirely from what lies beyond itself, from which it received. The remaining existents — by which I mean the Intellects that surge in a first surging — are multiplied according to what I have said concerning their essences and concerning those whose existence precedes them, back to the First Intellect, their existence being, along with this, from that toward which they turned in devotion, which is beyond them. So the later in existence is not multiplied as the prior in existence is multiplied. The need of what is later in existence to be multiplied, so as to intellect all existents, is a binding necessity; whereas the First in existence is entirely free of that need, perfect as it is. If this is so, then it is clear that what is a compendium of the images of the two worlds is not the First Intellect, but the Intellects that surge from the abode of nature in a second surging.

Then we say:

The First Intellect has no need to intellect what is beneath it, nor to form any image, for its essence is, from eternity: completeness, subsistence, unity, and immunity from change. It has these qualities only by reason of that from which it came to be. And since its being is eternal, complete, subsistent, singular, and immune from transformation solely by reason of that from which it came to be — exalted be He — it is impossible that it intellect anything beneath it, for it has no need of such intellection.

And we likewise say:

What is later in existence, from the second surging, is required to intellect what precedes it and so to become a compendium of the images, because its completeness — the ground of its subsistence — is acquired through that intellection, that is, through intellecting what precedes it in existence. Since it needs, for its completeness, to intellect those whose existence preceded it among the ranks upon whom the interpretive summons was established — which is the second worship — so that their stations may be made plain, it is required to teach them and to intellect them. In teaching and intellecting them, its essence is multiplied on the side of the multitude of its intellected objects, while being unified on the other side — its own essence — so that in that unity it resembles what precedes it in existence. Our book known as Rahat al-Aqlِ, The Peace of the Intellect, contains of the evidences of legal proof for this, and of the religious depictions, what suffices and heals the seeker for the sake of his return. May God make us among those who follow His good pleasure in whatever brings distress or delight, and who follow His friends therein.

**Section Ten of Chapter Eight**

The author of al-Islah said:

The error belongs entirely to those who made decree the prior and destiny the posterior. They found that the learned had named the posterior by the name of destiny — which is indeed its true name — because all the essences surged from the prior in an appointed measure, and that surging was destiny itself, so the sages called it by that name. Then the phrase "decree and destiny" came to be spoken together among people, and as decree was placed before destiny in speech — one says "this is decree and destiny" — its meaning was hidden from them. They assigned decree to the first and destiny to the second, and from this the error arose. This is what we have said: all the essences surged from the prior in an appointed measure, and that surging was destiny itself — it is the compendium of the essences. Confirming this is the saying of God, exalted be He:

> إِنَّا كُلَّ شَىْءٍ خَلَقْنَهُ بِقَدَرٍۢ
>
> "Indeed, We have created everything according to a measure."

For just as all the essences surged from the First — which is the command in appointed potency — and were destiny itself, so likewise from the second there appeared, in act, out of matter and form, and that act was decree itself. And within destiny there occurs change and alteration, and it is the book of which God, exalted be He, said:

> يَمْحُوا۟ ٱللَّهُ مَا يَشَآءُ وَيُثْبِتُ
>
> "Allah erases what He wills and confirms."

Destiny is the essence of the posterior, as we have said, and decree is its act, for the posterior is what is appointed in measure from the prior. The conclusion of his saying in this section is: destiny is the name of the posterior, because all the essences surged from the prior in an appointed measure, as God, exalted be He, said — "Indeed, We have created everything according to a measure" — and decree is its act.

The author of al-Islah said:

As for what he invoked concerning decree and destiny from the saying of God, exalted be He — "Indeed, We have created everything according to a measure" — he has erred in the interpretation of this verse. Its interpretation is as we shall expound and state, for by it He intends only the pure Ipseity.

We say:

The meaning of إِنَّا, "We," — by which He intends the Ipseity — is that which no attribute and no negation of attribute taints. "Everything," كُلَّ شَىْءٍ, by which He intends the Intellect, which is all things. "We have created it," خَلَقْنَهُ, by which He intends the image of the two worlds among them. And "according to a measure," بِقَدَرٍ, by which He intends the posterior, upon which the settling of the matter rests, so that all the spiritual and bodily images appeared from between the two in the manifest — the pen, the tablet, the writing, and the Writer. The Writer corresponds to إِنَّا, "We," and He is the pure Ipseity; the Pen corresponds to the Intellect, which is the pen of pens; "We have created it" corresponds to the Tablet; the writing corresponds to the letters that appear upon the Tablet; and "according to a measure" corresponds to the Tablet that is receptive to all the forms of the letters. For just as all bodily and spiritual things — the simple and the dense — fall under the intelligible letters that appear from the Writer, by the Pen, upon the Tablet, so likewise God, exalted be He, brought forth all higher and lower images by means of the Intellect and the Soul, and nothing was withheld from between the two — just as nothing of things is withheld from the ordered letters that appear in act from the Writer upon the Tablet. This is the saying of the author of al-Nusrah in refutation.

We say:

The two contenders have brought forward in this section what cannot stand firm, neither for this one in his emendation, nor for that one in his refutation. The author of al-Islah, in holding that destiny is the name of the posterior and arguing for it from the saying of God, exalted be He — "Indeed, We have created everything according to a measure" — has already been answered by what came before in the discussion of destiny and decree, from which it is plain that destiny may not be predicated of the posterior. The author of al-Nusrah, for his part, rested his counter on what he interpreted from the verse, and this interpretation is neither balanced against nor congruent with the matter.

For he said, interpreting: God, exalted be He, by إِنَّا, "We," intends only the pure Ipseity that no attribute and no negation of attribute taints. But the word إِنَّا, "We," refers only to the limit from whose side the Prophet draws supply by his standing in the divine place within the world — not the pure Ipseity itself. How could "We" fall upon the pure Ipseity, when between Him and it lies the intermediary of utter negation? It falls rather upon the near intermediary — the Messenger of God ﷺ — in the outpouring upon him, and the meaning is the Messenger, since he is the one who conveys on His behalf.

**Section Eleven of Chapter Eight**

He said: "Everything," by which he intends the Intellect, which is all things. But "everything" in this place is not the Intellect, for we have already shown that the First Intellect is not multiplied as that whose existence is "everything" is multiplied — which is what the community needs of the two comprehensive worships that gather the sciences, outwardly in deed and inwardly in the understanding that leads to deliverance from the torment of the two worlds.

**Section Twelve of Chapter Eight**

He said: "We have created it" — by which he does not intend what he went to, for "We have created it" means "We have appointed it in measure." I mean: We made the form of the revelation and the Law a form within whose potency lies everything of the sciences and religious works the community needs to ward off pain from the soul in this world and the next. It is as though He said, speaking on behalf of God, exalted be He: all that We have created according to a measure — concerning the matter of the two worships in knowledge and deed which the community needs in its religion and its worldly life — We appointed in measure within what We brought of the revelation and the Law; that is, We placed it within its potency, that it might come forth in interpretation to the one who merits it.

**Section Thirteen of Chapter Eight**

As for the saying of the author of al-Islah that decree is the posterior act, he has hit the mark in one respect: what comes out into act from the world of nature comes out by its outpouring, is completed by it, and attains completeness from its side. For this reason decree came to be predicated of the Speaker, since his coming out into act and his attaining perfection are by its outpouring and its support.

**Section Fourteen of Chapter Eight**

He said: All the essences surged from the prior in an appointed measure. This contains a contradiction, for the posterior is an essence that surged from the prior, yet it is not appointed in measure. What his saying intends is rather that surging from which the bodily world came to be — which is appointed in measure so that things might come from it — as we explained in the chapter of destiny, not the posterior.

**Section Fifteen of Chapter Eight**

As for the saying of the author of al-Nusrah, in the passage where at its head stands "We have created it" — by which he intends the image of the two worlds from between them — and "according to a measure," by which he intends the posterior upon which the settling of the matter rests, so that all spiritual and bodily images appeared from between the two: the existence of things is not such that what is bodily was and what is non-bodily was in the manner he described, by reason of the corruption we have shown in the foundation upon which he built his speech. Were it not that a full account of the order in which the existence of existents rests upon the First lies outside the scope of this writing, we would have brought it; and its mention will not fail to appear, God willing, in our epistles and our books.

**Section Sixteen of Chapter Eight**

The author of al-Islah said:

As for the interpretation of the saying of the Messenger of God ﷺ:

>
> "I flee from the decree of God to His destiny,"

he meant only to flee from the subtle support of the prior to the exposition of the posterior, as a man flees from fire, which is a sign of support, to water, which is a sign of exposition. For he wished to flee from the thing that has already been sealed and decreed, whose repelling cannot be contrived, to destiny, which has not yet been decreed.

This is the account, and it is not well-ordered. The leaning wall is a structure that has leaned so far that it is about to fall, and it stands for one of the ranks who has resolved upon breaking his covenant and upon hypocrisy, and has inclined from obedience to transgression; for when he breaks his covenant he falls from his station, as God, exalted be He, said:

## 15. Fleeing to God's Measure

The author of al-Islah completed his point: to set a man upright signifies the speed of his walking, for to follow a man in his walking means the statement, the proof, the haste, and the quickness of pace. The Messenger of God ﷺ would address those bent on hypocrisy with outward preaching and warning, not with the inner declaration. The measure governs the outward, as we have explained, and the decree governs the inward. His fleeing from the decree of God to His measure was on account of this wall — wary lest it fall, wary for himself — and not a fleeing from the dignity God Almighty had bestowed on him, nor from the degree to which God had raised him.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

He is, upon the seeking of increase, the more eager.

The words *Flee from God's decree to His measure* are not to be interpreted as the author of al-Islah imagines — that the Messenger was fleeing from the rank of the Follower to the rank of the Preceder. By his own claim, the prophets (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ) forever seek ascent, and the seeking of height is not the seeking of depth. We say, by the help of God Almighty and His blessing, that the meaning here turns on three senses, and each one establishes that the decree belongs to the Preceder and the measure to the Follower.

The first, and it is the greatest, the most fitting, and the most exact in meaning: what is intended by his fleeing from the decree of God to His measure is this. From the Preceder, God willing, comes the support; from the Follower comes the structuring; from the Speaker comes the composing of the Law; and from the Foundation comes the interpretation of the whole. The interpretation, which is the end, most resembles the support, and the composing most resembles the structuring. The Speaker (ع), in this saying, was making plain that his composing of the Laws resembles the Follower in its structuring of the world — and that he does not resemble the Preceder. He fled from the decree of God, which is the Preceder, refusing to liken himself to it in the origination of support, and turned toward his measure, which is the interpretation, to which he likened himself.

The second sense: his informing his community that he draws benefit from the First by means of the Second, so that in everything he wished to stand upon from the First, he returned to it by way of the Second. It is as if he said: In all that I wish to grasp, I return to the one who is the link between me and the Intellect — and that is the Second.

The third sense, and it is the most honored: what the author of the book mentioned — his fleeing from the intensity of the light of the support of the Preceder toward the support of the Follower, since اَلْجَدّْ, with his spiritual power, could not bear it without his very identity nearly dissolving. That is his saying in the story of Moses (ع). This is their statement.

We say:

The author of al-Islah, in interpreting the verse, took a course that conforms to how the matter truly stands concerning the decree and the measure, and it is well done. Better still had he said this: the Prophet ﷺ was a prophet who reported hidden things — the events of existence, what was yet to come, what lay within men's breasts — by what God Almighty had singled out for him of revelation. He wished to make plain how what اَلْجَدّْ cast to him was opened to him. So he said: I return — which is his saying, *I flee.* I mean: through اَلْجَدّْ I cross, from the side of the ranks of God Almighty, the whole of what is on the point of coming to be among the happenings and events of existence — this is the decree of God that inevitably goes forth into act — toward His measure, which is God's creating of the souls and the horizons and their motions that point to what He has measured within them to be, so that what has been made plain in the door of revelation is opened to me. For this God Almighty said:

> سَنُرِيهِمْ آيَتِنَا فِى ٱلأٓفَاقِ وَفِىٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَهُمْ أَنَّهُ ٱلْحَقُّ

We shall show them Our signs upon the horizons and within their own selves, until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth.

And He said of those who reflect upon the creation of the heavens and the earth:

> رَبَّنَا مَا خَلَقْتَ هَذَا بَطِلًۭا

Our Lord, You did not create this in vain.

This does not merely point to whether a thing exists early or late among the events of being; rather it points to that thing and gives it utterance. It is as if he said: In what اَلْجَدّْ casts to me, I turn wholly to the measure, so that it is glimpsed or opened by taking form before me and being pictured to me. That is his saying: *I flee from the decree of God to the measure.* This is a kind of revelation, and the book *Rahat al-Aql* contains the explanation of it.

### The Seventeenth Section of the Eighth Chapter

The manner in which the author of al-Nusrah pursues his answer reveals his surrender to the author of al-Islah. He brought forward nothing to overturn what al-Islah built his argument upon, except the three meanings he adduced, reckoning them his own. He held that each of them establishes that the decree belongs to the Preceder and the measure to the Follower — not because the Speaker resembles the Follower in his composing of the Law, as al-Islah argued, but because the Speaker returns, in all he wishes to grasp, to the Follower. Even were he to establish this, it would still oblige him to establish that the decree belongs to the Preceder and the measure to the Follower, and yet there is no correspondence at all between the decree and the measure on the one hand and the meanings he set out on the other. Had the meaning of the decree and the measure conformed to the state of the First and the Second — fitted to them from some angle — it would have been allowable to say that the decree and the measure fall upon them from that angle. But the decree means what has already been said of it, and so too the measure, and it is not fitting to predicate these of the First and the Second, for they are free of what the meaning of these two terms would require. Whoever applies them in this way is like one who says of honey, whose nature is sweetness, that it is aloes, whose nature is bitterness.

### The Eighteenth Section of the Eighth Chapter

His likening, in the second sense, the Speaker to the Follower does not conform to the law of the guiding call. Within the world of body and the world of religion, the Speaker holds the station of the Preceder, and the station of the guardian in both of them corresponds to the station of the Follower in the world of origination. The Speaker and the Foundation are thus two likenesses of the Preceder and the Follower, and two likenesses of the sun and the moon.

### The Nineteenth Section of the Eighth Chapter

The third sense he adduced — making it the ground of the Speaker's fleeing, namely that it was from the intensity of the light of the support of the Preceder toward the support of the Follower — runs contrary to what is due. The interpretation he cited in evidence, concerning the state of اَلْجَدّْ, calls for full investigation and further reckoning, and its discussion will come. In sum, the saying of the author of al-Islah in this section is the stronger.

### The Twentieth Section of the Eighth Chapter

The author of al-Islah said, drawing upon the verse in the story of Moses:

> رَبِّ أَرِنِىٓ أَنظُرْ إِلَيْكَ قَالَ لَن تَرَىٰنِى وَلَكِنِ ٱنظُرْ إِلَى ٱلْجَبَلِ فَإِنِ ٱسْتَقَرَّ مَكَانَهُۥ فَسَوْفَ تَرَىٰنِى فَلَمَّا تَجَلَّىٰ رَبُّهُۥ لِلْجَبَلِ

My Lord, show me, that I may look upon You. He said: You shall not see Me, but look toward the mountain; if it settles in its place, then you shall see Me. And when his Lord disclosed Himself to the mountain,

> جَعَلَهُۥ دَكًّۭا وَخَرَّ مُوسَىٰ صَعِقًۭا

He made it crumble to dust, and Moses fell down thunderstruck.

Moses sought to have unveiled to him more than had been measured for him of the rank of the Preceder, so he was told: *You shall not see Me, but look toward the mountain.* God thereby made known to him that he would not attain that, and bade him keep to what flows to him by اَلْجَدّْ, making known that even the one who asked him could not — for all its subtlety — attain the like of what Moses sought. How then could he attain what he cannot see? God says: *If it settles in its place, then you shall see Me. And when his Lord disclosed Himself to the mountain, He made it crumble to dust, and Moses fell down thunderstruck.* When Moses knew that he had erred in seeking what he could not receive, he humbled himself before his Lord, and when he came to himself he said:

> سُبْحَنَكَ تُبْتُ إِلَيْكَ

Glory be to You, I turn to You in repentance.

This is his saying.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

*My Lord, show me, that I may look upon You. He said: You shall not see Me* means: you have not the strength, and you cannot endure that rank with patience. *But look toward the mountain* — and it is اَلْجَدّْ, for all his nearness to Me. If he settles upon his rank, the one I have given him, and bears what I have not disclosed to you, then you too shall bear that. But اَلْجَدّْ did not bear it when it was disclosed to him, and his very identity nearly dissolved. This is his saying.

We say:

The interpretation of this verse, as each of them has given it, lowers Moses. For having placed him in the station of the prophets and the highest rank — among those who commune with the upper ranks — they then cast him out of the company of the knowing and counted him among the ignorant, alleging that he sought what he could not grasp from the ranks above him. How could that be, with the whole matter passing him by? The true interpretation of the verse is other than what they proposed. The address to Moses was not concerning the upper ranks; it was an address from the lower ranks, at a time when he had not yet reached the degree of Speaker. He asked of the master of the affair what its hour had not yet come, for he was in an age of interval, and the people of truth lay beneath affliction, veiling, and concealment by their opponents and adversaries. Moses saw in himself a power by which he reckoned it possible — in that age of his, were he commanded — to lift that ordeal from the people of truth, to take vengeance upon the enemies of God, and to govern the community by the governance of truth, just as the Foundation (ع) had once reckoned, by the power he found in himself, what he reckoned, and then repented of it when he knew he had erred, whereupon the sun was returned to him and restored, as we have set out in our epistles by interpretation.

So Moses asked of the master of the affair — who is Shuayb (شُعَيْب), to whom he had betaken himself, and who is his Lord — after Shuayb had made his command clear to him regarding what Moses found in himself of power and capacity for governance. That is his saying, *My Lord, show me, that I may look upon You,* meaning: command me, that I may order this affair and lift the distress. He said: *You shall not see Me,* meaning: you shall not see Me do any of that, nor shall I invest you with a command at this time, for to everything there is an hour and a season. This is not the season for bringing the community out of what they are upon — following their own whims — nor the time for returning affairs to the canons of governance, nor will what I say to you be brought to completion. Shuayb (ع) wished to alert Moses to his error in the best of ways, and to teach him that what he sought is hard and will not be brought to completion in that age at the hands of one possessed of power, aid, and readiness — how then at Moses' own hands? Such a completion would need to come from a man of great standing in the call, mighty in worldly leadership, possessed of aid, wealth, men, and dominion. He said to Moses: I have come to know that this affair will not be completed in this age — it is hard and cannot be measured out. My refraining is not out of stinginess toward you, nor a preference to delay you from your station. But look toward the mountain — meaning this man of worldly power, readiness, and capacity. *If it settles in its place, then you shall see Me:* if he stands firm in what he has been commanded, and is enabled, with the power and aid he possesses, to spread out the ordinances of the governance of truth, and it is completed for him, then you shall see Me do what you asked concerning those people. *And when his Lord disclosed Himself to the mountain* — meaning, when He commanded him to raise up the beacon of truth — *He made it crumble to dust:* the affair grew straitened upon the people, they did not accept the command, the staff was split, and the affair fell into confusion and disorder upon him, his leadership standing on the brink of ruin as the people rose against him. Moses then knew that what he had reckoned could be completed at his hands in that hour was a reckoning of the impossible, and that the master of the affair — who is his Lord — was more knowing of the matter than he. He awoke, by that alerting, from what had been confused upon him, and said: *Glory be to You, I turn to You in repentance.* He recanted his saying, and repented, and held to patience, as the author of al-Nusrah had commanded him, until the term in which he had been hired elapsed and the time came. Then there came from him what came, and his saying was verified — *then you shall see Me* — the affair was surrendered, and you do what you wish. This is the interpretation of the verse, not what is reckoned by the one who lowers and raises, which does not correspond.

### The Twenty-First Section of the Eighth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

As for his argument by the saying, *And He created everything and measured it out in a measuring,* this is the clearest of proofs that the measure belongs to the Follower and the decree — which is the creating — to the Preceder, since the meaning of *And He created everything* is that by it He intended the Preceder, for the Intellect is all things, and all things are the Intellect.

We say:

The discussion of the measure and the decree has already gone before, and it was made plain there that the decree and the measure may not be predicated of the Preceder and the Follower. What God Almighty intends by His saying,

> وَخَلَقَ كُلَّ شَىْءٍۢ فَقَدَّرَهُۥ تَقْدِيرًۭا

And He created everything, then measured it out in a measuring,

is not what the author of al-Nusrah proposed. We have already shown the corruption of that reading and that it befits neither the Preceder nor the Follower to have the decree and the measure said of them. What is intended in His saying *And He created everything, then measured it out in a measuring* is that He placed everything pertaining to the affair of religion and its welfare — outwardly and inwardly — within the power of the Revelation and the Law, and measured it out according to how the Revelation and the Law stand in their encompassing of all the sciences that pertain to religion and its welfare. These sciences exist within the two of them by potency, and interpretation draws them forth.

### The Twenty-Second Section of the Eighth Chapter

His saying that the Intellect is all things and all things are the Intellect is a proposition that does not hold. The bodily world, with all it gathers, is not an intellect; it is a thing. The finest sense in which the saying *the Intellect is all things and all things are the Intellect* may be attributed is this: the Intellect, which is the very command of God Almighty, is the cause of the existence of all things — bodily and otherwise — and the existence of all things is from the Intellect and directed toward its purpose. That is what he intended.

### The Twenty-Third Section of the Eighth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

God has granted the prophets license in the inner meanings of the Laws, and dispensed them from employing them, in His saying:

> لَيْسَ عَلَى ٱلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّلِحَتِ جُنَاحٌۭ فِيمَا طَعِمُوٓا۟ إِذَا مَا ٱتَّقَوا۟ وَّآمَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّلِحَتِ ثم اتقوا وآمنوا واحسنوا ان الله يحب المحسنين

There is no blame upon those who believe and do righteous deeds for what they have partaken, so long as they are God-fearing and believe and do righteous deeds; then are God-fearing and believe; then are God-fearing and do good. Indeed, God loves the doers of good.

We say:

The verse contains, neither in its outward nor its inward sense, any mention of a license for the prophets to abandon the employing of the Laws. I do not know how this escaped him — that he loosed such a saying without examining it fully. The exposition and interpretation of this verse run contrary to what he proposed. God Almighty, knowing that souls do not attain felicity, reach the degree of perfection, or pass from the limit of potency to the limit of act except by work and knowledge, laid upon all mankind the establishing of the two worships — in knowledge and in deed — so that by them they might attain the two felicities in the two abodes. Since souls do not reach their perfection and felicity save by the two worships, any soul that abandons both, or one of them, is far from attaining perfection. This holds throughout the soul's bond of life: it stands before God until the time of its passing, whereupon it is either in the Garden, if it kept both, or it abandoned both or one and is in the Fire, until God forgives it, as we have set out in our books. We have shown, in what has already gone before of this discussion, that souls do not stand save by work and by the reforming of character — which is the greatest striving, as the Messenger of God ﷺ said — and by acquiring the virtues through discipline and knowledge, in relation to what precedes them in existence, according to what the interpretive call requires. If the states of souls in worship are of this nature, how is it allowable for license to be granted to any soul, or to the Speakers, or to those beneath them among the ranks, to abandon the work and the perfecting of souls — both the complete and the deficient — by it?

No. The verse carries an interpretation other than the one he gave. It bears a particular meaning for those who stand in the call — the summoners to the affirmation of the oneness of God Almighty and the knowledge of His ranks by way of the Imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ). The Speaker knew that his community would differ after him: each faction would set itself apart with a doctrine it invented from its own opinions and deficient minds; they would choose, over the guiding community for whom God has appointed guides, those for whom God has made no light. He knew that the summoners would suffer from this divided community every hardship — opposition, injury, and the impugning of their doctrine and their masters, the pure Imams. He therefore granted to the summoners who stand in the call a license to look into sciences not of their own belief, upon conditions he set forth, so that those sciences might serve them as equipment in their disputations. He said:

> لَيْسَ عَلَى ٱلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّلِحَتِ جُنَاحٌۭ فِيمَا طَعِمُوٓا۟ إِذَا مَا ٱتَّقَوا۟ وَّآمَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّلِحَتِ

There is no blame upon those who believe and do righteous deeds for what they have partaken, so long as they are God-fearing and believe and do righteous deeds,

meaning: there is no blame — no straitness — upon those who stand in the call to the oneness of God and the knowledge of His ranks by way of the deputies, who bring respondents to faith when they join themselves to the rope of God and hold fast by loyalty to them against doubts and confusions in the religion of God, and who bring them to the degree of perfection and attainment — no blame upon these in what they have partaken, that is, in what they have read of the sciences not of their own belief, so that those sciences might serve them as a weapon for fighting the adversaries in establishing the truth. And this is upon three conditions:

The first: *so long as they are God-fearing and believe and do righteous deeds* — meaning, so long as their path is straight in obedience to God Almighty, in following His Messenger (ع), in belief in His ranks, and in establishing the two worships together according to what he brought.

The second: *then are God-fearing and believe* — meaning, they obey the Messenger in his legatee and in the one who stands in the affair in his place, and they establish the call to him, outwardly and inwardly.

The third: *then are God-fearing and do good* — meaning, they obey the Imam in the one he sets up and invests with the affair, and they do good in the call to the oneness of God Almighty under his imamate.

If they do this and hold to these conditions, there is no harm in their looking into the doctrines and sciences that belong to other peoples, in pondering them, and there is no straitness in their religions in doing so — for these are an aid to them in what they need to withstand the enemies and repel whoever seeks to impugn the ranks. This is the interpretation of the verse, and it contains no license to abandon the employing of the Law; it is only a license to look into the sciences upon the conditions mentioned. The whole of the matter is this: every interpretation that leads to making lawful what God has forbidden, or to forbidding what He has made lawful, or to altering the ranks of the ranks of God Almighty — an interpretation that becomes a slur upon the Law and the rites of the upright religion, a summons to abandon working by it and to seek felicity in refraining from it — is a corrupt interpretation. Our masters, the pure Imams, are quit before God Almighty of what he interpreted, and of every interpretation that opposes what the Prophet ﷺ brought.

And if the decree were the end, it would not be right to name it a decree, for the decree comes before all else, and things come to be after the decree — the decree is for some thing, whereas the completion is not for a thing; rather the completion is for the very thing that has become complete.

## 16. Did Adam Bring a Law?

On the Judgment Between the Two Who Draw Near
The Law of Adam and the اَلْوِلَايَة of Noah (ع)

Chapter One

The author of al-Islah said:

Whoever claims that the first of the speaker-prophets (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ) had no Shariah is mistaken. He errs again in counting that one among those called "determined" — for, as we have explained, determination occurs only through the abrogation of a Shariah or through the renewal of another. The first Pronouncer had no prior Shariah to abrogate, and so the name of "determined" cannot fall upon him, as we have already stated. He was, rather, the first to draw the lines of religion in command and prohibition.

Anyone who studies what the other Pronouncers laid down in their apparent laws — the subtleties inferred from those laws, the recommended meanings of the parables that are called interpretation, which is the manifest sense of the words of the wise — will recognize that every line the Pronouncers drew, every command, prohibition, and act of worship, constitutes law, whether small or great, few or many. Each speaker is given only the measure of what he is commanded, and he does not exceed it. Some composed much, and some were restricted to little, confining themselves to the drawing of the statutes and their wording. We see this in the fact that fasting in the Law of Moses (ع) is less than in the Law of Jesus, and in the Law of Jesus there is more than in the Law of Muhammad. The rest of the rulings in religion likewise increase and decrease according to the difference in their cycles and the measure of what was commanded to each.

No one should suppose that this great cycle — the longest of all these cycles — passed away with no work accomplished, that its people departed without benefit, and that there were no apparent statutes by whose rulings they conducted themselves. For if there were no work, no benefit, and no apparent statutes, there can be no interpretation, no inward, and no true summons. The summons rests upon the Shariah; interpretation requires a revelation; the inward requires the outward; the meaning requires the parable. And if there is no outward, there is no inward, no summons, and no Shariah — which would leave creation neglected and the people in ignorance. Knowledge is joined with work, and that is the root of wisdom among the wise, as they have said:

> الْحِكْمَةُ هِيَ عَمَلٌ مَعَ عِلْمٍ
>
> "Wisdom is action with knowledge."

It is impossible to say that the people of that cycle were in such a state.

Rather, we say:

The first Pronouncer merited the name of "speaker" by virtue of composing a Shariah. Without that, he would not have been counted among the six Pronouncers, even if his commission had required no more than laying a foundation that calls to the التَّوْحِيد of Allah Almighty. And if he had no Shariah and no summons, there was no statute by which those who belonged to him in his cycle might affiliate after him — no basis upon which to raise the appended ranks after the pattern of the rest of the Pronouncers. This is a sickly belief. It is not permissible to say that in that cycle there was no revelation and no Shariah, for command and prohibition come only through the inspiration of Allah, and the manifesting of Allah's command is revelation. When command, prohibition, and revelation are established, the Shariah is established as the manifestation of the statutes. This indicates that the first Speaker had a Shariah — over and above what the compelling proof indicates, according to what the wise have said. Allah Almighty said:

> وَاتْلُ عَلَيْهِمْ نَبَأَ ابْنَيْ آدَمَ بِالْحَقِّ إِذْ قَرَّبَا قُرْبَانًا فَتُقُبِّلَ مِنْ أَحَدِهِمَا وَلَمْ يُتَقَبَّلْ مِنَ الْآخَرِ
>
> "And recite to them the news of the two sons of Adam in truth, when they offered a sacrifice, and it was accepted from one of them and not accepted from the other" (the verse).

The offering is one of the laws, and it is the most firmly established of the causes of the Shariah. Allah has commanded it in every Shariah and has made it the root of devotion and purity; for this reason the offering is called a rite. Allah Almighty said:

> وَلِكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ جَعَلْنَا مَنْسَكًا لِيَذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَى مَا رَزَقَهُمْ مِنْ بَهِيمَةِ الْأَنْعَامِ
>
> "And for every nation We have appointed a rite, that they may pronounce the name of Allah over what He has provided them of the beasts of cattle."

And He said:

> وَلِكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ جَعَلْنَا مَنْسَكًا هُمْ نَاسِكُوهُ فَلَا يُنَازِعُنَّكَ
>
> "And to every nation We have appointed an act of devotion which they perform, so let them not dispute with you" (the verse).

The rite is the place where the offering is brought near. In the hadith:

> اَلْعَرْجَاءُ تَجُرُّ رِجْلَهَا إِلَى اَلْمَنْسَكِ
>
> "The lame ewe drags her leg to the rite."

The rite itself is the offering. A man is called a rite-keeper when he is known for religion, scrupulousness, and purity. The offering is called devotion because it is the summit of the statutes of religion and the summit of nearness to Allah Almighty in every Shariah and among all the nations — the most firmly established of the causes of the Shariah. It is called an offering because by it they draw near to Allah Almighty; and through their nearness to Allah Almighty arise the first causes of obedience and disobedience, of disbelief and faith, of command and prohibition. It is the first of the statutes of the Shariah.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The author of al-Islah has argued for the affirmation of a Shariah for Adam (ع) by the saying of Allah Almighty, "And recite to them the news of the two sons of Adam in truth, when they offered a sacrifice." He held that the offering belongs only to the statutes of the laws — the slaughter of sheep and camels — after he had drawn out at length the distinction between determination and Shariah, gathering the greater part of his witnesses from the poetry of the Arabs, under which there is no benefit and no proof that verifies what he wished to establish.

The matter in the interpretation of this verse is not as he supposed. If the offering in this passage were what belongs only to the apparent laws — the slaughter of sheep and camels — his argument would hold. But the offering in this passage is something that does not belong to the laws at all: it is the raising up of the proofs from beneath their hands, and of those who stand in their place when they depart the world. This story has an interpretation manifest to the wise.

Had he read this story to its end, it would have become clear to him that Adam had no Shariah, for one of his two sons did not bury the corpse of his brother and did not know how, until Allah sent a raven scratching in the earth to show him how to bury his brother's corpse. The covering of the dead and his burial, in whatever manner, is one of the laws; and had Adam had a Shariah, neither its place nor the acting upon it would have been hidden from him. This is his saying.

We say:

The Shariah of Adam (ع) is established on both grounds: what the author of al-Islah adduced does establish it, and what the author of al-Nusrah brought forward does not undo it. The author of al-Islah argued for the affirmation of the Shariah — one of the statutes of the Pronouncers in the worship of Allah Almighty and the road to the knowledge of the causes — from the apparent sense of the Book: from the verse in which the offering is mentioned, from the universally affirming verse in His saying, "And for every nation We have appointed a rite, that they may pronounce the name of Allah" (the verse), and from the other verse, "And to every nation We have appointed an act of devotion which they perform." Thus it is established.

As for the author of al-Nusrah's saying that the matter in the interpretation of this verse is not as he supposed: this does not contradict the saying of the author of al-Islah. The author of al-Islah did not argue from the interpretation of this verse but from its outward sense. And since the saying of the author of al-Islah affirms the Shariah of Adam, and the reply of the author of al-Nusrah does not undo it, the Shariah remains fixed as it was established.

Chapter Two

The author of al-Islah said:

Were the offering what belongs only to the apparent laws — the slaughter of sheep and camels — it would have been as he described. But the offering in this passage, according to his own explanation, is what does not belong to the laws. This too does not contradict the saying of the author of al-Islah. Rather, it makes binding what he made binding. For his saying — "only what is in the apparent laws" — is itself an affirmation of the offering, since the offering is one of the statutes of religion and of the apparent Shariah, even though he pressed the claim that what is intended is what belongs to the outward.

His saying that the offering in this place does not belong to the apparent Shariah hardly serves to undo the saying of the author of al-Islah. What the author of al-Nusrah has said follows the course of an assertion that is not established except by evidence. The outward of this verse may stand and exist just as its inward exists — especially since the verse is not of the kind that runs as a parable whose interpretation is sought while its outward is abandoned. Since the outward of the verse affirms the work and is fixed, the statutes of the Shariah of Adam are fixed.

Chapter Three

Then the author of al-Islah said:

That the offering is the raising up of the proofs from beneath their hands — and of those who stand in their place — makes it binding that Adam had something to which these call. The proofs and the ranks that are set up are set up only to call to what the Speaker proclaims and draws, in which lies the welfare of those summoned in this world and in religion, in the Hereafter and in the beginning. Adam is not free of one of two things. Either he is a father because he laid down the Shariah and enacted the statutes that are the arbiter; or he prescribed nothing and manifested no statute — small or great — of the laws of worship, in which case he has no need to raise up proofs and send forth summoners who stand in his place, since he left nothing that must be preserved and by which the nation is governed. And by the very affirmation of the author of al-Nusrah, through his interpretation, that Adam raised up the proofs, the Shariah and the statutes are made binding. So the Shariah belongs to Adam.

Chapter Four

And after this we say:

Since the need for the prophets was for the teaching of the nation what would make it happy in the Hereafter and in this world, and would guide it to its welfare in this world and in religion, and since Adam was a prophet, it is necessary that he was a teacher and a guide. And if it is necessary that he was a teacher and a guide, then it is necessary that there existed that which he taught his nation, by which it is made happy and guided to its welfare. And if it is necessary that this existed, it can be nothing other than the laws of worship, which are the Shariah. So the Shariah of Adam (ع) is fixed. This is confirmed by the saying of Allah Almighty:

> كَانَ النَّاسُ أُمَّةً وَاحِدَةً
"The people were one nation" — that is, in ignorance —

> فَبَعَثَ اللَّهُ النَّبِيِّينَ مُبَشِّرِينَ وَمُنْذِرِينَ وَأَنْزَلَ مَعَهُمُ الْكِتَابَ بِالْحَقِّ لِيَحْكُمَ بَيْنَ النَّاسِ فِيمَا اخْتَلَفُوا فِيهِ
>
> "so Allah sent the prophets as bearers of good tidings and warners, and sent down with them the Book in truth, to judge between the people in that wherein they differed" (the verse).

Adam is one of the prophets, and if he was a prophet, Allah did not send him except to give good tidings, to warn, to judge, and to decide. And good tidings, warning, judgment, and decision come only through the statutes and ordinances. So the statutes, which are the Shariah, are fixed.

Chapter Five

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Had he read this verse to its end, it would have become clear to him that Adam had no Shariah, since one of his two sons did not bury the corpse of his brother and did not know how, until Allah sent a raven scratching in the earth to show him how to bury his brother's corpse. For the covering of the dead and his burial, in whatever manner, is one of the laws; and had Adam had a Shariah, the taking of it would not have been hidden from his sons.

This is a saying issued without inquiry and without examination.

We say:

Had the author of al-Islah relied upon sophistry, as the author of al-Nusrah did against him in the matter of the verse he adduced concerning the meaning of the offering — when he said, "the matter in the interpretation of the verse is not as he supposed" — then the author of al-Nusrah would not be free, in his own support, of one of two things. Either he must give the verse he adduced in denying the Shariah of Adam an interpretation in which he stands alone and by which he argues, and then his argument in denying what he seeks to deny of the Shariah of Adam has already fallen, for his argument first rested upon the outward of the verse; or he must give it no interpretation at all, and let it run according to what its outward conveys of its meaning, and then it does not indicate any of what he intended.

The verse contains no indication that the son of Adam, when the raven scratched in the earth and he saw it, covered his brother and buried him, so that the proof by it should stand for him. The verse is:

> فَبَعَثَ اللَّهُ غُرَابًا يَبْحَثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ لِيُرِيَهُ كَيْفَ يُوَارِي سَوْأَةَ أَخِيهِ قَالَ يَا وَيْلَتَا أَعَجَزْتُ أَنْ أَكُونَ مِثْلَ هَذَا الْغُرَابِ فَأُوَارِيَ سَوْأَةَ أَخِي فَأَصْبَحَ مِنَ النَّادِمِينَ
>
> "So Allah sent a raven scratching in the earth, to show him how to hide the corpse of his brother. He said, 'Woe to me! Am I unable to be like this raven and hide the corpse of my brother?' And he became one of the regretful."

It mentions that he saw, but it gives no indication that he buried him.

Chapter Six

Moreover, the interpretation of the verse, inward and outward alike, does not point to any of that. Each of the adversary and the friend had, in his worship of Allah Almighty, something that was his own offering — something by which he drew near to Allah Almighty. The adversary held that worship is nothing but the outward works alone, and that deliverance comes only through them, and that by this belief, and by sincerity in it, he would become the one who stands in the place of the Messenger (ع). The friend held that the worship of Allah Almighty is nothing but the outward — which is the work — together with the inward — which is the knowledge — and that deliverance comes only through both together, and that the one who stands in the place of the Messenger is the one whom the Messenger has advanced and been pleased with. So the two of them contended over this one day and disputed before the Prophet, each making plain his belief concerning that by which he worshipped Allah. That is the meaning of His saying, "when they offered a sacrifice" — that is, each of them held to something concerning what draws him near to Allah Almighty and to His Messenger. "And it was accepted from one of them" — the wasi — meaning the Speaker who stands in the place of Allah was pleased with his belief concerning Allah and concerning His worship. "And it was not accepted from the other" — the adversary — meaning the Speaker was not pleased with his belief, for it was not in its right form. The Speaker (ع) was content with the belief of the wasi and content with him, yet without declaring it openly, out of fear that the adversary would accuse him of inclining toward the friend over himself. So he said:

> إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ أَتْقَاكُمْ
>
> "Indeed, the most honored of you before Allah is the most God-fearing of you."

The adversary had heard him say, "The most God-fearing of you is Abu Bakr." Hearing that from the Messenger, he reckoned that the verse just recited had been recited only out of contentment with the belief and the way of the wasi — and that this was the one who would stand after him. He rose up with the conviction that he had overcome the friend, and said to him, "I will surely slay you" — meaning: I will surely drive you from this belief, which he was not pleased with in you, and from the affair you reckon to be yours. The friend said to him:

> إِنَّمَا يَتَقَبَّلُ اللَّهُ مِنَ الْمُتَّقِينَ
>
> "Allah accepts only from the God-fearing,"

meaning that the one who stands in the place of Allah — the Messenger — sets no one in his place and is pleased with the belief of no one save the God-fearing: those who join knowledge with work and draw near to Allah Almighty by upholding the marks of both together. Then the adversary turned to slander and revilement of the friend, and to the claim that he knew nothing; so the two of them brought their case before the Prophet ﷺ concerning their belief, and he was pleased with the friend's belief over the belief of the other. The friend said:

> لَئِنْ بَسَطْتَ إِلَيَّ يَدَكَ لِتَقْتُلَنِي مَا أَنَا بِبَاسِطٍ يَدِيَ إِلَيْكَ لِأَقْتُلَكَ إِنِّي أَخَافُ اللَّهَ رَبَّ الْعَالَمِينَ
>
> "If you stretch out your hand against me to slay me, I will not stretch out my hand against you to slay you; indeed, I fear Allah, the Lord of the worlds,"

meaning: if you assault me, loose your tongue against me, diminish my standing, speak of me otherwise than is due, and refuse to accept what I believe concerning the worship of Allah, the Lord of the worlds — I will not do the like to you, nor slander you, for I am under the authority of the Speaker who stands in the place of Allah, who is the master of the cycle and the lord of the Imams, and under his obedience. I fear him, and I do not go before him.

The opponent paid no heed to these words from the guardian. He turned away from what was right and obeyed his own soul, and so killed his brother. Then his soul rose against the other — that opponent through whom nothing was loosed, nor bound, nor taken in counsel, except through him. That is, when the first opponent brought back to him what had befallen the second, he said: there is no belief concerning Allah and His worship except what I have said. What the guardian had said he pronounced sharp error; and he made it seem right to himself to diminish the guardian, bar him from his rank, and charge him with knowing nothing of worth. So he thrust him from his station, overcame him, and declared: there is no worship except by this outward form, and none by what this man says. Thus he became one of the losers, for he worshipped Allah by obedience to the Speaker while withholding obedience from the Foundation, and by the outward while neglecting the inward.

Then Allah sent a raven:

> (فَبَعَثَ ٱللَّهُ غُرَابًۭا يَبْحَثُ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ)
>
> "So Allah sent a raven scratching in the earth."

That is, when the term drew out and the hour of the opponent's passing drew near, the guardian wished to make the argument binding upon him, so that his parting from the world would come after a reminder. So he sent to him — and this is the meaning of His saying "So Allah sent" — meaning that the one standing in the place of the Speaker, who himself stands in the place of Allah, sent a man from among his companions to make plain to the opponent his affair and his station: what Allah, the Most High, had kept hidden in His silence concerning the seeking of his right, and to open a way out for him from what he had done, and to move him to return the truth to its people. This is the meaning of the raven's scratching in the earth — to show him how to deliver himself from what he had brought upon his own soul, and to guide him to his duty. The man whom the guardian of Allah sent for this purpose was Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr (مُحَمَّدُ بْنُ أَبِي بَكْرٍ), for he is bound to the guardian by way of religion and to the opponent by way of lineage. Muhammad did what he was sent to do, that the opponent might be roused to face his crime; his soul softened, and he inclined toward binding himself to allegiance. Yet the second opponent prevailed over his judgment and barred him from his purpose — from the acknowledgment of the guardian's right that he had set his mind upon. So he grieved, and said within himself, when he was prevented:

> (يَا وَيْلَتَا أَعَجَزْتُ أَنْ أَكُونَ مِثْلَ هَٰذَا الْغُرَابِ)
>
> "O woe to me! Was I unable to be like this raven?"

That is: would that I stood in the position of this man — meaning Muhammad his son — who, though a stranger to the guardian, is his like in keeping the covenant of Allah and His religion and in preserving the secrets of the guardian of Allah. So he became one of those who regret what they let slip of following the guardian at the first, and regret the deed that would carry him at the last to the abode of the Hereafter. We seek refuge in Allah from breaking the covenant and then pleading it as proof. And if the matter is thus, the author of al-Nusrah has nothing in his objection by way of this verse, except the saying of the author of al-Islah concerning what it makes obligatory of the ordinances of the Law for Adam.

### The Seventh Section of the Ninth Chapter

It has been said that laws, dispensations, ordinances, and prescribed practices may increase and decrease according to the age in which the Speaker arises, and according to what is needed for setting the community aright. The narrations have come down that the washing of the dead, the embalming, the shrouding, and the burial are from the Law of Adam. If the reports of this have come in unbroken succession, as has been mentioned, then this verse — in what it relates of the raven and the scratching in the earth at the killing of the son of Adam — runs only in the course of the parables whose interpretation is to be sought; its outward sense is not to be taken as it stands, in the manner we have set out and interpreted. So the Law belongs to Adam, and the point is established.

### The Eighth Section of the Ninth Chapter

Then we say: the objection by this verse, meant to overturn the ordinances of the Law of Adam, is itself annulled. For the burial of the dead is not among those matters whose ordinances are lifted by the lifting of their standing — namely purity, prayer, the zakat, and the remaining acts — such that by the lifting of its ordinance the remaining ordinances of the laws, which are the foundations of worship, would be lifted as well. Rather, it is one kind among the kinds of what is done with the dead: burning with fire, casting into water, bearing to a cave or a desert, burying in the earth, or giving to the beasts of prey. If it is one kind among the kinds of an ordinance of the Law, whose place may be taken by another, then it cannot be that by the lifting of this one kind the ordinances of the whole Law would be annulled. And if this is barred, the Law is established for Adam.

### The Ninth Section of the Ninth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Adam was not the possessor of a Law; for had he been the possessor of a Law, the command of Allah to the angels that they prostrate to him would have been through his mediation.

We say:

By that saying the author of al-Nusrah has himself made it obligatory that Adam is the possessor of a Law. For the command of Allah to the angels to prostrate — though outwardly not from the side of Adam — was only through his mediation, by the proof that the command to prostrate to him is impossible unless he is a Mustafa, and the command to prostrate to him is not impossible only because he is a Mustafa. And it has been established that he is a Mustafa, by the saying of Allah, the Most High:

> (إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ ٱصْطَفَىٰٓ آدَمَ وَنُوحًۭا)
>
> "Indeed Allah chose Adam and Noah."

And if he is a Mustafa, it is impossible that Allah command one below the Mustafa except through the mediation of the Mustafa. So the command of Allah to the angels to prostrate to Adam was only through his mediation; and if it was through his mediation, then the Law is established for him by that very saying.

### The Tenth Section of the Ninth Chapter

Then the command of Allah to prostrate to Adam, by His saying "Prostrate to Adam" (ٱسْجُدُوا۟ لِآدَمَ), is like His command to the Children of Israel, by His saying:

> (يا بني اسرائيل اذكروا نعمتي التي انعمت عليكم وأوفوا بعهدي أوف بعهدكم وإياي فارهبون وآمنوا بما انزلت مصدقاً لما معكم ولا تكونوا أول كافر ولا تشتروا بآياتي ثمناً قليلاً وإياي فاتقون، وَلَا تَلْبِسُوا الْحَقَّ بِالْبَاطِلِ وَتَكْتُمُوا الْحَقَّ وَأَنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتُوا الزَّكَاةَ وَارْكَعُوا مَعَ الرَّاكِعِينَ)
>
> "O Children of Israel, remember My favor which I bestowed upon you, and fulfill My covenant, and I shall fulfill your covenant, and have awe of Me alone. And believe in what I have sent down confirming what is with you, and be not the first to disbelieve in it, and sell not My signs for a small price, and be mindful of Me. And confound not the truth with falsehood, nor conceal the truth while you know. And establish the prayer, and give the zakat, and bow with those who bow."

Though this appears in a form that does not indicate that he is a mediator, yet by the mediation of Muhammad it reached them. And if it were permissible to say that Muhammad has no Law on account of the bareness of the verse — in its mention of Allah's command to the Children of Israel — from what would indicate that the command is through his mediation, since the two verses are set together in respect of their not indicating a single original mediator, this would be impossible. For the command of Allah, the Most High, to the Children of Israel, though outwardly stripped of any mention of a mediator, was only through the mediation of Muhammad. And if that is impossible, then Adam is the possessor of the Law, and the Law is established for him.

### The Eleventh Section of the Ninth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The angels are not charged, nor is the practice of the Law obligatory upon them, for they do not disobey Allah for the blink of an eye; rather they glorify the praise of their Lord and do what they are commanded.

We say:

The angels who were charged — had they not been among those who bear a charge, He, the Most High, would not have charged them with the prostration to Adam, nor commanded them by His saying "Prostrate to Adam." The angels who are not charged are those who were never in the abode of body, and never stood in potency, but came forth directly into act; they are the foremost forefathers in the abode of eternity. What is meant by the charged angels, however, is the assembly who were in the abode of nature and who possessed the virtues by which they resemble those in the abode of eternity — the angels of origination and of prompting, according to what we have shown before — since none goes forward except one who is in the abode of origination and prompting. As for prostration, and the like of prostration among the ordinances of the worships and the acts, these are found only in the world of nature. And those who are charged are charged only with the establishing of the two worships, that by them they may attain of increase and of the blessings of felicity what Allah exalts their standing by, and attain the perfection that earns them abiding and permanence. For this reason there was the charging of the angels and the summoning of them to the act.

### The Twelfth Section of the Ninth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Had the prostration of the angels to Adam been a Law for them, Adam would have said, "Allah commands you to prostrate and to obey me," and he would have striven against whoever opposed him in the command of Allah, as was the case with all the Speakers.

We say:

The author of al-Islah might reply: the fact that Adam did not say "Allah commands you to prostrate to me and to obey me" does not prove that prostration falls outside the ordinances of the Law. The Prophet never said to the Children of Israel, "Allah commands you to establish the prayer and to pay me the zakat," yet no one takes that as proof that the prayer, the zakat, and everything else he commanded them fall outside the ordinances of the Law. Moreover, if striving against opponents were a condition for establishing a Law, Jesus (ع) would have had no Law, since he did not strive — and that conclusion is plainly unsound.

### The Thirteenth Section of the Ninth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Furthermore, Allah, the Most High, does not mention in the Quran that Adam had a people and followers, nor does He mention his being sent, as He does with others, when He said:

> (إِنَّآ أَرْسَلْنَا نُوحًا)
>
> "Indeed We sent Noah."

Nor did He mention him within the roll of the Law, when He said:

> (شَرَعَ لَكُم مِّنَ ٱلدِّينِ مَا وَصَّىٰ بِهِۦ نُوحًۭا وَٱلَّذِىٓ أَوْحَيْنَآ إِلَيْكَ وَمَا وَصَّيْنَا بِهِۦٓ إِبْرَهِيمَ وَمُوسَىٰ وَعِيسَىٰٓ أَنْ أَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلدِّينَ وَلَا تَتَفَرَّقُوا۟)
>
> "He has ordained for you of the religion what He enjoined upon Noah, and that which We revealed to you, and what We enjoined upon Abraham and Moses and Jesus: that you establish the religion and be not divided therein."

We say:

The author of al-Nusrah has neither dealt justly in his objection nor pondered the saying of the author of al-Islah in his book. Adam is in fact mentioned in the Quran, and his followers and his people are the angels who were commanded to prostrate to him and who learned the names from him, as Allah, the Most High, commanded in His saying:

> (يَا آدَمُ أَنْبِئْهُمْ بِأَسْمَائِهِمْ)
>
> "O Adam, inform them of their names."

As for the mention of his being sent, no sending is greater than his. The messengers are sent for one purpose: to serve as the vicegerents of Allah in His earth, since Allah, the Most High, is beyond apprehension; and Allah, the Most High, has stated plainly in His Book that He made Adam a vicegerent in the earth, to whom its people turn in whatever they need of the affairs of their religion. As for his absence from the roll of those who ordained the Law in that verse, the author of al-Islah explains: Adam was not mentioned among them because he was not among the possessors of firm resolve and was not a man of determination. Now, if it were valid to exclude Adam from the company of those who brought Laws simply because his name is not mentioned alongside the five, then by the same reasoning — since his name is also absent from the company of those to whom revelation was given, in His saying, the Most High:

> (إنا أوحينا اليك كما أوحينا إلى نوح والنبيين من بعده وأوحينا إلى إبراهيم وإسماعيل وإسحاق ويعقوب والأسباط وعيسى وأيوب ويونس وهرون وسليمان)
>
> "Indeed We revealed to you as We revealed to Noah and the prophets after him; and We revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, the tribes, Jesus, Job, Jonah, Aaron, and Solomon."

— it would be equally valid to exclude him from that company as well. And that is impossible. The sound position is therefore that of the author of al-Islah, not of the other.

### The Fourteenth Section of the Ninth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Furthermore, we ought to understand the cause that makes a Law necessary before we can soundly claim that Adam had no Law.

We say:

The cause that makes a Law obligatory is the neglect and abandonment of the limits, the people's following of the opponent, and their attributing to him what Allah did not place in him. None of this was present, nor did it appear, in the age of Adam. The learned ones who came before him, and his own people, held fast to the limits, and not one of them violated a single limit — as they themselves declared when they said:

> (أَتَجْعَلُ فِيهَا مَن يُفْسِدُ فِيهَا وَيَسْفِكُ ٱلدِّمَآءَ وَنَحْنُ نُسَبِّحُ بِحَمْدِكَ وَنُقَدِّسُ لَكَ)
>
> "Will You place therein one who works corruption in it and sheds blood, while we glorify Your praise and sanctify You?"

The establishing of the Law was therefore not obligatory upon Adam. This is his saying.

We say:

The true cause of a Law's obligatory existence is other than what the author of al-Nusrah proposes — namely, the neglect of limits, their suspension, and the people's following of the opponent. None of that makes a Law obligatory or establishes one. We stand in his place instead and say: the cause of the obligatory existence of the Law, which is the ordinances of the acts, we have set out in our book known as al-Masabih fil-Imamah. The most immediate of these causes is the need of people — each standing at their different station — for ordinances that protect them from destruction: ordinances that preserve life, guard what is private, safeguard property, and provide redress in the necessary disputes that arise among them. Without such ordinances, human affairs would break down. The strong would turn upon the weak, driven by rage at whatever wealth and possessions they hold, and the fires of discord would be kindled among them. Since it is impossible, in the wisdom of the Maker, the Most High, that He should deprive His creation of what alone guards them from destruction, the ordinances that constitute the Law are therefore of necessary existence.

## 17. The Six Gates of Necessity

The author of al-Nusrah held that we must also understand why the Law exists, so that we may rightly claim Adam (عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ) had no law of his own. His argument was this: the ground of the Law's obligation is the neglect of proper limits, their disruption, and creation's pursuit of their opposites, together with creatures claiming for themselves what Allah did not place in them. This condition, he said, neither existed nor appeared in Adam's time, for the learned beings who preceded him, and his own people, steadily kept those limits without exception — not one of them transgressed a single limit. They themselves testified to this when they said:

> ( أَتَجْعَلُ فِيهَا مَنْ يُفْسِدُ فِيهَا وَيَسْفِكُ الدِّمَاءَ وَنَحْنُ نُسَبِّحُ بِحَمْدِكَ وَنُقَدِّسُ لَكَ )
>
> "Will You place in it one who corrupts it and sheds blood, while we glorify You with praise and sanctify You?"

No obligation therefore fell upon Adam to establish the Law. That is his argument.

We say that the true reason the Law must exist is other than what the author of al-Nusrah identified. The neglect of limits, their disruption, and creation's following of their opposites do not in themselves make a law obligatory or establish one. We answer for ourselves. As for why the Law must exist — the Law being the ordinances governing deeds — we have treated this in our book known as al-Masabih fi al-Imamah. The nearest of these reasons is this: people need, first of all and according to their station, the very means of their survival — the ordinances that guard them from destruction. These are the ordinances by which blood is protected, by which homes, women, and wealth are made secure, and by which disputes in necessary matters are settled. Without such ordinances, life would grind to a halt: the strong would prey upon the weak, drawn by rage at what they possessed, and the fires of discord would be lit. Since it is impossible in the wisdom of the Maker, the Most High, that He deprive His creation of what guards them from destruction, the ordinances that constitute the Law are of necessary existence.

We say further that those ordinances became necessary because human souls do not, each on its own, hold back from one another in their respective domains. From their interactions arise situations that cannot be managed without ordinances to calm the community, bind its firm bonds, and make each person safe from every other. And since these ordinances are of necessary existence, we say that some are necessary at all times and seasons, while others are not — present in one era and absent in another, growing and diminishing.

What is of necessary existence comprises:

- Drawing near to Allah, the Most High
- The ordinance of restraint by force, and of retribution upon souls and persons
- The ordinance of marriage
- The division of wealth, including inheritance and the like
- The ordinance for concealing the dead
- The ordinance for extracting rights and returning them to their owners

This is because human beings possess three souls.

The first is the rational soul, which possesses discrimination and thought. It knows that it has a Creator, submits to Him by nature, and through that submission draws near to Him. In drawing near, it is moved toward ordinances by which it fulfills its aim, whether those ordinances are many or few.

The second is the sensitive soul. Through its irascible faculty, a person grows angry, and this faculty loves to overcome, to subdue, to take revenge, to wrong, to kill, and to deal unjustly. This drives the need for ordinances by which one person's violence is turned back from another, and by which the right of the wronged is extracted from the aggressor — whether in the soul by force, in the body by retribution, or through what runs in the course of blood-money. Through its appetitive faculty, the sensitive soul seeks pleasures and seeks marriage of necessity, driving the need for ordinances that establish this and remove from among them the ruin of jealousy, which would otherwise lead to killing.

This soul also demands that all wealth be its own, by whatever means, so that ordinances are needed to make each person safe from the cheating, cunning, and deceit that would otherwise lead to domination and fighting. And since these souls stir in their greed toward the pursuit of livelihood, dealings become necessary; since treachery and cunning are rooted in human nature, ordinances are needed by which claimant and denier alike proceed in extracting the right for its owner. Death, too, is inescapable, and people cannot do without concealing their dead — whether by burial, by burning, by casting into the valleys, or otherwise — for without this, harm would return upon them. This too demands ordinances.

These are six gates that run in the manner of genera, and beneath them fall the kinds whose existence is necessary among human beings without exception.

As for what is not necessary but is found in greater measure at one time and lesser at another — these are the ordinances in the worship of Allah, the Most High, and in drawing near to Him through the knowledge of His limits, by which completeness is attained. These fall under the genus of drawing near to Allah.

All of these ordinances belong to the Law. There must first be one who lays them down and inscribes them among human beings at the beginning of their existence. Since these ordinances exist with the existence of human beings, and Adam was of human beings, together with his angels, the Law is established for him.

As for whoever came before Adam, their ruling is the ruling of one who stands at the end of the seventh cycle — like for like. They were not free of the necessary ordinances we have described; they were free only of the ordinances that fall under the genus of worship in drawing near to Allah, the Most High, which we have said may increase at one time and decrease at another, and by which the second completeness is gained. Allah, the Most High, therefore sent Adam to teach them and others what they had not known, and to bring them to completeness where they fell short. For though they were distinguished above others by merit, they fell short of the degree of completeness by their leaving off the work of worship. He therefore laid down the Law, affirmed it, and called them to the two acts of worship — in knowledge and in deed. Some followed and obeyed; others refused and turned aside, holding to the six that had preceded, from the beginning of the cycles. Such was the affair of those people.

The author of al-Nusrah said that Noah (عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ) was obliged to establish it — meaning the Law — because before his cycle there had been cycles given to violating the limits and following their opposites, such as al-Wadd (اَلْوَدّ), al-Suwa (*al-Suwaa* (اَلسُّوَاع)), Yaghuth (*Yaghooth* (يَغُوث)), and others. It therefore became obligatory upon Noah to establish it, so that whoever perished might perish upon clear proof, and whoever lived might live upon clear proof.

We say that this argument does not establish that Adam had no law; on the contrary, it establishes that he had ordinances and a law. The author has affirmed as necessary that a cycle turned upon the violation of limits and the following of opposites. But violation befalls the followed one only through the follower's abandoning of his command and his ordinance. By the author's own argument, the establishment of violation presupposes the ordinances that constitute the Law — the very ordinances whose violation occurred. The Law is therefore established for Adam.

The author of al-Islah said that it has been related that Allah, mighty and majestic, commanded Adam to pitch a tent that He sent down to him from the Garden, and to make it his qiblah and the qiblah of his children. Seth then built after him the place of the Law, made its rulings firm, gathered into it revelation and interpretation, and established its branches, its roots, and the firm reckoning of its obligations. We say that the position of the author of al-Islah is sound, since Adam has a law and ordinances, as do the other speaker-prophets.

The author of al-Nusrah said that in the composition of the world and its ordering, in the matter of pointing to the divine unity, there is that which makes one independent of the laying down of deeds and stations. He further argued that the laying down of deeds and signs has in fact removed many of creation from the divine unity and cast them into likening Him to creatures — something utterly impossible. His argument ran as follows: the composition of the world, the ordering and wonders of His wisdom, what lies in the joining of bodies together, the pairing of contrary natures within differing bodies, and the measured forms — all of this points to the divine unity in a way that renders superfluous the laying down of deeds, of like actions and differing stations signifying agreeing meanings, of likenesses drawn upon a standing thing compared, and of proofs built upon a clear thing indicated. What is found in these proofs and deeds — contradictory as they are in outward aspect — and in the likenesses struck to establish known limits, is itself proof of the One, since the firm, wrought, and wondrous workings of the world and of souls make one independent of deeds and signs. Indeed, he said, many of these signs, deeds, and outward words have removed most of creation from the divine unity and cast them into a likening that does not befit even the least of His creatures. In sum, his position is that the composition and ordering of the world, in pointing to the divine unity, renders the laying down of deeds and stations unnecessary, and that the laying down of deeds and signs has in fact driven many of creation away from the divine unity and into likening.

We say that this position renders the sending of the messengers a superfluity of which there is no need. If the world is itself firm and wrought and points to the divine unity, and the messengers (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ) were sent to call toward and point to the divine unity, then to send them when a proof already stands — doing precisely what they were sent to do — is a needless redundancy. I do not know how this escaped him, since it is plain that the world's being firm and wrought is not sufficient in pointing to the divine unity if there is no one present who knows it and makes it clear. One benefits from the knowledge of the world's composition, its ordering, and the arrangement of its existents only when there is one whose rank has risen in knowledge because the limits have taught him the manner of the deeds and signs that revelation and the Law gather together — so that he reflects, draws out, and infers what he seeks of the knowledge of the limits of Allah, the Most High, and of His signs whose knowledge is made obligatory upon human beings. Allah, the Most High, says:

> [ حَتَّى إِذَا جَاءُوا قَالَ أَكَذَّبْتُمْ بِآيَاتِي وَلَمْ تُحِيطُوا بِهَا عِلْمًا ]
>
> "Until, when they come, He says: Did you deny My signs when you did not encompass them in knowledge?"

Now if you did know, then the foremost in existence are the noble substances that are the angels brought near. So it is also when deeds, signs, alerting, and teaching are needed — for not everyone who has knowledge, works, and is alerted is able to draw from existing things the true inference; and how much less is the one who has no knowledge at all. In sum, any position that leads to the nullifying of the ranks of the limits is not our belief, nor the way of our masters, may. Perhaps this position was only planted by adversaries in the book, to be ascribed to its author, and Allah knows best.

Furthermore, the deeds are not laid down for pointing to the divine unity alone, but for the setting straight of the soul in worship, for its likeness to the highest assembly, and for the acquiring, through discipline, of the noble morals and generous habits by which the soul is made ready for union with what is above it and drawn out of the baseness of potency — so that the honor of the intellect may be rescued by devotion to its obligations and attention to its marks. Were it not for the deeds and rites within the community, and for the completeness of souls through persevering in them and holding to them, souls would perish. It is for this that Allah, the Most High, did not grant the angels their claim when they supposed that the worship they were engaged in was sufficient in the matter of nearness to Allah, the Most High, and of completeness for them — that they had no need, in what they were upon, of a vicegerent to teach them anything or to command them to work. He said:

> [ إِنِّي أَعْلَمُ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ ]
>
> "Indeed, I know what you do not know."

Allah, the Most High, said this because the states of being, by His decree and His wisdom, are held wavering between potency and act — standing at one time in potency, at another in act. A thing, therefore, when it reaches the uttermost limit of what it may attain in potency or act, returns backward: if it was in potency, toward act, and if it was in act, toward potency. It is like the hot thing that, when it reaches the limit of heat, the heat subsides and coldness appears, as with what dissolves among bodies and the like; and like the cold thing that, when it reaches the limit of coldness, the coldness subsides and heat appears, as with snow; and like the congealed thing whose parts are gathered from the mountains, which, when it reaches the end of that for which it congealed — namely dryness — dissolution appears in its parts, so that they scatter and return to sand; and so too with the other existents in the abode of nature. Knowledge and ignorance in the world of coming-to-be and passing-away follow the same law: at one time ignorance stands in act and knowledge in potency, meaning that ignorance is widespread through the abundance of its people, and knowledge is lost through the scarcity of its people.

This was the time when ignorance held full dominion and had run its course in act, while knowledge had retreated inward and reached the furthest limit of its potency. Both had come about through the effacing of the teachers' traces and the disruption of the signs of the laws and the deeds, worn down by the length of the age and the passage of time. By the laws I mean here those ordinances and rites whose existence is not necessary.

Those named after the angels — the finest among them in themselves, and their excellent ones who bore knowledge in that age, and whoever worships Allah upon an edge — held that what they were upon was sufficient in the matter of worship and of completeness. But the affair was other than they reckoned, for their completeness depended on knowledge of the limits foremost in existence, who belong to the abode of origination and the first sending-forth, and on knowledge of those later in existence, such as the second sending-forth, and on acting by that which brings them to completeness.

When it became clear that their time was the time in which the cycle of ignorance reached its completion — that they were not in the cycle of knowledge nor in the cycle of works — He, the Most High, saw that to leave them as they were, now that ignorance had reached its utmost and the time had become the time of knowledge's beginning to appear, would run counter to wisdom. So He, mighty and majestic, said in effect: I know of ignorance's reaching its end in the world in act, and the completion of its cycle and its appearance; and of knowledge's reaching its end in the world in potency, and the return of repentance to it in appearing, and the standing need — in its appearing in act — for the raising up of teachers and guides: what you do not know.

So Allah sent Adam, making him a vicegerent for Himself, that knowledge might be made manifest, that he might bring them to completeness in act, guide them to the knowledge of the limits, and teach them what they had not known. This was the beginning of the affair — the coming forth of knowledge from potency into act, through the existence of its people, from his days onward; and its reaching-out extends to the one who arises, may the peace of Allah be upon him. Likewise it is said that when the one who arises appears, knowledge becomes in act, meaning it becomes general and its people multiply, while ignorance sinks within, meaning its people grow few and its root is cut off. What is said by way of narration — that the treasures of the earth appear in his days and it casts them out to its surface — is a pointing to the one who arises and to the standing of knowledge in act, so that all that has been deposited in the depths of the revealed books and in the knotted symbols is made manifest.

And since the angels, for all the existence of the heavens, the goodness of their ordering, and the firmness of the composition of the world's existents, had no knowledge, nor completeness, nor any inference from these — except through what Adam taught them and called them to, of the work — what the author of al-Nusrah mentioned was not fully examined, and the deeds, the signs, and the likenesses were not things one could do without in the matter of inference.

These deeds, these signs, and these likenesses are indispensable for inference concerning monotheism, politics, the correction of the soul, and its discipline in knowing the limits and in bringing them out into act. That being so, Adam is the author of a law and of customs to which he called, and which pointed to the limits of Allah and to His unity.

Chapter Twenty-One of Title IX

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Does the author of this discourse say that the creation in the great cycle did not know, since there was no apparent law resembling one another among them? Or does he say that the الْقَائِم, in raising the represented laws, nullifies the knowledge of monotheism and brings the people out to the assertion of divesting? For if the creation was not ignorant of monotheism, and if in the cycle of the الْقَائِم likewise they do not remain ignorant of it while the laws are raised, then it would have been permissible for Adam to have called the people to monotheism without signs and without the striking of likenesses such as these, by his mere word.

And we say:

Our earlier treatment of knowledge and ignorance — of how each of them is at one time in potentiality and at another in act — already holds within it the explanation of the state in which the creation stood in the great cycle. That they stood at the utmost limit of ignorance, by the very admission of the author of al-Nusrah, makes Adam's call and his being a teacher of the landmarks of monotheism to his followers a mere superfluity without need. For he has made it obligatory that the people already knew monotheism; and if they already knew monotheism, then Adam's calling them to the thing they already knew would be nothing but an unneeded superfluity. And that is impossible.

For the people were not in need of that by which the completeness of their souls is attained, and so Allah would not have sent Adam to them, nor made him His caliph among them, nor chosen him out from among them, nor made obedience to him — and a returning to him in what they did not know — obligatory upon them. What the assembly named the angels said, when they said,

> وَنَحْنُ نُسَبِّحُ بِحَمْدِكَ وَنُقَدِّسُ لَكَ

"And we glorify You with Your praise and sanctify You," was only their estimation that they were sufficient in glorification, sanctification, and monotheism, and that what they were already in was enough in the matter of worship and in the knowledge of the limits. But Allah knew of their affair what His mercy made obligatory: that Adam be chosen out from among them and sent to them, so that He might make him a caliph in teaching them, calling them to that by which their completeness is reached — of work and of knowledge together — which leads to the knowledge of the limits in which monotheism is made sound. For this He said,

> إِنِّىٓ أَعْلَمُ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

"I know what you do not know," because He, exalted be He, knew that they were deficient, that ignorance had reached its end in act, that the work had come to begin in manifestation, and that they did not know that. So it became obligatory in wisdom to establish the office of teaching, which is the cause of the spreading of knowledge and the multiplying of its people. So Allah chose Adam, and he laid down a law, and he taught, as Allah, exalted be He, said:

> وعلم آدم الأسماء كلها ثم عرضهم على الملائكة فقال انبثوني بأسماء هؤلاء ان كنتم صادقين قالوا سبحانك لا علم لنا إلا ما علمتنا انك انت العليم الحكيم قال يا آدم انبئهم باسمائهم فلمًا انبأم باسمائهم قال : أَلَمْ أَقُل لَّكُمْ إِنِّىٓ أَعْلَمُ غَيْبَ ٱلسَّمَوَتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ

"And He taught Adam the names, all of them, then He presented them to the angels and said: Inform Me of the names of these, if you are truthful. They said: Glory be to You, we have no knowledge except what You have taught us; You are the Knowing, the Wise. He said: O Adam, inform them of their names. And when he had informed them of their names, He said: Did I not say to you that I know the unseen of the heavens and the earth," — the verse. So the people were not knowing, and because they were so, Adam came to them as a teacher for them.

Chapter Twenty-Two of Title IX

And we say:

As for his saying and his affirmation that the creation was not ignorant of monotheism from the first: had they not been ignorant, they could not have deduced it except from the side of those who give aid, in which lies the completeness of their essences and their forms; and there would have been no cause for Adam to be chosen out from among them to be the caliph of Allah, exalted be He, in teaching them what holds their good sooner and later. And it is impossible that this conception be pictured in those people alongside the granting to them of a rank that would be their own.

As for his saying "nor in the cycle of the الْقَائِم are they ignorant": if what he means is the state of the summons in the cycle of the الْقَائِم before his manifestation and before his existence from the world of nature — when it is preserved by his caliphs and his summoners after the completion of the cycle of Muhammad al-Mustafa (ع) and his House, up to his time and the appearance of his signs in the world — then it is true. For knowledge at that time has raised high the beacon of its manifestation through the multitude of the learned. It is the time of which Allah, exalted be He, said,

> يَوْمًۭا يَجْعَلُ ٱلْوِلْدَنَ شِيبًا
>
> "A day that will make the children gray-haired."

But if what he means by his saying "in the cycle of the الْقَائِم they are not ignorant" is the state after the الْقَائِم — the one who is the possessor of the seventh cycle, and of the decisive utterance, and of the distinguishing of the people of the Garden from the people of the Fire, and of the closing of the door of teaching and of repentance — then it is not sound. For they do not remain knowing, nor are they of the people of knowledge, since that time is the time for the waters to overflow and be swallowed inward, and the time for dryness and barrenness to prevail and appear. And the words of the author of al-Nusrah on that matter are not those of one who has examined it to its end.

---

Chapter Twenty-Three of Title IX

In sum, the author of al-Nusrah brought forward these sayings and these citations only in support of the author of al-Mahsul in his refutation of the law of Adam. And Adam, in his summoning of them and in his being a prophet who teaches and guides, is not free of one of these: either he called them by encouragement toward the Garden and the neighborhood of the Lord of the Worlds, or he called them by warning of the Fire and of remoteness from the mercy of the best of creators, or he called them by both together.

If he called them by encouragement, then encouragement — when the thing encouraged is not sensible — cannot be except by the striking of likenesses and by reports about it in terms of what is known to the senses: the kinds of pleasures and blessings and the good places, of dwellings, gardens, rivers, and trees, that its picture may fall in their minds. And that is a form of speech that requires interpretation. If he called them by warning, then likewise his speech cannot be except by the striking of likenesses for them of that which is feared, drawn from the world of sense, that they may picture it; and in its interpretation it runs the course of the others that have gone before. And if he called them by both together, then his speech is not free of an apparent sense to be acted upon, and so it requires interpretation. If that is so, then the three divisions together make obligatory a fixed apparent sense established for Adam, followed by an interpretation. Adam, then, is the possessor of a law and of manifest customs.

Chapter Twenty-Four of Title IX

Then we say:

Adam is not free of one of these: either he called the people of his time to the obedience of Allah, exalted be He, and to obedience of him, by wisdom — or not by wisdom. He does not do so devoid of wisdom, for that is impossible, and to hold it of him is a going out from religion. So if he called them by wisdom, and wisdom requires knowledge and work together, then Adam is the possessor of a law that gathers knowledge and work, which are the worship of Allah, and by which perfection is attained and the ascent to the stations of honor.

Chapter Twenty-Five of Title IX

As for the الْقَائِم and the saying that he raises the laws, we say:

To hold that belief is unsound. For the estimation of his existence from the world of nature does not arise by the way of the second emanation, because he does not nullify anything that pertains to the worship of Allah, exalted be He. Rather he is a seal for cycles that have passed, and a completion of a matter that was completed in his place. He removes the limits from their ranks by the falling of the referral in teaching about them, and the fullness of the matter in the new creation, of which Allah, exalted be He, said,

> بَلْ هُمْ لَيْسَ مِنْ خَلْقٍ جَدِيدٍ

after He has established a decree in the souls of any from whom there would run the course of a foundation among the possessors of the empty cycles. For this Allah, exalted be He, said, making plain the possessor of the Resurrection,

> واذا السماء انفطرت وإذا الكواكب انتشرت

"And when the heaven is cleft asunder, and when the planets are scattered." And He said,

> إذا الشمس كورت واذا النجوم انكدرت واذا الجبال سيرت واذا العشار عطلت
>
> "When the sun is folded up, and when the stars fall, and when the mountains are set moving, and when the pregnant camels are left untended."

All these likenesses are struck upon the limits and the summonses in their passing away from their ranks, not upon the laws and the customs of the works, whose landmarks — with the passing of time — are effaced by the absence of teachers, except what was necessary in its existence, according to what we have already mentioned. And that time in which this act appears — the closing of the door of teaching and the ceasing of the summonses on the part of the possessor of the greatest Resurrection — is the beginning of the return of knowledge to potentiality, so that it is swallowed inward, and the beginning of the increase of ignorance and its standing in act. So the appearance of the signs in that manner suffices the one who is sufficient and has come of age. May Allah make us and the community of the believers of the people of the divine graces, by the following of His friends, and may He gather us with Muhammad and Ali and the pure Imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ), the lights of His purity, by His bounty and His mercy.

Chapter Twenty-Six of Title IX

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Everyone who says that monotheism is not grasped except by the signs and the works has thereby ruled that the First Intellect, the Soul, the angels, the اَلْجَدّْ, the الْفَتْح, and the Khayal did not know monotheism, since there were no signs and no works among them or between them.

And we say:

The one who says that does not mean the First Intellect, nor the high limits, but rather the intellects that are in the world of the body — which is the second emanation — since their attaining of completeness is by teaching and by obligation, by knowledge, by signs and works, and by a standing in acquisition. As for the First Intellect and the transcendent limits that are separate from bodies, they did not attain completeness by any of that; rather they gained their perfections from the very beginning of their existence, by origination and by emanation. And it is not permissible that the ruling of this saying be run upon those. And as for what the author of al-Nusrah brought forward concerning the meaning of the seven stars, their fixed positions, their return, and their rectitude, and concerning the six days in which Allah created the heavens and the earth and what is between them, and as for what he mentioned of the resolve — the author of al-Islah has left nothing of the interpretation of that and its meanings that he did not bring forward, and in it is sufficiency, dispensing with an Arabization in the interpretation that scarcely balances and does not match.

Chapter Twenty-Seven of Title IX

The author of al-Islah said:

The single line does not necessitate the opening except by the appearance of the second line, for the line indicates the Speaker, and the opening indicates the law. And the form of a thing does not of necessity appear except after its completion. Just as the opening appeared after the second line, likewise the law of the first was not completed except by the appearance of the second Speaker. So when the second Speaker appeared, the law of the first was completed, and its form appeared in perfection. And likewise the six openings are completed by the seven lines, for there is no law before the first of the Speakers, and no law at the appearance of the seventh; rather the six laws are from the Speakers whose seventh is the الْقَائِم. And if you draw seven lines, six openings appear from among them, according to these likenesses: one, two, three, four, five, six. And were the analogy to be drawn upon that.

## 18. Cycles, Prophets, and the Ark

Were the analogy to hold — that the first Speaker has no law — then five openings would have to appear among the seven lines. But six openings appeared among those seven lines. By this reckoning, the seven lines became the masters of the seven cycles, the six openings became the six laws, and the law was made obligatory upon Adam (ع).

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Regarding the chapter of the six openings among the seven lines, and the inference drawn from the lines upon the Prophets and from the openings upon the six laws: the matter is not as he stated. The seven lines stand for the higher letters, since every Prophet inscribed one of those letters; and the six openings stand for the cycles of the Imams — between Adam and Noah, the seven Imams of the first cycle; between Noah and Abraham in the second cycle; between Abraham and Moses in the third; and so on up to Muhammad (ع). No further cycle remains for the two Qaims except the cycle of their successors and substitutes in the world. Thus six openings fell across five lines, inasmuch as between the cycles of the Imams there fell five Speakers who were the masters of the laws, in this likeness:

Adam - Noah - Abraham - Moses - Jesus - Muhammad - al-Qaim
the foundation - the foundation - the foundation - the foundation - the imams - the imams - the imams

Just as no Imams come after the الْقَائِم, he is not charged with establishing the law. And likewise, since no Imams came before Adam, Adam too was not charged with establishing the law. For if establishing the law must be preceded by Imams, it must equally be followed by them. The foundation of Noah (ع) preceded the cycle of Adam's foundation, so Noah was followed by Imams, and therefore the duty of establishing the law fell to him. The same holds for Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad. But the الْقَائِم was preceded by the cycle of Muhammad and is followed by no Imams; therefore the duty of establishing the law does not fall to him. Likewise, Adam was followed by the Imams of his cycle but was preceded by no Imams; therefore the duty of establishing the law did not fall to him. This is what they say.

We say:

The statement of the author of al-Islah agrees with what he has indicated, save for one passage within his words — his saying that "just as the opening appeared after the second line, so the law of the first was not fulfilled except by the appearance of the second Speaker." This is an unsound saying and not upright. The law of Adam is itself its own foundation, and it was Adam who raised up its foundation. If it were permissible to say that the law of Adam was not fulfilled until the coming of the Speaker who holds the second cycle, it would likewise be permissible to say that the law of Muhammad is incomplete and is fulfilled only by the coming of the الْقَائِم, who holds the seventh cycle (ع) — and that is among the things impossible to say, for the law is a completed stature that admits no increase and no decrease. Had the author of al-Islah said instead that the first cycle, which belongs to Adam, was not fulfilled except by the appearance of the holder of the second cycle, who is Noah — and that likewise no cycle is fulfilled except by the appearance of the one that follows it — placing "the first cycle" in the position of his phrase "the law," his words would have been consistent and balanced. And Allah forgives us and him through His mercy.

Chapter Twenty-Eight of Title IX

As for the saying of the author of al-Nusrah concerning the line and the opening, and his interpretation of the six openings upon the Imams: it is sound, provided that the lines stand for the Speakers and not for the higher letters, and that the openings stand for the duration of their command in the cycle and not for the foundations. Even so, this does not require that Adam have no law. On the contrary, it makes the law obligatory precisely through the presence of the Imams after him. For the Imams are present in order to preserve the station of the Speaker, his law, and his designs within his community; were it not for that purpose, no obedience to an Imam after a Speaker would be obligatory. As for the five lines and the six openings, the author of al-Islah may take them and set the lines upon the five Possessors of Resolve and the openings upon the cycles of the masters of the six laws, since the foremost thing in interpretation is that the thing be identical and balanced when it is stated, so that its place in the souls is the more exalted.

In sum, we say:

The cycle is of two kinds: a major cycle and a minor cycle. The major cycle belongs to the Speakers, whose place the Imams after them preserve within their community. The minor cycle belongs to the completing foundations, who seal the weeks. If the lines are interpreted upon the masters of the major cycles — that is, the Speakers — then the openings can only be the duration of their governance of their community through the Imams who come after them in the cycle, a cycle sealed by the coming of the last of their kind; and its measure is known. If they are interpreted upon the masters of the seven minor cycles — that is, the completing Imams in every cycle — then the openings can only be the duration of the command of the Imams in each week, together with their completers whose cycle is sealed by the seventh of them; and their number is known, as is the number of Imams in each cycle, and the number of the appendages according to the existing higher bodies already described. This will be set down in another book, if Allah the Exalted permits.

Chapter Twenty-Nine of Title IX

The author of al-Nusrah said:

By way of example, the words of Allah the Exalted:

> كَمَا بَدَأَكُمْ تَعُودُونَ
>
> "As He originated you, so shall you return."

That is: as the beginning of the matter was — which was not from the side of the realities — so shall the end of the matter be.

We say:

Even if this is an interpretation, its proper face is to say what corresponds. The words of Allah the Exalted refer to what comes to pass after the الْقَائِم: the return of knowledge, after its going forth into act and its abundance, to potency and to fewness; and the coming of all people after him to be as they were in the time of Adam; and the return of ignorance, after its having been in potency and fewness, to act and to abundance — by reason of the measured wisdom therein. Blessed be Allah, the best of creators.

Chapter Thirty of Title IX

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Again, had the author of al-Islah considered what lies within his name — I mean Adam — by way of indication that he had no law, that would have become plain to him. For his name consists of three letters: Alif, Dal, and Mim (اَلِفٌ، دَالٌ، مِيمٌ). These letters are neither compounded nor composed; each stands apart from the others. This is a clear indication that Adam was not the author of the composition of the law. But the five Prophets who came after him have names that are composed and compounded — such as Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad (نُوحٌ، إِبْرَاهِيمُ، مُوسَى، عِيسَى، مُحَمَّدٌ) — which is evidence that each of them was the master of a law. This is what he said.

We say:

If the separateness of the letters of Adam's name requires that Adam have no law, then the fact that these letters are not in themselves separate requires that he have a law. The Alif of the name is composed of alif, lam, and fa (اَلِف، لَام، فَاء); the Mim is composed of mim, ba, and mim (مِيمٌ، بَاءٌ، مِيمٌ); and the Dal likewise. If separateness does not oblige a law for him, then non-separateness does oblige a law. Yet inference of this kind is an impossibility and does not hold. Were it sound, it would follow that the الْقَائِم is the author of composition, since his name, as the Prophet ﷺ reported it, is Muhammad — and Muhammad is composed of four letters. And since it is impossible that the الْقَائِم be the master of a law while his name is composite, it is equally void that the separateness of the letters of Adam's name should indicate that he is not the author of composition and of a law — just as it is void that the joining of the letters of the name of the الْقَائِم, who is Muhammad, and its being composite, should indicate that he is the master of a law. When that is void, no weight attaches to inference of this sort. The law of Adam is established, and praise be to Allah, Lord of the Worlds.

Chapter Thirty-One of Title IX

The author of al-Islah said:

As for the saying that Noah was the first to raise up his foundation, and the likening of him to the ship, enough has already been said of it in the chapter on the foundations.

We say:

Noah was singled out by the ark because he was the first of the Possessors of Resolve and the first to undertake the abrogation of the laws and to compose a new law. His law stood in the station of the ship, and that by which he abrogated the former law stood in the station of the flood — for by the flood he destroyed the people of the earth, save those who answered his law and boarded his ship, that is, who established obedience to his foundation. The meaning is that his law endured through his lifetime; and when he had perfected it, he committed the matter to his foundation, commanded him to raise up the summons upon his law, and commanded the appendages to hearken to him, so that they attained the flowing grace only through him. Whoever obeyed his foundation was in the ship, and whoever disobeyed him and held to the first law was drowned. Do you not see that those who worshipped Wadd, Suwa, Yaghuth, Yauq, and Nasr (وَدّاً، وَسُوَاعاً، وَيَغُوثَ، وَيَعُوقَ، وَنَسْراً) were described as drowned by the flood? For He said:

> مِنْ خَطِيئَاتِهِمْ اُغْرِقُوا فَاُدْخِلُوا النَّارَ
>
> "Because of their sins they were drowned, then made to enter the Fire."

The author of al-Nusrah said, and we say:

If the station of the ship is like the station of the outward law, then whoever held to it from among the people of the outward was saved — and the veiled, true summons is annulled. Moreover, what is outward in the Book of Allah the Exalted is other than what the author of al-Islah maintained. For Allah the Exalted described the beginning of the command of Noah, his being sent to his people, his calling them to his law, and then commanded him to fashion the Ark. This account is set down in the Sura of Hud; and had the ship been the outward law, Allah would have commanded him first to fashion the Ark. But the story turns upon the fact that Noah first composed the outward law and called creation to make use of it, and only then was commanded to fashion the Ark — meaning, to raise up his legatee. The evidence of this is the words of Allah the Exalted:

> وَٱصْنَعِ ٱلْفُلْكَ
>
> "And fashion the Ark,"

up to His words:

> وَكُلَّمَا مَرَّ عَلَيْهِ مَلَأٌۭ مِّن قَوْمِهِۦ سَخِرُوا۟ مِنْهُ
"And whenever a company of his people passed him, they mocked him" —

meaning that whenever Noah raised up his legatee, the nobles of his people mocked him and marveled at him, because he was the first to inaugurate the raising up of the legatee; and for that they mocked him. This is what they said.

We say:

As for the saying of the author of al-Islah and his interpretation of the ship upon the outward of the new law: whoever entered beneath its rulings found it to be for him in the station of a ship, delivering him from abasement, humiliation, and every kind of affliction, just as the one within a ship is delivered from drowning; and this meaning is admissible. As for the author of al-Nusrah and his interpretation of the ship upon the summons and the legatee: whoever entered the guiding summons and bound himself to obedience to the foundation was made safe from drowning in error — from turning away from the unity of Allah the Exalted and the knowledge of His limits — that summons being for him in the station of guidance. Both interpretations are sound in this respect, the one in the matter of this world and the other in the matter of religion, and neither of them diminishes the other, for each of them stands upon its own head.

Chapter Thirty-Two of Title IX

As for the saying of the author of al-Nusrah that Noah was the first to raise up the legatee — by the interpretation that came to him — it does not hold. For the prophethood of Adam and his law are established, and it is confirmed that Seth (ع) was his legatee, the foremost in the law and its establisher under him. Reason itself indicates that each of the Speakers must of necessity have a legatee who takes his benefit from him, especially since every mover of necessity has a moved, the mover being one even if many are moved from him. And since the Speaker in his age is a mover of all souls by his summons to seek subsistence through the raising up of the two worships, he must of necessity have a moved who runs in the course of a learner from him, even if many learners learn from him; and the true learner from him is the one whom we name the legatee. Adam, being a Speaker, must have had a legatee, and so must every Speaker. And since Adam did raise up a legatee for himself, it is not permissible to hold any belief that contradicts that.

Chapter Thirty-Three of Title IX

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Following his interpretation of what Allah the Exalted related concerning the son of Noah:

> سَـَٔاوِىٓ إِلَىٰ جَبَلٍۢ يَعْصِمُنِى مِنَ ٱلْمَآءِ
>
> "I shall take refuge on a mountain that will shield me from the water,"

and Noah's reply:

> لَا عَاصِمَ ٱلْيَوْمَ مِنْ أَمْرِ ٱللَّهِ
"There is no shield today from the command of Allah" —

he held that the matter was, before Noah, in the hands of the appendages, since the son did not know the rank of the legatee; and that had Adam possessed a legatee, his son would have known of it. This is what he said.

We say:

This saying of his contradicts what he himself said in the chapter of the cycles: that the Imams after Adam took charge of his cycle. I do not know how this escaped him. I suspect that the copy of the book has been altered — added to and diminished — through the fewness of those who attend to the matter of religion and their neglect of correcting what they write by collation and by reading it back to the people of knowledge and religion. And Allah is the one whose help is sought.

The interpretation of "the son of Noah" is that this son ascribed to Noah was not of his loins. Rather, he was a man of great worldly standing whom Noah had advanced — like the adversary in the cycle of Muhammad our Prophet ﷺ. He took his benefit from Noah, learned what Noah had, and by his own strength became a son to him in rank, so that he reckoned it within himself, and the people too imagined that he would be his legatee. But when the command of Allah the Exalted came — that Noah fashion the Ark, that is, that he confer the legateeship upon another below him in accordance with what Allah had commanded — Noah called the people who followed him to enter beneath his command and obedience. That is His saying:

> ٱرْكَبُوا۟ فِيهَا
>
> "Embark in it."

So this man held back from what the others had entered into — obedience to his foundation — refusing to enter the ship. Noah called him with these words:

> يا بني اركب معنا ولا تكن مع الكافرين
"O my son, embark with us, and be not with the disbelievers" —

calling him to obedience and to binding himself to the command, especially with the desire that he return to his beginning, reckoning that he would act as Noah had seen of his former goodly compliance with his commands. But upon Noah's calling him, and his despairing utterly of that rank he had hoped for, he grew hypocritical and arrogant and refused to obey his foundation in what had come to pass. Noah said that the command of Allah lay upon him: were he not to relinquish his headship, it would be nullified; and the like of this adversary inclined against our guardian. Noah admonished him at one time and warned him at another, saying that the command of Allah lay in it, and that if he did not heed the admonition his headship would depart and abasement would encompass him, and he would see none to take his part nor to help him in his affair; and that is His saying:

> لَا عَاصِمَ ٱلْيَوْمَ مِنْ أَمْرِ ٱللَّهِ
>
> "There is no shield today from the command of Allah."

When the son heard this from him, he showed rebellion and said, in his ignorance, that this matter would not be brought to completion for Noah, that he should set the whole of the headship within his own house; and that even if constraint and injury and harm appeared, he would be supported by chiefs who would strengthen his hand and help him to ward off abasement from himself; and that is his saying:

> سَـَٔاوِىٓ إِلَىٰ جَبَلٍۢ يَعْصِمُنِى مِنَ ٱلْمَآءِ
>
> "I shall take refuge on a mountain that will shield me from the water."

The water here is that which is poured out upon the force that slays, and the mountain is that which he clings to of men and partisans. So he set his purpose, and he and his like took up his cause, and they perished in his cycle, and there remained only those who bound themselves to obedience to his foundation.

Allah has set down such tidings in His noble Book as an admonition to the community concerning what befalls the command of the foundation through him who is intransigent toward it and does not bind himself to its obedience. Now He indicates it through our Prophet's word, "O children of Adam"; now He points to it by the she-camel of Salih; and now by the son of Noah — that they may know that the states of the cycle of Muhammad are like the states of the bygone cycles, and that He did not single out Muhammad and his foundation with the intransigence of their community toward them and its hostility toward them. This is a custom that has run through all the Prophets and legatees, reaching to the time of the seventh. The refuge sought by the son of Noah was not to the appendage, but to the people of the outward who were in a state of hypocrisy like his own; he sought their aid, and the sword encompassed them by the power of Allah, and Allah purified the earth of them. That is the flood that overspread outwardly and inwardly. So likewise shall it be in the cycle of Muhammad: some of it has already come true and passed, and some of it shall come true and come to pass by the leave of Allah, and none knows that save the discerning one among the people of the Alawi summons, whose lights Allah has spread abroad.

## 19. The Creator of the Thing and the No-Thing

The First Section of the Tenth Chapter

We say:

The author of al-Mahsul, may Allah have mercy on him, worked tirelessly in service of the دَعْوَة and its sons. He opened the doors of its landmarks through the books he composed, which stand as enduring monuments over their expanses. When Sheikh Abu Hatim, may Allah have mercy on him, then corrected what he corrected in that work, most people concluded that the correction was a fault against the author of al-Mahsul. They attacked him on the strength of the apology the corrector offered in his own book, faulting the work and overturning it. That judgment is corrupt — a turning away from the truth. The matter is otherwise than they supposed.

The speaker (ع), when he laid down the order of things, knew that the sciences of religion are too vast for any single soul beneath the imam to encompass or to preserve in a single utterance. He therefore arranged many persons beneath the imamate to cooperate with one another across all those sciences — their awareness and their preservation of them — just as the twelve حُجَّج cooperate. Each of them benefits from those to whom Allah the Exalted has entrusted the reins of guidance among the righteous imams, receiving from them in the abstruse matters, inner and outer, according to each one's capacity and strength in reception and deduction. They stand in relation to others in guidance as the imams stand in exposition and instruction. Religion, through them, resembles a single person who holds all his parts together: they are to religion as the senses are to that person, perceiving things on its behalf. If one sense misses something, another perceives it — and that second perception is no defect against the one that missed it, nor against any of the others.

If that is so, then Abu Hatim's correction, may Allah raise his degree, of what he corrected from the book of al-Mahsul, is no fault against him, no deficiency against the author of al-Mahsul, and no challenge against either of them. It is only what we have said: the persons ranked for the correction of the sciences of religion are like the senses. If one sense fails to perceive something in the natural world, another does not fail it, but cooperates over it — discharging the right of religion and confirming the obligation owed to the Lord of the Worlds.

Since that is so, and since I have seen in the book of al-Mahsul what was most binding upon Abu Hatim and most obligatory for him to expound and correct — something he neither corrected nor spoke upon, but passed over — the truth of cooperation obliged me to discharge the right of religion by clarifying what is folded within what he brought forward: namely, what it is not permissible to hold as belief in the chapter of monotheism and the first Creator (الْمُبْدِع الْأَوَّل), setting aside the other branches, which — if Allah aids by deferring the term — we shall discuss in other books. So I did it. It is not permissible for me to apologize for that, since all souls are, for religion, one — even as I apologize to my own soul. May Allah accept from us what we offer to draw near to Him of the guardianship (walaya) that leads to His good pleasure, Lord of the Worlds, and gather us by His grace — the past and the renewed, the foremost of us and the latecomer alike — with our pure loyalists (mawali) in the Abode of Holiness. He is Munificent, Generous.

The Second Section of the Tenth Chapter

The author of al-Mahsul said:

In the chapter of monotheism: He is the Creator of the thing and of the no-thing, the rational, the illusory, the intellectual, the logical. I mean what falls beneath these elements, and what does not fall beneath them.

We say:

By this saying he has necessitated that among the existents which are from Allah the Exalted, falling beneath His origination (إِبْدَاع), there is something not perceived by reason, nor by illusion, nor by thought, and of which logic gives no report — yet he would make belief in its existence permissible, even though that existence is impossible. Whatever is not perceived by reason, illusion, or thought, and of which logic gives no report, if it had existence, could only stand in one of three relations to the intellect: prior to it in existence, simultaneous with it, or posterior to it.

It cannot be prior to the intellect. The intellect is the very act behind which is none but Allah the Exalted — it issued from Him, it came to be from Him. It is pure origination, the true existence (اَلْوُجُودُ الْحَقُّ), the first existent from Allah, glory be to Him.

Nor can its existence be simultaneous with the very intellect. The intellect, at its coming-to-be, is a single essence, not two. If this supposed thing shared existence with the intellect while remaining distinct from it, it would follow that Allah the Exalted — from whom it came to be — is a manifold originating cause, or that there is with Him another; exalted is He above that with a great exaltation. It belongs to an effect that its existence accord with its cause. Two distinct effects require either two causes, or a single cause made manifold by relations, such that the existence of each effect traces to a distinct relation within that cause rather than to the other's relation within it. So it is impossible for two distinct effects to exist from a single cause whose essence is preserved from difference, with no relations or additions. Since simultaneous existence with the intellect requires this, it is impossible — for Allah the Exalted is sanctified above every mode of multiplicity, above additions and othernesses, and above whatever would make Him a cause in that manner.

Nor can its existence be posterior to the intellect. If it were posterior to the intellect and derived from it, then knowledge of it would not escape the minds, nor would its definition be difficult. The minds would perceive it and define it, just as they have defined all other existents — sensible and intelligible alike — in the arena of affirmation and negation. Since its existence is not free of these three positions, and each position makes its existence impossible, it is established that its existence is impossible, and to hold it as belief is misguidance.

The Third Section of the Tenth Chapter

Had the author of al-Mahsul not added, after affirming the thing and the no-thing, that Allah originated them both — meaning what falls beneath these elements and what does not fall beneath them — we might have supposed that by "the thing" he intended what is described among bodies, and by "the no-thing" what carries none of the attributes of bodies, from the world of origination, relying on the error of those who hold such a position. But since he brought forward what folds within itself the affirmation of the impossible, it results only in one's believing, among the existents, in something that shares with Allah in His transcendence in some manner — and from that, corruption spreads widely. We have already shown that this is impossible.

Then we say:

"The thing" is known to be a common term beneath which fall all the existents: substances and accidents, sensible and intelligible alike. His intention in saying "the no-thing" can only be one of two things. The first is what some of the sages held: that it is an affirmation of that which is other than a thing — an affirmation stripped of what confines the thing to its two divisions of substances and accidents, raised to the corrected form of the privative proposition. They alluded to it, saying: it is like our saying "not-the-man," which is an affirmation of everything that is not a man, whereby the one reported of is stripped from us by existence, without affirming what belongs to no thing. They said: it belongs to the particle لا, when by it is intended a specific entity so as to remove it from existence — negating it of what it is, without affirming of it something other than itself — as in the saying "the thing," which is a universal entity under which every existent falls. When we apply the particle لا to it, we say لا شيء, and the very entity of the thing is made to vanish from what it is as a thing, without affirmation, as something other than it. Or, if by it is intended a particular entity within that universal entity, and something other is affirmed:

then if his intention is the affirmation of that which is a not-thing, his saying that Allah is its originator is an error. The no-thing is that which no attribute befalls and which is in no state like that of substance or accident. The negation of the attributes of the thing from Allah the Exalted is a transcendence and a sanctification of Him — He is neither substance nor accident, and the attributes of the thing are no attribute of His, nor from Him, nor is He like them. So it is as though by saying "the originator of the thing and the no-thing" he has said: the originator of His own essence, which is other than the thing, and the originator of the thing, which is other than His essence. And that is an error.

And if his intention is the negation of the very entity of the thing, his saying that Allah the Exalted is its originator is equally an error. The thing is the origination; it is the very entity of existence. The no-thing is the negation of origination and the nullification of existence. If the no-thing is the negation of origination, how can it be sound that He is its originator — when He has negated it from that impossible thing?

The Fourth Section of the Tenth Chapter

The author of al-Mahsul said:

The Originator of things, not from a thing; He alone, and no thing with Him, everlastingly. So when we say "He, and no thing with Him," we have negated the thing and the no-thing, and made them both originated; and we have negated every form, simple and composite, from His identity (huwiyya); and we have made everything upon which speech falls, or upon which speech does not fall, an originated effect in truth, finite; and that the no-thing is after the thing, since the coming-to-be of its name came to be only at the appearing of the thing.

We say:

Enough has already passed in our discourse upon the no-thing. His saying, "the Originator of things, not from a thing only, He and no thing with Him, everlastingly," folds within it what it is not permissible to hold as belief. For "no-thing," with the sages, means the affirmation of that which is not a thing, according to what we have mentioned. So when he says "He, and no thing with Him," it is as though he said "He, and that which is not a thing, with Him, everlastingly" — and that is associationism (الشِّرْك).

In sum: there is, among the languages existing between mankind, no expression adequate to speak of Allah the Exalted in a manner befitting Him. All that composed and simple words and letters announce is originated. Words, letters, and languages are instruments by which people make one another understand what is within themselves. They are originated, and they indicate only what is — like themselves — originated, as we have explained in our treatise known as al-Rawda.

He, the Exalted, is beyond the minds and the perceptions, behind a veil from the understandings and from thought, and the ways of inquiry are cut off short of Him. When one is driven to affirm Him — knowing that all that tongues pronounce and lips move with falls upon what He has originated and brought into being, glory be to Him — there is no expression that can be borrowed for affirming Him without affirming an attribute or a plurality, save the expression هو, which Allah the Exalted Himself affirmed after the exception in His saying:

> شَهِدَ ٱللَّهُ أَنَّهُۥ لَآ إِلَهَ إِلَّا هُوَ

Allah bears witness that there is no god but He.

He said "but He," and did not say "but Allah," nor "but other than Allah"; He said "He." Likewise Imam al-Muizz (ع) pointed to this in his book known as تَأْوِيل al-Sharia, saying that it is "He" only. For this expression, though borrowed, is — from the standpoint of the minds — an affirmation of that which is outside them, without attribute or plurality.

This being so, the path of affirming Him, the Exalted, by using the particle لا to negate every kind of existent more fully attains the intent than the bare safety from likening (تَشْبِه) and stripping (tatil), and is more fitting and worthier in the view of the scholars and the jurists among the people of religion.

The Fifth Section of the Tenth Chapter

And his saying:

"If we say He, and no thing with Him, we have negated the thing and the no-thing, and made them both originated."

From where, then, does the existence of what is said to be a no-thing among what falls beneath origination become sound? And how can the existence of what is said to be a no-thing — prior to origination and other than Allah — be such that its negation would be sound? Nay: there is nothing but the thing, and the thing that is the willed-thing (al-mashi'); and the willed-thing is that which we have negated to be existent, in that there is, in the languages, that which expresses it — because of the greatness of His affair, the Exalted, and the shortfall of composed, originated letters from signifying in a manner befitting His majesty, the Exalted.

The thing that is the willed-thing is other than Allah, who brought it into being not from a thing — it has an entity and a right in existence across all its divisions. The no-thing, then, is the negation of the very entity of the thing, which is pure origination, as our discourse has already established. If the no-thing is the negation of the very entity of origination — which is the very entity of existence and the true existence — how does it come to be, in existence, an originator? Those sayings are not sound.

The Sixth Section of the Tenth Chapter

And his saying:

"We have negated every form, simple and composite, from His identity, and we have made everything upon which speech falls, or upon which speech does not fall, an originated effect in truth."

We say:

How does that upon which speech does not fall — the speech from which all the existents came to be — become, all of it, an originated effect? That is an error.

The Seventh Section of the Tenth Chapter

And his saying:

"The no-thing is after the thing, since the coming-to-be of its name came to be from it at the appearing of a thing."

The no-thing is not a name that falls upon an entity having a share in existence. "The thing" is a name, and the لا — the particle of negation — has negated it. The negation of a name does not become a name for something other than what the name was a name of, nor does it make a report about something other than what the sages mentioned: that folded within it is the affirmation of that which has no name. Our earlier discourse already contains the answer to this, and in it there is sufficiency.

The Eighth Section of the Tenth Chapter

The author of al-Mahsul said:

In the chapter of the first Creator: the Maker, the Exalted, originated the world at a single stroke — that is, He originated the intellect as a single whole, and there emerged within it, by His determining, the forms of the two worlds and what is in them, without those forms being pointed to by their identities before the intellect. Rather the intellect's knowledge falls upon them, and they are, with it, known. Yet the intellect has preceded them in act; so whether in act or in potency, it and the forms are all together.

We say:

This knowledge — the intellect he describes as originated, within which emerged the forms of the two worlds and what is in them — cannot be the first intellect existent from Allah the Exalted. That which is prior in existence is independent of conceiving the means by which it exists: its existence is bound to its Originator, who brought it into being. It is not incumbent upon it to know more than its own essence, the fact that it is originated, the fact that its existence is not by its own essence but by another — which is its Originator — and the fact that it is a cause for the existence of what is below it. It is like the imam, who is not obliged to know others below him, and knows no more than that he is the cause of deliverance for those below him and a mediator between the one above him and the one below him. Rather it is incumbent upon him to know the one above him, and incumbent upon those below to know him and to know those whose existence and rank preceded him, so that they may be delivered thereby. For this reason, posteriority in existence obliges the posterior to conceive all that preceded it in existence among the limits, that this may earn it subsistence and complete existence.

## 20. Creativity Is the Creator

If this is so, then what he has described is the intellect that comes forth from the world of nature, not the First Intellect — and this confirms what we have already said. For he holds that the intellect brought forth within itself the images of the worlds, without those images being indicated by their own identities prior to the intellect; rather, the intellect's knowledge falls upon them, down to his words: as for in potency, it and the images are all together. That saying of his affirms what subsists in potency and speaks of that whose existence is not yet in act. But the First Intellect does not subsist in act by way of potency, and it cannot be said that anything within it subsists in potency; for if that were so, something of the same kind of existence, subsisting already in act, would have to precede it in order to bring it out into act. Yet it is void that anything should exist alongside the First Intellect or precede it. Once that is established, the words of the author of al-Mahsul apply only to what is posterior in existence — that which has no further existence behind it, and whose substance is realized through knowing what preceded it among the worlds. That is the second thing emanated from the world of nature, like the Speaker who possesses the images of the worlds; and the sum of those images has come to subsist in act and to be emanated in act.

In the ninth chapter of the tenth title, the author of al-Mahsul said: Since the cause of the intellect was the unity of the Almighty, and unity is eternal, the intellect became eternal.

Upon this we say: his words make necessary something that precedes the First Intellect in its existence — namely, the cause he calls unity. We have said, however, that nothing at all precedes the intellect in its existence save God; therefore the intellect is worthier of eternity, since it is impossible for anything else to exist. Unity, then, is not the cause of the intellect in the sense of something distinct from it that precedes it. Rather, it is the very essence of the intellect, and the intellect is the very essence of unity, and it is the One. It is the cause and it is the effect; it is creativity and it is the Creator; it is the Perfect and the Complete; it is the Eternal and the Everlasting; it is Existence and the Existent — all by a single essence. The meanings these names carry are merited only by relating it to that from which its existence comes, which is God the Almighty, or by relating it to what lies below it, or by relating it to itself, because the essence in its existence is manifold. Sufficient proof of this is the author's own word in this chapter concerning creativity: the Creator, from the standpoint of the Creator, is one, and its form is the form of the intelligible. If that is so, and unity is the very essence of the intellect, then his claim that the intellect became eternal through the eternity of its cause is impossible; for the intellect is not something separate from the cause that became eternal through it — rather, it is the cause, and the cause is it. An attribute of that kind — that its eternity derives from the eternity of its cause — holds true only of the emanated intellects that come forth from the world of nature, such as the Speakers (النُّطَقَاءُ) and the Foundations (الْأُسُس) and their followers (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ), whose intellects are one thing and that by which they become eternal is another; for the cause of their sanctification and eternity is those separate eternal intellects called the angels brought near, together with the extension of their lights to them and their union with those intellects. The words of the author of al-Mahsul, then, bear upon the intellects that arise in the world of nature, not upon the measure in the world of creation. Since his words and their meanings are directed toward the intellects in the abode of nature, to conceive that great and majestic limit, and its rank, under this description is impossible.

In the tenth chapter of the tenth title, we then say: the meaning of his words, that unity is the cause of the intellect, cannot escape one of these senses — either that unity stands to the intellect in the place of its Originator, the Almighty, whom he calls unity; or that it stands to it in the place of prime matter; or that it stands to it in the place of form among the existents in the abode of body; or that it stands to it in the place of the end for whose sake it was brought into being, being its very self and essence.

It is void that it stand to it in the place of its Originator. For if unity is the cause of the intellect, and a cause is a thing, and a thing is something whose existence is not by itself, then he would in effect have made God the Almighty — whom he calls unity — a creator who came into being after having been the Creator, by making Him dependent on another in the very sense his own words convey; and that is impossible. Moreover, since unity is other than God, and whatever is other than God is a created, originated thing, he would necessarily have made God — whom he calls unity — a created, originated thing; and this is impossible.

It is void that it stand to it in the place of prime matter among the existents, because of the impossibility that anything exist and move through existence alongside God the Almighty such that something else be found with Him, as the argument has already shown.

It is void that it stand to it in the place of form among the existents by which it was brought to be, because form cannot exist except through something that stands to it in the place of matter, and the existence of matter alongside God the Almighty has been shown void, since it is through matter that form would exist. Furthermore, God the Almighty is far above being multiplied by forms, which are the mark of origination, such that He should become a creator who came into being after having been the Creator. Exalted and magnified is God above that.

It is void that it stand to it in the place of the end for whose sake it was brought into being, because the intellect was not brought into being for the sake of unity. Had it been brought into being for unity, no multiplicity would have been brought into being; yet multiplicity exists, and therefore it was not for the sake of unity that it was brought into being.

When these senses of his words — that unity is the cause of the intellect — are all shown void and excluded, it is established that the essence of unity and the cause are one and the same. And since the intellect is the very essence of unity and the cause, his saying that the intellect became eternal through the eternity of its cause, intending by this the First, which is the First Existent, is not sound.

In the eleventh chapter of the tenth title, the author of al-Mahsul said: Since the form was made manifest within it from unity, likewise the abstracted form became eternal before it, because the intellect became eternal in its entirety, not in part.

We say: this is an attribute of something in whose potency lies that form made manifest within it, and it is impossible to say this of that majestic limit which subsists in act, not in potency. If that is so, his words are sound only with regard to the emanated intellects — such as the intellects of the Speakers (النُّطَقَاءُ), the Foundations (الْأُسُس), the Imams, and their followers — which came forth into full intellect through their whole, which is the separate intellect called the angel brought near, surnamed the Pen, in its entirety, and through the eternity of their intellects. The intellects of those who follow them in truth realize this and confirm it. The author of al-Mahsul adds, following what he mentioned: Had the forms not been originated with eternity, they would have no subsistence and no return to the cradle of their source from which they came forth with the form; they would perish and vanish, and there would be no hope, no fear, no reward, and no punishment; for everything whose cause is eternal within it thereby becomes eternal also. That saying of his requires that the forms he mentions — those which have no subsistence and no connection with their sources except through the eternal power — are those in the world of nature, from among the souls that have no subsistence in act and gain no subsistence and permanence except through those who attained eternity and perfection. Were it not for these, reward and punishment would be void, as he says; for it is through the Speaker and those who stand in his place that this is made sound. The meaning of his words is therefore sound only with regard to the intellects emanated from the world of nature, below the world of creation.

In the twelfth chapter of the tenth title, the author of al-Mahsul said: Since the intellect was caused by the eternity whose interpretation with us is the composite saying, the Word of God, and there was no third mediator between the two, the intellect became like it.

We say: his words lead to difficulties like those already raised, and they do not befit the First Intellect. The First Intellect is not an essence of which this can be said, since it is the very essence of the Word; nor is it something other than the Word, nor something that once was and then became like it. All of that pertains to the intellects that go forth from potency into act in the abode of nature — those which become so by receiving the effects of the Word, that is, the First Intellect, and through union with it become like it. This is confirmed by his further words: Since the form of the act became, by the Word, the form of the intellect, the intellect thereby became a cause of what is below it among things, and became, by the Word, encompassing them by what the Creator sees within it. For that whose form became, through the Word, a form for it, and which became a cause of what is below it — this implies that it once was without being a cause and without its form being the form of the Word. What is of this rank is only the intellects of the Speakers, not that Creator-intellect originated without mediator, which was never other than the Word, nor other than the cause of the existent whose existence is first, beyond which all descriptions are cut off. Magnified and exalted is He. What we have already said concerning that great limit is sufficient, without need of further extension. That being so, to treat that great limit as running in the same course as these lower limits is impossible.

In the thirteenth chapter of the tenth title, the author of al-Mahsul said: Since this is so, it was named Perfect by creativity; for creativity is from the Creator, and by perfect creativity there arises only the perfect.

We say: we have already addressed this. His words are mistaken in two respects. The first is that he applied the name intellect to the Creator and made it like its Originator, calling it perfect — and this is association, which may not be said; for God is set apart by His own selfhood from the perfection and the perfect that exist only because He originated both. Nor may it be said that the creator, through perfect creativity, produces only the perfect in the sense that creativity itself is the perfect. The second is that he applied the name creativity to what belongs to the world of nature, and that is impossible; for the one who truly deserves the name creativity is that whose existence precedes all things, not that which subsists in act or in potency. That being so, what he said may only be accepted in the sense in which it is sound.

In the fourteenth chapter of the tenth title, the author of al-Mahsul said: The intellect bestows the forms only from its cause, which is the Word, just as the sun bestows upon those who kindle from its light, not from its body. And since its bringing forth of what is in it is from the power of its cause, which is the Word, and its bestowing them thus is from it, it became clear that the Word is what became the cause of the things that come forth from the intellect, not the identity of the intellect, save that the intellect became a mediator between the Word and what is below it.

We say: the intellect he mentions is the intellects of the Speakers and their followers, which kindle from their cause — which is other than they — not the First Intellect, which is the First Creator. The First Intellect has no need of another for the existence of what comes to be from it; it is itself the cause and the very Word, and from it and from its own essence is the existence of what exists. This is why we said that it is the Perfect from which the perfect came to exist, needing nothing outside its own essence, being itself the cause of the existence of things. Were something other than the First Intellect to precede it, that which precedes would have to be the First Creator, not it; and it would follow that the order of the existents fell into disorder, which is impossible. As for his saying that the intellect became a mediator between the Word and what is below it, we showed the impossibility of this earlier, at the opening of our book.

There is no harm in adding a further point, and so we say: how can anything precede the First Intellect, so as to serve as a mediator between it and what is below it, when the intellect is the very essence of the Word, which is the cause existing from God the Almighty? Were something permitted to precede it, that which precedes could only be either of the same kind of existence as the intellect, or not. If it is of the same kind, it cannot precede the intellect, since their ranks and limits are equal, both being of one kind. If it is not of the same kind, then it is either a thing or not a thing. If it is a thing, it is an object for the apprehension of intellects, and the intellect is prior to what it apprehends, not the reverse. If it is not a thing, then it is not the Word, but the One who transcends above the qualities that a thing deserves in its divisions. Glory be to Him, exalted with great exaltation. In whatever way the question is approached, it remains impossible for anything to precede the First Intellect; for there is nothing above the Thing except the Thing, exalted. That being so, it is the Word, and it is the cause, and it is unity, and it is the intellect, as we have explained above.

> وحسبنا الله ونعم الوكيل

And God is sufficient for us, and He is the best Trustee.

In the fifteenth chapter of the tenth title, the author of al-Mahsul said: Creativity is a mediator between the Creator and the created, the created being an effect of the influencer upon the influenced. And he said: The form of creativity is intermediate between the doer and the deed; its existence is from the side of the creative doer, and the effect of the form is found from the side of the Creator in the deed. This form, I mean creativity, came to be in the Creator, glory be to Him.

We say: these words enclose within them an affirmation of something that God did not originate, bring about, or create anew — and this is contrary to what the affirmers of divine unity hold. For by his words he made creativity other than the Creator, and thereby made it necessary that creativity be a form in the Creator; and by further asserting that what bears the form he calls creativity — what he terms the influencer within it — is fixed, uninvented, and unoriginated, he required what is not permissible.

For creativity is not something other than the Creator. If what he conceives as Creator were other than creativity, its existence could only be either alongside God the Almighty or by itself. It is void that its existence be by itself, for then it would be divided within itself while yet not ceasing to be alongside God the Almighty. If its existence is alongside God the Almighty, this can only be in one of two ways: either resembling Him — and exalted is God above that — as a likeness to Him, making the two into two likenesses, each of them specified through whatever accounts for their duality and their specification as two distinct things, which itself arises only from some necessitating cause prior to both and from which their existence derives; or not resembling and not a likeness, in which case it is an opposite. The existence of two opposites, however, is possible only through something further that preserves their existence, since the existence of one of two opposites entails the annulment of the other's existence. Their existence therefore requires something prior to both that preserves them — which implies that the Creator the Almighty is Himself created, and that something prior and worthier of divinity precedes Him, exalted and magnified is He; and that is even more firmly prevented and more impossible.

Since the existence of what his words require cannot escape these two aspects, and both aspects are void, it is void that its existence be by itself; and once that is void, it is established that its existence is from God the Almighty. Creativity, then, has its existence from God the Almighty, and therefore neither creativity nor the Creator may be called by its name in priority over the other. That being so — and since it is void that existence proceed from that from which the existent issues as anything other than one — it is established that creativity is the Creator. And since creativity is the Creator, what the author of al-Mahsul said is mistaken; let it be set aside, if God the Almighty wills. We have now arrived at what we promised at the beginning of the book.

We say: unity and the knowledge of the limits are a difficult matter, yet in them lie the life of souls, their subsistence, and their deliverance from the world of nature and its transmutations. Since it is difficult, and God the Almighty knew that not all His servants attain the degree by which they reach perfection through knowing the limits of God and His unity, He singled out from among them, in every age, one man through perfection and election, such as the Imam (ع). By perfection I mean that he is complete in receiving the overflow that courses through the world of nature from the side of the angels brought near, so that he is thereby a guardian over them and one who raises them to His whole. God the Almighty therefore made obedience to him and love of him obligatory, that they might thereby become part of His whole, out of His mercy toward them. There is no path to this except by holding fast to the two acts of worship, and rendering the right of both in knowledge and in deed, outwardly and inwardly, and being eager to preserve them. And that is only through the remembrance of death and the parting it brings from family, child, kin, and homeland, and through the certainty that there is no arrival at the return except through provision, and no provision except what is stored up — gained only through an obedience whose obligations are performed, a duty whose right is rendered, a good that is done, a righteous deed that is worked, and a knowledge of the realities of things that is acquired. May God place us and the company of the believers throughout the easts of the earth and its wests among those sealed with the good, and grant us and them the intercession of Muhammad and Ali and the pure Imams, and our Imam, the Ruler by the Command of God, and his rightly guiding fathers, in the holy land, by His grace and mercy.

With that we seal the book with praise to God, Lord of the Worlds, and prayer upon the best of the first and the last, Muhammad and his family, the radiant and blessed, and the rightly guiding Imams. And we say:
