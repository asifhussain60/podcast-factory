# Kitab al-Riyad: A Judgment Between the Two Reformers

## 1. The Freedom to Disagree

The Islamic world at the close of the third century of the Muhammadan Hijra bore the stamp of its sciences, its philosophy, and its letters, and these shaped both its cultural sphere and the society that lived within it. New currents of thought, tangled disagreements over doctrine, and a debate that would not fall silent moved through that world without ever coming to rest in a single party or settling in a single place. When I set out to understand where such differences began, I find how useful it is to weigh the opinions of the writers and scholars who lived in that distant age, who left behind them scientific traces and fresh modes of expression suited to their time, to their grasp of philosophical questions, and to the reach of their effort in interpreting texts and explaining verses. Only by weighing all of this can I arrive at a reasonable view, one that carries me nearer to what is real and true.

These disputes were seldom content to remain what they had once been, the spoken exchanges raised in the salons and the centers of learning. They grew instead into long treatises that came to occupy the pages of books and publications. Among the groups that contributed a generous share to this field were the Ismailis. Yet I find it strange that I never see this group conclude its verbal argument with the other groups before it enters upon another argument of an altogether new kind, a debate carried on among those who called others to its own cause. In this I read a clear proof of the intellectual freedom that governed their lives, a freedom from the constraints that hold sway over conservative societies, that press upon such societies a settled balance and oblige them to shun anything anomalous and to keep clear of any departure from what they have inherited.

The first debate, or in more exact terms the first scholarly discussion, opened new horizons for the Ismailis. It carried them into an atmosphere of wide intellectual liberty and made them a subject of conjecture and of talk across the Islamic world. It began with the appearance of the book *al-Mahsul*, The Harvest.

This book came into circulation at the beginning of the fourth century after the Hijra, the tenth century of the common era. It is attributed to the great preacher of Khurasan, Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Nasafi, who was credited with turning the Samanid state in Transoxiana toward Ismailism, and who was put to death in the year 331 AH, 942 AD, as recorded in the book *al-Farq bayn al-Firaq* by its author Abd al-Qahir al-Baghdadi. Here I meet something very strange. Hujjat al-Iraqayn, the greatest exponent of this cause and its foremost philosopher, Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani, examined this very subject and probed it deeply, yet he never named the author of *al-Mahsul*. His discussion fell instead upon Abu Hatim al-Razi and Abu Yaqub al-Sijistani. There must then be substantial reasons behind this silence that account for it, and it may be that the book *al-Mahsul* is wrongly assigned to al-Nasafi at all.

Whatever the truth of that, the acclaim that *al-Mahsul* won among the Ismaili thinkers and the ranks of their scholars in that day moved Abu Hatim al-Razi, the chief preacher in the northwest of Persia, to compose a book in which he examined what had been said in *al-Mahsul*. He called his book *al-Islah*, The Correction. The opinions that al-Razi set forth plainly provoked a reaction in another great preacher, Abu Yaqub al-Sijistani, who judged them contrary to the truth and to first principles. This is what led him to write a book contesting al-Razi's views, which he called *al-Nusra*, The Defense, in which he stood by *al-Mahsul* and pressed his attack on Abu Hatim al-Razi without mercy. It appears that *al-Nusra* stands among al-Sijistani's earliest works, as al-Kirmani himself hints in his own book.

Not long afterward, al-Kirmani surveyed the whole matter in a full and searching review and composed his precious treatise *al-Riyad*, a judgment between the two reformers, as he named them, the authors of *al-Islah* and *al-Nusra*. With uncommon skill he appraised the opinions set out in the three books and subjected them to a scholarly criticism. He set them beside the Law of the Guiding Mission, which is to say beside the philosophical and doctrinal theories held officially by the Fatimid court of his day. On the whole I find that al-Kirmani consistently prefers al-Razi's theories over those of al-Sijistani, and that he faults the latter because they do not conform to the truth and because they lean toward deviation. Beyond this, he observes that al-Razi passed over some of the statements found in *al-Mahsul*, and so he brings them forward himself, as I note in the tenth chapter of the book *al-Riyad*.

The Islamic world at the close of the third century of the Muhammadan Hijra bore, as I read it, the stamp of its sciences, its philosophy, and its literature. New currents, tangled doctrinal disagreements, and an unending theological argument governed its cultural sphere and its learned society, coming to rest with no single party and settling in no single place. How useful it is, then, for the researcher who wishes to study in depth the origins of those differences to try to weigh the opinions of the writers and scholars who lived in that distant age. They left scientific traces and novel modes of expression suited to their time and to their understanding of philosophical questions, and to the reach of their effort in interpreting texts and construing verses, so that from all this he may form a reasonable view through which he passes to reality and to the truth.

Those disputes, more often than not, spilled beyond the oral discussions that used to be raised in the salons and the scientific centers, growing into long treatises that showed themselves upon the pages of books and pamphlets. Among the sects that contributed a large share to this field were the Ismailis. Yet it is very strange to me that we do not see this group finish its theological argument with the other groups before it comes forward and plunges into another argument, of an altogether new kind. It is an argument among the very men who summoned others to their cause. In this there is a clear proof of an intellectual freedom that prevailed in their lives, a release from the constraints that dominate conservative societies and that draw a boundary around them, binding them to no irregularity and to no departure from the old inherited plan.

The first argument, or in more precise terms the first scientific discussion, that opened new horizons for the Ismailis and carried them into an atmosphere of far-reaching scientific liberation, and made them a subject of conjecture and hearsay throughout the Islamic world, was the appearance of the book *al-Mahsul* (The Harvest). This book was put into circulation at the beginning of the fourth century AH, the tenth AD, and is attributed to the great Syrian preacher

> محمد بن احمد النسفي

Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Nasafi, who had the credit of turning the Samanid state toward Ismailism and who was put to death in the year 331 AH (942 AD), as is stated in the book

> الفرق بين الفرق

*al-Farq bayn al-Firaq* (The Difference Between the Sects) by its author Abd al-Qahir al-Baghdadi. It is very strange to me that the Proof of the Two Iraqs, its greatest preacher and its supreme philosopher,

> حميد الدين احمد الكرماني

Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani, who discussed the subject and went deeply into examining it, does not come to mention the author of *al-Mahsul* at all. His discourse was directed instead upon

> أبي حاتم الرازي ، وأبي يعقوب السجستاني

Abu Hatim al-Razi and Abu Yaqub al-Sijistani. There must, then, be fundamental reasons of their own for this silence, reasons that justify it, and perhaps the book *al-Mahsul* is attributed to al-Nasafi in error.

However that may be, the acclaim that the book *al-Mahsul* won among the thinkers of the Ismailis and the ranks of their scholars at that time spurred Abu Hatim al-Razi, the foremost preacher in northwestern Persia, to compose a book in which he discussed what had come in *al-Mahsul*. He named his book *al-Islah* (The Correction). It is plain that the opinions al-Razi advanced provoked a reaction in another great preacher named Abu Yaqub al-Sijistani, who held them contrary to the truth and to first principles, and this is what led him to set about composing a book in which he challenged al-Razi's views. He named it *al-Nusra* (The Defense), and in it the author upheld *al-Mahsul* and attacked Abu Hatim al-Razi without mercy. It appears that *al-Nusra* is among al-Sijistani's earliest works, as al-Kirmani hints in his book. Not long afterward, al-Kirmani surveyed the whole subject in a full survey and composed his precious treatise *al-Riyad*, a judgment between the two reformers, as he called them, that is, between the authors of

> الاصلاح والنصرة

*al-Islah* and *al-Nusra*. With surpassing skill he appraised the opinions that had come in the three books and criticized them by a scholarly criticism, setting them beside the

> قانون الدعوة الهادية

Law of the Guiding Mission, or in other words beside the official philosophical and doctrinal theories of the Fatimid court of his day.

It is also worth remarking on the scientific ideas that these works contain. A philosophical body of this kind deserves to be studied, compared, and scrutinized deeply, but by ill fortune the two books

> المحصول والنصرة

*al-Mahsul* and *al-Nusra* have been lost. As for the book *al-Islah* by al-Razi and *al-Riyad* by al-Kirmani, they are still preserved.

## 2. The Two Rivals

To my mind, al-Kirmani's *al-Riyad* (الرياض) stands among the important works that give voice to the philosophical theories of Ismailism in the fourth century of the Hijra. In it he sits in judgment between the two rivals, the authors of الاصلاح والنصرة, weighing their opinions against the standard of the guiding mission, قانون الدعوة الهادية. The controversy had first grown out of al-Nasafi's book المحصول and out of al-Sijistani's reply to it, so that the two books المحصول والنصرة set the terms of the whole dispute, and it was al-Kirmani who at last set that exchange in order. I have obtained two copies of *al-Riyad*, the first from India and the second from Tehran, and I was charged with editing the book, explaining it, and writing its footnotes and commentary. Before I take up its opinions, its arguments, and its statements in any detail, I must give a brief account of the lives of the three philosophers who took part in the discussion: Abu Hatim al-Razi, al-Sijistani, and al-Kirmani.

### Al-Razi

He is Abu Hatim Ahmad ibn Hamdan al-Laythi al-Warsanani al-Razi, whom the Ismailis name, in reverence, our master Abu Hatim. He was a preacher of the mission in the land of Rayy, then moved to Baghdad and made it his residence. The date of his birth is not known exactly. His activity in the mission fell during the imamate of Muhammad al-Mahdi Billah (Ubayd Allah), founder of the Fatimid state in the Maghrib. He was distinguished by the sweetness of his speech and by the eloquence for which he was famous. He won over al-Marwazi, the prince of Rayy, who was a strict Sunni, and kept steadily at the work of persuading the princes of those regions to enter the mission, until he became one of the standards of politics and religion, and one of the guides of the Islamic scholarly renaissance in the late third and early fourth centuries, taking refuge in the matters of theology and philosophy. The great orientalist Paul Kraus said of him:

> كان من كبار دعاة الاسماعيلية ولعب دوراً هاماً في الشؤون السياسية في طبرستان والديلم ولا سيما في الري واصفهان حتى استجاب له كبار رجال الدولة مثل اسفار بن شيرويه ومرداويخ القائد وغيرهما

He was a leading preacher of the Ismailis and played an important part in political affairs in Tabaristan and Daylam, and especially in Rayy and Isfahan, until the chief men of the state responded to him, men such as Asfar ibn Shirawayh, Mardawij the commander, and others.

In truth, Abu Hatim al-Razi was one of the standards of the scholarly renaissance not within Ismailism alone but across the wider Islamic world. He helped to spread the philosophical teachings through all the eastern lands, and above all within the reach of the general Islamic culture. For all of this, he was not spared the persecution of his enemies in Daylam, and was driven into hiding in the last years of his life. He died in the year 322 of the Hijra, after the Fatimid al-Qaim had taken charge of the affairs of the Ismaili imamate in the Maghrib, having lived, as some historians say, sixty-two years.

He was a contemporary of Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi, the famous physician and holder of well-known philosophical views, in which he broke with many of Aristotle's theories, natural and metaphysical, denying any reconciliation between philosophy and religion and holding that philosophy is the only road to the reform of the individual and of society. Between the two men named al-Razi there were fierce and repeated disputations, attended by a number of scholars and political leaders. Abu Hatim set these debates down in his book *Alam al-Nubuwwa* (أعلام النبوة), and though he never names the man he is refuting, calling him only "the atheist," there is conclusive evidence that this atheist was none other than Abu Bakr al-Razi. The great philosopher al-Kirmani, Hujjat al-Iraqayn, has noted in his book *al-Aqwal al-Dhahabiyya* that debates over prophethood and the ritual duties of the Law took place between Abu Bakr al-Razi and Abu Hatim in the region of Rayy, in the days of Mardawij and in his presence, and that Abu Hatim was able to humble Abu Bakr and to cast off his views in *Alam al-Nubuwwa*, in pages that overflow with charm and wonder, closing off against the arrogant and the stubborn every means of evasion and escape.

The most important of his works is the book *al-Zina* (الزينة), on the Arabic letters, their derivations, the reasons behind their forms, and the meanings of words. It is a great book on the authentic and the borrowed Arabic vocabulary in which the Quran was articulated and which the Muslims gave force, and out of which the Islamic terminology took shape. This book runs to twelve hundred pages, and he dedicated it to the Fatimid imam who succeeded Muhammad al-Mahdi Billah (Ubayd Allah). To him belong also *Alam al-Nubuwwa*, *al-Islah* (الاصلاح), *al-Jami* (الجامع), and others.

### Al-Sijistani

Abu Yaqub Ishaq al-Sijistani, also called al-Sijzi, stands at the forefront of those scholars who struggled, labored, and gave themselves wholly to the setting down of cosmic philosophical principles resting upon doctrinal and intellectual foundations, upon solid scientific bases and firm pillars that do not tremble whatever crises fall upon them. Indeed he is among those who sacrificed all they possessed to spread that teaching and to circulate it through other lands, until late in his life he was accused of unbelief and atheism by the conservative public, and was at last killed for the sake of his faith. Al-Sijistani played an important part in the field of philosophy and rendered the noblest intellectual services in the third century of the Hijra. His intellectual mark appeared in his disciple Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani, Hujjat al-Iraqayn, who walked in his path, called to his teachings, and drank from the overflow of his springs. Once we recognize that al-Kirmani studied philosophy in al-Sijistani's school of thought, we may set al-Sijistani in the first rank among the Muslim scholars, the thinkers, and the famous philosophers of the world.

He lived alongside the esoteric Ismaili mission in the Age of Manifestation, that is, during the flourishing of the Fatimid state and its emergence as an Islamic state with a body of civilization, of learning, of society, and of politics. Yet he lived in a country whose people held to a doctrine other than his own, and so he was compelled to take *taqiyya* (التقية) as his cover, and to be most cautious in his movements and his preaching. For this reason his life has come down to us clouded with a certain obscurity, and we may not be blamed if we have not been able to reach a full knowledge of his biography or to lay hold of everything concerning it. It is much the same elsewhere: only a little has reached us of the great preacher al-Nasafi, al-Nakhshabi, the author of *al-Mahsul* (المحصول), beyond what the authors have mentioned of his efforts and of his contact with Nasr ibn Ahmad the Samanid in Transoxiana, until that Samanid embraced the Ismaili mission. We know little, too, of Abu Hatim al-Razi, the linguist and author of the oldest work in the science of the Arabic language, the book *al-Zina*, and little of Hujjat al-Iraqayn Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani himself, for all that most of his writings and works have survived down to us, and though al-Muayyad fil-Din Hibatallah al-Shirazi, the friend and disputant of Abu al-Ala al-Maarri, wrote his biography in his own hand. So we say of the obscurity that surrounds the lives of the senior men of the Ismaili mission, the hujjas and the preachers of the islands in Syria, Iran, Yemen, and elsewhere: their lives are wrapped in the deepest obscurity, and the books in which they recorded their own biographies have been lost, with only a little remaining of them.

He is traced to Sijistan, a province in the south of Khurasan, and to a Persian family; it is said that his was the family of the Persian hero Rustam (رستم), while others say he was of Iraqi origin, that his grandfather came from Kufa and settled in Sijistan. Some of the researchers who have studied his philosophy claim that he died in the year 331 of the Hijra, but this view does not agree with the historical reality, for it is known of al-Sijistani that he was the teacher of al-Kirmani, and al-Kirmani remained alive until the year 411. At what time, then, did al-Kirmani take from him the philosophical sciences of the mission? There is an explicit passage in al-Sijistani's own book *al-Iftikhar* (الافتخار) in which he states that he composed it in the year 360, and *al-Iftikhar* is in turn mentioned in al-Kirmani's *al-Riyad*, which means that al-Sijistani was a preacher in the province of Bukhara during the caliphate of the imam al-Muizz li-Din Allah al-Fatimi (المعز الدين الفاطمي). The sense of this is that he was a contemporary of the great preacher Jafar ibn Mansur al-Yamani (جعفر بن منصور اليمني), and of the learned jurist, the qadi al-Numan ibn Muhammad ibn Hayyun al-Maghribi al-Tamimi (النعمان بن محمد بن حيون المغربي التميمي), the chief judge of the Fatimid state, and of other great authors and scholars of the mission in that golden and radiant age of learning.

There is no better proof of al-Sijistani's scholarly worth than the books he left behind him. He left, as we have said, a rich body of scholarly writing that is reckoned among the deepest ever written in philosophy, numbering more than thirty titles. Perhaps the most famous of his books are *Ithbat al-Nubuwwat* (أثبات النبوات), *Kashf al-Mahjub*, *Tuhfat al-Mustajibin*, and *al-Yanabi* (الينابيع). This last he divided into forty springs, and it came to rank among the books whose depth is rarely surpassed. He seems to have composed it for a particular class of readers, the preachers and those of the highest ranks in the mission, and those whose philosophical studies had reached their summit. His reason for dividing it into forty springs goes back to his wish that each spring should carry the interpretation belonging to one of the forty limits who make up the supreme council of the mission.

Among his other books, which the great traveler and famous Persian poet Nasir-i Khusraw named in his *Zad al-Musafirin*, are these: *The Foundations of the Dawa*; *The Interpretation of the Laws*; *The Iris of Blessings*, also called *The Iris of Survival*; *The Brilliant Message*; *Kitab al-Iftikhar*; *The Scales*, which is divided into nineteen scales; *The Ladder of Salvation*; *al-Nusra* (النصرة), *The Victory*; *The Hooks*; *Amusements of Sorrows*; *Secrets of the Return*; *Sermons on Ethics*; *The Stranger on the Meaning of the Elixir*; *Sociable of Hearts*; *Composition of Spirits*; *Security from Confusion*; *Closets of Evidence*; *The Proof*; and others besides.

### Al-Sijistani on the divine

In these lines I set down in brief al-Sijistani's views on the divine, as he expressed them across most of his writings. He holds that the Originator of all originated things is an Ancient Creator, Exalted and firmly rooted in the bringing forth of primacy, and that the world of beings and originated things is itself brought into being, since, were it not so, something prior to it must have brought it into being. Were the world ancient and prior to the Creator, it would be impossible for His might to attach itself to that which is co-eternal, or for His being to rest upon nothingness, and it would then require another to bring it into being. He is exalted above the grasp of the attributes, so that He is not reached by any sense, does not fall under any gaze, is not perceived by sight, is not defined by any genus, does not enter into conjecture, and is not seen by the eyes. He is not described by the senses, nor grasped by analogy, nor likened to men. He is set apart from any contrary that would stand against Him, any equal that would match Him, any likeness of anything. He is exalted above the likeness of things that are bounded. The imaginations are bewildered before the character of His might, the understandings fall short of the description of His kingdom, and the eyes are dulled from grasping His greatness. He has no equal and no likeness; He is without rival and without opposite, for an opposite would be a contrary that stands against Him. He has pointed to His own identity through His creation and His works, and to His names through His prophets, so that the intellect has no field and no likeness by which to attain His heavens, since to place the created thing alongside its Originator is impossible. He is the cause of every existent, for He is the Originator of the originated and the Inventor of the invented, the cause of the being of all beings, the Lord of every thing, its Creator, its Perfecter, and the One who carries it to the best of its states. He is too exalted for any thought to bound Him or any measure to encompass Him. He has no names, for names were coined for the things He brought into being; and He has no attributes, for the attributes belong to essences; and the letters of language cannot arrive at the pronouncing of His name, nor be applied to Him in any part, since they are all among the things He invented. All the names He created He made names for the things He originated. He is Ancient, before pre-eternity, the Owner of the source of primacy in the order of being, for from Him the first limit burst forth and the first existent overflowed, while He remains the Originator of the originated, the Cause of causes, the Maker of created things, the Perpetual Existent by His own singleness and His self-sufficiency, the Owner of the first act of bringing into being. He is the first Number, which He made the origin of numbers, just as He made the Intellect the origin of existing things and the Articulator the origin of the world of religion. To all of this it is added that He is attained by none of the attributes, that He is not a body nor within a body, that no one who reasons reasons out His essence, and that no sense senses Him. He is not an image and not a matter, and there is nothing in the languages by which He may be expressed. He exists, yet it is not right to say that He exists after the manner of the existents that have come to be from Him; and the proof of Him is drawn out from the existence of the other existents, since there is no effect without a cause and nothing exists save by what makes its existence necessary. The existents lean one upon another in their being, and that on which some of them lean is itself, among the existents, not fixed in being and not existent of itself.

### The order of the existents

After this al-Sijistani passes to the existents in their order and their sequence, and says:

The Originator brought nothing into being at the first of creation except the Intellect, and He bounded within its essence the forms of all the originated things. To the Intellect is given the name of the Pen, because by the pen the inscriptions of creation appear from the very beginning. It is called the Throne, that is, a seat for the One who is seated upon it, and by His being seated upon it His majesty is known, above whoever is beneath Him. It is called the First, meaning that the primacy from which the created things appeared, that is, all that exists and all upon which a stamp is set, is the earliest to receive the marks of wisdom before the remaining limits, because of its nearness to that source and its union with it, which is knowledge and command taken in a single sense. It may therefore be said that the act of the Intellect precedes its power, and this excellence is found in no existent besides it, for all the limits below it have their powers preceding their acts; this excellence belongs to the Intellect in particular, that by it the Intellect may be whole and complete. The Intellect is called the Decree, in that by the Intellect the soul brings to pass the grasp of what is known and the winning of what is sought, and it may be said that the Intellect is the decree of God, mighty and majestic, among His creation. The Intellect is likewise called the *hayula*, prime matter, meaning that by the Intellect there is a subsisting of what wells up of the forms; and it is called the Sun, meaning that by the Intellect the realities are seen.

Then comes the Soul, which is the second creation, welling up out of the first. It is named "Soul" (نفس) only because it breathes (يتنفس) continually to draw benefit, so that the steady succession of its breathing is the subsisting of creation. The Soul is called the Tablet, meaning that what is loosed from the Intellect of the lights of wisdom is inscribed upon the Soul, and from the Soul it reaches, by its outflow, whatever pours forth from it, in the measure of its purity and refinement. The Soul is called the King, and the meaning of this is that the Soul is the property of the Intellect and its handmaid, for by the Soul the excellence of the Intellect appears, just as by the servant the excellence of the sovereign appears. The Soul is called the Follower, meaning that the one who follows the Intellect in the receiving of the marks of the Word is the Soul, and it may be said that the Soul, by its power, follows after the Intellect by its act. The Soul is called Destiny, meaning that what unites with the Soul of the benefits of the Intellect has measure and limit encompassing it. The Soul is called the Image, and the meaning of this is that the Soul was conceived out of the very essence of the Intellect and its radiance, and that whenever it resolves to overtake the Intellect and to descend into its station, its light is effaced, just as the moon draws its light from the light of the sun, and when the two meet in one station the moon's light is effaced. Of the Intellect and the Soul together it is said, in a single word, that they are the Two Principles (الاصلان).

These are some of al-Sijistani's philosophical views on the divine, which I have set out in brief and joined to what I have been able to recover of his biography. Perhaps they give the compelling proof of his firm command of the sciences and of his deep skill in laying out their texts in a pleasing order and an exquisite arrangement.

### Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani

He is an extraordinary scholarly figure whose life-history is enveloped in obscurity, a great philosopher who lived in a radiant scholarly age, a noble preacher who traced across the pages of thought the soundest inquiries and the deepest lines, and who left to the generations a number of works of which the least that can be said is that they are a precious treasure and an enduring heritage. The philosophers of the Islamic world reckon him the greatest scholar the Ismaili school produced.

## 3. The Judge and His Cosmos

The great preacher and Yemeni historian Idris Imad al-Din mentioned him in his book, saying:

"هو أساس الدعوة التي عليه عمادها وبه علا واستقام منارها وبه استبانت المشكلات وانفرجت المعضلات"

"He is the foundation of the dawa upon which it rests and by which it is raised up; through him its beacon was set straight, and by him problems were identified and dilemmas resolved." The Syrian Ismaili preacher Nur al-Din Ahmad described him in his book *Fusul wa Akhbar* (Chapters and Reports), saying:

"لو ان الدعوة الاسماعيلية لم تنتج غير الكرماني لكفاها فخراً ومجداً ولكان ذلك كافياً"

"If the Ismaili dawa had produced nothing but al-Kirmani, that alone would have been ample pride and glory, and that alone would have been enough."

His impact and stature appeared during the reign of the Fatimid Caliph al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah. His famous title was *Hujjat al-Iraqayn*, the Hujjah of the Two Iraqs, meaning that he had charge of the affairs of the dawa's cultural centers throughout Persia and Iraq. In Cairo his standing was that of a Hujjah, one of the twelve hujjahs charged with managing the affairs of the dawa, the intellectual vanguard of the world. He was later appointed head of *Dar al-Hikma*, the House of Wisdom, in Cairo, the cultural institution that we may rightly call the first university established in the world.

He travelled in a delegation to Cairo in 408 AH at the request of al-Sadiq al-Mamun, then the foremost of the Fatimid state's preachers in the era of al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah, at a moment when religious battles were heating up and new movements were flooding the market of innovation with calls aimed at exaggeration and at deviation from the realities and the foundations of the faith. He gave talks and lectures in *Dar al-Hikma*, and produced a great many researches and books, the most famous of which is *Risalat al-Waiza* (the Preacher's Message), composed in reply to al-Hasan al-Farghani, who held that the Ruler al-Hakim was divine by the command of God. Among his other works are the epistles known as *al-Mabasim* (the Good Tidings) and *al-Masabih* (the Lamps), in which he was able to prove the Imamate as a necessary cosmic reality by means of reasoned, compelling arguments, arguments richly furnished with Hebrew, Syriac, and Persian expressions drawn from the books of the prophets of the heavenly utterance. He then took up the Platonic opinions as the basis for his researches and deployed them with a skill no one before him had brought to bear. All of this served as a general call to consolidate the philosophical and intellectual system, to raise its level, and to erase every trace of doubt, controversy, suspicion, and dispute.

In these lines I will not attempt to introduce the philosopher al-Kirmani merely as one of the Ismaili preachers who played an important role in the field of thought during the reign of the Fatimid Caliph al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah, or merely as one of the philosophers who served the Fatimid dawa with clear intellectual services. I present him, rather, as a philosopher among the philosophers of the world, one who walked upon the stage of universal philosophy and exerted everything in him to found a philosophical school upon a new intellectual foundation, drawn from the modern, innovative theories of his time and from the mental systems then current. This applies in particular to the theory of the unity of being, which the Second Teacher, al-Farabi, and the Chief Sheikh, Ibn Sina, hold, here expanded by him in exposition and somewhat departing from the general curriculum laid down for the Ismaili preachers, whether those who came before him or those of his own age, who were obliged to follow a well-defined general framework whose limits they could not transgress or cross.

Returning to al-Kirmani's writings, and reading them with careful analysis of what I see in them, he has resolved that there is contradiction, disharmony, and an attempt on the part of some of the existents to erase others, yet that these existents persist in spite of this dissonance and this contradiction; for none of them is lost by the presence of an opposite, but rather they are all preserved under existence. All of this is identical to the theory of the Second Teacher on creation, in which he says:

"حفظ ادامة وجود الشيء الذي ليس وجوداً لذاته ادامة لا تتصل بشيء من العلل غير ذات المبدع"

"The preservation of the continuance of the existence of a thing that does not possess, in itself, a permanence unrelated to any of the contingents, other than the Creator." And Ibn Sina, in the course of speaking of the possible and the necessary among the other existents, gives an account that clarifies the meaning of the continuity of existence among existents, saying:

"أما كون العلل ممكن الوجود في نفسه واجب الوجود لغيره فليس يناقض كونه دائم الوجود بغيره"

"As for the fact that the contingents can exist in themselves, while not being obligated by the existence of others, this does not contradict their being always present in others." What these three philosophers, the Second Teacher, the Chief Sheikh, and the Hujjah of the Two Iraqs, go to in this regard nearly approaches what the philosopher Descartes said in the modern era:

"اذ يوجد من الفعل الذي يحفظ الله به العالم وبين الفعل الذي خلقه به"

"There is, between the act by which God preserves the world and the act by which He created it, no real difference." This is what is known in his philosophy as the theory of continuous creation.

In fact, al-Kirmani shared something with the Muslim philosophers, and was influenced by some of them, especially those who took up Greek philosophy. He is distinguished from them, however, by the fact that, when he presented these philosophies, he stamped them with the marks of novelty, wit, and innovation, and was the precursor of the modern European philosophers and the ancient Oriental renewers. Accordingly, I may say that al-Kirmani left behind books and produced ideas that should be looked upon not only as Ismaili intellectual views, but as Islamic philosophical views of a high, public-facing order, looking into the essence of things and into the deep intellectual aspects in a steady, elaborated manner in which genius and brilliance are manifest.

Yes, al-Kirmani spoke within the Neoplatonic systems, and exerted all his efforts to apply some of their materials in his attractive philosophical and rhetorical style; and he worked to prove the matter of Prophethood and the Imamate from both the philosophical and the religious sides. On this point I can say that he upheld the theory that adherence to the outward Shariah leads to scriptural worship, while the further reach of philosophical interpretation and discussion of the boundaries (al-hudud, الحدود) is reserved for those permitted ones who have attained a high level in philosophy and science. I find all these features when I look closely at *The Illuminating Message*, *The Lamps in Proving the Imamate*, *Alerting the Guide and the Guided*, and *Rest of the Mind* (*Rahat al-Aql*). Beyond this, al-Kirmani is a major asset in developing the intellectual system and expanding theoretical discourse, adding new elements, broadening certain topics, laying down the basic rules of interpretation, and treating contradiction and similarity, especially on the subject of the Imamate, which had been the focus of unstable and shifting opinions and theories. For this reason his book *Rest of the Mind* defined the rules, origins, ranks, and status of the Imamate, and set new foundations, systems, and order for it.

Al-Kirmani was prominent in the doctrine of the call to existence, in the negation of essence (al-aysiyya, الايسية) and substance (al-laysiyya, الليسية), in the doctrine that the divine attributes are subject to absolute negation, in the doctrine of the call to Tawhid, in the doctrine of the call concerning the two principles of origination, in the elaboration of emanation, and in the question of priority between the Imamate and the Prophethood, all in a purely scientific, logical manner. He did not stop there. At the outset he gathered the two first principles, the Active Intellect and the Universal Soul, together with the system of the ten Platonic intellects upheld by the Second Teacher, al-Farabi. He set the world of origination, the world of intellects, the spiritual world, and the corporeal celestial world, the world of stars and planets, alongside the physical world below the lunar orbit, and the world of religion, the world of the boundaries of religion; he then drew geographical and astronomical diagrams for it, together with terrestrial and corporeal plans that came out highly artistic and creative, showing his ingenuity of expression and his eminence in philosophy.

On returning to *Rest of the Mind* and reviewing what is stated in it, I see that it is full of formulations and arguments concerning the apostolic statements about God and the negation of the divine attributes. Among the positions he stated and supported: that no attribute attaches to Allah; that He is not a body nor in a body; that He neither rationalizes Himself nor senses Himself; that He is not in a form or in a substance; that He has no opposite and no likeness; that no language can express Him; and that He has no rank among existents. This indicates the breadth of his knowledge across the various sciences, especially the rational ones. On this the Mutazila and the theologians (mutakallimun) agree, and he agrees with Ibn Rushd. On the argument for the existence of God I see him agreeing with Descartes, even if they differ in the details and the method of expression. If I add a little further research, I see that he folded into his solutions and ideas some of the views of philosophers who preceded him and of his contemporaries, Plato, Aristotle, al-Kindi, al-Farabi, and Ibn Sina. I note from al-Baqillani, al-Baghdadi, and al-Ghazali that they ruled the Ismailis, by their denial of the attributes, to have fallen into invalidity (al-tatil, التعطيل). But al-Kirmani responded to this charge, saying that outright denial is to direct the particle of negation "لا" against the identity itself, as in saying "لا هو" (He is not) or "لا اله" (there is no god). This is not what the Ismailis say. Their negation is the negation of the attributes alone; the direction of the negative particle "لا" for them is toward the attributes, leaving the identity untouched.

However the matter stands, al-Kirmani gave special attention to this subject in his book *Rest of the Mind*. And in this book I see him departing from all philosophers, writers, historians, and scientists: instead of dividing his book into the usual books, chapters, and sections that other men of thought and authors employ, he likens his book to a city encircled by seven walls. Whoever enters it must pass through the seven walls, and to pass through them he must face seven gates (mashari, مشارع) branching off from each wall, except the seventh wall. As for the seventh and last wall encircling the city, it has fourteen gates. I will not enter here into an exposition of what al-Kirmani meant by his walls and gates, for fear of going on at length; but this does not stop me from affirming his superiority in philosophy, the power of his thinking, and the depth of his grasp of the essence of things.

Al-Kirmani published a number of works, the most important of which include: *The Divine Treatise*, *The Treatise of the Systems*, *The Treatise of Light*, *The Illuminating Treatise*, *The Necessary Treatise*, *The Guiding Treatise*, *The Preacher's Treatise*, *The Sufficient Treatise* (*al-Kafi*), *Alerting the Guide and the Guided*, *Wrists of the Guide* (*Maasim al-Huda*), *The Golden Sayings*, *Separating the Discourse and Distinguishing the Manifest Truth from Doubt*, *The Treatise of the Returner*, *The Treatise of the Index*, *Quantities and Facts*, *The Treatise on Tawhid in the Promised Land*, *The Crown of Intellects* (*Taj al-Uqul*), *The Balance of the Mind*, *The Book of Criticism and Obligation*, *Psychological Adequacy*, *The Book of Scales*, *The Baghdadi and Basran Sessions*, *al-Shaari's Treatise on the Elite*, *Rest of the Mind*, and *The Treatise of the Week of the Role of the Cover*.

Finally, I must say that al-Kirmani is among the philosophers who weigh heavily on our philosophical world; the study of his writings and his output is of great importance. It gives a clear picture of the impact of philosophy on the history of thought for those interested in Oriental Studies and Islamic Philosophy.

## 5. The Perfect and Perfection

This is the first chapter, from the book *al-Riyad*, concerning the ruling between the two disputants, in the chapter on what was discussed regarding the soul, which is the first emanation.

Section One

The author of *al-Islah* said: the soul is complete in itself, because it was emitted from the First Intellect complete. It is a complete emanation from the complete, because the Intellect is perfection.

The author of *al-Nusra* said, in refutation: he does not recognize that perfection is better and more complete than the perfect. For perfection is an attribute while the perfect is what is described; perfection is what is borne while the perfect is the bearer; and what is described, the bearer, is better and more complete than the attribute and the borne. For the perfect is described by perfection and bears it, while perfection is the attribute of the perfect, predicated upon it. This is what they say.

And we say: what the author of *al-Islah* means, in saying that the soul is complete because it is emitted from perfection, is not that perfection is an attribute or no attribute, or that the perfect is described or not described. Rather, what he means is that perfection is like the cause from which the existence of the effect comes to be. For he saw that perfection is the cause of the perfect, and precedes it in rank; for were it not for perfection, the perfect would not exist. The proof of this is that if perfection is lifted away in the imagination, the perfect is lifted away with its lifting, like the source-forms from which the past act, the future, the agent, and the object are derived: when these are lifted away, whatever is derived from them upon conjugation is lifted away with their lifting. So he treated the First Intellect, since it was first in existence and from it the existents came to be, as the cause, and said: it is perfection; and he treated what he called the emanating soul as the effect, and said: it is the perfect. And since this was the intention of the author of *al-Islah*, his statement that the First Intellect is perfection is true. And since it is true, what the author of *al-Nusra* said in this manner is an overreach against the author of *al-Islah*.

Section Two

Then the author of *al-Nusra*'s saying that the perfect is better than perfection, while the discourse here about perfection and the perfect is about the First Principle, which is the First Effect and the First Emanation, and not about any other, necessitates that the nobility belong to the effect and not to its cause. The proof of this is that the perfect is perfect only by perfection, without which it would not be perfect; and whatever a thing's being is by, that thing is its cause. And if that is so, and the perfect's existence is by perfection, then perfection is the cause of the perfect, and the perfect is the effect of perfection. And if the perfect is the effect of perfection, and he assigned nobility and merit to the perfect over perfection, then by that assignment he has necessitated that the effect be nobler than its cause, and that is impossible. For whatever exists by the existence of something such that, when that something is lifted away, its existence is lifted away with its lifting, that existence by which it exists is below it in nobility and precedence. And since that is impossible, the author of *al-Nusra* in what he brought forward was overreaching against the author of *al-Islah*.

Section Three

The author of *al-Nusra* said, secondly: perfection is an attribute and the perfect is what is described; perfection is what is borne and the perfect is the bearer. This as an indication of his claim that the perfect is nobler than perfection, and that the bearer is nobler than the borne.

We say: this is not true without qualification. For we find, from the side of the beings and the second emanation, what is borne yet nobler than its bearer. Such as the nutritive power, which is the proximate cause of the existence of the growth power and is its bearer, while the growth power is a form borne within it and its completion, and growth is nobler than it. And such as the growth power, which is the proximate cause of the existence of the sensitive power and is its bearer, while the sensitive is a form borne within it and its completion, and the sensitive is nobler than it. And such as the sensitive power, which is the proximate cause of the existence of the speaking power and is its bearer, while the speaking power is a form borne within it and its completion. And the nobility of this is not like the nobility of that.

Then, because it is impossible, from the side of origination and the first emanation, that the First Intellect be borne and the Second Intellect emanating from it be the bearer, or that the perfect, which is the First Intellect, be an attribute of the perfect, which is the Second Intellect. And since that is so, the author of *al-Nusra*'s inference in his refutation, by what he brought forward, was an overreach against the author of *al-Islah*; and the meanings of his statement in it would be true only if carried upon the natural things, which are far from what the author of *al-Islah* speaks of, namely the originative and emanative principles.

The statement of the author of *al-Islah* is correct with regard to the world of origination and emanation; and the statement of the author of *al-Nusra*, even though the author of *al-Islah* is mistaken about it, is correct with regard to the world of bodies and their accidents.

Section Four

And we say, in consideration of this: perfection is a name applying to the First Thing, which is the First Cause; and it is the First Perfect, which is the First Effect. Its perfection is by the addition of its existence to that from which its existence comes, and its perfection is by its addition to that upon which its essence rests, and that is as with origination and the Originator upon this order. Origination is not something other than the Originator; rather it is that by which the Originator is itself. And what is it? It is a single essence: when it is added to that from which its existence came, it is origination; and when it is added to that upon which its essence rests, of the mark of temporal occurrence, it is originated. So the First Intellect is perfection, and it is the perfect in truth. And it deserves to be perfect only because existence of its own kind is precluded. For the perfect thing is that outside which there is nothing like it in its species, as with the existents in natural things, let alone in origination. The body, too, is a name applying to a thing perfect in its three dimensions, so that outside it there is nothing like it; rather whatever is like it is from it, and counted as of it. And likewise the sun is perfect: there exists nothing outside it that is like it in its species.

In sum, the existence of what is below the First Intellect, in relation to it, is a deficient existence; except that that deficiency is not in its essence but in its rank, in relation to what is above it. For all things are perfect in their essences, since their existence comes from the perfection that is the First Intellect, and there is no deficiency in them. For were they deficient in their essences, their existence from perfection would be impossible, and it would likewise be impossible that from the deficient there should come to be something other than it, since the deficient is precluded from producing something that itself needs the completion of its own essence, let alone the existence of something other than it from it. So deficiency attaches to them only when they are added, by what is above them in rank, in relation to another, and not in their essence; because the essence, inasmuch as essences are of a single thing, is like the numbers, which are perfect in their essences and whose deficiency is only according to their addition to others by which their existence is; and like all the existents of the world, which are perfect, with no deficiency in them, inasmuch as they are bodies, and deficient inasmuch as their ranks in the forms, and differing when some forms are nobler than others, like the human being, who is the noblest of all the forms, and is in his essence perfect like the others, and deficient in relation to what is above him. And if the matter is thus in bodily things, then the way of what is not a body, upon this order in respect of ranking, is likewise, like for like.

And therefore Allah, exalted, said:

> سنريهم آياتنا في الآفاق وفي أنفسهم حتى يتبين لهم أنه الحق

"We shall show them Our signs in the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth." (the verse.) That is: We shall establish for them the Imams and the limits, in the manifest and the hidden, that they may be guided to what the state of the observed bodies is, and of the souls whose actions exist, upon what had been hidden from them of knowing the limits of Allah, which are the truth, so that the truth of the form may become clear to them.

Section Five

The author of *al-Islah* said: what is in need of perfection is the act of the soul, not the essence of the soul; because the soul's act is completed only in time, and the emanation of the soul is with time, just as the First Intellect and time are one, because the First Originator, and origination, and time, and perfection are one existent. The Creator originated all the originated things at once. And the soul, though it needs to derive benefit from the First, this is not a deficiency in its essence, just as the First was not deficient though it was in need of the command. Completion and deficiency appear in the act of the soul, not in its essence.

The author of *al-Nusra* said: he did not recognize that the Intellect does not stand in need of anything in the way the soul needs to derive benefit from the Intellect. The state of the Intellect and the matter is not like the state of the soul and the Intellect; for the matter, from the side of the Intellect, is nothing other than the Intellect, rather their identity is one identity. And it is impossible to say that a thing needs what it itself is; rather the need of a thing for a thing is only when it is other than it. This is what they say.

We say: the statement of the author of *al-Nusra* is a reply to what the author of *al-Islah* maintained, of the Intellect's need for the command only. And since the discourse was about the principles that come out of the natural world, the statement of the author of *al-Nusra* in that is closer to the truth than the statement of the author of *al-Islah*. For what is fit to be said to be in need of perfection, and to be completed only in time, because it is an emanation from the natural world and not from outside it, is the souls that attain the degree of perfection, that is, the ascent to the degree of the second perfection, only in time, and whose existence is an emanative existence. So if the statement of the author of *al-Islah* is carried upon this aspect, it is correct.

Section Six

And the statement of the author of *al-Islah*, that the Intellect and time are one, because the Originator, and origination, and time, and perfection are one existent, is not correct. For time is the number of the motions of the degrees of the celestial sphere; it is the imagined duration between the motion of the first degree of it and the motion of what follows it of the other degree, to where the first had been. And origination, which is the Intellect, and perfection, its existence precedes the sphere by the motion of whose degrees the existence of time comes to be. And to attach the existence of time to the existence of the First Intellect, and to hold that belief, contradicts what the law of the guiding Dawa rests upon. And we have explained, concerning the coming-to-be of time, and what precedes it in existence, and the indication of it from the ordinances of the prophetic Law, in our book known as راحة العقل (Peace of Mind), what suffices the seekers.

Section Seven

The author of *al-Nusra* said: were it not for the Intellect's denial of divinity of itself, the Following one would not have been able to carry its knowledge beyond the rank of the Intellect to the Originator. What deficiency is more evident than this, if it knows the Originator only by way of the First?

We say: that may not be believed of the Following one. For the power flowing in all the noble existents in the world of unity gives them the knowledge that they have an Originator; and were it not for that power, nothing would have existence or subsistence, and it would vanish away. For this reason every thing deserves the name of oneness; and though it be multiple in its essence, its share of that power is unseen, and the justice in it is evident. And it is impossible to conceive of a single one of the human kind that it does not know it has a Creator, or that it imagines it has no Creator, let alone of that great and majestic Limit. Rather we say: it apprehends that from which its existence comes, that it is the True Object of worship, even if there be in it a god. Then, if we apply to the First Intellect what he mentioned, and to the Following one what he said of it, it would be obligatory that the Asas, whose standing is in the world of body, stand in the place of the Following one in the world of Intellect, since, were it not for the Speaker, one would not know one has a god. But the matter is not so; for each of the two has a portion from Allah, and the Speaker precedes it in rank only by his nearness to the First and his coming after it. And therefore Allah said:

> لا الشمس ينبغي لها أن تدرك القمر ولا الليل سابق النهار وكل في فلك يسبحون

"It is not for the sun to overtake the moon, nor does the night outstrip the day; and each is swimming in an orbit." And all, from the side of movement, are one, and by ranks they differ.

Section Eight

The author of *al-Nusra* said: and enough for you, of the deficiency, is that all the ancients placed the soul in the station of the form, and the Intellect in the station of prime matter and material, and they said: forms ever yearn and long for materials, and materials have no subsistence without forms.

We say: his statement that the soul is a form is true; but his statement that the Intellect is in the station of prime matter and material, since the discourse was about the First Intellect, was an overreach against the author of *al-Islah*, addressing him with that. For "Intellect" is a shared name, and what he said is not correct concerning what was separate of the originative and first emanative intellects; rather it is correct concerning what is in the world of nature of the other intellects, and the intellect in potency, and the intellect in act. And since the discourse was about what is separate, then to carry what is said upon what is natural, and to contradict by it, was an overreach. And his statement that forms ever long and yearn for materials, and materials have no subsistence without forms, is an incorrect statement. For forms do not long for materials, since their existence is not for the sake of materials, nor are they what acquire materials; rather it is materials that yearn for forms, since their existence is for the sake of forms, and it is they that acquire forms; and matter is added in relation to the base form and the nobler form.

And since that is so, and he has run the soul in the course of the form, and the Intellect in the course of matter, and has ruled that materials have no subsistence without forms, it is as though he has necessitated that the existence of the Intellect be by the soul. And how can that belief hold concerning what its existence was an originative existence? Or how can it be conceived that the Intellect be the cause of the existence of the Intellect and the soul, while the discourse is about the separate that comes out of the world of nature? Never. For it is known that the Following one's existence is by the Preceding one, not the Preceding one's existence by the Following one; and that is a plain fallacy, arising only from his carrying it upon what is said about the natural things. As for what is in the world of origination and the first emanation, the matter of the existents from the abode of nature, of the Intellect and the soul, which are of the category of the second emanation, is only as he mentioned, and it is correct from this aspect.

## 6. Nothing Stands Beside the First

The author of al-Nusra said:

The Intellect is the first mean, and the Soul is the second mean; and the first mean is nearer to the Cause than the second mean. The nearness and rank of one thing to another can arise only from perfection and nobility, and remoteness can arise only from deficiency and want of nobility.

We say: this statement holds no true meaning for the originative and emanative first Intellects. For to be a mean requires two ends, and it is not admissible to say that the First Intellect is a mean, since that from which its existence comes is not in a state like its own, such that there would be an end for it on that side as its coming-to-be from that gave it an end, and because it stands, in existence, in a state like its own. How could one so much as imagine that the One which is the first of ones is a mean, when nothing precedes it of its own kind of existence, so that it might stand as a mean between that and the two? Even to suppose this, that the First Intellect is a mean, is impossible, because its existence is a first existence, and that whose existence is a first existence is not called a mean. The mean deserves to be called a mean only in that something of its own kind of existence precedes it, so that there is an affinity between the two, and something also comes after it, suited to it in some respects, so that it has an affinity with the one from one face and with the other from another face: these are its two ends, and it is a mean between them. But as for the First Intellect, there is no affinity at all between it and its Originator, exalted be His mention, nor is it of the kind of His existence, so that it should be a mean between Him and what is below it; nor is there any occasion for it to be a mean between itself and its Originator. As for that which is between it and its Originator, the cause by which it exists, that is other than it; and what is called the mediator between it and what is below it is the very essence of origination, the Cause that is said to be at once originating and originated, as we have shown in *The Illuminated Epistle* and in our well-known book *Peace of Mind*.

If that is so, there is no sense in the author of al-Nusra's inference from this in support of what he sought to defend, for what has already been said concerning deficiency and nobility is sufficient. Deficiency attaches to the Following only in relation to the First Intellect, not in its own essence; for its essence is upright, with no deficiency in it, and it possesses completeness, while in relation to what precedes it, it is of lesser rank. It is like the Speaker and the Foundation, the Sun and the Moon: each of the two, the Foundation and the Moon, is perfect in its own essence, with no deficiency in it; yet when set beside the other that is its counterpart form, the Speaker and the Sun, it is deficient in rank. The author of al-Nusra binds the Following to deficiency by relating it to what precedes it, while the author of al-Islah binds its essence to perfection, because it emanates from the Perfection that is its Cause. What this one says on his side is correct, and what that one says on his side is also true; except that the author of al-Nusra wrongs the author of al-Islah, taking him where he did not intend and where his purpose does not lie.

The author of al-Nusra said:

If he says, "I do not mean," that is, the author of al-Islah, "that the Intellect is like the Soul in the matter of perfection," we say to him: then it is either better than the Soul or more deficient than it. He will not claim that the Soul is superior to the Intellect; so it remains for him to say that the Soul is deficient.

We say: the author of al-Islah may well not say what the author of al-Nusra has undertaken to report on his behalf. Rather he says that all things are perfect in their own essences, and that none of them merits deficiency except in relation to what is of higher degree than it, so that there belongs to the higher a rank that does not belong to this thing set beside it. And once its connection is to that, the manner of the wrong done to him is plain.

The author of al-Nusra said:

Among the clearest proofs of the deficiency of the Soul and the perfection of the Intellect is the saying of God, exalted be He:

> ومن آياته الليل، والنهار، والشمس، والقمر

"And among His signs are the night, the day, the sun, and the moon."

The night and the day stand for the two Foundations, and the sun and the moon for the two Roots; the sun of them for the First, and the moon for the Following. There is no disagreement among the people of knowledge and wisdom that the moon is of less light than the sun, less in light and radiance, and that it draws its light from the sun, and that the sun bestows light upon it. This is what he says.

We say: it is clear from his own saying that the moon is of less light than the sun, less in light and radiance, and that it draws its light from the sun, and that its deficiency attaches to it only by relating it to the sun; but as for its essence, there is no deficiency in it, for were it deficient it would not have received the light from the sun. Since its essence is perfect, it stood ready to receive from the sun what it is able to receive. And so, since the discourse of the author of al-Islah is about the perfection of the essence, and the discourse of the author of al-Nusra is about its deficiency in its states and its accidents by which its completeness comes, there is a difference between the two statements; and the author of al-Islah is like one wronged, in that he is taken where his purpose in what he says does not lie, nor his intent.

The author of al-Nusra said:

Among what also indicates the deficiency of the Soul is that the wise set the Intellect in the station of the spirit to the Soul, and set the Soul in the station of the body to the Intellect; and they did this only because they knew of the deficiency of the Soul and the completeness of the Intellect, so they likened the Soul to the deficient thing and the Intellect to the complete thing.

We say: by that saying he has added a further clarification of what we have said, that the deficiency is not in its essence but in other things, its states, even though the meaning of his words holds true only of the beings and the second emanation. For the drawing of benefit belongs only to the souls that are in the world of nature, that they may reach their second completeness; in their own essence they are complete and perfect, save that they are deficient in what they do not yet possess of the intelligibles, which run to them as the spirit runs to the body. But the second, which is the first emanation, its way is not the way of these souls, for its completeness and its perfection were given at the beginning of its existence, not through time and the drawing of benefit, but only through a deficiency of rank below what precedes it, and through precedence and posteriority.

The author of al-Nusra said:

Were there no deficiency in the Soul but that nature lies upon its horizon, still the Soul has two ends: one end toward the Intellect and one end toward nature. The Intellect is not so, its two ends being one toward the Word and the other toward the Soul, and nothing of it clings to dark nature, because of its perfection and its completeness. Yet were an end of the Soul to cling to nature, because nature lies upon its horizon, in that there would be, for whoever contemplates it, that upon which the observer would come to stand upon the truth of the deficiency of the Soul and the perfection of the Intellect. This is what he says.

We say: this statement proceeds without order. For the lying of nature upon the horizon of the Following, which he named the Soul, does not earn it any deficiency; rather it indicates a perfection belonging to it, for nature moves toward it because nature is deficient in relation to it, while it, in relation to nature, is noble and perfect, singled out by what nature is not singled out by, together with the essential reality of both, I mean the essence of the Following and the essence of nature, both perfect, inasmuch as they emanate from Perfection. As for his saying that the Intellect has two ends, one toward the Word and the other toward the Soul: the Intellect is not a thing other than the Word, nor is the Word a thing other than the Intellect; rather the essence is one and the entity is one, and it is by relation that it merits the names of scattered meanings. What has already been said on this sense, when the applying to it of the name "mean" was mentioned, is not hidden. And as for his saying that nothing of it clings to dark nature, because of its perfection and its completeness, while an end of the Soul has clung to nature because nature lies upon its horizon: it is not the Following that has clung to nature and loved it and desired it; rather it is nature that has loved it, and it is nature that moves toward it and seeks its union. To hold that, and to picture that an end of the Following has clung to nature, is an error. The best of the ways to turn the meaning of his words toward what is right is that his purpose in saying it was that the Following pours forth upon nature, so that he has called its outpouring a clinging.

The author of al-Islah said:

The deficient is the act of the Soul, not the essence of the Soul; and it is its act that is perfected, not its essence.

The author of al-Nusra said:

This statement is self-contradictory. For if the act is deficient, that cannot but be either from a deficiency in the essence of the agent, or from a deficiency in the substance out of which the agent acts, or from a deficiency in both. If the deficiency of the act is from a deficiency in the essence of the agent, then he has affirmed the deficiency of the Soul, since it is the agent. If it is from a deficiency in the substance, then he has affirmed an eternal substance, and this is a departure from the doctrine of religion and an inclination to the doctrine of Abu Zakariyya al-Razi and of those who hold the pre-eternity of matter; and it has already been suspected of the author of this book that he inclined to that, because he made time pre-eternal along with the Intellect, which is the pith of his doctrine. And if the deficiency of the act is from a deficiency in both, then he has held both sayings at once, affirming the deficiency of the Soul, since it is the agent, and affirming the pre-eternity of matter. Whichever he says, he has affirmed the deficiency of the Soul. This is what he says.

We say: the saying of the author of al-Islah, and the setting forth of his purpose in what he said of deficiency and perfection, has already been given. As for the saying of the author of al-Nusra in what he said, it is sophistry, wrong done, and a departure from the bound of knowledge. For he said first, after dividing the matter of deficiency into the three parts mentioned, that if the deficiency of the act is from the essence of the agent, and the agent is the Soul, then the Soul is deficient; yet the author of al-Islah has made plain that deficiency does not attach to the essence of the Soul, which is the Following, but to its act, in the sense of the thing acted upon, and so he is freed of this division. He said secondly that if the deficiency is from the essence of the substance, then he has affirmed an eternal substance, since Perfection and the Perfect, which are the First and the Second, have affirmed of them an eternity that is not a first eternity; so how much more the deficient? If that is so, then deficiency is only in the substances that receive their perfection, not in that from which perfection is received; even as it is well known in natural science that deficiency arises from the side of the recipients, not from the side of that which is received from. It is as with the heat of the sun, which bodies whose nature is quick to be affected receive, like wax, which is softened by it, while other bodies refuse to receive it, like stone and iron and the like, which are not quickly affected: the source from which it is drawn, the heat, is one, and the deficiency is in the others. And he said thirdly that he inclined to the doctrine of Abu Zakariyya in the manner he set out; and that is a departure from the bound of knowledge and a transgression. So he did him no justice, neither in the framing of the division, nor in what follows it, when he charged him with holding both sayings, deficiency and the pre-eternity of matter.

The author of al-Nusra said:

As for his pleading that the Soul became perfect only because it emanated from the First Intellect, which is Perfection, and that what emanates from Perfection cannot but be perfect, this is not right. For it does not follow that the Soul must be perfect because it emanates from the perfect Intellect; and were that necessary, then what emanates from the Soul would also be perfect, namely matter and form, and what emanates from matter and form would be perfect as well, on and on until it reached the single, indivisible individuals, so that all of them would be perfect. It follows from this saying that the whole world is perfect, with no deficiency in it, nor in anything of it.

We say: the gist of his saying is that if the Soul were perfect by its being emanated from the First Intellect, which is Perfection, then matter and form, by their being emanated from the Soul, would be perfect as well, and likewise what is emanated from all of matter and form, down to the individuals as he supposes, and the whole of what is in the world would be perfect, with no deficiency in it. We have already shown what the author of al-Islah aimed at in saying that the Soul is perfect, and the saying of the author of al-Nusra in this chapter is not thoroughly examined. For matter is not emanated from the Soul, nor are the individuals, as he supposed, indivisible; rather all the individuals are divided into their parts, and matter is emanated together with the Soul from that which precedes it in existence, not from it. The proof is that the Soul, which is the Following, its existence is not a first existence, so there is not found with it a like of its own kind of existence, nor does another rise above it in rank, as with the First, which is the First Intellect; rather its existence is a second existence. And that whose existence is a second existence, its existence is not except that there is with it an existence of its own kind, such that it and that one share in the kind. For that from which this Soul, which is the Following, came to be is the First Thing, which nothing of its own kind of existence precedes, and which is one in its existence. This was only for the sake of the transcendence of God, exalted be He, who brought it into existence free of relations and additions and multiplicity and all that by which a thing is described in its kinds, which they have found, I mean the relations and the additions, to be, in the active causes, a cause for that which comes to be from them being more than one; since the existence of multiplicity in the things caused is according to what their causes and grounds possess of relations. So, since God, exalted be He, is too prior for another to precede Him, or for another to exist with Him, or for Him to be diversified by attributes and relations, and since He, exalted be He, is the Existentiator, the Originator, the Cause that has no cause, it was impossible, with His purity and His holiness and His exaltation above multiplicity, that there should come to be from Him except one. And this one, which is the First Intellect, since it stood in two relations, one of them its being a cause for the existence of what is below it, and the other its being an effect, since it is originated and brought into being, it was necessary that what comes to be from it should be two, according to the two relations in which its essence stands. And that which came to be from the first relation is the second Intellect, and from the other relation the matter of which the spheres are made, and the rest of the existents of the world of body. Then, when the existent passes beyond the one into coming-to-be, it is only two, since there is not

For after the rank of the one in existence there is nothing except two, and after the individual there is nothing except the pair, which is plurality. If the first thing in the number of existents is one, then what follows it, when the intellect conceives it, is two, which is the pair, that is, the multitude. And if it is two, then one of the two is what we call the Soul, and the other is what we call al-hayula, the matter from which come the spheres, the stars, and all that lies beneath them.

### The Sixteenth Section of the First Part

Now the foundation of religion and its ordinances rests upon the balancing of the existents and their correspondences, so that their arrangement may point to the existents among the higher limits that are hidden from sight. The Book and the Sharia exist from the side of the Speaker, and the establishment of the Foundation stands as their interpreter, likewise from the side of the Speaker. This is the clearest indication that what proceeds from the First Intellect, which in its own world is like the Speaker in the world of body, is the Follower, and that Follower is the Soul and al-hayula together, just as the Book and the Foundation are the two that exist from the side of the Speaker.

Witness to this is what the Prophet did when he joined the Book and the 'Itra, whose existence is from him, when he said:

> إلا أني تارك فيكم الثقلين كتاب الله وعترتي ، حبل ممدود من السماء الى الأرض ، طرف منه بيد الله وطرف منه بايديكم، فتمسكوا بها لن تضلوا ما ان تمسكتم بهما، وقد سألت ربي ان يردا عليّ الحوض كهاتين

"Indeed, I leave among you the two weighty things: the Book of God and my 'Itra, a rope stretched from heaven to earth, one end of it in the hand of God and one end of it in your hands. Hold fast to them, and you will never go astray so long as you hold to them. I have asked my Lord that the two return to me at the basin like these two."

And he joined the two forefingers of both his hands together and said:

> ولا اقول كهاتين

"And I do not say like these two," pointing with the forefinger and the middle finger of a single hand, one of them going before the other, and saying: whoever helps them helps me, and whoever forsakes them forsakes me.

His likening of the two, in their existence, to his two forefingers is that neither of them precedes the other. So he set himself, in the abode of body, in the course of the First Intellect in the abode of origination. And he set what he brought of the Book, and the one he coupled with it from his 'Itra, who is the Foundation, together with the rest of the Imams (عليهم السلام), in the course of what proceeds from the First Intellect as the Second and as al-hayula, according to what we mentioned in the book Rahat al-Aql. So al-hayula does not manifest its nobility, nor the beautiful form that lies within its potency, except by the power of the Soul, which is the Follower, and by the shining of the light within the two of them. Just as the Book and the Sharia do not proclaim themselves, nor do they disclose the wonders of wisdom that lie within them and beneath them, except by the interpretation of the Foundation and the one who stands in his place. Thus al-hayula, in its existence, is from the First Intellect together with the Second.

### The Seventeenth Section of the First Part

Then we say: We said that the First Intellect has nothing of the kind of its own existence existing along with it, because its existence, whereby it exists, is only a first existence, and the first cannot be more than one. Were it otherwise, the paths of professing divine unity would break down. For if it had a like in its existence, then the existence of the two of them, were they together, would be caused, since their existence would not proceed from itself; and this would require something prior to the two, from which their existence comes, and that is forbidden and absurd.

For the existence of twoness is a being multiplied in essences, and multiplicity in essences does not derive its existence from a multiplicity in essences; rather its existence is from what precedes it, from that which is one in essence from one aspect and multiple in meanings from another. And that which is one in essence yet multiple in meanings has its existence not through itself but through another which is not multiplied, neither in meanings nor in essence.

Thus it has become clear that if the First Intellect had along with it a like of the kind of its existence, the two of them would be originated from a one that precedes them; then the existence of that one would be by its being multiple in meanings, not through itself, but through what precedes it and renders it one. And since there is nothing above the Intellect, it is void that it should have a like of the kind of its existence. So it is established that it is one. And in the establishment that the Intellect is one lies the fact that what proceeds from it is two, namely the Soul and what is called al-hayula. And since the existence of the Soul and al-hayula is together, according to what we have shown, it becomes plain that the statement that al-hayula appears from the Soul is an error.

### The Eighteenth Section of the First Part

Now as for what the author of al-Nusra committed himself to, in his statement concerning the deficiency of al-hayula and the Image, and the deficiency of the world and all that is in it, we say:

al-hayula and the Image are both complete, and the world with all it contains is perfect and complete, and its parts in themselves are complete and perfect. The proof is that the complete is that outside of which there exists nothing of the kind of its own existence. The world is the sum of al-hayula and the Image, together with all the parts of body; and body in itself is complete in its three dimensions, so that nothing which its definition entails is found outside it, but rather within it. Therefore it is complete. And if it is complete, then the author of al-Nusra's denial that the world should be complete and perfect is among the impossible; and since it is impossible, his speech is only a forced overreach.

It is enough, as an indication of the perfection of the world, what he himself said in his book known as al-Iftikhar, in the chapter of the Command, where he said:

> وصورة استغناء العالم بكليته عن غير سواه عدم الغير ، واكتفاء الحكمة بما في كلية العالم من الغنية والكمال

"The form of the world's self-sufficiency in its totality, needing nothing other than itself, is the nonexistence of any other, and wisdom's contentment with the richness and perfection that lie in the totality of the world."

I do not know how this escaped him; perhaps he composed the book al-Iftikhar after the book al-Nusra. As for the disparity found among the parts of the world, on account of which the author of al-Nusra charges it with deficiency, that is only by the addition of some of them to others in respect of their forms and states, which is the first perfection that exists. For some parts of the world are given their first perfection, and their second, at the outset; and some are given their second perfection only at the last, by acquisition. So the discourse has made plain that the deficiency of which the author of al-Nusra speaks lies only in the states that supervene upon the natural essences, which appear when some of them are added to others; while the complete of which the author of al-Islah speaks is only the essences whose existence is a first existence.

### The Nineteenth Section of the First Part

The author of al-Nusra said: If, because the Soul emanates from the Intellect, which is the Complete, the Soul must have stature, then likewise, because the Soul emanates from the Intellect, which is the first intermediary and the first originator, the Soul must be an intermediary like the First, and an originator, such that the Soul attains of the virtues what the Intellect attains, and likewise the whole world and its individuals. This is his statement.

We say: The demonstration that it is impossible to call the Intellect the first intermediary has already been advanced, and in sum what he has brought forward is a corrupt proposition. For when a thing's existence proceeds from the requirement of another thing, in the sense that one is a cause and the other an effect, it does not follow that the effect shares with the cause in all its states, such that its very identity would be the cause's identity and its reality the cause's reality; for then nothing could be said of it except what is said of that same identity, and it would no longer be a distinct existence, nor could it be said to proceed from the cause by being that very cause. Yet, though it need not share with the cause in all its states, it must share with it in some of its states; for if it shared with it in none of its states, it would not be an effect of it, and the cause would not have given it existence.

And since the way of the existence of things in this multiplicity is so, it must share with its cause in one respect and differ from it in another. And when this is necessary, it follows that the Follower, called Soul, which emanates from the First Intellect that is the cause of its existence, must be like it in one respect and unlike it in another. And since it must be like it in one respect and not like it in another, it becomes plain that it is not necessary, when the existence of the Follower called Soul is from the Intellect, that its being should be like the Intellect in all its states; nor does it follow that what is said of the Intellect, in respect of its being the cause, must be said of the Follower called Soul. This refutes what the author of al-Nusra said: that if it were necessary, it would be necessary.

### The Twentieth Section of the First Part

The author of al-Nusra said: This has many parallels in the sensible world, such as thought and the partial soul. For the partial soul that is in us is in the position of the Intellect, and thought is in the position of the Soul. Yet thought, even though it emanates from the soul, may not be said to be like the soul, because thought perceives what it perceives part by part, and time after time, whereas the soul perceives the whole of what it perceives without time. This is his statement.

We say: The soul, I mean the soul of the human being, has acts, and each of its acts has a name proper to it. When it seeks to grasp some thing, that seeking is called thought (al-fikr). When it regards the forms of the known things that have come to be within its essence, that regarding is called retention (al-hifz). When it imprints its essence with the marks it captures by way of inference or by way of the senses, that imprint is called conception (al-tasawwur). And when it knows that outside itself there are things it wishes to conceive, that will is called striving (al-nuzu) toward them.

Since it has acts, and thought is an act of it that does not pass beyond its own essence so as to have an existence other than its existence, how can the likening of thought be sound to the Follower called Soul, which has an existence other than the existence of the Intellect? And how can it be said that thought perceives part by part, when perception belongs to nothing but the essence of the soul through its act which is called thought within itself? And how can it be sound that the perception of the soul, I mean the soul of the human being, should be of the whole without time, when it has no way to hunt for knowledge except by way of the senses and inference, passing from their particulars to their universals in time?

Then it is not the case that, because thought may not be said to be like the soul, it necessarily follows that the Second Intellect, which is called the Universal Soul, is not like the First Intellect. For thought from the souls of human beings does not run in the course of the Second Intellect from the First Intellect. The Second Intellect is a second existence from origination, subsisting through itself, even though in existence it is one rank below the First. But thought has no existence separate from the soul; rather it is the very identity of the soul, and has no subsistence through itself. For every act among the acts of the soul has a cause on account of which it needs to act, namely its being in the world of nature; and when the soul departs from that world, these acts are annulled by the annulment of their cause, and nothing remains subsisting except the essence conceived as pure Intellect.

Then he made the Second Intellect, called the Soul, proceed from the First Intellect in the course of thought from the soul; and since the existence of thought is no existence other than the existence of the soul, which he likened to the Intellect, and since thought has no perception of the soul through its own essence, it is as though he had denied that the Second Intellect, called the Soul, has any existence other than the existence of the First Intellect, and any perception like the perception of the First. And that is impossible; for the Follower has an existence other than the existence of the First, and a perception like the perception of the First. It does not deserve to be called an originator, just as the First Existent does not deserve to be called emanated. Both are pure intellects; each has encompassed its own essence and preserved its essence by its essence; and they are alike in one respect and unlike in another, according to what the discourse upon this has already set down.

And since this is so, there is no sense in likening thought to what he likened it to, nor any sense in making the human soul stand in the position of the First Intellect, nor thought within it stand in the position of the Universal Soul. For the nobility of the human soul and its passage from potency to act lie only in acquisition, and acquisition comes only from the senses as deed and from conception as knowledge. And since the nobility of souls lies in acquisition, part of which is by thought and inference as conception, and since thought belongs to the souls only that they may be ennobled by it, it is as though he had made the cause of the nobility of the First Intellect to be the Second Intellect, called the Soul. And that is an inverted proposition. For the nobility of the Follower and its rank come from that which precedes it in existence, which is the Antecedent, because the nobility of the Antecedent and its rank come from what comes after it. And the ruling upon that whose existence is a second existence, in perfection and completeness, is that it be the reverse of that whose existence is a first existence. So neither does the one run in the course of the other in this, nor the other in the course of the one, even though they share from another aspect. Yet the author of al-Nusra has passed judgment upon that whose existence is a first existence by what belongs to that whose existence is a second existence. And that is impossible.

## 7. The Soul That Forgets

Chapter Twenty-One of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said:

One of the clearest signs of the deficiency of the Soul and the perfection of the First Intellect is that the First, and the author of al-Islah, all said that the act belongs to the Second, and they attributed nothing to the First; for action is always a sign of deficiency and need, while the abandonment of action is evidence of perfection and self-sufficiency. That is to say, action is only for the sake of drawing a benefit or repelling a harm.

Abu Zakariyya said:

The Initiator, by way of manipulation and tampering, is already the Soul, and the people of religion do not hold this. So there remains nothing but the drawing of a benefit or the repelling of a harm; and wherever this is found, there is deficiency. The Soul, in this, seeks to draw the benefit, which is its reaching the end destined for it, and so it begins with action. The Intellect, having no rank above it, is not deficient and does not move to action in any way; but the Soul, having found a station above it, remained in motion, abiding in its quest. By this method we say what we say concerning the deficiency of the Soul and the integrity of the Intellect. This statement is clear, and praise be to God.

The sum of his saying in this chapter is that the Second is the doer, and that action is only for drawing a benefit or repelling a harm; that the drawing of benefit and the repelling of harm belong only to deficiency; that the Intellect, not being deficient, is still from action, its stillness being that there is no rank above it for it to seek; and that the Soul, having a rank above it, moved toward it to attain it.

We say: Had the words of the author of al-Islah been about the Soul that is the soul of human beings, the saying of the author of al-Nusra would have been the more fitting expression. But since his words were about the Following that is named "the Soul," the saying of the author of al-Nusra, and his inference from the condition found in human souls, that they act only to draw a benefit, to repel a harm, or to tamper, to invalidate his statement and to overturn his ruling that the Following is complete and perfect, is a manifest prejudice. For it has already been said in sum that what is affirmed of that whose existence is a first existence, in respect of completeness and perfection, is not affirmed of that whose existence is a second existence, nor is it affirmed of that whose existence was a first existence.

The statement of the author of al-Nusra concerning the act and its divisions is not correct, for not every act is for drawing a benefit, or repelling a harm, or futility. An act may proceed from an agent such that its benefit returns to the object of the act, not to the one from whom the act issues. The proof of this is that heating is an act of the fire, yet this act does not return to the fire, and neither benefit nor harm accrues to it; rather the benefit and the harm fall upon whatever the heating, which is its act, reaches. So too the act of the sun in illuminating the world, and the enabling of human beings to dispose of their affairs by their own will through it, and its rising, and the coming to be of plants and animals and minerals through its heat: its benefits return to others, not to itself; it neither benefits from this act nor is harmed by it. So too the act of the whales that are in the Sea of Oman, and others called "the dolphin" (الدلفين), whose nature is such that, when a drowning man is drowning in the sea, they aid him to escape and push him toward the shore; yet that act does not return to them with benefit or harm; rather the benefit reaches the object of the act.

If this is so, and it is found among natural things that do not reason, even though they are deficient in their states and in need of others in their action, then there is nothing to deny in the pure, abstract, complete intellects, which were granted their perfections at the very first of their existence, that they should perform acts greater than these acts which appear from the natural things, acts that their perfections require of them, according to the nearness of their ranks and the loftiness of their noble degrees; noble acts, not for the sake of drawing a benefit to themselves, nor of repelling a harm from themselves, but for the sake of others. So these are acts of wisdom, not acts of drawing benefit, nor of repelling harm, nor of futility. Nay, the perfect power from which the perfect, well ordered act proceeds exists only in the perfect.

Moreover, the Following, which he named "the Soul," is not in motion as he claimed, so that what befalls the natural things would befall it; for that motion belongs only to bodies, with their bodies and their natural forms. The Following is higher in rank than the natural things, though it is below the First in rank, as we have made clear in the book Rahat al-Aql. By what we have brought forward, his prejudice and his argument, which do not hold concerning the one spoken of, have become clear, and the statement of the author of al-Islah, that the Following is complete in completeness, stands, as we explained earlier. It is not permissible to predicate motion and rest of what lies in the abode of origination, namely the First and Second Intellects and the upper letters; for we have shown that these two belong only to what is a body or possessed of a body. And in this there is sufficiency.

Chapter Twenty-Two of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said:

The upshot of the statement of the author of al-Islah is that the act of the Soul is perfected, not the essence of the Soul, even though the Soul with him is complete; for on this view the act of the Soul would become a soul, and this is impossible with a manifest impossibility, since acts that are never seen, in what is observed, to become agents. An example of this is the writer and the writing, and the builder and the building: the writing does not become a writer, nor does the building become a builder; rather it is the man, who is a writer in potency and a builder in potency, who becomes a writer and a builder in actuality. Likewise we say of the Soul and the Intellect: the Intellect is complete in actuality, and the Soul is deficient in actuality, subsisting in potency, and it comes to subsist in actuality when it gains completeness. But that its act should become complete is impossible.

We say:

The aim of the author of al-Islah in his saying that the act of the Soul is perfected, and not the essence of the Soul, is this: it is that which attains the second perfection from the Soul's side, that which proceeds from it in the manner of what it pours forth upon, so that it issues from potency to the measure of the act. And since the aim of his saying, that the act of the Soul is perfected, is what issues by the Soul's overflowing from potency into act and attains the second perfection, and since nothing issues from the limit of potency into act except by what subsists in actuality, then it is not to be denied that, when what subsisted in potency issues into act and attains the second perfection by acquisition, it should be like that which subsists in actuality in what it was granted of perfection at the first of its existence. So they share in that they both subsist in actuality, even though each of them attained what they share not from the side from which the other attained it. It is not impossible that they be two likes in respect of their sharing; for it is known that everything gathered with another thing in some meaning of the meanings is, in that meaning, its like. The witness to this is what our saying yields: everything that goes out from the limit of potency to the limit of act subsists in actuality; and everything subsisting in actuality subsists by the occurrence of the second perfection to it; and everything that goes out from the limit of potency to act has the second perfection occur to it. So if the subsistence of what subsists in actuality is by the occurrence of the second perfection to it, and the subsistence of what subsisted in potency and then came to subsist in actuality is likewise by the occurrence of the second perfection to it, then they share and are alike in that they both attained the second perfection, even though from another side they differ. And since what we have brought forward is neither impossible nor to be denied, but rather necessary, the statement of the author of al-Nusra is nothing but prejudice.

Chapter Twenty-Three of Part One

Then the author of al-Nusra said:

This is impossible with a manifest impossibility, for acts have never once been seen, in what is observed, to become agents, according to what he adduced concerning the matter of the writer and the building, by a proof that testifies to the impossibility of what he claimed to be impossible.

We say:

Acts are, in truth, the agents. For the writing, which he mentioned to be the act of the writer, is the very thing that converses with its reader and addresses him. Were the writing, in its forms and its tracing, not, together with its being an act belonging to another, an agent upon the very soul of its reader, as though it spoke on behalf of its author and translated him and imprinted him in its forms, its reader would not know what is in the soul of its writer. And the building, in the form which the builder brought into being out of what was in his soul, is what conveys to the observer its form by its arrangement and its disposition, so that he conceives it. Were the form of the building not subsisting in actuality, so as to impress upon the soul of the observer, which possesses its forms, its observer would not conceive it. Likewise the act of the striker, which is the striking, is the agent that produces the pain in the one struck, so that it pains him, in the manner we mentioned in our two well known epistles, al-Rawdah (الروضة) and al-Mudiah (المضيئة). And the saying of God, exalted is He, is the firmest proof that acts are agents as well, where He says, exalted is the Speaker:

Then the aim of the author of al-Nusra in his saying, that we have never seen acts in what is observed become agents at all, is that the very acts have not become the very same as their agent in what is observed, just as the writing has not become the very same as its writer, nor has the building become the very same as its builder. But the author of al-Nusra wished to defend the one he represents by prejudice against the author of al-Islah, may God sanctify their souls; and many associates wrong one another.

Chapter Twenty-Four of Part One

The twenty-ninth chapter of Part One. The Soul That Forgets

Whatever exists under such conditions is itself the evidence of its deficiency, and so the objection that the author of al-Nusra (النصرة) raises against the author of al-Islah (الاصلاح) is an impossible objection.

Chapter Thirty of Part One

He said: The Follower is deficient because it forgets its world and does not remember it.

But how can this be valid? Forgetting does not occur except after knowledge. If these souls had existed beforehand while they were knowing, it would have been impossible for alteration to overtake them by their descent into the abode of nature after they had dwelt in majesty and attained the perfection that is intellectual knowledge. For the existence of what exists in the world of origination and emanation, among the intellects, is an existence resembling what is necessary and fixed; it is not overtaken by any alteration of what it was, and for that reason it is called eternal. And how should the conditions overtake the eternal, so that it possesses knowledge at one time and is stripped of it at another? The abode in which it resides is not the abode of alteration that he has asserted for it by his word "نسيّ," that it forgot.

In sum I say: if these souls were bestowed upon these persons from that world, and came in order to learn and to be trained in the sciences, then their state, the ignorance in which they stand at the very outset of their existence here, cannot but be either in their essence or by an accident. If they are ignorant in their essence, then they are barred from knowing anything at all, and ignorance is intrinsic to them, which is impossible, since we find the soul coming to know after not knowing. And if the ignorance is by an accident, that accident is not free from being either in that world or in this one. If it befell them in that world, and that world is the abode of eternity and completeness, not the abode of alteration and deficiency, then it is impossible that an accident should befall them by which they are altered. And if it befell them in this world, and their end is to come to know and to return to the majesty they were in at first, before alteration overtook them, then that too is impossible in wisdom, since their remaining in the first state is more fitting for them than that they be altered from it and then return to it. It is void, then, in every case, to hold that they receive an accident, whether ignorance or knowledge, in this world, given that their existence is from another source. For if a thing's existence is not from this lower world, then for it to receive such an accident is impossible, since it is by completeness that it is what it is, and what is derived from it is the completeness that is the blessing of that world, "لأهل العلم," for the people of knowledge, not of this world. And when that is so, and it is impossible that their ignorance be in their essence or that an accident from that world befall them, then it is void that their arrival was from that world; and once it is void that their arrival was from that world, it is void to say that they forgot their world.

Chapter Thirty-One of Part One

Then, if its arrival is from the world of origination, its existence is a first existence, on the side of origination and emanation from the most excellent rank. And whatever has this manner of existence stands in actuality, and whatever stands in actuality is barred from receiving any accident. For the reception of accidents belongs to the way in which things that stand in potentiality are divided, and, in sum, to whatever hovers between potentiality and actuality in the abode of the body. But that whose existence is in the abode of origination has been granted its perfection and completeness at the very beginning of its existence; it is eternal, and it stands in that state. It is therefore impossible that it should receive any accident, or that anything should overtake it by which it is altered from its existence. And when that is so, then on the view of the author of al-Nusra, that it forgot, forgetting would be an impossible accident for it; so it is void to say of it that it forgot its world.

Chapter Thirty-Two of Part One

Then I say: Forgetting befalls only those souls that are prepared, from the world of nature, to receive knowledge, by the entry upon them of accidents from the causes to which their existence is owed. States then overtake them belonging to that which they follow in their coming to be. These are the richest of souls, when they are trained by the rites of the Law, and when the things prior to them in existence, up to the First Principle, have been grasped by the intellect. We call this an intellect and a second emanation, just as the Follower is a first emanation, according to what we have explained in our well-known book (براحة العقل), Peace of Mind. It is impossible that these souls should come from the world of the intellect, for which there is no evidence whatever. And what the author of al-Nusra himself set down, in the forty-fourth key of his well-known book (المقاليد), al-Maqalid, concerning the impossibility of these souls coming from the world of the intellect, is sufficient as a proof refuting what he said elsewhere in his defense, and confirming what we have set down. I do not know how this escaped him. By my reckoning, the book al-Maqalid was composed after the book of al-Nusra. May God gather its author with our pure masters in the Abode of Holiness, and seal our religion and our world with good, by His grace.

Chapter Thirty-Three of Part One

Then he said: The intellect, by reason of its completeness and perfection, does not forget its world, does not incline toward nature, and does not desire it. This is a correct statement, if it is said of those souls that have acquired their perfection through knowledge and works, and have thereby become attached to the abode of nature and come to resemble the outgoing intellects in completeness, like the souls of the Speakers and the Foundations who stand in their place (عليهم السلام). As for the outgoing intellects, which are the First, the Second, the Previous, the Follower, the Creative, and the Emanated, the First is higher and loftier than to hold any measure for the natural world, let alone to incline toward it; rather, its overflow upon it is continuous, that by it what is not complete may be completed.

Then he said: Were the soul as complete as the intellect, it would have held fast to what it beheld in its world, and would not have desired what is with its effect, so as to entail that it had been altered from its first state; yet it did not hold fast to that state. Enough has already been said on this meaning.

And he said: His words, that it did not desire what is with its effect, amount to affirming that nature is its effect. But how can it be true to say that nature is the effect of souls, and that its existence is by the existence of the persons who belong to the world of nature? Or how is one to conceive that the heavens, the planets, the natures, and the things generated should be from them, when they are confined within persons whom number encompasses? Or how is it true that the complete should desire, in its own states, what is beneath it? That is impossible; and if it is impossible, then it follows that the existence of the natural world is together with the First Emanator, and that the completeness of the existents within it is by his overflow. To expound this is a discourse that lays bare the partisanship of the author of al-Nusra against the author of al-Islah, even though the words of the author of al-Nusra are sound in the manner we have explained, not in the manner he himself intended in his rebuttal.

Chapter Thirty-Four of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said: The Wise One stated, in logic, that all things exist in four senses: either in the essences of things, or in thought, or in speech, or in writing. The things existing in their own essences are those the intellect has encompassed, none of which escapes its encompassing, since there exists nothing that has no essence; and were it not that whatever has an essence cannot elude the intellect, the intellect would not have encompassed all that has an essence. As for the soul, it has encompassed only what thought has first presented to its encompassing; and if among things there is that which has an essence which thought has not first presented for it to grasp, then the soul has not encompassed all that has an essence, and it remains deficient in relation to the rank of the intellect, since it has not encompassed all that has an essence, whereas the intellect has encompassed all that has an essence, and there is nothing that has no essence. This is among the clearest indications of the perfection of the intellect and the deficiency of the soul. This is what he said.

We say: The discourse on this is not well ordered. What the Wise One meant by his words, if indeed he said them, is to show that the existents fall into four divisions. As for their being in the essences of things, he means that among things there is that whose existence is outside the soul, and that has a meaning in its essence by which it is what it is, like the body, whose essence exists outside the soul, together with the meaning by which it is what it is in its essence, which is length, breadth, and depth.

He said: And as for their being in thought, he means that among things there is that which has no essence outside the soul, but whose existence is in the mind, like genus, matter, place, and the circles that are traced in the soul, by way of supposition, from the motion of the spheres, and the like.

He said: And as for their being in speech, he means that among things there is that whose existence is in speech, like propositions of affirmation and negation concerning essences that are not changed by them.

He said: And as for their being in writing, he means that among things there is that whose existence is in writing, and whose existence takes its rule from it, like the divine matters overflowing from the world of the intellect upon the Speakers and the aided ones (عليهم السلام), which, if they are not fixed by composed letters, and their forms not wholly deposited so that the soul may have a conception and an existence through them, their existence and the benefit of them come to nothing.

The Wise One had confined the existents to these four divisions, though under examination the divisions diminish and fall back to what is below them. It is therefore possible that the Wise One meant, by his words, to point out that writing stands, in exposition, in place of what exists in speech; that speech stands in place of the soul in what it wishes to make plain; and that the soul, in turn, stands in place of those things outside it, by conceiving them and taking their place through attaching itself to them, in the sense of becoming their like by acquiring the meanings by which they are what they are. There is nothing in the words of the Wise One that serves as evidence for what he wished to defend, namely the deficiency of the one spoken of, from that great rank. But if the words of the author of al-Nusra are carried upon what is in the world of nature, upon the souls that have risen in rank and become intellects standing in actuality, which are called a second emanation, and which are still standing in potentiality below the First Emanator in the world of Holiness, then he is right.

Chapter Thirty-Five of Part One

Here the author of al-Nusra made it obligatory, in his words, to interpret the saying of the Wise One so that the deficient one has thought; but this is not sound, since his discourse concerns the Follower, of whom the author of al-Islah speaks. For thought, in the souls existing in the world of nature, is a power in them to acquire what they do not yet possess the image of; and the end of this power is that they may become independent of it. The proof is that the soul has no need of thought in what it has already encompassed, whose image is present with it, and in which it stands in actuality; it needs thought only in the things it has not yet encompassed, whose image, by which it stands in actuality and attains the degree of perfection and independence, it has not yet acquired. And since the soul at last becomes independent of thought,

I say: The Follower, from which the overflow and blessing come upon the existents of the world, I mean the things generated, was granted its perfection at the first, and, by what we have already said concerning the perfection it attained at the beginning of its existence, was made independent. And when that is so, the partisanship of the author of al-Nusra against the author of al-Islah in what he discussed has become plain, and it has appeared that his statement is sound only in what is natural, not in what the Abode of Holiness contains.

Chapter Thirty-Six of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said: As for his saying that the deficiency is in the action of the soul, not in its essence, because the action of the soul is completed only through time, this is error and mistake, since it is not necessary that whatever is done in time be deficient. If the cause of the deficiency of things were time alone, then it would be necessary that whatever is done in a longer time be the more deficient, and whatever is done in a shorter time be the more complete; yet we see the contrary in what is observed, for the things that may be the more complete are, the shorter the time, the more complete by that very fact. Were the matter as the author of al-Islah described, it would be necessary that it be the reverse. Time, then, is not the cause of the deficiency of things or of their perfection; rather, the deficiency of a thing comes from the deficiency of the agent, or from the deficiency of the matter, or from the deficiency of both together. This is what he said.

We say: The author of al-Islah did not claim, nor say, that action in time is deficient. Rather he said that whatever meaning is folded within it, and attains its completeness through time and duration, is deficient, and that once it attains it, it is complete; and that everything existing in what is observed has a completeness which, once it receives it, it is complete. It has already been said that the meaning of his words, that the deficiency is in the action of the soul, not in its essence, is that the soul does the act by which it attains the degree of perfection from its own side and acquires the image by which it becomes complete through its overflow. So the words of the author of al-Islah, that the deficiency is in the action of the soul, not in its essence, indicate nothing but that the deficiency is in the receptive essence of the soul, upon which the Follower overflows, so that it cannot receive it all at once, but only through time, days, months, and years. And when it is thus, the words of the author of al-Islah, upon the ground of which he speaks, are sound; and the words of the author of al-Nusra, if they are carried upon natural things, are sound; but he wished to be an advocate for his teacher. May God gather them and us with the masters (عليهم السلام).

Chapter Thirty-Seven of Part One

The author of al-Islah said: The Creator, exalted be He, originated the intellect and time together in a single instant. The author of al-Nusra said: It is known that time is the measure of motion, and motion belongs to the spheres; so it follows from this saying that the spheres, and the time generated from their motions, were together with the intellect in a single instant, and it becomes permissible to say that the located body and the corruptible beings were together with the intellect in a single instant. And if it is permissible to say that the intellect and time were together in a single instant, then it follows, by this analogy, that time receives of the excellences and the lights overflowing upon the intellect the like of what the intellect receives, so that it too is complete, which is impossible. And if it is permissible to say that the intellect and time were together in a single instant, then they cannot but be either two things or a single thing; and if they are a single thing, then a plural wording has been used of them.

It remains, then, to weigh the claim itself and see where it holds and where it fails.

Chapter Thirty-Seven of Part One

The author of al-Islah said: The Most High Creator (al-Bari) originated the intellect and time together, in a single instant.

The author of al-Nusra said: It is known that time is the measure of motion, and that motion belongs only to the heavens. From this it must follow that the heavens, and the time generated from their motions, were present with the intellect at once; and one might equally say that whatever has place, and the corruptible beings, were present with the intellect at once. Yet if it is permitted to say that the intellect and time were together in a single instant, then it follows, by this analogy, that time receives of the virtues and lights overflowing upon the intellect the like of what the intellect receives, so that it too becomes complete, and that is impossible. And granting that the intellect and time were together in a single instant, they are not without being either two things or one thing. If they are one thing, then the plural wording does not apply to a single thing by way of its names, for it is impossible to say that the stone was together with the rock, or the blade together with the knife. And if they are two distinct things, then consider: the intellect, when it apprehended its own essence, did so either without time or with time. If it apprehended its own essence without time, then it was, and time was not. And if it apprehended its own essence only with time, then time is prior to it; but what is prior to the intellect is not time; it is eternity and pre-eternity.

We say: The substance of his statement, though it falls short in its proofs and its demonstration, is that the existence of time together with the intellect is impossible. This is sound in our doctrine, and there has already passed in this book enough of the argument on the impossibility of anything being present together with the first intellect to make plain that the matter stands otherwise than the author of al-Islah set it down in this chapter. Yet the author of al-Nusra erred in his saying that what precedes the intellect is not time but the eternal and the pre-eternal, for there is nothing at all prior to the first intellect, as we have made clear in what has already been said in this book of ours and in the book Rahat al-Aql (راحة العقل). What is sound, and upon which the rule of belief and of the doctrine concerning the eternal and the pre-eternal rests, is that these belong to the world of origination, as we have explained in the epistle known as al-Rawda (الروضة); and in what we set down there is enough to spare us any lengthening here.

Chapter Thirty-Eight of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said, bringing forward the saying of Banidh Qalis (بانذ قليس) by way of argument: that the intellect became complete only because it submitted to nothing except the Creator, mighty and exalted, while the soul became deficient because it submitted to nature.

We say: The philosophers, and the foremost among them, however excellent they were in their own age, remained under the sway of error in much of what they discussed of intelligible things. Had they lived in our time and seen the springs of grace, how they overflow from the House of Revelation with the realities of things as they truly are, in the manner of the Alawite call, then, for all their striving on their own behalf, they would have done themselves justice; they would have held back from much of what they believed about what lies hidden from the senses, and would have rested content with the traces of the one for whom God has set a light in the obscurities of things. For in their own times they followed their own opinions and their own intellects in what they pursued, until each of them arrived, in his belief, only at what his own writings attest, just as the people of the outward do in the Hanafi community when they lean upon their own intellects in treading the paths of religion. So their opinions diverged, and there was disparity among them: some of them were more often right, and some of them were more often wrong. The reliance is upon what the balance of religion bears witness to, which is the horizons and the souls; and in what has already been said upon this, and in the showing that a belief holding the matter to be as they held it is false, there is enough to spare us all contrivance and lengthening. For Banidh Qalis brought forward nothing but what the author of al-Nusra brought forward in his argument, and the saying of Banidh Qalis is more apt to be assailed.

We ask God to illumine our intellects, to seal our affairs with felicity, and to grant us success in good words and in righteous, sound deeds, by His bounty, His generosity, His gentleness, and His grace.

## 8. Motion, Stillness, and the Bodiless Intellect

This second part of Kitab al-Riyad concerns the adjudication between the two disputants on the question that was raised about the intellect that is the first creation.

Chapter One of Part Two.

The author of al-Islah said:

We say: motion and stillness are two effects united in the first and the second. For when the first became creative, it united with creativity, and that union had two effects upon it in potentiality. We said two effects because creativity and the creative are two, one of them being creativity itself and the other being the creative itself; and creativity and the creative, though they are two, are understood as one essence, which is the whole. These two effects exist in the first in potentiality; then the second is emitted from the first, so the emission has two effects upon the intellect, one being the emission itself and the other being the union of the two origins in the matter. One of the two effects is motion, which is higher than the other, which is stillness, just as one of the two origins, which is the first, is higher than the other, which is the second. The motion of the first is the conveyance to the second of all the essences, and stillness is the second's acceptance of the benefit of the first of all the essences and its inclusion of them within itself. I do not say that motion and stillness are born of the soul by the power learned from the intellect; rather, I say they are two effects of the soul, by the power learned from the intellect, in the image and the form, so that the image and the form became the foundation of this world.

The sum of his statement in this section is that motion and stillness are two effects united in the first and the second, that they exist in the first in potentiality, that one of them, namely motion, is higher than the other, that motion is the benefit of the first and stillness the acceptance by the second of that benefit, that the soul received them by the power learned from the intellect in the image and the form, that the first, when it became creative, united with creativity, and that creativity and the creator are two, even if they are one thing.

The author of al-Nusra said:

This statement contains some confusion, for motion is only for the sake of seeking. As for the motion of bodies, it is a seeking of place, as fire moves upward to seek its place and earth moves downward to seek its place; and the motion of souls is to seek benefit. Wherever seeking is established, need is established there, for seeking arises only from some need, and it is impossible to say that the intellect needs something for which it would move. Even if it were permissible to ascribe motion to the two origins, ascribing it to the soul is more fitting than to the intellect, because the soul needs to benefit from the intellect and moves toward it with the motion of longing, the motion of the effect toward its cause. And if it is established that motion belongs to the soul, then stillness belongs to the intellect, for it is still in what it receives of the virtues and lights bestowed upon it by its cause, not seeking increase, because its cause has already poured upon it of the lights and the radiance a measure beyond which the intellect knows no increase for which it would move in seeking. Yet the intellect has not poured forth upon the soul all that was poured upon it, but only a part, so that the soul might dwell by what was poured upon it, while it withheld a part so that the soul might move in seeking it.

The sum of his statement is that motion is only for need, that the intellect is not in need and so does not move, that ascribing motion to the soul is more fitting than ascribing it to the intellect, that motion belongs to the soul and stillness to the intellect, and that the intellect has not poured forth upon the soul all that it has, but only a part, so as to be the cause of the soul's motion in seeking the rest. This is the whole of what the two of them say.

We say: as for the saying of the author of al-Islah, that motion and stillness are two effects united in the first and the second, existing in the first in potentiality and in the second in actuality, this affirms that the first is at rest and in motion in potentiality, since motion and stillness are in it in potentiality, and that the second is in motion and at rest in actuality, since motion and stillness are in it in actuality; and that is impossible in several respects. And the saying of the author of al-Nusra on this point, that the intellect is not in need and therefore is not in motion, is the correct one. For motion, in all its kinds, is found only in natural bodies, and the first and second intellects, being of the first existence, are not bodies, so that they might be in motion or at rest. The proof is that a body requires that it be matter and form together, and that each of the two needs the other in existence, so that its existence is not from God, may He be glorified, as a first existence; for that whose existence is from God as a first existence is an essence with no otherness in it, being the very act that is the first existence from Him, may He be exalted and magnified, and the essence of the act is one essence, not two. If its existence were not a first existence, it would be necessary that another precede it in existence, and it would be something else. So it follows from the argument that if the intellect were a body, another would precede it in existence, whereas it is void that anything precede the first intellect in its existence, since it is the essence of the act that is the cause and the creation, and behind it there is nothing but God the Creator, the Originator, may He be exalted, from whom is its existence. And if it is void that anything precede it in existence, it is void that it be a body; and if it is void that it be a body, and there is no motion except in what is a body, then it is void that there be motion in the intellect; and if it is void that there be motion in it, then the saying of the author of al-Islah, that the intellect has motion in potentiality, is void.

Chapter Two of Part Two.

The author of al-Islah said:

We said two effects because creativity and the creative are two, one of them being creativity itself and the other the creative itself; and creativity and the creative, though they be two, are nothing but a whole. This is a picture that may not be held as a belief, for the matter of creativity and the creative is contrary to what he mentioned, that they are two. Rather, creativity is the creative and the creative is creativity, and there is nothing there but one. When it is referred to that from which it has its existence, it is a creating, by its being an act of Him and the very essence of the act; and when it is referred to itself, it is a created, by its being an object of another, Him from whom is its existence.

Chapter Three of Part Two.

Concerning what the author of al-Islah said, that motion and stillness are in the first in potentiality and in the second in actuality, we say: that whose existence is a first existence can be nothing but pure act and the reality of act. Were it permissible for motion and stillness to be in the first in potentiality, it would follow that the second is more noble than the first, since the ruling of what subsists in actuality, as regards nobility, is not like the ruling of what subsists in potentiality; and it is impossible that the second precede the first in nobility. What he mentioned is among the attributes of that which is later in existence, which needs, in coming forth, an act and something subsisting in actuality. But the first intellect is intellect in actuality, and so likewise is the second, and what follows them of the intellects that are separate from matter; and it is not permissible to hold as a belief, of what is beyond nature among the high limits, what necessarily belongs to a being of the abode of nature and is proper to it.

Chapter Four of Part Two.

As for the saying of the author of al-Islah, that motion and stillness are two effects of the soul, by the power learned from the intellect, in the image and the form, his meaning is that the bodies, which are the matter and the form together, are such that whatever is moved among them is moved by the natural power whose existence within them is from what is outside them, from the first and second intellects, a motion of longing. To place the two effects, mover and moved, in the matter and the form is impossible; but that this is learned from the intellect, in the sense that its existence is from it, is sound in this respect, and this is the best sense that may be attributed to what he said.

Chapter Five of Part Two.

As for the saying of the author of al-Nusra, that motion belongs to the soul and stillness to the intellect, it is not permissible to hold that as a belief concerning those transcendent limits. We say in summary: the world of origination, comprising the preceding and the following, and the pure intellects, which are the high separate letters distinct from bodies, are too high and too exalted for motion or stillness to be imagined in them. For motion, in that which is in motion, comes only from a mover within it that is other than it, and by the two of them is the existence of its essence; and whatever has the existence of its essence by the existence of differing things is an essence of deficiency and need. And since the following, which he named a soul, is of the world of origination, which is proper to completeness and perfection, and its essence is not an essence of deficiency or need, it is void that there be difference within its essence; and if it is void that there be in it what is other than its essence, it is void that it be in motion, and void that motion be in it. Moreover, motion is that by which the thing in which it resides is found passing from its first state to another, whether in the essence or in its states. And the following, which he named a soul, has an eternal existence, by virtue of its arising, from the side of origination, in the second, and its being intellect; and if it is eternal, it is impossible that it pass from what it was upon at first, so it is void that there be motion in it. The saying concerning stillness is just like the saying concerning motion: the first intellect has no stillness, and the following, which he named a soul, has no motion. The author of al-Nusra, in what he said, intended only those souls and intellects that are in the abode of nature, emitted with a second emission; the being there is moved, according to what we have explained in our book known as براحة العقل, for motion and stillness are proper to the world of the body and what is in it, being among the causes of its perfection, apart from what is proper to the abode of origination. But he transferred the meaning, out of eagerness to satisfy the deficiency he inclined toward in support of his master. May God lodge them in the abode of His generosity, and gather them and us with our pure masters (صلوات الله عليهم اجمعين).

Chapter Six of Part Two.

The author of al-Nusra said that motion is for need, and that it is more fitting to the soul than to the intellect. It has already been made clear, from the words that went before, that motion and stillness befit only what is a body or what is in a body, and that the first and second, of whom the discourse speaks, are not bodies, so the ruling of motion and stillness does not apply to them, as we have shown in what preceded of our discourse in our book known as براحة العقل. His saying holds true for what is natural by way of the second emission, not for what is originated, whose existence is by the first emission. Nor is motion in every thing for need, as he mentioned; motion in a natural thing may be among the causes of its perfection, as with the motion of the spheres, whose motions are not for a need in their own essence to attain what they were not given at the first of their being, but rather are a completion belonging to them. Such too is the motion of the speaking one and his striving in gathering the word upon worship and his exertion in the affairs of governance; that is a completion incumbent upon him, for something is required of him, by which he is completed, after his attaining perfection and his rising to the rank of the speaking one. Since this is so, the saying of the author of al-Nusra without qualification, that motion is for need, is not thorough; his ruling is sound only in some natural things, on the ground that it is not separable from them, as we have mentioned.

Chapter Seven of Part Two.

The author of al-Nusra said that the intellect did not pour forth upon the soul all that it has, but only a part. His meaning in this, that the first is distinguished by a rank of precedence and a loftiness of station by what is not proper to what is below it, which he named a soul, is the best sense that may be attributed to his words by interpretation. Otherwise, were the matter as he described it, and did each of the limits withhold from pouring forth upon another some part of what it has, then it would be conceivable of the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وآله) that the blessings reaching him, as an overflow from the world of holiness, were such that he did not make them clear in their fullness to the legatee and his followers, and did not teach them to anyone, and they came to nothing with the ceasing of any benefit from them, so that the legatee (عليه السلام) would be left doubtful in his perfection and his worthiness and his standing in the place of the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وآله), and the obedience made obligatory toward him would be doubtful in its obligation, held merely as opinion. That is not permissible to hold as a belief, neither of the speaking one, nor of the legatee, nor of any of the limits.

Rather we say: the blessings are poured out and bestowed from the side of all the limits, and the reason that those blessings by which perfection is attained in all beings do not reach a being is not any withholding on the part of the limits, but rather the incapacity of the receivers to accept them. Each attains the blessings according to the strength of the receiver: some receive fully, others receive a part; and this is found among natural things, and among compassionate teachers and eager learners. It is not permissible for any of the limits to withhold benefit from one who would benefit from it, so long as he remains a receiver, standing in his right, when he wills, out of fear of his rising to a rank; for that would be before the spiritual birth. And the speaking one (صلى الله عليه وآله) recited what came to him from God, and said:

ولا تقتلوا اولادكم خشية املاق ومن فعل ذلك فقد أثم وتعدى طوره واسقط نفسه عن مرتبة الفاضلين

Do not kill your children for fear of poverty; and whoever does that has sinned, and exceeded his proper stage, and cast himself down from the rank of the excellent. How, then, could it be permissible to lock up the spiritual wealth, which is the sciences and the luminous blessings, from its owners, when the proof of their worthiness stands, and the Prophet has commanded that it be delivered to them, where He says, exalted is the Speaker:

فان أنستم منهم رشداً فادفعوا اليهم اموالهم ولا تأكلوها اسرافاً وبداراً

If you perceive in them sound judgment, then deliver to them their wealth, and do not consume it wastefully and in haste. No indeed: the blessings are poured out only that they may be conveyed to those who deserve them and settled in their proper place, as we have explained in our letters of interpretation and in our assemblies at Basra and Baghdad. May God make us, and a company of our brethren, among those who render the trust to its owners and fulfill its conditions. And perhaps the author of al-Nusra brought forward what he brought forward by way of analogy.

Chapter Eight of Part Two.

The author of al-Nusra said:

Moreover, action is the substance of encompassing, and by it things are encompassed, and this is not the state of what is in motion, for what is in motion does not encompass things; rather it needs something still, at the utmost of stillness, that from its stillness may encompass things. Were the intellect in motion, it would encompass nothing, and the soul would encompass nothing by benefiting from it. Since we have found it encompassing all things, so that nothing escapes it, and it encompasses whoever attains and acquires of its lights and virtues, we then know that the intellect is at the utmost of stillness, and no motion may be imagined in it; while the soul moves only to reach the inmost of the intellect, and then to become still. It does not move in order to encompass by benefiting from the intellect what it wishes to encompass; and so long as the soul is in motion, it does not encompass, nor does it attain the virtues and the lights, until it becomes still by benefiting from the intellect, and only then does it encompass things. The soul is thus in motion, a motion of subsistence, becoming still by benefiting from the one who is still. This is what he said.

We say: the discourse has already passed, on the point that the intellect is not in motion, in a measure that spares us length. It does not follow that, if the intellect is not in motion, it must be still in the manner in which the state of bodies is found, which are not free of being either still or in motion; for its existence is not like the existence of bodies, which are not free of that. In what preceded of the discourse there is sufficiency and evidence that motion and stillness do not befit it. As for what he made necessary by his saying, that the soul, so long as it is in motion, encompasses nothing and attains no virtues, its meaning is not sound, neither for what is separate, from the second, as we mentioned, nor for what is not separate. For the souls that are in the abode of nature acquire only by being in motion in their bodies, through the use of the senses, and inference, and benefiting; and to hold that the matter is so, when a proof is set up for it, suffices as a text upon the continuance of the error in it.

Chapter Nine of Part Two.

Then his saying, that the soul is in motion, a motion of subsistence, becoming still by benefiting from the one who is still, is true when a meaning is assigned to it, and false when it is carried upon the surface of his words. What makes it true is that one takes, of the soul, the natural souls, and it is said of his saying that the soul is in motion because the natural souls, which subsist in potentiality, are in motion for acquisition; and of his saying a motion of subsistence, because their acquisition drives them toward subsistence; and of his saying it becomes still by benefiting, because it passes from potentiality to actuality by acquiring and benefiting and hunting knowledge and virtues, in knowledge and in action; and of his saying from the one who is still, from the one who subsists in actuality. And what makes it false is that his saying is carried upon its surface, for the second is not in motion, nor is the intellect still, since they are not of the abode of the body, that motion and stillness should be necessary to them. Care must be turned to knowing the ranks of the limits, lest the affair of conceiving things in their realities be disordered, and lead to the corruption of the substance of souls. May God illumine our intellects with their ink, and grant us the happiness of following the pure ones of the stages of the divine sciences and the treasuries of the holy lights (صلوات الله عليهم).

## 9. Light, Darkness, and the Matter Below

The Third Chapter of the Book of Riyadh, on the judgment among the *hudud* concerning what was said about the soul and *al-Hayuli* (الهيولى): whether they resemble the First or not.

**Section One**

The author of al-Islah said:

Concerning what the author of al-Nusrah has established, we do not say that nature and the dark *al-Hayuli* are born from the Second as the Second is born from the First. For that world is the world of honor and virtue, while this world is the world of darkness and turbidity. The Second, though born from the First, resembles him; and the state of *al-Hayuli* is not like that state.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

This statement is wrong. For were that world all light with no darkness in it, and this world all darkness with no light in it, no connection would fall between this world and that world. Rather we say that the soul has two extremes. One of them is toward the intellect, and it lies upon its horizon, and it is the luminous, noble side. The other is toward nature, and nature lies upon its horizon, and in it is some darkness and turbidity, because nature falls upon its horizon. And we likewise say that nature has two ends. One of them is toward the partial soul, and in it is some light, because it falls upon the horizon of the soul. The other faces the elements, and it is the dark and turbid side in which there is no light. By these divisions we speak of the soul and of nature, of their darkness and their light. We do not say that that whole world is a world of light with no darkness in it, nor that this whole world is a world of darkness with no light in it. For were it as he described, there would be between them neither neighboring nor pairing.

He said:

Then it stands established that the dark *al-Hayuli* is born from the soul, though it does not resemble it, just as the soul is born from the First, though it does not resemble him. *Al-Hayuli* fell dark by reason of its distance from the First Cause, on account of its being born from the soul. Likewise the diminution fell in the soul, because it is farther from the First Cause, by which I mean the Preceding One (السابق).

This is his statement.

We say:

The form of what the author of al-Islah has said in his book is other than what the author of al-Nusrah has established. For the kernel of his saying, which is an answer to the saying that the Second is born from the First and does not resemble him, is that he said: We do not say so. Rather we say that the Second is born from the First, and does not resemble him, yet is his counterpart and his likeness, even though the First is higher in rank and nobler in station. This is like the male and the female in all genera and substances: the male, though higher and nobler than the female, is nonetheless the counterpart of his fellow and its likeness.

Then we say:

The Second is nobler and more elevated than that its way with what is below it should be like its way with the First. For the First and the Second are two counterparts, and they are the mine of honor, of perfection, and of elevation. There is no honor above them. God made them the mine of perfect honor and perfect light, of complete elevation and complete knowledge, with no ignorance there, no turbidity, and no darkness whatsoever. They are the two foundations of the higher world, which is the mine of purity, of sanctity (القدس), and of life. And *al-Hayuli* is a trace of the Second, which is the substance of this world, a turbid dark substance not to be described by mercy or by power. The Second manifested it by act out of himself, by the power derived from the First, and then brought forth from it the form, which is nature, by act.

Then the author of al-Islah said:

It is not permissible to say that *al-Hayuli* is born from him. Rather we say that it is a trace of him, and therefore does not resemble him. But the Second is born from the First and does resemble him, even though the First is higher in rank.

This is his statement, and the sum of it is this: that the Second issues from the First and is like him, though the First is higher in rank; and that *al-Hayuli* is a trace of the Second, its substance a dark and turbid substance, because the way of *al-Hayuli* with the Second is not like the way of the Second with the First. For the First and the Second are counterparts, whereas *al-Hayuli* does not resemble the Second, being a trace of him.

And if this is the sum of his saying, then what the author of al-Nusrah answered is no answer. For the author of al-Islah did not lay it down in his statement that that whole world is light and radiance. He only said, describing the state of the First and the Second, that God Most High made them the mine of honor and elevation. That saying of his is no proof that there is no existence for what is not in a state like theirs. And in what has already passed as an indication that *al-Hayuli* is not born from the Second there is sufficiency, with no need to lay the discourse open and lengthen it with what might turn suspicions upon us regarding what is not our aim in this section.

We say:

*Al-Hayuli* does not resemble the Second, nor the First, because its existence, in its issuing from the First, is by the lower relation that we set forth in our earlier words and in our book known as *Rahat al-Aql* (راحة العقل). In that is sufficiency.

**Section Two of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

This statement is wrong, because were that world all light with no darkness in it, and this world all darkness with no light in it, no connection would fall between this world and that world.

We say:

This statement, inasmuch as it fixes upon the author of al-Islah what he did not say, is an error; but inasmuch as it is his own words, it is sound. For in the world of origination and issuance there is that which is shared: it suits what is above it by being issued, and it suits what is below it by being a body. It is possessed of two matters, resembling neither the First from whom its existence comes; it resembles the Second from one aspect and does not resemble him from another. The aspect from which it resembles him is its being issued from him. The aspect from which it resembles the body is its being a natural body. And that is nature. When it is joined to the world of origination and issuance, it is a life flowing from that world into what is below it, giving it what it is able to receive of succession and of conception. And when it is joined to this world, it is a principle of motion and stillness in the thing it is in essentially, not by way of accident. So nature came to be, even though in its existence it is issued, or an intermediary between the two issuings, the First and the Second.

**Section Three of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The soul, by which I mean the Second, has two extremes. One of them is toward the intellect, upon its horizon, and it is the luminous, noble side. The other is toward nature, upon its horizon, and it is the side in which there is some darkness.

We say:

That is not sound. The proof is that whatever has extremes is possessed of dimensions measured by area; and the Second, which he named a soul in the way we have set forth, is not a body; and if it is not a body, then it is void that it should have two extremes. Then, by his saying, he made it necessary that it has a luminous extreme upon the horizon of the intellect, and an extreme in which is some darkness upon the horizon of nature, distinct from the intellect, and distinct from the First by what he mentioned of its being upon the horizon of nature. But it is impossible that in the Second there should be anything by which it is distinct from the First, because its existence, in its issuing, is from the nobler relation that is in the First, namely its being a cause. For we have already said that the First, by its being existent, is not in its own essence both cause and effect. It is cause by relation to what is below it, which is the nobler relation in the First, and effect by relation to itself, which is the lower relation in the First. So from its intellection of its essence as a cause there issues from it its like, since it is the way of a cause that its effect be from it. And from its intellection of its essence as an effect there issues from it what is not its like, its issuance being from the lower relation in the First, namely its being an effect.

Witness to what we have mentioned is the state upon which stands the Foundation (al-Asas), which is the standing likeness of the Second in the knowledge of the body, and its being not distinct from the Speaker (al-Natiq). For all that was existent in the Foundation is existent in the Speaker from whom the existence of the Foundation came, and thus it is not distinct from him. Yet not all that belongs to the Speaker is existent in the Foundation, for the Speaker has that by which the Foundation is distinct from him, namely that in which the Foundation does not share. So it has become clear, by the Foundation's sharing with the Speaker in what is existent in it, and by there being found in it nothing that is not found in the Speaker, that it is not distinct from him. And since it is not distinct from him, and since it is the likeness by which, and by whose rank, the existence of the Second and its rank are inferred, then the Second is not distinct from the First. And since it is not distinct, its existence, as he mentioned it, namely upon the horizon of nature, is impossible.

**Section Four of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

*Al-Hayuli* fell dark only by reason of its distance from the First Cause, on account of its being born from the soul.

We say:

*Al-Hayuli*'s being unlike the First, and the Second's being unlike, is not by reason of distance from the First in respect of the relation in which the First stands as effect, since the First is a gathering of two relations: a relation by its being cause, and a relation by its being effect. The existence of what comes to be from the relation that is effect is not like the existence of what comes to be from the relation that is cause, since the cause is ever nobler than the effect, and prior to it in rank and in nature. For of the First it is said that it is cause, and it is said that it is effect; yet it is a single essence, which acquires these relations by the additions.

**Section Five of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The diminution fell in the soul only because it is farther from the First Cause, by which I mean the Preceding One.

We say:

There is no diminution in the Second, which he named a soul, as we have already mentioned; nor is it far from the First; rather it is his like. The diminution is a necessary attribute, not the very nature of the soul. The soul became deficient not from the side of its own essence, which is its perfection, but from the side of its second perfection, which it acquires by benefit.

**Section Six of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The relation of nature's birth from the soul is no greater than the soul's birth from the intellect, since the soul, relative to the intellect, is less than nature relative to the soul. And if it is permitted that the soul be born from the intellect, with its perfection and its nobility and its nearness to the First Cause, it is permitted that nature be born from the soul, since nature is more like the soul in all its states, in its acts and its powers, than the soul is like the intellect in its acts and its powers. For nature, among the ancients, is marked as the principle of motion, and some of them said it is the active power; and if you look upon nature from these two limits, the fuller portion and the ampler share are hers. For the soul, among the ancients, is the moving, abiding substance, and it is withal marked with intellect, while there is nothing in the intellect of the mark of the definition of the soul, nor is the soul defined by the definition of the intellect. For the definition of the intellect is that it is the first of things, that it is the Originated, that it is the first effect, and the first intermediary; and it is not permitted to say that the soul is the first of things, nor that it is originated, nor that it is the first effect. So if it is permitted that there be born from the intellect, with its perfection and its nobility and its nearness to the Word (الكلمة), a like of the soul, which is not defined by the definition of the intellect, and this is not to be denied, then it is permitted that there be born from the soul a like of nature, which resembles in its limits the limits of the soul, and this is not to be denied. And had one looked upon the thing from the aspect of consideration, this measure of knowledge would not have been hidden. Had the author of al-Islah stood upon what is in nature of wonders and of wisdom, and upon what is in its acts of splendor and nobility and goodness and beauty, by reason of which the partial soul desires to incline toward it and to follow it, he would have known and been certain that it is born from the soul, though it does not resemble it. For causes ever find comely the order of their effects. From this the partial soul desired to follow nature. Every goodness and splendor and nobility and adornment is from the higher, noble world; and had nature not been born from the soul, it would never have been able to tell of goodness and beauty and adornment. But because it is born from the soul, it told what it saw in the world of its cause of beauty and nobility, a deficient telling, short of the telling of the soul. So know this.

This is his statement.

We say:

In what has already passed of the discourse upon the intellect, the soul, and nature, and upon the existence of that which precedes another in existence, and the rest, there is folded the sound clarification of what he has brought; and we have no need to lengthen it with certitude upon the error that has persisted in conception. And in that is sufficiency.

**The First Section**

The author of the Reform said:

According to what the author of al-Nusrah has established: as for the parts that are within us, they are traces of the first essence, not parts of it. For our union is by the traces that come from the first essence, not by its parts; and the union of the First is not by its parts, because the First is united by the very Word. The parts that are within us, though they are traces of the First, are not parts of it, nor is it united by them; rather they are united by its traces, as we have shown in the union of its products with our traces within them. Nor is the union of the first essence with the Word of this kind, for the first essence is united with the very Word, until it and the Word became one thing. This is his statement.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

This statement holds confusion and manifest contradictions. For he said that the parts within us are traces of the first essence, yet how can the parts be traces, when they are essences subsisting in themselves? And traces have no subsistence in themselves. If he says that the parts within us are traces of the first essence, then, once the name "parts" has fallen upon them, he is obliged to show that they are parts of some essence. Were they not parts of the first essence, he would not have called them parts of some essence at all; and once it is established that they are parts of some essence, it is void that they should be traces, because the parts of a thing are not traces of another. This is his statement.

We say:

The statement of the author of al-Nusrah, that the souls are essences subsisting in themselves after acquisition, and that traces cannot subsist in themselves, is true. Yet the statement of the author of the Reform, and his refusal to give the souls the name of parthood, arose because he saw that the things which admit of division are the bodies, which are never free of the nine accidents and are grasped by the five senses. The soul is not a body, nor is that which overflows upon it a body; so he did not see fit to give it the name "part," lest he set it in the course of bodies and it fail to correspond to what its own essence is and to what it has told him of itself. Since he did not intend that, he said "traces," and named them as things that come to have essence and to endure. What he means by this is that these souls have their coming-to-essence and their endurance by the traces of those separate essences that reach them from the side of the limits, who are the intermediaries (عليهم السلام). If this is so, then it appears that the intention of the author of the Reform, in saying "traces," is that which runs in the soul in the course of the form: that by which the essentiality of the souls is completed, and by which the soul endures when it receives it. This is what he made necessary where he says: and we said only that honor and merit belong to these worlds because we received the trace of that noble essence in full, and were prepared to receive it. So it is established that by "trace" he intends that which is received; and that what the author of al-Nusrah intends by "part" is that which runs in the soul in the course of the hayula, which is that which receives. And this is by way of likeness and approximation to the understandings.

**The Second Section**

As for the statement of the author of al-Nusrah, that the souls are parts of the Universal Soul, it rests upon no proof. For that which has been divided, its whole is the sum of all those parts; and if its whole is the sum of all those parts, then he has made it necessary that the souls are parts of the Universal Soul. But concerning the souls in the abode of this world, the matter is drawn out by the proof already established, earlier and later: that the Second, which he named the Soul, has its existence not in the abode of the body, which is the abode of this world. And since it is not in this world, it is void that the Second should be a whole for these souls; and once it is void that it be a whole for these souls, then these are not parts of it.

**The Third Section**

Then his assertion that they are parts of the Universal Soul does not hold together with his intention that the Universal Soul is the Second. For the matter concerning the soul, if the souls are parts of the Second, is not free of one of these cases: either it is the whole of them, which is the Second, in the abode of this world; or some of them are in it and some in the higher world; or it is not of the Second by way of parthood in this world at all. It is void that the whole which is the Second should be in the entire abode of this world, since what is in this world subsists by potentiality and has no passage into act except by that which subsists in act; and the first Intellect is prevented from being that by which the souls subsisting in potentiality pass into act, because of what this would require of the precedence of that whose existence is impossible for God, glorified be He, upon which the discourse has already passed in a book other than this. And it is likewise void that some of them be in this world and some in the higher world, since the part in the higher world, were it like this one in the world, subsisting by potentiality just as this one is, would stand in the very condition of this one; and that is void by the proof established that the existence of the Second is an existence subsisting in act, not like the existents of this world's abode, which subsist by potentiality. So, after the voidness of the two divisions, it is established that the Second is nothing of parthood in this world, and it is void that the souls should be parts of it.

**The Fourth Section**

After the matter of the parts, we note that a whole gives to all that is under it what it gives, in all its states, according to it. So if these souls were parts of the Second, and we have found them neither knowing nor wise, then the whole whose parts they are would be of this same condition; and to hold such a thing of that mighty limit is unbelief, we seek refuge in God, when it is known that the substances of the Messengers (عليهم السلام) are from it, and that it is the proximate cause of the overflow upon the lower world. So the souls, then, are not parts of the Second. And when it was void that these souls be parts, we said: the souls are of the nature of those separate things whose existence is a creative existence; and their appearance and their existence are only from between the natural world and the world of emanation. The soul is a natural power that attains what it acquires from the side of the limits through obedience and the two worships; and there is emitted from its own self an abiding, abstracted form, which resembles the abstracted things in the abode of the Hereafter from one aspect, and differs from them from another.

Hence we say: it is the second emanation; and our epistles on the Return (al-Maad) contain the mention of that, God willing.

**The Fifth Section**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Likewise, our traces united with our products do not receive our traces after we have finished the work, nor do the products prepared to receive from us receive any further, except by the entry of corruption into them and their change from their states; for they are not parts of us, nor are they essences subsisting in themselves, but rather vanishing accidents. This is his statement.

We say:

What is meant by this is that bodies reach a limit in receiving what is theirs to receive, a limit beyond which they receive nothing further, like iron heated in the fire: once it has reddened, that is its utmost, and it grows no hotter; and like a black body, which grows no blacker, for it may reach in blackness a limit beyond which it is not further blackened. That meaning is more fitting to be held than what his own phrase requires. And it is not the traces that are the receiver; rather the receiver is other than they, namely the body, which is the substrate for the succession of the accidents and the traces upon it.

**The Sixth Section**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

But the speaking souls within us do not cease to acquire from the light of the higher, noble world, and to increase thereby in nobility within themselves, without any change occurring in themselves, nor by the entry of corruption into them from the Soul when it wishes to benefit them. So it is established that they are parts and essences of the Soul, not traces of it.

We say:

The matter, as to the soul being unbounded in receiving, is as he said, and it is one of the proofs that the soul is not a body. But the matter, as to the soul being parts of the Second, is not as he held and set forth by what it entails and folds within it, of which the discourse has already passed. The best that can be ascribed to his statement, for one who intends by "parts" this, is that these souls, which are the receiver, when they intellect their own essences as such, are then one thing with what they have intellected: it is they, and they are it, distinguished from it only by rank. So it is like the part of a thing in which is found what is found in the whole of it, their way in their own essence being one and the same from this aspect.

**The Seventh Section**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Also, to say that the speaking souls existing in this world are not parts of the Universal Soul nullifies prophethood, the imamate, the executorship, revelation, and the sacred laws. For it would then not be permitted for the soul to perceive that which influences it, nor to encompass it, nor to benefit from it. Yet the prophets (عليهم السلام) claimed that they beheld the noble upper world, and stood upon the spiritual limits, and benefited from what they conveyed to those after them of light and exposition, from those above them, from the two roots and the three branches, and that they beheld the angels. Were their pure souls only traces of the Universal Soul, they would not have been able to comprehend what was revealed to them, nor to stand upon those spiritual realities that are parts of the Universal Soul.

We say:

The discourse has already established that the souls are not parts, nor traces, and that they are a second emanation; and that what is meant by the one who called them "traces" is that by which the essentiality of the souls is completed and which earns them endurance; and, in the statement of the one who called them "parts," that they, by intellecting their own essence as such, are like those things that are their cause. Prophethood is not nullified by this, nor the imamate, nor anything else; for it is established that these ranks are not from this aspect. The prophets (عليهم السلام) were able to perceive those things absent from them of the upper world only after they attained the rank of perfection, and became thereby in completeness at its utmost, so that when they return to their own essences it is as though they have perceived those transcendent essences by their very being, they and those essences being, from the aspect of completeness, one thing, with no difference save in rank. We have set out, in our book known as Rahat al-Aql (راحة العقل), enough of that to suffice and to heal, that it may be known.

**The Eighth Section**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Rather, it is necessary to say that the Intellect is creative through the Word, the Soul is the image of the Intellect, nature is generated from the Soul, and the speaking souls are parts of the Universal Soul, so that by this order tawhid, reward, punishment, prophethood, the imamate, the sacred laws, and revelation are made sound.

We say:

The soundness of tawhid, of reward and punishment, of prophethood and the imamate, of the mission and the laws drawn from revelation, together with their establishment, is not from the aspect that the Intellect is creative through the Word and that the Soul is the image of the Intellect; rather their establishment is from another aspect, which we have set out in our epistles and our writings concerning them. As for the Intellect, it may not be said that it is creative through the Word, for that would require the precedence of the Word over the Intellect, with the disorder this brings into the paths of tawhid, according to what we have expounded in our discourse.

Then we say:

The Intellect is nothing but the very Word, and the Word is nothing but the very Intellect. This is confirmed by the statement of our master the Imam al-Muizz li-Din Allah (ع) in the book Tawil al-Sharia (تأويل الشريعة), where he speaks of the intelligibles: that the Word is an expression of the Intellect, and that it merits these names by relation. And there has already passed, in our exposition and our discourse upon the Creator, the Intellect, the Word, and what is said of the world of creation and the first emanation, in the Illuminating Epistle (الرسالة المضيئة) and the other epistles, what suffices the seeker. As for the Soul and nature, they are two emanations from the first Intellect, each according to its own relation; and for that reason one of them became more excellent than the other, the more excellent being the first emanation, the Soul, and the speaking souls being the second emanation. So what occurred in the first of them is emanated in the last of them. Our epistles alone on the Return (al-Maad) contain of the mention of that what makes lengthening unnecessary, for the expansion of speech upon the like of this in every place is forbidden, and a gesture suffices the seeking student, while another discards it for the difficulty of its reach and the hardship of knowing it. God grants success to the believing knower for the knowledge of that according to his strength in sincerity to God and to His friends, and aids him when he is sincere to His face, exalted be He, and gives his allegiance to Him and to His friends. May God make us among the followers, the receivers, who fulfill His covenant; and may He grant us success in His praise, by His grace and His glory.

## 11. The Fruit of Two Worlds

The fifth book of the Riyad concerns the judgment between the two disputants, upon the question of human beings being the fruit of the physical world, and it is of this that I now speak.

In the first section of this book, the author of al-Islah said:

Man is the fruit of this whole world, and it is for his sake that this world was created in all its parts: its origins and its foundations. The first of these is the Hayula, which is the principle of the individuals that are born of it by motion and rest; then the individuals, which are the foundation of the vehicles; then the compounds, which are the very productions of this whole world; and the natures out of which the form of the human being is composed, the form prepared to receive the three souls, which are the vegetative, the sensitive, and the speaking.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

To say that human beings are the fruit of this world is likewise the saying of the materialists, those who did not affirm the survival of the soul after the dissolution of bodies. For if human beings were the fruit of this world, this world would have held him fast and would not have let him pass to another world.

But we would say:

Human beings are the fruit of the highest world, the honorable and illuminating world. For this reason the prophets (عليهم السلام) came with their warning, that men might provide themselves for the passage from the house of mortality to the house of survival, and from this world to the abode of the Return. For the prophets knew that this world is not a home, and that man is not the fruit of this world. So the author of al-Nusrah said.

We say:

Each of the two is truthful in what he said from one side, and each is untruthful from another. The side on which they are both truthful is that each of them has said of human beings what truly belongs to him. The side on which they are both untruthful is that each denies the other its share, denies, that is, that man bears the title of fruit of both worlds together. For human beings issue from between the two worlds. From this world man receives his first perfection, and from that world his second. The end of the natural world is to give its existent his first perfection, which is the bringing forth of the bodily form by way of the circling bodies, the luminous planets, and the proximate causes, into the disposition by which he is ready to accept what belongs to him. And how could he not accept it, when he is that world's fruit, beyond which there is well nigh nothing. The end of that higher world is to give to what has been received from the existents of the natural world its second perfection, which is the effusion upon the spiritual image, the thing naturally disposed and intrinsic to it, by which it becomes substantial and endures: the knowledge of the Oneness of Allah Almighty by means of all the hudud (عليهم السلام), and obedience to them in the two modes of worship, in knowledge and in deed. That is man's fruit. What corrects what we have said in this matter is the saying of Mawlana Amir al-Muminin al-Muizz li-Din Allah Maad ibn Ismail (ع) in the book *The Interpretation of Sharia* (كتاب تأويل الشريعة), that man is the purpose of the second world and the end of the action of nature.

Now to the second section, concerning the objection of the author of al-Nusrah, that if human beings were the fruit of this world, this world would have held him fast and would not have let him pass from it to another world. By this very objection he has handed his opponent the means to turn the matter back upon him.

For his opponent says:

If, as you have said, man is the fruit of the higher world, then that world would hold him fast and would not let him return to these persons, so that he should forget, which is impossible, according to your own writing and requirement. This is no argument that proves what he intends to demonstrate in the abstract. Neither is the sending of the prophets, whom he urges toward provision, a proof that human beings are the fruit of this world. For the human being who is in truth a human being is such by the conjoining of the effusion of the two worlds: from the natural world, that which runs in the channel of the Hayula, and from the world of the Intellect, that which runs in the channel of the form. The one comes by intermediaries, and the other by intermediaries.

In the third section, the author of al-Islah said:

The Soul emanates from the First Intellect in perfection; the Hayula emanates from the Soul in perfection; and the form emanates from the Hayula in perfection. So the whole was perfect.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

If the matter is as he has said, that the perfection of the Soul is like that of the Intellect, then his statement about what is spread out upon the Hayula, the form, and the compounds is void and self-contradicting. For it is not permissible to imagine that these fashioned things come to be except from the pairing of two, one of them perfect and self-sufficient and the other deficient and in need, so that a marriage may take place between them, as is found among things witnessed, such as the heaven and the earth. The heaven is as the male: perfect and self-sufficient in all its motions, its orbits being manifest. The earth is deficient, in the rank of the female, her need for him established. For this reason a marriage took place between them, and from between them appeared the various kinds of metals, differing in their forms, and the plants, and the animals, not one of which appears actually in the heaven or in the earth or in the mothers, but which were in them in potency. When they were married, all of them appeared in actuality. Had their state not been as we have mentioned, the one deficient and the other perfect, nothing would have appeared from them. So the author of al-Nusrah said.

We say:

As for the statement of the author of al-Islah, that the Soul is the second, emanating from the First Intellect in perfection, it is correct, according to what has already been spoken above. As for his saying that the Hayula emanates from the Soul, we have already shown that its existence is not from the Soul, which is the second, but from the First Intellect. Its existence and the second are together; neither precedes the other, and neither has existence in abstraction from the other, save in the soul by way of imagination. Outside the soul they exist only together. As for the statement of the author of al-Nusrah, that it is not permissible to imagine that the fashioned things come to be except from the pairing of one perfect and self-sufficient with another deficient and in need, so that they may be coupled by what lies between them, and his likening of this to the heaven and the earth, he has refuted himself by the very words that follow. For he says that from between them appeared the various kinds of metals, differing in their forms, and the plants, and the animals, not one of which appears actually in the heaven or in the earth or in the mothers, because they were in potency. And this is to say that if any of these things, I mean the generated beings, does not appear actually in the heaven and the earth because it is in potency, then the heavens and the earth are equal in respect of their being in potency, and no superiority of one over the other appears. It is not the case that when existents come to be from between two things, one of the two must be perfect and the other deficient. We have already shown that deficiency is disclosed in things not in their selves but in their ranks, when some are added to others, and in their actions, when they do not come to be except by something other than that which completes their existence, while in their selves they are perfect. For if their selves were not perfect in the existence that befits them, no action could proceed from them, neither by themselves nor by another. The state in which the existents are found bears witness to this. The heaven, which he singled out for perfection and artistry, is deficient in its action, since its action is not completed except by that upon which it turns; and the earth, which he singled out for deficiency and need, has its deficiency not in its self but in its action, for its action is not completed except by that which turns upon it. So too with the animal and the newborn. And if this is so, then the saying of the author of al-Nusrah, that the statement of the author of al-Islah is a contradictory and prejudiced statement, is itself the contradictory and prejudiced statement.

In the fourth section, the author of al-Nusrah said:

He did not answer what was raised at the end of this section, that the name of perfection falls upon the Originator, glorified be He, because it falls upon perfect origination.

We say:

The author of al-Islah mentioned nothing of the sort. What he reported from the sages in the book of al-Islah, that they said the perfect Originator has perfect origination, and that the origination which is the perfection could be nothing but perfect, is a saying than which none could be more correct. By that saying he established that the perfect and the perfection are the origination and the originated, not the Originator, glorified be He. The name of perfection, by what he mentioned, does not fall upon the Originator, glorified be He, nor does his speech comprise anything of that. The claim of the author of al-Nusrah here is prejudice against the author of al-Islah.

In the fifth section, the author of al-Nusrah said:

We all say that origination is the cause of the originated, and that it and the originated, from the side of the originated, are one thing, and it is perfect; and that what is originated by perfect origination could be nothing but perfect. Nor are we obliged to say that the Originator, glorified be He, is perfect, or that He is a cause, or that He, glorified and exalted, and the originated are one thing.

We say:

That is a statement correct in its meaning, save that we do not say that the originated by perfect origination could be nothing but perfect because origination is the perfection. Rather we say that the originated by the origination which is the perfection could be nothing but perfect; that the originated is the origination; and that it is the cause, the effect, the perfection, and the perfect. These names follow one upon another over it according to the relations, because there is a multiplicity there in the essences, so that the meanings of these names fall upon them all, while the thing there is one. In what we have set down upon this, in this book and in others, there is sufficiency without prolonging.

In the sixth section, the author of al-Nusrah said:

If it is established that this world is the act of the Soul, and that it is withal in the utmost order and arrangement, with perpetual circling motions, then we know and are certain that in her act she sought to attain the rank withheld from her before she began to act. Since this is so, and since there was in the world nothing more honorable than the human being, nor anything more disposed to receive the effects of the Soul from her and in which her benefits are more efficacious, we know and are certain that human beings are the fruit of the Soul's attainment of the end appointed for her from the side of her cause. So understand that.

We say:

It has already gone forth from our speech, in the matter of the perfection of the second, which in his account is the Universal Soul, what indicates that in her act she did not seek to attain a rank she had not gained. The Universal Soul, which is the second, effuses upon this world not to draw to herself what is not hers, for Allah enriched her, when He brought her into being, by perfection, so that she seeks nothing that was not given her. Rather her act is to establish wisdom in the bringing forth of what is in potency into act, and to make what is base excellent, and what is later in existence to follow upon what went before. And whoever considers it well finds that the benefit returns upon that which is brought forth into act, and upon him who is under the charge of striving toward perfection, not upon her who is neither charged nor in need. To believe otherwise of that mighty hadd is error and sin. And since this is so, and since the human being has two sides, one of them natural and one of them divine, he is neither the fruit of this world purely nor the fruit of that world purely, but the fruit of both worlds together. He is the last in the beings, united with eternity by what he has acquired in time. Therefore it has been said:

> ما جرى في أولهم، جرى في آخرهم

What came to pass in the first of them came to pass in the last of them.

And whoever has known from that the stations of the prophets, the guardians, the imams, and the righteous (عليهم السلام), he is disposed for deliverance. May Allah gather us with our pure masters in the House of Holiness.

As for the seventh section, and the saying of the author of al-Nusrah that every doer does what he does only in seeking to attain what he wills, and especially when the act proceeds from the doer with motion, order, and arrangement, and his holding that this is not binding upon the First Originator and upon origination, because the act there is by way of metaphor, not with motion, nor order, nor arrangement, but rather its first is its last, and its first and its middle are the utmost of its utmost, and its utmost is its middle, there being in it no order and no arrangement; but the order and the arrangement are in that which the second gains, from the lights of Him in whom there is no arrangement and no order, and He is the First Intellect; and the Soul comes to resemble the Intellect greatly, so that when she looks into it toward Him she apprehends the powers of things that are without order and without arrangement, apprehending things rather all at once, and this belongs to her only when she is united with the Intellect and has driven back the material things of dark nature:

We say:

How can what he said be sound, when order and arrangement are found only from the Intellect? And how can it be sound, when the things taken all at once are made excellent only by the Intellect, and the Soul, in which the effects of the Intellect leave no impress, is on a level with the beasts? It is for this reason that Allah has lifted knowledge from the Soul until the time when the effects of the Intellect leave their impress upon her. We have already said above that the proposition concerning doers is not universal, so that not every doer seeks by his act to attain a want or a purpose. What I reckon he intended by that saying is that the essence of the First Intellect is not composite, so as to have arrangement and order. But as for apprehension, the Intellect apprehends only by a true apprehension.

## 12. Body, Form, and the Two Letters of 'Be'

Here I speak of motion and stillness, of hyle and of form, and of how the two authors before me have reasoned about them.

**Section One**

The author of al-Islah said:

As for the discourse on hyle and form, they are the spirit of motion and stillness, just as the body is to them. The first hyle is the imagined motion within composite things, and the hyles, taken all together, are three. What has been said on this matter in the present book carries much confusion, and so I will state it in brief. Motion and stillness stand to hyle and form as the soul stands to the body, because hyle and form are to motion and stillness as the body is to them. The soul is subtler than the body, and the body denser than the soul; and just so, motion and stillness are subtler than hyle and form, while hyle and form are denser than motion and stillness. To represent the subtler by the soul and the denser by the body is more fitting than to represent the denser by the body and the subtler by the soul. This is clear, and its proof lies with it.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The case of soul and body, and the measure taken between them, does not rest upon the subtle and the dense alone. For were the matter as he has said, then the perceptible substances would have to be bodies for the accidents, since they are denser than the accidents, and the accidents would have to be souls for those substances, since they are subtler than them. Rather, we are obliged to look to the definition of body and the definition of soul, and then to liken whatever resembles the definition of the soul to the soul, and whatever resembles the definition of the body to the body. So we say: we found the body to be that which receives the three dimensions, namely length, width, and depth; and we found the soul to be that whose parts resemble one another, near enough. In the same way we found that motion is divided into three divisions, as the body is divided, namely motion from the center, motion toward the center, and motion around the center. For this reason motion must resemble the body when it is divided, splitting as the body splits into length, width, and depth. Hyle is not so, for the parts of hyle resemble one another. And because it is more fitting to liken motion to the body when the two are alike in the matter of division, and to liken hyle to the soul when the parts are alike, than to liken motion to the soul, one of the two, namely divided motion, resembles the body, and the other is the body that is not divided; while hyle resembles the soul, being undivided, and the other is the body that is divided. This is what they said.

We say:

In this section the two of them have gone far astray in their representation and their likening. They have both built upon a corrupt foundation on which no proof can stand, so that neither the likeness nor the thing likened to it can be made to hold. The author of al-Islah has laid it down that motion and stillness are as the soul to hyle and form, and this is impossible. For motion is the act of the mover, and the mover is what we call the soul, the self; and stillness, likewise, is the mover's leaving off the moving of what it moves. If, then, the mover is the soul, to represent motion, which is its act, by the soul is an impossible representation, and the same holds for stillness. And when this is shown to be void for motion and stillness, it is shown void as well that motion and stillness should be hyle and form. Moreover, the way of hyle and form is not the way of motion and stillness. For motion and stillness, though each is an act, are two contraries that do not meet in a single place, in a single state, at a single time; whereas hyle and form exist together, neither parting from the other, save that one of them is the agent, which is the form, and the other is that which is acted upon within it, which is the hyle. Our well-known book The Rest of the Mind (راحة العقل) gathers the explanation of this.

**Section Two**

Then he was led to say that the soul is subtler than the body, on the ground that the soul is a body, and this is impossible. The soul cannot be a body such that density or subtlety should attach to it, for density and subtlety belong only to that which is a body, as air, which is a body, is marked by subtlety, and earth, which is a body, is marked by density. The soul is the self, and the proofs have been established that it is not a body. And if the self is not a body, then it is impossible to argue that it is subtler or denser, or to represent it by such terms.

**Section Three**

The author of al-Nusrah came forward and promised to set the definition of body and of soul. He defined the body by its receiving the three dimensions, and defined the soul by the likeness of its parts to one another, drawing it out in the manner of water, fire, and air, each of which is alike in its parts. This is impossible, in view of what has already been said, that the soul is not a body, so that this cannot attach to it. The soul and the self are two synonymous names for one thing, and that thing is not a body. For were it a body, there would attach to it, of the nine qualities and accidents, what attaches to a body, and it could be grasped by a sense. But since it is not grasped by a sense, it cannot be perceived, and the nine accidents are neither imagined in it nor present in it; and since the bodily accidents are not present in it, it is void that it should be a body. Our well-known book The Lamps (المصابيح) holds of this what heals.

**Section Four**

Then he made motion, by its division into three sections, resemble the body, in the way he has mentioned, that the body is divided into length, width, and depth; and this is impossible. For the three sections into which he alleges the body is divided, which are the three dimensions, each one of these three dimensions is not a body, nor is it called a body; whereas each section of motion is itself a motion, and is called a motion. How, then, can the body, which is divided by form into nine sections, as we have shown in our well-known book Milestones of Religion (معالم الدين), be likened to motion, which is divided into three sections, or into six? That is far-fetched indeed. The discourse in this chapter, from the author of al-Mahsul, the author of al-Islah, and the author of al-Nusrah, is confused and without order.

We say:

Motion is that by which the very thing is changed, whether by place, or by coming-to-be, or by passing-away, or by increase, or by decrease, or by alteration; and its occurrence is from the mover, which is the principle within the thing in which it resides, by nature and not by accident. Hyle and form are what emanates from the First by the lower relation within it, as the second emanates by the nobler relation within it. Hyle has no existence outside the soul at all, except together with form; and from it are the heavens and the earth and what is between them. God, exalted, brought all of this into being from the First Intellect, which He originated not from any thing. Exalted be God with a great exaltation.

**Section Five**

The author of al-Islah said:

The First Cause, which is origination, and which the learned name the word of the Maker, mighty and exalted, has for its form:

> كن

"Be." This is two letters, a كاف (kaf) in motion and a نون (nun) at rest. The كاف and the نون of the word stand in the place of the body, while motion and stillness stand in the place of the soul. On this pattern the likeness lies in motion and stillness as the two things effected by the word, and the First Cause, which is the word, is the hyle of the whole world. On this same pattern it must be said of motion and stillness in all substances, from hyle and form down to what is below them, throughout all the worlds. The sum of the discourse on the three hyles is this:

1. The first hyle is imaginary, and it is motion and stillness; it is the very substance of hyle and its form.
2. The second hyle comprises the individuals and the things generated from the first hyle.
3. The third hyle is the four composite mothers derived from the second hyle.

Then we say:

Hyle and its form are the act of the command, which is origination. Just as we have said that hyle is imaginary, so too origination is imaginary: its essence appears only in the First Originated.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

As for the ground the author of al-Islah took in كن, saying that the كاف is in motion and the نون at rest, and that this stands in the place of the body while motion and stillness stand for it in the place of the soul, this is not granted to him. These two letters are used to speak of the matter here only because there is, at that level, a كاف, a نون, a motion, or a stillness; for it cannot be said that above the First Effect there is motion or stillness or anything of this manner of speech. Rather, there is only pure origination and the complete cause. This is what they say.

We say:

The saying of the author of al-Islah, that the First Cause, which is origination, is a word of God, exalted, and that its form is these two letters, is a remote analogy in the way he intended it, and it scarcely holds. For our saying "form" requires something for which it is a form, and the word has nothing for which its form would be a form. When we say "form," meaning what is above nature, we mean the abstract form; and when we say "form," meaning what is in the natural world, we mean the form that is in matter. Had he said instead that origination, which is the word of God, exalted, and His command, as expressed by كن, is two letters, one in motion and the other at rest, and that this points to an essence gathering two attributes, then he would have drawn from it a proof of that noble Essence and its attributes.

He said:

It gathers two states, just as the word كن gathers two states, motion and stillness. He then referred the two states to what belongs to that Essence of the two noble relations, one of which is its being a cause, the other its being an effect. He set the motion whose end is stillness against the relation which is the cause whose end is the effect, and the stillness whose end is motion against the relation which is the effect whose end is the cause, not because there is motion or stillness there, but only by way of making the likeness understood. Had it been so, it would have been more fitting, and what he said would have met the very state of the thing spoken of, which is the thing likened by the likeness.

**Section Six**

Then he said:

The first hyle is imaginary, and he made it motion and stillness; then he made motion and stillness one with hyle, and form is not hyle, but rather motion and stillness, they are hyle and form. For hyle is a substance emanating from the First, in the manner already discussed, and from it is the lower world. As for motion and stillness, their existence is in bodies according to the principles from which their appearing comes; and hyle and form together are the body, the body's being lying in hyle and form.

**Section Seven**

Then he said:

When he made the first hyle out of the matter which is origination, saying that the first hyle is imaginary, then origination is imaginary, its essence appearing only in the First Originated. His saying entails that there existed, from eternity, together with the Maker, exalted be His majesty above that, something in which origination appeared, so that by origination it became originated; and this is a grave error. For the doctrine and the belief concerning the divine unity is that whatever is other than God, exalted, is originated and brought to be in time, having no reality in existence at all; and if that is so, then origination is nothing but the Originated. We have established the proof of this in what has preceded of our epistles, in a measure that suffices.

**Section Eight**

As for the saying of the author of al-Nusrah:

In what he stated, and in the way he opposed the author of al-Islah with it, it is correct, except that his saying that it should not be said that above the First Effect there is motion and stillness is an affirming of them in the First Effect and in what is below it. Yet we have shown that the First Effect is not in motion, that motion does not befit it, nor stillness either, since it is other than a body.

**Section Nine**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Contradiction is in bodies, and there is no contradiction in souls.

We say:

Bodies, insofar as they are bodies, have no difference among them and no contradiction. Difference and disparity fall only in the forms that are within them, the forms that move them, not in respect of their being bodies.

And his saying:

That it is more fitting to liken that in which contradiction is found to what is likewise, the matter is as he said, if the very thing spoken of is such.

## 13. The Small World

The Small World

Here I set myself to judge between the two, and I speak now, in the sections that follow, of the divisions of the world.

**The First Section**

The author of al-Islah said:

The whole world is composed of four jewels, four structures, and four classes of beings: three of them without humankind, the fourth being humankind. The knotted and the dissolved are all one essence. We do not say that the pair of humankind is the form of the horse; rather, the pair of the horse and its form is what belongs to the horse's own essence, just as gold is paired with silver, and ruby with emerald, so too the horse with its like. And the horse's likeness lies among the animals, in the elephant, the falcon, the lion, and the rest. Since all of these are animals, it is not permissible to say that the horse is the pair and form of humankind, for humankind is alone in possessing the rational soul and has no peer among the parts of the world and its generated beings. Rather, humankind is the counterpart of the world, because it gathers within itself all the jewels of the world. For this reason it is named the small world, since it stands as a counterpart that collects all the world's parts.

We say:

His claim that humankind has no peer among the parts of the world, and that humankind is the counterpart of the whole world, follows from his saying that the world is divided into four parts, that the horse is not in the form of humans, and that humans have no peer among the world's parts but are the equivalent of the whole world.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The state of pairing is not only the state of union; the state of pairing is also the state of acceptance.

We say:

This dense lower world is the pair of the subtle upper world, and if you look between the two you will find the distance between them greater than the distance between the horse and the human. The horse is not of the human race except insofar as it shares in the genus of life. He made this dense lower world a pair of the spiritual world by virtue of the connection between them in the matter of benefit and beneficence: the forces of the upper world are joined to this world, and this world receives those forces from the upper world. For this he made this world the pair of that world. Likewise the human and the horse: he made the horse the human's pair because it accepts teaching from him more than the other animals accept it. This is the substance of his saying, that the horse was made a pair for humankind because it accepts teaching from him as no other animal does.

We say:

The two who investigated these matters did not rely upon sound research. The author of al-Islah divided the world into four jewels, four structures, and four classes including humankind, and that is impossible, for the divisions of the world are more numerous than what he set down. The word "world" names many things, from the center to the encompassing ocean: the sphere, the planet, fire, air, water, earth, and the generated beings. From the plain sense of his words it is clear that he means the generated beings of the world, not the world and its generated beings together. These are three genera, with no fourth, and under each of them fall many species. The first is the nearest of the four elements, and the first of it is the minerals, which are everything whose existence is confined to one place in the earth rather than another without its parts increasing; and this gathers together what melts and what does not, what can be beaten out and what cannot. The second is the plant, which is all that grows from the earth, and this gathers together the herb and the tree. The third is the animal, which is all that moves among those that have species, and this gathers together what flies and what does not. The generated beings of the world do not exceed this, and the human, whom he named the fourth, is of the genus of animal, a species among its species, set apart by consciousness.

**The Second Section**

He said:

That humankind has no peer and that the horse has no pair. This is impossible from two angles. The first is that the human is a species within the genus of animal, and insofar as it is a species it is a pair to the other species of its genus, since all species are forms of one another in respect of their being species. If humankind is a species, then it is a pair to the other species. The second is that humankind, being similar in its structure, morals, actions, conditions, motions, and natures to all the beings that preceded it in existence, is for that very reason called the small world, and so must be a pair to all things according to the correspondence and likeness within it. If this is so, then humankind, when it is good, virtuous, wise, religious, knowing, and pure, is married to the angels by the correspondence between them; when it is courageous, brave, bold, and ready to help, its pair is the lions; when it is unjust, cruel, and murderous, its pair is the wolves; when it is deceitful, its pair is the foxes; when it is a thief and a traitor, its pair is the crows, the magpies, and the cats; when it possesses guile, cunning, and force, its pair is the leopard; and when it is given to fornication, immorality, amusement, and play, its pair is the monkeys. Upon this pattern, whatever a person trains himself to resemble he comes to resemble, whether by his actions, his morals, his customs, or in any manner of his ways, and it becomes a pair to him. If this likeness is established, then it is plain that his saying is not exhausted where he claimed that humankind has no peer, for humankind has forms and pairs from many sides.

**The Third Section**

As for the author of al-Nusrah, he spoke rightly regarding the meaning of the genus, yet he erred in his saying that the horse was made a pair for humankind because it accepts the teaching of humankind. For if it becomes a pair by way of acceptance and teaching, then the horse has no greater claim than any other to be its pair, since among the animals there are those besides the horse that accept teaching and discipline; and if it is by way of species, then again the horse has no greater claim than the rest of the animal species to be its pair. In that case it has many pairs, not one alone.

In sum we say:

The pair of humankind, in truth, can only be the angels. And what we mean by "humankind" here are those possessed of all the virtues, such as the prophets, the guardians, and the imams (عليهم السلام), and those who follow in their footsteps, who uphold their obedience and worship Allah with both acts of worship. Not those beneath them among the people who are in the form of humankind while their souls are the souls of beasts and predators, as we have said and shown, like the monkeys, the donkeys, the foxes, the beasts of prey, and the wolves. Those who follow the traces of the Imams (عليهم السلام), and above them the prophets and the guardians (عليهم السلام), are distinguished from the beasts by their striving in obedience through the two acts of worship and their holding fast to command and prohibition, though they resemble the beasts in form. In this way they come to resemble the guardians (عليهم السلام), and so they are saved in their company and among them and none other. This accords with the verse:

> وَمَنْ يَتَوَلَّهُمْ مِنْكُمْ فَإِنَّهُ مِنْهُمْ

"And whoever takes them as guardian from among you, he is one of them." May Allah place us among those whose gathering and raising is with the prophets, the guardians, and the pure Imams (عليهم السلام).

**The Fourth Section**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

He mentioned Bandhaqlis, who said:

The bestial soul returns to the rational soul through love; and the rational soul, when it deems good what returns to it from her, inclines toward her with benefit and mastery and finds her image beautiful. There is no image among the bestial souls more beautiful than the image of the horse, nor any more disposed to receive the traces of the rational soul. For this he made it a pair for it. This is plain.

We say:

Had the author of al-Nusrah drawn his evidence from the Book of Allah, Lord of the Worlds, and from the words of our masters the pure Imams (عليهم السلام), for what he intends concerning the matter of the soul by way of interpretation, what is found there would have freed him from clinging to the words of Bandhaqlis. For had he and his like among the ancients lived in our time, they would have needed to draw from the seas of the knowledge of the House of Revelation, overflowing from the treasuries of Allah in His earth, and from our pure masters (عليهم السلام); and they would have turned back from much of what they believed concerning what is hidden from the senses, of which they spoke, the intellects and their ranks, the soul and its principles, and believed otherwise. For what Bandhaqlis intended in his saying is other than what the author of al-Nusrah took it to be, namely that the bestial soul is the horse and the rational soul is humankind, once it is returned to the balance of consideration and taken upon the face that is sound. Rather, since the sensory soul, which is the bestial one, is such that it accepts the ruling of the rational soul when no obstacle hinders it, and the rational soul, when it sees the other accepting its ruling, inclines toward it with benefit and sets it right, we say: by "the sensory soul" he means whoever is in the form of humankind, whose nature it is to accept teaching, discipline, and reason, and not the horse; and by "the rational soul" he means the teachers, who are the prophets, the guardians, the imams (عليهم السلام), and their followers, whose nature it is to incline toward whoever is in the form of humankind, to teach them their interests in their religion and their world, to benefit them in their right guidance, and to raise them in degrees according to their acceptance, they being in truth humankind, and not every one of the people. This agrees with what is indicated by the interpretation of the saying of Allah Almighty:

> يَا أَيَّتُهَا النَّفْسُ الْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ ارْجِعِي إِلَى رَبِّكِ رَاضِيَةً مَرْضِيَّةً

"O reassured soul, return to your Lord, pleased and pleasing" (the verse). And to carry his statement upon the face that is sound is preferable to carrying it upon the face that is not sound. The horse is no more accepting than one in the form of humankind, nor is its image better than his image; nor are the guardians, the imams, and their followers less compassionate toward those below them among some of the people than men are in the sport and gentling of the horse; rather, they are far more so. And since this is so, and there is nothing in the plain words of Bandhaqlis to show that there is no image among the bestial souls more beautiful than the image of the horse, nor is the case, among the animal images that exist, such as to surpass the beauty of the horse's image, nor is the matter as he said, then to single out the horse to be the pair of humankind on the side of teaching and beauty of image, and to argue by it, is impossible.

**The Fifth Section**

The author of al-Islah said:

Humankind is the counterpart of the whole world and is preferred to it by a noble essence that is not of this world. His body stands opposite all bodies in their genera, the solid and the dissolved, because in his body there is something corresponding to each genus of them, such as bone, flesh, nerve, veins, and cartilage, and all the flowing substances that issue from his body, such as blood, semen, and sweat, and what flows from the eye, the ear, and the mouth, and the rest of his interior. All of these are of the essence of bodies and of their kinds, and they correspond to the kinds of the essence that is one of the three essences of the world, namely the essence of bodies, which is not united with any of the three souls. His growth stands opposite the growing kinds among the species of trees in their varieties, and this is of the kinds of the second essence, united with the growing soul. His sense stands opposite the animal in its differing kinds, and this is of the kinds of the third essence, united with the sensory soul, the bestial soul. This is his correspondence to the three jewels of the world, and he stands alone in the fourth rank, so that he became the fourth essence, which is the essence of the rational soul, alone in this station. The substance of his saying is that humankind is the counterpart of the whole world, that his body stands opposite all the bodies of the world in the difference of their forms, his growth opposite the plants, his sense opposite the animal, and that he possesses that by which he surpasses all and stands alone, which is the rational soul that is his.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

As for his saying that the dissolved and the contracted are one essence, that is not to be rejected. Rather, the dissolved, the contracted, the growing, the sensory, and the rational are all one essence in respect of being essence; but in respect of their divisions and properties, the contracted essence is not the dissolved essence, and the dissolved is not the contracted. They are distinguished only in the locus where they are distinguished, not in the locus where they are not distinguished. He needed to distinguish between them only because he made the human a mediator between the dense bodily world and the subtle spiritual world. For in him is all that is in the two worlds, and so everything in the lower world is likened to him, until nothing of it is left out. He therefore set the contracted bodies opposite his body, his body likewise being contracted, as the bodies are contracted; then he set the dissolved bodies opposite his growth, on account of the dissolution within them, for dissolution occurs only in the motion of the jewels that dissolve, and likewise growth occurs only by motion, so that when the two resemble each other in respect of motion, each of them is likened to its companion. He set the growing bodies opposite his sense, and set the animal opposite his speech. So the remainder of the world's bodies, contracted, dissolved, growing, and sensory, is gathered in him, and he surpasses the rest of the jewels by speech. The substance of his saying is that humankind resembles the world until nothing of it is left out, for his body stands opposite the contracted bodies, his growth opposite the dissolved bodies, his sense opposite the growing bodies, and his speech opposite the animal, and that he surpasses by speech all the rest of the jewels.

We say:

The disagreement between the two in this section is that the author of al-Islah set, opposite the human's growth, the plant of the greater world that grows and develops, while the author of al-Nusrah set opposite it the dissolved bodies; and the author of al-Islah set, opposite the human's sense, the animal of the greater world, while the author of al-Nusrah set opposite his sense the growing bodies; and the author of al-Islah made the rational soul the very thing by which the human is set apart, preferred above the world, and made its master, while the author of al-Nusrah set that opposite the animal. The saying of the author of al-Islah is the closer, since what he likens is of the genus of that to which it is likened, and it has a conformity that the saying of the author of al-Nusrah does not have. In sum, the human is not the counterpart of this world only, but the counterpart of both worlds together, being the collector of all the things that preceded him in existence and, by what is in him, resembling both of them. The matter concerning him is upon this, and not upon what he said.

**The Sixth Section**

We said that the human, who is humankind, is the counterpart of the two worlds because he gathered the multiplicity existing between origination and the second emanation, then became one, so that in himself he became parallel to the world of origination and emanation, and by his body, the collector of its parts, parallel to the bodily world, and so parallel to both worlds. As for his resemblance to this world: the world is entirely body, of it the subtle, which is that which is high, and of it the dense, which is that which is low; and the human body is likewise subtle and dense, the subtle being that which is high and the dense that which is low. In the world there are planets and encompassing spheres, and in the human body likewise there is the skin encompassing the whole body and the rest that clothes the bone; and what runs in the course of the planets, such as the two ears, the two eyes, the nose, and the mouth. In the human there is likewise a mover that moves, which is the soul. In the world there are the four elements, and in the human likewise the four natures. In the world there are rivers, valleys, and waters of differing tastes, and mountains and minerals, and in the human likewise, as the author of al-Islah set forth, the bones and cartilage, the flesh and the blood, and what flows from him and what issues out. In the world there is what grows and develops without sensing, and in the human likewise there is what grows and develops without sensing, such as the hair and the nails, and his body grows and increases from its smallness to its greatness. In the world there is the sensing animal, and likewise in the human the sensory faculties. The whole world is structures and compositions, and by what exists in the human the resemblance to the world is complete, though all that we have set down here in summary is detailed in our book known as The Peace of Mind. As for his resemblance to the world of origination and the first emanation: that world is the world of intellect, the world of glorification, praise, and purification, the world of unity and deification, the world of virtues, the world of generosity, and the world of bounties. And the human, who is humankind, is likewise the collector of these virtues, in intellect and glorification, in unity and deification, and in bounty and generosity toward others and in himself. That world has marked him, and by his body he has resembled this world; and being thus, he came to be distinguished from those that preceded him in existence by rank alone. May Allah make us among those who follow the traces of His friends, and let us not be excluded from the intercession of His chosen ones, by His grace and His bounties.

**The Seventh Section**

And the saying of the author of al-Islah, that humankind is preferred over the world by the noble essence that is not of this world, is not to say that the essence which he denied to be of this world is of that world, I mean the high and noble upper world, according to what was disclosed to him. Rather, since it is neither of that world only nor of this world only, we said that it is of both worlds together. For his state in existence between the two worlds is like the state of the child in his existence between his two parents: it is not permissible to attribute him to one of them apart from the other, since it is by them both together, and from them, that he came to be. What may be attributed of him to this world is that which runs in him in the course of matter, which is the receptive; and what may be attributed of him to that world is that which runs in him in the course of form, which is the received, as with the child, who has from the side of his two parents, the higher and the nobler, his form, and from the other side, which is below that, the receptive matter. The proofs of this are many, found in our book known as The Peace of Mind. And Allah Almighty grants us success toward the abode of His religion, and provides us the intercession of Muhammad His prophet, and the Imams of his pure family, and his household (عليهم السلام).

## 14. Decree and Destiny

Chapter Eight of the Book of Riyad: On the Judgment between the Two Contenders, of which he spoke in the chapter of Decree and Destiny.

**Section One of Chapter Eight**

The author of al-Islah said:

As for the saying that the decree is prior and the destiny posterior, this is an error, because destiny is before decree, and the prior is before the posterior. It is not permissible that the decree, which comes after destiny, should be identified with that which is before the posterior. Decree and destiny are two words whose meaning is known from ordinary speech: destiny is estimation, and decree is elaboration, and there is no elaboration except after estimation, so the first is before the second by the farthest reach of priority. The witnesses to this from the Book of God are His words:

قضي الامر الذي به تستفتيان
"The matter concerning which you two sought counsel has been decreed."

meaning that it has been separated out and brought to an end; and His words:

فاذا قضيت الصلاة
"When you have finished the prayer,"

meaning, when you have completed it. The decree is like a garment that the tailor first estimates: before he cuts it he estimates it, and he adds and subtracts, widens and narrows; but once he has cut it he has decreed it and made an end of it, and after that no increase in it is possible. That is the likeness of decree and destiny. The upshot of his saying is this: decree is not predicated of the prior, for it comes after destiny; and destiny is not predicated of the posterior, for it comes before decree. Destiny is estimation and decree is elaboration, and as long as the thing remains within estimation it can be increased, decreased, and widened, but once it has passed into elaboration and been decreed, no increase in it and no decrease from it is possible.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Decree is not elaboration, for the state of the very act of elaboration within the decree is no less than the state of the separated thing itself, since the separated thing is fixed upon its form by a decree that requires this, just as the separated thing is likewise established only by what has been decreed for it. As for destiny, it is estimation, as he has said, except that there is no estimation save by a judgment that requires that estimation. As for the witnesses he cited from the Book of God to the effect that decree is a bringing to completion, these are not to be dismissed, for the sages have said: when God, exalted be He, originated the Intellect, He brought the two worlds to completion, since the Intellect is the sum of the images of the two worlds, and nothing of the images of the worlds is absent from the Intellect, so it is pure completion. Yet the author of al-Islah has erred in likening destiny and decree to the garment that the tailor estimates. He made the estimation correspond to destiny and the cutting correspond to the decree, and this analogy does not stand upright. What he ought to have done is to make the image of the sewing that resides in the very soul of the tailor correspond to the decree, because it is spiritual; and the estimation by which the tailor estimates that sewing correspond to destiny, for that too is a spiritual thing that has not yet emerged to the limit of intellect, and this is the meaning of the image of the two origins. Then the cutting which he performs after estimation, upon the bodily boundaries that have emerged from the first and the second, corresponds to what appears in body. If the matter is as we have described, then decree precedes destiny, just as the sewing precedes the estimation, and by that sewing the tailor is made ready for the estimation by which he estimates. So it is established that decree is prior and destiny is posterior. This is the upshot of his saying:

Decree is ahead of destiny, and there is no estimation except by a judgment, so decree is prior and destiny posterior.

We say:

The interpretation the two contenders give of decree and destiny, each according to what he has invoked, is an impossible interpretation, even granting the meaning of the two words as we have shown it, in whatever way they assign decree and destiny to the prior and the posterior. For the author of al-Islah has committed himself, by his saying that destiny is estimation and decree is elaboration, and by the analogy he brought forward in the matter of the garment and the image of the sewing, to holding that destiny is what stands in potency, what could yet be, and that decree is what stands in act, what has already come out from the door of possibility, which is potency, into actuality. Now if this were the meaning of destiny and decree, then it is impossible to predicate them of the prior and the posterior at all, in any respect, since neither of the two stands in potency, nor was in potency and then emerged into act, according to the proofs we have already set down.

**Section Two of Chapter Eight**

The author of al-Nusrah has committed himself, by his saying that there is no estimation except by a judgment that requires that estimation, and by his representing the decree with the craft of sewing from which the estimation proceeds, to making destiny posterior, while yet conceding that the meaning of destiny is, as the author of al-Islah established it, the existence of an existent by potency. But it is not permissible to predicate this of the posterior, since it has been established from what we said above that the posterior already stands in act; and if it were permissible, then destiny would not be posterior. And if destiny is not the posterior, then decree has all the more claim to be the prior, and is even further from being predicated of it, since the posterior was never in potency such that it then went out into act to answer to what its own word conveys of the state of the prior. It is likewise impossible that decree and destiny be predicated of the prior and the posterior. And since this is impossible, we say that the statement of the two men is correct only in the manner we shall now explain, not in the manner to which they went, for decree shares its meaning with several things, and those things all belong to the world of body, not to the world of origination; and likewise destiny.

**Section Three of Chapter Eight**

As for decree, it points, first, to the Speaker who was in potency and then emerged into act by the support of God, exalted be He, and to those who take his place (عليهم السلام), who have reached in their attainment the rank parallel to the first upsurge. All of these, in their standing in act, in their command, prohibition, and decree within the summons that is already established in act, having been in potency, answer to the meaning conveyed by the word "decree" (عليهم السلام). This is what the author of al-Nusrah intends, and upon this his saying and his placing of decree first are sound.

**Section Four of Chapter Eight**

Decree points, second, to what is drawn out by interpretation from the potency of the revelation and the Law, from the sciences that are not set forth explicitly within them but reside within them in potency, into exposition and act, by which souls are brought out from the limit of potency to the limit of act. This is what the author of al-Islah intends by his interpretation of the word "decree."

**Section Five of Chapter Eight**

Decree points, third, to what emerges into act and to the latent unfolding of events and transformations appointed to arise in the world through the movement of the moving things and the stillness of the still, those that admit of no reversal. For this reason it is said, when some matter comes to pass and is realized in act, that there is no repelling the decree of God. These are the things of which decree is predicated.

**Section Six of Chapter Eight**

As for destiny, it points, first, to the promised Qaim, foretold and heralded from the time of Adam (ع), and to his summons, whose safekeeping and upholding are borne by the Imams and the Messengers (عليهم السلام) through their summoners in the period of concealment, holding out its force in that he is appointed to come forth from estimation into act and existence. His summons, though established, stands from the side of the ranks of the cycle of the Prophet (ع) and his family in the concealment that runs in the manner of what is standing in potency, not in the manner of the manifest that runs as what is standing in act. For this reason the inner meaning of the Night of Determination, Laylat al-Qadr (ع), is he, and to him point the allusions of the shaykhs in their saying:

كوني قدر
"Be, O destiny."

**Section Seven of Chapter Eight**

Destiny points, second, to what the Prophet (ع) and his family brought from the revelation, the Law, the rulings, and the parables that hold within their potency the sciences and the knowledge the soul needs in its passage to maturity and act, being cast in a form that gathers all of that together. For this reason no complete science is found upon its outer face except through interpretation drawn out of it, and the saying of the author of al-Islah in this direction points to this meaning of destiny.

**Section Eight of Chapter Eight**

Destiny points, third, to the world with all its parts, which has within its power to come forth into being and act through the passage of days, months, years, events, and the coming-to-be whose existence proceeds from it by the perpetual movement of the movers over what is beneath them, of necessity, since these are appointed within it. For this reason it is said: what God has appointed will be, for its coming-to-be is by way of the necessity of its existence; and though the term be long, it is as one already existing in act. So God, exalted be He, said:

إِنَّهُمْ يَرَوْنَهُۥ بَعِيدًۭا وَنَرَاهُ قَرِيبًا
"They see it as far off, but We see it as near."

Whatever was in estimation, appointed to exist, is decree once it exists, since the word "decree" signals what was in potency and then came out into act. These are the meanings of decree and destiny.

If one ponders the words of the author of al-Islah, and his saying that destiny precedes decree, the soundness of its meaning holds only with respect to what the Prophet (ع) and his family brought of the revelation and the Law, which precedes the decree that is the interpretation, as he himself did and interpreted. And the saying of the author of al-Nusrah, that decree precedes destiny, if it is pondered, its meaning is sound only with respect to the Speaker (ع) who stands in act, who is the decree that precedes what he brought of the revelation and the Law, which is destiny.

**Section Nine of Chapter Eight**

The author of al-Nusrah said, reporting from the learned sages:

When God, exalted be He, originated the Intellect, He brought the two worlds to completion, because it is the gathering place of the images of the worlds.

We say:

The Intellect that is the gathering place of the images of the worlds is not the First originated Intellect, nor the second Intellect, which is the second upsurge; rather it is the Intellect that ascends from the world of nature by acquisition, in deed and in knowledge, to the parallel of the first upsurge, so that it surges a second surging and thereby becomes a compendium of the images of the worlds in all their multitude, its very essence imprinted with the forms of all existents, as is the case with the Enunciators, the Executors, and the Imams (عليهم السلام). The First Intellect is too exalted to be multiplied by this kind of multiplication, since its existence is originative, and originative existence is multiplied only according to what it intellects of its own essence, namely that it is a cause and an effect, and that its existence is only from that which is beyond itself, from which it received. The remaining existents, I mean the Intellects that surge in a first surging, are multiplied according to what I have said of their essences and of that whose existence precedes them, back to the First Intellect, their existence being, together with this, from that toward which they turned in devotion, which is beyond them. So the later in existence is not multiplied as the prior in existence is multiplied. Its need to be multiplied for the sake of intellecting all existents is a binding necessity, whereas the First in existence is entirely free of that need in the completeness of its being. If this is so, then it is clear that the one which is a compendium of the images of the two worlds is not the First Intellect, but the Intellects that surge from the abode of nature in a second surging.

Then we say:

The First Intellect does not require that it intellect what is beneath it, nor that it form any image, by reason of what its essence is from eternity: completeness, subsistence, unity, and immunity from change. Rather it is so only by reason of that from which it came to be. And since its being is eternal, complete, subsistent, one and singular, immune from transformation, only by reason of that from which it came to be, exalted be He, it is impossible that it intellect anything beneath it, for it has no need of that.

And we likewise say:

The one that is later in existence, from the second surging, is required to intellect what precedes it in existence and so to become a compendium of the images, because the completeness in which its subsistence lies it acquires by its intellecting of that, I mean of what precedes it in existence. And since it needs, for its completeness, its intellecting of that whose existence preceded it among the ranks upon whom the interpretive summons was established, which is the second worship, so that their stations might be made plain, it is required to teach them and to intellect them. And when it teaches them and intellects them, its essence is thereby multiplied from the side of the multitude of its intellected objects, and is unified from the other side, which is its own essence, so that in that unity it resembles what precedes it in existence. Our book known as راحة العقل, The Peace of the Intellect, contains of the evidences of legal proof for this, and of the religious depictions, what suffices and heals the seeker for the sake of his return. May God make us among those who follow His good pleasure in whatever brings distress or delight, and who follow His friends (عليهم السلام) therein.

**Section Ten of Chapter Eight**

The author of al-Islah said:

It is only the error of those who made decree the prior and destiny the posterior. For they found that the learned had named the posterior by the name of destiny, which is indeed its true name, because all the essences surged from the prior in an appointed measure, and that surging was destiny itself, so the sages named it destiny. Then in the speech of the people "decree and destiny" came to be uttered together, and they were found to place decree ahead of destiny in speech, so that one says "this is decree and destiny." The meaning of decree and destiny was hidden from them, so they assigned decree to the first and destiny to the second, and from this side the error occurred. This is as we said: that all the essences surged from the prior in an appointed measure, and that surging was destiny itself, and it is the compendium of the essences. Confirming this is the saying of God, exalted be He:

إِنَّا كُلَّ شَىْءٍ خَلَقْنَهُ بِقَدَرٍۢ
"Indeed, We have created everything according to a measure."

For just as all the essences surged from the First, which is the command in appointed potency, and were destiny itself, so likewise there appeared from the second, in act, out of matter and form, and that act was decree itself. And within destiny there occurs change and alteration, and it is the book of which God, exalted be He, said:

يَمْحُوا۟ ٱللَّهُ مَا يَشَآءُ وَيُثْبِتُ
"Allah erases what He wills and confirms."

Destiny is the essence of the posterior, as we have said, and decree is its act, because the posterior is what is appointed in measure from the prior. The upshot of his saying in this section is: destiny is the name of the posterior, because all the essences surged from the prior in an appointed measure, as God, exalted be He, said, "Indeed, We have created everything according to a measure," and decree is its act.

The author of al-Islah said:

As for what he invoked concerning decree and destiny from the saying of God, exalted be He, "Indeed, We have created everything according to a measure," he has erred in the interpretation of this verse. Its interpretation is as we shall expound and state, for by it He intends only the pure Ipseity.

We say:

The meaning of إِنَّا, "We," by which He intends the Ipseity, is that which no attribute and no negation of attribute taints; and "everything," كُلَّ شَىْءٍ, by which He intends the Intellect, which is all things; and "We have created it," خَلَقْنَهُ, by which He intends the image of the two worlds among them; and "according to a measure," بِقَدَرٍ, by which He intends the posterior, upon which the settling of the matter rests, so that all the spiritual and bodily images appeared from between the two in the manifest, which is the pen, the tablet, the writing, and the Writer. The Writer stands over against إِنَّا, "We," and He is the pure Ipseity; the Pen stands over against the Intellect, which is the pen of pens; "We have created it" stands over against the Tablet; the writing stands over against the letters that appear upon the Tablet; and "according to a measure" stands over against the Tablet that is receptive to all the forms of the letters. For just as all bodily and spiritual things, the simple and the dense, fall under these intelligible letters that appear from the Writer, by the Pen, upon the Tablet, so likewise all the higher and lower images God, exalted be He, brought forth by means of the Intellect and the Soul, and nothing of them was withheld from between the two, just as nothing of things is withheld from the ordered letters that appear in act from the Writer upon the Tablet. This is the saying of the author of al-Nusrah in refutation.

We say:

The two contenders have brought forward in this section what cannot stand firm, neither for this one in his emendation, nor for that one in his refutation. For the author of al-Islah, when he held that destiny is the name of the posterior and argued for it by the saying of God, exalted be He, "Indeed, We have created everything according to a measure," has already been met by what preceded in the discussion of destiny and decree, from which it is grasped that destiny may not be predicated of the posterior. And the author of al-Nusrah made his counter to the author of al-Islah's saying rest upon what he interpreted from the verse, and this is neither balanced against nor congruent with the matter. For he said, interpreting:

God, exalted be He, by إِنَّا, "We," intends only the pure Ipseity that no attribute and no negation of attribute taints. But the word إِنَّا, "We," is only at the limit from whose side the Prophet draws supply by his standing in the divine place within the world, not the pure Ipseity itself. And how could "We" fall upon the pure Ipseity, when between Him and it lies the intermediary of utter negation? Rather it falls upon the near intermediary of the Messenger of God (ع) in the outpouring upon him, and the meaning is the Messenger, since he is the one who conveys on His behalf.

**Section Eleven of Chapter Eight**

He said:

"Everything," by which he intends the Intellect, which is all things. But "everything" in this place is not the Intellect, for we have already shown that the First Intellect is not multiplied as that whose existence is "everything" is multiplied, which is what the community needs of the two comprehensive worships that gather the sciences, outwardly by deed and inwardly by the conception that leads to deliverance from the torment of the two worlds.

**Section Twelve of Chapter Eight**

He said:

"We have created it," by which he does not intend what he went to, for "We have created it" means "We have appointed it in measure." I mean: We have made the form of the revelation and the Law a form within whose potency lies everything of the sciences and the religious works the community needs to ward off pains from the soul in this world and the next. It is as though He said, speaking on behalf of God, exalted be He: "We, everything We have created according to a measure," of the matter of the two worships in knowledge and deed which the community needs in its religion and its worldly life, We have appointed in measure within what We brought of the revelation and the Law, an appointing; that is, We placed it within its potency, that it might come forth, in interpretation, to the one who merits it.

**Section Thirteen of Chapter Eight**

As for the saying of the author of al-Islah that decree is the posterior act, he has hit the mark from the side that what comes out into act from the world of nature comes out by its outpouring, and by it is completed, and from its side attains completeness. For this reason decree came to be predicated of the Speaker, since his coming out into act and his attaining perfection are by its outpouring and its support.

**Section Fourteen of Chapter Eight**

He said:

All the essences surged from the prior in an appointed measure. In this there is a contradiction, for the posterior is an essence, and it is surged from the prior, yet it is not appointed in measure. What is meant by his saying is rather that surging from which the bodily world came to be, which is appointed in measure so that things might come from it, as we explained in the chapter of destiny, not the posterior.

**Section Fifteen of Chapter Eight**

As for the saying of the author of al-Nusrah, in the passage where at its head stands "We have created it," by which he intends the image of the two worlds from between them, and "according to a measure," by which he intends the posterior upon which the settling of the matter rests, so that all the spiritual and bodily images appeared from between the two: the matter in the existence of things is not such that what is bodily was and what is non-bodily was, in the manner he mentioned, by reason of the corruption we have shown in the foundation upon which he built his speech. And were it not that discourse upon the order in which the existence of existents rests upon the First lies outside the bound of writing, we would have brought it, and its mention will not fail to appear, God willing, in our epistles and our books.

**Section Sixteen of Chapter Eight**

The author of al-Islah said:

As for the interpretation of the saying of the Messenger of God (ع):

"I flee from the decree of God to His destiny,"

he meant only to flee from the subtle support of the prior to the exposition of the posterior, as a man flees from fire, which is a sign of support, to water, which is a sign of exposition. For he wished to flee from the thing that has already been sealed and decreed, whose repelling cannot be contrived, to destiny, which has not yet been decreed.

This is the account, and it is not well-ordered. ## 15. Fleeing to God's Measure

The author of al-Islah completed his point: the setting of him upright signifies the speed of the walking, for to follow a man in his walking signifies the statement, the proof, the haste, and the quickness of the pace. So the Messenger of God (ع) used to address those bent upon hypocrisy with preaching and with warning in the outward form, without the statement in the inward. The measure is upon the outward, as we have explained, and the decree is upon the inward. His fleeing from the decree of God to His measure was for the sake of this wall, wary that it should fall, wary for himself, and not a fleeing from the dignity with which God Almighty had honored him, the degree to which God had raised him.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

He is, upon the seeking of increase, the more eager.

It is not that the words *Flee from God's decree to His measure* are to be interpreted as the author of al-Islah imagines, namely that the Messenger was fleeing from the rank of the Follower to the rank of the Preceder, for by his own claim the prophets (عليهم السلام) forever seek ascent, and the seeking of the height is not the seeking of the depth. But we would say, by the help of God Almighty and His blessing, that the meaning here turns upon three senses, and each one of them establishes that the decree belongs to the Preceder and the measure belongs to the Follower.

The first, and it is the greatest of them, the most fitting, and the most exact in meaning, is that what is intended in his fleeing from the decree of God to His measure is this: from the Preceder, God willing, comes the support; from the Follower comes the structuring; from the Speaker comes the composing of the Law; and from the Foundation comes the interpretation of the whole. The interpretation, which is the end, most resembles the support, and the composing most resembles the structuring. So the Speaker (ع), in this saying, was making plain that he is, in his composing of the Laws, like the Follower in its structuring of the world, and that he is not like the Preceder. He fled from the decree of God, which is the Preceder, and did not resemble it in the aspect that is the origination of the support, toward his measure, which is the interpretation, to which he likened himself.

The second sense is his informing his community that he draws benefit from the First by means of the Second, so that in everything he wished to stand upon from the First, it was by means of the Second that he returned to it. It is as if he said: I return, in all that I wish to grasp, to the one who is the link between me and the Intellect, and that is the Second.

The third sense, and it is the most honored, is what the author of the book (ع) mentioned: his fleeing from the intensity of the light of the support of the Preceder to the support of the Follower, as al-Jadd, with his spiritual power, could not bear it until his very identity nearly dissolved. That is his saying in the story of Moses (ع). This is their statement.

We say:

As for the author of al-Islah, he took, in interpreting the verse, a course conforming to how the matter truly stands in the meaning of the decree and the measure, and it is well done. Better still had he said that, since the Prophet (ع) was a prophet who reported the hidden things among the events of existence and the happenings, and what lay within men's breasts, by what God Almighty had singled out for him of revelation, he wished to make plain how there was opened to him what was cast to him by al-Jadd. So he said: I return, which is his saying *I flee*. I mean: by al-Jadd I cross, from the side of the ranks of God Almighty, the whole of what is on the point of coming to be among the happenings and the events of existence, which is the decree of God that inevitably goes forth into act, toward His measure, which is God's creating of the souls and the horizons and their motions that point to what He has measured within them to be, so that there is opened to me what has been made plain in the door of revelation. And for this God Almighty said:

> سنريهم آياتنا في الآفاق وفي أنفسهم حتى يتبين لهم انه الحق

We shall show them Our signs upon the horizons and within their own selves, until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth.

And He said of those who reflect upon the creation of the heavens and the earth:

> ربنا ما خلقت هذا باطلاً

Our Lord, You did not create this in vain.

This does not point to the mere precedence of a thing's existence or the lateness of its existence among the events of being; rather it points to it and gives it utterance. It is as if he said: I return, in what is cast to me by al-Jadd, wholly to the measure, so that it is glimpsed, or opened, by its taking form before me and being pictured to me. And that is his saying, *I flee from the decree of God to the measure.* This is a kind of revelation, and the book *Rahat al-Aql* contains the explanation of it.

### The Seventeenth Section of the Eighth Chapter

As for the author of al-Nusrah, his manner in the answer he pursued is proof of his surrender to the author of al-Islah, for he brought forward nothing that would overturn what the author of al-Islah built his words upon and set out, except the meanings he adduced, reckoning them his own. He held that each of them establishes that the decree belongs to the Preceder and the measure belongs to the Follower, and not because the Speaker is like the Follower in his composing of the Law, as al-Islah put it, but on account of the Speaker's returning, in all he wished to grasp, to the Follower. Even were he to establish this, it would still oblige him to establish that the decree belongs to the Preceder and the measure to the Follower, and there is no correspondence at all between the decree and the measure on the one hand and the meanings he set out on the other. Had the meaning of the decree and the measure conformed to the state of the First and the Second, and been fitted to them from some aspect, it would have been allowable to say that the decree and the measure fall upon them from that aspect. But the decree means what has already been said of it, and so too the measure, and it is not fitting to say these of the First and the Second, for they are innocent of what the meaning of the two terms would require. Whoever says these of them is as one who says of honey, whose nature is sweetness, that it is aloes, whose nature is bitterness.

### The Eighteenth Section of the Eighth Chapter

As for his likening, in the second sense, the Speaker to the Follower, it does not conform to the law of the guiding call, because the Speaker, in his standing within the world of body and the world of religion, holds the station of the Preceder, and the station of the guardian in the two of them is the station of the Follower in the world of origination. So the Speaker and the Foundation are two likenesses of the Preceder and the Follower, and two likenesses of the sun and the moon.

### The Nineteenth Section of the Eighth Chapter

As for the third sense he adduced, making it the ground of the Speaker's fleeing, namely that it was from the intensity of the light of the support of the Preceder toward the support of the Follower, it runs contrary to what is due. And the interpretation concerning the state of al-Jadd, which he cited in evidence, calls for full investigation and a further reckoning, and the discussion of it will come. In sum, the saying of the author of al-Islah in this section is the stronger.

### The Twentieth Section of the Eighth Chapter

The author of al-Islah said, drawing upon the verse in the story of Moses (ع):

> رب ارني انظر اليك قال لن تراني ولكن انظر الى الجبل فان استقر مكانه فسوف تراني فلما تجلى ربه للجبل

My Lord, show me, that I may look upon You. He said: You shall not see Me, but look toward the mountain; if it settles in its place, then you shall see Me. And when his Lord disclosed Himself to the mountain,

> جعله دكا وخر موسى صعقا

He made it crumble to dust, and Moses fell down thunderstruck.

This one sought to have unveiled to him more than had been measured for him of the rank of the Preceder, so it was said to him: You shall not see Me, but look toward the mountain. Thus He made known to him that he would not attain that, and He bade him keep to what flows to him by al-Jadd, and He made known that the one who asked him could not, for all its subtlety, attain the like of what he sought. How then could he attain what he does not see? And He says: If it settles in its place, then you shall see Me. And when his Lord disclosed Himself to the mountain, He made it crumble to dust, and Moses fell down thunderstruck. When Moses knew that he had erred in seeking what he could not receive, he humbled himself before his Lord, and when he came to himself he said:

> سبحانك تبت إليك

Glory be to You, I turn to You in repentance.

This is his saying.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

*My Lord, show me, that I may look upon You. He said: You shall not see Me* means: you have not the strength, and you cannot endure with patience upon that rank. *But look toward the mountain,* and it is al-Jadd, for all his nearness to me. If it settles upon its rank, the one I have given him, and bears what I have not disclosed to you, then you too shall bear that. But al-Jadd did not bear it when it was disclosed to him, and his identity nearly dissolved. This is his saying.

We say:

The interpretation of this verse, as each of the two of them has interpreted it, lowers Moses (ع). For when they placed him in the station of the prophets and the highest rank, among those who commune with the upper ranks, they then cast him out of the company of the knowing and counted him in the ranks of the ignorant, alleging that he sought what he could not grasp from the ranks above him. How could that be, and the whole matter passing him by in it? The interpretation of the verse is other than what they went to. For the address to Moses (ع) was not with the upper ranks; rather it was an address to him from the lower ranks, at a time when he had not yet reached the degree of Speaker. He asked of the master of the affair what its hour had not yet come, for he was in an age of interval, and the people of truth were beneath affliction, veiling, and concealment from the side of the opponents and the adversaries. Moses (ع) saw in himself a power by which he reckoned it possible, in that age of his, were he commanded, to lift that ordeal from the people of truth, to take vengeance upon the enemies of God, and to govern the community by the governance of truth, just as the Foundation (ع) had reckoned, by the power he found in himself, what he reckoned, and then repented of it when he knew that he had erred, whereupon the sun was returned to him and restored, as we have set out in our epistles by interpretation.

So Moses (ع) asked of the master of the affair, and he is Shuayb, when he betook himself to him, and he is his Lord, this thing, after he had made his command clear to him regarding what he found in himself of power and capacity for governance. That is his saying, *My Lord, show me, that I may look upon You,* meaning: command me, that I may order this affair and lift the distress. He said: *You shall not see Me,* meaning: you shall not see Me do any of that, nor shall I invest you with a command at this time, for to everything there is an hour and a season, and this season is not the season of taking these, meaning the community, from what they are upon of following their own whims, nor the time of returning affairs to the canons of governance, nor will what I say to you be brought to completion. Shuayb (ع) wished to alert him to his error in his request in the best of ways, and to teach him that what he sought is hard, and will not be brought to completion in that age of his at the hands of one possessed of power, aid, and readiness, so how then should it be at his own hands? For it came from the side of a man of great place in the call, mighty in worldly leadership, possessed of aid, wealth, men, and dominion. He said to Moses (ع): I have come to know that this affair will not be completed in this age, and that it is hard and cannot be measured out. It is not that my refraining is out of stinginess toward you, or a preference to delay you from your station; but look toward the mountain, meaning this man who possesses worldly power, readiness, and capacity. *If it settles in its place, then you shall see Me,* meaning: if he stands firm in what he has been commanded, and is enabled, with the power and aid he has, to spread out the ordinances of the governance of truth, and it is completed for him, then you shall see Me do what you asked concerning those people. *And when his Lord disclosed Himself to the mountain,* meaning, when He commanded him to raise up the beacon of truth, *He made it crumble to dust,* that is, the affair grew straitened upon the people, so they did not accept the command, and split the staff, and the affair fell into confusion and disorder upon him, and his leadership stood upon the brink of ruin as the people rose against him. So Moses (ع) knew then that what he had reckoned would be completed at his hands in that hour was a reckoning of the impossible, and that the master of the affair, who is his Lord, was more knowing of the matter than he. He awoke from what had been confused upon him by that alerting, and he said: *Glory be to You, I turn to You in repentance.* He recanted his saying, and regretted, and held to patience with what the author of al-Nusrah had commanded him, until that term in which he had been hired elapsed, and the time came, and there came from him what came, and his saying was verified, *then you shall see Me:* the affair was surrendered, and you do what you wish. This is the interpretation of the verse, not what is reckoned by the one who lowers and raises, which does not correspond.

### The Twenty-First Section of the Eighth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

As for his argument by the saying, *And He created everything and measured it out in a measuring,* this is the clearest of proofs that the measure belongs to the Follower, and the decree, which is the creating, to the Preceder, since the meaning of His saying *And He created everything* is that by it He intended the Preceder, for the Intellect is all things, and all things are the Intellect.

We say:

The discussion of the measure and the decree has already gone before, and it was made plain with it that the decree and the measure may not be said of the Preceder and the Follower. What is intended by the Almighty's saying,

> وخلق كل شيء فقدره تقديراً

And He created everything, then measured it out in a measuring,

is not what the author of al-Nusrah went to, for we have already shown its corruptness, and that it befits neither the Preceder nor the Follower that the decree and the measure be said of them. Rather, what is intended in His saying *And He created everything, then measured it out in a measuring* is that He set everything pertaining to the affair of religion and its welfare, outwardly or inwardly, within the power of the Revelation and the Law, and measured it out in a measuring according to how the Revelation and the Law stand in their comprehending of all the sciences that pertain to religion and its welfare; and these are within the two of them by potency, and the interpretation brings them forth from them.

### The Twenty-Second Section of the Eighth Chapter

As for his saying that the Intellect is all things and all things are the Intellect, this is a proposition that does not hold. The bodily world, with all it gathers, is not an intellect; it is a thing. The finest thing to which the meaning of the saying, *the Intellect is all things and all things are the Intellect,* may be attributed is this: that the Intellect, which is the very command of God Almighty, is the cause of the existence of all things, bodily and other than bodily, and the existence of all things is from the Intellect and toward its purpose. This is what he intended.

### The Twenty-Third Section of the Eighth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

God has granted the prophets license in the inner meanings of the Laws, and dispensed them from employing them, in His saying:

> ليس على الذين آمنوا وعملوا الصالحات جناح فيما طعموا اذا ما اتقوا وآمنوا وعملوا الصالحات ثم اتقوا وآمنوا واحسنوا ان الله يحب المحسنين

There is no blame upon those who believe and do righteous deeds for what they have partaken, so long as they are God-fearing and believe and do righteous deeds; then are God-fearing and believe; then are God-fearing and do good. Indeed, God loves the doers of good.

We say:

The verse contains, neither in its outward nor in its inward, any mention of a license for the prophets to abandon the employing of the Laws. I do not know how this escaped him, that he loosed such a saying without examining it fully. The exposition of this verse and its interpretation are contrary to what he went to. For God Almighty, when He knew that souls do not attain felicity, nor reach the degree of perfection, nor pass from the limit of potency to the limit of act, except by work and knowledge, laid upon the whole of mankind the establishing of the two worships, in knowledge and in deed, that by them they might attain the two felicities in the two abodes. And since souls do not attain their perfection and felicity save by the two worships, then whichever soul abandons the two of them, or one of them, is far from attaining perfection. This holds for the soul so long as it remains within the bond of life: it stands between His hands until the time of its passing, whereupon it is either in the Garden, if it kept the two, or it abandoned the two or one of them and is in the Fire, until God forgives it, as we have set out in our books. We have shown, in what has gone before of the discussion, that souls do not stand save by work and by the reforming of character, which is the greatest striving, as the Messenger of God (ع) said, and by their acquiring of the virtues through discipline and knowledge, in relation to what precedes them in existence, according to what the interpretive call requires. And if the states of souls in worship are in this likeness, how then is it allowable that license be granted to a soul, or to the Speakers, or to those beneath them among the ranks, to abandon the work and the perfecting of souls, the complete of them and the deficient by it?

No. The verse has an interpretation other than the one he interpreted, and in it is a special bearing upon those who stand in the call, the summoners to the affirmation of the oneness of God Almighty and the knowledge of His ranks by way of the Imams (عليهم السلام). For the Speaker (ع), when he knew that his community would differ after him, that each faction would set itself apart with a doctrine it invents by its own opinions and its deficient minds, and that they would choose, over the guiding community for whom God has appointed the guides, those for whom God has made no light, and knew that the summoners would suffer from the divided community every hardship in the community's setting itself against them in doing them injury and in impugning their doctrine and their masters, the pure Imams (عليهم السلام), granted to the summoners who stand in the call a license to look into the sciences that are not of their own belief, upon conditions he set forth, that these might be for them as a piece of equipment in their disputations. He said:

> ليس على الذين آمنوا وعملوا الصالحات جناح فيما طعموا اذا ما اتقوا وآمنوا وعملوا الصالحات

There is no blame upon those who believe and do righteous deeds for what they have partaken, so long as they are God-fearing and believe and do righteous deeds,

meaning: there is no blame upon those who stand in the call to the oneness of God and the knowledge of His ranks by way of the deputies (عليهم السلام), who bring the respondents to faith when they join themselves to the rope of God and hold fast, by loyalty to them, against doubts and confusions in the religion of God, and who bring them to the degree of perfection and attainment, no blame, that is, no straitness, in what they have partaken, that is, in what they read of the sciences that are not of their belief, that these might be for them as a weapon with which they fight the adversaries in establishing the truth. And that is upon three conditions:

The first, *so long as they are God-fearing and believe and do righteous deeds,* meaning: so long as their path is straight in obedience to God Almighty, in following His Messenger (ع), in belief in His ranks, and in the establishing of the two worships together according to what he brought.

The second, *then are God-fearing and believe,* meaning: and they obey the Messenger in his legatee and in the one who stands in the affair in his place, and establish the call to him, outwardly and inwardly.

The third, *then are God-fearing and do good,* meaning: and they obey the Imam in the one he sets up and invests with the affair, and do good in the call to the oneness of God Almighty under his imamate.

For if they do this and stand by these conditions, then there is no harm upon them in looking into those doctrines and sciences that belong to the peoples, and in pondering them, and there is no straitness in their religions in doing so, for these are an aid to them in what they need in withstanding the enemies, and against whoever seeks to impugn the ranks. This is the interpretation of the verse, and in it there is no license to abandon the employing of the Law; it is only a license to look into the sciences upon the conditions mentioned. And the whole of the matter is this: every interpretation that leads to making lawful what God has forbidden, or to forbidding what He has made lawful, or to altering the ranks of the ranks of God Almighty, and that becomes a slur upon the Law and the rites of the upright religion, and a summons to abandon working by it and to acquire felicity by refraining from it, is a corrupt interpretation. Our masters, the pure Imams (عليهم السلام), are quit before God Almighty of what he interpreted, and of every interpretation that opposes what the Prophet (ع) brought.

## 16. Did Adam Bring a Law?

On the Judgment Between the Two Who Draw Near
The Law of Adam and the Wilayah of Noah (ع)

Chapter One

The author of al-Islah said:

Whoever claims that the first of the speaker-prophets (عليهم السلام) had no Shariah is mistaken. He is mistaken again when he counts that one among those who are "determined," according to what we have explained, for determination occurs only by the abrogation of a Shariah or by the renewing of another. The first Pronouncer had no Shariah before him that he might abrogate, and so the name of determination does not fall upon him, as we have already stated. He was rather the first to draw the lines of religion in command and prohibition. And whoever works to benefit from what the rest of the Pronouncers drew in their apparent laws, from which the subtleties are inferred, the recommended meanings of the parables that are called interpretation, which is the manifest sense of the words of the wise, will know that every line the Pronouncers drew (عليهم السلام), every command, prohibition, and act of servitude, is a law, whether small or great, few or many. Each speaker is given only the measure of what he is commanded, and he does not exceed it. Some of them composed much, and some were restricted to little, holding to the drawing of the statutes and their wording, as we see that fasting in the Law of Moses (ع) is less than what it is in the Law of Jesus (ع), and in the Law of Jesus there is more than what is in the Law of Muhammad (ع). Likewise the rest of the rulings in religion increase and decrease according to the difference of their cycles and the measure of what they were commanded.

No one should hold that this great cycle, which was the longest of these cycles, passed away with no work done, that its people departed without benefit, and that there were no apparent statutes upon whose rulings they conducted themselves. For if there were no work, no benefit, and no apparent statutes, then there is no interpretation, no inward, and no true summons. The summons is raised upon the Shariah; interpretation requires a revelation; the inward requires the outward; the meaning requires the parable. And if there is no outward, then there is no inward, no summons, and no Shariah, so that creation is left neglected and the people are ignorant. For knowledge is joined with work, and that is the root of wisdom among the wise, as they have said:

الحكمة هي عمل مع علم
"Wisdom is action with knowledge."

It is impossible to say that the people of that cycle were in such a state.

Rather, we say:

The first Pronouncer merited the name of "speaker" for his composing of a Shariah; were it not for that, he would not have been counted among the six Pronouncers, even had it been required of him only to establish a foundation that calls to the Tawhid of Allah Almighty. And if he had no Shariah and no summons, then there was no statute by which those who belonged to him in his cycle might affiliate after him, so that they would raise the appended ranks upon the pattern of the rest of the Pronouncers. This is a sickly belief. It is not permissible to say that there was in that cycle no revelation and no Shariah, because command and prohibition come only by the inspiration of Allah, and the manifesting of the command of Allah is revelation. When command, prohibition, and revelation are established, the Shariah is established as the manifestations of the statutes. This indicates that the first Speaker had a Shariah, over and above what the compelling proof indicates according to what the wise have said. Allah Almighty said:

وَاتْلُ عَلَيْهِمْ نَبَأَ ابْنَيْ آدَمَ بِالْحَقِّ إِذْ قَرَّبَا قُرْبَانًا فَتُقُبِّلَ مِنْ أَحَدِهِمَا وَلَمْ يُتَقَبَّلْ مِنَ الْآخَرِ
"And recite to them the news of the two sons of Adam in truth, when they offered a sacrifice, and it was accepted from one of them and not accepted from the other" (the verse).

The offering is one of the laws, and it is the most firmly established of the causes of the Shariah. Allah has commanded it in every Shariah and has made it the root of devotion and purity, and for this reason the offering is called a rite. Allah Almighty said:

وَلِكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ جَعَلْنَا مَنْسَكًا لِيَذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَى مَا رَزَقَهُمْ مِنْ بَهِيمَةِ الْأَنْعَامِ
"And for every nation We have appointed a rite, that they may pronounce the name of Allah over what He has provided them of the beasts of cattle."

And He said:

وَلِكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ جَعَلْنَا مَنْسَكًا هُمْ نَاسِكُوهُ فَلَا يُنَازِعُنَّكَ
"And to every nation We have appointed an act of devotion which they perform, so let them not dispute with you" (the verse).

The rite is the place in which the offering is brought near. In the hadith:

العرجاء تجر رجلها إلى المنسك
"The lame ewe drags her leg to the rite."

The rite itself is the offering. A man is called a rite-keeper when he is known for religion, scrupulousness, and purity, and the offering is called devotion because it is the summit of the statutes of religion and the summit of nearness to Allah Almighty in every Shariah and among all the nations. This is the most firmly established of the causes of the Shariah. It is called an offering because by it they draw near to Allah Almighty, and by their nearness to Allah Almighty come the first causes of obedience and disobedience, of disbelief and faith, of command and prohibition. It is the first of the statutes of the Shariah.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The author of al-Islah has argued for the affirmation of a Shariah for Adam (ع) by the saying of Allah Almighty, "And recite to them the news of the two sons of Adam in truth, when they offered a sacrifice." He held that the offering belongs only to the statutes of the laws, after he had drawn out at length the distinction between determination and Shariah and gathered the greater part of his witnesses to it from the poetry of the Arabs, with no benefit beneath them and no proof that verifies what he wished to establish. The matter in the interpretation of this verse is not as he supposed. For if the offering in this place were what belongs only to the apparent laws, the slaughter of sheep and camels, it would have been as he mentioned. But the affair of the offering in this passage is what does not belong to the laws at all: it is the raising up of the proofs from beneath their hands, and of those who stand in their place when they depart the world. This story has an interpretation manifest to the wise. Had he read this story to its end, it would have become clear to him that Adam (ع) had no Shariah, since one of his two sons did not bury the corpse of his brother and did not know how, until Allah sent a raven scratching in the earth to show him how to bury his brother's corpse. For the covering of the dead and his burial, in whatever manner, is one of the laws; and had Adam had a Shariah, its place would not have been hidden from him, nor the acting upon it. This is his saying.

We say:

The Shariah of Adam (ع) has been established both by the fact that what the author of al-Islah adduced does establish it, and by the fact that what the author of al-Nusrah brought forward does not undo him in it. For the author of al-Islah argued for the affirmation of the Shariah, which is one of the statutes of the Pronouncers in the worship of Allah Almighty and the road to the knowledge of the causes, from the apparent sense of the Book: by the verse in which the offering is mentioned, and by the universally affirming verse in His saying, "And for every nation We have appointed a rite, that they may pronounce the name of Allah" (the verse), and by the other verse, "And to every nation We have appointed an act of devotion which they perform." Thus it is established. And the saying of the author of al-Nusrah, that the matter in the interpretation of this verse is not as he supposed, does not contradict the saying of the author of al-Islah, since the author of al-Islah did not argue for what he affirmed by the interpretation of this verse, but by its outward sense. And since the saying of the author of al-Islah affirms the Shariah of Adam (ع), and the saying of the author of al-Nusrah in reply to him does not undo it, the Shariah remains as it was established, fixed.

Chapter Two

The author of al-Islah said:

Were the offering what belongs only to the apparent laws, the slaughter of sheep and camels, it would have been as he mentioned. But the affair of the offering in this passage is what does not belong to the laws, according to his own explanation, and this too does not contradict the saying of the author of al-Islah. Rather it makes binding what he made binding, for his saying, "only what is in the apparent laws," is itself an affirmation of the offering, since the offering is one of the statutes of religion and of the apparent Shariah, even though he pressed the claim that what is intended is what belongs to the outward. And what he said, that the offering in this place does not belong to the apparent Shariah, hardly serves to undo the saying of the author of al-Islah, for what the author of al-Nusrah has said follows the course of an assertion that is not established except by evidence. It is permissible that the outward of this verse stand and exist just as its inward exists, especially since the verse is not of the kind that runs as a parable whose interpretation is sought while its outward is abandoned. And since that is so, and the outward of the verse affirms the work and is fixed, the statutes of the Shariah of Adam (ع) are fixed.

Chapter Three

Then the author of al-Islah said:

That the offering is the raising up of the proofs from beneath their hands and of those who stand in their place makes it binding that Adam had something to which these call, for the proofs and the ranks that are set up are set up only to call to what the Speaker proclaims and draws, in which lies the welfare of those summoned in this world and in religion, in the Hereafter and in the beginning. Adam (ع) is not free of one of two things. Either he is a father because he laid down the Shariah and enacted the statutes that are the arbiter, or he prescribed nothing and manifested no statute, small or great, of the laws of worship, in which case he has no need to raise up proofs and send forth summoners who stand in his place, since he left nothing that must be preserved and by which the nation is governed. And by the very affirmation of the author of al-Nusrah, through his interpretation, that Adam raised up the proofs, the Shariah and the statutes are made binding. So the Shariah belongs to Adam (ع).

Chapter Four

And after this we say:

Since the need for the prophets (عليهم السلام) was for the teaching of the nation what would make it happy in the Hereafter and in this world, and would guide it to its welfare in this world and in religion, and since Adam (ع) was a prophet, it is necessary that he was a teacher and a guide. And if it is necessary that he was a teacher and a guide, then it is necessary that there existed that which he taught his nation, by which it is made happy and guided to its welfare. And if it is necessary that this existed, then it can be nothing other than the laws of worship, which are the Shariah. So the Shariah of Adam (ع) is fixed. This is confirmed by the saying of Allah Almighty:

كَانَ النَّاسُ أُمَّةً وَاحِدَةً
"The people were one nation," that is, in ignorance,

فَبَعَثَ اللَّهُ النَّبِيِّينَ مُبَشِّرِينَ وَمُنْذِرِينَ وَأَنْزَلَ مَعَهُمُ الْكِتَابَ بِالْحَقِّ لِيَحْكُمَ بَيْنَ النَّاسِ فِيمَا اخْتَلَفُوا فِيهِ
"so Allah sent the prophets as bearers of good tidings and warners, and sent down with them the Book in truth, to judge between the people in that wherein they differed" (the verse).

Adam (ع) is one of the prophets, and if he was a prophet, Allah did not send him except to give good tidings, to warn, to judge, and to decide. And good tidings, warning, judgment, and decision come only through the statutes and ordinances. So the statutes, which are the Shariah, are fixed.

Chapter Five

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Had he read this verse to its end, it would have become clear to him that Adam had no Shariah, since one of his two sons did not bury the corpse of his brother and did not know how, until Allah sent a raven scratching in the earth to show him how to bury his brother's corpse. For the covering of the dead and his burial, in whatever manner, is one of the laws; and had Adam (ع) had a Shariah, the taking of it would not have been hidden from his sons. This is a saying issued without inquiry and without examination.

We say:

Had the author of al-Islah relied upon sophistry, as the author of al-Nusrah did against him in the matter of the verse he adduced concerning the meaning of the offering, when he said, "the matter in the interpretation of the verse is not as he supposed," then the author of al-Nusrah would not be free, in his own support, of one of two things. Either he must give the verse he adduced in denying the Shariah of Adam (ع) an interpretation in which he stands alone and by which he argues, and then his argument in denying what he seeks to deny of the Shariah of Adam (ع) has already fallen, for his argument first rested upon the outward of the verse; or he must give it no interpretation at all, and let it run according to what its outward conveys of its meaning, and then it does not indicate any of what he intended. The verse contains no indication that the son of Adam, when the raven scratched in the earth and he saw it, covered his brother and buried him, so that the proof by it should stand for him. The verse is:

فَبَعَثَ اللَّهُ غُرَابًا يَبْحَثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ لِيُرِيَهُ كَيْفَ يُوَارِي سَوْأَةَ أَخِيهِ قَالَ يَا وَيْلَتَا أَعَجَزْتُ أَنْ أَكُونَ مِثْلَ هَذَا الْغُرَابِ فَأُوَارِيَ سَوْأَةَ أَخِي فَأَصْبَحَ مِنَ النَّادِمِينَ
"So Allah sent a raven scratching in the earth, to show him how to hide the corpse of his brother. He said, 'Woe to me! Am I unable to be like this raven and hide the corpse of my brother?' And he became one of the regretful."

It mentions that he saw, but it gives no indication that he buried him.

Chapter Six

Moreover, the interpretation of the verse and its inward likewise do not indicate any of that. For each of the adversary and the friend held, in the worship of Allah Almighty, to something that was his offering, by which he draws near to Allah Almighty. The adversary held that worship is nothing but the outward of the works alone, and that deliverance is only by it, and that by this belief and by sincerity in it he would become the one who stands in the place of the Messenger (ع). The friend held that the worship of Allah Almighty is nothing but the outward, which is the work, together with the inward, which is the knowledge, and that deliverance is only by the two of them together, and that the one who stands in the place of the Messenger (ع) is the one whom the Messenger (ع) has advanced and been pleased with. So the two contended over that one day and disputed over it before the Prophet, and each of them made his belief plain concerning that by which he worshiped Allah. That is His saying, "when they offered a sacrifice," that is, each of them held to something concerning that which draws him near to Allah Almighty and to His Messenger. "And it was accepted from one of them," who is the wasi, that is, the Speaker who stands in the place of Allah was pleased with his belief concerning Allah and concerning His worship. "And it was not accepted from the other," who is the adversary, that is, the Speaker was not pleased with his belief, since it was upon other than its right face. The Speaker (ع) was content with the belief of the wasi concerning himself that he was content with, without declaring it openly, out of fear that the adversary should ascribe to him an inclining toward the friend over himself. So he said:

إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ أَتْقَاكُمْ
"Indeed, the most honored of you before Allah is the most God-fearing of you."

And the adversary had heard him (ع) say, "The most God-fearing of you is Abu Bakr." So together with his hearing that from the Messenger, he reckoned that the verse he recited was recited only because of his contentment with the belief and the way of the wasi, and that this one was the one who would stand after him. He rose up, and in the heart of the adversary was the thought that he had overcome the friend, and he said to him, "I will surely slay you," that is, I will surely repel you from this belief that he was not pleased with in you, and from the affair that you reckon is yours. The friend said to him:

إِنَّمَا يَتَقَبَّلُ اللَّهُ مِنَ الْمُتَّقِينَ
"Allah accepts only from the God-fearing,"

meaning that the one who stands in the place of Allah, who is the Messenger, sets no one in his place and is pleased with the belief of no one save the God-fearing, who join knowledge with work and draw near to Allah Almighty by raising up the marks of both together. Then the adversary turned to slander and reviling of the friend, and to the claim that he knew nothing, so the two of them brought their case before the Prophet (ع) concerning their belief, and he was pleased with the friend's belief over the belief of the other. The friend said:

لَئِنْ بَسَطْتَ إِلَيَّ يَدَكَ لِتَقْتُلَنِي مَا أَنَا بِبَاسِطٍ يَدِيَ إِلَيْكَ لِأَقْتُلَكَ إِنِّي أَخَافُ اللَّهَ رَبَّ الْعَالَمِينَ
"If you stretch out your hand against me to slay me, I will not stretch out my hand against you to slay you; indeed, I fear Allah, the Lord of the worlds,"

that is, if you assault me, and loose your tongue against me, and lower my measure, and speak of me otherwise than is due, and are not content with what I believe concerning the worship of Allah, the Lord of the worlds, then I will not do the like of that to you, nor slander you, for I am beneath the command of the Speaker who stands in the place of Allah, who is the master of the cycle and the lord of the Imams, and beneath his obedience. I fear him, and I do not advance before him.

The opponent paid no heed to this word from the guardian. He turned away from what was right and obeyed his own soul, and so he killed his brother. His soul then rose against the other, and he set upon him, that opponent with whom nothing was loosed, nor bound, nor taken in counsel except through him. That is, when the first opponent brought back to him what had befallen the second, he said to him: there is no belief concerning Allah and His worship except what I have said. What he said, meaning what the guardian had said, he pronounced sharp error; and he made the matter fair to himself in lowering the guardian, barring him from his rank, and charging him with knowing nothing of worth. So he thrust him from his station, overcame him, and declared: there is no worship except by this outward form, and none by what this man says to you. Thus he became one of the losers, for he worshipped Allah by obedience to the Speaker without obedience to the Foundation, and by the outward without the inward.

Then Allah sent a raven:

> (فبعث الله غراباً يبحث في الارض)

"So Allah sent a raven scratching in the earth."

That is, when the term was drawn out and the hour of the opponent's passing drew near, the guardian wished to make the argument binding upon him, so that his parting from the world would come after a reminder. So he sent to him, and this is the meaning of His saying "So Allah sent," that the one standing in the place of the Speaker, who himself stands in the place of Allah, sent a man from among his companions to make plain to him his affair and his station, and what Allah, the Most High, held in secret in his silence about seeking his right, and to appoint for him a way out from what he had done, and to move him to return the truth to its people. This is the meaning of the raven's scratching in the earth, to show him how to deliver himself from what he had brought upon his own soul, and to guide him to his duty. The man whom the guardian of Allah sent for this purpose is Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr, for he is among those bound to the guardian by way of religion, and to the opponent by way of lineage. So Muhammad did what he was sent to do, that the opponent might be roused to his crime; and his soul softened, and he inclined toward binding himself to his allegiance. Yet the second opponent prevailed over his judgment and barred him from his purpose, from the acknowledgment of the guardian's right that he had set his mind upon. So he grieved, and said within himself, when he was prevented:

> (يا ويلتا أعجزت أن أكون مثل هذا الغراب)

"O woe to me! Was I unable to be like this raven?"

That is: would that I were in the standing of this man, meaning Muhammad his son, who, though a stranger to the guardian, is his like in guarding the covenant of Allah and His religion and in keeping the secrets of the guardian of Allah. So he became one of those who regret what they let slip of following the guardian at the first, and regret the deed that would carry him at the last to the abode of the Hereafter. We seek refuge in Allah from breaking the covenant and then pleading it as proof. And if the matter is thus, the author of al-Nusrah has nothing in his objection by way of this verse, except the saying of the author of al-Islah concerning what it makes obligatory of the ordinances of the Law for Adam (ع).

### The Seventh Section of the Ninth Chapter

It has been said that laws, dispensations, ordinances, and prescribed practices may increase and decrease according to the age in which the Speaker arises, and according to what is needed for setting the community aright. The narrations have come down that the washing of the dead, the embalming, the shrouding, and the burial are from the Law of Adam (ع). And if the reports of this have come in unbroken succession, as has been mentioned, then this verse, in what it relates of the raven and the scratching in the earth at the killing of the son of Adam, runs only in the course of the parables whose interpretation is to be sought; its outward sense is not to be taken as it stands, in the manner we have set out and interpreted. So the Law belongs to Adam (ع), and the point is established.

### The Eighth Section of the Ninth Chapter

Then we say: the objection by this verse to overturn the ordinances of the Law of Adam (ع) is itself annulled. For the burial of the dead is not among those matters whose ordinances are lifted by the lifting of their standing, namely purity, prayer, the zakat, and the remaining acts, such that by the lifting of its ordinance the remaining ordinances of the laws, which are the foundations of worship, would be lifted. Rather it is one kind among the kinds of what is done with the dead: burning with fire, or casting into water, or bearing to a cave or a desert, or burying in the earth, or giving to the beasts of prey. And if it is one kind among the kinds of an ordinance of the Law, whose place may be taken by another, it cannot be that by its lifting the ordinance of burial would annul the ordinances of the whole Law. And if this is barred, the Law is established for Adam (ع).

### The Ninth Section of the Ninth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Adam (ع) was not the possessor of a Law; for had he been the possessor of a Law, the command of Allah to the angels that they prostrate to him would have been through his mediation.

We say:

By that saying the author of al-Nusrah has himself made it obligatory that Adam (ع) is the possessor of a Law. For the command of Allah to the angels to prostrate, though outwardly not from the side of Adam (ع), was only through his mediation, by the proof that the command to prostrate to him is impossible unless he is a Mustafa, and the command to prostrate to him is not impossible only because he is a Mustafa. And it has been established that he is a Mustafa, by the saying of Allah, the Most High:

> (إن الله اصطفى آدم ونوحاً)

"Indeed Allah chose Adam and Noah."

And if he is a Mustafa, it is impossible that Allah command one below the Mustafa except through the mediation of the Mustafa. So the command of Allah to the angels to prostrate to Adam was only through his mediation; and if it was through his mediation, then the Law is established for him by that saying.

### The Tenth Section of the Ninth Chapter

Then the command of Allah to prostrate to Adam (ع), by His saying "Prostrate to Adam" (اسجدوا لآدم), is like His command to the Children of Israel, by His saying:

> (يا بني اسرائيل اذكروا نعمتي التي انعمت عليكم وأوفوا بعهدي أوف بعهدكم وإياي فارهبون وآمنوا بما انزلت مصدقاً لما معكم ولا تكونوا أول كافر ولا تشتروا بآياتي ثمناً قليلاً وإياي فاتقون، ولا تلبسوا الحق بالباطل وتكتموا الحق وأنتم تعلمون وأقيموا الصلاة وآتوا الزكاة واركعوا مع الراكعين)

"O Children of Israel, remember My favor which I bestowed upon you, and fulfill My covenant, and I shall fulfill your covenant, and have awe of Me alone. And believe in what I have sent down confirming what is with you, and be not the first to disbelieve in it, and sell not My signs for a small price, and be mindful of Me. And confound not the truth with falsehood, nor conceal the truth while you know. And establish the prayer, and give the zakat, and bow with those who bow."

Though this appears in a form that does not indicate that he is a mediator, yet by the mediation of Muhammad it reached them. And if it were permissible to say that Muhammad has no Law on account of the bareness of the verse, in its mention of Allah's command to the Children of Israel, from what would indicate that the command is through his mediation, since the two verses are set together in respect of their not indicating a single original mediator, this would be impossible. For the command of Allah, the Most High, to the Children of Israel, though outwardly stripped of any mention of a mediator, was only through the mediation of Muhammad. And if that is impossible, then Adam is the possessor of the Law, and the Law is established for him.

### The Eleventh Section of the Ninth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The angels are not charged, nor is the practice of the Law obligatory upon them, for they do not disobey Allah for the blink of an eye; rather they glorify the praise of their Lord and do what they are commanded.

We say:

The angels who were charged, had they not been among those who bear a charge, He, the Most High, would not have charged them with the prostration to Adam, nor commanded them by His saying "Prostrate to Adam." And the angels who are not charged are those who were never in the abode of body, nor ever stood in potency, but came forth into act; they are the foremost forefathers in the abode of eternity. What is meant by the charged angels, however, is the assembly who were in the abode of nature, and who possessed the virtues by which they resemble those in the abode of eternity, the angels of origination and of prompting, according to what we have shown before, since none goes forward except one who is in the abode of origination and prompting. As for prostration, and the like of prostration among the ordinances of the worships and the acts, these are found only in the world of nature. And those who are charged are charged only with the establishing of the two worships, that by them they may attain of increase and of the blessings of felicity what Allah exalts their standing by, and attain the perfection that earns them abiding and permanence. For this reason there was the charging of the angels and the summoning of them to the act.

### The Twelfth Section of the Ninth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Had the prostration of the angels to Adam been a Law for them, Adam would have said, "Allah commands you to prostrate and to obey me," and he would have striven against whoever opposed him in the command of Allah, as was the case with all the Speakers.

We say:

The author of al-Islah may reply: it is not because Adam did not say "Allah commands you to prostrate to me and to obey me" that prostration must therefore not be among the ordinances of the Law. For the Prophet did not say to the Children of Israel "Allah commands you to establish the prayer and to give me the zakat," and that was no proof that the prayer, the zakat, and all that he commanded them are not among the ordinances of the Law. Then, were the laws established by their bringer being one who strives, Jesus (ع) would have had no Law, since he did not strive; and this is not sound.

### The Thirteenth Section of the Ninth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Moreover, Allah, the Most High, did not mention in the Quran that Adam (ع) had a people and followers, nor did He mention his being sent as He mentioned others, when He said:

> (إنا أرسلنا نوحاً)

"Indeed We sent Noah."

Nor did He mention him within the roll of the Law, when He said:

> (شرع لكم من الدين ما أوصى به نوحاً والذي أوحينا اليك وما وصينا به إبراهيم وموسى وعيسى أن أقيموا الدين ولا تتفرقوا)

"He has ordained for you of the religion what He enjoined upon Noah, and that which We revealed to you, and what We enjoined upon Abraham and Moses and Jesus: that you establish the religion and be not divided therein."

We say: the author of al-Nusrah has not dealt justly in his objection, nor pondered the saying of the author of al-Islah in his book. As for Adam, he is mentioned in the Quran, and his followers and his people are the angels commanded to prostrate to him, who learned the names from him, according to what Allah, the Most High, commanded by His saying:

> (يا آدم أنبئهم بأسمائهم)

"O Adam, inform them of their names."

As for the mention of his being sent, there is no sending greater than his sending. For the sending of the messengers was made obligatory only that they be the vicegerents of Allah in His earth, since Allah, the Most High, is beyond apprehension; and Allah, the Most High, has stated plainly in His Book that He made him a vicegerent in the earth, to whom its people return in what they need of the affair of their religions. As for his not being mentioned within the roll of those who ordained the Law in the verse, the author of al-Islah has said: he was not mentioned with them because he was not among the possessors of firm resolve, and was not a man of determination; and if it were permissible for Adam to be taken out of the company of the possessors of laws because his name is not mentioned with the five (عليهم السلام), it would likewise be permissible, on account of his not being mentioned within the company of those to whom revelation was given, in His saying, the Most High:

> (إنا أوحينا اليك كما أوحينا إلى نوح والنبيين من بعده وأوحينا إلى إبراهيم وإسماعيل وإسحاق ويعقوب والأسباط وعيسى وأيوب ويونس وهرون وسليمان)

"Indeed We revealed to you as We revealed to Noah and the prophets after him; and We revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, the tribes, Jesus, Job, Jonah, Aaron, and Solomon."

that he be taken out of their company as well; and that is impossible. So the saying is the saying of the author of al-Islah, and not of the other.

### The Fourteenth Section of the Ninth Chapter

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Furthermore, we ought to know the cause of the existence of the Law, so that it may be sound for us to say that Adam (ع) had no Law.

We say:

The cause of the obligatory existence of the Law is the neglect of the limits and their suspension, and the people's following of the opponent, and their claiming for him what Allah did not place in him. And this meaning was not present, nor did it appear, in the age of Adam, since the learned who were before him, and his people, were holding to the limits, and not one of them opposed a single limit, as they themselves reported when they said:

> (أتجعل فيها من يفسد فيها ويسفك الدماء ونحن نسبح بحمدك ونقدس لك)

"Will You place therein one who works corruption in it and sheds blood, while we glorify Your praise and sanctify You?"

So the establishing of the Law was not obligatory upon Adam. This is his saying.

We say: the cause of the obligatory existence of the Law is other than what the author of al-Nusrah went to in his saying, namely the neglect of the limits and their suspension and the people's following of the opponent; for that does not make a Law obligatory, nor establish it. Rather we take his place and say: as for the cause of the obligatory existence of the Law, which is the ordinances of the acts, we have set it out in our book known as al-Masabih fil-Imamah. The nearest of these causes is the need of people, at the first, upon their differing ranks, in their subsistence, for what they may guard themselves by from destruction, of the ordinances by which blood is preserved and the private parts, and the properties, are protected, and by which redress is made in the necessary matters that arise among them. Were these not present, their affairs would come to a standstill, and some of them would destroy others, the strong aiming at one below him in strength out of anger at what he possesses of wealth and the like, and the fires of discords would be kindled among them. Then, since it is barred, in the wisdom of the Maker, the Most High, that He deprive His creation of what would guard them from destruction, the ordinances that are the Law are therefore of necessary existence.

## 17. The Six Gates of Necessity

The author of al-Nusrah said that we must also come to know the reason the Law exists, so that it may then be sound for us to claim that Adam (عليه السلام) had no law of his own. He argued that the ground of the Law's obligation is the neglect of the limits, their disruption, and creation's pursuit of their opposites, together with their claiming for Him what Allah did not place in him. This meaning, he said, neither existed nor appeared in Adam's day, for the learned who were before him and his people alike kept steadily to the limits, and not one of them transgressed a single limit, as they themselves testified when they said:

> ( أَتَجْعَلُ فِيهَا مَنْ يُفْسِدُ فِيهَا وَيَسْفِكُ الدِّمَاءَ وَنَحْنُ نُسَبِّحُ بِحَمْدِكَ وَنُقَدِّسُ لَكَ )

"Will You place in it one who corrupts it and sheds blood, while we glorify You with praise and sanctify You?" And so, he concluded, no obligation fell upon Adam to establish the Law, given all this. Such is his statement.

We say that the reason for the necessity of the Law's existence is other than what the author of al-Nusrah reached when he named the neglect of the limits, their disruption, and creation's following of the opposite. That does not oblige a law, nor does it establish one. Rather, we answer on our own behalf. As for the reason the Law must exist, the Law being the ordinances of deeds, we have set it out in our book known as al-Masabih fi al-Imamah. The nearest of these reasons is the need of the people, first of all and according to their ranks, in their very survival, for that by which they are guarded against destruction: the ordinances by which blood is preserved, and by which their hearths, their women, and their wealth are made secure, and by which they are ransomed in the necessary matters that arise among them. Were these ordinances not to exist, their affairs would come to a standstill, and some of them would destroy others: the strong would set upon the one beneath him in strength out of rage at the wealth and the rest that such a one possesses, and the fires of discord would be kindled among them. Then, since it is barred in the wisdom of the Maker, the Most High, that He deprive His creation of what guards them from destruction and keeps it constant, the ordinances that are the Law are of necessary existence.

Then we say that their existence, by which we mean the ordinances of the laws, became a necessary existence because the souls belonging to human beings do not, each of them, hold back from one another in the acts proper to each, so that from those acts there arises what cannot do without ordinances by which the community is stilled, taking hold of their firm bonds, and by which one of them is made safe from another. And once the ordinances are of necessary existence, we say that some of them are necessary at every time and season, while some are not, existing at one time and not at another, increasing and decreasing.

As for what is of necessary existence, it is drawing near to Allah, the Most High; the ordinance of restraint by force and of retribution upon souls and persons; the ordinance of marriage; the division of wealth, such as inheritance and the like; the ordinance for concealing the dead; and the ordinance for extracting rights and returning them to their owners. This is because human beings have three souls. The first is the rational soul, which possesses discrimination and thought; it knows that it has a Creator, submits to Him by nature, and by that submission draws near to Him, and in its drawing near it is driven to ordinances by which it fulfils its aim, whether those ordinances are many or few. The second is the sensitive soul that belongs to him; through its irascible part he is angered when he is angered, and it loves to overcome, to subdue, to take revenge, to wrong, to kill, and to deal unjustly, so that he is driven to ordinances by which the violence of one is turned back from another, and by which the right of the wronged is taken from the wrongdoing aggressor, whether in the soul by force, or in the body by retribution, or by what runs in the course of blood-money. Through its appetitive part he seeks pleasures and seeks marriage of necessity, so that he is driven to ordinances by which he takes hold, and which remove from among them the ruin of jealousy that would drive them to killing.

This soul demands that all wealth be its own, by whatever way it may, of necessity, so that ordinances are needed by which one is made safe from another against the cheating, cunning, and deceit that would lead them to overpowering and fighting. And these souls, in their greed, stir in the seeking of livelihood, and dealings become necessary, so that, treachery and cunning being in the natures, ordinances are needed upon which the claimant and the denier proceed in extracting the right for its owner. Death, too, is necessary among them, and they cannot do without concealing the person of the dead from among them, whether by burial, by burning, by casting into the valleys, or otherwise, for else harm would return upon them, so that one is driven to adopt one of these acts.

These are six gates that run in the manner of genera, and beneath them fall the kinds whose existence is necessary among human beings without fail. As for what is not necessary, and is found at one time greater and at another lesser, these are the ordinances in the worship of Allah, the Most High, and in drawing near to Him through the knowledge of His limits, by which completeness is attained, and this falls under the genus of drawing near to Allah. All of these ordinances belong to the Law, and there must first be one who lays them down and inscribes them among human beings at the beginning of their existence. And since these ordinances exist with the existence of human beings, and Adam (عليه السلام) was of the human beings, together with his angels, the Law is established for him.

As for whoever was before Adam, his ruling is the ruling of one who is at the end of the seventh cycle, like for like. He was not free of the necessary ordinances we have mentioned; he was free only of the ordinances that fall under the genus of worship in drawing near to Allah, the Most High, which we have said may increase at one time and decrease at another, and by which the second completeness is gained. So Allah, the Most High, sent Adam (عليه السلام) to teach them and others what they had not known, and to bring them to completeness where they were deficient, for though they were distinguished above others by merit, they fell short of the degree of completeness by their leaving off the work of worship. He therefore laid down the Law, affirmed it, and called to the two acts of worship, in knowledge and in deed. Some of them followed and obeyed, and some refused and turned aside upon the six that went before, at the beginning of the cycles. Such was the affair of those people.

The author of al-Nusrah said that Noah (عليه السلام) was obliged to establish it, meaning the Law, because before his cycle there had been cycles given to violating the limits and following the opposites, such as al-Wadd (الود), al-Suwa (السواع), Yaghuth (يغوث), and others, so that it became obligatory upon Noah (عليه السلام) to establish it, that whoever perished might perish upon clear proof, and whoever lived might live upon clear proof.

We say that this saying of his does not require that Adam had no law; rather it requires that he had ordinances and a law. For he has affirmed as necessary that a cycle turned upon the violation of the limits and the following of opposites, and violation befalls the one followed only from the follower, by his abandoning his command and his ordinance. So the establishment of violation by his own saying makes the ordinances that are the Law, and by which the violation occurred, established for Adam (عليه السلام); and therefore the Law is established for him.

The author of al-Islah said that it has been related that Allah, mighty and majestic, commanded Adam (عليه السلام) to pitch a tent which He sent down to him from the Garden, and to make it his qiblah and the qiblah of his children; then Seth built after him the place of the Law, made its rulings firm, gathered into it revelation and interpretation, and established its branches and its roots and the firm reckoning of its obligations. We say that the saying of the author of al-Islah is established, since Adam has a law and ordinances, as have the other speaker-prophets.

The author of al-Nusrah said that in the composition of the world and its ordering, in the matter of pointing to the divine unity, there is that which makes one independent of the laying down of deeds and stations, and that the laying down of deeds and signs has removed many of creation from the divine unity and cast them into likening Him to creatures, and this is exceedingly impossible. He argued that the composition of the world, the ordering and wonders of His wisdom, and what lies in the composition of the world of bodies, the joining together of bodies, the pairing of contrary natures within differing bodies, and the measured persons, all point to the divine unity in a way that makes one independent of the laying down of deeds, of like actions and differing stations that signify agreeing meanings, of likenesses drawn upon a standing thing likened, and of proofs standing upon a clear thing indicated. What is in these proofs and in the deeds that are contradictory by the way of the outward, and what is in these likenesses struck to establish known limits, is itself a proof of the One, since the firm, wrought, and wondrous acts of the world and of the souls make one independent of deeds and signs. Indeed, he said, many of these signs, deeds, and outward words have removed most of creation from the divine unity and cast them into a likening that does not befit the least of His creatures. The sum of his saying is that in the composition of the world and its ordering, in the matter of pointing to the divine unity, there is that which makes one independent of the laying down of deeds and stations, and that the laying down of deeds and signs has removed many of creation from the divine unity and cast them into likening.

We say that this saying of his makes the sending of the messengers a superfluity of which there is no need. For if the world is itself firm and wrought and points to the divine unity, and the sending of the messengers (عليهم السلام) is for the call and for pointing to the divine unity, then to send them while a proof already stands, doing that for which they were sent, is a superfluity of which there is no need. I do not know how this escaped him, when it is plain that the world's being firm and wrought is not sufficient in the matter of pointing to the divine unity if there is none present who knows it and makes it plain. One benefits from the knowledge of the world's composition, its ordering, and the arrangement of its existents only when there is one whose rank has risen in knowledge through the limits' having taught him the manner of the deeds and the signs that revelation and the Law gather together, so that he reflects, draws out, and infers from it what he seeks of the knowledge of the limits of Allah, the Most High, and of His signs whose knowledge is made obligatory upon human beings. Allah, the Most High, says:

> [ حَتَّى إِذَا جَاءُوا قَالَ أَكَذَّبْتُمْ بِآيَاتِي وَلَمْ تُحِيطُوا بِهَا عِلْمًا ]

"Until, when they come, He says: Did you deny My signs when you did not encompass them in knowledge?" But if you did know, then the foremost in existence are the noble substances that are the angels brought near. So it is with the affair when there is need of the deeds, the signs, the alerting, and the teaching, since not everyone who has knowledge and works and is alerted is able to infer from the existents the true inference; and how much less when one does not know at all. In sum, the saying that leads to the nullifying of the ranks of the limits is a saying neither of our belief nor of the way of our masters, may (عليهم السلام). Perhaps this saying was only planted by the adversaries in the book, that it might be ascribed to the author of the book, and Allah knows best.

Furthermore, the deeds are not laid down for the divine unity alone, but for the setting straight of the soul in worship, for likeness to the highest assembly, and for the acquiring, through discipline, of the noble morals and generous habits by which the soul is made ready for union with what is above it, and for its being drawn out of the baseness of potency, that the honor of the intellect may be rescued by devotion to its obligations and attention to its marks. Were it not for the deeds and rites within the community, and for the completeness of souls through persevering in them and taking hold of them, souls would perish. It is for this that Allah, the Most High, did not grant the angels their claim when they supposed that the worship they were upon was sufficient in the matter of nearness to Allah, the Most High, and of completeness for them, and that they had no need, in what they were upon, of a vicegerent to teach them anything or to command them to work. He said:

> [ إِنِّي أَعْلَمُ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ ]

"Indeed, I know what you do not know." Allah, the Most High, said this because the states of being, by His decree and His wisdom, are held wavering between potency and act: at one time standing in potency, at another standing in act. And a thing, therefore, when it reaches the end of what it may reach of potency and act, returns backward: if it was in potency, toward act, and if it was in act, toward potency. It is like the hot thing that, when it reaches the limit of heat, the heat sinks away and coldness appears, as with what dissolves among bodies and the like; and like the cold thing that, when it reaches the limit of coldness, the coldness sinks away and heat appears, as with snow; and like the congealed thing whose parts are gathered from the mountains, which, when it reaches the end of that for which it congealed, namely dryness, dissolution appears in its parts, so they scatter and return to sand; and the like of these among the existents in the abode of nature. Knowledge and ignorance in the world of coming-to-be and passing-away are likewise: at one time ignorance stands in act and knowledge in potency, in the sense that ignorance is general through the abundance of its people, and knowledge lost through the fewness of its people.

The time was the time of ignorance's dominion and its reaching its end in act, and of knowledge's sinking within and reaching the limit of its end in potency, through the effacing of the traces of the teachers and the disruption of the marks of the laws and the deeds by the length of the age and the passing of the times. By the laws here I mean those ordinances and rites whose existence is not necessary. Those named after the angels, the best of them in themselves and their excellent ones who bore knowledge in that age, and whoever worships Allah upon an edge, related that what they were upon was sufficient in the matter of worship and completeness. But the affair was other than they estimated, since they were in need, for their completeness, of the knowledge of the limits foremost in existence, who are in the abode of origination and the first sending-forth, and of the knowledge of those later in existence, like the second sending-forth, and of working by that which gains them completeness. When they knew that their time was the time in which the cycle of ignorance reached its completeness, and that they were not in the cycle of knowledge nor in the cycle of works, He, the Most High, knew that to leave them as they were, while ignorance had reached its utmost and the time had become the time of the beginning of knowledge's appearance, would run counter to wisdom. So He, mighty and majestic, said in effect: I know of ignorance's reaching its end in the world in act, and the completion of its cycle and its appearance; and of knowledge's reaching its end in the world in potency, and the return of repentance to it in appearing, and the standing need, in its appearing in act, for the raising up of teachers and guides, what you do not know.

So Allah sent Adam (عليه السلام), making him a vicegerent for Himself, that knowledge might be shown forth, and that he might bring them to completeness in act, guide them to the knowledge of the limits, and teach them what they had not known. This was the beginning of the affair in the coming forth of knowledge from potency into act, through the existence of its people, from his days (عليه السلام); and its reaching-out extends to the one who arises, may the peace of Allah be upon him. Likewise it is said that when the one who arises (عليه السلام) appears, knowledge becomes in act, meaning that it becomes general and its people multiply, while ignorance sinks within, meaning that its people grow few and its root is cut off. And what is said by way of narration, that the treasures of the earth appear in his days and it casts them out to its surface, is a pointing to the one who arises and to the standing of knowledge in act, so that all that has been deposited in the depths of the revealed books and in the knotted symbols is shown forth. And since the angels, together with the existence of the heavens, the goodness of their ordering, and the firmness of the composition of the world's existents, did not have knowledge, nor had they completeness, nor any inference from these, except by what Adam (عليه السلام) taught them and called them to of the work, what the author of al-Nusrah mentioned was not fully examined, and the deeds, the signs, and the likenesses were not things one could do without in the matter of inference.

These deeds, these signs, and these likenesses are indispensable in the matter of inference from them concerning monotheism, politics, the correction of the soul, and its discipline in knowing the limits and in bringing them out into act. If that is so, then Adam (ع) is the author of a law and of customs to which he called, and which pointed to the limits of Allah and to His unity.

Chapter Twenty-One of Title IX

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Does the author of this discourse say that the creation in the great cycle did not know, since there was no apparent law resembling one another among them? Or does he say that the Qaim (ع), in raising the represented laws, nullifies the knowledge of monotheism and brings the people out to the assertion of divesting? For if the creation was not ignorant of monotheism, and if in the cycle of the Qaim likewise they do not remain ignorant of it while the laws are raised, then it would have been permissible for Adam (ع) to have called the people to monotheism without signs and without the striking of likenesses such as these, by his mere word.

And we say:

There has already gone forth from our discourse a treatment of knowledge and ignorance, and of how each of them is at one time in potentiality and at another in act, and in that is folded the explanation of the state in which the creation stood in the great cycle. Their being at the utmost limit of ignorance, according to the very saying of the author of al-Nusrah, makes Adam's call (ع) and his being a teacher of the landmarks of monotheism to his followers a mere superfluity that was not needed. For he has made it obligatory that the people already knew monotheism, and if they already knew monotheism, then Adam's calling them to the thing they already knew would be nothing but a superfluity of which there was no need. And that is impossible.

For the people were not in need of that by which the completeness of their souls is attained, and so Allah would not have sent Adam to them, nor made him His caliph among them, nor chosen him out from among them, nor made obligatory upon them obedience to him and a returning to him in what they did not know. The saying of the assembly named the angels, when they said,

ونحن نسبح بحمدك ونقدس لك

"And we glorify You with Your praise and sanctify You," was only their estimation that they were sufficient in glorification, sanctification, and monotheism, and that what they were already in was enough in the matter of worship and in the knowledge of the limits. But Allah knew of their affair what His mercy made obligatory, that Adam be chosen out from among them and sent to them, so that He might make him a caliph in teaching them, calling them to that by which their completeness is reached, of work and of knowledge together, which leads to the knowledge of the limits in which monotheism is made sound. For this He said,

اني اعلم ما لا تعلمون

"I know what you do not know," because He, exalted be He, knew that they were deficient, that ignorance had reached its end in act, that the work had come to begin in manifestation, and that they did not know that. So it became obligatory in wisdom to establish the office of teaching, which is the cause of the spreading of knowledge and the multiplying of its people. So Allah chose Adam, and he laid down a law, and he taught, as Allah, exalted be He, said:

وعلم آدم الأسماء كلها ثم عرضهم على الملائكة فقال انبثوني بأسماء هؤلاء ان كنتم صادقين قالوا سبحانك لا علم لنا إلا ما علمتنا انك انت العليم الحكيم قال يا آدم انبئهم باسمائهم فلمًا انبأم باسمائهم قال : الم اقل لكم اني اعلم غيب السماوات والارض

"And He taught Adam the names, all of them, then He presented them to the angels and said: Inform Me of the names of these, if you are truthful. They said: Glory be to You, we have no knowledge except what You have taught us; You are the Knowing, the Wise. He said: O Adam, inform them of their names. And when he had informed them of their names, He said: Did I not say to you that I know the unseen of the heavens and the earth," the verse. So the people were not knowing, and because they were so, Adam came to them as a teacher for them.

Chapter Twenty-Two of Title IX

And we say:

As for his saying and his affirmation that the creation was not, at the first, in the cycle of the Qaim (ع), ignorant of monotheism: had they not been ignorant, they could not have deduced it except from the side of those who give aid, in which lies the completeness of their essences and their forms; and there would have been no cause for Adam to be chosen out from among them to be the caliph of Allah, exalted be He, in teaching them what holds their good sooner and later. And it is impossible that this conception be pictured in those people together with the granting to them of a rank that would be their own.

And as for his saying, "nor in the cycle of the Qaim are they ignorant": if what he means is the state of the summons in the cycle of the Qaim (ع) before his manifestation and before his existence from the world of nature, when it is preserved by his caliphs and his summoners after the completion of the cycle of Muhammad al-Mustafa (ع) and his House, up to his time and the appearance of his signs in the world, then it is true. For knowledge at that time has raised high the beacon of its manifestation by the multitude of the learned. It is the time of which Allah, exalted be He, said,

يوم يجعل الولدان شيباً

"A day that will make the children grey-haired." But if what he means by his saying, "in the cycle of the Qaim they are not ignorant," is the state after the Qaim (ع), who is the possessor of the seventh cycle, and of the decisive utterance, and of the distinguishing of the people of the Garden from the people of the Fire, and of the closing of the door of teaching and of repentance, then it is not sound. For they do not remain knowing, nor are they of the people of knowledge, since that time is the time for the waters to overflow and be swallowed inward, and the time for dryness and barrenness to prevail and appear. And the words of the author of al-Nusrah in that are not those of one who has examined the matter to its end.

Chapter Twenty-Three of Title IX

In sum, the author of al-Nusrah only brought forward these sayings and these citations in support of the author of al-Mahsul in his refutation of the law of Adam. And Adam, in his summoning of them and in his being a prophet who teaches and guides, is not free of one of these: either he called them by encouragement toward the Garden and the neighborhood of the Lord of the Worlds, or he called them by warning of the Fire and of remoteness from the mercy of the best of creators, or he called them by both together.

If he called them by encouragement, then encouragement, when the thing encouraged is not sensible, cannot be except by the striking of likenesses and by reports about it in terms of what is known to the senses, of the kinds of pleasures and blessings and the good places, of dwellings, gardens, rivers, and trees, that its picture may fall in their minds. And that is a form of speech that requires interpretation. If he called them by warning, then likewise his speech cannot be except by the striking of likenesses for them, of that which is feared, drawn from the world of sense, that they may picture it, and in its interpretation it runs the course of the others that have gone before. And if he called them by both together, then his speech is not free of an apparent sense to be acted upon, and so it requires interpretation. If that is so, then the three divisions make obligatory a fixed apparent sense established for Adam, followed by an interpretation. So Adam, then, is the possessor of a law and of manifest customs.

Chapter Twenty-Four of Title IX

Then we say:

Adam (ع) is not free of one of these: either he called the people of his time to the obedience of Allah, exalted be He, and to obedience of him, by wisdom, or not by wisdom. And he does not do so devoid of wisdom, for that is impossible, and to hold it of him is a going out from religion. So if he called them by wisdom, and wisdom requires knowledge and work together, then Adam, then, is the possessor of a law that gathers knowledge and work, which are the worship of Allah, and by which perfection is attained and the ascent to the stations of honor.

Chapter Twenty-Five of Title IX

As for the Qaim (ع) and the saying that he raises the laws, we say:

To hold that belief is unsound. For the estimation of his existence from the world of nature does not arise by the way of the second emanation, because he does not nullify anything that pertains to the worship of Allah, exalted be He. Rather he is a seal for cycles that have passed, and a completion of a matter that was completed in his place. He removes the limits from their ranks by the falling of the referral in teaching about them, and the fullness of the matter in the new creation, of which Allah, exalted be He, said,

بل هم ليس من خلق جديد

after He has established a decree in the souls of any from whom there would run the course of a foundation among the possessors of the empty cycles. For this Allah, exalted be He, said, making plain the possessor of the Resurrection,

واذا السماء انفطرت وإذا الكواكب انتشرت

"And when the heaven is cleft asunder, and when the planets are scattered." And He said,

إذا الشمس كورت واذا النجوم انكدرت واذا الجبال سيرت واذا العشار عطلت

"When the sun is folded up, and when the stars fall, and when the mountains are set moving, and when the pregnant camels are left untended." All these likenesses are struck upon the limits and the summonses in their passing away from their ranks, not upon the laws and the customs of the works, whose landmarks, with the passing of time, are effaced by the absence of teachers, except what was necessary in its existence, according to what we have already mentioned. And that time in which this act appears, of the closing of the door of teaching and the ceasing of the summonses on the part of the possessor of the greatest Resurrection, is the beginning of the return of knowledge to potentiality, so that it is swallowed inward, and the beginning of the increase of ignorance and its standing in act. So the appearance of the signs in that manner suffices the one who is sufficient and has come of age. May Allah make us and the community of the believers of the people of the divine graces, by the following of His friends, and may He gather us with Muhammad and Ali and the pure Imams (عليهم السلام), the lights of His purity, by His bounty and His mercy.

Chapter Twenty-Six of Title IX

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Everyone who says that monotheism is not grasped except by the signs and the works has thereby ruled that the First Intellect, the Soul, the angels, the Jadd, the Fath, and the Khayal did not know monotheism, since there were no signs and no works among them or between them.

And we say:

The one who says that does not mean the First Intellect, nor the high limits, but rather the intellects that are in the world of the body, which is the second emanation, since their attaining of completeness is by teaching and by obligation, by knowledge, by signs and works, and by a standing in acquisition. As for the First Intellect and the transcendent limits that are separate from bodies, they did not attain completeness by any of that; rather they gained their perfections from the very beginning of their existence, by origination and by emanation. And it is not permissible that the ruling of this saying be run upon those. And as for what the author of al-Nusrah brought forward concerning the meaning of the seven stars, their fixed positions, their return, and their rectitude, and concerning the six days in which Allah created the heavens and the earth and what is between them, and as for what he mentioned of the resolve, the author of al-Islah has left nothing of the interpretation of that and its meanings that he did not bring forward, and in it is sufficiency, dispensing with an Arabization in the interpretation that scarcely balances and does not match.

Chapter Twenty-Seven of Title IX

The author of al-Islah said:

The single line does not necessitate the opening except by the appearance of the second line, for the line indicates the Speaker, and the opening indicates the law. And the form of a thing does not of necessity appear except after its completion. Just as the opening appeared after the second line, likewise the law of the first was not completed except by the appearance of the second Speaker. So when the second Speaker appeared, the law of the first was completed, and its form appeared in perfection. And likewise the six openings are completed by the seven lines, for there is no law before the first of the Speakers, and no law at the appearance of the seventh; rather the six laws are from the Speakers whose seventh is the Qaim. And if you draw seven lines, six openings appear from among them, according to these likenesses: one, two, three, four, five, six. And were the analogy to be drawn upon that.

## 18. Cycles, Prophets, and the Ark

Were the analogy then drawn to say that the first of the Speakers has no law, five openings would have to appear between the seven lines. But six openings appeared from among the seven lines, and by this the seven lines became the masters of the seven cycles, the six openings became the six laws, and the law was made obligatory upon Adam (ع).

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Concerning the chapter of the six openings between the seven lines, and his inference from the lines upon the Prophets and from the openings upon the six laws, the matter is not as he stated. Rather, the seven lines stand for the higher letters, since every Prophet inscribed a single letter of them; and the six openings stand for the cycles of the Imams: between Adam and Noah, who are the seven Imams; between Noah and Abraham in the second cycle; between Abraham and Moses in the third cycle; and so on up to Muhammad (ع). No further cycle remains for the two Qaims save the cycle of their successors and substitutes in the world. Thus six openings fell across five lines, inasmuch as between the cycles of the Imams there fell five Speakers who were the masters of the laws, in this likeness:

Adam - Noah - Abraham - Moses - Jesus - Muhammad - al-Qaim
the foundation - the foundation - the foundation - the foundation - the imams - the imams - the imams

Just as no Imams occur after the Qaim (ع), so he is not charged with establishing the law; and likewise, since no Imams occurred before Adam (ع), he too was not charged with establishing the law. For if the law, in its establishment, must be preceded by Imams, it is likewise followed by them. The foundation of Noah (ع) preceded the cycle of the foundation of Adam (ع), so Noah was followed by Imams, and therefore the establishment of the law fell to him. Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad (عليهم السلام) were the same. But the Qaim (ع) was preceded by the cycle of Muhammad (ع) and is followed by no Imams, and therefore the establishment of the law does not fall to him. Likewise Adam was followed by the Imams of his cycle and was preceded by no other Imams, and therefore the establishment of the law did not fall to him. This is what they say.

We say:

The statement of the author of al-Islah agrees with what he has indicated, save for his saying, within the body of his words, that "just as the opening appeared after the second line, so the law of the first was not fulfilled except by the appearance of the second Speaker." This is a sick saying and not upright. For the law of Adam (ع) is itself its own foundation, and he it was who raised up its foundation for it. Were it permissible that the law of Adam was not fulfilled until the coming of the Speaker who holds the second cycle, it would likewise be permissible to say that the law of Muhammad (ع) is not complete and is fulfilled only by the coming of the Qaim, who holds the seventh cycle (ع); and that is among the impossibilities of speech, for the law is a completed stature that requires no increase and no decrease. Had the author of al-Islah instead said that the first cycle, which belongs to Adam (ع), was not fulfilled except by the appearance of the holder of the second cycle, who is Noah (ع), and that likewise no one of the cycles is fulfilled except by the appearance of the one that follows it, so that he set "the first cycle" in place of his phrase "the law," his words would have been consistent and balanced. And Allah forgives us and him through His mercy.

Chapter Twenty-Eight of Title IX

As for the saying of the author of al-Nusrah concerning the line and the opening, and his interpretation of the six openings upon the Imams, it is sound, provided that the lines stand for the Speakers and not for the higher letters, and that the openings stand for the duration of their command in the cycle and not for the foundations. Even so, this does not require that Adam (ع) have no law; rather it makes the law obligatory precisely by the presence of the Imams after him. For the presence of the Imams is on account of the duty to preserve the station of the Speaker, his law, and his designs within his community; and were it not for that, no obedience to an Imam after a Speaker would be obligatory. As for the five lines and the six openings, the author of al-Islah may take them and set the lines upon the five Possessors of Resolve and the openings upon the cycles of the masters of the six laws, since the foremost thing in interpretation is that the thing be identical and balanced when it is stated, so that its place in the souls is the more exalted.

In sum, we say:

The cycle is of two kinds, a major cycle and a minor cycle. The major cycle belongs to the Speakers, whose place the Imams after them preserve within their community; and the minor cycle belongs to the completing foundations, who seal the weeks. If the lines are interpreted upon the masters of the major cycles, who are the Speakers, then the openings can only be the duration of their governance of their community through the Imams who come after them in the cycle that is sealed by the coming of the last of their like, and its measure is known. And if they are interpreted upon the masters of the seven minor cycles, who are the completing Imams in every cycle, then the openings can only be the duration of the command of the Imams in each week, together with their completers whose cycle is sealed by the seventh of them; and their number is known, as is the number of Imams in each cycle, and the number of the appendages according to the existing higher bodies already described. This will be set down in a book other than this one, if Allah the Exalted permits.

Chapter Twenty-Nine of Title IX

The author of al-Nusrah said:

By way of example, the words of Allah the Exalted:

((كما بدأكم تعودون))
"As He originated you, so shall you return."

That is, as the beginning of the matter was, which was not from the side of the realities, so shall the end of the matter be.

We say:

Even if this is an interpretation, the proper face of it is to say what corresponds. The words of Allah the Exalted are a reference to what comes to pass after the Qaim (ع): the return of knowledge, after its going forth into act and its abundance, to potency and to fewness; and the coming of all people after him to be as they were in the time of Adam; and the return of ignorance, after its having been in potency and fewness, to act and to abundance, by reason of the measured wisdom therein. Blessed be Allah, the best of creators.

Chapter Thirty of Title IX

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Again, had the author of al-Islah looked at what lies in his name, I mean Adam, of the indication that he had no law, that would have become plain to him. For his name is of three letters, which are Alif, Dal, and Mim (الف، دال، ميم), and they are neither compounded nor composed; rather each letter of them stands apart from the other, a clear indication that he was not the author of the composition of the law. But the five Prophets who came after him have names that are composed and compounded, such as Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad (نوح، إبراهيم، موسى، عيسى، محمد), which is evidence that each of them was the master of a law. This is what he said.

We say:

If the separateness of the letters of Adam's name requires that Adam (ع) have no law, then the fact that these letters are in themselves not separate requires that he have a law. For the Alif of the name is composed of alif, lam, and fa (الف، لام، فاء); the Mim of it is composed of mim, ba, and mim (ميم، باء، ميم); and likewise the Dal. If separateness does not oblige a law for him, then non-separateness does oblige a law. Yet inference of this kind is an impossibility and does not hold. For were it sound, it would follow that the Qaim (ع) is the author of composition, since his name, as the Prophet (ع) reported it, is Muhammad, and Muhammad is composed of four letters. And since it is impossible that the Qaim be the master of a law while his name is composite, it is void that the separateness of the letters of Adam's name should indicate that he is not the author of composition and of a law, just as it is void that the joining of the letters of the name of the Qaim (ع), who is Muhammad, and its being composite, should indicate that he is the master of a law. When that is void, no weight attaches to inference of this sort. So the law of Adam (ع) is established, and praise be to Allah, Lord of the Worlds.

Chapter Thirty-One of Title IX

The author of al-Islah said:

As for the saying that Noah (ع) was the first to raise up his foundation, and the likening of him to the ship, enough has already been said of it in the chapter on the foundations.

We say:

Noah (ع) was singled out by the ark because he was the first of the Possessors of Resolve, and the first to undertake the abrogation of the laws and to compose a new law. His law was in the station of the ship, and that with which he abrogated the former law was in the station of the flood, for by it he destroyed the people of the earth save those who answered his law and boarded his ship, that is, who established obedience to his foundation. The meaning is that his law endured through his lifetime; and when he had perfected it, he committed the matter to his foundation and commanded him to raise up the summons upon his law, and commanded the appendages to hearken to him, so that they attained the flowing grace only through him. Whoever obeyed his foundation was in the ship, and whoever disobeyed him and held to the first law was drowned. Do you not see that those who worshipped Wadd, Suwa, Yaghuth, Yauq, and Nasr (وداً، وسواعاً، ويغوث، ويعوق، ونسراً) were described as drowned by the flood, for He said:

((من خطيئاتهم اغرقوا فادخلوا النار))
"Because of their sins they were drowned, then made to enter the Fire."

The author of al-Nusrah said, and we say:

If the station of the ship is like the station of the outward law, then whoever held to it from the people of the outward was saved, and the veiled, true summons is annulled. Moreover, what is outward in the Book of Allah the Exalted is other than what the author of al-Islah went to. For Allah the Exalted described the beginning of the command of Noah (ع), and his being sent to his people, and his calling them to his law, and then commanded him to fashion the Ark. This account is set down in the Sura of Hud; and had the ship been the outward law, Allah would have commanded him first to fashion the Ark. But the story turns upon the fact that he first composed the outward law and called creation to make use of it, and only then commanded him to fashion the Ark, meaning to raise up his legatee. The evidence of this is the words of Allah the Exalted:

((واصنع الفلك))
"And fashion the Ark,"

up to His words:

((وكلما مرّ عليه ملأ من قومه سخروا منه))
"And whenever a company of his people passed him, they mocked him,"

meaning that whenever Noah (ع) raised up his legatee, the nobles of his people mocked him and marveled at him, because he was the first to inaugurate the raising up of the legatee; and for that they mocked him. This is what they said.

We say:

As for the saying of the author of al-Islah and his interpretation of the ship upon the outward of the new law, it is because whoever entered beneath its rulings had it become for him in the station of a ship, so that he was delivered from abasement and humiliation and every kind of affliction, just as the one within a ship is delivered from drowning; and this meaning is admissible. As for the author of al-Nusrah and his interpretation of the ship upon the summons and the legatee, it is because whoever entered the guiding summons and bound himself to obedience to the foundation was made safe from drowning in error, away from the unity of Allah the Exalted and the knowledge of His limits, that summons being for him in the station of guidance. Both interpretations are sound in this respect, the one in the matter of this world and the other in the matter of religion, and neither of them diminishes the other, for each of them stands upon its own head.

Chapter Thirty-Two of Title IX

As for the saying of the author of al-Nusrah that Noah was the first to raise up the legatee, by the interpretation that came to him, it does not hold. For the prophethood of Adam (ع) and his law are established, and it is confirmed that Seth (ع) was his legatee, the foremost in the law and its establisher under him. Reason itself indicates that each of the Speakers must of necessity have a legatee who takes his benefit from him, especially since every mover of necessity has a moved, the mover being one even if many are moved from him. And since the Speaker in his age is a mover of all souls by his summons to seek subsistence through the raising up of the two worships, it must of necessity be that he have a moved who runs in the course of a learner from him, even if many learners learn from him; and the true learner from him is the one whom we name the legatee. Adam, being a Speaker, must have a legatee, and so must every Speaker. And since Adam did raise up a legatee for himself, it is not permissible to hold any belief that contradicts that.

Chapter Thirty-Three of Title IX

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Following his interpretation of what Allah the Exalted related concerning the son of Noah:

((سآوي الى جبل يعصمني من الماء))
"I shall take refuge on a mountain that will shield me from the water,"

and Noah's reply:

((لا عاصم اليوم من امر الله))
"There is no shield today from the command of Allah,"

he held that the matter was, before Noah, in the hands of the appendages, since the son did not know the rank of the legatee; and that had Adam possessed a legatee, his son would have known of it. This is what he said.

We say:

This saying of his contradicts what he himself said in the chapter of the cycles: that the Imams after Adam (ع) took charge of his cycle. I do not know how this escaped him. I suspect that the copy of the book has been altered, added to, and diminished through the fewness of those who attend to the matter of religion and their neglect of correcting what they write by collation and by reading it back to the people of knowledge and religion; and Allah is the one whose help is sought.

The interpretation of "the son of Noah" is that the son ascribed to Noah was not of his loins. Rather he was a man of great worldly standing, whom Noah had advanced, like the adversary in the cycle of Muhammad our Prophet (ع). He took his benefit from Noah, learned what he had, and by his own strength became a son to him in rank, so that he reckoned it within himself, and the people too imagined that he would be his legatee. But when the command of Allah the Exalted came, that he fashion the Ark, that is, that Noah confer the legateeship upon another below him in accordance with what Allah had commanded, Noah called the people who followed him to enter beneath his command and obedience, and that is His saying:

((اركبوا فيها))
"Embark in it."

So this man held back from what others had entered into, namely obedience to his foundation, by his refusal to enter the ship. Noah (ع) called him with his words:

((يا بني اركب معنا ولا تكن مع الكافرين))
"O my son, embark with us, and be not with the disbelievers,"

calling him to obedience and to binding himself to the command, especially with the desire that he return to his beginning, reckoning that he would act as Noah had seen of his former goodly compliance with his commands. But upon Noah's calling him, and his despairing utterly of that rank he had hoped for, he grew hypocritical and arrogant and refused to obey his foundation in what had come to pass. Noah said that the command of Allah lay upon him: were he not to relinquish his headship, it would be nullified; and the like of this adversary inclined against our guardian (ع). Noah admonished him at one time and warned him at another, saying that the command of Allah lay in it, and that if he did not heed the admonition his headship would depart and abasement would encompass him, and he would see none to take his part nor to help him in his affair; and that is His saying:

((لا عاصم اليوم من امر الله))
"There is no shield today from the command of Allah."

When the son heard this from him, he showed rebellion and said, in his ignorance, that this matter would not be brought to completion for Noah, that he should set the whole of the headship within his own house; and that even if constraint and injury and harm appeared, he would be supported by chiefs who would strengthen his hand and help him to ward off abasement from himself; and that is his saying:

((سآوي الى جبل يعصمني من الماء))
"I shall take refuge on a mountain that will shield me from the water."

The water here is that which is poured out upon the force that slays, and the mountain is that which he clings to of men and partisans. So he set his purpose, and he and his like took up his cause, and they perished in his cycle, and there remained only those who bound themselves to obedience to his foundation.

Allah has set down such tidings in His noble Book as an admonition to the community concerning what befalls the command of the foundation through him who is intransigent toward it and does not bind himself to its obedience. Now He indicates it through our Prophet's word, "O children of Adam"; now He points to it by the she-camel of Salih; and now by the son of Noah; that they may know that the states of the cycle of Muhammad are like the states of the bygone cycles, and that He did not single out Muhammad and his foundation (عليهم السلام) with the intransigence of their community toward them and its hostility toward them. This is a custom that has run through all the Prophets and legatees, reaching to the time of the seventh. The refuge sought by the son of Noah was not to the appendage, but to the people of the outward who were in a state of hypocrisy like his own; he sought their aid, and the sword encompassed them by the power of Allah, and Allah purified the earth of them. That is the flood that overspread outwardly and inwardly. So likewise shall it be in the cycle of Muhammad (ع): some of it has already come true and passed, and some of it shall come true and come to pass by the leave of Allah, and none knows that save the discerning one among the people of the Alawi summons, whose lights Allah has spread abroad.

## 19. The Creator of the Thing and the No-Thing

The Creator of the Thing and the No-Thing

The First Section of the Tenth Chapter

We say:

The author of al-Mahsul (المحصول), may Allah have mercy on him, when he exerted himself, and strove, and discharged the right of the dawa and its sons, and opened the doors of the landmarks by what he composed of his books that stand over their expanses; and when Sheikh Abu Hatim, may Allah have mercy on him, corrected of it what he corrected: most people supposed that this correction was a fault in the author of al-Mahsul, and so they attacked him on account of the apology which the author of the correction made for that in his book. They faulted it and overturned it. That is a corrupt judgment, an act inclining away from the truth, and the matter is other than what they imagined.

For the speaker (ع), when he knew, at the time of laying down the order of things, that the sciences of religion are too many for a single soul beneath the imam to encompass, or to be able to preserve them all in a single utterance, arranged for them many persons beneath the imamate, that they might cooperate among themselves over all the sciences, their awareness, and their retention, like the twelve hujjas. So each of them benefits, from those to whom Allah the Exalted has entrusted the reins of guidance among the righteous imams (عليهم السلام), in the abstruse matters, their inner and their outer, according to their capacities and their strength in reception and deduction, that they might be to others in guidance as the imams (عليهم السلام) are in exposition and instruction. Religion, through them, is like a single person who gathers his parts together, and they are to it like the senses that perceive things: if one of them misses something, another perceives it, without this second perception being a defect against the one that missed the perception, or against the others.

If that is so, then Abu Hatim's correction, may Allah raise his degree, of what he corrected from the book of al-Mahsul, is no fault against him, nor a deficiency against the author of al-Mahsul, nor a challenge against either of them. It is only what we have said: that the persons ranked for the correction of the sciences of religion are like the senses. If one of the senses misses to perceive some thing of the world of nature, the other does not miss it, but cooperates over it, discharging the right of religion and confirming the obligation owed to the Lord of the Worlds.

Since that is so, and since I have seen in the book of al-Mahsul what was most binding upon Abu Hatim, most obligatory for him to expound and to correct, which he did not correct, nor speak upon, but passed over, the truth of cooperation obliged me to discharge the right of religion by clarifying what is folded within what he brought forward, of what it is not permissible to hold as belief in the chapter of monotheism and the first Creator (المبدع الأول), apart from the other branches which, if Allah aids by deferring the term, we shall discuss in other than this book. So I did it. It is not permissible for me to apologize for that, since the souls are, for religion, all one, even if I apologize to my own soul. Allah accepts from us what we offer to draw near to Him of the guardianship (walaya) that leads to the good pleasure of Allah, Lord of the Worlds, and gathers us, by His grace, both the past and the renewed, the foremost of us and the latecomer, with our pure loyalists (mawali) in the Abode of Holiness; He is Munificent, Generous.

The Second Section of the Tenth Chapter

The author of al-Mahsul said:

In the chapter of monotheism: He is the Creator of the thing and of the no-thing, the rational, the illusory, the intellectual, the logical. I mean what falls beneath these elements, and what does not fall beneath them.

We say:

By this saying he has necessitated that among the existents which are from Allah the Exalted, falling beneath His origination (ibda), there is that which is not perceived by reason, nor by illusion, nor by thought, and of which no report is given by logic. Yet its existence is impossible, while he would make belief in it licit. For that which is not perceived by reason, illusion, or thought, and of which logic gives no report, if it had existence, could not but be either prior to the intellect in its existence, or with the intellect in its existence, or posterior to the intellect in its existence.

It is impossible that it be prior to the intellect, for the intellect is the very act behind which is none but Allah the Exalted, from whom it issued and from whom it came to be. It is pure origination, and the true existence (الوجود الحق) which is the first existent from Allah, glory be to Him.

It is impossible, too, that its existence be together with the very intellect; for the intellect is the very intellect that is the origination, and the intellect, at its coming-to-be, is but a single essence, not two. Further, were its existence to be with the intellect, they being distinct yet sharing in existence, it would follow that Allah the Exalted, from whom it came to be, is a manifold originating cause, or that there is with Him another; exalted is He above that with a great exaltation. For it belongs to the effect that its existence be according to its cause, and their being two distinct effects requires two causes, or a cause made manifold by relations, such that the existence of each of them is from a relation within it other than the other's, primarily. So it is impossible that two distinct effects exist from a single cause whose essence is preserved from difference by relations and additions. And if its existence with the intellect requires this, then its existence is impossible, for Allah the Exalted is sanctified above every mode of multiplicity, and additions, and othernesses, and above whatever would make Him a cause.

It is impossible, too, that its existence be posterior to the intellect; for were its existence posterior to the intellect and from it, then knowledge of it would not escape the minds, nor would its definition be hard for them. Rather they would perceive it and define it, just as they defined all the other existents, sensible and intelligible, in the arena of affirmation and negation. Since, then, in its existence it is not free of the three divisions, and each division of them necessitates that its existence be impossible, it is established that its existence is impossible, and to hold it is misguidance.

The Third Section of the Tenth Chapter

Then, had the author of al-Mahsul not said, following his affirmation of the thing and the no-thing, that Allah originated them both, I mean what falls beneath these elements and what does not fall beneath them, we would have said that by "the thing" he intended what is described among bodies, and by "the no-thing" what is not described with the attributes of bodies, from the world of origination, upon the error of him who says that, believing it. But since he brought forward what folds within it the affirmation of the impossible, it resulted only in one's believing, among the existents, in something that shares with Allah in His transcendence in some manner, so that corruption becomes general. And we have shown that this is impossible.

Then we say:

"The thing" is known to be a common term under which fall all the existents, of substances and accidents, sensible and intelligible. His intention in his saying "the no-thing" cannot but be either what some of the sages held, that it is an affirmation of that which is other than a thing, an affirmation stripped of what confines the thing to all its two divisions, of substances and accidents, and raised to the corrected form of the privative proposition, and they alluded to it, saying: that is like our saying "not-the-man," which is an affirmation of everything that is not a man, whereby the one reported of is stripped from us by existence, without affirming what belongs to no thing. They said: it belongs to the particle لا, when by it is intended a specific entity so as to remove it from existence, negating it of that which it is, without affirming of it something other than itself, as in his saying "the thing," which is a universal entity under which every existent falls: when we intend it by the particle لا, we say لا شيء, and the very entity of the thing becomes made to vanish from that in which it is a thing, without affirmation, it being other than it. Or, if by it is intended a particular entity within that entity, and something other is affirmed:

then if his intention is the affirmation of that which is a not-thing, his saying that Allah is its originator is an error. For the no-thing, being that which no attribute befalls, and which is not in a state like that of being a substance or an accident, and the negation of the attributes of the thing from Allah the Exalted being a transcendence and a sanctification, He, the Exalted, being neither substance nor accident, then the attributes of the thing are no attribute of His, nor from Him, nor is He, the Exalted, like them. So it is as though by his saying "the originator of the thing and the no-thing" he said: the originator of His own essence, which is other than the thing, and the originator of the thing, which is other than His essence. And that is an error.

And if his intention is the negation of the very entity of the thing, his saying that Allah the Exalted is its originator is an error. For the thing is the origination, and it is the very entity of existence, and the no-thing is the negation of origination and the nullification of existence. And if the no-thing is the negation of origination, how can it be sound that He is its originator, when He has negated it from that impossible thing?

The Fourth Section of the Tenth Chapter

The author of al-Mahsul said:

The Originator of things, not from a thing; He alone, and no thing with Him, everlastingly. So when we say "He, and no thing with Him," we have negated the thing and the no-thing, and made them both originated; and we have negated every form, simple and composite, from His identity (huwiyya); and we have made everything upon which speech falls, or upon which speech does not fall, an originated effect in truth, finite; and that the no-thing is after the thing, since the coming-to-be of its name came to be only at the appearing of the thing.

We say:

Enough has already passed of the discourse upon the no-thing. His saying, "the Originator of things, not from a thing only, He and no thing with Him, everlastingly," folds within it what is not permissible to hold as belief. For "no-thing" is, with the sages, the affirmation of that which is not a thing, according to what we have mentioned. So when he says "He, and no thing with Him," it is as though he said "He, and that which is not a thing, with Him, everlastingly," and that is associationism (shirk).

In sum: there is not, among the tongues existing between mankind, that by which expression can be made of Allah the Exalted in a manner befitting Him. For all that the composed and simple words and letters announce is originated, and words, letters, and languages are instruments for mankind in making one another understand what is within themselves. They are originated, and they indicate only what is, like their own state, originated, as we have explained in our treatise known as al-Rawda (الروضة).

He, the Exalted, being beyond the minds and the perceptions, and behind a veil from the understandings and thought, the ways of inquiry are cut off short of Him. When one is driven to affirm Him, after the knowledge that all that the tongues pronounce and the lips move with falls upon what He has originated and brought into being, glory be to Him, there is no expression borrowed for affirming Him without affirming an attribute or a plurality, save the expression هو, which Allah the Exalted affirmed after the exception in His saying:

شهد الله انه لا إله إلا هو

Allah bears witness that there is no god but He.

He said "but He," and did not say "but Allah," nor "but other than Allah"; rather He said "He." Likewise Imam al-Muizz (ع) pointed to it in his book known as Tawil al-Sharia (تأويل الشريعة), saying that it is "He" only. For this expression, though borrowed, is, from the minds, an affirmation of that which is outside them, without attribute or plurality.

This, whereas the treading of the path of affirming Him, the Exalted, by the use of the particle لا in the negation of every kind of the existents, is more attaining to the intent than the bare safety from likening (tashbih) and stripping (tatil), and is more fitting and worthier, in the view of the scholars and the jurists among the people of religion.

The Fifth Section of the Tenth Chapter

And his saying:

"If we say He, and no thing with Him, we have negated the thing and the no-thing, and made them both originated." From where, then, is the existence sound of what is said to be a no-thing among what falls beneath origination? And the existence of what is said to be a no-thing, it being prior to origination, and it being other than Allah, such that its negation would be sound? Nay: there is nothing but the thing, and the thing that is the willed-thing (al-mashi); and the willed-thing is that which we have negated to be existent, in that there is, in the languages, that which expresses it, because of the greatness of His affair, the Exalted, and the shortfall of the composed, originated letters from signifying in a manner befitting His majesty, the Exalted.

The thing that is the willed-thing is other than Allah, who brought it into being, not from a thing, it having an entity and a right in existence upon all its divisions. Then the no-thing is a negation of the very entity of the thing, which is pure origination, according to what discourse has already passed upon. And if the no-thing is the negation of the very entity of origination, which is the very entity of existence and the true existence, how does it come to be, in existence, an originator? Those sayings are not sound.

The Sixth Section of the Tenth Chapter

And his saying:

"We have negated every form, simple and composite, from His identity, and we have made everything upon which speech falls, or upon which speech does not fall, an originated effect in truth."

We say:

How does that upon which speech does not fall, the speech from which all the existents came to be, become, all of it, an originated effect? That is an error.

The Seventh Section of the Tenth Chapter

And his saying:

"The no-thing is after the thing, since the coming-to-be of its name came to be from it at the appearing of a thing."

The no-thing is not a name that falls upon an entity having a share in existence. For "the thing" is a name, and the لا, which is the particle of negation, has negated it; and the negation of the name does not become a name for other than that of which the name was a name, nor is report made by it of other than what the sages mentioned: that folded within it is the affirmation of that which has no name. Discourse has already passed upon that which contains the answer to it, and in it is sufficiency.

The Eighth Section of the Tenth Chapter

The author of al-Mahsul said:

In the chapter of the first Creator: that the Maker, the Exalted, originated the world at a single stroke; that is, He originated the intellect as a single whole, and there emerged within it, by His determining, the forms of the two worlds and what is in them, without those forms being pointed to by their identities before the intellect. Rather the intellect's knowledge falls upon them, and they are, with it, known. Yet the intellect has preceded them in act; so, whether in act or in potency, it and the forms are all together.

We say:

This knowledge, which is the intellect that he mentioned as originated, within which emerged the forms of the two worlds and what is in them, cannot be the first intellect existent from Allah the Exalted, first of all. For that which is prior in existence is independent of conceiving that by which it exists, since its existence is bound to its Originator who brought it into being. It is not incumbent upon it to know more than its knowledge of its own essence, and that it is originated, and that its existence is not by its own essence but by another, which is its Originator, and that it is a cause for the existence of what is below it. It is like the imam, who is not obliged to know others below him, and knows no more than his knowledge that he is the cause of deliverance for those below him, and a mediator between the one above him and the one below him. Rather it is incumbent upon him to know the one above him, and incumbent upon others to know him, and to know others whose existence and rank preceded him, that they may be delivered thereby. For this reason posteriority in existence obliges the posterior to conceive all that preceded it in existence among the limits, that this may earn it subsistence and complete existence.

## 20. Creativity Is the Creator

If this is so, then what he has described is the intellect that emanates from the world of nature, not the First Intellect, and this confirms what we have said. For he holds that it brought forth within it the images of the worlds without those images being pointed to by their own identities before the intellect, but rather that the knowledge of the intellect falls upon them, down to his words: as for in potency, it and the images are all together. That saying of his affirms what subsists in potency, and reports of that whose existence is not in act. But the First Intellect does not subsist in act by way of potency, and it cannot be said that within it there is anything subsisting in potency; for were it so, there would have to precede it, of its own kind of existence, something subsisting in act, so as to bring it out into act. Yet it is void that anything should exist together with the First Intellect, or precede it. And once that is void, the saying of the author of al-Mahsul holds true only of that which is posterior in existence, behind which there is no further existence, and whose substance is realized by knowing what preceded it in existence among the worlds. That is the second thing emanated from the world of nature, like the Speaker who possesses the images of the worlds; and the sum of these images has come to subsist in act and to be emanated in act.

In the ninth chapter of the tenth title, the author of al-Mahsul said: Since the cause of the intellect was the unity of the Almighty, and unity is eternal, the intellect became eternal.

Upon this we say: By his saying he has made necessary something that precedes the First Intellect in its existence, namely the cause he named unity. But we have said that nothing at all precedes the intellect in its existence beyond God; so it is worthier of eternity, since it is impossible that anything else exist. Unity, then, is not the cause of the intellect, other than it such that it precedes the intellect; rather it is the very essence of the intellect, and the intellect is the very essence of unity, and it is the One. It is the cause and it is the effect; it is creativity and it is the Creator; it is the Perfect and the Complete; it is the Eternal and the everlasting; it is Existence and the Existent, by a single essence. The meanings these names require are merited only by relating it to that from which its existence comes, which is God the Almighty, or by relating it to what is below it, or by relating it to itself, because the essence in its existence is manifold. Sufficient proof of this is his own word in this chapter concerning creativity: the Creator, from the standpoint of the Creator, is one, and its form is the form of the intelligible. If that is so, and unity is the very essence of the intellect, then his saying that the intellect became eternal by the eternity of its cause is impossible; for the intellect is not something other than the cause such that it became eternal through it, but rather it is the cause and the cause is it. An attribute like this, that its eternity is from the eternity of its cause, holds true only of the emanated intellects that come from the world of nature, such as the Speakers (النطقاء) and the Foundations (الأسس) and their followers (عليهم السلام), whose intellects are one thing and that by which they become eternal another; for the cause of their being sanctified and eternal is those separate eternal intellects called the angels brought near, and the extension of their lights to them, and their union with them. The words of the author of al-Mahsul, then, bear upon the intellects that arise in the world of nature, not upon the measure in the world of creation. And since his words and their meanings are directed toward the intellects in the abode of nature, to conceive that great and majestic limit, and the rank, under this description, is impossible.

In the tenth chapter of the tenth title, we then say: The meaning of his words, that unity is the cause of the intellect, cannot escape one of these senses: either that it stands to it in the place of its Originator, the Almighty, which he named unity; or that it stands to it in the place of prime matter; or that it stands to it in the place of form among the existents in the abode of body; or that it stands to it in the place of the end for whose sake it was brought into being, being its very self and essence. It is void that it stand to it in the place of its Originator; for if unity is the cause of the intellect, and a cause is a thing, and a thing whose existence is not by itself, then he would in effect have made God the Almighty, whom he named unity, a creator originated after having been a Creator, by making Him dependent on another in the very sense his own words convey; and that is impossible. Then, since unity is other than God, and that which is other than God is a created, originated thing, he would have made it necessary that God, whom he named unity, be a created, originated thing; and this is impossible. It is void that it stand to it in the place of prime matter among the existents, because of the impossibility that anything exist and run in existence together with God the Almighty, so that something else be found with Him, as the argument has preceded. It is void that it stand to it in the place of form among the existents by which it was found, because form cannot exist except through that which stands to it in the place of matter, and the existence of matter together with God the Almighty has been shown void, by which form would exist; and further, God the Almighty is far above being multiplied by forms, which are the mark of origination, so that He should become a creator after having been the Creator. Exalted and magnified is God above that. And it is void that it stand to it in the place of that for whose sake it was brought into being, because the intellect was not brought into being for the sake of unity; for had it been brought into being for unity, no multiplicity would have been brought into being. Yet multiplicity has been found; so it was not for the sake of unity that it was brought into being. And when these senses of his words, that unity is the cause of the intellect, are shown void and prevented from being, it is established that the essence of unity and the cause are one and the same, and its right. And since the intellect is the very essence of unity and the cause, his saying that the intellect became eternal by the eternity of its cause, while intending by that saying the First, which is the First Existent, is not sound.

In the eleventh chapter of the tenth title, the author of al-Mahsul said: Since the form was made manifest within it from unity, likewise the abstracted form became eternal before it, because the intellect became eternal in its entirety, not in part.

We say: This is an attribute of something in whose potency lies that form made manifest within it, and it is impossible to say this of that majestic limit which subsists in act, not in potency. And if it is so, his saying is sound only of the emanated intellects, such as the intellects of the Speakers (النطقاء), the Foundations (الأسس), the Imams, and their followers (عليهم السلام), which came forth into intellect by their whole, which is the separate intellect called the angel brought near, surnamed the Pen, in its entirety and the eternity of their intellects. The intellects of those who follow them in truth realize this and confirm it. The author of al-Mahsul says, following what he mentioned: Had the forms not been originated with eternity, they would have no subsistence and no return to the cradle of their source from which they came forth with the form; they would perish and vanish, and there would be no hope, no fear, no reward, and no punishment; for everything whose cause is eternal within it thereby becomes eternal also. That saying of his requires that the forms he mentioned, which have no subsistence and no connection with their sources except by the eternal power, are those in the world of nature, from among the souls that have no subsistence in act and gain no subsistence and permanence except through those who attained eternity and perfection. Were it not for these, reward and punishment would be void, as he mentioned; for it is by the Speaker and those who stand in his place that this is made sound. So the meaning of his words is sound only of the intellects emanated from the world of nature, below the world of creation.

In the twelfth chapter of the tenth title, the author of al-Mahsul said: Since the intellect was caused by the eternity whose interpretation with us is the composite saying, the Word of God, and there was no third mediator between the two, the intellect became like it.

We say: That saying of his leads to meanings like those already mentioned, and it does not befit the First Intellect. It is not an essence of which this may be said, since it is the very essence of the Word, nor is it something other than the Word, nor something that was and then became like it. All that pertains to the intellects that go forth from potency into act in the abode of nature, which become so by accepting the effects of the Word, that is, the First Intellect, and by union with it become like it. This is confirmed by his saying: Since the form of the act became, by the Word, the form of the intellect, the intellect thereby became a cause of what is below it among things, and became, by the Word, encompassing them by what the Creator sees within it. For that whose form became, by the Word, a form for it, and which became a cause of what is below it, implies that it once was without being a cause and without its form being the form of the Word; and that which is of this rank is only the intellects of the Speakers, not that Creator-intellect originated without mediator, which was never other than the Word, nor other than the cause of the existent whose existence is first, beyond which the descriptions are cut off. Magnified and exalted is He. In what we have already said concerning this great limit there is sufficiency, without prolonging. And that being so, to run that great limit in the course of these lower limits is impossible.

In the thirteenth chapter of the tenth title, the author of al-Mahsul said: Since this is so, it was named Perfect by creativity; for creativity is from the Creator, and by perfect creativity there arises only the perfect.

We say: We have already spoken of this. His saying is mistaken in two respects. The first is that he applied the name intellect to the Creator and made it like its Originator, of whom he said it is perfect, and this is association, which may not be said; for God is set apart by His own selfhood from the perfection and the perfect that exist by His originating them both. Nor is it said that the creator, by perfect creativity, is only the perfect, such that creativity itself be the perfect. The second is that he laid the name of creativity upon what belongs to the world of nature, and that is impossible; for the one who deserves the name of creativity is that whose existence precedes all things, not the thing that subsists in act or in potency. That being so, what he said may not be believed except in the sense in which it is sound.

In the fourteenth chapter of the tenth title, the author of al-Mahsul said: The intellect bestows the forms only from its cause, which is the Word, just as the sun bestows upon those who kindle from its light, not from its body. And since its bringing forth of what is in it is from the power of its cause, which is the Word, and its bestowing them thus is from it, it became clear that the Word is what became the cause of the things that come forth from the intellect, not the identity of the intellect, save that the intellect became a mediator between the Word and what is below it.

We say: The intellect he mentions is the intellects of the Speakers and their followers, which kindle from their cause, which is other than they, not the First Intellect, which is the First Creator. For the First Intellect has no need of another in the existence of what comes to be from it, being itself the cause and the very Word, from which and from whose essence is the existence of what exists. For this reason we said that it is the Perfect from which the perfect came to exist, needing nothing outside its essence, being the cause of the existence of things. Were something other than it to precede it, that which precedes would have to be the First Creator, not it; and it would follow that the matter led to a disorder in the ranks of the existents, which is impossible. As for his saying that the intellect became a mediator between the Word and what is below it, we have shown the impossibility of the matter earlier, at the opening of our book.

There is no harm in adding, so we say: How can anything precede the First Intellect, so as to be a mediator between it and what is below it, when the intellect is the very essence of the Word, which is the cause existing from God the Almighty? Were it permissible for something to precede it, that precedent could not but be either of the kind of its existence, and then it is void that it precede it, since their ranks and limits are equal, being of one kind; or not of the kind of its existence, and then it is either a thing or not a thing. If it is a thing, it is an object for the apprehension of intellects, and what the intellect apprehends, the intellect is prior to, not it. And if it is not a thing, then it is not the Word, but the One transcendent above the qualities that a thing deserves in its divisions. Glory be to Him, exalted with great exaltation. In whatever way it is taken, the impossibility stands that anything precede the First Intellect; for there is nothing above the Thing except the Thing, exalted. And that being so, it is the Word, and it is the cause, and it is unity, and it is the intellect, as we have explained above.

> وحسبنا الله ونعم الوكيل

And God is sufficient for us, and He is the best Trustee.

In the fifteenth chapter of the tenth title, the author of al-Mahsul said: Creativity is a mediator between the Creator and the created, the created being an effect of the influencer upon the influenced. And he said: The form of creativity is intermediate between the doer and the deed; its existence is from the side of the creative doer, and the effect of the form is found from the side of the Creator in the deed. This form, I mean creativity, came to be in the Creator, glory be to Him.

We say: This speech encloses within it the affirmation of what God did not originate, nor bring about, nor create anew, which is contrary to what the affirmers of unity hold. For by his saying he made creativity other than the Creator, and thereby made it necessary that creativity be a form in the Creator; then by his saying he made it necessary that that for which the form he named creativity is a form, and which he named an influencer within it, be fixed, uninvented, unoriginated. It is not permissible to believe this. For creativity is not something other than the Creator; for if what he conceives as Creator were other than creativity, it could not but be either that its existence is with God the Almighty or that its existence is by itself. It is void that its existence be by itself, for then it would be divided within itself while yet not ceasing to be with God the Almighty. Its existence with God the Almighty could not but be either resembling Him, exalted is God above that, and a likeness to Him, so that the two would be two likenesses, each of them specified by that through which the duality and the specification in two things occurred, which occurs only by a necessitating cause of their being so, prior to them and from which is their existence; or not resembling and not a likeness, and then it would be an opposite, and the existence of two opposites occurs only by another that preserves their existence for them, since in the existence of one of the two opposites is the annulment of the existence of the other. So their existence necessitated something prior to them that preserves their existence for them, which implies that the Creator the Almighty be created, and that there precede Him what is prior and worthier of divinity, exalted and magnified is He; and that is more strongly prevented and more impossible. And since the existence of what he made necessary cannot be free of two aspects, and both aspects being void, it is void that its existence be by itself; and once it is void that its existence be by itself, it is established that its existence is from God the Almighty. And creativity has its existence from God the Almighty; so neither of the two, creativity and the Creator, is such that one may be called creativity or Creator prior to the other. And that being so, and it being void that existence proceed from that which the existent issues from as anything but one, it is established that creativity is the Creator. And since creativity is the Creator, what the author of al-Mahsul said is mistaken; so let it be avoided, if God the Almighty wills. And now we have come to what we promised at the beginning of the book.

We say: Unity and the knowledge of the limits are a hard matter, yet in them lie the life of souls, their subsistence, and their deliverance from the world of nature and its transmutations. Since it is hard, and God the Almighty knew that not all His servants attain the degree by which they reach perfection through knowing the limits of God and His unity, He singled out from among them, in every age, one man by perfection and election, such as the Imam (ع). By perfection I mean that he is complete in receiving the overflow coursing through the world of nature from the side of the angels brought near, so that he is thereby a guardian over them and one who raises them to His whole. So God the Almighty made obligatory obedience to him and love of him, that they might thereby become part of His whole, out of His mercy toward them. There is no way to this except by holding fast to the two acts of worship, and rendering the right of both in knowledge and in deed, outwardly and inwardly, and being eager to preserve them. And that is only through the remembrance of death and the parting it brings from family, child, kin, and homeland, and through certainty that there is no arrival at the return except by provision, and no provision except what is stored up, gained only by an obedience whose obligations are performed, a duty whose right is rendered, a good that is done, a righteous deed that is worked, and a knowledge of the realities of things that is acquired. May God place us and the company of the believers in the easts of the earth and its wests among those sealed with the good, and grant us and them the intercession of Muhammad and Ali and the pure Imams, and our Imam, the Ruler by the Command of God, and his rightly guiding fathers.
