# Light, Darkness, and the Matter Below

The Third Chapter of the Book of Riyadh, on the judgment among the *hudud* concerning what was said about the soul and *al-Hayuli* (الهيولى): whether they resemble the First or not.

**Section One**

The author of al-Islah said:

Concerning what the author of al-Nusrah has established, we do not say that nature and the dark *al-Hayuli* are born from the Second as the Second is born from the First. For that world is the world of honor and virtue, while this world is the world of darkness and turbidity. The Second, though born from the First, resembles him; and the state of *al-Hayuli* is not like that state.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

This statement is wrong. For were that world all light with no darkness in it, and this world all darkness with no light in it, no connection would fall between this world and that world. Rather we say that the soul has two extremes. One of them is toward the intellect, and it lies upon its horizon, and it is the luminous, noble side. The other is toward nature, and nature lies upon its horizon, and in it is some darkness and turbidity, because nature falls upon its horizon. And we likewise say that nature has two ends. One of them is toward the partial soul, and in it is some light, because it falls upon the horizon of the soul. The other faces the elements, and it is the dark and turbid side in which there is no light. By these divisions we speak of the soul and of nature, of their darkness and their light. We do not say that that whole world is a world of light with no darkness in it, nor that this whole world is a world of darkness with no light in it. For were it as he described, there would be between them neither neighboring nor pairing.

He said:

Then it stands established that the dark *al-Hayuli* is born from the soul, though it does not resemble it, just as the soul is born from the First, though it does not resemble him. *Al-Hayuli* fell dark by reason of its distance from the First Cause, on account of its being born from the soul. Likewise the diminution fell in the soul, because it is farther from the First Cause, by which I mean the Preceding One (السابق).

This is his statement.

We say:

The form of what the author of al-Islah has said in his book is other than what the author of al-Nusrah has established. For the kernel of his saying, which is an answer to the saying that the Second is born from the First and does not resemble him, is that he said: We do not say so. Rather we say that the Second is born from the First, and does not resemble him, yet is his counterpart and his likeness, even though the First is higher in rank and nobler in station. This is like the male and the female in all genera and substances: the male, though higher and nobler than the female, is nonetheless the counterpart of his fellow and its likeness.

Then we say:

The Second is nobler and more elevated than that its way with what is below it should be like its way with the First. For the First and the Second are two counterparts, and they are the mine of honor, of perfection, and of elevation. There is no honor above them. God made them the mine of perfect honor and perfect light, of complete elevation and complete knowledge, with no ignorance there, no turbidity, and no darkness whatsoever. They are the two foundations of the higher world, which is the mine of purity, of sanctity (القدس), and of life. And *al-Hayuli* is a trace of the Second, which is the substance of this world, a turbid dark substance not to be described by mercy or by power. The Second manifested it by act out of himself, by the power derived from the First, and then brought forth from it the form, which is nature, by act.

Then the author of al-Islah said:

It is not permissible to say that *al-Hayuli* is born from him. Rather we say that it is a trace of him, and therefore does not resemble him. But the Second is born from the First and does resemble him, even though the First is higher in rank.

This is his statement, and the sum of it is this: that the Second issues from the First and is like him, though the First is higher in rank; and that *al-Hayuli* is a trace of the Second, its substance a dark and turbid substance, because the way of *al-Hayuli* with the Second is not like the way of the Second with the First. For the First and the Second are counterparts, whereas *al-Hayuli* does not resemble the Second, being a trace of him.

And if this is the sum of his saying, then what the author of al-Nusrah answered is no answer. For the author of al-Islah did not lay it down in his statement that that whole world is light and radiance. He only said, describing the state of the First and the Second, that God Most High made them the mine of honor and elevation. That saying of his is no proof that there is no existence for what is not in a state like theirs. And in what has already passed as an indication that *al-Hayuli* is not born from the Second there is sufficiency, with no need to lay the discourse open and lengthen it with what might turn suspicions upon us regarding what is not our aim in this section.

We say:

*Al-Hayuli* does not resemble the Second, nor the First, because its existence, in its issuing from the First, is by the lower relation that we set forth in our earlier words and in our book known as *Rahat al-Aql* (راحة العقل). In that is sufficiency.

**Section Two of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

This statement is wrong, because were that world all light with no darkness in it, and this world all darkness with no light in it, no connection would fall between this world and that world.

We say:

This statement, inasmuch as it fixes upon the author of al-Islah what he did not say, is an error; but inasmuch as it is his own words, it is sound. For in the world of origination and issuance there is that which is shared: it suits what is above it by being issued, and it suits what is below it by being a body. It is possessed of two matters, resembling neither the First from whom its existence comes; it resembles the Second from one aspect and does not resemble him from another. The aspect from which it resembles him is its being issued from him. The aspect from which it resembles the body is its being a natural body. And that is nature. When it is joined to the world of origination and issuance, it is a life flowing from that world into what is below it, giving it what it is able to receive of succession and of conception. And when it is joined to this world, it is a principle of motion and stillness in the thing it is in essentially, not by way of accident. So nature came to be, even though in its existence it is issued, or an intermediary between the two issuings, the First and the Second.

**Section Three of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The soul, by which I mean the Second, has two extremes. One of them is toward the intellect, upon its horizon, and it is the luminous, noble side. The other is toward nature, upon its horizon, and it is the side in which there is some darkness.

We say:

That is not sound. The proof is that whatever has extremes is possessed of dimensions measured by area; and the Second, which he named a soul in the way we have set forth, is not a body; and if it is not a body, then it is void that it should have two extremes. Then, by his saying, he made it necessary that it has a luminous extreme upon the horizon of the intellect, and an extreme in which is some darkness upon the horizon of nature, distinct from the intellect, and distinct from the First by what he mentioned of its being upon the horizon of nature. But it is impossible that in the Second there should be anything by which it is distinct from the First, because its existence, in its issuing, is from the nobler relation that is in the First, namely its being a cause. For we have already said that the First, by its being existent, is not in its own essence both cause and effect. It is cause by relation to what is below it, which is the nobler relation in the First, and effect by relation to itself, which is the lower relation in the First. So from its intellection of its essence as a cause there issues from it its like, since it is the way of a cause that its effect be from it. And from its intellection of its essence as an effect there issues from it what is not its like, its issuance being from the lower relation in the First, namely its being an effect.

Witness to what we have mentioned is the state upon which stands the Foundation (al-Asas), which is the standing likeness of the Second in the knowledge of the body, and its being not distinct from the Speaker (al-Natiq). For all that was existent in the Foundation is existent in the Speaker from whom the existence of the Foundation came, and thus it is not distinct from him. Yet not all that belongs to the Speaker is existent in the Foundation, for the Speaker has that by which the Foundation is distinct from him, namely that in which the Foundation does not share. So it has become clear, by the Foundation's sharing with the Speaker in what is existent in it, and by there being found in it nothing that is not found in the Speaker, that it is not distinct from him. And since it is not distinct from him, and since it is the likeness by which, and by whose rank, the existence of the Second and its rank are inferred, then the Second is not distinct from the First. And since it is not distinct, its existence, as he mentioned it, namely upon the horizon of nature, is impossible.

**Section Four of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

*Al-Hayuli* fell dark only by reason of its distance from the First Cause, on account of its being born from the soul.

We say:

*Al-Hayuli*'s being unlike the First, and the Second's being unlike, is not by reason of distance from the First in respect of the relation in which the First stands as effect, since the First is a gathering of two relations: a relation by its being cause, and a relation by its being effect. The existence of what comes to be from the relation that is effect is not like the existence of what comes to be from the relation that is cause, since the cause is ever nobler than the effect, and prior to it in rank and in nature. For of the First it is said that it is cause, and it is said that it is effect; yet it is a single essence, which acquires these relations by the additions.

**Section Five of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The diminution fell in the soul only because it is farther from the First Cause, by which I mean the Preceding One.

We say:

There is no diminution in the Second, which he named a soul, as we have already mentioned; nor is it far from the First; rather it is his like. The diminution is a necessary attribute, not the very nature of the soul. The soul became deficient not from the side of its own essence, which is its perfection, but from the side of its second perfection, which it acquires by benefit.

**Section Six of the Third Chapter**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The relation of nature's birth from the soul is no greater than the soul's birth from the intellect, since the soul, relative to the intellect, is less than nature relative to the soul. And if it is permitted that the soul be born from the intellect, with its perfection and its nobility and its nearness to the First Cause, it is permitted that nature be born from the soul, since nature is more like the soul in all its states, in its acts and its powers, than the soul is like the intellect in its acts and its powers. For nature, among the ancients, is marked as the principle of motion, and some of them said it is the active power; and if you look upon nature from these two limits, the fuller portion and the ampler share are hers. For the soul, among the ancients, is the moving, abiding substance, and it is withal marked with intellect, while there is nothing in the intellect of the mark of the definition of the soul, nor is the soul defined by the definition of the intellect. For the definition of the intellect is that it is the first of things, that it is the Originated, that it is the first effect, and the first intermediary; and it is not permitted to say that the soul is the first of things, nor that it is originated, nor that it is the first effect. So if it is permitted that there be born from the intellect, with its perfection and its nobility and its nearness to the Word (الكلمة), a like of the soul, which is not defined by the definition of the intellect, and this is not to be denied, then it is permitted that there be born from the soul a like of nature, which resembles in its limits the limits of the soul, and this is not to be denied. And had one looked upon the thing from the aspect of consideration, this measure of knowledge would not have been hidden. Had the author of al-Islah stood upon what is in nature of wonders and of wisdom, and upon what is in its acts of splendor and nobility and goodness and beauty, by reason of which the partial soul desires to incline toward it and to follow it, he would have known and been certain that it is born from the soul, though it does not resemble it. For causes ever find comely the order of their effects. From this the partial soul desired to follow nature. Every goodness and splendor and nobility and adornment is from the higher, noble world; and had nature not been born from the soul, it would never have been able to tell of goodness and beauty and adornment. But because it is born from the soul, it told what it saw in the world of its cause of beauty and nobility, a deficient telling, short of the telling of the soul. So know this.

This is his statement.

We say:

In what has already passed of the discourse upon the intellect, the soul, and nature, and upon the existence of that which precedes another in existence, and the rest, there is folded the sound clarification of what he has brought; and we have no need to lengthen it with certitude upon the error that has persisted in conception. And in that is sufficiency.
