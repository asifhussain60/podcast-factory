# Nothing Stands Beside the First

The author of al-Nusra said: The Intellect is the first mean, the Soul is the second mean, and the first mean is closer to the Cause than the second mean. The proximity and rank of one thing to another can arise only from perfection and nobility, and distance can arise only from lack and absence of nobility.

We say: This statement is not true with regard to the creative and emanative Intellects. Being a mean requires two ends, and it is not admissible to say that the First Intellect is a mean unless its existence be in such a state that on one side it has an end and on the other side too it has an end, so that it stands in a similar state in existence on both sides. How then is it permissible to say of that which is the first of the firsts that it is a mean, when nothing precedes it in existence such that it might be intermediate between that and the two terms? Even to suppose that the First Intellect is a mean is impossible, because its existence is a first existence, and what is a first existence is not called a mean. Even the mean deserves the name of mean only by virtue of having beyond it something of its own kind of existence, so that there is an affinity between the two, and likewise something later than it, suitable to it in some respects: an affinity with that one from one face and with this one from another face, so that it has two faces and is intermediate between them. As for the First Intellect, there is no affinity at all between it and its Creator, and it is not of the kind of his existence, so it is not a mean between it and what is below it, nor is there occasion for its being a mean between it and its Creator.

As for that which is between it and its Creator that caused it to exist, that is something else. And what is said to be the mediator between it and what is below it is the very essence of origination, the Cause that is said to be the originating Cause, as we have shown in the Epistle (The Illuminated) and in our well-known book Peace of Mind. If that is the case, then there is no sense in the inference the author of al-Nusra has drawn here in support of what he seeks to uphold, for what he has already advanced concerning deficiency and nobility is sufficient.

Deficiency is necessary for the First Intellect only in addition to its predecessor, not in itself, for its essence is upright and has no deficiency in it, and to it belongs perfection; yet in relation to that which precedes it, it lacks rank. It is as with the Speaker, the Foundation, the Sun, and the Moon. Each one of these, the Foundation and the Moon, is perfect in itself, lacking nothing in it; yet when added to the other that is its counterpart form, the Speaker and the Sun, it is deficient in its rank. So the author of al-Nusra fastens deficiency upon the second by adding it to what precedes it, while the author of al-Islah fastens upon its essence its completeness, because it emanates from the Perfection that is its Cause. Thus what the one says on his side is correct, and what the other says on his side is also true. Only, the author of al-Nusra presses hard upon the author of al-Islah from where he did not mean and where it was not his aim.

The author of al-Nusra said: If he says, that is, the author of al-Islah, "I do not say that the Intellect is like the Soul in the chapter of perfection," we say to him: then it is either more excellent than it or less than it. He will not affirm excellence for it over the Intellect; it remains that he should say it is more deficient.

We say: The author of al-Islah may well not say what the author of al-Nusra takes it upon himself to report from him. Rather he says: all things are perfect in their essences, and none of them merits any deficiency except in addition to what is higher in degree than it, so that there is found for the higher a rank not found for this thing added to it. And once his statement is bound to that, the face of the injustice done to him has become clear.

The author of al-Nusra said: Among the clearest indications of the deficiency of the Soul and the completeness of the Intellect is the saying of Allah, exalted be He:

> ( ومن آياته الليل ، والنهار ، والشمس ، والقمر )

And of His signs are the night, the day, the sun, and the moon. So the night and the day stand for the two Foundations, and the sun and the moon for the two Roots, the sun of them for the First and the moon for the second. There is no disagreement among the people of knowledge and wisdom that the moon is more deficient in light than the sun, less in light and radiance, and that it draws light from the sun, while the sun bestows the light upon it. This is his saying.

We say: It has become clear by his own saying that the moon is more deficient in light than the sun, less in light and radiance, and that it draws light from the sun, and that deficiency befalls it only by its addition to the sun. But its essence has no deficiency in it, for were it deficient it would not accept the light from the sun. Since its essence is complete, it stands able to accept from the sun what it is fit to accept. So when the saying of the author of al-Islah bears upon the completeness of the essence, and the saying of the author of al-Nusra bears upon the deficiency in its states and in its accidents, which are its perfection, there is a difference between the two sayings; and the author of al-Islah becomes like one wronged, pressed upon by what was not his aim in his saying nor his intent.

The author of al-Nusra said: Among the things that likewise indicate the deficiency of the Soul is that the sages set the Intellect at the place of the spirit to the Soul, and set the Soul at the place of the body to the Intellect. They did this only because of what they knew of the deficiency of the Soul and the perfection of the Intellect, so they likened the Soul to the deficient thing and likened the Intellect to the complete thing.

We say: By this saying he has only added a further clarification of what we have said, that the deficiency is not in its essence but in other things among its states, even if the sense of his words holds only for the beings of coming-to-be and the second emanation. For the drawing-of-benefit belongs only to the souls that are in the world of nature, that they may attain their second perfection; while in their essence they are complete and perfect, deficient only by what is not with them of the intelligibles, which run to them in the way the spirit runs to the body. As for the second, which is the first emanation, its way is not the way of these souls, because its completeness and perfection were given at the first of its existence, not through time and drawing-of-benefit, but only by a deficiency of rank below that which precedes it, and by priority and posteriority.

The author of al-Nusra said: Even were there in the Soul no deficiency save that nature falls upon its horizon, still the Soul has two extremities, one of them toward the Intellect and one toward nature; whereas the Intellect is not so (for its two extremities are, one toward the Word and the other toward the Soul), and nothing of it clung to dark nature, because of its completeness and perfection. And if an extremity of the Soul has clung to nature, by nature's falling upon its horizon, then in that there is, for whoever contemplates it, that upon which the observer may stand as to the truth of the deficiency of the Soul and the perfection of the Intellect. This is his saying.

We say: This statement issues without order. For the falling of nature upon the horizon of the second, which he named Soul, does not earn it deficiency; rather it points to a completeness in it toward nature, since nature moves toward it, being deficient in addition to it, while it, in addition to nature, is noble and complete by its being singled out with what nature is not singled out with, together with the reality of the two essences, I mean the essence of the second and the essence of nature, both complete inasmuch as they emanate from the Perfection.

As for his saying that the Intellect has two extremities, one toward the Word and the other toward the Soul: the Intellect is not a thing other than the Word, nor is the Word a thing other than the Intellect, but the essence is one and the entity is one, and by relation it merits the names whose meanings are scattered, as has already been advanced in this sense at the mention of calling it a mean, in a way that is not hidden. And as for his saying that nothing of it clung to dark nature because of its completeness and perfection, while an extremity of the Soul has clung to nature by nature's falling upon its horizon: it is not the second that clung to nature and loved it and desired it; rather it is nature that loved it, and it is nature that moves toward it and joins itself to it. To believe that, and to picture that an extremity of the second clung to nature, is error. The best of the ways to turn the meaning of his saying toward the truth is that his aim in that saying is that the second overflows upon nature, and its overflowing he has called clinging.

The author of al-Islah said: The deficient is the act of the Soul, not the essence of the Soul; only its act is completed, not its essence.

The author of al-Nusra said: This statement is self-contradictory, because if the act is deficient, that does not escape being either from the deficiency of the essence of the agent, or from the deficiency of the matter from which the agent acts, or from the deficiency of both. If the deficiency of the act is from the deficiency of the essence of the agent, then he has affirmed the deficiency of the Soul, since it is the agent. If it is from the deficiency of the matter, then he has affirmed an eternal matter, and this is a departure from the way of religion and a leaning toward the doctrine of Abu Zakariyya al-Razi and those who hold the eternity of prime matter, and I have supposed of the author of this book that he inclined to that, because he made time eternal along with the Intellect, and that is the core of his doctrine. And if the deficiency of the act is from the deficiency of both, then he has held both sayings together, and has affirmed the deficiency of the Soul, since it is the agent, and has affirmed the eternity of prime matter; so whichever he says, he has affirmed the deficiency of the Soul. This is his saying.

We say: The saying of the author of al-Islah, and the exposition of his aim in what he said concerning deficiency and completeness, has already been advanced. As for the saying of the author of al-Nusra in what he said, it is sophistry and unjust pressure and a going out from the bound of knowledge.

For he said, first, after he had divided the matter of deficiency into the three mentioned divisions, that if the deficiency of the act is from the essence of the agent, and the agent is the Soul, then the Soul is deficient. But the author of al-Islah has made clear that the deficiency does not befall the essence of the Soul, which is the second, but rather its act in the sense of its object, and so he is freed from this division.

He said, second, that if the deficiency is from the essence of the matter, then he has affirmed an eternal matter; for the Perfection and the perfect, which are the First and the second, are affirmed as eternal by an eternity that is not a first eternity, so how much less the deficient? And if that is so, then the deficiency is only in the matters that receive their completeness, not in that from which completeness is received; just as it is well known in the natural sciences that deficiency falls only from the side of the recipients, not from the side of that which is received from. Consider the heat of the sun, which is received by bodies whose nature is quick to be acted upon, like wax, which softens by it, while other bodies refuse to accept it, like stone and iron and the like, which are not quickly acted upon: the source from which the heat is drawn is one, and the deficiency is in what is other than it.

He said, third, that the author of al-Islah leaned toward the doctrine of Abu Zakariyya in the manner he described, and that is a going out from the bound of knowledge and a transgression. So he did him justice neither in the frame of the division nor in what follows it, when he charged him with holding both sayings, the deficiency and the eternity of prime matter.

The author of al-Nusra said: As for his plea that the Soul became complete only because it emanated from the First Intellect, which is Perfection, and that it is not permitted that what emanates from Perfection be other than complete, this is not true. For it does not follow that the Soul be complete because it emanates from the complete Intellect; and were that necessary, then what emanates from the Soul would also be complete, which is prime matter and form, and what emanates from prime matter and form would be complete too, and so on until it reached the singular, indivisible individuals, so that all of them would become complete. And there follows from this saying that the whole world is complete, with no deficiency in it, nor in anything of it.

We say: The sum of his saying is that if the Soul were complete by being emanated from the First Intellect, which is Perfection, then prime matter and form, by their being emanated from the Soul, would be complete also, and likewise whatever is emanated from all of prime matter and form, as he claims, down to the individuals, and the whole of what is in the world would be complete, with no deficiency in it. We have already set forth what the author of al-Islah intends in his saying that the Soul is complete.

The saying of the author of al-Nusra in this chapter is not carried through to the end, for prime matter is not emanated from the Soul, nor are the individuals, as he claimed, indivisible; rather all the individuals are divisible into their parts, and prime matter is emanated together with the Soul from that which precedes it in existence, not from it. The proof is that the Soul, which is the second, its existence is not a first existence, so there is not found with it a like of its own kind of existence, nor is there above it in rank another like the First, which is the First Intellect; rather its existence is a second existence. And that whose existence is a second existence, its existence is only along with an existence of its own kind of existence, such that it and that other share in kind. For that from which this Soul which is the second came to be, is the First Thing, which nothing of its own kind of existence precedes, and which is one in its existence. This is only for the sake of the transcendence of Allah, exalted be He, who brought it into being, above relations and additions and multiplicity and all that a thing is described by in its divisions which they found, I mean the relations and additions in the acting causes as a cause, that what comes to be from them be more than one; for the coming-to-be of multiplicity in the effects is according to what their causes and grounds present to them of relations.

So since Allah, exalted be He, is prior to any other being prior to Him, or any other being together with Him, or His being differentiated by attributes and relations and additions, and since He, exalted be He, is the Bringer-into-being, the Originator, the Ground that has no ground, it is impossible, together with His purity and His holiness and His exaltation above multiplicity, that there come to be from Him except one. And this one, which is the First Intellect, since it stood in two relations, one of them its being a cause for the existence of what is below it, and the other its being an effect, inasmuch as it is originated and brought forth, it must be that what comes to be from it is two, according to what its essence carries of the two relations. So that which came to be from the first relation is the second Intellect, and from the other relation is the prime matter, from which are the spheres and the rest of the existents of the world of body. Then, when the existent passes beyond the one into coming-to-be, it is not except two, since there is not.

after the rank of the one in existence there is nothing except two, and after the individual there is nothing except the pair, which is plurality. Since the first existent in the number of existents is one, what follows it, once the intellect conceives it, is two, which is the pair, or the multitude. And when there are two, one of them is what I call the Soul (النفس), and the other is what I call prime matter (الهيولى), from which come the spheres, the planets, and what is below them.

The sixteenth section of the first book.

Then, since the foundation of religion and its ordinances was laid upon the balancing of the existents and their correspondences, so that their arrangement might point to the existents among the limits that are hidden from sight, and since the existence of the Book and the Shariʿah is from the side of the Speaker (الناطق), while the establishment of the Foundation (الأساس) is derived from them, likewise from the side of the Speaker, this is the more indicative that what comes to be from the First Intellect (العقل الأول), which in its own world is like the Speaker in the world of body, is the Follower (التالي), which is the Soul and prime matter together, just as the Book and the Foundation exist from the side of the Speaker. What testifies to this is what the Prophet did in joining the Book and the ʿItrah (العترة), whose existence is from him, when he said:

> إلا أني تارك فيكم الثقلين كتاب الله وعترتي، حبل ممدود من السماء الى الأرض، طرف منه بيد الله وطرف منه بايديكم، فتمسكوا بها لن تضلوا ما ان تمسكتم بهما، وقد سألت ربي ان يردا عليّ الحوض كهاتين

"Indeed, I leave among you the two weighty things: the Book of God and my ʿItrah, a rope extended from the sky to the earth, one end of it in the hand of God and one end of it in your hands. Hold fast to them and you will not go astray so long as you hold to them, and I have asked my Lord that the two be returned to me at the Basin like these two." He joined the two index fingers of both his hands, and then he said:

> ولا اقول كهاتين

"And I do not say like these two," and he pointed with two fingers of one hand, the index and the middle, one of which precedes the other. And he said:

> ناصرهما لي ناصر، وخاذلهما لي خاذل

"Their helper is a helper to me, and their forsaker is a forsaker to me."

His likening them, in existence, to his two index fingers is because neither of them precedes the other. So he made himself, in the abode of body, run the course of the First Intellect in the abode of origination (الابداع), and he made what he brought of the Book, together with what he joined to it of his ʿItrah, which is the Foundation, and the rest of the Imams (صلوات الله عليهم), run the course of what came to be from the First Intellect as the Second, and of prime matter, according to what I mentioned in the book Rāḥat al-ʿAql (راحة العقل). Thus prime matter does not manifest its nobility, nor does the beautiful form (الصورة) that lies within its potential appear, except by the power of the Soul, which is the Follower, and by the shining of the light within them both. In the same way, the Book and the Shariʿah do not proclaim themselves, nor do they display the wonders of the wisdom within them and beneath them, except through the interpretation (تأويل) of the Foundation and the one who stands in his place. Prime matter, then, has its existence from the First Intellect together with the Second.

The seventeenth section of the first book.

Then I say: I said that the First Intellect has nothing alongside it of the kind of its own existence, because its existence does not come to it except as a first existence, and the First is not other than one. Otherwise the paths of affirming the divine unity would be disordered. For if it had a likeness in its existence, then, were the two together, their existence would be caused by that from which their existence came, and this would require something preceding them, and from which their existence comes, something whose existence is forbidden and impossible. For the existence of duality is a coming-to-be multiplied in essences, and multiplicity in essences does not have its existence from a multiplicity in essences; rather its existence is from what precedes it, which is one in essence from one side and multiple in meanings from another. And the one in essence, multiplied in meanings, has its existence not by its own essence but by another, which is not multiplied, neither in meanings nor in essence.

By this it has become clear that if the First Intellect had alongside it something of the kind of its existence, the two would be brought into being from a one preceding them, and then the existence of that one would be by its being multiplied in meanings, not in its essence but by what precedes it and renders it one. And since there is nothing above the Intellect, it is void that it should have a likeness of the kind of its existence. So it is established that it is one. And in the establishing of the Intellect as one lies the coming-to-be of what proceeds from it as two, which are the Soul and what is named prime matter. And since the existence of the Soul and prime matter is together, as I have shown, it has appeared that the claim that prime matter appears from the Soul is in error.

The eighteenth section of the first book.

Now for what the author of al-Nuṣra (صاحب النصرة) has bound himself to in his statement concerning the deficiency of prime matter and the form, and the deficiency of the world with all that is in it, I say: prime matter and the form are perfect, and the world with all that is in it is complete and perfect, and its parts in their essences are complete and perfect. The proof is that the perfect is that outside of which nothing of the kind of its own existence is found. The world, which is the sum of prime matter and the form together with all the parts of body, and body, which is in its essence perfect in its three dimensions, has nothing that its limit binds to it except that it is within it and not outside it, and so it is perfect. And if it is perfect, then the position of the author of al-Nuṣra, that the world should be complete and perfect, is refuted, and the impossible follows; and if it is impossible, then his speech is contradiction. It suffices, as an indication of the perfection of the world, to cite what he said in his book known as al-Iftikhār (الافتخار), in the chapter of "the command," where he said:

> وصورة استغناء العالم بكليته عن غير سواه عدم الغير، واكتفاء الحكمة بما في كلية العالم من الغنية والكمال

"The form of the world's independence, in its entirety, from anything other than itself is the non-being of the other, and the sufficiency of wisdom with what lies in the totality of the world of richness and perfection." I do not know how this escaped him; perhaps he composed the book al-Iftikhār after the book al-Nuṣra. As for the disparity found among the parts of the world, on account of which the author of al-Nuṣra charges it with deficiency, that arises only from the addition of some of them to others in respect of their forms and states, which is the First Perfection already present. Among the parts of the world there are those that were given their First Perfection, and the second as a first; and among them are those given their Second Perfection last, by acquisition. So the speech has made plain that the deficiency of which the author of al-Nuṣra speaks lies only in the later states attaching to the natural essences, which appear when some are added to others; while the Perfect of which the author of al-Iṣlāḥ (صاحب الاصلاح) speaks is only the essences whose existence is a first existence.

The nineteenth section of the first book.

The author of al-Nuṣra said: if it were necessary, on the ground that the Soul is emanated from the Intellect, which is the perfection, that the Soul have full stature, then it would likewise be necessary, on the ground that the Soul is emanated from the Intellect, which is the first intermediate and the first originator, that the Soul be an intermediate like the First, and an originator, so that the Soul attains of the excellences what the Intellect attains, and likewise the whole world and all individuals. This is his statement.

I say: the demonstration that it is impossible to call the Intellect the first intermediate has already been advanced. In sum, what he has brought is a corrupt proposition. For when something has its existence from the existence of some other thing, in the sense that one is a cause and the other an effect, it is not necessary that it share with it in all its states, so that its very identity would be that other's identity and its reality that other's reality; for then nothing could be said of it except what is said of that very identity, and it would no longer be more of a root, and it could not then be said that it came to be from it by being what it is. Nor, from the fact that it is not necessary that it share with it in all its states, does it follow that it must share with it in some of its states, since, if it did not share with it in some of its states, it would not be the cause of its existence, nor would it give it existence. And when the way of the existence of things in multiplicity is upon this, it is necessary that it share with it in one respect and differ from it in another. And when that is necessary, it follows that the Follower named Soul, being among the things emanated from the First Intellect, the cause of its existence, must be a likeness to it in one respect and different in another. And when it must be a likeness to it in one respect and not a likeness in another, it has appeared that it is not necessary, when the existence of the Follower named Soul is from the Intellect, that its being be a likeness of the Intellect in all its states; nor is it necessary that there be said of the Follower named Soul what is said of the Intellect in respect of its being its cause. So the statement of the author of al-Nuṣra, that if it were necessary it would be necessary, is refuted.

The twentieth section of the first book.

The author of al-Nuṣra said: for this there are many parallels among the things witnessed, such as the idea (الفكرة) and the partial soul (النفس الجزئية). For the partial soul that is in us is in the place of the Intellect, and the idea is in the place of the Soul. And the idea, even though it is emanated from the Soul, may not be said to be like the Soul, because the idea perceives what it perceives part after part, and time after time, whereas the Soul perceives the whole of what it perceives without time. This is his statement.

I say: the Soul, I mean the soul of human beings, has actions, and each action of them has a name proper to it. When it seeks the perception of some thing, that seeking of it is called thought (الفكر). When it regards what has come to be in its essence of the forms of the things known, that regarding is called memory (الحفظ). When it engraves its essence with what it captures of the marks, whether by way of inference or by way of the senses, that engraving is called conception (التصور). And when it knows that outside it there are things it desires to conceive, that will is called inclination (النزوع). Since it has actions, and the idea is an action of it that does not pass beyond its essence so as to have an existence other than its own existence, how can the likening of thought to the Follower named Soul be sound, when the Follower named Soul has an existence other than the existence of the Intellect, and how can it be said that thought perceives part after part, when perception belongs to nothing but the essence of the Soul by its action which is called the idea within its essence? Or how is it correct that the perception of the Soul, I mean the soul of human beings, be of the whole without time, when it has no way to hunt the objects of knowledge except from the side of the senses and from inference, from the side of their particulars to their universals, in time?

Then it does not follow, from the fact that the idea may not be said to be like the Soul, that it is necessary that the Second Intellect, which is named the Universal Soul (النفس الكلية), not be like the First Intellect, since the idea, drawn from the souls of human beings, does not run the course of the Second Intellect from the First Intellect. For the Second Intellect is a second existence from origination, subsisting by its own essence, even though in existence it is one rank below the First; whereas thought has no existence by which it stands apart from the Soul, but is the very identity of the Soul, and has no subsistence by its own essence. For every action among the actions of the Soul has a cause on account of which it needs to act, which is its being in the world of nature; and when it parts from that world, these actions cease from it by the ceasing of the cause, and nothing remains subsisting except the essence conceived as a pure intellect.

Then he made the Second Intellect, named the Soul, proceed from the First Intellect after the manner of thought from the Soul; and since the existence of thought was no existence other than the existence of the Soul which he likened to the Intellect, and since thought had no perceiving of the Soul by its own essence, it is as though he had denied that the Second Intellect, named the Soul, has any existence other than the existence of the First Intellect, or any perception like the perception of the First. And that is impossible, for the Follower has an existence other than the existence of the First, and it has a perception like the perception of the First; and it does not deserve to be called an originator, just as the First Existent does not deserve to be called emanated. Both are pure intellects; each of them has encompassed its own essence and has been preserved by its own essence, and they are alike in one respect and unlike in another, according to what was said before. And if this is so, there is no sense in likening the idea to what he likened it to, and no sense either in making the soul of human beings hold the place of the First Intellect, nor the idea within it hold the place of the Universal Soul. For the nobility of the human soul, and its coming out from potency into act, lies only in acquisition (الاكتساب), and acquisition comes only from the side of the senses as work, and from the side of conception as knowledge. And when the nobility of souls lies in acquisition, part of which comes to be by thought and inference as conception, and the idea belongs to the souls only that they may be ennobled by it, it is as though he had made the cause of the nobility of the First Intellect to be the Second Intellect, named the Soul. And that is an inverted proposition, for the nobility of the Follower and its rank are by what precedes it in existence, which is the antecedent, since the nobility of the antecedent and its rank are by what comes after it. And the ruling upon that whose existence is a second existence, in perfection and completeness, is that it be the reverse of that whose existence is a first existence; so this one does not run in that respect the course of that one, nor does that one run the course of this one, even though they share from another side. Yet the author of al-Nuṣra has judged that whose existence was a first existence by what belongs to that whose existence is a second existence, and that is impossible.

The twenty-first section of the first book.

The author of al-Nuṣra said: among the clearest proofs of the deficiency of the Soul, and the perfection of the First Intellect, is that
