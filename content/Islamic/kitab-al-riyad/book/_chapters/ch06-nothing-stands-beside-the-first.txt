# Nothing Stands Beside the First

The author of al-Nusra said:

The Intellect is the first mean, and the Soul is the second mean; and the first mean is nearer to the Cause than the second mean. The nearness and rank of one thing to another can arise only from perfection and nobility, and remoteness can arise only from deficiency and want of nobility.

We say: this statement holds no true meaning for the originative and emanative first Intellects. For to be a mean requires two ends, and it is not admissible to say that the First Intellect is a mean, since that from which its existence comes is not in a state like its own, such that there would be an end for it on that side as its coming-to-be from that gave it an end, and because it stands, in existence, in a state like its own. How could one so much as imagine that the One which is the first of ones is a mean, when nothing precedes it of its own kind of existence, so that it might stand as a mean between that and the two? Even to suppose this, that the First Intellect is a mean, is impossible, because its existence is a first existence, and that whose existence is a first existence is not called a mean. The mean deserves to be called a mean only in that something of its own kind of existence precedes it, so that there is an affinity between the two, and something also comes after it, suited to it in some respects, so that it has an affinity with the one from one face and with the other from another face: these are its two ends, and it is a mean between them. But as for the First Intellect, there is no affinity at all between it and its Originator, exalted be His mention, nor is it of the kind of His existence, so that it should be a mean between Him and what is below it; nor is there any occasion for it to be a mean between itself and its Originator. As for that which is between it and its Originator, the cause by which it exists, that is other than it; and what is called the mediator between it and what is below it is the very essence of origination, the Cause that is said to be at once originating and originated, as we have shown in *The Illuminated Epistle* and in our well-known book *Peace of Mind*.

If that is so, there is no sense in the author of al-Nusra's inference from this in support of what he sought to defend, for what has already been said concerning deficiency and nobility is sufficient. Deficiency attaches to the Following only in relation to the First Intellect, not in its own essence; for its essence is upright, with no deficiency in it, and it possesses completeness, while in relation to what precedes it, it is of lesser rank. It is like the Speaker and the Foundation, the Sun and the Moon: each of the two, the Foundation and the Moon, is perfect in its own essence, with no deficiency in it; yet when set beside the other that is its counterpart form, the Speaker and the Sun, it is deficient in rank. The author of al-Nusra binds the Following to deficiency by relating it to what precedes it, while the author of al-Islah binds its essence to perfection, because it emanates from the Perfection that is its Cause. What this one says on his side is correct, and what that one says on his side is also true; except that the author of al-Nusra wrongs the author of al-Islah, taking him where he did not intend and where his purpose does not lie.

The author of al-Nusra said:

If he says, "I do not mean," that is, the author of al-Islah, "that the Intellect is like the Soul in the matter of perfection," we say to him: then it is either better than the Soul or more deficient than it. He will not claim that the Soul is superior to the Intellect; so it remains for him to say that the Soul is deficient.

We say: the author of al-Islah may well not say what the author of al-Nusra has undertaken to report on his behalf. Rather he says that all things are perfect in their own essences, and that none of them merits deficiency except in relation to what is of higher degree than it, so that there belongs to the higher a rank that does not belong to this thing set beside it. And once its connection is to that, the manner of the wrong done to him is plain.

The author of al-Nusra said:

Among the clearest proofs of the deficiency of the Soul and the perfection of the Intellect is the saying of God, exalted be He:

> ومن آياته الليل، والنهار، والشمس، والقمر

"And among His signs are the night, the day, the sun, and the moon."

The night and the day stand for the two Foundations, and the sun and the moon for the two Roots; the sun of them for the First, and the moon for the Following. There is no disagreement among the people of knowledge and wisdom that the moon is of less light than the sun, less in light and radiance, and that it draws its light from the sun, and that the sun bestows light upon it. This is what he says.

We say: it is clear from his own saying that the moon is of less light than the sun, less in light and radiance, and that it draws its light from the sun, and that its deficiency attaches to it only by relating it to the sun; but as for its essence, there is no deficiency in it, for were it deficient it would not have received the light from the sun. Since its essence is perfect, it stood ready to receive from the sun what it is able to receive. And so, since the discourse of the author of al-Islah is about the perfection of the essence, and the discourse of the author of al-Nusra is about its deficiency in its states and its accidents by which its completeness comes, there is a difference between the two statements; and the author of al-Islah is like one wronged, in that he is taken where his purpose in what he says does not lie, nor his intent.

The author of al-Nusra said:

Among what also indicates the deficiency of the Soul is that the wise set the Intellect in the station of the spirit to the Soul, and set the Soul in the station of the body to the Intellect; and they did this only because they knew of the deficiency of the Soul and the completeness of the Intellect, so they likened the Soul to the deficient thing and the Intellect to the complete thing.

We say: by that saying he has added a further clarification of what we have said, that the deficiency is not in its essence but in other things, its states, even though the meaning of his words holds true only of the beings and the second emanation. For the drawing of benefit belongs only to the souls that are in the world of nature, that they may reach their second completeness; in their own essence they are complete and perfect, save that they are deficient in what they do not yet possess of the intelligibles, which run to them as the spirit runs to the body. But the second, which is the first emanation, its way is not the way of these souls, for its completeness and its perfection were given at the beginning of its existence, not through time and the drawing of benefit, but only through a deficiency of rank below what precedes it, and through precedence and posteriority.

The author of al-Nusra said:

Were there no deficiency in the Soul but that nature lies upon its horizon, still the Soul has two ends: one end toward the Intellect and one end toward nature. The Intellect is not so, its two ends being one toward the Word and the other toward the Soul, and nothing of it clings to dark nature, because of its perfection and its completeness. Yet were an end of the Soul to cling to nature, because nature lies upon its horizon, in that there would be, for whoever contemplates it, that upon which the observer would come to stand upon the truth of the deficiency of the Soul and the perfection of the Intellect. This is what he says.

We say: this statement proceeds without order. For the lying of nature upon the horizon of the Following, which he named the Soul, does not earn it any deficiency; rather it indicates a perfection belonging to it, for nature moves toward it because nature is deficient in relation to it, while it, in relation to nature, is noble and perfect, singled out by what nature is not singled out by, together with the essential reality of both, I mean the essence of the Following and the essence of nature, both perfect, inasmuch as they emanate from Perfection. As for his saying that the Intellect has two ends, one toward the Word and the other toward the Soul: the Intellect is not a thing other than the Word, nor is the Word a thing other than the Intellect; rather the essence is one and the entity is one, and it is by relation that it merits the names of scattered meanings. What has already been said on this sense, when the applying to it of the name "mean" was mentioned, is not hidden. And as for his saying that nothing of it clings to dark nature, because of its perfection and its completeness, while an end of the Soul has clung to nature because nature lies upon its horizon: it is not the Following that has clung to nature and loved it and desired it; rather it is nature that has loved it, and it is nature that moves toward it and seeks its union. To hold that, and to picture that an end of the Following has clung to nature, is an error. The best of the ways to turn the meaning of his words toward what is right is that his purpose in saying it was that the Following pours forth upon nature, so that he has called its outpouring a clinging.

The author of al-Islah said:

The deficient is the act of the Soul, not the essence of the Soul; and it is its act that is perfected, not its essence.

The author of al-Nusra said:

This statement is self-contradictory. For if the act is deficient, that cannot but be either from a deficiency in the essence of the agent, or from a deficiency in the substance out of which the agent acts, or from a deficiency in both. If the deficiency of the act is from a deficiency in the essence of the agent, then he has affirmed the deficiency of the Soul, since it is the agent. If it is from a deficiency in the substance, then he has affirmed an eternal substance, and this is a departure from the doctrine of religion and an inclination to the doctrine of Abu Zakariyya al-Razi and of those who hold the pre-eternity of matter; and it has already been suspected of the author of this book that he inclined to that, because he made time pre-eternal along with the Intellect, which is the pith of his doctrine. And if the deficiency of the act is from a deficiency in both, then he has held both sayings at once, affirming the deficiency of the Soul, since it is the agent, and affirming the pre-eternity of matter. Whichever he says, he has affirmed the deficiency of the Soul. This is what he says.

We say: the saying of the author of al-Islah, and the setting forth of his purpose in what he said of deficiency and perfection, has already been given. As for the saying of the author of al-Nusra in what he said, it is sophistry, wrong done, and a departure from the bound of knowledge. For he said first, after dividing the matter of deficiency into the three parts mentioned, that if the deficiency of the act is from the essence of the agent, and the agent is the Soul, then the Soul is deficient; yet the author of al-Islah has made plain that deficiency does not attach to the essence of the Soul, which is the Following, but to its act, in the sense of the thing acted upon, and so he is freed of this division. He said secondly that if the deficiency is from the essence of the substance, then he has affirmed an eternal substance, since Perfection and the Perfect, which are the First and the Second, have affirmed of them an eternity that is not a first eternity; so how much more the deficient? If that is so, then deficiency is only in the substances that receive their perfection, not in that from which perfection is received; even as it is well known in natural science that deficiency arises from the side of the recipients, not from the side of that which is received from. It is as with the heat of the sun, which bodies whose nature is quick to be affected receive, like wax, which is softened by it, while other bodies refuse to receive it, like stone and iron and the like, which are not quickly affected: the source from which it is drawn, the heat, is one, and the deficiency is in the others. And he said thirdly that he inclined to the doctrine of Abu Zakariyya in the manner he set out; and that is a departure from the bound of knowledge and a transgression. So he did him no justice, neither in the framing of the division, nor in what follows it, when he charged him with holding both sayings, deficiency and the pre-eternity of matter.

The author of al-Nusra said:

As for his pleading that the Soul became perfect only because it emanated from the First Intellect, which is Perfection, and that what emanates from Perfection cannot but be perfect, this is not right. For it does not follow that the Soul must be perfect because it emanates from the perfect Intellect; and were that necessary, then what emanates from the Soul would also be perfect, namely matter and form, and what emanates from matter and form would be perfect as well, on and on until it reached the single, indivisible individuals, so that all of them would be perfect. It follows from this saying that the whole world is perfect, with no deficiency in it, nor in anything of it.

We say: the gist of his saying is that if the Soul were perfect by its being emanated from the First Intellect, which is Perfection, then matter and form, by their being emanated from the Soul, would be perfect as well, and likewise what is emanated from all of matter and form, down to the individuals as he supposes, and the whole of what is in the world would be perfect, with no deficiency in it. We have already shown what the author of al-Islah aimed at in saying that the Soul is perfect, and the saying of the author of al-Nusra in this chapter is not thoroughly examined. For matter is not emanated from the Soul, nor are the individuals, as he supposed, indivisible; rather all the individuals are divided into their parts, and matter is emanated together with the Soul from that which precedes it in existence, not from it. The proof is that the Soul, which is the Following, its existence is not a first existence, so there is not found with it a like of its own kind of existence, nor does another rise above it in rank, as with the First, which is the First Intellect; rather its existence is a second existence. And that whose existence is a second existence, its existence is not except that there is with it an existence of its own kind, such that it and that one share in the kind. For that from which this Soul, which is the Following, came to be is the First Thing, which nothing of its own kind of existence precedes, and which is one in its existence. This was only for the sake of the transcendence of God, exalted be He, who brought it into existence free of relations and additions and multiplicity and all that by which a thing is described in its kinds, which they have found, I mean the relations and the additions, to be, in the active causes, a cause for that which comes to be from them being more than one; since the existence of multiplicity in the things caused is according to what their causes and grounds possess of relations. So, since God, exalted be He, is too prior for another to precede Him, or for another to exist with Him, or for Him to be diversified by attributes and relations, and since He, exalted be He, is the Existentiator, the Originator, the Cause that has no cause, it was impossible, with His purity and His holiness and His exaltation above multiplicity, that there should come to be from Him except one. And this one, which is the First Intellect, since it stood in two relations, one of them its being a cause for the existence of what is below it, and the other its being an effect, since it is originated and brought into being, it was necessary that what comes to be from it should be two, according to the two relations in which its essence stands. And that which came to be from the first relation is the second Intellect, and from the other relation the matter of which the spheres are made, and the rest of the existents of the world of body. Then, when the existent passes beyond the one into coming-to-be, it is only two, since there is not

For after the rank of the one in existence there is nothing except two, and after the individual there is nothing except the pair, which is plurality. If the first thing in the number of existents is one, then what follows it, when the intellect conceives it, is two, which is the pair, that is, the multitude. And if it is two, then one of the two is what we call the Soul, and the other is what we call al-hayula, the matter from which come the spheres, the stars, and all that lies beneath them.

### The Sixteenth Section of the First Part

Now the foundation of religion and its ordinances rests upon the balancing of the existents and their correspondences, so that their arrangement may point to the existents among the higher limits that are hidden from sight. The Book and the Shari'a exist from the side of the Speaker, and the establishment of the Foundation stands as their interpreter, likewise from the side of the Speaker. This is the clearest indication that what proceeds from the First Intellect, which in its own world is like the Speaker in the world of body, is the Follower, and that Follower is the Soul and al-hayula together, just as the Book and the Foundation are the two that exist from the side of the Speaker.

Witness to this is what the Prophet did when he joined the Book and the 'Itra, whose existence is from him, when he said:

> إلا أني تارك فيكم الثقلين كتاب الله وعترتي ، حبل ممدود من السماء الى الأرض ، طرف منه بيد الله وطرف منه بايديكم، فتمسكوا بها لن تضلوا ما ان تمسكتم بهما، وقد سألت ربي ان يردا عليّ الحوض كهاتين

"Indeed, I leave among you the two weighty things: the Book of God and my 'Itra, a rope stretched from heaven to earth, one end of it in the hand of God and one end of it in your hands. Hold fast to them, and you will never go astray so long as you hold to them. I have asked my Lord that the two return to me at the basin like these two."

And he joined the two forefingers of both his hands together and said:

> ولا اقول كهاتين

"And I do not say like these two," pointing with the forefinger and the middle finger of a single hand, one of them going before the other, and saying: whoever helps them helps me, and whoever forsakes them forsakes me.

His likening of the two, in their existence, to his two forefingers is that neither of them precedes the other. So he set himself, in the abode of body, in the course of the First Intellect in the abode of origination. And he set what he brought of the Book, and the one he coupled with it from his 'Itra, who is the Foundation, together with the rest of the Imams (عليهم السلام), in the course of what proceeds from the First Intellect as the Second and as al-hayula, according to what we mentioned in the book Rahat al-'Aql. So al-hayula does not manifest its nobility, nor the beautiful form that lies within its potency, except by the power of the Soul, which is the Follower, and by the shining of the light within the two of them. Just as the Book and the Shari'a do not proclaim themselves, nor do they disclose the wonders of wisdom that lie within them and beneath them, except by the interpretation of the Foundation and the one who stands in his place. Thus al-hayula, in its existence, is from the First Intellect together with the Second.

### The Seventeenth Section of the First Part

Then we say: We said that the First Intellect has nothing of the kind of its own existence existing along with it, because its existence, whereby it exists, is only a first existence, and the first cannot be more than one. Were it otherwise, the paths of professing divine unity would break down. For if it had a like in its existence, then the existence of the two of them, were they together, would be caused, since their existence would not proceed from itself; and this would require something prior to the two, from which their existence comes, and that is forbidden and absurd.

For the existence of twoness is a being multiplied in essences, and multiplicity in essences does not derive its existence from a multiplicity in essences; rather its existence is from what precedes it, from that which is one in essence from one aspect and multiple in meanings from another. And that which is one in essence yet multiple in meanings has its existence not through itself but through another which is not multiplied, neither in meanings nor in essence.

Thus it has become clear that if the First Intellect had along with it a like of the kind of its existence, the two of them would be originated from a one that precedes them; then the existence of that one would be by its being multiple in meanings, not through itself, but through what precedes it and renders it one. And since there is nothing above the Intellect, it is void that it should have a like of the kind of its existence. So it is established that it is one. And in the establishment that the Intellect is one lies the fact that what proceeds from it is two, namely the Soul and what is called al-hayula. And since the existence of the Soul and al-hayula is together, according to what we have shown, it becomes plain that the statement that al-hayula appears from the Soul is an error.

### The Eighteenth Section of the First Part

Now as for what the author of al-Nusra committed himself to, in his statement concerning the deficiency of al-hayula and the Image, and the deficiency of the world and all that is in it, we say:

al-hayula and the Image are both complete, and the world with all it contains is perfect and complete, and its parts in themselves are complete and perfect. The proof is that the complete is that outside of which there exists nothing of the kind of its own existence. The world is the sum of al-hayula and the Image, together with all the parts of body; and body in itself is complete in its three dimensions, so that nothing which its definition entails is found outside it, but rather within it. Therefore it is complete. And if it is complete, then the author of al-Nusra's denial that the world should be complete and perfect is among the impossible; and since it is impossible, his speech is only a forced overreach.

It is enough, as an indication of the perfection of the world, what he himself said in his book known as al-Iftikhar, in the chapter of the Command, where he said:

> وصورة استغناء العالم بكليته عن غير سواه عدم الغير ، واكتفاء الحكمة بما في كلية العالم من الغنية والكمال

"The form of the world's self-sufficiency in its totality, needing nothing other than itself, is the nonexistence of any other, and wisdom's contentment with the richness and perfection that lie in the totality of the world."

I do not know how this escaped him; perhaps he composed the book al-Iftikhar after the book al-Nusra. As for the disparity found among the parts of the world, on account of which the author of al-Nusra charges it with deficiency, that is only by the addition of some of them to others in respect of their forms and states, which is the first perfection that exists. For some parts of the world are given their first perfection, and their second, at the outset; and some are given their second perfection only at the last, by acquisition. So the discourse has made plain that the deficiency of which the author of al-Nusra speaks lies only in the states that supervene upon the natural essences, which appear when some of them are added to others; while the complete of which the author of al-Islah speaks is only the essences whose existence is a first existence.

### The Nineteenth Section of the First Part

The author of al-Nusra said: If, because the Soul emanates from the Intellect, which is the Complete, the Soul must have stature, then likewise, because the Soul emanates from the Intellect, which is the first intermediary and the first originator, the Soul must be an intermediary like the First, and an originator, such that the Soul attains of the virtues what the Intellect attains, and likewise the whole world and its individuals. This is his statement.

We say: The demonstration that it is impossible to call the Intellect the first intermediary has already been advanced, and in sum what he has brought forward is a corrupt proposition. For when a thing's existence proceeds from the requirement of another thing, in the sense that one is a cause and the other an effect, it does not follow that the effect shares with the cause in all its states, such that its very identity would be the cause's identity and its reality the cause's reality; for then nothing could be said of it except what is said of that same identity, and it would no longer be a distinct existence, nor could it be said to proceed from the cause by being that very cause. Yet, though it need not share with the cause in all its states, it must share with it in some of its states; for if it shared with it in none of its states, it would not be an effect of it, and the cause would not have given it existence.

And since the way of the existence of things in this multiplicity is so, it must share with its cause in one respect and differ from it in another. And when this is necessary, it follows that the Follower, called Soul, which emanates from the First Intellect that is the cause of its existence, must be like it in one respect and unlike it in another. And since it must be like it in one respect and not like it in another, it becomes plain that it is not necessary, when the existence of the Follower called Soul is from the Intellect, that its being should be like the Intellect in all its states; nor does it follow that what is said of the Intellect, in respect of its being the cause, must be said of the Follower called Soul. This refutes what the author of al-Nusra said: that if it were necessary, it would be necessary.

### The Twentieth Section of the First Part

The author of al-Nusra said: This has many parallels in the sensible world, such as thought and the partial soul. For the partial soul that is in us is in the position of the Intellect, and thought is in the position of the Soul. Yet thought, even though it emanates from the soul, may not be said to be like the soul, because thought perceives what it perceives part by part, and time after time, whereas the soul perceives the whole of what it perceives without time. This is his statement.

We say: The soul, I mean the soul of the human being, has acts, and each of its acts has a name proper to it. When it seeks to grasp some thing, that seeking is called thought (al-fikr). When it regards the forms of the known things that have come to be within its essence, that regarding is called retention (al-hifz). When it imprints its essence with the marks it captures by way of inference or by way of the senses, that imprint is called conception (al-tasawwur). And when it knows that outside itself there are things it wishes to conceive, that will is called striving (al-nuzu') toward them.

Since it has acts, and thought is an act of it that does not pass beyond its own essence so as to have an existence other than its existence, how can the likening of thought be sound to the Follower called Soul, which has an existence other than the existence of the Intellect? And how can it be said that thought perceives part by part, when perception belongs to nothing but the essence of the soul through its act which is called thought within itself? And how can it be sound that the perception of the soul, I mean the soul of the human being, should be of the whole without time, when it has no way to hunt for knowledge except by way of the senses and inference, passing from their particulars to their universals in time?

Then it is not the case that, because thought may not be said to be like the soul, it necessarily follows that the Second Intellect, which is called the Universal Soul, is not like the First Intellect. For thought from the souls of human beings does not run in the course of the Second Intellect from the First Intellect. The Second Intellect is a second existence from origination, subsisting through itself, even though in existence it is one rank below the First. But thought has no existence separate from the soul; rather it is the very identity of the soul, and has no subsistence through itself. For every act among the acts of the soul has a cause on account of which it needs to act, namely its being in the world of nature; and when the soul departs from that world, these acts are annulled by the annulment of their cause, and nothing remains subsisting except the essence conceived as pure Intellect.

Then he made the Second Intellect, called the Soul, proceed from the First Intellect in the course of thought from the soul; and since the existence of thought is no existence other than the existence of the soul, which he likened to the Intellect, and since thought has no perception of the soul through its own essence, it is as though he had denied that the Second Intellect, called the Soul, has any existence other than the existence of the First Intellect, and any perception like the perception of the First. And that is impossible; for the Follower has an existence other than the existence of the First, and a perception like the perception of the First. It does not deserve to be called an originator, just as the First Existent does not deserve to be called emanated. Both are pure intellects; each has encompassed its own essence and preserved its essence by its essence; and they are alike in one respect and unlike in another, according to what the discourse upon this has already set down.

And since this is so, there is no sense in likening thought to what he likened it to, nor any sense in making the human soul stand in the position of the First Intellect, nor thought within it stand in the position of the Universal Soul. For the nobility of the human soul and its passage from potency to act lie only in acquisition, and acquisition comes only from the senses as deed and from conception as knowledge. And since the nobility of souls lies in acquisition, part of which is by thought and inference as conception, and since thought belongs to the souls only that they may be ennobled by it, it is as though he had made the cause of the nobility of the First Intellect to be the Second Intellect, called the Soul. And that is an inverted proposition. For the nobility of the Follower and its rank come from that which precedes it in existence, which is the Antecedent, because the nobility of the Antecedent and its rank come from what comes after it. And the ruling upon that whose existence is a second existence, in perfection and completeness, is that it be the reverse of that whose existence is a first existence. So neither does the one run in the course of the other in this, nor the other in the course of the one, even though they share from another aspect. Yet the author of al-Nusra has passed judgment upon that whose existence is a first existence by what belongs to that whose existence is a second existence. And that is impossible.
