# The Arbiter

His book, *Rahat al-Aql*, the Rest of the Mind, is one of those precious works that scarcely finds an equal among the books of the philosophers, or among what remains of their legacy, in strength, durability, and depth. Demand for it was small, its circulation slow, and its readership limited to a special class of brilliant scholars and philosophers capable of mastering it.

The great preacher and Yemeni historian Idris Imad al-Din mentioned him in his book *Uyun al-Akhbar*, saying:

> ((هو أساس الدعوة التي عليه عمادها وبه علا واستقام منارها وبه استبانت المشكلات وانفرجت المعضلات))

"He is the foundation of the da'wa upon which it rests and by which it is raised up; through him its beacon was set straight, and by him problems were identified and dilemmas resolved." The Syrian Ismaili preacher Nur al-Din Ahmad described him in his book *Fusul wa Akhbar*, Chapters and Reports, saying:

> ((لو ان الدعوة الاسماعيلية لم تنتج غير الكرماني لكفاها فخراً ومجداً ولكان ذلك كافياً))

"If the Ismaili da'wa had produced nothing but al-Kirmani, that alone would have been ample pride and glory, and that alone would have been enough."

His impact and his stature appeared during the reign of the Fatimid Caliph al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah. His famous title was *Hujjat al-Iraqayn*, the Hujjah of the Two Iraqs, meaning that he had charge of the affairs of the da'wa's cultural centers throughout Persia and Iraq. In Cairo his standing was that of a Hujjah, one of the twelve hujjahs charged with managing the affairs of the da'wa, the intellectual vanguard of the world. He was later appointed head of *Dar al-Hikma*, the House of Wisdom, in Cairo, the cultural institution that I may rightly call the first university established in the world.

He travelled in a delegation to Cairo in 408 AH at the request of al-Sadiq al-Ma'mun, then the foremost of the Fatimid state's preachers in the era of al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah, at a moment when religious battles were heating up, new movements were arising, and the market of innovation was flooded with calls aimed at exaggeration and at deviation from the realities and foundations of the faith. He gave talks and lectures in *Dar al-Hikma* and produced a great many researches and books, the most famous of which is *Risalat al-Wa'iza*, the Preacher's Message, composed in reply to al-Hasan al-Farghani, who held that al-Hakim was divine by the command of God. Among his other works are the epistles *al-Mabasim*, the Good Tidings, and *al-Masabih*, the Lamps, in which he was able to prove the Imamate as a necessary cosmic reality by reasoned and compelling arguments, arguments richly furnished with Hebrew, Syriac, and Persian expressions drawn from the books of the prophets of the heavenly utterance. He then took up the Platonic opinions as the basis for his researches and deployed them with a skill no one before him had brought to bear. All of this served as a general call to consolidate the philosophical and intellectual system, to raise its level, and to erase every trace of doubt, controversy, suspicion, and dispute.

In these lines I will not attempt to introduce the philosopher al-Kirmani merely as one of the Ismaili preachers who played an important role in the field of thought during the reign of al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah, nor merely as one of the philosophers who served the Fatimid da'wa with clear intellectual services. I present him, rather, as a philosopher among the philosophers of the world, one who walked upon the stage of universal philosophy and exerted everything in him to found a philosophical school upon a new intellectual foundation, drawn from the modern and innovative theories of his time and from the mental systems then current. This applies in particular to the theory of the unity of being, which the Second Teacher, al-Farabi, and the Chief Sheikh, Ibn Sina, hold. Here he expanded it in exposition and departed somewhat from the general curriculum laid down for the Ismaili preachers, whether those who came before him or those of his own age, who were obliged to follow a well-defined general framework whose limits they could not transgress or cross.

Returning to al-Kirmani's writings and reading them with careful analysis of what I see in them, he has resolved that there is contradiction, disharmony, and an attempt on the part of some of the existents to erase the others, yet that these existents persist in spite of this dissonance and this contradiction; for none of them is lost by the presence of an opposite, but rather they are all preserved under existence. All of this is identical to the theory of the Second Teacher on creation, in which he says:

> (( حفظ ادامة وجود الشيء الذي ليس وجوداً لذاته ادامة لا تتصل بشيء من العلل غير ذات المبدع ))

"The preservation of the continuance of the existence of a thing that does not possess, in itself, a permanence unrelated to any of the contingents, other than the Creator." And Ibn Sina, in the course of speaking of the possible and the necessary among the other existents, gives an account that clarifies the meaning of the continuity of existence among existents, saying:

> (( أما كون العلل ممكن الوجود في نفسه واجب الوجود لغيره فليس يناقض كونه دائم الوجود بغيره ))

"As for the fact that the contingents can exist in themselves, while not being obligated by the existence of others, this does not contradict their being always present in others." What these three philosophers, the Second Teacher, the Chief Sheikh, and the Hujjah of the Two Iraqs, go to in this regard nearly approaches what the philosopher Descartes said in the modern era:

> (( اذ يوجد من الفعل الذي يحفظ الله به العالم وبين الفعل الذي خلقه به ))

"There is, between the act by which God preserves the world and the act by which He created it, no real difference." This is what is known in his philosophy as the theory of *continuous creation*.

In fact, al-Kirmani shared something with the Muslim philosophers and was influenced by some of them, especially those who took up Greek philosophy. He is distinguished from them, however, by the fact that, when he presented these philosophies, he stamped them with the marks of novelty, wit, and innovation, and was the precursor of the modern European philosophers and of the ancient Oriental renewers. Accordingly, I may say that al-Kirmani left behind books and produced ideas that should be looked upon not only as Ismaili intellectual views, but as Islamic philosophical views of a high, public order, looking into the essence of things and into the deep intellectual aspects in a steady, elaborated manner in which genius and brilliance are manifest.

Yes, al-Kirmani spoke within the Neoplatonic systems and exerted all his efforts to apply some of their materials in his attractive philosophical and rhetorical style, and he worked to prove the matter of Prophethood and the Imamate from both the philosophical and the religious sides. On this point I can say that he upheld the theory that adherence to the outward Shari'ah leads to scriptural worship, while the further reach of philosophical interpretation and discussion of the boundaries (الحدود) is reserved for those permitted ones who have attained a high level in philosophy and science. I find all these features when I look closely at *The Illuminating Message*, *The Lamps in Proving the Imamate*, *Alerting the Guide and the Guided*, and *Rahat al-Aql*, the Rest of the Mind. Beyond this, al-Kirmani is a major asset in developing the intellectual system and expanding theoretical discourse, adding new elements, broadening certain topics, laying down the basic rules of interpretation, and treating contradiction and similarity, especially on the subject of the Imamate, which had been the focus of unstable and shifting opinions and theories. For this reason his book defined the rules, origins, ranks, and status of the Imamate, and set new foundations, systems, and order for it.

Al-Kirmani was prominent in the doctrine of the call to existence, in the negation of essence (الايسية) and substance (الليسية), in the doctrine that the divine attributes are subject to absolute negation, in the doctrine of the call to Tawhid, in the doctrine of the call concerning the two principles of origination, in the elaboration of emanation, and in the question of priority between the Imamate and the Prophethood, all in a purely scientific and logical manner. He did not stop there. At the outset he gathered the two first principles, the Active Intellect and the Universal Soul, together with the system of the ten Platonic intellects upheld by the Second Teacher, al-Farabi. He set the world of origination, which is the world of intellects, the spiritual world, and the corporeal celestial world, which is the world of stars and planets, alongside the physical world, which is what lies below the lunar orbit, and the world of religion, which is the world of the boundaries of religion. He then drew geographical and astronomical diagrams for it, together with terrestrial and corporeal plans that came out highly artistic and creative, showing his ingenuity of expression and his eminence in philosophy.

On returning to the Rest of the Mind and reviewing what is stated in it, I see that it is full of formulations and arguments concerning the apostolic statements about God and the negation of the divine attributes. Among the positions he stated and supported are these: that no attribute attaches to Allah; that He is not a body nor in a body; that He neither rationalizes Himself nor senses Himself; that He is not in a form or in a substance; that He has no opposite and no likeness; that no language can express Him; and that He has no rank among existents. This indicates the breadth of his knowledge across the various sciences, especially the rational ones. On this the Mu'tazila (المعتزلة) and the theologians (المتكلمة) agree, and he agrees with Ibn Rushd. On the argument for the existence of God I see him agreeing with Descartes, even if they differ in the details and in the method of expression. If I add a little further research, I see that he folded into his solutions and ideas some of the views of philosophers who preceded him and of his contemporaries, Plato, Aristotle, al-Kindi, al-Farabi, and Ibn Sina.

I note from al-Baqillani, al-Baghdadi, and al-Ghazali that they ruled the Ismailis, by their denial of the attributes, to have fallen into invalidity (التعطيل). But al-Kirmani answered this charge, saying:

> (( ان التعطيل الصريح انما يكون بأن يتوجه فعل حرف النفي ((لا)) نحو ((الهوية)) قصداً، كأن يقال مثلاً ((لا هو)) و ((لا اله)) وليس هذا مما تقول به الاسماعيلية ، اذ ان النفي عندهم هو نفي الصفات وحدها ، وتوجيه فعل حرف النفي ((لا)) انما ينصب عندهم على الصفات دون الهوية ))

"Outright denial is to direct the action of the particle of negation, لا, no, against the identity itself, as in saying لا هو, He is not, or لا اله, there is no god. This is not what the Ismailis say. Their negation is the negation of the attributes alone; the direction of the negative particle لا for them is set upon the attributes, leaving the identity untouched."

However the matter stands, al-Kirmani gave special attention to this subject in his book, the Rest of the Mind. And in this book I see him departing from all philosophers, writers, historians, and scholars: instead of dividing his book into the usual books, chapters, and sections that other men of thought and authors employ, he likens his book to a city encircled by seven walls. Whoever enters it must pass through the seven walls, and to pass through them he must face seven gates (المشارع) branching off from each wall, except the seventh. As for the seventh and last wall encircling the city, it has fourteen gates. I will not enter here into an exposition of what al-Kirmani meant by his walls and his gates, for fear of going on at length, but this does not stop me from affirming his superiority in philosophy, the power of his thinking, and the depth of his grasp of the essence of things.

Al-Kirmani published a number of works, the most important of which include the following: *The Divine Treatise*, *The Treatise of the Systems*, *The Treatise of Light*, *The Illuminating Treatise*, *The Necessary Treatise*, *The Guiding Treatise*, *The Preacher's Treatise*, *The Sufficient Treatise* (*al-Kafi*), *Alerting the Guide and the Guided*, *Wrists of the Guide* (*Maʿasim al-Huda*), *The Golden Sayings*, *Separating the Discourse and Distinguishing the Manifest Truth from Doubt*, *The Treatise of the Returner*, *The Treatise of the Index*, *Quantities and Facts*, *The Treatise on Tawhid in the Promised Land*, *The Crown of Intellects* (*Taj al-ʿUqul*), *The Balance of the Mind*, *The Book of Criticism and Obligation*, *Psychological Adequacy*, *The Book of Scales*, *The Baghdadi and Basran Sessions*, *al-Shaʿari's Treatise on the Elite*, *Rahat al-Aql*, and *The Treatise of the Week of the Role of the Cover*.

Finally, I must say that al-Kirmani is among the philosophers who weigh heavily upon our philosophical world, and that the study of his writings and his output is of great importance. It gives a clear picture of the impact of philosophy upon the history of thought for those interested in Oriental Studies and Islamic Philosophy.
