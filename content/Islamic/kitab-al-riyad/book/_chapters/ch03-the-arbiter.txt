# The Arbiter

He lived and rose to prominence in the era of the Fatimid state, and his book *Rahat al-Aql*, the Rest of the Mind, stands among those rare works that scarcely find an equal, in strength, durability, and depth, among the books of the philosophers or among what remains of their legacy. Demand for it was small, its currency slow, its readership confined to a special class of brilliant scholars and philosophers able to master it.

The great preacher and Yemeni historian Idris Imad al-Din mentioned him, and said:

> ((هو أساس الدعوة التي عليه عمادها وبه علا واستقام منارها وبه استبانت المشكلات وانفرجت المعضلات))

"He is the foundation of the daʿwa upon which it rests and by which it is raised up; through him its beacon was set straight, and by him problems were identified and dilemmas resolved."

And the Syrian Ismaili preacher Nur al-Din Ahmad described him in his *Fusul wa Akhbar*, the Chapters and Reports, and said:

> ((لو ان الدعوة الاسماعيلية لم تنتج غير الكرماني لكفاها فخراً ومجداً ولكان ذلك كافياً))

"If the Ismaili daʿwa had produced nothing but al-Kirmani, that alone would have been ample pride and glory, and that alone would have been enough."

His impact and his standing appeared in the reign of the Fatimid Caliph al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah. His famous title was *Hujjat al-Iraqayn*, the Hujjah of the Two Iraqs, meaning that the affairs of the daʿwa's cultural centres throughout Persia and Iraq were in his charge. In Cairo his rank was that of a Hujjah, one of the twelve hujjahs entrusted with managing the affairs of the daʿwa across the world. He was afterward made head of *Dar al-Hikma*, the House of Wisdom in Cairo, the cultural institution that we may rightly call the first university established in the world.

He came in a delegation to Cairo in 408 AH, at the request of al-Sadiq al-Ma'mun, then the foremost of the Fatimid state's preachers in the era of al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah, at a moment when the religious battles were growing hot, new movements were rising, and the market of innovation was busy with calls aimed at exaggeration and at deviation from the realities and foundations of the faith. In *Dar al-Hikma* he gave his lessons and lectures, and produced a great many researches and books. The most famous of them is *Risalat al-Waʿiza*, the Preacher's Message, written in reply to al-Hasan al-Farghani, who held that al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah was divine. Among his other works are the two epistles *al-Mabasim*, the Good Tidings, and *al-Masabih*, the Lamps, in which he was able to prove the Imamate as a necessary cosmic reality by reasoned and compelling arguments, arguments richly furnished with Hebrew, Syriac, and Persian expressions drawn from the books of the prophets of the heavenly utterance. He then took the Platonic opinions as the basis of his researches and deployed them with a skill no one before him had brought to bear. All of this served as a general call to consolidate the philosophical and intellectual system, to raise its level, and to erase every trace of doubt, controversy, suspicion, and dispute.

However the matter stands, in these lines I will not attempt to present the philosopher al-Kirmani merely as one of the Ismaili preachers who played an important part in the field of thought during the reign of al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah, nor merely as one of the philosophers who rendered the Fatimid daʿwa clear intellectual service. I present him, rather, as a philosopher among the philosophers of the world, one who walked upon the stage of universal philosophy and spent everything in him to found a philosophical school upon an intellectual foundation new for his age, upon fresh and inventive theories that carried real weight, and upon rational systems that accord with the theory of the unity of being held by the Second Teacher, al-Farabi, and the Chief Sheikh, Ibn Sina. This he expanded in exposition, departing somewhat from the general curriculum laid down for the Ismaili preachers, whether those before him or those of his own age, who were bound to walk by a well-studied general framework whose limits they could not cross.

Returning to al-Kirmani's writings, reading them closely and analysing what is in them, I find that he settled the theory that there is contradiction and disharmony among the existents, and an attempt on the part of some of them to erase the others, and yet that these existents persist in spite of this dissonance and this contradiction, none of them lost by the presence of an opposite, all of them preserved under existence. All of this is identical to the theory of the Second Teacher on creation, in which he says:

> (( حفظ ادامة وجود الشيء الذي ليس وجوداً لذاته ادامة لا تتصل بشيء من العلل غير ذات المبدع ))

"The preservation of the continuance of the existence of a thing that does not possess, in itself, a permanence unconnected with any of the contingents other than the essence of the Creator."

And Ibn Sina, in the course of speaking of the possible and the necessary among the other existents, gives an account by which the meaning of the continuity of existence upon the existents is made plain, saying:

> (( أما كون العلل ممكن الوجود في نفسه واجب الوجود لغيره فليس يناقض كونه دائم الوجود بغيره))

"As for the contingents being possible of existence in themselves, and necessary of existence through another, this does not contradict their being ever-existent through another."

What these three philosophers, the Second Teacher, the Chief Sheikh, and the Hujjah of the Two Iraqs, hold in this regard comes near to what the philosopher Descartes said in the modern age:

> (( اذ يوجد من الفعل الذي يحفظ الله به العالم وبين الفعل الذي خلقه به ))

"That there is, between the act by which God preserves the world and the act by which He created it, no real difference."

This is what is known in his philosophy as the theory of *continuous creation*.

In truth, al-Kirmani shared something with the Muslim philosophers and was influenced by some of them, especially those who took up Greek philosophy. He is distinguished from them, however, in that, when he set forth these philosophies, he stamped them with the marks of novelty, wit, and invention, and was ahead of the modern European philosophers and the ancient Oriental renewers in his renewal. On this ground one may say that al-Kirmani left behind books and produced ideas that ought to be regarded not merely as Ismaili intellectual views, but as Islamic philosophical views of a high and public order, looking into the essence of things and into the deep rational matters in a steady and elaborated manner in which genius and brilliance are manifest.

Yes, al-Kirmani spoke within the Neoplatonic systems, and spent all his effort in applying some of their materials in his attractive philosophical and rhetorical style, and he laboured to prove the matter of Prophethood and the Imamate from both the philosophical and the religious sides. On this I can say that he upheld the theory that adherence to the outward Shariʿa leads to a scriptural worship, and that this worship, which includes within it interpretation (taʾwil) and philosophical discourse, is reserved for the permitted ones (al-maʾdhunun), those who have reached a high level in philosophy and the sciences. We find all these features when we look closely into *The Illuminating Message*, into *The Lamps in Proving the Imamate*, into *Alerting the Guide and the Guided*, and into *Rahat al-Aql*, the Rest of the Mind. Beyond this, al-Kirmani has a great merit in developing the intellectual system and expanding the particular theoretical discourse, adding new elements, broadening certain topics, laying down the basic rules of interpretation, and treating both contradiction and resemblance, especially on the subject of the Imamate, around which unstable and unsettled opinions and theories had turned. For this reason his book *Rahat al-Aql* fixed its rules, its origins, its ranks, and its centre, and set for it new foundations, systems, and order.

Al-Kirmani was prominent in the doctrine of the call to existence, in the negation of essence (al-aysiyya) and substance (al-laysiyya), in the doctrine of the absolute negation of the attributes from God, in the doctrine of the call to Tawhid, in the doctrine of the call concerning the two principles, origination (al-ibdaʿ) and emanation (al-inbiʿath), in the matter of Imami exaltation, and in the question of priority between the Imamate and the Prophethood, all in a purely scientific and logical manner. He did not stop there, but at the very outset gathered the two first principles, the Active Intellect and the Universal Soul, together with the system of the ten Platonic intellects upheld by the Second Teacher, al-Farabi. He set the world of origination, which is the world of intellects, the spiritual world, alongside the celestial corporeal world, which is the world of the orbits and the stars, and the physical world, which is what lies below the orbit of the moon, and the world of religion, which is the world of the boundaries (hudud) of religion; then he drew for these the geographical, astronomical, terrestrial, and bodily diagrams that came out most artful and inventive, and that showed his skill of expression and his high standing in philosophy.

On returning to *Rahat al-Aql* and reviewing what is stated in it, one sees it filled with formulations and proofs on the annulling of essence (al-aysiyya) from God and the negation of the divine attributes. Among what he stated and affirmed: that God, exalted be He, is not attained by any attribute; that He is neither a body nor in a body; that no intellect conceives His essence, and no sense perceives Him; that He is not a form nor a substance; that He has no opposite and no like; that there is nothing in the languages by which He may be expressed; and that He has no rank among the existents. This indicates the breadth of his acquaintance with the various sciences, above all the rational ones. In this he agrees with the Muʿtazila and the theologians (al-mutakallimun), and he accords with Ibn Rushd; and in the matter of grounding causality (al-ʿilliyya) upon the existence of God we see him according with Descartes, though the two differ in the particulars and the manner of expression. If we add to this a little further research, we see that he folded into his solutions and his ideas some of the views of the philosophers who came before him and of his contemporaries, such as Plato, Aristotle, al-Kindi, al-Farabi, and Ibn Sina.

We note, on returning to what al-Baqillani, al-Baghdadi, and al-Ghazali wrote, that they judged the Ismailis, by their denial of the attributes, to have fallen into divestment (al-taʿtil). But al-Kirmani repelled this charge, and said that outright divestment is only for the act of the particle of negation, لا, "no," to be directed toward the identity (al-huwiyya) deliberately, as when one says, for example, لا هو, "He is not," and لا اله, "there is no god"; and that this is not what the Ismailis say, since with them the negation is the negation of the attributes alone, and the act of the particle of negation لا is levelled, in their view, upon the attributes and not upon the identity.

However the matter stands, al-Kirmani gave special care in his book *Rahat al-Aql* to this subject, and in this book we see him departing from all the philosophers, writers, historians, and scholars. Instead of dividing his book into the customary books, chapters, and sections, as other men of thought and authorship have done, he likened his book to a city encircled by seven walls, so that whoever enters it must cross the seven walls, and to cross them he must meet seven gates (mashariʿ) branching off from each wall, except the seventh and last wall encircling the city, which has fourteen gates. I will not enter here into an exposition of what al-Kirmani intended by his walls and his gates, for fear of length; yet this does not keep me from affirming his high reach in philosophy, the power of his thinking, and the depth of his grasp of the essence of things.

Al-Kirmani produced a number of works, some of which have been published, and among the most important are: *al-Risala al-Durriyya*, *Risalat al-Nazm*, *al-Risala al-Wadiʾa*, *al-Risala al-Mudiʾa*, *al-Risala al-Lazima*, *al-Risala al-Hadiya*, *al-Risala al-Waʿiza*, *al-Risala al-Kafiya*, *Tanbih al-Hadi wa-l-Mustahdi*, *Maʿasim al-Huda*, *al-Aqwal al-Dhahabiyya*, *Fasl al-Khitab wa-Inabat al-Haqq al-Mutajalli ʿan al-Irtiyab*, *Risalat al-Maʿad*, *Risalat al-Fihrist*, *al-Maqadir wa-l-Haqaʾiq*, *Risalat al-Tawhid fi al-Maʿad*, *Taj al-ʿUqul*, *Mizan al-ʿAql*, *Kitab al-Naqd wa-l-Ilzam*, *al-Kayl al-Nafsi*, *Kitab al-Maqayis*, *al-Majalis al-Baghdadiyya wa-l-Basriyya*, *Risalat al-Shaʿara fi al-Khawass*, *Rahat al-Aql*, and *Risalat Usbuʿ Dawr al-Satr*.

Finally, it must be said that al-Kirmani is among the philosophers who weigh heavily in our philosophical world, and that the study of his writings and his output is of great importance, giving a clear picture of the impact of philosophy upon the history of thought for those concerned with Oriental studies and Islamic philosophy.
