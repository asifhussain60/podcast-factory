# The Freedom to Disagree

The Islamic world at the close of the third century of the Muhammadan Hijra bore the stamp of its sciences, its philosophy, and its letters. New currents, tangled doctrinal disagreements, and an unending theological argument moved through its cultural sphere and its learned society, coming to rest with no single party and settling in no single place. How useful it is, then, for the researcher who wishes to study the origins of these differences in depth: to weigh the opinions of the writers and scholars who lived in that distant age, who left behind scientific traces and fresh modes of expression proper to their time and to their grasp of philosophical questions, and to measure the reach of their effort in interpreting texts and construing verses, so that he may arrive at a reasonable view that carries him toward reality and truth.

The disputes I speak of did not, for the most part, remain confined to the spoken discussions once raised in salons and centers of learning. They grew into long treatises that appeared on the pages of books and pamphlets. Among the groups that contributed a large share to this field were the Ismailis. Yet it is a strange thing that I do not find this group finishing its verbal argument with other groups before it enters upon another argument of an altogether new kind: a debate among its own preachers. In this lies clear proof of an intellectual freedom that prevailed in their lives, a release from the constraints that grip conservative societies, that draw a boundary about them and bind them to shun anomaly and to keep to the inherited pattern of old.

The first debate, or in more exact terms the first scholarly discussion, that opened new horizons for the Ismailis, that carried them into an atmosphere of far-reaching scientific liberation, and that made them a subject of conjecture and talk throughout the Islamic world, was the appearance of the book *al-Mahsul* (المحصول), The Harvest.

This book entered circulation at the beginning of the fourth century of the Hijra, the tenth century of the Christian era. It is attributed to the great preacher of Khurasan,

> محمد بن احمد النسفي

Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Nasafi, who was credited with turning the Samanid state in Transoxiana toward Ismailism, and who was put to death in the year 331 AH (942 AD), as recorded in the book

> الفرق بين الفرق

*al-Farq bayn al-Firaq* (The Difference Between the Sects) by its author Abd al-Qahir al-Baghdadi. It is very strange that the exponent of the two Iraqs and their foremost philosopher,

> حميد الدين احمد الكرماني

Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani, who took up this subject and went deeply into its examination, made no mention of the author of *al-Mahsul*. His discussion was fixed instead upon

> أبي حاتم الرازي ، وأبي يعقوب السجستاني

Abu Hatim al-Razi and Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani. There must, then, be fundamental reasons that justify this silence, and perhaps the book *al-Mahsul* is wrongly ascribed to al-Nasafi.

Whatever the truth of that matter, the acclaim that *al-Mahsul* won among the Ismaili thinkers and the ranks of their scholars in that day moved Abu Hatim al-Razi, the chief preacher of northwestern Persia, to compose a book in which he examined what *al-Mahsul* had said. He called his book *al-Islah* (The Correction). It is plain that the opinions al-Razi advanced stirred a reaction in another great preacher named Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani, who judged them contrary to the truth and to first principles. This is what drove him to compose a book contesting al-Razi's views, which he called *al-Nusra* (The Defense), a work in which its author took up the defense of *al-Mahsul* and assailed Abu Hatim al-Razi without mercy. It appears that *al-Nusra* is among al-Sijistani's earliest works, as al-Kirmani himself hints in his book.

Not long afterward, al-Kirmani surveyed the whole subject in a full and thorough review and composed his precious treatise *al-Riyad* (The Gardens), a judgment between the two authors, as he named them, the author of *al-Islah* and the author of *al-Nusra*:

> الاصلاح والنصرة

With consummate skill he appraised the opinions set out in the three books and subjected them to a scholarly criticism, and he set them side by side with the Law of the Guiding Mission,

> قانون الدعوة الهادية

which is to say the philosophical and doctrinal theories held officially by the Fatimid court of his day.

It is also worth noting the scholarly ideas that these works contain. A philosophical body of thought such as this deserves to be studied, examined, and scrutinized closely. But by an unhappy chance the books

> المحصول والنصرة

*al-Mahsul* and *al-Nusra* have both been lost. As for al-Razi's *al-Islah* and al-Kirmani's *al-Riyad*, they remain preserved to this day.
