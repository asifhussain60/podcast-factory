# The Two Reformers

*Kitab al-Riyad*

Al-Kirmani's book *al-Riyad* stands among the important works that give voice to the philosophical theories of Ismailism in the fourth century of the Hijra. Before I enter into the opinions, sayings, arguments, and statements it contains, I must set down a brief overview of the lives of the three philosophers who took part in the discussion, namely al-Razi, al-Sijistani, and al-Kirmani.

**Al-Razi**

He is Abu Hatim Ahmad ibn Hamdan al-Laythi al-Warsanani al-Razi, and in Ismaili circles his name is given, in reverence, as our master Abu Hatim the Exalted. He was a preacher of Ismailism in the land of Rayy, and then moved to Baghdad and made it his residence. The date of his birth is not known exactly. His activity in the Ismaili da'wa fell during the imamate of Muhammad al-Mahdi Billah (Ubayd Allah), the founder of the Fatimid state in the Maghrib. He was distinguished by the sweetness of his speech and by the eloquence for which he was famous. He won over al-Marwazi, the prince of Rayy, a strict Sunni, and persuaded the princes of those regions to enter into Ismailism, until he became one of the standards of politics and religion, and one of the guides of the Islamic scientific renaissance in the late third and early fourth centuries of the Hijra. He took refuge in the theological and philosophical sciences. The orientalist Paul Kraus said of him: "He was a leading preacher of the Ismailis and played an important role in political affairs in Tabaristan and Daylam, especially in Rayy and Isfahan, until the chief statesmen responded to him, such as Asfar ibn Shirawayh, Mardawij the commander, and others."

In truth, Abu Hatim al-Razi was one of the standards of the scientific renaissance not only within Ismailism, but in the Islamic world more broadly, and he contributed to the spread of philosophical teachings in all the eastern lands, and especially within the wider sphere of Islamic culture. For all this, he was not spared the persecution of his enemies in Daylam, and he was forced into hiding in the last years of his life. He died in 322 AH, after the Fatimid al-Qa'im had taken charge of the affairs of the Ismaili imamate in the Maghrib, at the age of sixty-two, as some historians have said.

He was a contemporary of Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi, the famous physician and author of well-known philosophical views, in which he departed from many of Aristotle's theories, both natural and metaphysical, denying the reconciliation of philosophy and religion, and holding that philosophy is the only path to the reform of the individual and of society. Between the two Razis there were violent and repeated discussions, attended by some scholars and political leaders. Abu Hatim recorded these discussions in his book *A'lam al-Nubuwwa*; and although he does not name the man he is criticizing, calling him only the atheist, there is conclusive evidence that this atheist was none other than Abu Bakr al-Razi.

The great philosopher al-Kirmani, Hujjat al-Iraqayn, has noted in his book *al-Aqwal al-Dhahabiyya* that discussions concerning prophethood and the legal rituals took place between Abu Bakr al-Razi and Abu Hatim on the island of Rayy in the days of Mardawij, and in his presence, and that Abu Hatim was able to humble Abu Bakr al-Razi and to refute his views in *A'lam al-Nubuwwa*, in pages that overflow with force and with wonder, closing off, for the arrogant and the stubborn, every means of evasion and flight.

His most important work is the book *al-Zina*, on the Arabic letters, their derivatives, the reasons for their forms, and the meanings of words. It is a great book on authentic Arabic vocabulary, both native and borrowed, on the frame within which the Qur'an was articulated and which the Muslims gave the force of usage, and from which Islamic terminology took its shape. This book runs to twelve hundred pages, and he dedicated it to the Fatimid imam who succeeded Muhammad al-Mahdi Billah (Ubayd Allah). To him also belong the books *A'lam al-Nubuwwa*, *al-Islah*, *al-Jami'*, and others.

**Al-Sijistani**

Abu Ya'qub Ishaq al-Sijistani, also called al-Sijzi, is reckoned to be at the forefront of those scholars who struggled, labored, and dedicated themselves to establishing cosmic philosophical principles upon doctrinal intellectual foundations, upon solid scientific bases, and upon pillars firm of frame that do not tremble whatever crises may befall them. Indeed, he is among those who sacrificed all they possessed in order to spread these teachings and to circulate them in other lands, until, late in his life, he was accused of blasphemy and atheism by the conservative public, and was at last killed for the sake of his faith. Al-Sijistani played an important role in the field of philosophy and rendered the noblest intellectual services in the third century of the Hijra. His intellectual mark appeared in his disciple Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani, Hujjat al-Iraqayn, who walked in his method, called to his teachings, and drew from the flow of his springs. And once we know that al-Kirmani studied philosophy in al-Sijistani's school of thought, we may place al-Sijistani in the first rank among the scholars and thinkers of the Muslims and the renowned philosophers of the world.

He was a contemporary of the esoteric Ismaili da'wa in the Age of Manifestation, that is, during the flourishing of the Fatimid state and its emergence as an Islamic state of civilizational, scientific, social, and political stature. Although he lived in a country whose people held to a doctrine that differed from his own madhhab, he was compelled to take taqiyya as a screen and to be most cautious in his movements and his preaching. For this reason his life has come down to us with a certain obscurity, and one may not be blamed if we have not been able to arrive at a full knowledge of his biography or to lay hold of everything concerning it. In the same way, only a little has reached us about the great preacher al-Nasafi, al-Nakhshabi, the author of *al-Mahsul*, beyond what authors have mentioned of his efforts and of his contact with Nasr ibn Ahmad the Samanid in Transoxiana, until that Samanid embraced the Ismaili da'wa. We likewise know little about Abu Hatim al-Razi, the linguist and author of the oldest book in the science of the Arabic language, the book *al-Zina*; and little about Hujjat al-Iraqayn Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani, despite the survival of most of his writings and works, for had not al-Mu'ayyad fi'l-Din Hibatallah al-Shirazi, the friend and disputant of Abu al-Ala al-Ma'arri, written his biography with his own hand, we should have been able to know nothing of him. And so it is with the obscurity that surrounds the lives of the great men of the Ismaili da'wa, the hujjas and the preachers of the districts of Syria, Iran, Yemen, and elsewhere: their lives are shrouded in the deepest obscurity, and the books in which they recorded their biographies have been lost, with only a little of them remaining.

He is traced to Sijistan, a province in the south of Khurasan, from a Persian family said to be the family of the Persian hero Rustam; and there are those who say that he was of Iraqi origin, that his grandfather came from Kufa and settled in Sijistan. Some researchers who studied his philosophy claim that he died in 331 AH. But this view does not accord with the historical reality, for it is known of al-Sijistani that he was the teacher of al-Kirmani, and al-Kirmani remained alive until the year 411 AH. At what time, then, did al-Kirmani take from him the philosophical sciences of the da'wa? There is an explicit text in the book *al-Iftikhar* by al-Sijistani himself, in which he mentions that he composed it in 360 AH; and *al-Iftikhar* is in turn mentioned in al-Kirmani's *al-Riyad*, which is to say that al-Sijistani was a preacher in the province of Bukhara during the caliphate of the imam al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah al-Fatimi. The meaning of this is that he was a contemporary of the great preacher Ja'far ibn Mansur al-Yamani, and of the learned jurist the qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad ibn Hayyun al-Maghribi al-Tamimi, chief judge of the Fatimid state, and of others among the great authors and scholars of the da'wa in that radiant golden age of learning. Nothing shows the scholarly worth of al-Sijistani more than the books and writings he left behind him, composed in Arabic, with a portion of them composed in Persian; al-Biruni referred to them in his books, and al-Baghdadi mentioned them in his *al-Farq bayn al-Firaq*.

Al-Sijistani left behind, as I have said, an abundant body of scholarly writing that is reckoned among the deepest that has been written in philosophy, numbering more than thirty titles. Perhaps the most famous of his books are *Ithbat al-Nubuwwat*, *Kashf al-Mahjub*, *Tuhfat al-Mustajibin*, and *al-Yanabi'*, which he divided into forty springs (yanabi'), and which has become a book that is rarely surpassed in depth. It appears that he composed it for a particular class of readers, the preachers and the holders of the highest ranks in the da'wa, and those who had reached the summit in their philosophical studies. As for the reason for his dividing it into forty springs, it goes back to his wish that each spring should carry the interpretation of one of the forty limits who make up the highest council of the da'wa.

Among his books also, mentioned by the great traveler and famous Persian poet Nasir-i Khusraw in his *Zad al-Musafirin*, are: The Foundations of the Da'wa; The Interpretation of the Laws; The Iris of Blessings, or The Iris of Survival; The Brilliant Message; the *Kitab al-Iftikhar*; The Scales, which is divided into nineteen scales; The Ladder of Salvation; The Victory; The Hooks; Amusements of Sorrows; Secrets of the Return; Sermons on Ethics; The Stranger on the Meaning of the Elixir; Sociable of Hearts; Composition of Spirits; Security from Confusion; Closets of Evidence; The Proof; and others.

In the lines that follow I summarize al-Sijistani's views on theology, as he expressed them across most of his writings. He holds that the Originator of all originated things is an Ancient, Exalted, and deep-rooted Creator in the bringing-forth of firstness, and that the world of beings and originated things is contingent, brought into time; for were it not so, there would have to be something prior to it that had brought it into being. And were the world ancient, prior to the Creator, it would be impossible for His omnipotence to be bound to that which is ancient, or His existence to that which is nothing, and it would require an originator to originate it. He is exalted above the grasp of the attributes: He is not attained by sense, does not fall under sight, is not perceived by the eyes, is not characterized by any genus, does not occur to conjecture, is not seen by the eyes, is not described by the senses, is not grasped by analogy, and is not likened to men. He is purified of any contrary that would oppose Him, or any equal that would match Him, or any likeness of any thing; He is exalted above the likeness of things that are bounded. The imaginations are bewildered in the description of His omnipotence, the understandings fall short of the character of His kingdom, and the eyes are dulled before the grasp of His greatness. He has no like and no likeness; He is without equal and without opposite, for an opposite is opposed only by a rival. He has pointed to His identity by His creation and His works, and to His names by His prophets; and the intellect has no field in which to attain His heaven, and no room for likening, since to liken the Originator to what He has originated is impossible.

He is the cause of every existent, for He is the Originator of the originated and the Inventor of inventions, the cause of the being of all beings, the Lord of every thing, its Creator, its Perfecter, and the One who brings it to the best of states. He is too exalted for any thought to bound Him or any measure to encompass Him. He has no names, for names were coined for the things He has brought into being, and no attributes, for attributes belong to essences; and the letters of language cannot reach the pronouncing of His name, nor may anything of them be applied to Him, since they are all among the things He has invented, and all the names He has originated He made names for the things He has originated. He is Ancient, before eternity, the possessor of the source of firstness in due order, for from Him the first limit issued forth and the first existent overflowed, while He is the Originator of the originated, the Cause of causes, the Maker of what is made, the Ever-Existing by His singleness and His self-sufficiency, and the possessor of the first act of bringing-forth. It was the first number, which He made the origin of numbers, just as He made the Intellect the origin of existents, and the Articulator the origin of the world of religion. To all this is added that He is attained by no attribute, that He is not a body nor within a body, that no reasoner reasons out His essence, and no sense senses Him, and that He is neither an image nor a matter, and that there is nothing in the languages by which He may be expressed. He exists, but it is not correct that He should be an existent of the kind of the existents that came to be from Him. As for the inference to Him, it is drawn from the existence of the other existents, in that there is no effect without a cause and no existent except by that which necessitates its existence, and that existents rest one upon another in their being, while some of that upon which the others rest are themselves not fixed in existence and are not existent.

After this, al-Sijistani passes on to the existents in their order and sequence, and says: The Originator brought into being, at the first of creation, nothing but the Intellect, and in its essence He enclosed the forms of all originated things. To the Intellect the name Pen is added, because by the Pen the inscriptions of creation appear from the beginning. The Intellect is called the Throne, that is, a seat for the one who sits upon it, and by his sitting upon it his majesty is known over him who is beneath him. The Intellect is called the First, meaning that the firstness from which the created things appeared, that is, all that exists and all that is stamped upon it, is prior in receiving the effects of wisdom before the remaining limits, on account of its nearness to that source and its union with it, which is knowledge and command, both being one in meaning. And it may be that the act of the Intellect precedes its power, and this excellence is found in no existent besides it, for all the limits beneath it have their powers preceding their acts; this excellence belongs to the Intellect alone, that it may be by it complete and perfect. The Intellect is called the Decree (qada'), inasmuch as by the Intellect the Soul attains the grasp of what is known and the winning of what is sought, and it may be said that the Intellect is the decree of God, mighty and majestic, among His creation. The Intellect is also called *al-hayuli*, the prime matter, meaning that by the Intellect the forms that gush forth are given their subsistence; and it is called the Sun, meaning that by the Intellect the realities are seen.

Then comes the Soul, which is the second creation gushing forth from the first creation. It was named Soul (نفس) only because it forever breathes to draw benefit, so that the succession of its breathing is the subsistence of creation. The Soul is called the Tablet, meaning that what has been split off from the Intellect, of the lights of wisdom, is inscribed upon the Soul, and from the Soul is joined the flowing that issues from it, in measure of its purity and its refinement. The Soul is called the King, meaning that the Soul is the possession of the Intellect and its handmaid, for by the Soul the excellence of the Intellect appears, just as by the servant the excellence of the master appears. The Soul is called the Follower, meaning that the one who follows the Intellect in the matter of receiving the effects of the Word is the Soul; and it may be that the Soul, by its power, follows the Intellect by its act. The Soul is called Destiny (qadar), meaning that what unites with the Soul, of the benefits of the Intellect, is such that measuring and delimiting encompass it. The Soul is called the Image, and the meaning of this is that the Soul was formed from the essence of the Intellect and its radiance, and that whenever it aspires to reach it, so as to take its rank, its light is effaced, just as the moon draws its light from the light of the sun, and when they gather in one station, its light is effaced. Of the Intellect and the Soul together it is said, in a single word, the Two Principles.

These are some of al-Sijistani's philosophical views on theology, which I have set out in brief and joined to what I have been able to recover of the story of his life. Perhaps they furnish the compelling proof of his command of the sciences and of his deep-rooted gift for setting down texts in an attractive order and an exquisite arrangement.

**Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani**

A remarkable scholarly figure whose life-history is enveloped in obscurity; a great philosopher who lived in a radiant scholarly age; a noble preacher who inscribed upon the pages of thought the soundest of researches and the deepest of lines, and left to the generations a number of works of which the least that can be said is that they are a precious treasure and an enduring heritage. The philosophers of the Islamic world consider him the greatest scholar that the Ismaili school produced.
