# The Two Reformers

Al-Kirmani's book الرياض (al-Riyad) stands among the important works that give voice to the philosophical theories of Ismailism in the fourth century of the Hijra. Two copies of it have come into my hands, the first from India and the second from Tehran, and it has fallen to me to investigate the book, to explain it, and to set down its notes and commentary. Before I enter into the opinions, sayings, arguments, and statements it contains, I owe the reader a brief account of the lives of the three philosophers who took part in the discussion, namely al-Razi, al-Sijistani, and al-Kirmani.

The first of them, al-Razi, is Abu Hatim Ahmad ibn Hamdan al-Laythi al-Warsanani al-Razi, whom Ismailis call, in reverence, our master Abu Hatim. He was a preacher of the Ismaili da'wa in the land of Rayy, then moved to Baghdad and made it his residence. The date of his birth is not known with any precision. His work in the da'wa fell during the imamate of Muhammad al-Mahdi Billah (Ubayd Allah), founder of the Fatimid state in the Maghrib. He was marked by the sweetness of his speech and the eloquence for which he is remembered. He won over al-Marwazi, the prince of Rayy, who was a strict Sunni, and he laboured to persuade the princes of those regions to enter Ismailism, until he became one of the standards of politics and religion and one of the guides of the Islamic scholarly awakening in the late third and early fourth centuries, leaning always toward the theological and philosophical sciences. The great Orientalist Paul Kraus said of him:

> "كان من كبار دعاة الاسماعيلية ولعب دوراً هاماً في الشؤون السياسية في طبرستان والديلم ولا سيما في الري واصفهان حتى استجاب له كبار رجال الدولة مثل اسفار بن شيرويه ومرداويخ القائد وغيرهما."
>
> "He was a leading preacher of the Ismailis and played an important role in political affairs in Tabaristan and Daylam, especially in Rayy and Isfahan, until the chief statesmen responded to him, such as Asfar ibn Shirawayh, Mardawij the commander, and others."

In truth Abu Hatim al-Razi was a standard of the scholarly awakening not within Ismailism alone but in the wider Islamic world, and he spread philosophical teaching across the eastern lands, and especially within the compass of the general Islamic culture. For all this he was not spared the persecution of his enemies in Daylam, and in his last years he was forced into hiding. He died in 322 AH, after the Fatimid al-Qa'im took charge of the affairs of the Ismaili imamate in the Maghrib, having lived, as some historians say, sixty-two years.

He was a contemporary of Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi, the famous physician and holder of well-known philosophical views, who broke with many of Aristotle's natural and metaphysical theories, denied any reconciliation of philosophy and religion, and held that philosophy alone is the road to the reform of the individual and of society. Between the two Razis there ran fierce and repeated disputations, attended by scholars and by political leaders, and Abu Hatim set these down in his book أعلام النبوة (A'lam al-Nubuwwa, Signs of Prophethood). Though he never names the man he refutes, calling him only "the atheist," there is decisive evidence that this atheist was none other than Abu Bakr al-Razi. The great philosopher al-Kirmani, Hujjat al-Iraqayn, records in his book الأقوال الذهبية (al-Aqwal al-Dhahabiyya, The Golden Sayings) that disputations over prophethood and the ritual duties of the Law took place between Abu Bakr al-Razi and Abu Hatim in Rayy in the days of Mardawij and in his presence, and that Abu Hatim was able to humble Abu Bakr and to cast out his views, in pages of أعلام النبوة (A'lam al-Nubuwwa) that overflow with force and wonder, closing off to the arrogant and the obstinate every path of escape and flight.

His principal works are these:

1. الزينة (al-Zina), on the Arabic letters, their derivations, the reasons for their forms, and the meanings of words. It is a weighty book on the authentic and the borrowed Arabic vocabulary in which the Qur'an spoke and to which the Muslims gave currency, and from which the Islamic technical terms took their shape. It runs to twelve hundred pages, and he dedicated it to the Fatimid imam who succeeded Muhammad al-Mahdi Billah (Ubayd Allah).
2. أعلام النبوة (A'lam al-Nubuwwa).
3. الاصلاح (al-Islah).
4. الجامع (al-Jami'), and others besides.

Abu Ya'qub Ishaq al-Sijistani, also called al-Sijzi, stands in the front rank of those scholars who struggled and laboured and gave themselves wholly to founding a cosmic philosophy upon firm doctrinal and intellectual foundations, upon solid scholarly bases and fixed pillars that no crisis could shake. Indeed he was among those who sacrificed all they possessed to spread that philosophy and carry it into other lands, until in his last years the conservative public accused him of unbelief and heresy, and he was at last killed for the sake of his faith. He played a great part in the field of philosophy and rendered the noblest intellectual service in the third century of the Hijra, and his mark appears in his disciple Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani, Hujjat al-Iraqayn, who walked his road, called men to his teaching, and drank from the flow of his springs. Once we know that al-Kirmani studied philosophy in al-Sijistani's school of thought, we may set al-Sijistani in the first rank among the renowned Muslim scholars, thinkers, and philosophers of the world.

He lived alongside the esoteric Ismaili da'wa in the Age of Manifestation, the season when the Fatimid state flourished and stood forth as an Islamic state of civilizational, scholarly, social, and political stature. Yet he lived among a people whose madhhab differed from his own, and so he was forced to take التقية (taqiyya) for a screen and to move and to preach with the utmost caution. For this reason his life has reached us wrapped in a certain ambiguity, and we are not to be blamed if we cannot know his biography in full or lay hold of everything concerning it. The same obscurity hangs over the other great men of the Ismaili da'wa, the hujjas and the preachers of the islands in Syria, Iran, Yemen, and elsewhere, for their lives are shrouded in mystery and the books in which they wrote their own biographies have been lost, only a little remaining.

He is traced to Sijistan, a province in the south of Khurasan, and to a Persian family said to descend from the Persian hero Rustam, though some say he was of Iraqi origin, his grandfather having come from Kufa to settle in Sijistan. Certain researchers who studied his philosophy claim that he died in 331 AH, but this does not accord with the historical facts, for it is known that al-Sijistani was the teacher of al-Kirmani, and al-Kirmani remained alive until 411 AH. At what time, then, would al-Kirmani have taken from him the philosophical sciences of the da'wa? There is a plain statement in al-Sijistani's own book الافتخار (al-Iftikhar) that he composed it in 360 AH, and الافتخار (al-Iftikhar) is in turn mentioned in al-Kirmani's الرياض (al-Riyad). He was, then, a preacher in the province of Bukhara during the caliphate of the imam al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah al-Fatimi, which means he was a contemporary of the great preacher Ja'far ibn Mansur al-Yamani, and of the learned jurist the qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad ibn Hayyun al-Maghribi al-Tamimi, chief judge of the Fatimid state, and of other great authors and scholars of the da'wa in that golden age of manifest learning.

Al-Sijistani left behind him a rich body of scholarly writing that ranks among the deepest ever set down in philosophy, more than thirty titles in number. The most famous of them are:

1. إثبات النبوات (Ithbat al-Nubuwwat), Proofs of Prophecy.
2. كشف المحجوب (Kashf al-Mahjub), Unveiling the Veiled.
3. تحفة المستجيبين (Tuhfat al-Mustajibin), Masterpiece of the Respondents.
4. الينابيع (al-Yanabi'), which he divided into forty springs, and which has come to be counted a work whose depth is rarely surpassed. He seems to have composed it for a particular class of readers, the preachers and the holders of the highest ranks in the da'wa, men whose philosophical study had reached its summit; and he divided it into forty springs because he wished each spring to interpret one of the forty limits that make up the supreme council of the da'wa.

Among his other books, named by the great traveller and Persian poet Nasir-i Khusraw in his زاد المسافرين (Zad al-Musafirin, Provisions for Travellers), are:

1. The Foundations of the Da'wa.
2. The Interpretation of the Laws.
3. The Iris of Blessings, also called The Iris of Survival.
4. The Brilliant Message.
5. كتاب الافتخار (Kitab al-Iftikhar).
6. The Scales, which is divided into nineteen scales.
7. The Ladder of Salvation.
8. النصرة (al-Nusra), The Victory.
9. The Hooks.
10. Amusements of Sorrows.
11. Secrets of the Return.
12. Sermons on Ethics.
13. The Stranger on the Meaning of the Elixir.
14. Sociable of Hearts.
15. Composition of Spirits.
16. Security from Confusion.
17. Closets of Evidence.
18. The Proof, and others besides.

In what follows I summarize al-Sijistani's views on the divine as he expressed them across most of his writings.

He holds that the Creator of all creators is Ancient, Exalted, and Primordial, prior in the order of being, and that the world of beings and created things is originated in time, for were it not originated, something would have to precede it that had brought it into being. Even were the world supposed old, the Creator is prior to it, since it is impossible that might should attach to what is co-eternal with Him, or that His existence should be ascribed to nothingness or to necessity. He transcends every attribute one might fasten upon Him. He is not attained by sense, does not fall under the eye, is not perceived by sight, and is not seen; He is not described by the senses, nor grasped by analogy, nor likened to His creatures; He is exalted above rival, equal, and every like. Exalted is He above the bewildered guesses of the imagination concerning His might, above the understanding that falls short of the nature of His kingdom, a kingdom whose greatness is beheld yet never grasped. He has no likeness and no counterpart, no equal and no opposite, for an opposite would be a rival. He points to His identity through what He has created, and to His names through His prophets. The intellect has no foothold and no analogy by which to reach His heights, for any analogy that sets the creature beside its Creator is impossible. He is the cause of every existent, the Creator of creators and the Inventor of inventors, the cause of all beings, the Lord of all things, their Originator, their Perfecter, and the One who brings them to the best of their states. No thought bounds Him and no estimation encompasses Him. He has no names, for names are coined for what exists among His creation, and no attributes, for attributes belong to essences; and the letters of language cannot reach the pronunciation of His name, since the letters themselves are among the things He has invented. All the names He created He made names for the things He created. He is Ancient, before pre-eternity, the Owner of the source of primacy in the order of being.

From Him the first limit emerged and the first One overflowed, while He remains the Creator of creators, the Originator, the One who brings forth from nothing, the Perpetual whose being subsists in His own singularity and self-sufficiency. The first act of bringing-into-being was joined to the first number, which He made the origin of numbers, just as He made the Intellect the origin of created things and the first speaker of the world of religion. Beyond all this, He acquires none of the attributes. He is not a body nor in a body, He does not know Himself as a knower knows, He is not perceived by any sense, He is in no image and no form and no substance, and there is nothing in any tongue by which He may be expressed. He exists, yet it is not right to say that He exists after the manner of those existents that have come to be from Him, nor is His existence inferred from the existence of other things as an effect is inferred from its cause. He does not come to be without a cause, and He exists only as He must, while contingent beings lean one upon another in their existence, some resting on others, and some of them are not fixed in being and do not endure.

Al-Sijistani then passes to the existents in their order and sequence, and says:

At the beginning of creation the Creator brought nothing into being except the Intellect (العقل), and within its essence He bounded the images of all created things. The Intellect is given the name Pen (القلم), because by the pen the inscriptions of creation are set down from the first. It is called the Throne (العرش), that is, the seat of the One enthroned upon it, whose Majesty knows itself exalted above being settled upon it. It is called the First, meaning that the primacy from which creatures emerged, all that exists and all that is imprinted upon it, precedes the rest of the limits in receiving the effects of wisdom, because of its nearness to that source and its union with it, which is knowledge and command taken in one sense. Hence it is allowed to say that the act of the Intellect precedes its power, a virtue found in nothing but it, for in all the limits below it the powers precede the acts; this virtue belongs to the Intellect alone, that by it the Intellect may be whole and complete. It is called the Decree (القضاء), because by the Intellect the soul resolves to perceive what it learns and to win what it seeks, and because the Intellect is the decree of God, mighty and exalted, among His creation. It is called al-hayuli (الهيولى), prime matter, meaning that by the Intellect there is a strength to receive the images that break forth. And it is called the Sun (الشمس), meaning that by the Intellect the realities are seen.

Then comes the Soul (النفس), the second creation, which breaks forth from the first. It is named Soul (nafs) because it breathes (yatanaffas) without ceasing to draw benefit, so that the succession of its breathing is the very texture of creation. The Soul is called the Tablet (اللوح), meaning that what is cleft from the Intellect of the lights of wisdom is inscribed upon the Soul, and from the Soul it passes on in the flow that issues from it, in measure of its purity and fineness. It is called the King (الملك), meaning that the Soul is the property of the Intellect and its handmaid, for through the Soul the virtue of the Intellect appears, as through the vassal the virtue of the king appears. It is called the Follower (التالي), meaning that what recites after the Intellect in receiving the effects of the Word is the Soul, and that the Soul, by its power, recites the Intellect through its act. It is called Destiny (القدر), meaning that what unites with the Soul of the benefits of the Intellect is encompassed by measure and determination. And it is called the Image (الصورة), meaning that the Soul is conceived from the very essence of the Intellect and its radiance, and that whenever it desires to overtake the Intellect and take its station, its light is effaced, as the moon draws its light from the sun and, when the two meet in one place, the moon's light is effaced. Of the Intellect and the Soul together it is said, in a single word, the Two Principles (الاصلان).

These are some of al-Sijistani's philosophical views on the divine, set out in brief and joined to what I have been able to recover of his life. Perhaps they give the surest proof of his command of the sciences and of his gift for laying out their texts in beautiful order and exquisite arrangement.

There remains Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani. He was an extraordinary scholarly presence whose life history is wrapped in obscurity, a great philosopher who lived in a bright age of learning, a noble preacher who inscribed on the pages of thought the soundest inquiries and the deepest lines, and who left to the generations after him a number of works of which the least that can be said is that they are a precious treasure and an enduring legacy. The philosophers of the Islamic world reckon him the greatest scholar the Ismaili school ever produced.
