It is worth remarking, too, upon the scholarly ideas these works hold; a philosophical body of thought such as this deserves to be studied, examined, and scrutinized deeply. Yet by ill fortune the two books *al-Mahsul* and *al-Nusra* have been lost, while *al-Islah* of al-Razi and *al-Riyad* of al-Kirmani are preserved still.

Al-Kirmani's book *al-Riyad* is one of the important works that give voice to the philosophical theories of Ismailism in the fourth century of the Hijra. I have obtained two copies of it: the first from India, and the second from Tehran, both sent to me by the orientalist W. Ivanow on behalf of the Ismaili Society in India, which asked me, as one of its working members, to edit the book, to explain it, and to write its footnotes and commentary. Before I enter into the details and review the opinions, sayings, arguments, and statements the book contains, I must offer a brief glance at the lives of the three philosophers who took part in the discussion, namely al-Razi, al-Sijistani, and al-Kirmani.

The first of the three is al-Razi. He is Abu Hatim Ahmad ibn Hamdan al-Laythi al-Warsanani al-Razi, whom the Ismailis call, in honor, our master Abu Hatim. He was a preacher of Ismailism in the land of Rayy, then moved to Baghdad and made it his residence. The exact date of his birth is not known. His work in the Ismaili da'wa fell during the imamate of Muhammad al-Mahdi Billah (Ubayd Allah), the founder of the Fatimid state in the Maghrib. He was distinguished by the sweetness of his speech and the eloquence for which he was famous. He won over al-Marwazi, the prince of Rayy, who had been a strict Sunni, and he labored to persuade the princes of those regions to enter Ismailism, until he became one of the standards of politics and religion, and one of the guides of the Islamic scholarly renaissance in the late third and early fourth centuries. He took his refuge in the theological and philosophical sciences. The orientalist Paul Kraus said of him: "He was one of the great preachers of the Ismailis and played an important role in political affairs in Tabaristan and Daylam, and especially in Rayy and Isfahan, until the chief men of the state responded to him, such as Asfar ibn Shirawayh, Mardawij the commander, and others." In truth, Abu Hatim al-Razi was one of the standards of the scholarly renaissance not in Ismailism alone but in the Islamic world at large. He helped to spread philosophical teachings throughout the eastern lands, and especially within the wider circle of Islamic culture. For all of this he was not spared the persecution of his enemies in Daylam, and he was forced into hiding in the last years of his life. He died in the year 322 of the Hijra, after the Fatimid al-Qa'im had taken charge of the affairs of the Ismaili imamate in the Maghrib, having lived, as some historians say, sixty-two years.

He was a contemporary of Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi, the famous physician and holder of well-known philosophical views, in which he broke with many of Aristotle's natural and metaphysical theories, denied any reconciliation of philosophy and religion, and held that philosophy is the only road to the reform of the individual and of society. Between the two men called al-Razi there were violent and repeated debates, attended by a number of scholars and political leaders. Abu Hatim set these debates down in his book *A'lam al-Nubuwwa*, the Signs of Prophethood; and although he does not name the man whose views he criticizes, calling him only the atheist, there is decisive evidence that this atheist was none other than Abu Bakr al-Razi. The great philosopher al-Kirmani, Hujjat al-Iraqayn, notes in his book *al-Aqwal al-Dhahabiyya*, the Golden Sayings, that debates over prophethood and the ordinances of the Law took place between Abu Bakr al-Razi and Abu Hatim in the region of Rayy, in the days of Mardawij and in his presence, and that Abu Hatim was able to humble Abu Bakr al-Razi and to cast out his views in *A'lam al-Nubuwwa*, in pages that overflow with force and wonder, closing off against the arrogant and the obstinate every path of escape and flight.

His most important work is the book *al-Zina*, on the Arabic letters, their derivations, the reasons for their forms, and the meanings of words. It is a great book on the authentic and the borrowed Arabic vocabulary in which the Qur'an was articulated and to which the Muslims gave currency, and out of which Islamic terminology took shape. It runs to twelve hundred pages, and he dedicated it to the Fatimid imam who succeeded Muhammad al-Mahdi Billah (Ubayd Allah). To him belong also the books *A'lam al-Nubuwwa*, *al-Islah*, *al-Jami'*, and others.

The second of the three is al-Sijistani. Abu Ya'qub Ishaq al-Sijistani, also called al-Sijzi, stands at the forefront of those scholars who struggled, labored, and gave themselves to establishing cosmic philosophical principles upon doctrinal intellectual foundations, firm scholarly bases, and fixed pillars that do not tremble whatever crises befall them. Indeed he is among those who sacrificed all they possessed in order to spread these principles and carry them into other lands, until, late in his life, he was accused of unbelief and heresy by the conservative public, and was at last killed for the sake of his faith. He played an important role in the field of philosophy and rendered the noblest intellectual services in the third century of the Hijra. His intellectual mark appeared in his disciple Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani, Hujjat al-Iraqayn, who walked his road, called to his teachings, and drew from the flow of his springs. Once we know that al-Kirmani studied philosophy in al-Sijistani's school of thought, we may set al-Sijistani in the first rank among the famous scholars, thinkers, and philosophers of the Muslim world.

He lived alongside the esoteric Ismaili da'wa in the age of manifestation, the time when the Fatimid state flourished and stood forth as an Islamic state possessed of civilizational, scholarly, social, and political stature. Yet he lived in a country whose people followed a school different from his own, and so he was compelled to take dissimulation, taqiyya, as a screen, and to be most careful in his movements and his preaching. For this reason his life has come down to us with a certain obscurity, and we are perhaps not to be blamed if we cannot arrive at a full knowledge of his biography or lay hold of everything concerning it. Little indeed has reached us about the great preacher al-Nasafi, al-Nakhshabi, the author of *al-Mahsul*, beyond what authors have recorded of his efforts and of his contact with Nasr ibn Ahmad the Samanid in Transoxiana, until that Samanid embraced the Ismaili da'wa. We know little, too, about Abu Hatim al-Razi, the linguist and author of the oldest work in the science of the Arabic language, the book *al-Zina*; and about Hujjat al-Iraqayn Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani, despite the survival of most of his writings, and even though al-Mu'ayyad fi'l-Din Hibatallah al-Shirazi, the friend and disputant of Abu al-Ala al-Ma'arri, wrote his life in his own hand. So it is with the obscurity that surrounds the lives of the senior men of the Ismaili da'wa, the hujjas and the preachers of the islands in Syria, Iran, Yemen, and elsewhere: their lives are shrouded in the deepest obscurity, and the books in which they recorded their own biographies have been lost, with only a little of them remaining.

He is traced to Sijistan, a province in the south of Khurasan, and to a Persian family said to be the house of the Persian champion Rustam, though there are those who say he was of Iraqi origin, his grandfather having come from Kufa to settle in Sijistan. Some researchers who have studied his philosophy claim that he died in 331 of the Hijra, but this does not agree with the historical reality, for it is known that al-Sijistani was the teacher of al-Kirmani, and al-Kirmani remained alive until the year 411. At what time, then, did al-Kirmani take from him the philosophical sciences of the da'wa? There is a plain statement in al-Sijistani's own book *al-Iftikhar* that he composed it in the year 360, and *al-Iftikhar* is in turn mentioned in al-Kirmani's *al-Riyad*. He was, then, a preacher in the province of Bukhara during the caliphate of the imam al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah al-Fatimi, which means that he was a contemporary of the great preacher Ja'far ibn Mansur al-Yamani, and of the learned jurist, the qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad ibn Hayyun al-Maghribi al-Tamimi, the chief judge of the Fatimid state, and of other great authors and scholars of the da'wa in that golden age of manifest learning. Nothing shows the scholarly worth of al-Sijistani better than the books and writings he left behind him.

He left, as I have said, a rich body of scholarly writing that is counted among the deepest ever written in philosophy, more than thirty titles in number. Among the most famous of his books are these four:

1. *Ithbat al-Nubuwwat*, the Proofs of Prophecy.
2. *Kashf al-Mahjub*, the Unveiling of the Veiled.
3. *Tuhfat al-Mustajibin*, the Masterpiece of the Respondents.
4. *al-Yanabi'*, which he divided into forty springs, and which has come to be reckoned a work whose depth is rarely surpassed.

He appears to have composed this last for a particular class of readers, the preachers and those who hold the highest ranks in the da'wa, men whose philosophical study had reached its summit; and the reason he divided it into forty springs goes back to his wish that each spring should interpret one of the forty limits who make up the supreme council of the da'wa.

Among his other books, which the great traveler and famous Persian poet Nasir-i Khusraw records in his *Zad al-Musafirin*, the Provisions for Travelers, are the Foundations of the Da'wa; the Interpretation of the Laws; the Iris of Blessings, also called the Iris of Survival; the Brilliant Message; the *Kitab al-Iftikhar*; the Scales, divided into nineteen scales; the Ladder of Salvation; al-Nusra, the Victory; the Hooks; the Amusements of Sorrows; the Secrets of the Return; the Sermons on Ethics; the Stranger on the Meaning of the Elixir; the Sociable of Hearts; the Composition of Spirits; the Security from Confusion; the Closets of Evidence; the Proof; and others.

In the lines that follow I set out, in brief, al-Sijistani's views on the divine, as he expressed them across most of his writings. He holds that the Originator of all originated things is an Ancient, Exalted, and Primordial Creator, prior in the order of being; and that the world of beings and originated things is contingent and brought into time, for were it not so, something must necessarily have preceded and originated it. Were the world ancient and before the Creator, it would be impossible for His might to attach to what is pre-eternal, or His being to nothingness, and it would require some maker to bring it forth. He is exalted beyond the grasp of the attributes: He is not attained by sense, does not fall under a gaze, is not perceived by sight, is not marked by any genus, does not occur to conjecture, and is not seen by the eyes. He is not described by the senses, nor grasped by analogy, nor likened to men; He is kept pure of any opposing contrary, any matching equal, or the likeness of anything. Exalted is He above the likeness of the bounded. The imaginations are bewildered in describing His might, the understandings fall short of the character of His kingdom, and the eyes are dulled before the grasp of His greatness. He has no like and no likeness; He is without equal and without opposite, for an opposite is only opposed by a contrary. He has pointed to His identity by His creation and His traces, and to His names by His prophets, so the intellect has no field and no likening by which to reach His heights, since to liken the Originator to what He originates is impossible. He is the cause of every existent, for He is the Originator of originators and the Deviser of devices, the cause of the being of all beings, the Lord of everything and its Creator, its Perfecter, and the One who carries it to the best of states. He is too high to be bounded by any thought or encompassed by any measure. He has no names, for names were laid down for the things He brought into being, and He has no attributes, for the attributes belong to essences; and the letters of language cannot arrive at the pronouncing of His name, nor can any of them be applied to Him, since they are all among the things He devised, and all the names He originated He made names for the things He originated. He is Ancient, before the pre-eternal, the Owner of the source of firstness in order; for from Him the first limit burst forth and the first existent overflowed, while He remains the Originator of originators, the Cause of causes, the Fashioner of creatures, and the Enduring who exists by His singleness and His absoluteness, the Owner of the first act of bringing into being, the first number, which He made the origin of numbers, just as He made the Intellect the origin of existents and the Speaker the origin of the world of religion. To all of this it is added that He acquires none of the attributes, that He is not a body and is not in a body, that no reasoner reasons His essence and no sense perceives Him, that He is neither image nor matter nor substance, and that there is nothing in the languages by which He may be expressed. He exists, but it is not right to say that He exists in the manner of those existents that have come to be from Him, nor is His existence inferred from the existence of other existents in the way an effect is inferred from its cause. He does not come to be without a cause, and He exists only as He must exist; while contingent beings depend one upon another in their being, some resting upon others, and some of them are not fixed in existence and do not endure thereafter.

Al-Sijistani then moves on to the existents in their order and sequence, saying:

The Originator brought nothing into being at the beginning of creation except the Intellect, in whose essence are bounded the images of all created things. The name Pen is given to the Intellect, because by the pen the inscriptions of creation appear from the beginning. It is also called the Throne, that is, the seat of the One who sits upon it, whose majesty is known by His being seated to be exalted above whoever is beneath Him. The Intellect is also called the First, meaning that the firstness from which the creatures appeared, that is, all that exists and all that is imprinted upon it, is earlier than the rest of the limits in accepting the traces of wisdom, because of its nearness to that source and its union with it, which is knowledge and command taken in one sense. It is therefore permissible to say that the act of the Intellect preceded its power; and this virtue is found in no existent other than it, for in all the limits below it the powers precede the acts, while this virtue belongs to the Intellect in particular, so that by it the Intellect may be whole and complete. The Intellect is also called the Pronouncement, in that by the Intellect the soul resolves upon perceiving what is known and gaining what is sought; and it is permissible to say that the Intellect is the decree of God, mighty and exalted, among His creation. The Intellect is likewise called the prime matter, *al-hayuli*, meaning that by the Intellect there is the subsistence of the forms that gush forth; and it is called the Sun, meaning that by the Intellect the realities are seen.

Then the Soul, which is the second creation gushing forth from the first, is named a soul because it always breathes so as to draw benefit, that the frequency of its breathing may be the subsistence of creation. The Soul is called the Tablet, meaning that what is cleft off from the Intellect of the lights of wisdom is inscribed in the Soul, and from the Soul it is joined to what streams forth from it, in the measure of its purity and refinement. The Soul is called the King, meaning that the Soul is the possession of the Intellect and its handmaid, for by the Soul the virtue of the Intellect appears, just as by the servant the virtue of the sovereign appears. The Soul is called the Follower, meaning that what follows the Intellect in receiving the traces of the Word is the Soul; and it is permissible to say that the Soul by its power follows the Intellect by its act. The Soul is called Destiny, meaning that what unites with the Soul of the benefits of the Intellect is encompassed by measuring and determining. The Soul is called the Image, and the meaning is that the Soul was conceived from the essence of the Intellect and its radiance, and that whenever it aims to join the Intellect and take its station, its light is effaced, just as the moon draws its light from the light of the sun, and when they meet in one station its light is effaced. Of the Intellect and the Soul together it is said, in a single word, the Two Principles.

These are some of al-Sijistani's philosophical views on the divine, which I have presented in brief and joined to what I have been able to recover of his biography. They perhaps offer compelling proof of his command of the sciences and of his deep-rooted skill in setting down texts in attractive order and exquisite arrangement, which is perhaps what carried him to this depth.
