# The Two Reformers

Al-Kirmani's book *al-Riyad* is, to my mind, one of the important works that give voice to the philosophical theories of Ismailism in the fourth century AH. I have set myself to study it closely, to explain it, and to furnish it with the notes and commentary it deserves. Yet before I enter into its opinions, sayings, arguments, and statements, I must first offer a brief sketch of the lives of the three philosophers who took part in the discussion it records, namely al-Razi, al-Sijistani, and al-Kirmani.

Let me begin with al-Razi. He is Abu Hatim Ahmad ibn Hamdan al-Laythi al-Warsanani al-Razi, whom Ismaili circles honor with the name "our master Abu Hatim." He was a preacher of Ismailism in the land of Rayy, then moved to Baghdad and made it his residence. The date of his birth is not known exactly. His work in the Ismaili da'wa fell during the imamate of Muhammad al-Mahdi Billah (Ubayd Allah), the founder of the Fatimid state in the Maghrib. He was distinguished by the sweetness of his speech and by the eloquence for which he was famous. He drew to himself al-Marwazi, the prince of Rayy, who had been a strict Sunni, and he persuaded the princes of those regions to enter into Ismailism, until he became one of the standards of politics and religion and one of the guides of the Islamic scientific renaissance in the late third and early fourth centuries AH, always taking refuge in the theological and philosophical sciences. Of him the orientalist Paul Kraus said:

> "He was a leading preacher of the Ismailis and played an important role in political affairs in Tabaristan and Daylam, especially in Rayy and Isfahan, until the chief statesmen responded to him, such as Asfar ibn Shirawayh, Mardawij the commander, and others."

In truth, Abu Hatim al-Razi was a standard of the scientific renaissance not only within Ismailism but in the wider Islamic world, and he contributed to the spread of philosophical teaching throughout the eastern lands and especially within the general body of Islamic culture. For all this, he was not spared the persecution of his enemies in Daylam, and was driven into hiding in the last years of his life. He died in 322 AH, after the Fatimid al-Qa'im had taken charge of the Ismaili imamate in the Maghrib, at the age of sixty-two, as some historians report.

He was a contemporary of Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi, the famous physician and holder of well-known philosophical views, in which he broke with many of Aristotle's theories, natural and metaphysical, denying any reconciliation of philosophy and religion and holding that philosophy is the only road to the reform of the individual and of society. Between the two men of Rayy there took place violent and repeated debates, attended by some scholars and political leaders. Abu Hatim recorded these debates in his book *A'lam al-Nubuwwa*, and though he does not name the man he is refuting, calling him only "the atheist," there is conclusive evidence that this atheist was none other than Abu Bakr al-Razi. Al-Kirmani himself has noted that discussions about prophethood and the legal rituals took place between Abu Bakr al-Razi and Abu Hatim in Rayy in the days of Mardawij and in his presence, and that Abu Hatim was able to humble Abu Bakr and to cast down his views in *A'lam al-Nubuwwa*, in pages that overflow with force and marvel, closing off against the arrogant and the stubborn every avenue of evasion and flight.

The most important of Abu Hatim's works are:

1. *al-Zina*, on the Arabic letters, their derivations, the reasons for their forms, and the meanings of words. It is a great book on the authentic and the borrowed Arabic vocabulary in which the Qur'an was articulated and which the Muslims gave legal force, and from which Islamic terminology took shape. It runs to twelve hundred pages, and he dedicated it to the Fatimid imam who succeeded Muhammad al-Mahdi Billah.
2. *A'lam al-Nubuwwa*.
3. *al-Islah*.
4. *al-Jami'*, and others.

I turn now to al-Sijistani. Abu Ya'qub Ishaq al-Sijistani, or al-Sijzi, stands at the forefront of those scholars who strove and labored and gave themselves wholly to the founding of cosmic philosophical principles resting upon doctrinal intellectual foundations, upon solid scientific bases, and upon fixed pillars that do not tremble whatever crisis may befall them. Indeed he was among those who sacrificed all they possessed to spread these teachings and to carry them into other lands, until late in his life he was charged with unbelief and atheism by the conservative multitude, and was at last killed for the sake of his faith. He played an important part in the field of philosophy and rendered the noblest of intellectual services in the third century of the Hijra, and his intellectual mark appeared in his disciple Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani, who walked his path, called to his teachings, and drank from the abundance of his springs. When we recognize that al-Kirmani studied philosophy in al-Sijistani's school of thought, we may set al-Sijistani in the first rank among the famous Muslim scholars, thinkers, and philosophers of the world.

He lived alongside the esoteric Ismaili da'wa in the Age of Manifestation, in the flourishing of the Fatimid state and its emergence as an Islamic state of civilizational, scientific, social, and political stature. Yet because he lived in a land whose people held to a madhhab different from his own, he was compelled to take taqiyya (تقية) as a cover and to be most cautious in his movements and his preaching. For this reason his life has come down to us wrapped in a certain ambiguity, and we may not be blamed if we cannot know his biography fully. He is traced to Sijistan, a province in the south of Khurasan, and to a Persian family said to descend from the Persian hero Rustam, though some say his people were of Iraqi origin and that his grandfather came from Kufa and settled in Sijistan. Certain researchers who studied his philosophy claim that he died in 331 AH, but this does not accord with the historical facts, for it is known that al-Sijistani was the teacher of al-Kirmani, and al-Kirmani remained alive until the year 411 AH. At what time, then, would al-Kirmani have taken from him the philosophical sciences of the da'wa? There is an explicit statement in al-Sijistani's own *al-Iftikhar* that he composed that book in 360 AH, and *al-Iftikhar* is in turn mentioned in al-Kirmani's *al-Riyad*. He was thus a preacher in the province of Bukhara during the caliphate of the imam al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah al-Fatimi, which means that he was a contemporary of the great preacher Ja'far ibn Mansur al-Yamani and of the learned jurist the qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad ibn Hayyun al-Maghribi al-Tamimi, chief judge of the Fatimid state, and of other great authors and scholars of the da'wa in that golden age of manifest knowledge.

Al-Sijistani left behind a prolific body of scholarly writing that ranks among the deepest ever composed in philosophy, more than thirty titles in number. Among the most famous are:

1. *Ithbat al-Nubuwwat*, on the proofs of prophecy.
2. *Kashf al-Mahjub*, the unveiling of the veiled.
3. *Tuhfat al-Mustajibin*, the masterpiece of the respondents.
4. *al-Yanabi'*, which he divided into forty springs, a work whose depth is rarely surpassed. He appears to have composed it for a particular audience, the preachers and those who occupy the highest ranks of the da'wa, men whose philosophical study had reached its climax, and his reason for dividing it into forty springs was his wish that each spring should interpret one of the forty limits who compose the council of the highest da'wa.

Among his other books, which the great traveler and Persian poet Nasir-i Khusraw mentions in his *Zad al-Musafirin*, are:

1. *The Foundations of the Da'wa*.
2. *The Interpretation of the Laws*.
3. *The Iris of Blessings*, also called *The Iris of Survival*.
4. *The Brilliant Message*.
5. *Kitab al-Iftikhar*.
6. *The Scales*, divided into nineteen scales.
7. *The Ladder of Salvation*.
8. *The Victory*.
9. *The Hooks*.
10. *Amusements of Sorrows*.
11. *Secrets of the Return*.
12. *Sermons on Ethics*.
13. *The Stranger on the Meaning of the Elixir*.
14. *Sociable of Hearts*.
15. *Composition of Spirits*.
16. *Security from Confusion*.
17. *Closets of Evidence*.
18. *The Proof*, and others.

In what follows I summarize al-Sijistani's views on the divine, as he expressed them across most of his writings. He holds that the Creator of all creators is an Ancient, Exalted, and Primordial Creator, prior in the order of being, and that the world of beings and creatures is contingent and originated in time, since otherwise something would have to precede it and to have brought it into being. The Creator is prior to the world, for it is impossible to attribute might to what is co-eternal with Him, or to ascribe His existence to nothingness or to necessity. He transcends every attribute one might fix to Him. He is not attained by sense, does not fall under the eyes, is not perceived by sight, is not subject to conjecture, and is not seen. He is not described by the senses, nor grasped by analogy, nor likened to creatures, and He is exalted above any rival, any equal, or anything of the kind. Exalted is He above the bewildered guesses of the imagination concerning His might, and above the understanding that falls short of the character of His kingdom, a kingdom whose greatness is glimpsed without being grasped. He has no likeness and no counterpart, no equal and no opposite, for the very notion of opposition is against Him. He has pointed to His identity through what He has created and to His names through His prophets, and the intellect has no foothold and no analogy by which to reach His heights, since any analogy that sets the creature beside its Creator is impossible. He is the cause of every existent, for He is the Creator of creators and the Inventor of inventors, the cause of all beings, the Lord of all things, their Originator, their Perfecter, and the One who brings them to the best of their states. No thought bounds Him and no estimation encompasses Him. He has no names, for names are coined for what exists in His creation and not for attributes, and attributes belong to essences, and the very letters of language cannot reach the pronunciation of His name, since those letters are themselves among His inventions. All the names He created He made names for His creatures, while He Himself is the Ancient, the Pre-eternal Source, the Owner of the source of primacy in the order of being.

For from Him the first limit emerged and the first One overflowed, while He remains the Creator of creators, the Originator who reveals, the One who brings forth from nothing, and the Perpetual whose being subsists in His own individuality and steadfastness. The first act of bringing-into-being was joined to the first number, which He made the origin of numbers, just as He made the Intellect the origin of created things and the first articulator of the world of religion. Beyond all this, He acquires none of the attributes. He is not a body, nor in a body. He is not rational of Himself, nor perceived by the senses. He is not in any image, nor in any form, nor in any substance, and there is nothing in any language by which He may be expressed. He exists, yet it is not correct to say that He exists after the manner of those existents that have come into being from Him, nor is His existence inferred from the existence of other existents as an effect is inferred from its cause. He does not come to be without a cause, and He exists only as He must exist, while contingent beings depend upon one another for their existence, some resting upon others, and some of them are not fixed in being and do not endure.

Al-Sijistani then moves to the existents in their order and sequence, and says that the Creator brought nothing into being at the beginning of creation except the Intellect, in whose essence are bounded the images of all created things. The name "Pen" is given to the Intellect, because by the pen the inscriptions of creation appear from the very beginning. It is called "the Throne," that is, the seat of the One who sits upon it, whose Majesty knows Him to be exalted above being settled upon it. It is called "the First" and the primordial from which creatures emerged, meaning all that exists, and what is imprinted upon it precedes the other limits in receiving the effects of wisdom, because of its nearness to that source and its union with it, and that source is knowledge and command taken in one sense. It is therefore permissible to say that the act of the Intellect precedes its power, and this virtue is found in nothing other than it, for all the limits below it have their powers preceding their acts. This virtue belongs to the Intellect alone, so that perfection may reside in it. It is called "the Pronouncement," because by the Intellect the soul resolves to perceive what it learns and to obtain what is required of it, and one may say that the Intellect is the decree of God among His creation. It is called *al-hayuli* (الهيولى), prime matter, meaning that the Intellect has the strength to receive the images reflected upon it, and it is called "the Sun," meaning that by the Intellect the realities are perceived.

Then comes the Soul, which is the second creation, brought forth from the first. It is called "Soul," nafs (نفس), because it always breathes, yatanaffas (يتنفس), in order to draw benefit, and the constancy of that breathing is the very texture of creation. The Soul is called "the Tablet," meaning that what the Intellect sends down of the lights of wisdom is inscribed upon the Soul, and from the Soul flows what it gives forth, in the measure of its purity and refinement. It is called "the King," meaning that the Soul is the possession of the Intellect and its content, for in the Soul the virtue of the Intellect appears, just as the virtue of the king appears through his kingdom. It is called "the Follower," meaning that the one who follows the Intellect in receiving the effects of the Word is the Soul, and one may say that the Soul, by its power, recites the Intellect through its act. It is called "Destiny," meaning that what unites with the Soul among the benefits of the Intellect is encompassed by estimation and determination. It is called "the Image," meaning that the Soul is conceived from the very essence of the Intellect and its light, and that whenever it seeks to catch up with the Intellect and take its station, its own light is effaced, just as the moon draws its light from the sun, and when the two meet in one place the moon's light is effaced. And of the Intellect and the Soul together it is said in one word: "the Two Principles."

These are some of al-Sijistani's philosophical views on the divine, set out in brief and joined to whatever I have been able to recover of his life. Perhaps the surest proof of the depth to which they carried him is his mastery of the sciences and his gift for laying out their texts in attractive order and exquisite arrangement.

There remains Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani. He was an extraordinary man of learning whose life is shrouded in obscurity, a great philosopher who lived in a radiant age of knowledge, an eminent preacher who traced upon the pages of thought the soundest inquiries and the deepest lines, and who left to the generations a number of works of which the least that can be said is that they are a precious treasure and an enduring inheritance. The philosophers of the Islamic world count him the greatest scholar the Ismaili school produced.
