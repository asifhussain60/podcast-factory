# The Age of Open Argument

The Islamic world at the close of the third century of the Muhammadan Hijra bore the imprint of its sciences, its philosophy, and its letters. New currents, tangled doctrinal disagreements, and an unresting theological debate governed its cultural sphere and its learned society, coming to rest with no single party and settling in no single place. How useful it is, then, for the researcher who wishes to study the origins of these differences in depth, to weigh the opinions of the writers and scholars who lived in that distant age, who left behind them scientific traces and fresh modes of expression fitted to their time and to their grasp of philosophical questions, and to measure the reach of their effort in interpreting texts and expounding verses, so that he may then form a reasonable view that carries him toward reality and truth.

The disputes I have in mind did not, for the most part, stay within the bounds of the spoken discussions once raised in the salons and the centers of learning. They grew into long treatises that came out upon the pages of books and pamphlets. Among the groups that contributed a generous share to this field were the Ismailis. Yet it is very strange that I do not see this group finish its verbal argument with the other groups before it enters upon another argument of an altogether new kind, a debate among its own callers. In this there is clear proof of an intellectual freedom that prevailed in their lives, a release from the constraints that hold sway over conservative societies and draw a bound about them, obliging them not to break with the old inherited design nor to stray from it.

The first debate, or in more exact terms the first scholarly discussion, that opened new horizons for the Ismailis, that carried them into an atmosphere of wide scientific liberation, and that made them a subject of conjecture and report throughout the Islamic world, was the appearance of the book al-Mahsul (المحصول), The Harvest.

This book was put into circulation at the beginning of the fourth century of the Hijra, the tenth of the Christian era. It is attributed to the great Khurasani caller Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Nasafi:

> محمد بن احمد النسفي

to whom belonged the credit of turning the Samanid state in Transoxiana toward Ismailism, and who was put to death in the year 331 of the Hijra, 942 of the Christian era, as is stated in the book al-Farq bayn al-Firaq:

> الفرق بين الفرق

by its author Abd al-Qahir al-Baghdadi. It is very strange that the Proof of the Two Iraqs, its greatest exponent and its foremost philosopher, Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani:

> حميد الدين احمد الكرماني

who discussed the matter and went deep in his examination of it, made no mention of the author of al-Mahsul at all. His discourse was fixed instead upon Abu Hatim al-Razi and Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani:

> أبي حاتم الرازي ، وأبي يعقوب السجستاني

There must, then, be substantial reasons of a particular kind for this silence that justify it, and perhaps the book al-Mahsul is attributed to al-Nasafi in error.

However the matter stands, the favor that al-Mahsul won among the thinkers of the Ismailis and the ranks of their scholars at that time spurred Abu Hatim al-Razi, the chief caller in the northwest of Persia, to compose a book in which he discussed what had come in al-Mahsul, and he named his book al-Islah (الاصلاح), The Correction. It is plain that the opinions al-Razi set down provoked a reaction in another great caller named Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani, who held them to run counter to the truth and to first principles. This is what led him to write a book challenging al-Razi's views, which he named al-Nusra (النصرة), The Support. In it the author defended al-Mahsul and assailed Abu Hatim al-Razi without mercy. It appears that al-Nusra is among al-Sijistani's earliest works, as al-Kirmani himself hints in his book.

Not long afterward, al-Kirmani surveyed the whole subject in a full and searching review and composed his precious treatise al-Riyad (الرياض), a judgment between the two reformers, as he called them, meaning the authors of al-Islah and al-Nusra:

> الاصلاح والنصرة

With surpassing skill he appraised the opinions that had come in the three books and criticized them with a scholarly criticism, and he set them against the Law of the Guiding Mission:

> قانون الدعوة الهادية

that is, against the philosophical and doctrinal theories officially held by the Fatimid court in his day. In sum, I find that al-Kirmani always prefers the theories of al-Razi over those of al-Sijistani, and that he objects to the latter for their failure to conform to the truth and for their leaning toward deviation. Beyond this, he observes that al-Razi passed over some of the statements that had come in al-Mahsul, and so would bring them up himself, as I note in the tenth chapter of al-Riyad.

It is worth mentioning too that scientific ideas and a philosophical body of this kind deserve to be studied, compared, and scrutinized deeply. But by ill fortune the two books al-Mahsul and al-Nusra have been lost:

> المحصول والنصرة

As for the book al-Islah by al-Razi and al-Riyad by al-Kirmani, they are still preserved.
