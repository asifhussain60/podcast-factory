# The Age of Open Argument

The Islamic world at the close of the third century of the Muhammadan Hijra bore the imprint of its sciences, its philosophy, and its letters. New currents of thought, tangled doctrinal disagreements, and a debate that would not settle at any boundary or come to rest in any single place governed its cultural sphere and its learned society. I have found it useful, in tracing the origins of these differences, to weigh the opinions of the writers and scholars who lived in that distant age, who left behind them scholarly traces and fresh modes of expression suited to their time and to their grasp of the philosophical questions, and who exerted themselves in interpreting texts and expounding verses. Only by weighing them can one arrive at a reasonable view that carries him toward what is real and true.

These disputes did not, for the most part, remain within the oral discussions once raised in the salons and the centers of learning. They grew into long treatises that came forth upon the pages of books and circulated writings. Among the sects that contributed a large share to this field were the Ismailis. Yet it is a strange thing that I do not find this group bringing its verbal contention with other sects to a close before it enters upon a contention of a new kind altogether, a debate among its own callers. In this there is clear proof of the intellectual freedom that prevailed in their lives, a release from the constraints that dominate conservative societies and draw a boundary around them, binding them to shun all singularity and to keep clear of any departure from the inherited pattern.

The first debate, or in more exact language the first scholarly discussion, that opened new horizons for the Ismailis, carried them into an air of far-reaching scholarly liberation, and made them a subject of conjecture and report throughout the Islamic world, was the appearance of the book al-Mahsul, The Harvest.

This book was put into circulation at the beginning of the fourth century of the Hijra, the tenth of the Christian era. It is ascribed to the great preacher of Khurasan:

> محمد بن احمد النسفي

Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Nasafi, to whom is credited the turning of the Samanid state in Transoxiana toward Ismailism, and who was put to death in the year 331 of the Hijra, 942 of the Christian era, as is stated in the book:

> الفرق بين الفرق

al-Farq bayn al-Firaq, by its author Abd al-Qahir al-Baghdadi. It is very strange that the Proof of the Two Iraqs, its greatest exponent and its foremost philosopher:

> حميد الدين احمد الكرماني

Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani, who took up the subject and went deeply into its examination, made no mention of the author of al-Mahsul, but rather fixed his discussion upon:

> أبي حاتم الرازي ، وأبي يعقوب السجستاني

Abu Hatim al-Razi and Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani. There must, then, be fundamental reasons behind this silence that account for it, and it may be that the book al-Mahsul has been ascribed to al-Nasafi in error.

Whatever the case may be, the acclaim that al-Mahsul won among the thinkers of the Ismailis and the ranks of their scholars at that time moved Abu Hatim al-Razi, the chief preacher in the northwest of Persia, to compose a book in which he examined what had been set down in al-Mahsul. He named his book al-Islah, The Correction. It is plain that the opinions al-Razi advanced stirred a reaction in another great preacher named Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani, who held them to run counter to the truth and to first principles. This is what led him to compose a book contesting the views of al-Razi, which he named al-Nusra, The Vindication, in which its author took the side of al-Mahsul and assailed Abu Hatim al-Razi without mercy. It appears that al-Nusra is among the earliest of al-Sijistani's works, as al-Kirmani himself hints in his book.

Not long afterward, al-Kirmani surveyed the whole subject in a full and thorough review and composed his precious treatise, al-Riyad, as a judgment between the two reformers, as he named them, that is, the authors of:

> الاصلاح والنصرة

al-Islah and al-Nusra. With consummate skill he appraised the opinions set forth in the three books and subjected them to scholarly criticism, and he set them beside:

> قانون الدعوة الهادية

the Law of the Guiding Mission, or, in other words, beside the philosophical and doctrinal theories officially held by the Fatimid court in his day. In the main I find that al-Kirmani favors the theories of al-Razi over those of al-Sijistani at every turn, and faults the latter because they do not answer to the truth and incline toward deviation. Beyond this he remarks that al-Razi passed over certain of the statements that had appeared in al-Mahsul, and so he brings them forward himself, as is to be seen in the tenth chapter of the book al-Riyad.

It is also worth remarking that scholarly ideas and a philosophical body of this order deserve to be studied, set side by side, and scrutinized in depth. Yet by ill fortune the two books:

> المحصول والنصرة

al-Mahsul and al-Nusra have been lost. As for al-Islah by al-Razi and al-Riyad by al-Kirmani, they remain preserved to this day.
