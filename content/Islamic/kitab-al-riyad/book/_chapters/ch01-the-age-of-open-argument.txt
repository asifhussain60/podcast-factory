# The Age of Open Argument

When I turn my mind to the Islamic world at the close of the third century of the Muhammadan Hijra, I find it stamped through with the character of its sciences, its philosophy, and its letters. Its cultural sphere and its learned society were governed by new currents of thought, by branching disagreements of doctrine, and by a debate that came to rest at no limit and settled in no fixed place. How much it profits the one who would study the origins of these differences in depth to weigh the opinions of the writers and scholars who lived in that far era, who left behind them scientific traces and fresh modes of expression suited to their age and to their grasp of the philosophical questions, and who laboured to interpret the texts and to explain the verses. Only then can he form a sound view that carries him through to reality and to truth.

These disputes did not, for the most part, remain within the bounds of the spoken discussions once raised in the salons and the centres of learning. They grew into long treatises that stood out upon the pages of books and circulars. Among the sects that gave this field a large share was the community of the Ismailis. Yet it is a strange thing that I do not see this community bring its argument with the other sects to a close before it enters upon another argument of a wholly new kind, an argument among its own callers to the faith. In this there is a clear proof of the freedom of mind that governed their lives, a release from the constraints that rule over conservative societies and draw a boundary about them, binding them to shun all singularity and to keep to the old inherited design.

The first debate, or in a truer word the first scientific discussion, that opened new horizons for the Ismailis, that carried them into an air of wide scientific liberation and made them a subject of conjecture and report throughout the Islamic world, was the appearance of the book المحصول (al-Mahsul, The Harvest).

This book was set into circulation at the opening of the fourth century of the Hijra, the tenth of the Christian reckoning. It is ascribed to the great caller of Khurasan,

> محمد بن احمد النسفي
>
> (Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Nasafi)

to whom belongs the credit of turning the Samanid state in Transoxiana toward the Ismaili path, and who was put to death in the year 331 of the Hijra, in 942 of the Christian reckoning, as is set down in

> الفرق بين الفرق
>
> (al-Farq bayn al-Firaq, The Difference Between the Sects)

by its author Abd al-Qahir al-Baghdadi. And it is a very strange thing that I, whom they have named

> حميد الدين احمد الكرماني
>
> (Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani)

who discussed this matter and went deep into its examination, made no mention of the author of المحصول; rather my discourse rested upon

> أبي حاتم الرازي ، وأبي يعقوب السجستاني
>
> (Abu Hatim al-Razi and Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani)

There must then be substantial reasons of their own kind for this silence that justify it, and it may be that the book المحصول is ascribed to al-Nasafi in error.

However the matter stands, the approval that المحصول won among the thinkers of the Ismailis and the ranks of their scholars in that day stirred Abu Hatim al-Razi, the foremost caller in the northwest of Persia, to compose a book in which he discussed what had come in المحصول. He named his book الاصلاح (al-Islah, The Correction). It is plain that the opinions al-Razi advanced brought about a reaction in another great caller named Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani, who held them to run contrary to the truth and to first principles. This is what led him to compose a book in which he contended against al-Razi's views, and which he named النصرة (al-Nusra, The Vindication). In it the author took the side of المحصول and assailed Abu Hatim al-Razi without mercy.

Not long after, I reviewed the whole matter in a full review and composed my treasured treatise الرياض (al-Riyad, The Gardens), a judgment between the two reformers, as I called the authors of

> الاصلاح والنصرة
>
> (al-Islah and al-Nusra)

With every care I appraised the opinions set down in the three books and weighed them under a scientific criticism, and I set them against

> قانون الدعوة الهادية
>
> (the Law of the Guiding Mission)

or, in other words, against the philosophical and doctrinal teachings held in office by the Fatimid court of my day. In sum, I have preferred at every turn the theories of al-Razi over those of al-Sijistani, and I have objected to the latter for their failure to conform to the truth and their leaning toward deviation. Beyond this, I have noted that al-Razi passed over certain of the statements that had come in the book المحصول, and I have brought them forward to mention, as is seen in the tenth chapter of الرياض.

Ideas of learning such as these, and a philosophical body of this order, deserve to be studied, set side by side, and scrutinized to their depth. Yet as fortune would have it, the two books

> المحصول والنصرة
>
> (al-Mahsul and al-Nusra)

have been lost, while الاصلاح of al-Razi and my own الرياض remain preserved. Let the reader, then, hold fast to what survives, and follow with me the paths of this garden as they open between the two reformers.
