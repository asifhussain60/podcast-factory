# The Age of Open Argument

The world of Islam, as the third century of the Muhammadan Hijra drew toward its close, bore the stamp of learning, of philosophy, and of letters. Fresh calls, doctrinal disagreements that branched in every direction, and a debate in theology that never came to rest at any boundary nor stayed fixed in any one place all moved through its cultural sphere and its community of scholars. How useful it is for anyone who wishes to study the origins of these disagreements in depth to weigh the opinions of the writers and scholars who lived in that distant age, men who left behind them scholarly traces and new modes of expression suited to their time and to their grasp of philosophical questions, and who laboured at interpreting texts and expounding verses. Only then can such a reader arrive at a sound judgment that carries him toward the reality of the matter and its truth.

These disagreements, more often than not, did not stay within the bounds of the spoken discussions once raised in the salons and the centres of learning. They grew into long treatises that came out upon the pages of books and circulated writings. Among the groups that contributed a large share to this activity were the Ismailis. Yet it is a strange thing that I do not find this group finishing its argument in theology with the other groups before it enters upon another argument of an altogether new kind: a debate among its own callers.

In this there is a clear proof of the freedom of mind that governed their lives, a release from the constraints that hold sway over conservative societies, that draw a fixed boundary about them and bind them to a settled balance, forbidding them to break from the pattern or to depart from the inheritance handed down.

The first debate, or in more exact words the first scholarly discussion, that opened new horizons for the Ismailis and carried them into an air of wide scholarly liberty, and made them a subject of conjecture and of talk throughout the world of Islam, was the appearance of the book المحصول (al-Mahsul), The Harvest.

This book was put into circulation at the beginning of the fourth century of the Hijra, the tenth of the Christian era. It is ascribed to the great caller of Khurasan, Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Nasafi, who was credited with turning the Samanid state in Transoxiana to Ismailism, and who was executed in the year 331 of the Hijra, 942 of the Christian era, as recorded in the book الفرق بين الفرق (al-Farq bayn al-Firaq) by its author Abd al-Qahir al-Baghdadi. It is a very strange thing that حجة العراقين (Hujjat al-Iraqayn), the greatest exponent of the Ismailis and their foremost philosopher, حميد الدين احمد الكرماني (Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani), who took up this very subject and went deep into its examination, made no mention of the author of al-Mahsul. His discussion rested instead upon Abu Hatim al-Razi and Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani. There must be, then, fundamental reasons of their own for this silence, reasons that justify it; and it may be that the book al-Mahsul is ascribed to al-Nasafi in error.

Whatever the truth of the matter, the acclaim that al-Mahsul won among the thinkers of the Ismailis and the ranks of their scholars in that day spurred Abu Hatim al-Razi, the chief caller in the north-west of Persia, to compose a book in which he examined what al-Mahsul had said. He named his book الإصلاح (al-Islah). It is plain that the opinions al-Razi advanced stirred a reaction in another great caller named Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani, who held them to run counter to the truth and to first principles. This is what moved him to write a book contesting the views of al-Razi, which he called النصرة (al-Nusra), in which he defended al-Mahsul and assailed Abu Hatim al-Razi without mercy. It appears that al-Nusra is among the earliest of al-Sijistani's works, as al-Kirmani himself hints in his book.

Not long afterward, al-Kirmani surveyed the whole subject in a full and thorough review and composed his precious treatise الرياض (al-Riyad), a judgment set between the two reformers, صاحبي الإصلاح والنصرة, the authors of al-Islah and al-Nusra, as he called them. With consummate skill he appraised the opinions that had appeared in the three books and subjected them to a scholarly criticism, and he set them beside قانون الدعوة الهادية (the Law of the Guiding Mission); or, to put it otherwise, beside the philosophical and doctrinal positions officially held by the Fatimid court in his day. In the main I find that al-Kirmani always prefers the theories of al-Razi over those of al-Sijistani, and that he takes exception to the latter for failing to conform to the truth and for their leaning toward deviation. Beyond this, he observes that al-Razi passed over some of the statements set down in the book al-Mahsul, and so he brings them forward himself, as I note in the tenth chapter of the book al-Riyad.

It is worth remarking, too, upon the scholarly ideas these works hold. A body of philosophy such as this deserves to be studied, examined, and scrutinized with care; but, as ill fortune would have it, the two books al-Mahsul and al-Nusra have been lost, while the book al-Islah of al-Razi and al-Riyad of al-Kirmani are still preserved.
