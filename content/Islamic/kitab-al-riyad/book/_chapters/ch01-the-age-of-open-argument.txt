# The Age of Open Argument

The world of Islam, as the third century of the Muhammadan Hijra drew to its close, bore the stamp of its sciences, its philosophy, and its letters. These governed its cultural sphere and its learned society alike. New currents of summons, tangled disagreements over doctrine, and a debate in words that never came to rest at any limit nor settled in any fixed place, all of these pressed upon it at once. And I find it of the greatest use for the one who would study in depth the origin of those differences that he weigh the opinions of the writers and scholars who lived in that distant age, who left behind scientific traces and fresh modes of expression proper to their time, to their grasp of the philosophical questions, and to the reach of their effort in interpreting the texts and construing the verses. Only then can he form a reasonable view through which he may pass to what is real and true.

The disputes I speak of did not, for the most part, remain within the bounds of the spoken exchanges that used to be raised in the salons and the centers of learning. They grew into long treatises that stood forth upon the pages of books and circulated writings. Among the sects that gave this field a full and generous share were the Ismailis. Yet it is very strange that I do not see this group finish its argument in words with the other groups before it comes forward to plunge into another argument, and one of a new kind entirely: an argument among those who summoned to the cause themselves. In this there is a clear proof of an intellectual freedom that governed their lives, a release from the bonds that hold sway over cautious societies and draw a fence about them, binding them not to break from the pattern and not to stray from the inherited design.

The first argument, or to speak more exactly the first scholarly discussion, opened new horizons for the Ismailis. It carried them into an air of far-reaching scholarly liberty and made them a subject of guesswork and report throughout the world of Islam. This came with the appearance of the book *al-Mahsul*, The Harvest.

This book was set in circulation at the opening of the fourth century of the Hijra, the tenth of the Christian era. It is ascribed to the great preacher of Khurasan,

> محمد بن احمد النسفي
>
> Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Nasafi,

to whom is credited the turning of the Samanid state in Transoxiana toward Ismailism, and who was put to death in the year 331 of the Hijra, 942 of the Christian era, as is stated in the book

> الفرق بين الفرق
>
> al-Farq bayn al-Firaq,

by its author Abd al-Qahir al-Baghdadi. And it is very strange that the Proof of the Two Iraqs, its greatest exponent and its foremost philosopher,

> حميد الدين احمد الكرماني
>
> Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani,

who took up the subject and went deep into its examination, made no mention at all of the author of *al-Mahsul*. His discussion rather bore upon

> أبي حاتم الرازي ، وأبي يعقوب السجستاني
>
> Abu Hatim al-Razi and Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani.

There must then be fundamental reasons behind this silence that account for it, and it may be that the book *al-Mahsul* is ascribed to al-Nasafi in error.

However that may be, the favor that *al-Mahsul* won among the thinkers of the Ismailis and the ranks of their scholars in that day moved Abu Hatim al-Razi, the chief preacher in the northwest of Persia, to compose a book in which he took up what had been said in *al-Mahsul*. He named his book *al-Islah*, The Reform. It is plain that the opinions al-Razi advanced stirred a reaction in another great preacher named Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani, who held them to run contrary to the truth and to first principles. This is what led him to set about a book in which he contended against the views of al-Razi, and he named it *al-Nusra*, The Support. In it its author took the side of *al-Mahsul* and assailed Abu Hatim al-Razi without relenting. It appears that *al-Nusra* is among the earliest of al-Sijistani's works, as al-Kirmani himself hints in his book.

Not long afterward, al-Kirmani surveyed the whole matter in a full and thorough survey and composed his precious treatise *al-Riyad*, The Gardens, a judgment between the two reforming works, as he called them, that is, the authors of

> الاصلاح والنصرة
>
> al-Islah and al-Nusra.

With surpassing skill he appraised the opinions set down in the three books and weighed them in a scholarly criticism. He set them against

> قانون الدعوة الهادية
>
> the Law of the Guiding Mission,

that is to say, against the philosophical and doctrinal theories held officially by the Fatimid court in his day. In the whole of it I find al-Kirmani forever preferring the theories of al-Razi over those of al-Sijistani, and taking exception to the latter because they do not answer to the truth and lean toward deviation. Beyond this he notes that al-Razi passed over some of the statements that had appeared in *al-Mahsul*, so that he brings them up himself, as I observe in the tenth chapter of *al-Riyad*.

It is worth remarking, too, upon the scholarly ideas these works hold. A philosophical body of thought such as this deserves to be studied, examined, and scrutinized deeply. Yet by ill fortune the two books

> المحصول والنصرة
>
> al-Mahsul and al-Nusra

have been lost. As for the book *al-Islah* of al-Razi and *al-Riyad* of al-Kirmani, these are preserved still.
