# The Age of Open Argument

At the end of the third century of the Muhammadan Hijra, I find the Islamic world stamped through with the character of its sciences, its philosophy, and its literature. New currents, tangled doctrinal disagreements, and a running debate in the science of speech moved across its cultural sphere and its learned society, and this debate came to rest with no single party and settled in no single place. How useful it is, then, for the one who wishes to study in depth where such differences began, to weigh the opinions of the writers and scholars who lived in that distant age, who left behind scientific traces and fresh modes of expression that belonged to their time and to their grasp of philosophical questions, and who laboured to interpret texts and to explain verses, so that from all this he may form a reasonable view that carries him nearer to reality and truth.

The disagreements I speak of did not, for the most part, stay within the spoken discussions that were once raised in the salons and the centres of learning. They grew into long treatises that came out upon the pages of books and pamphlets. Among the sects that gave this field a large share were the Ismailis. Yet it is very strange that we do not see this group finish its argument in the science of speech with the other groups before it enters upon a further debate of an altogether new kind, a debate among its own callers.

In this there is clear proof of an intellectual freedom that governed their lives, a release from the constraints that rule over conservative societies and draw a boundary around them, binding them to shun all irregularity and to keep from any departure from the inherited plan.

The first debate, or in more exact words the first scientific discussion, that opened new horizons for the Ismailis, carried them into an atmosphere of far-reaching scientific liberation, and made them a subject of conjecture and report throughout the Islamic world, was the appearance of the book al-Mahsul, "The Harvest."

This book was put into circulation at the beginning of the fourth century after the Hijra, the tenth century of the Christian era. It is ascribed to the great preacher of Khurasan,

> محمد بن احمد النسفي
>
> Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Nasafi

who was credited with turning the Samanid state in Transoxiana toward Ismailism, and who was put to death in the year 331 of the Hijra, 942 of the Christian era, as is stated in the book

> الفرق بين الفرق
>
> al-Farq bayn al-Firaq

by its author Abd al-Qahir al-Baghdadi. It is very strange to me that Hujjat al-Iraqayn, the greatest exponent of this matter and its foremost philosopher,

> حميد الدين احمد الكرماني
>
> Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani

who took up the subject and went deeply into its examination, made no mention of the author of al-Mahsul. His discussion instead settled upon

> أبي حاتم الرازي ، وأبي يعقوب السجستاني
>
> Abu Hatim al-Razi and Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani.

There must then be fundamental reasons of a particular kind behind this silence that justify it, and it may be that the book al-Mahsul was ascribed to al-Nasafi in error.

Whatever the case may be, the acclaim that al-Mahsul won among the thinkers of the Ismailis and the ranks of their scholars in that time spurred Abu Hatim al-Razi, the chief preacher in the northwest of Persia, to compose a book in which he examined what had been said in al-Mahsul. He named his book al-Islah. It is clear to me that the opinions al-Razi advanced provoked a reaction in another great preacher named Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani, who held them to be contrary to the truth and to first principles. This is what led him to write a book in which he contested the views of al-Razi, a book he named al-Nusra, in which its author defended al-Mahsul and attacked Abu Hatim al-Razi without mercy. It appears that al-Nusra was among the earliest of al-Sijistani's works, as al-Kirmani himself hints in his own book.

Not long afterwards, al-Kirmani reviewed the whole subject in a full and thorough review and composed his precious treatise al-Riyad, a judgment between the two reformers, as he named them,

> الاصلاح والنصرة
>
> al-Islah and al-Nusra.

With surpassing skill he appraised the opinions that had come in the three books and criticised them with a scholarly criticism, and he set them beside the

> قانون الدعوة الهادية
>
> the Law of the Guiding Mission,

that is, beside the philosophical and doctrinal theories held officially by the Fatimid court in his day. In sum, we find that al-Kirmani always prefers the theories of al-Razi over the theories of al-Sijistani, and objects to the latter for their failure to conform to the truth and their leaning toward deviation. Beyond this, he notes that al-Razi passed over some of the statements that had come in al-Mahsul, and so he took it upon himself to mention them, as we observe in the tenth chapter of al-Riyad.

It is worth mentioning, too, that scientific ideas and a philosophical body of work such as these deserve study, comparison, and deep scrutiny. But by an unhappy turn, the two books

> المحصول والنصرة
>
> al-Mahsul and al-Nusra

have been lost. As for al-Islah by al-Razi and al-Riyad by al-Kirmani, these two remain preserved to this day.
