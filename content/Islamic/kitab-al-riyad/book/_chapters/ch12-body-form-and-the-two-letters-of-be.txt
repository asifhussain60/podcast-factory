# Body, Form, and the Two Letters of 'Be'

Here I speak of motion and stillness, of hyle and of form, and of how the two authors before me have reasoned about them.

**Section One**

The author of al-Islah said:

As for the discourse on hyle and form, they are the spirit of motion and stillness, just as the body is to them. The first hyle is the imagined motion within composite things, and the hyles, taken all together, are three. What has been said on this matter in the present book carries much confusion, and so I will state it in brief. Motion and stillness stand to hyle and form as the soul stands to the body, because hyle and form are to motion and stillness as the body is to them. The soul is subtler than the body, and the body denser than the soul; and just so, motion and stillness are subtler than hyle and form, while hyle and form are denser than motion and stillness. To represent the subtler by the soul and the denser by the body is more fitting than to represent the denser by the body and the subtler by the soul. This is clear, and its proof lies with it.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

The case of soul and body, and the measure taken between them, does not rest upon the subtle and the dense alone. For were the matter as he has said, then the perceptible substances would have to be bodies for the accidents, since they are denser than the accidents, and the accidents would have to be souls for those substances, since they are subtler than them. Rather, we are obliged to look to the definition of body and the definition of soul, and then to liken whatever resembles the definition of the soul to the soul, and whatever resembles the definition of the body to the body. So we say: we found the body to be that which receives the three dimensions, namely length, width, and depth; and we found the soul to be that whose parts resemble one another, near enough. In the same way we found that motion is divided into three divisions, as the body is divided, namely motion from the center, motion toward the center, and motion around the center. For this reason motion must resemble the body when it is divided, splitting as the body splits into length, width, and depth. Hyle is not so, for the parts of hyle resemble one another. And because it is more fitting to liken motion to the body when the two are alike in the matter of division, and to liken hyle to the soul when the parts are alike, than to liken motion to the soul, one of the two, namely divided motion, resembles the body, and the other is the body that is not divided; while hyle resembles the soul, being undivided, and the other is the body that is divided. This is what they said.

We say:

In this section the two of them have gone far astray in their representation and their likening. They have both built upon a corrupt foundation on which no proof can stand, so that neither the likeness nor the thing likened to it can be made to hold. The author of al-Islah has laid it down that motion and stillness are as the soul to hyle and form, and this is impossible. For motion is the act of the mover, and the mover is what we call the soul, the self; and stillness, likewise, is the mover's leaving off the moving of what it moves. If, then, the mover is the soul, to represent motion, which is its act, by the soul is an impossible representation, and the same holds for stillness. And when this is shown to be void for motion and stillness, it is shown void as well that motion and stillness should be hyle and form. Moreover, the way of hyle and form is not the way of motion and stillness. For motion and stillness, though each is an act, are two contraries that do not meet in a single place, in a single state, at a single time; whereas hyle and form exist together, neither parting from the other, save that one of them is the agent, which is the form, and the other is that which is acted upon within it, which is the hyle. Our well-known book The Rest of the Mind (راحة العقل) gathers the explanation of this.

**Section Two**

Then he was led to say that the soul is subtler than the body, on the ground that the soul is a body, and this is impossible. The soul cannot be a body such that density or subtlety should attach to it, for density and subtlety belong only to that which is a body, as air, which is a body, is marked by subtlety, and earth, which is a body, is marked by density. The soul is the self, and the proofs have been established that it is not a body. And if the self is not a body, then it is impossible to argue that it is subtler or denser, or to represent it by such terms.

**Section Three**

The author of al-Nusrah came forward and promised to set the definition of body and of soul. He defined the body by its receiving the three dimensions, and defined the soul by the likeness of its parts to one another, drawing it out in the manner of water, fire, and air, each of which is alike in its parts. This is impossible, in view of what has already been said, that the soul is not a body, so that this cannot attach to it. The soul and the self are two synonymous names for one thing, and that thing is not a body. For were it a body, there would attach to it, of the nine qualities and accidents, what attaches to a body, and it could be grasped by a sense. But since it is not grasped by a sense, it cannot be perceived, and the nine accidents are neither imagined in it nor present in it; and since the bodily accidents are not present in it, it is void that it should be a body. Our well-known book The Lamps (المصابيح) holds of this what heals.

**Section Four**

Then he made motion, by its division into three sections, resemble the body, in the way he has mentioned, that the body is divided into length, width, and depth; and this is impossible. For the three sections into which he alleges the body is divided, which are the three dimensions, each one of these three dimensions is not a body, nor is it called a body; whereas each section of motion is itself a motion, and is called a motion. How, then, can the body, which is divided by form into nine sections, as we have shown in our well-known book Milestones of Religion (معالم الدين), be likened to motion, which is divided into three sections, or into six? That is far-fetched indeed. The discourse in this chapter, from the author of al-Mahsul, the author of al-Islah, and the author of al-Nusrah, is confused and without order.

We say:

Motion is that by which the very thing is changed, whether by place, or by coming-to-be, or by passing-away, or by increase, or by decrease, or by alteration; and its occurrence is from the mover, which is the principle within the thing in which it resides, by nature and not by accident. Hyle and form are what emanates from the First by the lower relation within it, as the second emanates by the nobler relation within it. Hyle has no existence outside the soul at all, except together with form; and from it are the heavens and the earth and what is between them. God, exalted, brought all of this into being from the First Intellect, which He originated not from any thing. Exalted be God with a great exaltation.

**Section Five**

The author of al-Islah said:

The First Cause, which is origination, and which the learned name the word of the Maker, mighty and exalted, has for its form:

> كن

"Be." This is two letters, a كاف (kaf) in motion and a نون (nun) at rest. The كاف and the نون of the word stand in the place of the body, while motion and stillness stand in the place of the soul. On this pattern the likeness lies in motion and stillness as the two things effected by the word, and the First Cause, which is the word, is the hyle of the whole world. On this same pattern it must be said of motion and stillness in all substances, from hyle and form down to what is below them, throughout all the worlds. The sum of the discourse on the three hyles is this:

1. The first hyle is imaginary, and it is motion and stillness; it is the very substance of hyle and its form.
2. The second hyle comprises the individuals and the things generated from the first hyle.
3. The third hyle is the four composite mothers derived from the second hyle.

Then we say:

Hyle and its form are the act of the command, which is origination. Just as we have said that hyle is imaginary, so too origination is imaginary: its essence appears only in the First Originated.

The author of al-Nusrah said:

As for the ground the author of al-Islah took in كن, saying that the كاف is in motion and the نون at rest, and that this stands in the place of the body while motion and stillness stand for it in the place of the soul, this is not granted to him. These two letters are used to speak of the matter here only because there is, at that level, a كاف, a نون, a motion, or a stillness; for it cannot be said that above the First Effect there is motion or stillness or anything of this manner of speech. Rather, there is only pure origination and the complete cause. This is what they say.

We say:

The saying of the author of al-Islah, that the First Cause, which is origination, is a word of God, exalted, and that its form is these two letters, is a remote analogy in the way he intended it, and it scarcely holds. For our saying "form" requires something for which it is a form, and the word has nothing for which its form would be a form. When we say "form," meaning what is above nature, we mean the abstract form; and when we say "form," meaning what is in the natural world, we mean the form that is in matter. Had he said instead that origination, which is the word of God, exalted, and His command, as expressed by كن, is two letters, one in motion and the other at rest, and that this points to an essence gathering two attributes, then he would have drawn from it a proof of that noble Essence and its attributes.

He said:

It gathers two states, just as the word كن gathers two states, motion and stillness. He then referred the two states to what belongs to that Essence of the two noble relations, one of which is its being a cause, the other its being an effect. He set the motion whose end is stillness against the relation which is the cause whose end is the effect, and the stillness whose end is motion against the relation which is the effect whose end is the cause, not because there is motion or stillness there, but only by way of making the likeness understood. Had it been so, it would have been more fitting, and what he said would have met the very state of the thing spoken of, which is the thing likened by the likeness.

**Section Six**

Then he said:

The first hyle is imaginary, and he made it motion and stillness; then he made motion and stillness one with hyle, and form is not hyle, but rather motion and stillness, they are hyle and form. For hyle is a substance emanating from the First, in the manner already discussed, and from it is the lower world. As for motion and stillness, their existence is in bodies according to the principles from which their appearing comes; and hyle and form together are the body, the body's being lying in hyle and form.

**Section Seven**

Then he said:

When he made the first hyle out of the matter which is origination, saying that the first hyle is imaginary, then origination is imaginary, its essence appearing only in the First Originated. His saying entails that there existed, from eternity, together with the Maker, exalted be His majesty above that, something in which origination appeared, so that by origination it became originated; and this is a grave error. For the doctrine and the belief concerning the divine unity is that whatever is other than God, exalted, is originated and brought to be in time, having no reality in existence at all; and if that is so, then origination is nothing but the Originated. We have established the proof of this in what has preceded of our epistles, in a measure that suffices.

**Section Eight**

As for the saying of the author of al-Nusrah:

In what he stated, and in the way he opposed the author of al-Islah with it, it is correct, except that his saying that it should not be said that above the First Effect there is motion and stillness is an affirming of them in the First Effect and in what is below it. Yet we have shown that the First Effect is not in motion, that motion does not befit it, nor stillness either, since it is other than a body.

**Section Nine**

The author of al-Nusrah said:

Contradiction is in bodies, and there is no contradiction in souls.

We say:

Bodies, insofar as they are bodies, have no difference among them and no contradiction. Difference and disparity fall only in the forms that are within them, the forms that move them, not in respect of their being bodies.

And his saying:

That it is more fitting to liken that in which contradiction is found to what is likewise, the matter is as he said, if the very thing spoken of is such.
