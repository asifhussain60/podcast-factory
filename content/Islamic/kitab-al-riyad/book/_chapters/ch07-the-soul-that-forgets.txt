# The Soul That Forgets

Chapter Twenty-One of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said:

One of the clearest signs of the deficiency of the Soul and the perfection of the First Intellect is that the First, and the author of al-Islah, all said that the act belongs to the Second, and they attributed nothing to the First; for action is always a sign of deficiency and need, while the abandonment of action is evidence of perfection and self-sufficiency. That is to say, action is only for the sake of drawing a benefit or repelling a harm.

Abu Zakariyya said:

The Initiator, by way of manipulation and tampering, is already the Soul, and the people of religion do not hold this. So there remains nothing but the drawing of a benefit or the repelling of a harm; and wherever this is found, there is deficiency. The Soul, in this, seeks to draw the benefit, which is its reaching the end destined for it, and so it begins with action. The Intellect, having no rank above it, is not deficient and does not move to action in any way; but the Soul, having found a station above it, remained in motion, abiding in its quest. By this method we say what we say concerning the deficiency of the Soul and the integrity of the Intellect. This statement is clear, and praise be to God.

The sum of his saying in this chapter is that the Second is the doer, and that action is only for drawing a benefit or repelling a harm; that the drawing of benefit and the repelling of harm belong only to deficiency; that the Intellect, not being deficient, is still from action, its stillness being that there is no rank above it for it to seek; and that the Soul, having a rank above it, moved toward it to attain it.

We say: Had the words of the author of al-Islah been about the Soul that is the soul of human beings, the saying of the author of al-Nusra would have been the more fitting expression. But since his words were about the Following that is named "the Soul," the saying of the author of al-Nusra, and his inference from the condition found in human souls, that they act only to draw a benefit, to repel a harm, or to tamper, to invalidate his statement and to overturn his ruling that the Following is complete and perfect, is a manifest prejudice. For it has already been said in sum that what is affirmed of that whose existence is a first existence, in respect of completeness and perfection, is not affirmed of that whose existence is a second existence, nor is it affirmed of that whose existence was a first existence.

The statement of the author of al-Nusra concerning the act and its divisions is not correct, for not every act is for drawing a benefit, or repelling a harm, or futility. An act may proceed from an agent such that its benefit returns to the object of the act, not to the one from whom the act issues. The proof of this is that heating is an act of the fire, yet this act does not return to the fire, and neither benefit nor harm accrues to it; rather the benefit and the harm fall upon whatever the heating, which is its act, reaches. So too the act of the sun in illuminating the world, and the enabling of human beings to dispose of their affairs by their own will through it, and its rising, and the coming to be of plants and animals and minerals through its heat: its benefits return to others, not to itself; it neither benefits from this act nor is harmed by it. So too the act of the whales that are in the Sea of Oman, and others called "the dolphin" (الدلفين), whose nature is such that, when a drowning man is drowning in the sea, they aid him to escape and push him toward the shore; yet that act does not return to them with benefit or harm; rather the benefit reaches the object of the act.

If this is so, and it is found among natural things that do not reason, even though they are deficient in their states and in need of others in their action, then there is nothing to deny in the pure, abstract, complete intellects, which were granted their perfections at the very first of their existence, that they should perform acts greater than these acts which appear from the natural things, acts that their perfections require of them, according to the nearness of their ranks and the loftiness of their noble degrees; noble acts, not for the sake of drawing a benefit to themselves, nor of repelling a harm from themselves, but for the sake of others. So these are acts of wisdom, not acts of drawing benefit, nor of repelling harm, nor of futility. Nay, the perfect power from which the perfect, well ordered act proceeds exists only in the perfect.

Moreover, the Following, which he named "the Soul," is not in motion as he claimed, so that what befalls the natural things would befall it; for that motion belongs only to bodies, with their bodies and their natural forms. The Following is higher in rank than the natural things, though it is below the First in rank, as we have made clear in the book Rahat al-Aql. By what we have brought forward, his prejudice and his argument, which do not hold concerning the one spoken of, have become clear, and the statement of the author of al-Islah, that the Following is complete in completeness, stands, as we explained earlier. It is not permissible to predicate motion and rest of what lies in the abode of origination, namely the First and Second Intellects and the upper letters; for we have shown that these two belong only to what is a body or possessed of a body. And in this there is sufficiency.

Chapter Twenty-Two of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said:

The upshot of the statement of the author of al-Islah is that the act of the Soul is perfected, not the essence of the Soul, even though the Soul with him is complete; for on this view the act of the Soul would become a soul, and this is impossible with a manifest impossibility, since acts that are never seen, in what is observed, to become agents. An example of this is the writer and the writing, and the builder and the building: the writing does not become a writer, nor does the building become a builder; rather it is the man, who is a writer in potency and a builder in potency, who becomes a writer and a builder in actuality. Likewise we say of the Soul and the Intellect: the Intellect is complete in actuality, and the Soul is deficient in actuality, subsisting in potency, and it comes to subsist in actuality when it gains completeness. But that its act should become complete is impossible.

We say:

The aim of the author of al-Islah in his saying that the act of the Soul is perfected, and not the essence of the Soul, is this: it is that which attains the second perfection from the Soul's side, that which proceeds from it in the manner of what it pours forth upon, so that it issues from potency to the measure of the act. And since the aim of his saying, that the act of the Soul is perfected, is what issues by the Soul's overflowing from potency into act and attains the second perfection, and since nothing issues from the limit of potency into act except by what subsists in actuality, then it is not to be denied that, when what subsisted in potency issues into act and attains the second perfection by acquisition, it should be like that which subsists in actuality in what it was granted of perfection at the first of its existence. So they share in that they both subsist in actuality, even though each of them attained what they share not from the side from which the other attained it. It is not impossible that they be two likes in respect of their sharing; for it is known that everything gathered with another thing in some meaning of the meanings is, in that meaning, its like. The witness to this is what our saying yields: everything that goes out from the limit of potency to the limit of act subsists in actuality; and everything subsisting in actuality subsists by the occurrence of the second perfection to it; and everything that goes out from the limit of potency to act has the second perfection occur to it. So if the subsistence of what subsists in actuality is by the occurrence of the second perfection to it, and the subsistence of what subsisted in potency and then came to subsist in actuality is likewise by the occurrence of the second perfection to it, then they share and are alike in that they both attained the second perfection, even though from another side they differ. And since what we have brought forward is neither impossible nor to be denied, but rather necessary, the statement of the author of al-Nusra is nothing but prejudice.

Chapter Twenty-Three of Part One

Then the author of al-Nusra said:

This is impossible with a manifest impossibility, for acts have never once been seen, in what is observed, to become agents, according to what he adduced concerning the matter of the writer and the building, by a proof that testifies to the impossibility of what he claimed to be impossible.

We say:

Acts are, in truth, the agents. For the writing, which he mentioned to be the act of the writer, is the very thing that converses with its reader and addresses him. Were the writing, in its forms and its tracing, not, together with its being an act belonging to another, an agent upon the very soul of its reader, as though it spoke on behalf of its author and translated him and imprinted him in its forms, its reader would not know what is in the soul of its writer. And the building, in the form which the builder brought into being out of what was in his soul, is what conveys to the observer its form by its arrangement and its disposition, so that he conceives it. Were the form of the building not subsisting in actuality, so as to impress upon the soul of the observer, which possesses its forms, its observer would not conceive it. Likewise the act of the striker, which is the striking, is the agent that produces the pain in the one struck, so that it pains him, in the manner we mentioned in our two well known epistles, al-Rawdah (الروضة) and al-Mudi'ah (المضيئة). And the saying of God, exalted is He, is the firmest proof that acts are agents as well, where He says, exalted is the Speaker:

> وإن من شيء إلا يسبح بحمده

There is nothing but glorifies Him with His praise. For the "thing" is the act of the Maker, exalted is He, and it, together with being the act of an agent, is itself its glorification. And His saying, exalted is He:

> وأرسلنا الرياح لواقح

And We sent the winds as fecundators. The winds are a thing, and their fecundating is His act. The explanation of this having become clear, the objection of the author of al-Nusra against the author of al-Islah in this chapter is impossible.

Then the aim of the author of al-Nusra in his saying, that we have never seen acts in what is observed become agents at all, is that the very acts have not become the very same as their agent in what is observed, just as the writing has not become the very same as its writer, nor has the building become the very same as its builder. This is a correct statement, as he said it, for an act does not become the very same as that from which the act issued; rather it is an agent like its agent in respect of the existence of the act from it, even though the ranks differ and the causes vary. But the author of al-Nusra wished to defend the one he represents by prejudice against the author of al-Islah, may God sanctify their souls; and many associates wrong one another.

Chapter Twenty-Four of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said:

He likened the Intellect and the Soul, in their completeness and their deficiency, to a man and to the sperm, because the sperm is, in truth, the form of the man in potency, and it becomes upright like the man. Likewise the Soul is, in truth, the form of the Intellect, and it becomes complete like the Intellect when it gains from the Intellect its benefits destined for it. This is what he said.

We say:

His statement, that the sperm is in truth the form of the man in potency, and likewise that the Soul is in truth the form of the Intellect and becomes complete, is not correct. The sperm is not the form of the man, nor is the Soul, which is in potency, the form of the Intellect. For the sperm is matter made ready for form, void of forms, which then acquires them under conditions; and likewise the Soul is only a natural readiness, empty of forms, whose nature is to acquire the forms of the objects of knowledge, so that its essence is perfected by them. Had the Soul been the form of the Intellect in truth, it would follow that the Intellect must have that of which the form is a form, so that it would be one thing, and that of which the form is a form would be another thing. And how can that be true of what its existence is an originated existence, in which otherness is found only by relation, while the essence is one? Nay. This discourse and this likeness holds only of what is natural, and not of what is separate, parted from bodies.

Chapter Twenty-Five of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said:

Likewise, the rank of the speaker (nāṭiq) in the corporeal world is like the rank of the Intellect in the spiritual world, and the rank of the foundation (asās) is like the rank of the Following. So if it is permissible to say that the Soul is complete like the Intellect, it is permissible to say that the foundation is complete like the speaker. But that is not correct, because the rank of the foundation is below the rank of the speaker, and this has been made clear in the meaning of the prayer. This is what he said.

We say:

The Second is like the First, even though in rank it is below it, just as the foundation is like the speaker, even though in rank he is below him. For that Second is established in its existence, and there remains for it no degree, in what it attains afterward, by which it would gain completeness, just as the foundation is complete and perfect in its rank, with no degree remaining for it to ascend to that would give it a completeness not in its own essence. Sufficient as witness to this is the act of the Prophet (صلعم) and his indication of what we have said, in that he made it obligatory to slaughter camels by naḥr (النحر) and to slaughter others by dhabḥ (الذبح). For the naḥr is the delivering of the iron to the head of the heart (fuʾād) of the one slaughtered, so that whatever turbid blood is there may issue from him, corresponding to the degree of conveyance and the disclosure of the secrets and the departure of doubts and uncertainties in the ranks; and the dhabḥ corresponds to the taking of the covenant and the release in the summons, while the one slaughtered by naḥr corresponds to the foundation. It is as though the Prophet (صلعم), by manifesting this act, informed the community that followed him, on the one side, and the imams of his cycle, that he did not set the foundation in his own place in the summons to the inner call, which is the completion of the worship of God that leads to the affirmation of God's oneness and the knowledge of His bounds, until he had taught him what holds his perfection and disclosed to him what holds his completeness. So he raised his degree by conveyance, and his path in attaining perfection and completion is not like the path of any other of his community, by which one obtains from him the conveyance and the completeness.

The conveyance and the covenant were the last thing I set down, and the prayer itself bears witness to the order in which they fall. For the Prophet did not take the covenant from the sons of the call first and then inform them of the degree of perfection out of any contradiction. The conveyance is only for the sake of pointing and indicating: it shows that the natural souls, which stand below the Second, are first in potency and then issue forth by acquisition into act at the last, which is their perfection and their completion, so that each of them may believe of it what is fitting to believe. Nor did the matter stop at this single indication. He indicated the completion of the Follower from another side as well, in the two units of the dawn prayer, which stand upon the First and the Second. In the first unit, which is upon the First, he made it obligatory to recite the glorification, the praise, and the sura; and in the second unit he made obligatory the recitation of the Qunut in place of the glorification that belongs to the first, so that the second might stand in the same state as the first from the side of perfection. Their distinction then lies only in rank within existence: this is the beginning of existence, like the first unit, and that is the second of existence, like the second unit.

The twenty-sixth chapter of Part One. And we say, in answer to his saying that if it were permitted to call the soul as perfect as the Intellect, it would be permitted to call the Foundation like the Speaker, and that this is not right: how could it not be right, when the Foundation is the one who stands in the place of the Prophet and his family? Were he to fall short of standing in that place, or were he not sufficient, complete, and perfect as the Prophet is, God would not have enjoined obedience to him. How would God the Exalted enjoin obedience to one who is like others in deficiency? Neither of these can stand. Nor is it allowed in wisdom, while the Messenger is a messenger to all who are of his time and all who are born after him, and a proof upon His creation, that the community should be left without a like of the Messenger, so that no one is found among them as he is and the proof stands established against them. The Foundation, then, is like the Speaker who comes after him from one side, and unlike the Speaker from another side. It is proof enough that the disparity between them is in rank and not in essence that the author says the rank of the Foundation is below the rank of the Speaker, for he could argue by nothing except rank. It is clear from what we have set out that the position of the author of al-Iṣlāḥ has withstood the objection of the author of al-Nuṣra, and that his saying that the Second is deficient, though it is the saying of the author of al-Nuṣra, holds only in the sense in which it must rightly be said.

is the purification of the Speaker from the side of his Cultivator, who is the Second, the Higher being upon the Prior, since his highest Cultivator is upon the Prior. He said that the Speaker (ع) mentioned this meaning in the revelation, in His saying,

ثم دنا فتدلى

"Then he drew near and came down,"

meaning that the Speaker drew near to standing upon the rank of the Intellect, so that the Foundation hung down from him, and he was

قاب قوسين

by which he means the Prior. For the Prophet and his family, from the height of his rank, drew near until he reached a rank in which there was no veil between him and the Prior, just as the Prior had no separation between himself and the Word. The Foundation, he argued, did not reach this rank and station; and likewise the Second did not reach the rank of the Prior, but remained below it. This holds, he said, in every pair, for the male is ever the more complete and the more noble, and the female the more deficient, as with the heaven and the earth: the heaven in the place of the male, the earth in the place of the female, the heaven nobler and more complete than the earth, and the earth more deficient than the heaven. This is his saying.

And we say: the saying of the author of al-Nuṣra, that the rank of the Prior is higher than that of the Second, is a sound saying, and it is what the author of al-Iṣlāḥ also said. But that the rank of the Second is below the rank of the Prior does not indicate a deficiency in his essence. As for his saying that the Prophet and his family reached the rank of the Prior in completeness, in a measure the Foundation did not reach, this can hardly be sound. For the Foundation, though by his own essence he did not reach that degree, reached it by means of the Speaker, through his teaching him and disclosing to him the blessings that were sent down upon him. By his complete acceptance from him his soul was lit, and it was completed as the body of the moon is completed in light on the fourteenth night of the month. This is borne witness to by the conduct of the Prophet and his family in setting the sermon before the prayer on the day of the Friday gathering, and in setting it after the prayer on the day of the Fast-breaking. For he made the Friday, in one of the faces of interpretation, a face upon himself, since it is one of the seven days: his ascent of the pulpit for the sermon comes first, then the prayer, in that he attained the degree of perfection and completion and rose to the highest ranks of nobility from the side of the two Roots first, and then called to his law, to the acceptance of his revelation, and to obedience to his Foundation. And he made the day of Fast-breaking upon his Foundation, since it is one of the days of God the Exalted, by obedience in which the religion of God is completed. His setting the prayer before the sermon on that day is because his Foundation is under his obedience and under the acceptance of his command and his law: first he attained perfection and completion and rose in the ranks of nobility, and by the Speaker's mediation he was made acquainted with the degree of conveyance. So the Successor is complete in his essence, even though his attaining of the degree of completion is from the side of the Speaker, and his rank is below the rank of the Speaker. Nor is the existence of the female a deficient existence, that it might be argued in support of what he supports. Its existence is a complete existence. Were its deficiency on account of what is proper to the male and absent in it, then the existence of the male too would be deficient on account of what is proper to the female and absent in it, and the male would be ruled out from having a complete existence, which is absurd. The male, then, is complete in his essence, and the female complete in hers; only by the joining of the one to the other do their ranks become unequal, so that the female is below the male in rank. It is proof enough that the disparity is in ranks and not in essences that he says, "and likewise the Second did not reach the rank of the Prior," and that he says, "and the Foundation did not reach this rank." The saying of the author of al-Iṣlāḥ is therefore sound, that the Second is complete.

The twenty-eighth chapter of Part One. The author of al-Nuṣra said that among the proofs of the deficiency of the soul and the perfection of the Intellect is that many of the speaking souls submit to nature, and are led by it toward what it commands them, and incline to its appetites and pleasures, and forget their own knowledge and light and splendor, and find the world of nature and its forms and traces beautiful, and remember nothing of the splendor of their own world and its luminous pleasures, out of their deficiency. For, out of that deficiency, they desire the appetites and pleasures that are with nature, and turn away from their own world and its pleasures and beauty and splendor. But the Intellect, for its perfection and its beauty and its splendor and its completion, does not forget the pleasures of its own world and its beauty and splendor, and does not incline to nature, nor desire what is with it of its pleasures and appetites, and does not look toward it; and when it does look toward it, it looks only from the side of tenderness and mercy and disdain for what is with it, informing it of itself that what is with it is little when set against what is in its own world. Were the soul perfect like the Intellect, it would have held fast to what it saw in its own world, and would not have desired what is with the thing beneath it. This is his saying, and in it he says that many of the speaking souls submit to nature and are led by it in what it commands them, and incline to it for their deficiency.

And we say: the one that submits and inclines, that is led toward preferring nature and its pleasures, and toward following the natural appetites and abandoning the acts of worship, is the sensual soul, since it is of nature, and not the speaking soul that has become attached to it. The sensual soul is only restrained and held back from the doing of base and vile things when the traces of the Intellect work upon it by way of the bodily bounds, through teaching, so that it is restrained from committing what would destroy it, and so acts rightly. These souls are not the Follower that issues from the First Intellect, of whom the author of al-Iṣlāḥ speaks. Already in what has passed there is folded the point that the Follower is other than these souls, and these souls other than it, and that the Follower is of such completeness and perfection that nothing remains in his essence unattained by which he could be deficient, as is the state of these souls, which exist first with a first perfection and have a second perfection that they attain in time and by acquisition.

The twenty-ninth chapter of Part One. And we say, secondly, that the Follower, who is complete and perfect and who was given his perfection and completion at the very beginning of his existence all at once, is an existent, and by him and by his overflow the deficient one attains the acceptance of the degree of perfection. The proof of this is that the existents in the world of nature are in potency first and in act at the last, and there is no passing of what stands in potency into act except by the existence of what stands in act. And if there is no passing of what stands in potency into act except by the existence of what stands in act, and the thing that passes into act exists, then the existence of what stands in act is a necessary existence. And if the existence of what stands in act is a necessary existence, then in its existence it is either the First Intellect or another. That it should be the First Intellect is void, for the First Intellect cannot be prevented from having his act exist except by the existence of another, nor does he need alongside himself something existing outside him to act upon. The First Intellect is free, in his act, of any other than himself to act upon, being the complete one whose existence is from the One from whom he came to be, the Exalted and the Magnified, a single existence with no like in his existence and no other, so that there should be with him something for him to act upon. For if there were, the Worshipped, the Exalted, would become no longer the Worshipped. Enough has already been said of this, and more than we have set out here, in our book known as Rāḥat al-ʿAql. What is at this station of oneness has nothing with it for it to act upon, and so no multiplicity issues from it save by its acting within its own essence. And since its act is within its own essence, and its act is the act that brings out, by it and by its overflow into another, what stands in potency, then it is ruled void that what brings the existents in potency out into act should be the First Intellect. And since it is not the First Intellect, it is established that it is other than it, and it is the Second.

Witness to the soundness of our saying is that the Prophet and his family did not, for the greatness of his rank and his completeness, have any need of another among mankind to complete in his place his act, which is the revelation and the law. In that he stood alone by his essence, and from his essence there issued, according to what he drew from the One by whom he attained the degree of perfection, what he recited and what he laid down as law, complete. For this reason he ran his soul, in the abode of the body, in the revelation, along the course of the First Intellect in the very act of origination, that his acts and his rank might lead whoever contemplates them to soundness of belief in the knowledge of what is hidden from the senses among the first principles, by origination and by procession, so that the path of affirming God's oneness might be made whole. The Foundation, though he stands at the highest degree of perfection, did not have that rank by which he could stand alone in his act, in the ordering of the call and the interpretation and the teaching and the guidance, by his own essence. So he was not free, in his act, of what he found with him, which is the revelation and the law. Neither did the Foundation dispense with this, that is, with the revelation and the law, nor was the Speaker one who dispensed, in the world of the body, with a thing existing alongside him by which to be aided in his act, as the Foundation is aided. For this reason he ran the Foundation along the course of the Follower, that the manner in which his affair stands, in what we have mentioned, might be a proof for knowing the rank of the Second. The Prophet and his family run along the course of the First, who is completeness: he dispenses, in his act, with any other than himself, and is aided by nothing but his own essence. The Foundation runs along the course of the Second, who is completeness in that there is no act of his without the revelation and the law by which his act comes to be, like the Follower, who was not free, in his act, of another by which his act comes to be. And the following community runs along the course of what is beneath the sphere, in the world of nature, in which the passing of its existents from potency into act comes about by the overflow of the Second, since the souls standing in potency are in need of that by which they attain the degree of conveyance and perfection, from the side of the call of the Foundation. For just as the Second is the one who confirms the one made ready for acceptance, by the overflow upon him from the abode of nature, so too the Foundation, and whoever stands in his place in the call, is the one who brings the one who is desirous and obedient to the degree of conveyance and completion. For the Speakers, since they were of the world of nature, did not attain completion and the lofty degree from the side of the First Intellect, but from the side of the Second Intellect first; just as none of the like of us reaches the degree of conveyance from the side of the Speaker, but only from the side of the call of the Foundation first. They run from those above them as we run from them,

حذو النعل بالنعل

as one sandal matches the other. So the perfection of the Speakers and of those who stand in their place is from those above them, and our perfection is by them. May God gather us with them. The discourse has thus made clear that these souls are not the Follower.

The Soul That Forgets

Whatever exists under such conditions is itself the evidence of its deficiency, and so the objection that the author of al-Nusra (النصرة) raises against the author of al-Islah (الاصلاح) is an impossible objection.

Chapter Thirty of Part One

He said: The Follower is deficient because it forgets its world and does not remember it.

But how can this be valid? Forgetting does not occur except after knowledge. If these souls had existed beforehand while they were knowing, it would have been impossible for alteration to overtake them by their descent into the abode of nature after they had dwelt in majesty and attained the perfection that is intellectual knowledge. For the existence of what exists in the world of origination and emanation, among the intellects, is an existence resembling what is necessary and fixed; it is not overtaken by any alteration of what it was, and for that reason it is called eternal. And how should the conditions overtake the eternal, so that it possesses knowledge at one time and is stripped of it at another? The abode in which it resides is not the abode of alteration that he has asserted for it by his word "نسيّ," that it forgot.

In sum I say: if these souls were bestowed upon these persons from that world, and came in order to learn and to be trained in the sciences, then their state, the ignorance in which they stand at the very outset of their existence here, cannot but be either in their essence or by an accident. If they are ignorant in their essence, then they are barred from knowing anything at all, and ignorance is intrinsic to them, which is impossible, since we find the soul coming to know after not knowing. And if the ignorance is by an accident, that accident is not free from being either in that world or in this one. If it befell them in that world, and that world is the abode of eternity and completeness, not the abode of alteration and deficiency, then it is impossible that an accident should befall them by which they are altered. And if it befell them in this world, and their end is to come to know and to return to the majesty they were in at first, before alteration overtook them, then that too is impossible in wisdom, since their remaining in the first state is more fitting for them than that they be altered from it and then return to it. It is void, then, in every case, to hold that they receive an accident, whether ignorance or knowledge, in this world, given that their existence is from another source. For if a thing's existence is not from this lower world, then for it to receive such an accident is impossible, since it is by completeness that it is what it is, and what is derived from it is the completeness that is the blessing of that world, "لأهل العلم," for the people of knowledge, not of this world. And when that is so, and it is impossible that their ignorance be in their essence or that an accident from that world befall them, then it is void that their arrival was from that world; and once it is void that their arrival was from that world, it is void to say that they forgot their world.

Chapter Thirty-One of Part One

Then, if its arrival is from the world of origination, its existence is a first existence, on the side of origination and emanation from the most excellent rank. And whatever has this manner of existence stands in actuality, and whatever stands in actuality is barred from receiving any accident. For the reception of accidents belongs to the way in which things that stand in potentiality are divided, and, in sum, to whatever hovers between potentiality and actuality in the abode of the body. But that whose existence is in the abode of origination has been granted its perfection and completeness at the very beginning of its existence; it is eternal, and it stands in that state. It is therefore impossible that it should receive any accident, or that anything should overtake it by which it is altered from its existence. And when that is so, then on the view of the author of al-Nusra, that it forgot, forgetting would be an impossible accident for it; so it is void to say of it that it forgot its world.

Chapter Thirty-Two of Part One

Then I say: Forgetting befalls only those souls that are prepared, from the world of nature, to receive knowledge, by the entry upon them of accidents from the causes to which their existence is owed. States then overtake them belonging to that which they follow in their coming to be. These are the richest of souls, when they are trained by the rites of the Law, and when the things prior to them in existence, up to the First Principle, have been grasped by the intellect. We call this an intellect and a second emanation, just as the Follower is a first emanation, according to what we have explained in our well-known book (براحة العقل), Peace of Mind. It is impossible that these souls should come from the world of the intellect, for which there is no evidence whatever. And what the author of al-Nusra himself set down, in the forty-fourth key of his well-known book (المقاليد), al-Maqalid, concerning the impossibility of these souls coming from the world of the intellect, is sufficient as a proof refuting what he said elsewhere in his defense, and confirming what we have set down. I do not know how this escaped him. By my reckoning, the book al-Maqalid was composed after the book of al-Nusra. May God gather its author with our pure masters in the Abode of Holiness, and seal our religion and our world with good, by His grace.

Chapter Thirty-Three of Part One

Then he said: The intellect, by reason of its completeness and perfection, does not forget its world, does not incline toward nature, and does not desire it. This is a correct statement, if it is said of those souls that have acquired their perfection through knowledge and works, and have thereby become attached to the abode of nature and come to resemble the outgoing intellects in completeness, like the souls of the Speakers and the Foundations who stand in their place (عليهم السلام). As for the outgoing intellects, which are the First, the Second, the Previous, the Follower, the Creative, and the Emanated, the First is higher and loftier than to hold any measure for the natural world, let alone to incline toward it; rather, its overflow upon it is continuous, that by it what is not complete may be completed.

Then he said: Were the soul as complete as the intellect, it would have held fast to what it beheld in its world, and would not have desired what is with its effect, so as to entail that it had been altered from its first state; yet it did not hold fast to that state. Enough has already been said on this meaning.

And he said: His words, that it did not desire what is with its effect, amount to affirming that nature is its effect. But how can it be true to say that nature is the effect of souls, and that its existence is by the existence of the persons who belong to the world of nature? Or how is one to conceive that the heavens, the planets, the natures, and the things generated should be from them, when they are confined within persons whom number encompasses? Or how is it true that the complete should desire, in its own states, what is beneath it? That is impossible; and if it is impossible, then it follows that the existence of the natural world is together with the First Emanator, and that the completeness of the existents within it is by his overflow. To expound this is a discourse that lays bare the partisanship of the author of al-Nusra against the author of al-Islah, even though the words of the author of al-Nusra are sound in the manner we have explained, not in the manner he himself intended in his rebuttal.

Chapter Thirty-Four of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said: The Wise One stated, in logic, that all things exist in four senses: either in the essences of things, or in thought, or in speech, or in writing. The things existing in their own essences are those the intellect has encompassed, none of which escapes its encompassing, since there exists nothing that has no essence; and were it not that whatever has an essence cannot elude the intellect, the intellect would not have encompassed all that has an essence. As for the soul, it has encompassed only what thought has first presented to its encompassing; and if among things there is that which has an essence which thought has not first presented for it to grasp, then the soul has not encompassed all that has an essence, and it remains deficient in relation to the rank of the intellect, since it has not encompassed all that has an essence, whereas the intellect has encompassed all that has an essence, and there is nothing that has no essence. This is among the clearest indications of the perfection of the intellect and the deficiency of the soul. This is what he said.

We say: The discourse on this is not well ordered. What the Wise One meant by his words, if indeed he said them, is to show that the existents fall into four divisions. As for their being in the essences of things, he means that among things there is that whose existence is outside the soul, and that has a meaning in its essence by which it is what it is, like the body, whose essence exists outside the soul, together with the meaning by which it is what it is in its essence, which is length, breadth, and depth.

He said: And as for their being in thought, he means that among things there is that which has no essence outside the soul, but whose existence is in the mind, like genus, matter, place, and the circles that are traced in the soul, by way of supposition, from the motion of the spheres, and the like.

He said: And as for their being in speech, he means that among things there is that whose existence is in speech, like propositions of affirmation and negation concerning essences that are not changed by them.

He said: And as for their being in writing, he means that among things there is that whose existence is in writing, and whose existence takes its rule from it, like the divine matters overflowing from the world of the intellect upon the Speakers and the aided ones (عليهم السلام), which, if they are not fixed by composed letters, and their forms not wholly deposited so that the soul may have a conception and an existence through them, their existence and the benefit of them come to nothing.

The Wise One had confined the existents to these four divisions, though under examination the divisions diminish and fall back to what is below them. It is therefore possible that the Wise One meant, by his words, to point out that writing stands, in exposition, in place of what exists in speech; that speech stands in place of the soul in what it wishes to make plain; and that the soul, in turn, stands in place of those things outside it, by conceiving them and taking their place through attaching itself to them, in the sense of becoming their like by acquiring the meanings by which they are what they are. There is nothing in the words of the Wise One that serves as evidence for what he wished to defend, namely the deficiency of the one spoken of, from that great rank. But if the words of the author of al-Nusra are carried upon what is in the world of nature, upon the souls that have risen in rank and become intellects standing in actuality, which are called a second emanation, and which are still standing in potentiality below the First Emanator in the world of Holiness, then he is right.

Chapter Thirty-Five of Part One

Here the author of al-Nusra made it obligatory, in his words, to interpret the saying of the Wise One so that the deficient one has thought; but this is not sound, since his discourse concerns the Follower, of whom the author of al-Islah speaks. For thought, in the souls existing in the world of nature, is a power in them to acquire what they do not yet possess the image of; and the end of this power is that they may become independent of it. The proof is that the soul has no need of thought in what it has already encompassed, whose image is present with it, and in which it stands in actuality; it needs thought only in the things it has not yet encompassed, whose image, by which it stands in actuality and attains the degree of perfection and independence, it has not yet acquired. And since the soul at last becomes independent of thought,

I say: The Follower, from which the overflow and blessing come upon the existents of the world, I mean the things generated, was granted its perfection at the first, and, by what we have already said concerning the perfection it attained at the beginning of its existence, was made independent. And when that is so, the partisanship of the author of al-Nusra against the author of al-Islah in what he discussed has become plain, and it has appeared that his statement is sound only in what is natural, not in what the Abode of Holiness contains.

Chapter Thirty-Six of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said: As for his saying that the deficiency is in the action of the soul, not in its essence, because the action of the soul is completed only through time, this is error and mistake, since it is not necessary that whatever is done in time be deficient. If the cause of the deficiency of things were time alone, then it would be necessary that whatever is done in a longer time be the more deficient, and whatever is done in a shorter time be the more complete; yet we see the contrary in what is observed, for the things that may be the more complete are, the shorter the time, the more complete by that very fact. Were the matter as the author of al-Islah described, it would be necessary that it be the reverse. Time, then, is not the cause of the deficiency of things or of their perfection; rather, the deficiency of a thing comes from the deficiency of the agent, or from the deficiency of the matter, or from the deficiency of both together. This is what he said.

We say: The author of al-Islah did not claim, nor say, that action in time is deficient. Rather he said that whatever meaning is folded within it, and attains its completeness through time and duration, is deficient, and that once it attains it, it is complete; and that everything existing in what is observed has a completeness which, once it receives it, it is complete. It has already been said that the meaning of his words, that the deficiency is in the action of the soul, not in its essence, is that the soul does the act by which it attains the degree of perfection from its own side and acquires the image by which it becomes complete through its overflow. So the words of the author of al-Islah, that the deficiency is in the action of the soul, not in its essence, indicate nothing but that the deficiency is in the receptive essence of the soul, upon which the Follower overflows, so that it cannot receive it all at once, but only through time, days, months, and years. And when it is thus, the words of the author of al-Islah, upon the ground of which he speaks, are sound; and the words of the author of al-Nusra, if they are carried upon natural things, are sound; but he wished to be an advocate for his teacher. May God gather them and us with the masters (عليهم السلام).

Chapter Thirty-Seven of Part One

The author of al-Islah said: The Creator, exalted be He, originated the intellect and time together in a single instant. The author of al-Nusra said: It is known that time is the measure of motion, and motion belongs to the spheres; so it follows from this saying that the spheres, and the time generated from their motions, were together with the intellect in a single instant, and it becomes permissible to say that the located body and the corruptible beings were together with the intellect in a single instant. And if it is permissible to say that the intellect and time were together in a single instant, then it follows, by this analogy, that time receives of the excellences and the lights overflowing upon the intellect the like of what the intellect receives, so that it too is complete, which is impossible. And if it is permissible to say that the intellect and time were together in a single instant, then they cannot but be either two things or a single thing; and if they are a single thing, then a plural wording has been used of them.

It remains, then, to weigh the claim itself and see where it holds and where it fails.

Chapter Thirty-Seven of Part One

The author of al-Islah said: The Most High Creator (al-Bari) originated the intellect and time together, in a single instant.

The author of al-Nusra said: It is known that time is the measure of motion, and that motion belongs only to the heavens. From this it must follow that the heavens, and the time generated from their motions, were present with the intellect at once; and one might equally say that whatever has place, and the corruptible beings, were present with the intellect at once. Yet if it is permitted to say that the intellect and time were together in a single instant, then it follows, by this analogy, that time receives of the virtues and lights overflowing upon the intellect the like of what the intellect receives, so that it too becomes complete, and that is impossible. And granting that the intellect and time were together in a single instant, they are not without being either two things or one thing. If they are one thing, then the plural wording does not apply to a single thing by way of its names, for it is impossible to say that the stone was together with the rock, or the blade together with the knife. And if they are two distinct things, then consider: the intellect, when it apprehended its own essence, did so either without time or with time. If it apprehended its own essence without time, then it was, and time was not. And if it apprehended its own essence only with time, then time is prior to it; but what is prior to the intellect is not time; it is eternity and pre-eternity.

We say: The substance of his statement, though it falls short in its proofs and its demonstration, is that the existence of time together with the intellect is impossible. This is sound in our doctrine, and there has already passed in this book enough of the argument on the impossibility of anything being present together with the first intellect to make plain that the matter stands otherwise than the author of al-Islah set it down in this chapter. Yet the author of al-Nusra erred in his saying that what precedes the intellect is not time but the eternal and the pre-eternal, for there is nothing at all prior to the first intellect, as we have made clear in what has already been said in this book of ours and in the book Rahat al-Aql (راحة العقل). What is sound, and upon which the rule of belief and of the doctrine concerning the eternal and the pre-eternal rests, is that these belong to the world of origination, as we have explained in the epistle known as al-Rawda (الروضة); and in what we set down there is enough to spare us any lengthening here.

Chapter Thirty-Eight of Part One

The author of al-Nusra said, bringing forward the saying of Banidh Qalis (بانذ قليس) by way of argument: that the intellect became complete only because it submitted to nothing except the Creator, mighty and exalted, while the soul became deficient because it submitted to nature.

We say: The philosophers, and the foremost among them, however excellent they were in their own age, remained under the sway of error in much of what they discussed of intelligible things. Had they lived in our time and seen the springs of grace, how they overflow from the House of Revelation with the realities of things as they truly are, in the manner of the Alawite call, then, for all their striving on their own behalf, they would have done themselves justice; they would have held back from much of what they believed about what lies hidden from the senses, and would have rested content with the traces of the one for whom God has set a light in the obscurities of things. For in their own times they followed their own opinions and their own intellects in what they pursued, until each of them arrived, in his belief, only at what his own writings attest, just as the people of the outward do in the Hanafi community when they lean upon their own intellects in treading the paths of religion. So their opinions diverged, and there was disparity among them: some of them were more often right, and some of them were more often wrong. The reliance is upon what the balance of religion bears witness to, which is the horizons and the souls; and in what has already been said upon this, and in the showing that a belief holding the matter to be as they held it is false, there is enough to spare us all contrivance and lengthening. For Banidh Qalis brought forward nothing but what the author of al-Nusra brought forward in his argument, and the saying of Banidh Qalis is more apt to be assailed.

We ask God to illumine our intellects, to seal our affairs with felicity, and to grant us success in good words and in righteous, sound deeds, by His bounty, His generosity, His gentleness, and His grace.
