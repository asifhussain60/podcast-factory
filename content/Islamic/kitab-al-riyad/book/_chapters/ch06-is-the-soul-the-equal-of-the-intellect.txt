# Is the Soul the Equal of the Intellect?

From the book *al-Riyad*, concerning the ruling between the two disputants, in the chapter treating what was said of the soul, which is the first emanator.

**Section One**

The author of *al-Islah* said: the soul is complete in itself, because it was emitted from the First Intellect already complete; it is a complete emanation from the complete, for the Intellect is perfection.

The author of *al-Nusra* said, in rebuttal: he does not know that perfection is better and more complete than the perfect. For perfection is an attribute, while the perfect is what is described; perfection is what is borne, while the perfect is the bearer; and the described and the bearer are better and more complete than the attribute and what is borne. For the perfect is described by perfection and bears it, while perfection is the attribute of the perfect and is predicated upon it. This is what they say.

And we say: what the author of *al-Islah* intended by his words, when he said that the soul is complete because it is emitted from perfection, was not that perfection is an attribute or not an attribute, nor that the perfect is described or not described. Rather, what he intended is that perfection is like the cause from which the existence of the effect comes to be. For he saw that perfection is the cause of the perfect, prior to it in rank, since were it not for perfection the perfect would not be found. The proof of this is that if perfection is lifted away in the imagination, the perfect is lifted away with its lifting, just as with the source-forms from which the past verb, the future verb, the subject, and the object are emitted: when the source is lifted away, whatever should have come to be from it in conjugation is lifted away with it. So he treated the First Intellect, being first in existence and the one from which existing things were found, as the cause, and said: it is perfection. And he treated what he named the emanating soul as the effect, and said: it is the perfect. Since this was the intent of the author of *al-Islah*, his saying that the First Intellect is perfection is true; and since it is true, what the author of *al-Nusra* said in this manner is an overreach against the author of *al-Islah*.

**Section Two**

Then there is the saying of the author of *al-Nusra*, that the perfect is better than perfection. Since the discourse here about perfection and the perfect concerns the First Principle, which is the first effect and the first emanation, and nothing else, his saying would require that the nobility belong to the effect and not to its cause. For the proof is that the perfect, in its being perfect, was so only through perfection, without which it would not have been perfect; and whatever has its being through a thing, that thing is its cause. If this is so, and the perfect has its existence through perfection, then perfection is the cause of the perfect, and the perfect is the effect of perfection. And if the perfect is the effect of perfection, yet he assigns nobility and superiority to the perfect over perfection, then by that assignment he has required the effect to be nobler than its cause, which is impossible. For whatever has its existence through the existence of another, such that when the other is lifted away its own existence is lifted away with it, that existence through which it exists is beneath it in nobility and priority. And since this is impossible, the author of *al-Nusra*, in what he brought forward, has overreached against the author of *al-Islah*.

**Section Three**

The author of *al-Nusra* said a second time: perfection is an attribute and the perfect is what is described; perfection is what is borne and the perfect is the bearer. This he offered as evidence for his saying that the perfect is nobler than perfection, and that the bearer is nobler than what is borne.

We say: this is not sound without qualification. In our own experience, in the realm of coming-to-be and of the second emanation, we find something that is borne yet is nobler than its bearer. Such is the nutritive faculty, which is the proximate cause for the existence of the growing faculty and is its bearer; the growing faculty is a form borne within it and a completion of it, and the growing is nobler than that which bears it. And such is the growing faculty, which is the proximate cause for the existence of the sensitive faculty and is its bearer; the sensitive is a form borne within it and a completion of it, and the sensitive is nobler than that. And such is the sensitive faculty, which is the proximate cause for the existence of the rational faculty and is its bearer; the rational faculty is a form borne within it and a completion of it. And the nobility of this last is not like the nobility of those before it.

Yet from the side of origination and the first emanation it is impossible that the First Intellect should be what is borne, and the second Intellect emanating from it be the bearer, or that the perfect which is the First Intellect should be an attribute of the perfect which is the second Intellect. Since this is so, the inference of the author of *al-Nusra* in his rebuttal, by what he brought forward, is an overreach against the author of *al-Islah*. The meanings of his words in it are true only if they are carried over to the natural things, which are far removed from what the author of *al-Islah* is speaking of, namely the principles of origination and emanation. So the saying of the author of *al-Islah* is sound in what pertains to the world of origination and emanation; and the saying of the author of *al-Nusra*, though he errs in directing it against the author of *al-Islah*, is sound in what pertains to the world of bodies and their accidents.

**Section Four**

Beyond this we say: perfection is a name that falls upon the first thing, which is the first cause, and it is also the first perfect, which is the first effect. Its completeness is by the ascription of its existence to that from which its existence comes, and its completeness is by its ascription to that upon which its own essence rests. This is like origination and the originator on this same order. For origination is not a thing other than the originator; rather it is that by which the originator is itself. And what is it? It is a single essence: when it is ascribed to that from which its existence came, it is origination; and when it is ascribed to that upon which its essence rests, under the mark of coming-to-be, it is the originated. So the First Intellect is perfection, and it is the perfect in truth. It deserves to be the perfect because of the impossibility that anything of its own kind should exist beside it. For the perfect thing is that which has nothing outside itself resembling it in its kind, as with the beings found in the natural realm, let alone in origination. The body, too, is a name that falls upon a thing perfect in its three dimensions, so that nothing like it is found outside it; whatever resembles it belongs to it and is counted from within it. And likewise the sun is complete, for there exists nothing like it in its kind and outside it.

In sum, the existence of what is below the First Intellect, in ascription to it, is a deficient existence. Yet that deficiency is not in its own essence, but in its rank, in ascription to what is above it. For all things are complete in their own essences, since their existence is from the perfection that is the First Intellect, and there is no deficiency in them in themselves. Were they deficient in their own essences, their existence from perfection would be impossible; and it would also be impossible that from the deficient there should come anything other than itself, since the deficient, which is in need of the completing of its own essence, cannot give existence to something other than itself. Deficiency attaches to a thing only when it is ascribed to what is above it in rank, not in its own essence. For the essence, insofar as things are the essences of one same thing, is like the numbers, which are complete in their own essences yet grow deficient only in their ascription to something else by which they come to be. And it is like all the beings of the world, which are complete and lack nothing insofar as they are bodies, but are deficient in respect of their ranks in the forms, and differ when some forms are nobler than others, such as the human being, who is the noblest of all the forms. In itself the human being is complete like the rest, and deficient only in ascription to what is above him. If the matter stands thus in the corporeal realm, then so it is with what is not body on this order, by way of rank, like to like.

Therefore Allah, exalted is He, said:

> سنريهم آياتنا في الآفاق وفي أنفسهم حتى يتبين لهم أنه الحق

We shall show them Our signs in the horizons and within their own selves, until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth (the verse). That is: We shall raise up for them the Imams and the limits, in the outward and the inward, that these may guide them to the state upon which the witnessed bodies stand, and the souls whose actions are found, so that what had been hidden from them, the knowledge of the limits of Allah, which are the truth, may become clear to them, and the truth of the form become manifest.

**Section Five**

The author of *al-Islah* said: what stands in need of perfection is the act of the soul, not the essence of the soul, for the act of the soul is completed only in time, and the emanation of the soul is together with time, just as the First Intellect and time are one, since the First Originator, together with origination and time and perfection, is a single reality. So the Fashioner originated all the realities at a single stroke. And the soul, though it needs to draw benefit from the First, is not by that deficient in its essence, just as the First was not deficient though it stood in need of the matter. Completeness and deficiency appear in the act of the soul, not in its essence.

The author of *al-Nusra* said: he did not know that the Intellect stands in need of nothing among things in the way the soul needs to draw benefit from the Intellect. The relation of the Intellect to the matter is not like the relation of the soul to the Intellect, since the matter, from the side of the Intellect, is nothing other than the Intellect; their identity is one identity. It is impossible to say that a thing needs what it itself is; need of one thing for another arises only when the other is distinct from it. This is what they say.

We say: the saying of the author of *al-Nusra* is a reply to what the author of *al-Islah* maintained, namely the need of the Intellect for the matter only. And since the discourse concerns the principles that stand outside the world of nature, the saying of the author of *al-Nusra* in this is nearer to the truth than the saying of the author of *al-Islah*. For that of which it is fitting to say that it stands in need of perfection, and that it is completed only in time because it is an emanation from the world of nature and not from outside it, is the souls, which do not attain the degree of perfection, I mean the ascent to the degree of the second completion, except in time, whose existence is an emanated existence. So if the saying of the author of *al-Islah* is carried over to this sense, it is sound.

**Section Six**

As for the saying of the author of *al-Islah*, that the Intellect and time are one, because the Originator, together with origination and time and perfection, is a single reality, this is not sound. For time is the number of the motions of the degrees of the sphere, the imagined span between the motion of the first degree of it and the motion of the degree that follows, until the first returns to where it was. But origination, which is the Intellect, and its perfection, is prior in existence to the sphere by whose degrees' motion the existence of time comes to be. To attach the existence of time to the existence of the First Intellect, and to hold this as belief, is contrary to what stands in the law of the guiding call. We have already set forth, concerning the nature of time and of what is prior to it in existence, and the evidence for it from the deposits of the prophetic law, in our book known as *Rahat al-Aql*, what is sufficient for the seekers.

**Section Seven**

The author of *al-Nusra* said: were it not that the Intellect denies divinity of itself, the one that follows would have no way to carry its knowledge past the rank of the Intellect to the Originator. What deficiency is plainer than this, that he knows the Originator only by way of the First?

We say: this may not be held of the one that follows. For the power that runs through all the noble beings in the world of unity grants them the knowledge that they have an Originator; and were it not for that power, no thing would have existence or subsistence, and it would fall to nothing. For this reason every thing deserves the name of oneness, and though it be manifold in itself, its share of that power is not withheld, and the justice within it is plain. It is impossible to conceive that a single individual of the human kind should not know that it has a Creator, or should fancy that it has no Creator, let alone that great and majestic Limit. Rather we say: he apprehends that from which his existence comes as the true Object of worship, even while there is within him a directing toward the divine. Then, if we apply to the First Intellect what the author mentioned, and to the one that follows what he said, it would be obligatory that the Foundation, whose standing is in the world of the body, stand in the place of the one that follows in the world of the Intellect, since without the Speaker one would not know that one has a God. But the matter is not so, for each of the two has a portion from Allah; the Speaker is prior to him only in rank, by his nearness to the First and his being later than it. And therefore Allah said:

> لا الشمس ينبغي لها أن تدرك القمر ولا الليل سابق النهار وكل في فلك يسبحون

It is not for the sun to overtake the moon, nor does the night outstrip the day; and each swims in an orbit. All of them, from the side of their circling, are one, yet in their ranks they differ.

**Section Eight**

The author of *al-Nusra* said: and enough of the deficiency of the deficient is that all the ancients set down the soul in the place of the form, and the Intellect in the place of prime matter and matter, and said that the forms forever yearn and long toward the matters, and that the matters have no subsistence without the forms.

We say: his saying that the soul is a form is true; but his saying that the Intellect is in the place of prime matter and matter, since the discourse was upon the First Intellect, is an overreach against the author of *al-Islah*.

For in holding the author of al-Islah to that, I say: the word "intellect" is a shared name, and what the author of al-Nusra said is not true of what is supra-natural, namely the first creative and emanating Intellects. It is true only in the world of nature, of the other intellects: the intellect in potentia, the intellect passing from potentia to actuality, and the intellect in act. If the discourse was upon the supra-natural, then he has carried what is said of the natural onto what is supra-natural, and his contradiction was a pressing wrong.

He said that forms are forever longing and yearning toward the matters, and that the matters have no subsistence apart from the forms. This is not sound, for the forms do not exist as longing toward matter for the sake of matter, nor are they what matter acquires. Rather it is matter that yearns for the forms in order to exist, for the sake of the forms; and matter is what acquires the forms, matter being related both to the baser form and to the nobler form. If that is so, and he has run the Soul in the course of form and the Intellect in the course of matter, and has ruled that matter has no subsistence apart from form, then it is as though he has made the existence of the Intellect to be in the Soul. How then can such a belief hold for that whose existence was an originative existence? Or how can it be imagined that the Intellect is the cause of the existence of the Intellect and the Soul, when the discourse is upon the supra-natural, which stands outside the world of nature? Not so. It is known that the Next has its existence through the Prior, not the Prior its existence through the Next. This is a manifest fallacy, intended only to lead him to believe what is said about the natural things. As for what is in the world of origination and the first emanation, the matter of the existents of the abode of nature, from the Intellect and the Soul, which are of the order of the second emanation, is only as he mentioned, and from that standpoint it is sound.

On the matter of emanation, the author of al-Nusra said: the Intellect is the first mean, the Soul is the second mean, and the first mean is nearer to the Cause than the second mean. The nearness and rank of one thing to another can arise only from perfection and nobility, and distance can arise only from deficiency and want of nobility.

I say: this statement is not sound with regard to the creative and emanative Intellects. To be a mean requires two ends, and it is not admissible to say that the First Intellect is a mean unless its existence be such that on one side it has an end and on the other side an end, so that it stands in a like state in existence on both sides. How then is it admissible to say of that which is the first of the ones that it is a mean, when nothing precedes it in existence such that it might stand intermediate between that and the two terms? Even to suppose the First Intellect a mean is impossible, because its existence is a first existence, and what is a first existence is not called a mean. The mean deserves the name of mean only by having beyond it something of its own kind of existence, so that there is an affinity between the two, and likewise something after it, suited to it in some respects, so that it has an affinity with the one from one face and with the other from another face, and it has two ends and is intermediate between them. As for the First Intellect, there is no affinity at all between it and its Originator, exalted be His mention, nor is it of the kind of His existence, so that it might be a mean between Him and what is below it. Nor is there any occasion of its being a mean between it and its Originator. That which is between it and its Originator, by which it was caused to exist, is something else; and what is called the mediator between it and what is below it is the very essence of origination, the Cause that is said to be originating and caused, as I have shown in the Illuminated Epistle (الرسالة المضيئة) and in our well-known book Rahat al-Aql (راحة العقل). If that is so, there is no sense in the inference the author of al-Nusra draws here in support of what he wished to establish, for what he has already advanced concerning deficiency and nobility is sufficient. Deficiency attaches to the Next only in relation to the First Intellect, not in itself, for its essence stands with no deficiency in it and possesses perfection, while in relation to its predecessor it is deficient in rank, as with the Speaker, the Foundation, the Sun, and the Moon. Each of these, the Foundation and the Moon, is complete in itself, lacking nothing; yet when set beside the other that is its counterpart form, the Speaker and the Sun, it is deficient in rank. The author of al-Nusra binds the Next to deficiency by his relating it to its predecessor, and the author of al-Islah binds its essence to completeness because it emanates from the Perfection that is its cause. So the saying of the one is sound on its face, and the saying of the other is sound on its face also, except that the author of al-Nusra presses upon the author of al-Islah from where he did not intend and from what was not his aim.

The author of al-Nusra said: if he says, "I do not mean, speaking of the author of al-Islah, that the Intellect is like the Soul in the matter of completeness," we say to him: then it is either superior to it or more deficient than it; and he does not hold that it is superior to it, so it remains for him to say that it is more deficient.

I say: the author of al-Islah may well not say what the author of al-Nusra has undertaken to report of him. Rather he says: all things are complete in their essences, and none of them merits any deficiency except in relation to what is higher in degree than it, so that there is found for the higher a rank not found for this thing related to it. And since its dependence is upon that, the aspect of the wrong pressed upon him is made plain.

The author of al-Nusra said: among the clearest proofs of the deficiency of the Soul and the completeness of the Intellect is the saying of Allah the Exalted:

( ومن آياته الليل ، والنهار ، والشمس ، والقمر )

"And among His signs are the night, and the day, and the sun, and the moon."

The night and the day stand for the two Foundations, and the sun and the moon for the two Origins, the sun of them for the First and the moon for the Next. There is no dispute among the people of knowledge and wisdom that the moon is more deficient in light than the sun, and less in light and radiance, and that it draws its light from the sun, while the sun bestows the light upon it. This is his saying.

I say: it has become clear by his own saying that the moon is more deficient in light than the sun, and less in light and radiance, and that it draws its light from the sun, and that the deficiency attaches to it only by its being related to the sun, while its essence has no deficiency in it. For were it deficient it would not have received the light from the sun; and because its essence is complete it stood ready, by receiving from the sun what it is able to receive. When the speech of the author of al-Islah is upon the completeness of the essence, and the speech of the author of al-Nusra is upon its deficiency in its states and accidents by which its perfection comes, then there is a difference between the two sayings, and the author of al-Islah becomes as one wronged, being pressed with what was neither his aim in his saying nor his intent.

The author of al-Nusra said: among the things that also indicate the deficiency of the Soul is that the sages set the Intellect in the place of the spirit to the Soul, and set the Soul in the place of the body to the Intellect. They did this only because they knew of the deficiency of the Soul and the perfection of the Intellect, so they likened the Soul to the deficient thing and likened the Intellect to the perfect thing.

I say: by this saying he has added a clarification to what I said, that the deficiency is not in its essence but in something else, in the states, even though the sense of his words holds only for the beings of the second emanation. For the acquiring belongs only to the souls that are in the world of nature, that they may attain their second perfection, while in its essence the Soul is complete and whole, save that it is deficient in respect of the intelligibles it does not possess, which run to it in the course of the spirit to the body. As for the second, which is the first emanation, its way is not the way of these souls, because its perfection and completeness were given at the beginning of its existence, not through time and acquiring, but only by a deficiency in relation to the predecessor in rank, and by precedence and posteriority.

The author of al-Nusra said: even if there were no deficiency in the Soul but that nature falls upon its horizon, still the Soul has two extremities, one extremity toward the Intellect and one extremity toward nature; and the Intellect is not so, for its two extremities are one toward the Word and the other toward the Soul, and nothing of it clung to the dark nature, on account of its completeness and perfection. Had an extremity of the Soul clung to nature, because nature falls upon its horizon, there would be in that something which, when the observer contemplates it, he stands upon the reality of the deficiency of the Soul and the perfection of the Intellect. This is his saying.

I say: this statement proceeds without order, for the falling of nature upon the horizon of the Next, which he named Soul, does not earn it deficiency. Rather it indicates a completeness in it, in that nature moves toward it because nature is deficient in relation to it, while it, in relation to nature, is noble and complete by its being singled out for what nature is not singled out for, together with the fact that its own essence, I mean the essence of the Next and the essence of nature, are both complete, in that they emanate from the Perfection. As for his saying that the Intellect has two extremities, one toward the Word and the other toward the Soul: the Intellect is not a thing other than the Word, nor is the Word a thing other than the Intellect. Rather the essence is one and the entity is one, and it is by relation that it merits the names whose meanings are scattered, as has already been said in this sense when the assertion of its being a mean was mentioned, which is not hidden. And as for his saying that nothing of it clung to the dark nature on account of its completeness and perfection, while an extremity of the Soul clung to nature because nature falls upon its horizon: it is not the Next that clung to nature and loved it and desired it. Rather it is nature that loved it, and nature is that which moves toward it and joins itself to it. To believe that, and to imagine that an extremity of the Next clung to nature, is an error. The best of ways to turn the meaning of his saying toward soundness is that his aim in saying it was that the Next pours forth upon nature, so that its overflowing is what he called clinging.

The author of al-Islah said: what is deficient is the act of the Soul, not the essence of the Soul; and it is its act that is perfected, not its essence.

The author of al-Nusra said: this statement is contradictory, because if the act is deficient, that cannot but be either from the deficiency of the essence of the agent, or from the deficiency of the matter from which the act of the agent proceeds, or from the deficiency of both. If the deficiency of the act is from the deficiency of the essence of the agent, then he has affirmed the deficiency of the Soul, since it is the agent. And if that is from the deficiency of the matter, then he has affirmed an eternal matter, and this is a departure from the doctrine of religion and an inclining toward the doctrine of Abu Zakariyya al-Razi and those who held the pre-eternity of prime matter. I have suspected of the author of this book that he inclined to that, because he made time eternal along with the Intellect, and that is the substance of his doctrine. And if the deficiency of the act is from the deficiency of both, then he has held both sayings together, and affirmed the deficiency of the Soul, since it is the agent, and affirmed the pre-eternity of prime matter. Whichever he says, he has said: the Soul is deficient. This is his saying.

I say: the saying of the author of al-Islah, and the explanation of his aim in what he said of deficiency and completeness, has already gone before. The saying of the author of al-Nusra in what he said is sophistry and pressing and a going out from the bound of knowledge. For he said first, after he had divided the matter of deficiency into the three mentioned divisions, that if the deficiency of the act is from the essence of the agent, and the agent is the Soul, then the Soul is deficient. Yet the author of al-Islah has made clear that deficiency is not incumbent upon the essence of the Soul, which is the Next, but upon its act in the sense of its object, and so he is freed from this division. He said, second, that if the deficiency is from the essence of the matter, then he has affirmed an eternal matter; but the Perfection and the perfect one, which are the First and the Second, have their pre-eternity affirmed as a pre-eternity that is not first and original, so how much less the deficient? If that is so, then the deficiency is only in the matters that receive their completeness, not in that from which completeness is received. So it is well known in the natural sciences that deficiency arises from the side of the recipients, not from the side of that which is received from, as the heat of the sun, which the bodies apt for swift affection receive, like wax, which is softened by it, while other bodies refuse to receive it, like stone and iron and the like, which are not swiftly affected. The source received from is one, the heat is one, and the deficiency is in what is other than it. He said, third, that the author of al-Islah inclined to the doctrine of Abu Zakariyya in the manner he described, and that is a going out from the bound of knowledge and a transgression. So he did him no justice, neither in the framing of the division nor in what followed it, when he charged him with both sayings together, the deficiency and the pre-eternity of prime matter.

The author of al-Nusra said: as for his pleading that the Soul became complete only because it emanated from the First Intellect, which is a perfection, and that what emanates from the perfection can only be complete, this is not true. For it does not follow of necessity that the Soul be complete because it is emanated from the complete Intellect; and were that necessary, then what emanates from the Soul would also be complete, namely prime matter and form, and what emanates from prime matter and form would be complete too, until it reaches the individual, indivisible persons, so that they all become complete. From this saying it follows that the whole world is complete, with no deficiency in it, nor in anything of it.

I say: the sum of his saying is that, were the Soul complete by its being emanated from the First Intellect, which is the Perfection, then prime matter and form, by their being emanated from the Soul, would be complete too, and likewise all that is emanated from the whole of prime matter and form, by his claim, down to the individuals, and the whole of what is in the world would be complete, with no deficiency in it. I have already made clear, in what has gone before, the sense the author of al-Islah aimed at in his saying that the Soul is complete, and the sense of the author of al-Nusra in this section.

The author of al-Nusra held that the Soul is complete because it emanated from the first Intellect, which is perfect, on the ground that what emanates from perfection cannot itself be other than perfect. Yet what I have already set down against this is not an exhaustive treatment, and I must now complete it. For the Soul is not emanated from the Soul, nor are the individuals, as he claimed. The Soul is indivisible, whereas the individuals are all fragmented into their parts; and prime matter is brought into being together with the Soul, out of what precedes it in existence, not out of the Soul itself. The proof of this is that the Soul, which is the second in rank, does not have a first existence. Nothing exists alongside it that shares its own kind of existence, and nothing stands above it in rank but the First, which is the first Intellect. Its existence is a second existence, and whatever has a second existence cannot exist except with something of its own kind existing alongside it, so that the two are partners in kind.

That from which this Soul, the second, came into being is the first thing, which nothing of its kind precedes, and it is one in its existence. This was for the sake of the transcendence of God, exalted be He, who brought it into being free of the relations, additions, and multiplicity, and free of all that a thing is described by according to its divisions. For those very relations and additions in the acting causes are what make it possible that more than one thing should come to be from a cause, since multiplicity in effects follows the relations belonging to their causes. But since God, exalted be He, is too transcendent to be preceded by any other, or to have another exist with Him, or to be differentiated by attributes, relations, and additions, and since He, exalted be He, is the Originating Maker and the Cause that has no cause, it is impossible, with His purity, His holiness, and His exaltation above multiplication, that anything should come to be from Him except one.

This one, which is the first Intellect, stood in two relations. The one is its being a cause for the existence of what is below it; the other is its being an effect, since it is a thing originated and invented. It followed necessarily that what comes to be from it should be two, according to these two relations in its essence. What came to be from the first relation is the second Intellect; and what came to be from the other relation is prime matter, from which are the celestial spheres and the rest of the existents of the world of body. Then, when the existent passes beyond the one into coming-to-be, it can only be two. For after the rank of the one in existence there is nothing but two, and after the odd there is nothing but the even, which is multiplicity. Since the first existent in the number of existents is one, what follows it, when the Intellect apprehends it, is two, which is the even, or multiplicity. And since it is two, one of the two is what we name the Soul, and the other is what we name prime matter, from which are the spheres, the stars, and what is below them.

Then, since the foundation of the religion and its ordinances were laid upon the balancing of the existents and their correspondences, so that their arrangement should indicate the existents among the ranks that are hidden from the eyes, and since the Book and the Law came to be by way of the Speaker, and the establishing of the Foundation stood as the interpreter of them both, again by way of the Speaker, this was the clearest indication that what came to be from the first Intellect, which is in its own world as the Speaker is in the world of body, is the second, which is the Soul and prime matter together, just as the Book and the Foundation came to be by way of the Speaker. Bearing witness to this is what the Prophet did in joining together the Book and the Progeny, whose existence is from him, when he said:

> إلا أني تارك فيكم الثقلين كتاب الله وعترتي، حبل ممدود من السماء إلى الأرض، طرف منه بيد الله وطرف منه بأيديكم، فتمسكوا بها لن تضلوا ما إن تمسكتم بهما، وقد سألت ربي أن يردا عليّ الحوض كهاتين

I am leaving among you the two weighty things: the Book of God and my Progeny, a rope stretched from the heaven to the earth, one end of it in the hand of God and one end of it in your hands. Hold fast to them, and you will not go astray so long as you hold fast to them. I have asked my Lord that they be returned to me together at the Pool, like these two. And he joined together the two forefingers of both his hands. Then he said:

> ولا أقول كهاتين

I do not say like these two, and he pointed with the forefinger and the middle finger of one hand, the one of them going before the other, and said:

> ناصرهما لي ناصر، وخاذلهما لي خاذل

Whoever aids them has aided me, and whoever forsakes them has forsaken me. His likening of the two, in existence, to his two forefingers was because neither goes before the other. So he set himself, in the abode of body, in the course of the first Intellect in the abode of origination; and he set what he brought of the Book, and what he joined to it of his Progeny, who is the Foundation, together with the rest of the Imams (عليهم السلام), in the course of what came to be from the first Intellect as the second and as prime matter, in the manner I described in the book Rahat al-'Aql. Thus prime matter does not display its nobility, nor does the goodly form that lies within its potency appear, except by the power of the Soul, which is the second, and by the shining of the light upon them both. Just as the Book and the Law do not announce their own selves, nor make manifest the wonders of the wisdom within and beneath them, except by the interpretation of the Foundation and the one who stands in his place. Prime matter, then, has its existence from the first Intellect together with the second.

Then I say: I have said that the first Intellect has nothing of its own kind of existence alongside it because its existence, which came from that which is above it, is nothing but a first existence, and the First cannot be other than one. Beyond this, were it otherwise, the paths of declaring the divine unity would be thrown into disorder. For if it had a likeness in its existence, then the existence of the two together would be caused, and neither would go before the other, and this would require something preceding them both, and what their existence comes from would be an impossibility. For the existence of two-ness is a being-made-multiple in essences, and multiplicity in essences does not have its existence from a multiplicity in essences; rather it has its existence from what precedes it, which is one in essence from one side and multiple in meanings from another side. And that which is one in essence yet multiple in meanings has its existence not by its own essence but by another which is not multiple, neither in meanings nor in essence. It is thereby made clear that if the first Intellect had alongside it something of its own kind of existence, the two would be things brought to be from a one preceding them, and the existence of that one would be by its being multiple in meanings, not in its essence, but by what precedes it and renders it one. And since there is nothing above the Intellect, it is void that it should have a likeness of its own kind of existence. So it is established that it is one. In the establishing of the Intellect as one lies the being-two of what comes from it, namely the Soul and what is named prime matter. And since the Soul and prime matter come into existence together, in the way I have shown, it appears that the saying that prime matter appears out of the Soul is an error.

The author of al-Nusra bound himself to a claim of deficiency in prime matter and form, and of deficiency in the world and all that is in it. I say: prime matter and form are both complete, and the world with all that is in it is whole and complete, and its parts in their own essences are complete and whole. The proof is that the complete is that outside of which there is found nothing of its own kind of existence. The world, which is the sum of prime matter and form together with all the parts of body, and body, which in its own essence is complete in its three dimensions, has nothing whose limit binds it save that it is within it and not outside it. So it is complete; and since it is complete, the position of the author of al-Nusra, that the world should be complete, is refuted, and the impossible follows from him. And if the impossible follows, his words are mere overreaching. It is enough, against him, to cite what he himself said in his book known as al-Iftikhar, in the chapter of the Command, as an indication of the perfection of the world, where he said: the mark of the world's being sufficient in its totality, needing nothing besides itself, is the absence of any other, and the sufficiency of wisdom with what is in the totality of the world of richness and perfection. I do not know how this escaped him. Perhaps he composed the book al-Iftikhar after the book al-Nusra. As for the disparity found among the parts of the world, on account of which the author of al-Nusra fastens deficiency upon it, that is only by the addition of some parts to others in respect of their forms and their states, which is the first perfection already present. Among the parts of the world are those given their first perfection, and their second, at the outset; and among them are those given their second perfection only at the last, by acquisition. So the discourse has made it plain that the deficiency the author of al-Nusra speaks of belongs only to the later states that attach to the natural essences and appear when some are added to others, whereas the complete that the author of al-Islah speaks of is only the essences whose existence is a first existence.

The author of al-Nusra said: if it were necessary, because the Soul is emanated from the Intellect, which is the perfect, that the Soul be complete, then it would likewise be necessary, because the Soul is emanated from the Intellect, which is the first intermediary and the first originator, that the Soul be an intermediary like the First, and an originator, so that the Soul attains of the excellences what the Intellect attains, and likewise the whole world and the individuals. This is his saying.

I say: the demonstration of the impossibility of saying that the Intellect is the first intermediary has already been advanced. In sum, what he has brought forward is a corrupt proposition. For when a thing has its existence from another, in the sense that one of them is a cause and the other an effect, it does not follow that the effect shares in every one of its states, such that its identity would be its identity and its due its due. Were that so, nothing could be said of it except what is said of that very identity, and it would not be found to be more than one, and it could not then be said that it came to be from it in that it is itself. Yet neither does it follow, from its not sharing in all its states, that it should not share in some of its states. For if it did not share in some of its states, that one would not be a cause of its existence, nor would it bestow existence upon it. And since the way of the existence of things, in their being-made-multiple, is upon this, it is necessary that the effect resemble its cause in one respect and differ from it in another. Since that is necessary, it follows that the second, which is named the Soul, being emanated from the first Intellect that is the cause of its existence, must be a likeness of it in one respect and differing from it in another. And once it must be a likeness of it in one respect and not a likeness of it in another respect, it appears that it is not necessary, when the existence of the second, which is named the Soul, is from the Intellect, that it be like the Intellect in all its states, and it does not follow that what is said of the Intellect, in respect of its being the cause, should be said of the second which is named the Soul. Thus the saying of the author of al-Nusra, that if it were obligatory it would be obligatory, is driven back.

The author of al-Nusra said: this has many parallels among things witnessed, such as the thought and the particular soul. For the particular soul that is in us stands in the place of the Intellect, and the thought stands in the place of the Soul. And the thought, though it is emanated from the soul, may not be said to be like the soul, because the thought apprehends what it apprehends part after part, and time after time, whereas the soul apprehends the whole of what it apprehends without time. This is his saying.

I say: the soul, I mean the soul of the human being, has acts, and each act of it has a name proper to it. When it seeks the apprehension of some thing, that seeking is named thought. When it observes what has come to be in its essence of the forms of the things known, that observing is named memory. When it imprints its essence with what it hunts of the marks of knowledge, whether by way of inference or by way of the senses, that imprinting is named conception. And when it knows that outside itself there are things it desires to conceive, that desire is named yearning. Since it has acts, and the thought is an act of it that does not pass beyond its own essence into having an existence other than its existence, how can it be sound to liken the thought to the second, which is named the Soul and which has an existence other than the existence of the Intellect? And how can it be said that it apprehends part after part, when the apprehending belongs only to the essence of the soul by its act, which is named the thought, within its own essence? Or how can the apprehending by the soul, I mean the soul of the human being, be of the whole without time, when it has no way to hunt the objects of knowledge except by way of the senses and by inference, passing from the side of their particulars to their universals in time? And further, from the thought's not being permitted to be called like the soul, it does not follow as obligatory that the second Intellect, which is named the Universal Soul, should not be like the first Intellect. For the thought among the souls of human beings does not run in the course of the second Intellect from the first Intellect. The second Intellect, which is a second existence from the origination, subsists by its own essence, though in existence it is a rank below the First; whereas the thought has no existence set apart from the soul, but is the very soul itself, and it has no subsistence by its own essence. For every one of the soul's acts has a cause on account of which it needs to act, namely its being in the world of nature; so that when it parts from that world, these acts cease to be in it by the ceasing of their cause, and nothing remains subsisting except the essence conceiving as pure intellect. So he has run the second Intellect, named the Soul, from the first Intellect in the course of the thought from the soul, and the existence of the thought is not an existence other than the existence of the soul which he likened to the Intellect, nor does it have of its own essence the apprehending of the soul. It is as though he has denied that the second Intellect, named the Soul, should have an existence.

If the idea is not from the souls of human beings, then the Second Intellect runs the same course as the First Intellect, the First Intellect being the Second Intellect, which is the second existence of origination, self-contained, even though in existence it stands below the first rank. And the idea has no existence unique to itself apart from the soul; rather it is the very identity of the soul, and it has no subsistence of its own. For every act of the soul there is a cause on account of which it needs to act, and that cause is its being in the world of nature. If it is separated from that world, these acts are nullified by the nullification of their cause, and what remains is only the perceived self as pure Intellect. Then he made the Second Intellect, called the Soul, proceed from the First Intellect in the very way that thought proceeds from the soul, so that the existence of thought was no existence other than the existence of the Soul which he likened to the Intellect, nor did thought have of its own essence any apprehension of the soul. It is as though he had denied that the Second Intellect, called the Soul, has any existence other than the existence of the First Intellect, or any perception like the first perception. And that is impossible. For what follows has an existence other than the existence of the First, and a perception like the perception of the First. It is not fitting to say that it is creative, just as the First Being does not deserve to be called emanated. Both are pure intellects; each has encompassed itself and preserved itself in itself, and they are similar in one respect and dissimilar in another, as the discussion has already made clear.

If this is so, then there is no sense in likening the idea to that which he likens it to, and no sense either in making the human soul stand in the place of the First Intellect, nor in making the idea within it hold the rank of the Universal Soul. For the honor of the human soul, and its passage from potency to act, lies only in acquisition, and acquisition comes only from the senses as work and from conception as knowledge. If the honor of souls lies in the acquisition by which they conceive through thought and reasoning, and the idea belongs to souls only that they may be honored by it, then it is as though he had made the cause of the honor of the First Intellect to be the Second Intellect, called the Soul. And that is an inverted proposition. For the honor of what follows, and its rank, come from that which precedes it in existence, which is the prior; because the honor of the prior, and its rank, are ordered by what comes after it. The ruling upon that whose existence is a later existence in perfection and completion is that it should be the reverse of that whose existence is a first existence, so that the one does not run in the course of the other in this respect, nor the other in the course of the one, even if they share on some other side. Yet the author of al-Nusra has judged that whose existence was first by what belongs to that whose existence is later, and that is among the impossible.

Chapter Twenty-One of Part One.

The author of al-Nusra said: One of the clearest proofs of the deficiency of the Soul and the perfection of the First Intellect is that the ancients and the author of al-Islah all said that the act belongs to the Second, and they attributed nothing to the First, because action is ever a sign of deficiency and need, while the abandonment of action is evidence of perfection and self-sufficiency. That is to say, action is only for drawing a benefit, or repelling a harm, or for vain sport.

Abu Zakariyya said: That the Initiator of the act is the Soul by way of play and vain sport, the people of religion do not maintain. So there remains only the drawing of a benefit or the repelling of a harm, and wherever this is found, there is deficiency. The Soul, in its action, seeks to draw a benefit, which is its reaching the goal appointed for it, and for that reason it begins to act. The Intellect, since there is no rank above its own, and since it is complete and perfect, does not move to action in any way. The Soul, since it found a rank above itself, remained in motion, persisting in its quest. By this method we speak concerning the deficiency of the Soul and the completeness of the Intellect. This statement is clear, and praise be to Allah.

The gist of his saying in this chapter is that the Second is the doer, that action is only for drawing a benefit or repelling a harm, that the drawing of benefit and the repelling of harm belong only to deficiency, and that the Intellect, since it is not deficient, is at rest from action; the cause of its rest being that there is no rank above it for it to seek. The Soul, since there was a rank above it, moved toward it to attain it.

We say: had the words of the author of al-Islah been about the Soul that is the soul of human beings, the saying of the author of al-Nusra would have been the more fitting statement. But since his words were about that which is called the Soul, the saying of the author of al-Nusra, and his inference from the state of human souls, that they act only to draw a benefit, to repel a harm, or for sport, as grounds for invalidating his statement and refuting his ruling that the Following is complete and perfect, is a manifest prejudice. For it has already been said in sum that what is said of that whose existence is a first existence, in respect of completeness and perfection, is not to be said of that whose existence is a later existence; nor is what pertains to the later to be said of that whose existence was first.

And the statement of the author of al-Nusra concerning the act and its divisions is not sound, for not every act is for drawing a benefit, or repelling a harm, or for sport. There may be an act whose benefit returns upon the object of the act, not upon the one from whom the act issues. The proof of this is that heating is an act of fire, yet this act does not return upon the fire, and no benefit or harm accrues to it; rather the benefit and the harm return upon whatever the heating, which is its act, reaches. So too the act of the sun in illuminating the world, and the enabling of human beings to dispose of their affairs by their will through it, and, by its rising and by the coming to be of plants, animals, and minerals through its heating, its benefits return upon others and not upon itself; it neither benefits by this act nor is harmed. So too the act of the whales that are in the Sea of Oman, and others called the dolphin, whose way it is, when a drowning man is sinking in the sea, to aid him in escaping and to push him toward the shore; that act does not return upon them with benefit or with harm, but the benefit reaches the one acted upon.

If this is so, and is found among natural things that do not reason, that they perform such wondrous acts while being deficient in their states and dependent upon others in their action, then what is there to deny in the pure, abstract, perfect intellects, granted their perfections at the very first of their existence, that they should perform acts greater than these acts which appear from natural things? Their perfections require it, according to the nearness of their ranks and the loftiness of their noble grades, not for drawing a benefit to themselves nor for repelling a harm from themselves, but for the sake of others. So these acts are acts of wisdom, not acts of drawing benefit, nor of repelling harm, nor of sport. No indeed, the perfect power from which the perfect, well-ordered act issues is only in the perfect.

Then the Following, which he named a soul, is not in motion as he claimed, so that there should attach to it what attaches to natural things; for that motion belongs only to the bodies that have it and to their natural forms. It is of a rank higher than natural things, and it is of a rank below the Intellect, as we have made clear in the Book of the Repose of the Intellect. So by what we have brought forward his overreach and his argument have become plain, for they do not hold in the case of the one spoken of. And the saying of the author of al-Islah stands sound, that the Following is complete in completeness, as we have explained in what preceded. It is not permissible to apply to what is in the abode of origination, of the First and Second Intellect and the higher letters, the terms of motion and rest, for we have shown that these two belong only to what is a body or possesses a body, and in this there is sufficiency.

Chapter Twenty-Two of Part One.

The author of al-Nusra said: The consequence of the saying of the author of al-Islah is that it is the act of the soul that is completed, not the essence of the soul, while the soul, in his view, is complete; so that the act of the soul becomes a soul, and this is a plain and evident impossibility. For acts have never been seen, in observation, to become agents. An example of this is the writer and the writing, the builder and the building; for writing does not become a writer, nor does building become a builder, but rather the man, who is a writer in potency and a builder in potency, becomes a writer and a builder in act. And so we say of the soul and the intellect: the Intellect is complete in act, and the soul is deficient in act, subsisting in potency, and it comes to subsist in act when it gains completeness. As for its act becoming complete, that is impossible.

We say: the aim of the author of al-Islah in his saying that it is the act of the soul that is completed, not the essence of the soul, is that which attains the second perfection from its side, that which flows from it in the manner of what it pours forth upon, so that it issues from potency to the bound of act. If the aim of his saying, that the act of the soul is completed, is what issues by its overflow from potency into act and attains the second perfection, and if nothing crosses the bound from potency to act except by that which already subsists in act, then it is not to be denied, when that which subsisted in potency issues into act and attains the second perfection through acquisition, that it should be like that which subsists in act in the perfection given to it at the first of its existence. Thus they share in that both subsist in act, even though each of them attained what they share in not from the side from which the other attained it. Nor is it impossible that they be two likes in respect of their sharing, for it is known that everything gathered with some other thing in a meaning among the meanings is, in that meaning, its like, by the witness of what our statement yields: that everything issuing from the bound of potency to the bound of act subsists in act, and every subsistent in act subsists in act by the occurrence of the second perfection to it, and everything issuing from the bound of potency to act has the second perfection occur to it. If the subsistence of what subsists in act is by the occurrence of the second perfection to it, and the subsistence of what subsisted in potency and then came to subsist in act is by the occurrence of the second perfection to it, then they share and become alike in that both attained the second perfection, even though on some other side they differ. And since what we have brought forward is neither impossible nor to be denied, but rather necessary, the saying of the author of al-Nusra is nothing but overreach.

Chapter Twenty-Three of Part One.

Then the author of al-Nusra said: This is a plain and evident impossibility, for we have never seen acts, in observation, become agents, given what he brought forward concerning the matter of the writer and the builder, by a proof that testifies to the impossibility of what he mentioned as impossible.

We say: acts are in truth the agents. For the writing which he mentioned to be the act of the writer is the very thing that converses with its reader and addresses him. Had the writing, in its forms and its tracing, while being an act of another, not been an agent upon the soul of its reader, so that it is as though it speaks for its agent and interprets him and inscribes him by its forms, its reader would not come to know what is in the soul of its writer. And the building, by its form which the builder brought into being out of what was in his soul, is that which imparts its form to the beholder by its shape and its situation, until he conceives it. Had the form of the building not subsisted in act so as to impart it to the soul of the beholder, which takes on its forms, its beholder would not have conceived it. So too the act of the striker, which is the striking, is the agent that produces the pain in the one struck and pains him, in the manner we mentioned in our two well-known epistles, al-Rawdah and al-Inarah. And the saying of God the Exalted is a firmer proof still that acts are likewise agents, where He says, exalted be the Speaker:

(وان من شيء إلا يسبح بحمده)

"And there is nothing but that it glorifies Him with His praise." For the thing is the act of the Maker, exalted be He, and while being the act of an agent, it is His glorification. And His saying, exalted be He:

(وأرسلنا الرياح لواقح)

"And We sent the winds fertilizing." For the winds are a thing, and the fertilizing is His act. By the clarifying of this it has become plain that the protest of the author of al-Nusra against the author of al-Islah in this chapter is impossible.

Then the aim of the author of al-Nusra in his saying that we have never seen acts, in observation, become agents, is that the very acts have not become identical with their very agent in observation, just as writing has not become identical with its writer, nor has the building become identical with its builder. This is a correct saying, as he said it, for the act does not become identical with that from which the act issued; rather it is an agent like its agent in respect of the act's issuing from it, even though the ranks differ and the relations vary. But the author of al-Nusra wished to defend the one he stands in for by overreaching against the author of al-Islah, may God sanctify their two souls, and truly many associates wrong one another.

Chapter Twenty-Four of Part One.

The author of al-Nusra said: He likened the intellect and the soul, in their completeness and their deficiency, to the man and to the drop of seed, because the drop of seed is in truth the form of the man in potency, and it becomes upright, a man like the man. Likewise the soul is in truth the form of the intellect, and it becomes complete like the intellect when it gains from the intellect the benefits appointed for it. This is his saying.

We say: his saying, that the drop of seed is in truth the form of the man in potency, and likewise that the soul is in truth the form of the intellect and becomes complete, is not sound. For the drop of seed is not the form of the man, nor is the soul that is in potency the form of the intellect. The drop of seed is a matter prepared for conception, empty of forms, which it acquires under conditions; and likewise the soul is only a natural disposition, void of forms, whose nature it is to acquire the forms of the things known, so that its essence is completed by them. Were the soul in truth a form of the intellect, it would follow that the intellect must have that for which the form is a form, so that it would be one thing, and that for which the form is a form would be another thing. How can this be sound in what is of an originated existence, in which otherness is found only by relation while the identity is one? No indeed. This manner of speaking and likening holds only for what is natural, not for what is external, separate from bodies.

Chapter Twenty-Five of Part One.

The author of al-Nusra said: And again, the rank of the rational one in the corporeal world is like the rank of the intellect in the world.

We say, in answer to him: the Second is like the First, even though it stands in a rank below it, just as the Foundation (asās) is like the Speaker (nāṭiq), even though it stands in a rank below him. That Second is complete in its very existence. Nothing remains for it in the form of a degree to be attained later, by which it would afterward acquire completeness, just as the Foundation is complete and whole in its rank, with no further degree left for it to ascend to and so acquire a completeness it did not already possess in itself.

The deed of the Prophet is enough as a witness to what we have said, and his own indication points to it, in what he made obligatory when he required that the camel be sacrificed by piercing the throat (naḥr) rather than by cutting it (dhabḥ), and that other animals be slaughtered by cutting rather than by piercing. For the piercing of the throat is the driving of the iron to the head of the fuʾād of the pierced beast, so that whatever turbid blood is there may issue out. This stands for the degree of full attainment, the contemplation of the secrets, and the dispelling of doubts and confusions within the ranks. The cutting of the throat stands for the taking of the covenant and the release into the call, and the pierced beast stands for the Foundation.

It is as though the Prophet, by displaying this act, informed the community that followed him. The proof of his role is this: that the Foundation was not set in his place in the summons to the inner call, which is the completion of the worship of God that leads to the affirmation of God's oneness and the knowledge of His limits, until he first taught the Foundation what its perfection consisted in and made it aware of what its completeness consisted in. So he raised its degree by the imparting of the report, and its way of attaining perfection and completion is not like the way of the rest of his community, who receive the report and the completion only afterward, after the covenant has first been taken and instruction has second been given. The Prophet did this with his Foundation only to indicate that the Second, which stands over against the Foundation in the world of the Intellect, attained perfection first. There is no inconsistency in taking the covenant first from the children of the call and then conveying to them the degree of perfection. The conveying is only a pointer, an indication that the natural souls, which are below the Second, are at first in potency, and then issue forth by acquisition, at the last, into act, which is their perfection and completion, so that in each of these matters one may hold the belief that is fitting to hold.

Nor did he stop at this indication. He indicated the completeness of the Second from another side as well, in the two units (rakʿahs) of the dawn prayer, which stand for the First and the Second. In the first unit, which stands for the First, he made it obligatory to recite the glorification, the praise, and the sūra; and in the second unit he made obligatory the recitation of al-Qunūt in place of the glorification recited in the first, so that the second unit should be in the same state as the first with respect to perfection. Their only distinction, then, is in the matter of rank in existence: this one is the first of existence, like the first unit, and that one is the second of existence, like the second unit.

**Chapter Twenty-Six of Part One**

Then we say, in answer to his statement that, were it permissible to say the soul is as perfect as the Intellect, it would be permissible to say the Foundation is like the Speaker, and that this is not right: how could it fail to be right, when the Foundation is the one who stands in the place of the Prophet and his family? Were it to fall short of standing in his place, and were it not sufficient, complete, and whole like him, God would not have made obedience to it obligatory. How could God, exalted be He, impose obedience to one who is like others in deficiency? That cannot be, nor is it permissible in wisdom. For if the Messenger is a messenger to all who live in his time and to all who are born after him, and a proof against His creation, then it cannot be that the community is left without one like the Messenger present among them, so that the proof would stand against them without warrant. The Foundation, therefore, is like the Speaker after him from one side, and unlike the Speaker from another side. It is proof enough that the difference between them lies in rank and not in essence that the objector could argue only from rank, in his words, "because the rank of the Foundation is below the rank of the Speaker." It is now clear from what we have set out that the position of the author of *al-Iṣlāḥ* stands secure against the objection of the author of *al-Nuṣra*, and that his saying "the Second is deficient" is sound only in the particular sense in which it must be understood.

**Chapter Twenty-Seven of Part One**

The author of *al-Nuṣra* said, by way of argument:

Bowing (rukūʿ) is upon the Foundation, and prostration (sujūd) is upon the Speaker. The supplication in bowing,

> سبحان ربي العظيم وبحمده
>
> Glory to my Lord the Great, and to Him be praise,

is the purification of the pure one who bows, from the side of his greatest cultivator (murabbī), who is the Second. And the supplication in prostration,

> سبحان ربي الأعلى
>
> Glory to my Lord the Most High,

is the purification of the Speaker, from the side of his cultivator, who is the Second; and the Most High is the one who is upon the Prior, since his highest cultivator is the Prior. The Speaker (ع) has mentioned this meaning in the revelation, in His saying,

> ثم دنا فتدلى
>
> Then he drew near and came down.

We say:

The statement of the author of *al-Nuṣra* that the rank of the Prior is higher than the rank of the Second is a sound statement, and it is what the author of *al-Iṣlāḥ* also said. Yet the fact that the rank of the Second is below the rank of the Prior does not indicate any deficiency in its essence. As for his saying that the Prophet and his family reached, in standing upon the rank of the Prior, a completeness the Foundation did not reach, this can scarcely be sound. For the Foundation, even though by itself it did not reach that degree, reached the degree of completeness by means of the Speaker, through his teaching and through his making it aware of the blessings that were sent down upon him. By its receiving this from him with a total reception, its soul was illumined and became complete, as the disc of the moon becomes complete in light on the fourteenth night of the month.

The conduct of the Prophet and his family bears witness to this, in his placing the sermon before the prayer on the day of Congregation (Friday) and delaying it after the prayer on the day of the Feast of Breaking the Fast. For God made Friday, in one of the faces of interpretation (taʾwīl), stand for himself, since it is one of the seven days. His ascending the pulpit for the sermon comes first, and then the prayer, signifying that he attained the degree of perfection and completion and rose to the highest ranks of honor from the side of the two principles: he was first, and then he called to his sacred law, to the acceptance of his revelation, and to obedience to his Foundation. And he made the day of the Feast stand for his Foundation, for it is one of the days of God, exalted be He, on which, through obedience to Him, the religion of God is completed. His placing the prayer before the sermon signifies that his Foundation stands under obedience to Him and under the acceptance of His command and His law: first it received perfection and completion, and rose in the ranks of honor, and by the mediation of the Speaker it was made aware of the degree of full attainment. So the Successor (waṣī) is complete in his essence, even though his attainment of the degree of completeness is from the side of the Speaker and his rank is below the rank of the Speaker.

Nor is the existence of the female a deficient existence, such that one might argue from it in support of what the objector wishes to support. Rather, its existence is a complete existence. For if the female were deficient on account of what she lacks that is proper to the male, then the male too would be deficient on account of what he lacks that is proper to the female, and it would follow that the male's existence is not a complete existence, which is absurd. Since, then, the male is complete in himself and the female complete in herself, and it is only by the joining of the one to the other that their ranks come to be unequal, so that the female is below the male in rank, this is proof enough that the disparity is in ranks and not in essences. So too the Second has not reached the rank of the Prior. As for his saying that the Foundation did not reach this rank: the statement of the author of *al-Iṣlāḥ* is therefore sound, that the Second is complete.

**Chapter Twenty-Eight of Part One**

The author of *al-Nuṣra* said:

Among the proofs of the deficiency of the soul and the perfection of the Intellect is that many of the speaking souls submit to nature, and are led by it and by what it commands them, and incline to its desires and pleasures, and forget their own knowledge, light, and splendor, and find the world of nature, its forms and traces, pleasing, and remember nothing of the splendor of their own world and its luminous delights. This is because of their deficiency; and because of their deficiency they crave the desires and pleasures found with nature and turn away in aversion from their own world, its delights, its goodness, and its splendor. But the Intellect, on account of its perfection, its goodness, its splendor, and its completeness, does not forget the delights of its own world, its goodness, and its splendor. It does not incline to nature, nor crave the pleasures and desires found with it, nor look toward it; and if it does look toward it, it looks only from the side of compassion, mercy, and disdain for what nature has, informing nature, out of itself, that what nature possesses is little when set against what is in its own world. Had the soul been complete like the Intellect, it would have held firm upon what it beheld in its own world and would not have craved what is with its effect. This is what he said, arguing that many of the speaking souls submit to nature and are led to whatever it commands, and incline to it on account of their deficiency.

We say:

The submissive, inclining thing, led by self-indulgence and pleasures, following the desires of nature and abandoning acts of worship, is the sensual soul (al-nafs al-ḥissiyya), for it belongs to nature. It is not the speaking soul (al-nafs al-nāṭiqa) that has become attached to it. The sensual soul is only brought around and restrained from committing base and vile acts when the traces of the Intellect work upon it, by way of the bonds of discipleship, through instruction, so that it holds the soul back from perpetrating what would destroy it. These souls are not the Second that issues forth from the First Intellect, of which the author of *al-Iṣlāḥ* speaks. There has already passed enough in our discourse to imply that the Second is other than these souls, and these souls other than it, and that the Second is of such completeness and perfection that nothing remains in its essence unattained, so that it should be deficient by it, as is the case with these souls, which are at first existent in a first perfection and have a second perfection that they attain through time and acquisition.

**Chapter Twenty-Nine of Part One**

We say, secondly, that the Second, which is complete and whole, and which was granted its perfection and completeness at the very beginning of its existence all at once, is existent, and it is through it and through its overflow that the deficient thing comes to receive the degree of perfection. The proof of this is that the existents in the world of nature are at first in potency and then, at the last, in act; and that which stands in potency has no passage into act except through the existence of that which already stands in act. Since there is no passage from potency into act except through the existence of that which stands in act, and since that which passes into act is existent, the existence of that which stands in act is a necessary existence. And if the existence of that which stands in act is a necessary existence, then in its existence it must be either the First Intellect or another.

It is ruled out that it should be the First Intellect, because of the impossibility that the First Intellect's own act should exist except through the existence of another, and because that would require of the Intellect that there be alongside it something existing outside it, so that it might act upon it. But the First Intellect is self-sufficient in its act, needing nothing outside its own essence to act upon, being the complete one whose existence is from Him from whom it came to be, exalted and magnified, an existence that is one, having no parallel in its existence and no other beside it, so that this other might be present with it for it to act upon. For were that so, the Worshipped, exalted be He, would become no longer Worshipped. Enough has already passed in our discourse upon this, and more than what we have set out here, in our book known as راحة العقل (Rāḥat al-ʿAql, The Repose of the Intellect). That which stands at this station of oneness has nothing present with it for it to act upon, and so no multiplicity issues from it except by its acting in its own essence. And since its act is in its own essence, and its act is what brings the potential thing forth by it and by its overflow into another, it is ruled out that the one through which the existents pass from potency into act should be the First Intellect. And since it is not the First Intellect, it is established that it is other than it, and is the Second.

The truth of what we say is borne witness to by this: that the Prophet and his family did not need, for all the greatness of his rank and his completeness, any other of mankind to complete in his place his work, which is the revelation and the sacred law.

...he had no need of any other among mankind to complete his deed in his place, the deed that is the revelation and the law. In that uniqueness, being himself and issuing forth from himself according to what he derived from that which granted him his degree, the Prophet (ع) found what he recited and prescribed it in its perfection. So he conducted himself, in a bodily mode, in the descent (tanzīl), along the course of the First Intellect, in that same creative manner, so that his action in its rank would lead whoever contemplates it to the soundness of belief, to knowing what is hidden from the senses among the principles of origination (ibdāʿ) and renewal (inbiʿāth), so that the path of monotheism (tawḥīd) would remain unaltered.

As for the Foundation, even though he stands at the highest degree of perfection, he did not possess that degree by which he could be alone in his action, ordering the affair of the call (daʿwa), the interpretation (taʾwīl), the teaching, and the guidance by himself. So he did not dispense with what he found alongside him, which is the revelation and the sharīʿa. I do not mean that the Foundation dispenses with this, that is, with the revelation and the sharīʿa, nor that the Speaker dispensed with something existing alongside him in the bodily world through which he acts, as the Foundation does. For that reason his action follows the course of the Second.

What obtains in him is the very thing I have mentioned as proof of knowing the rank of the Second. The Prophet (ع) and his family follow the course of the First, who is the perfect one, dispensing with anything other than himself in his action and employing nothing other than himself. The Foundation follows the course of the Second, in whom there is no action without the revelation and the sharīʿa, without which he does not dispense in his action, just as the Second does not dispense with that through which its action comes to be. The subordinate community follows the course of what lies beneath the sphere, in the natural world, in which its existents pass from potentiality to actuality through the overflow of the Second, since the souls that stand in potentiality need that by which they attain the degree of arrival and perfection, which comes from the call of the Foundation.

Just as the Second is the one who supports the one prepared for acceptance from the world of nature by its addition upon it, so too the Foundation, and whoever stands in his place in the call, is the one who brings the willing and obedient to the degree of arrival and completeness. For the Speakers, being from the world of nature, did not attain their completeness and their lofty degree from the side of the First Intellect, but from the side of the Second Intellect first, just as none of us reaches the degree of arrival from the side of the Speaker, but from the side of the Foundation's call first. So they run their course from those above them as we run ours from them, as the saying goes:

> حذو النعل بالنعل

sandal matching sandal, the one exactly as the other. The perfection of the Speakers and of those who stand in their place is from those above them, and our perfection is through them. May God gather us with them. The discourse has thus made plain that these souls are not the Second, so that whatever is found of their conditions is evidence of their deficiency, and that the objection of the author of al-Nusra against the author of al-Islah on this ground is an impossible objection.

**Chapter Thirty of Part One**

He said:

The Second is deficient because it forgets its world and does not remember it. How can this be sound, when forgetting occurs only after knowledge? If these souls had existence before these individual persons, and were knowing, it would be impossible for change to come over them by their coming into the abode of nature, after they had dwelt in majesty and attained the perfection that is intellectual knowledge. For the existence of what exists in the world of origination and emanation, among the intellects, is an existence resembling what is necessary in the bodily world, upon which no change befalls of what it was. For this reason it is called eternal, and how can conditions come over what is eternal, so that knowledge belongs to it at one time and is stripped from it at another? The abode in which it dwells is not the abode of change that he has affirmed for it by his word "forgot."

> نسي

In sum I say: if these souls came to these persons from that world, and came in order to learn and to be trained in the sciences, then their state of ignorance at the outset of their existence in this world is not free of one of two cases. It is either from their essence or by accident. If they are ignorant in their essence, then they are barred from knowing anything, and ignorance is intrinsic to them, which is impossible, for we find that the soul comes to know after not knowing. And if it is by accident, then that accident is not free of having befallen them either in that world or in this one. If it befell them in that world, and that is the abode of eternity and completeness, not the abode of change and deficiency, then it is impossible for an accident to befall them by which they are altered. And if it befell them in this world, and their end is to know and to return to the majesty they were in before change befell them, then that is impossible in wisdom, since their remaining in the first state is more fitting for them than their being altered from it and then returning to it. So it is void to hold that they accept an accident, whether ignorance or otherwise, in this world, given that their existence is not from this lower world. For their existence not being from this lower world is impossible to reconcile with this, since through the completeness that is in them the benefit derived is the completeness that is the blessing of that world, "for the people of knowledge," not of this world. If that is so, and it is impossible that their ignorance be from their essence or by an accident whose origin is from that world, then the claim that their arrival is from that world is void; and once it is void that their arrival is from that world, it is void to say that they forgot their world.

**Chapter Thirty-One of Part One**

Then, if their arrival is from the world of origination, their existence is first of all on the side of origination and emanation from the highest relation, and whatever has this manner of existence stands in actuality. What stands in actuality is barred from accepting an accident, since the acceptance of accidents follows the way that things standing in potentiality are divided, that is, whatever hovers between potentiality and actuality within the abode of the body. But that whose existence is in the abode of origination has been granted its perfection and completeness at the very beginning of its existence, and it is eternal. Whatever is in this state, its condition is such that it is impossible for it to accept an accident, or for anything to come over it by which it would be altered out of its existence. If that is so, and it is the view of the author of al-Nusra that it forgot, forgetting being for it an impossible accident, then the claim that it forgot its world falls away.

**Chapter Thirty-Two of Part One**

Then I say: forgetting befalls only the souls prepared, from the world of nature, to accept knowledge, through the entry of accidents upon them by way of the causes from which their existence comes. States then come over that which they follow in their coming to be. And they are the richest of souls, when they are trained by the rites of the sacred law and grasp intellectually the things that precede them in existence, up to the First Principle. This we call the intellect and the second revival, just as the Second is the first revival, according to what I explained in my well-known book Rahat al-Aql. It is impossible for these souls to arrive from the world of intellect, for which there is no proof at all. It suffices, from what the author of al-Nusra himself mentioned in the forty-fourth key (iqlīd) of his well-known book al-Maqālīd concerning the impossibility of these souls arriving from the world of intellect, as an indication of the refutation of what he said in his defense, and of the soundness of what I have set forth. I do not know how that escaped him. As I reckon it, the book al-Maqālīd was composed after the book al-Nusra. May God gather its author with our pure friends in the abode of holiness, and seal our religion and our world with good, by His grace.

**Chapter Thirty-Three of Part One**

Then his saying, that the intellect, in its completeness and perfection, does not forget its world, does not incline toward nature, and does not desire it, is a sound saying, if it is said of the souls that have acquired their perfection through knowledge and works, so that they became attached in the abode of nature and came to resemble the intellects that have gone forth in perfection, like the souls of the Speakers and the Foundations who stand in their place (عليهم السلام). As for the intellects that have gone forth, which are the First, the Second, the Preceding, the Following, the Originator, and the first Emanated, these are too high and too exalted for the natural world to have any measure with them, let alone any inclination toward it. Their overflow upon it is only continuous, so that through it what is not yet perfect may be perfected.

Then his saying, that if the soul were complete like the intellect it would have held fast to what it beheld in its world, and would not have desired what comes about in its effect, is an affirmation that it was altered from its first state and did not hold fast to it; and enough has already been said on this meaning.

And his saying, that it did not desire what is with its effect, is an affirmation that nature is its effect. But how can it be sound to say that nature is the effect of souls, and that its existence comes about through the existence of the persons who belong to the world of nature? And how can one conceive that the heavens, the planets, the natures, and the generated things are from them, when they are confined to persons whom number encompasses? And how can it be sound that the complete one should desire, in its conditions, what is beneath it? That is impossible. And if it is impossible, then it necessarily follows that the existence of the natural world is with the first Emanated, and that the perfection of the existents within it is by its overflow. To explain this is a matter that lays bare the bias of the author of al-Nusra against the author of al-Islah, even though the words of the author of al-Nusra are sound in the manner I have explained, not in the manner he intended in his rebuttal.

**Chapter Thirty-Four of Part One**

The author of al-Nusra said:

The Sage mentioned in logic that all things exist in four senses: either in the essences of things, or in thought, or in speech, or in writing. The things that exist in their essences are those that the intellect has encompassed, none of which escapes its encompassing, for there exists nothing that has no essence, and if it were not the thing that has an essence it would have escaped the compass of the intellect. So the intellect has encompassed all that has an essence. But the soul has encompassed only what thought has presented for it to encompass. And since among things there is that which has an essence which thought has not presented for it to encompass, the soul therefore has not encompassed all that has an essence, and it remains deficient below the rank of the intellect, since it has not encompassed all that has an essence, whereas the intellect has encompassed all that has an essence, and there is nothing that has no essence. This is among the clearest of things regarding the perfection of the intellect and the deficiency of the soul. This is his saying.

I say: the discourse in this matter is not well ordered. What the Sage meant by that saying, if indeed he said it, was to show that the existents are of four divisions.

The first, that a thing is in the essences of things: what he means is that among things there is that whose existence is outside the soul and which has a meaning in itself by which it is what it is, like the body, whose essence exists outside the soul, and the meaning by which it is what it is in itself, which is length, breadth, and depth.

And he said, in thought: what he means is that among things there is that which has no essence outside the soul, but whose existence is in the mind, like genus, matter, place, and the circles that are traced in the soul from the motion of the celestial spheres by supposition, and the like.

And he said, in speech: what he means is that among things there is that whose existence is in speech, like propositions by affirmation and negation concerning essences, essences that are not changed thereby.

And he said, in writing: what he means is that among things there is that whose existence is in writing, and the ruling of whose existence is by it, like the overflowing divine matters from the world of intellect upon the Speakers and the sustained ones (عليهم السلام), which, if they are not fixed by the composed letters and their forms are not wholly deposited so that the soul may have through them a conception and an existence, their existence and the benefit of them fall away. The Sage had restricted the existents to these four divisions, although upon inquiry the divisions diminish and fall back to what lies beneath them. It may therefore be that the Sage wished by that saying to indicate that writing serves as a deputy, in exposition, for what exists in speech; that speech serves as a deputy for the soul in what it wishes to expose; and that the soul likewise serves as a deputy for those things outside it by conceiving them, standing in their place by its attachment to them in the sense of becoming like them through the acquisition of the meanings by which they are what they are. There is nothing in the words of the Sage that would be a proof of what he wished to support, namely the deficiency of the one spoken of below that great degree. If the words of the author of al-Nusra were carried upon what is in the natural world, the souls that have risen in rank and become intellects standing in actuality, called the second revival, which are still standing in potentiality beneath the first Emanated in the world of holiness, then he would be right.

**Chapter Thirty-Five of Part One**

Here the author of al-Nusra has made it obligatory, in his saying interpreting the words of the Sage, that the deficient one has thought; and that is not sound, given that his discourse is upon the Second, of which the author of al-Islah speaks. For thought, in the souls existing in the world of nature, is a power belonging to them for acquiring what they do not yet have the form of. The end of this power is that it be dispensed with, by the proof that the soul does not need thought regarding what it has already encompassed, whose form it has obtained, and in which it stands in actuality. It needs thought only in the things it has not yet encompassed, whose form it has not yet acquired, the form by which it stands in actuality and attains the degree of perfection and self-sufficiency. And when the soul at last dispenses with thought,

We say: the Second, from which the outpouring and the blessing descend upon the existents of the world, by which I mean the newborns, gave its perfection first and was enriched by what we have already set down concerning the perfection it attained at the very beginning of its existence, so that it became richer still. If this is so, then the partisanship of the author of al-Nusra against the author of al-Islah stands exposed in what he argued, and it becomes clear that the soundness of his words holds only for what is natural, not for what the Holy House contains.

The author of al-Nusra said: as for the one who held that the deficiency lies in the action of the soul and not in its essence, on the ground that the action of the soul cannot be completed except in time, this is a mistake and an error, for it does not follow that whatever is done in time is deficient. Were the cause of deficiency in things nothing but time, it would have to follow that whatever takes a longer time is the more deficient, and whatever takes a shorter time is the more complete. Yet we observe the opposite in what is witnessed, for the things that are more complete are often those accomplished in a shorter time, and would be the less complete for taking longer. Had the matter been as the author of al-Islah described, the case would have to run to the contrary. Time, then, is not the cause of the deficiency of things or of their perfection. Rather, the deficiency of a thing comes from the deficiency of the agent, or from the deficiency of the matter, or from the deficiency of both together.

We say: the author of al-Islah neither claimed nor said that the action itself is deficient by being in time. What he said is that whatever attains its completeness only through time and duration is, until then, deficient, and that once it attains it, it is complete, and that everything present in what is witnessed has a completeness which, once received, makes it complete. He had already advanced the point that the meaning of his words, that the deficiency is in the action of the soul and not in its essence, is this: that the soul should do that by which it attains the degree of perfection from its own side and acquires the form through which it becomes complete by that soul's outpouring. His saying that the deficiency is in the action of the soul and not in its essence indicates nothing other than that the deficiency is in the receptive essence of the soul upon which the Second pours its outpouring, an essence that cannot receive all at once but only across time, days, months, and years. If it is so, then the words of the author of al-Islah, taken upon the ground on which he speaks, are sound, and the words of the author of al-Nusra, if they are carried upon the natural order, are also sound. Yet he wished only to be an advocate for his teacher.

حشرهم الله وایانا مع الموالي صلوات الله عليهم

May God gather them and us together with the loyal, the blessings of God be upon them.

The author of al-Islah said: the Most High Creator, al-Bari, originated the intellect and time together in a single act. The author of al-Nusra said: it is known that time is the measure of motion, and motion belongs only to the spheres, so it must follow from this saying that the spheres, and the time generated from their motions, were present with the intellect in a single act. It would then be permissible to say that the bodies capable of place and the corruptible beings were present with the intellect in that single act as well. And if it is permissible to say that the intellect and time were together in one act, then this analogy compels us to say that time receives of the virtues and the lights poured upon the intellect just as the intellect receives them, so that time too would be complete, which is impossible. Moreover, granting that the intellect and time were together in one act, they are not free of being either two things or a single thing. If they are a single thing, then a plural expression does not apply to a single thing by its names, for it is impossible to say that the stone was with the rock, or the tide was with the knife. And if they are two distinct things, then the intellect grasped its own essence either without time or with time. If it grasped its own essence without time, then it was without time; and if it grasped its essence only with time, then time precedes it, yet what the intellect precedes is not time, but eternity and pre-eternity.

We say: the substance of his words, though wanting in proof and demonstration, is that the coexistence of time with the intellect is impossible, and this is correct in our doctrine. We have already established that it is impossible for anything to be co-present with the first intellect, and that suffices for the point, so that the matter is other than what the author of al-Islah stated in this section. But where the author of al-Nusra said that what precedes the intellect is not time but the eternal and the pre-eternal, he erred, for nothing at all stands ahead of the first intellect, as we have shown in what has gone before in this book of ours and in the book راحة العقل. What is correct, and upon which the law of belief and doctrine rests concerning the eternal and the pre-eternal, is that it belongs to the world of origination, as we have explained in the epistle known as al-Riyad, and in what we set down there is enough without further length.

The author of al-Nusra said, adducing the saying of Banidhaqlis (بانذ قليس) in support of his argument: the intellect became complete because it submitted to nothing except al-Bari, the Mighty and Majestic, while the soul became deficient because it submitted to nature.

We say: the philosophers, and the foremost among them, virtuous though they were in their own age, remained under the sway of error in much of what they discussed of the intelligibles. Had they lived in our time and seen how the springs of grace overflow from the House of Revelation with the realities of things as they truly are, according to the Alawite call, then, for all their striving on their own behalf, they would have done themselves justice, would have withheld judgment upon much of what they believed concerning what lies beyond the senses, and would have been content to follow the traces of the one to whom God has granted a light in the hidden depths of things. For in their times they followed their own opinions and intellects in what they pursued, so that each of them arrived in his belief only at what his own writings indicate, just like the people of the outward meaning in the upright creed when they leaned upon their intellects in treading the paths of religion. Their opinions diverged, and among them there was disparity: some struck the truth more often, and some erred more often. The reliance is upon what the balance of religion attests, namely the horizons and the souls; and in what has already been said concerning this, and in the showing that such a belief as they held is false, there is enough without affectation or length. So Banidhaqlis brought forward nothing but what the author of al-Nusra brought forward in his argument, and the saying of Banidhaqlis is more fit to be set against as a reproach.

ونسأل الله ان ينور عقولنا ، ويختم بالسعادة أمورنا ، وللخيرات والاعمال الخيرات الصالحات يوفقنا ، بمنه وجوده ولطفه وكرمه

We ask God to enlighten our intellects, to seal our affairs with happiness, and to grant us success in good deeds and in righteous, virtuous works, by His grace, His bounty, His kindness, and His generosity.

Now to the adjudication between the two disputants concerning the intellect that is the first origination.

The author of al-Islah said: motion and stillness are two effects united in the First and the Second. For when the First originated, it became united with origination, and that union had two effects within it in potentiality. We say two effects because origination and the originated are two, the one being the essence of origination and the other the essence of the originated; and origination and the originated, though two, are yet not two but one, which is the whole. These two effects are present in the First in potentiality; then the Second is emitted from the First, and that emission has two effects within it by the intellect, the one being the essence of the emission and the other the essence of the emitted. So the two origins are united by the command. One of the two effects is motion, and it is higher than the other, which is stillness, just as one of the two origins, namely the First, is higher than the other, which is the Second. Motion is the First's bestowing upon the Second of all the existents, and stillness is the Second's reception of the benefit from the First of all the existents and its comprehending of them. I do not say that motion and stillness are generated from the soul by the power derived from the intellect. Rather I say that they are two effects of the soul, by the power derived from the intellect, in matter and form, and it is for this that matter and form became the foundation of this world.

The author of al-Nusra said: this saying contains some raving, for motion occurs only for the sake of a thing sought. As for the motion of bodies, it is a seeking of places, as the motion of fire upward seeks its place and the motion of earth downward seeks its place; and the motion of spirits is a seeking of benefit. Whenever a seeking is established, a need is established there, for seeking arises only from some need. It is impossible to say that the intellect is in need of a thing so that it should move toward it. And even were it permissible to ascribe motion to the two origins, its ascription to the soul is more fitting than to the intellect, since the soul needs to draw benefit from the intellect, moving toward it with the motion of longing, the motion of the effect toward its cause. If it is established that motion belongs to the soul, then stillness belongs to the intellect, for the intellect is still in what it receives of the virtues and lights poured upon it by its principle, seeking no increase, because its principle has poured upon it of the lights and radiance a measure beyond which the intellect knows no increase that it should move to seek. Yet the intellect has not poured upon the soul the whole of what was poured upon itself, but only a part of it, so that the soul might rest in what was poured upon it and grasp it, and has withheld with itself a part, so that the soul might move in seeking it. So motion arises only from need, the intellect is not in need such that it should move, the ascription of motion to the soul is more fitting than to the intellect, motion belongs to the soul and stillness to the intellect, and the intellect has not poured upon the soul all that it has but only a part, so as to be the cause of the soul's motion in seeking the rest. This is the sum of what both of them say.

We say: as for the saying of the author of al-Islah that motion and stillness are two effects united in the First and the Second, that in the First they are in potentiality and in the Second in actuality, this affirms that the First is at rest and in motion in potentiality, since motion and stillness are in it in potentiality, and that the Second is in motion and at rest in actuality, since motion and stillness are in it in actuality, and that is impossible from several sides. The saying of the author of al-Nusra in this, that the intellect is not in need and therefore is not in motion, is correct, because motion in all its kinds is found only in natural bodies. The first and second intellects, being a first existence, are not bodies, and so it is not admissible that they be in motion or at rest. The proof is that a body requires that it be matter and form together, and that the one need the other in existence, so that its existence is not a first existence from God, glorified be He. For that whose existence is a first existence from God, glorified be He, is an essence with no otherness in it, being the very act which is the first existence from Him, exalted and magnified, and the essence of that act is one essence, not two. If its existence were not a first existence, another would have to precede it in existence. So it follows that if the intellect were a body, another would precede it in existence, and it is void that anything precede the first intellect in its existence, since it is the very essence of the act which is the cause and the origination, and behind it there is nothing but God, the Originator, the Bringer-into-being, exalted be He, from whom is its existence. And if it is void that anything precede it in existence, it is void that it be a body; and if it is void that it be a body, and there is no motion except in what is a body, it is void that there be motion in the intellect; and if it is void that there be motion in it, the saying of the author of al-Islah that the intellect has motion in potentiality is annulled.

The author of al-Islah said: we said two effects because origination and the originated are two, the one being the essence of origination and the other the essence of the originated, and origination and the originated, though two, are yet not two but the whole. This is a form that may not be believed, for the matter in origination and the originated is other than what he mentioned. They are not two. Origination is the originated, and the originated is origination, and there is nothing there but one. When it is referred to that from which its existence comes, it is origination, being an act belonging to that source and being the essence of the act; and when it is referred to its own self, it is the originated, being an effect of another from whom its existence comes.

Then, upon his saying that motion and stillness in the First are in potentiality and in the Second in actuality, we say: that whose existence is a first existence is nothing but pure act and the very truth of act. Were it admissible that motion and stillness be in the First in potentiality, then the ruling of the Second would be nobler than that of the First, since the ruling of what subsists in actuality, in nobility, is not as the ruling of what subsists in potentiality, and it is impossible that the Second precede the First in nobility. What he mentioned belongs rather to the attributes of that whose existence comes later, which needs, in its coming forth, both act and something already subsisting in actuality. The first intellect is intellect in actuality, and so likewise is the second, and what follows it of the intellects that are separate from matter. It is not admissible to hold, of what necessarily belongs to an existent of the realm of nature and is proper to it, that it applies to what lies outside that realm among the high limits.

The author of al-Islah said: motion and stillness are two effects of the soul, by the power derived from the intellect, in matter and form.

What is meant is that the bodies, which are matter and form taken together, are moved, that which is moved among them, by the natural force whose presence within them comes from what lies outside the first and second intellects. This is a motion of longing. So when he placed the two effects, the mover and the moved, in matter and form, the claim taken in that way is impossible; yet it is sound in the sense that what is learned from the intellect is learned because its existence is from the intellect. Understood thus, it is correct, and this is the best interpretation to which his words may be referred.

In the fifth section of Part Two, the author of al-Nusra said: Motion belongs to the soul and stillness to the intellect, so it is not permissible to believe such a thing of those transcendent limits.

We say in summary: The world of origination, which gathers the Preceding and the Following, and the pure intellects, which are the upper letters separate from bodies, are higher and more exalted than that motion or stillness should be imagined in them. For motion, in whatever is moved, comes only from a mover within it that is other than itself, and by that mover the existence of its essence subsists. And when a thing's essence exists only through the existence of differing things, its essence is an essence of deficiency and need. But the Following, which he named the soul, belongs to the world of origination, which is distinguished by completion and perfection; its essence is not an essence of deficiency or need. So it is void that there be within its essence any differing thing; and if it is void that there be upon its essence what is other than itself, then it is void that it be other than itself, and void that it be moved, and void that there be motion in it.

Moreover, motion is that by which the thing in which it resides is found passing from its first state to a second, whether in its essence or in its states. But the Following, which he named the soul, has an eternal existence, since it falls, by way of origination, in the Second, being an intellect. And since it is eternal, its transfer from that upon which it was first found is impossible, so it is void that there be motion in it. What is said of stillness is exactly what is said of motion: the First intellect has no stillness, and the Following, which he named the soul, has no motion.

Rather, by what he said the author of al-Nusra intended those souls and intellects that are in the House of Nature, sent forth by a second emanation; the generated thing is moved, according to what we explained in our well-known book Rahat al-Aql. For motion and stillness are proper to the world of body and what is in it, since they are among the causes of its perfection, and not to what belongs to the House of Origination. But he transferred the meaning, pressing it too far into the deficiency he inclined toward in support of his sheikh. May God house them in the abode of His honor, and gather them, and us, with our pure masters (عليهم السلام).

In the sixth section of Part Two, the author of al-Nusra said: Motion is for need, and it is more proper to the soul than to the intellect.

It has already been shown from the foregoing words that motion and stillness befit only what is a body or is in a body. The First and the Second of whom we are speaking are not a body, so the attribution of motion and stillness to them is barred, as we have shown earlier in our well-known book Rahat al-Aql. His statement holds true of what is natural, by way of the second emanation, not of what is originated, whose existence is by the first emanation. Nor is motion in everything for need, as he mentioned. Motion in a natural thing may be among the causes of its perfection, such as the motion of the spheres: their motions are not for a need in their own essence to attain what they were not granted at the first instant of their creation, but rather for a completeness belonging to them. Such too is the motion of the natiq (ع) and his striving to gather the word upon worship, and his exertion in the affairs of governance; that is out of a completeness required of him, because something is required of him by which he is completed after attaining perfection and rising to the rank of natiq while he yet exists. Since this is so, the saying of the author of al-Nusra, taken absolutely, that motion is for need, is not exhaustive. His ruling is sound only of some natural things, on the ground that motion is inseparable from them, as we have mentioned.

In the seventh section of Part Two, the author of al-Nusra said: He does not pour out all that he has upon the soul, but only a part.

What he meant by this is that the First is distinguished, by its rank of precedence and the loftiness of its station, by what is not shared by what is below it, which he named the soul; and this is the best that his words can be made to mean by interpretation. Otherwise, were the matter as he described, and did each of the hudud withhold from pouring out upon another some of what it has, then it would be imagined of the Prophet (ع) that the blessings reaching him from the world of al-Quds as an outpouring were not made clear by him in their fullness to the wasi and his followers, and that he taught them to no one, so that their benefit lapsed and they came to nothing. Then the wasi (ع) would become doubtful in his perfection and his merit and his standing in the place of the Prophet (ع), and the obedience owed to him would become doubtful in its obligation, a mere conjecture. To believe such a thing is not permissible, neither of the natiq, nor of the wasi, and in sum, nor of the hudud.

Rather we say: The blessings are poured out and bestowed from the side of all the hudud. The cause of any absence of those blessings, by which perfection is attained in all existents, is not a withholding on the part of the hudud, but the incapacity of the receivers to receive them. Each receives the blessings according to the strength of the receiver: some receive fully, and some receive only what they can. This is found among natural things, among compassionate teachers and eager learners. It is not permitted to any of the hudud to withhold its benefit from one who would draw benefit, so long as he is a receiver standing in his right, when he wills, out of fear that he might rise to a rank; for that would be before the spiritual birth. Concerning this the natiq (ع) spoke from God the Exalted, and said:

ولا تقتلوا اولادكم خشية املاق ومن فعل ذلك فقد أثم وتعدى طوره واسقط نفسه عن مرتبة الفاضلين

Do not kill your children for fear of poverty; whoever does that has sinned, has overstepped his proper stage, and has cast himself down from the rank of the excellent.

How, then, could it be permitted to imprison the spiritual wealth, which is the sciences and the luminous blessings, away from their owners, when the proof of their deserving stands, and the Prophet (ع) has commanded that it be handed over to them, saying, exalted is He who says:

فان أنستم منهم رشداً فادفعوا اليهم اموالهم ولا تأكلوها اسرافاً وبداراً

If you find in them sound judgment, hand over to them their wealth, and do not consume it wastefully and in haste.

No indeed: the blessings are poured out only that they may be conveyed to those who deserve them and settled in their proper place, as we have explained in our hermeneutic epistles and in our Basran and Baghdadi assemblies. May God make us, and a company of our brethren, those who render the trust to its owners and fulfill its conditions. Perhaps the author of al-Nusra, by way of analogy, advanced what he advanced.

In the eighth section of Part Two, the author of al-Nusra said: Action, moreover, is the substance of encompassing, and by it things are encompassed; and this is not the condition of what is moved, for the moved does not encompass things, but rather needs something at rest, at the utmost of rest, so that from its rest it may encompass things. Were the intellect moved, it would encompass nothing, and the soul would encompass nothing by drawing benefit from it. And since we found it encompassing all things, nothing escaping it, and found that whoever encompasses does so by what he attains and gains of its lights and its excellences, we knew then that the intellect is at the utmost of rest, and no motion is imagined in it; while the soul moves only to reach the innermost of the intellect, and then to be still. It does not move so as to encompass, by drawing benefit from the intellect, what it wishes to encompass. So long as the soul is in motion it does not encompass, nor does it attain the excellences and the lights, until it is still by drawing benefit from it; then it encompasses things. The soul, then, is moved with a motion of subsistence, coming to rest by drawing benefit from the one who is at rest. This is what he said.

We say: The words have already passed that show the intellect is not moved, in a way that spares us any lengthening. It does not follow, when the intellect is not moved, that it must be at rest in the manner in which bodies are found, which are not free of being either at rest or moved; for its existence is not like the existence of bodies, which are not free of that. In what has already passed there is sufficiency, and an indication that motion and stillness do not befit it. As for what he made necessary by his saying that, so long as the soul is in motion it encompasses nothing and attains no excellences, its meaning is not sound, neither of what is separate from the Second, as we mentioned, nor of what is not separate. For the souls in the House of Nature acquire only by being moved with their bodies, through the use of the senses, and inference, and drawing benefit. He means that the matter is so when a proof of it is established; and that suffices as a statement of the persistence of the error in it.

In the ninth section of Part Two, he then said: The soul is moved with a motion of subsistence, coming to rest by drawing benefit from the one who is at rest. He is truthful in this if he assigns it a meaning, and in error if it is carried upon the surface of his words. What makes him truthful is that he take, by the soul, the natural souls, so that it is said of his statement that the soul is moved, because the natural souls, which subsist in potentiality, are moved for the sake of acquisition; and, in his phrase, with a motion of subsistence, because their acquisition drives them toward subsistence; and, in his phrase, it comes to rest by drawing benefit, because it passes from potentiality to actuality by acquiring and drawing benefit and hunting out cognitions and excellences, in knowledge and in action; and, in his phrase, from the one who is at rest, that is, from the one who subsists in actuality. What makes him in error is that his words be carried upon their surface, for the Second is not moved, nor is the intellect at rest as bodies are at rest, as though they were of the House of Body, which would compel motion and stillness upon them.

Care must be turned to knowing the ranks of the hudud, so that the matter of conceiving things in their realities not be disordered, and so lead to the corruption of the substance of souls. May God illumine our intellects by their aid, and grant us the happiness of following the pure ones through the stages of the divine sciences and the treasuries of the holy lights (عليهم السلام).
