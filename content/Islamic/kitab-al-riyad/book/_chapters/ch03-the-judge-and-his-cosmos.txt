# The Judge and His Cosmos

His book *Rest of the Mind* (راحة العقل) is one of those precious works that scarcely finds an equal among the writings of the philosophers, or among what remains of their legacy, in strength, durability, and depth. For that very reason demand for it was small and its circulation slow, its readership confined to a special class of brilliant scholars and philosophers capable of mastering it.

The great preacher and Yemeni historian Idris Imad al-Din mentioned him in his book with these words:

> هو أساس الدعوة التي عليه عمادها وبه علا واستقام منارها وبه استبانت المشكلات وانفرجت المعضلات

"He is the foundation of the da'wa upon which it rests and by which it is raised up; through him its beacon was set straight, and by him problems were made plain and dilemmas resolved." The Syrian Ismaili preacher Nur al-Din Ahmad described him in his *Fusul wa Akhbar* (Chapters and Reports) with words no less emphatic:

> لو ان الدعوة الاسماعيلية لم تنتج غير الكرماني لكفاها فخراً ومجداً ولكان ذلك كافياً

"Had the Ismaili da'wa produced none but al-Kirmani, that alone would have been ample pride and glory, and that alone would have sufficed."

His impact and his stature came to the fore in the reign of the Fatimid Caliph al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah. His famous title was *Hujjat al-Iraqayn*, the Hujjah of the Two Iraqs, meaning that he had charge of the cultural affairs of the da'wa throughout Persia and Iraq. In Cairo his standing was that of a Hujjah of a region, one of the twelve hujjahs charged with directing the affairs of the da'wa across the world. He was afterward appointed head of *Dar al-Hikma*, the House of Wisdom, in Cairo, the cultural institution that we may rightly call the first university ever established.

He came to Cairo in a delegation in the year 408 AH, at the request of al-Sadiq al-Ma'mun, then the foremost of the Fatimid state's preachers in the era of al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah. It was a moment when religious battles were heating up, when new movements were flooding the market of innovation with calls aimed at exaggeration and at deviation from the realities and the foundations of the faith. He delivered talks and lectures in *Dar al-Hikma* and produced a great many researches and books. The most famous of them is *Risalat al-Wa'iza*, the Preacher's Message, composed in reply to al-Hasan al-Farghani, who held that al-Hakim was divine by the command of God. Among his other works are the epistles known as *al-Mabasim*, the Good Tidings, and *al-Masabih*, the Lamps, in which he was able to prove the Imamate as a necessary cosmic reality by reasoned and compelling arguments, arguments richly furnished with Hebrew, Syriac, and Persian expressions drawn from the books of the prophets of the heavenly utterance. He then took up the Platonic opinions as the basis of his researches and deployed them with a skill no one before him had brought to bear, all of it a general call to consolidate the philosophical and intellectual order, to raise its level, and to erase every trace of doubt, controversy, suspicion, and dispute.

In these lines I will not attempt to introduce the philosopher al-Kirmani merely as one of the Ismaili preachers who played an important role in the field of thought during the reign of al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah, nor merely as one of the philosophers who rendered clear intellectual services to the Fatimid da'wa. I present him, rather, as a philosopher among the philosophers of the world, one who walked upon the stage of universal philosophy and exerted everything in him to found a philosophical school upon a new intellectual foundation, drawn from the innovative theories of his time and the mental systems then current. This is true above all of the theory of the unity of being held by the Second Teacher, al-Farabi, and the Chief Sheikh, Ibn Sina, here expanded by him in exposition and departing somewhat from the general curriculum laid down for the Ismaili preachers, whether those who came before him or those of his own age, who were bound to a well-defined framework whose limits they could not transgress.

Returning to al-Kirmani's writings, and reading them with careful analysis, I find that he resolved that there is contradiction and disharmony among the existents, and an attempt on the part of some of them to erase the others, yet that these existents persist in spite of this dissonance and this contradiction. None of them is lost by the presence of an opposite; rather, they are all preserved under existence. All of this is identical to the theory of the Second Teacher on creation, in which he says:

> حفظ ادامة وجود الشيء الذي ليس وجوداً لذاته ادامة لا تتصل بشيء من العلل غير ذات المبدع

"The preservation of the continuance of the existence of a thing that does not possess, in itself, a permanence unrelated to any of the contingents, other than the Creator." And Ibn Sina, speaking of the possible and the necessary among the existents, gives an account that clarifies the meaning of the continuity of existence, saying:

> أما كون العلل ممكن الوجود في نفسه واجب الوجود لغيره فليس يناقض كونه دائم الوجود بغيره

"As for the fact that the contingents can exist in themselves while not being obligated by the existence of others, this does not contradict their being always present in others." What these three philosophers, the Second Teacher, the Chief Sheikh, and the Hujjah of the Two Iraqs, arrive at in this regard comes near to what Descartes said in the modern era:

> اذ يوجد من الفعل الذي يحفظ الله به العالم وبين الفعل الذي خلقه به

"There is, between the act by which God preserves the world and the act by which He created it, no real difference." This is what is known in his philosophy as the theory of continuous creation.

In truth, al-Kirmani shared something with the Muslim philosophers and was influenced by some of them, especially those who took up Greek philosophy. He is distinguished from them, however, in that when he presented these philosophies he stamped them with the marks of novelty, wit, and innovation, and was the precursor of the modern European philosophers and of the ancient Oriental renewers. On that basis one may say that al-Kirmani left behind works and produced ideas to be looked upon not only as Ismaili intellectual views, but as Islamic philosophical views of a high order, probing the essence of things and the deep intellectual questions in a steady and elaborated manner in which genius is manifest.

Yes, al-Kirmani spoke within the Neoplatonic systems, and bent all his effort toward applying some of their materials in his attractive philosophical and rhetorical style, working to prove the matter of Prophethood and the Imamate from both the philosophical and the religious sides. On this point I can say that he upheld the theory that adherence to the outward Shari'ah leads to scriptural worship, while the further reach of interpretation and philosophical discourse, together with the discussion of the boundaries (ḥudūd), is reserved for the permitted ones who have attained a high level in philosophy and the sciences. All these features are found when we look closely at *The Illuminating Message*, *The Lamps in Proving the Imamate*, *Alerting the Guide and the Guided*, and *Rest of the Mind*. Beyond this, al-Kirmani is a major asset in developing the intellectual order and expanding theoretical discourse, adding new elements, broadening certain topics, laying down the basic rules of interpretation, and treating contradiction and resemblance, especially on the subject of the Imamate, which had been the focus of unstable and shifting opinions. For that reason his *Rest of the Mind* defined the rules, origins, ranks, and standing of the Imamate, and set new foundations, systems, and order for it.

Al-Kirmani was prominent in the doctrine of the call to existence, in the negation of essence (الايسية) and substance (الليسية), in holding the divine attributes subject to absolute negation, in the doctrine of the call to Tawhid, in the doctrine of the two principles of origination, in the elaboration of emanation, and in the question of priority between the Imamate and the Prophethood, all in a purely scientific and logical manner. He did not stop there. At the outset he gathered the two first principles, the Active Intellect and the Universal Soul, together with the system of the ten Platonic intellects upheld by the Second Teacher. He then set the world of origination, which is the world of intellects, the spiritual world, alongside the corporeal celestial world, which is the world of the stars and planets, alongside the physical world, which is all that lies below the lunar orbit, alongside the world of religion, which is the world of the boundaries of religion; and he drew for these worlds geographical, astronomical, terrestrial, and corporeal diagrams that came out highly artistic, betraying an ingenuity of expression and an eminence in philosophy.

On returning to *Rest of the Mind* and reviewing what is stated in it, one sees that it is full of formulations and arguments concerning the statements about God and the negation of the divine attributes. Among the positions he stated and supported: that no attribute attaches to Allah; that He is not a body nor in a body; that no intellect rationalizes His essence and no sense perceives Him; that He is not a form or a substance; that He has no opposite and no likeness; that no language holds what can express Him; and that He has no rank among the existents. This shows the breadth of his knowledge across the sciences, and the rational ones above all. On this the Mu'tazila and the theologians (المتكلمة) agree, and he agrees with Ibn Rushd; and on the argument for the existence of God we see him agreeing with Descartes, even if the two differ in the details and in the manner of expression. If we add a little further inquiry, we see that he folded into his solutions and ideas some of the views of the philosophers who preceded him and of his contemporaries, Plato, Aristotle, al-Kindi, al-Farabi, and Ibn Sina.

I note from al-Baqillani, al-Baghdadi, and al-Ghazali that they ruled the Ismailis, by their denial of the attributes, to have fallen into invalidity (التعطيل). But al-Kirmani answered this charge, saying:

> ان التعطيل الصريح انما يكون بأن يتوجه فعل حرف النفي «لا» نحو «الهوية» قصداً، كأن يقال مثلاً «لا هو» و«لا اله»، وليس هذا مما تقول به الاسماعيلية، اذ ان النفي عندهم هو نفي الصفات وحدها، وتوجيه فعل حرف النفي «لا» انما ينصب عندهم على الصفات دون الهوية

"Outright invalidation is that the act of the particle of negation «لا», 'no', be directed deliberately against the identity itself, as when one says «لا هو», 'He is not,' or «لا اله», 'there is no god.' This is not what the Ismailis say. Their negation is the negation of the attributes alone; for them the act of the negative particle «لا» falls upon the attributes and leaves the identity untouched." However the matter stands, al-Kirmani gave special attention to this subject in his *Rest of the Mind*.

In that book we see him departing from all philosophers, writers, historians, and scholars. Instead of dividing his work into the customary books, chapters, and sections that other men of thought employ, he likened it to a city encircled by seven walls. Whoever enters must pass through the seven walls; and to pass through them he must face seven gates branching off from each wall, except the seventh and last wall encircling the city, which has fourteen gates (مشارع). I will not enter here into an exposition of what al-Kirmani intended by his walls and his gates, for fear of going on at length; but that does not prevent me from affirming his high standing in philosophy, the power of his thinking, and the depth of his grasp of the essence of things. Al-Kirmani published many works besides these, among them *Rest of the Mind* first of all, together with a long series of treatises on the systems, on light, on guidance, on Tawhid, and on the ranks of the faith.

Finally, it must be said that al-Kirmani is among the philosophers who weigh heavily upon our philosophical world, and that the study of his writings and his output is of great importance. It gives a clear picture of the impact of philosophy upon the history of thought for all who care for Oriental studies and Islamic philosophy.
