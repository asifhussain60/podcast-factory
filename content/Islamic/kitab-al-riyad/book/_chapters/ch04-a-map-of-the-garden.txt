# A Map of the Garden

I see that al-Kirmani opens his book *Riyāḍ* by praising and glorifying God, as was the custom of all the scholars of those eras, and at the last he points to the Imam of his ruling time, the Fatimid ruling by the command of Allah. He then says:

> "I saw that Abu Hatim al-Razi had taken up the errors in the book *al-Maḥṣūl*. Then Abu Yaʿqub al-Sijistani came and defended the author of *al-Maḥṣūl*, insisting on the soundness of his argument and the strength of his theory, and that the matters he discussed were not Ismaili philosophical side-issues or branches that could be passed over, for to pass them over would be an affront to the creed and a disturbance of the basic principles and foundations.
>
> It seems that Abu Yaʿqub backed certain texts contrary to Abu Hatim al-Razi, attacking him, and in some places speaking in ways that bore no relation to the topic at all. In any event, certain passages in *al-Maḥṣūl* dealing with monotheism made it obligatory for Abu Hatim to correct and discuss them. He did not; he preferred instead to indulge in long stories on subjects of lesser importance, the historical material contained in the Torah, with which he filled his book, leaving *al-Maḥṣūl* uncorrected and neglected. He thereby produced a kind of intellectual confusion among the followers of the quiet daʿwa by setting different and divergent opinions before them, especially once they had taken to reading *al-Maḥṣūl*. This had an effect on the purity of the idea of the paths of monotheism, and led to corruption of the principles of Shariʿa and of the arrangement of the boundaries (*ḥudūd*)."

And so al-Kirmani states his own resolve in his own words:

> ولهذا السبب قررت ان اورد هنا الاقوال الاصلية الواردة في كتاب ( الاصلاح ) مصححاً بعض ما جاء في كتاب ((المحصول)) وما ورد في اقوال ((النصرة)) التي تدعم كتاب ((المحصول)) مقدماً رأيي وتعليقاتي عليها وغرضي من ذلك اظهار الاصول بالمعنى الصحيح والفصل بين الاثنين

"For this reason I decided to quote here the original statements contained in the book *al-Iṣlāḥ* that correct some of what is mentioned in the book *al-Maḥṣūl*, along with what has been mentioned in the statements of *al-Nuṣra* that support *al-Maḥṣūl*, presenting my own opinion and comments. My aim is to display the principles in their correct sense and to separate the two positions; once we have reached this point it becomes easy to expose the error, so that the correct teachings remain free of doubt and ambiguity, and far removed from every disparity and confusion."

Al-Kirmani divided his book *Riyāḍ* into ten chapters, each subdivided into several sections; and with his literary and scientific deftness, the strength of his exposition, and his learning, he selected what he saw as important and fundamental from the books *al-Iṣlāḥ* and *al-Nuṣra* and recast it in his own style, clear and concise, a kind of artistry wholly different from al-Razi's. Of those ten chapters I take up here the first five, and I lay them out in order.

1. **The first chapter, in thirty-eight sections, on the perfection of the soul.** The author of *al-Iṣlāḥ* says the soul is perfect, a perfect source issuing from perfection itself, because the intellect is perfection. The author of *al-Nuṣra* contradicts this, saying that the author of *al-Iṣlāḥ* does not realize that "the perfect" is higher and more complete than "perfection," because the second, perfection, is an attribute of the first and is therefore predicated of it. The perfect, which bears this attribute, is therefore higher, and the attribute attached to it is named after it; for the perfect exists, and when perfection exists it becomes the follower, not the followed. Here the debate between truth and fancy sharpens, and the author of *Riyāḍ* lifts the cover from the logical error: the author of *al-Iṣlāḥ* did not mean perfection in general, but the highest perfection, namely the intellect (*ʿaql*), which is the cause of the existence of the perfect soul that has come to be characterized by it. And the effect cannot stand higher than the cause. Al-Kirmani's view thus leads us to hold that al-Sijistani's attack on al-Razi was out of place and incorrect. Al-Kirmani then takes up al-Razi's opinion, in which he says that although the soul is perfect, its actions are not, since these actions depend on time, whereas the soul is the emanation of the intellect, and the intellect is a being composed in itself of origination, time, and perfection, unlike the soul, which is emanated within time and depends on the supply that comes from the intellect. This dependence does not impair the soul's substance nor render it base, just as the intellect's dependence on the divine will does not make it inferior. The substance is not the inferior of matter on account of this perfection or imperfection, which are born of the soul and are not its substance. These two quotations are drawn from the book *al-Iṣlāḥ*, and al-Kirmani strongly criticizes the opinions of *al-Nuṣra*, holding that al-Sijistani misunderstood these theories, likening him to a newcomer who entered the world of the ideals with no goal but material vanity. The author of *al-Nuṣra* had cited the saying of Empedocles in protest, and to it al-Kirmani replies:

> قال صاحب ((النصرة)) مورداً قول امباذقليس على سبيل الاحتجاج به انما صارت اما لأنه لم يخضع لشيء إلا الباري عز وجل وصارت النفس ناقصة لانها خضعت للطبيعة نقول : ان الفلاسفة المتقدمين منهم وان كانوا فضلاء في زمانهم فقد استمر عليهم سلطان الخطأ في كثير مما تكلموا عليه من العقليات ، ولو كانوا في زماننا ورأوا ينابيع البركات كيف هي فائضة من بيت الوحي بحقائق الأمور على ما هي عليه من الدعوة العلوية لكانوا مع اجتهادهم لانفسهم ينصفون انفسهم ويعبرون عن كثير من اعتقاداتهم وفيما غاب عن الحواس يقفون . ولكانوا لآثار من جعل الله له نوراً في غوامض الامور يقنعون . فانهم في ازمانهم اتبعوا آراءهم وعقولهم فيا نحوه فصار كل منهم يذهب في اعتقاده الى ما تدل عليه مصنفاته على ما هو عليه أهل الظاهر في الملة الحنيفية حين اعتمدوا على عقولهم في سلوك مسالك الديانة فاختلفت آراؤهم وبين أولئك تفاضل فمنهم من صوابه اكثر ومنهم من خطأه اكثر والمعول على ما يشهد به ميزان الديانة الذي هو الآفاق والانفس . فما المعنى فيا أورده امباذقليس الا ما أورده صاحب النصرة في احتجاجه، وقول امباذقليس انصب للطعن ونسأل الله ان ينور عقولنا ويختم بالسعادة أمورنا والخيرات والاعمال الصالحات يوفقنا بمنه ولطفه وكرمه

"The author of *al-Nuṣra* cited the saying of Empedocles in protest: 'It became as it is only because it was not subject to anything but the Almighty; the soul became deficient because it has been subject to nature.' We say: the philosophers who preceded them, even if they were virtuous in their time, remained under the authority of error in many of the matters they spoke of from their own minds. Had they been in our time, and had they seen the springs of grace and what overflows from the House of Revelation with the facts of things as they are, namely the ʿAlawi daʿwa, they would have used their diligence to do themselves justice, and would have expressed many of their beliefs in the absence of the senses. They would have been among those for whom Allah set a light in the mysteries of things. In their own time, however, they followed their own opinions and their own minds, each going in his belief in the direction his reason pointed him. Their works rest on the same ground as the Ẓāhirīs of the Hanafi sect when they relied on their own minds in walking the paths of religion: their opinions diverged, and among them there was a distinction, some closer to right, others closer to wrong; and they relied on what the balance of religion testifies to, namely the horizons and the souls. What then is the meaning of what Empedocles mentioned, beyond what the author of *al-Nuṣra* mentioned in his protest? The saying of Empedocles is aimed at our challenge. We ask God to enlighten our minds and to close our affairs and our acts of goodness with happiness, and to aid us through His kindness and generosity in good works."

2. **The second chapter, in nine sections, on the relationship between the intellect and the soul.** Al-Sijistani objects to al-Razi because the latter raised the topic of motion, repose, and stillness, the central topic that so often caused difficulty for the ancient philosophers. Here motion and stillness are taken as two effects united in "the First" and the Second. This supports what Abu Hatim al-Razi said: that the First was made originative and has become attached to and responsible for origination, since the truth of His station is a duality, the originator and the originated, and these two effects are present in "the First." When the Second was brought forth from the First, the bringing-forth itself became dualized, the same emanation and the same emanated, and so the two effects are linked to the two basic principles. As for motion, it is the benefaction (*fayḍ*) that the First releases upon the Second and which acts upon all assets and entities, while stillness is the acceptance of the First's command throughout all the basic entities. Al-Razi says: I do not say that motion and stillness are generated from the soul by a power received from the intellect; rather, I maintain that both are two effects of the soul, generated by the power produced by the intellect within the form and the image, and for this reason form and image have become the basis of this world. Al-Sijistani, for his part, answers in *al-Nuṣra*:

> ان هذا الكلام من نوع الهذيان لان الحركة تتولد من قصد خاص ((لطلبها))

"This argument is delirious, because motion is generated from a specific intention, to seek something," as the motion of physical bodies is intended either to fill the void above through the upward movement of flame, or through the downward movement of earth, and so on. From this al-Kirmani concludes that al-Razi was correct while al-Sijistani was wrong. The author of *al-Iṣlāḥ* had put it thus:

> قال صاحب الاصلاح ونقول : ان الحركة والسكون هما أثران متحدان بالأول والثاني لان الاول لما ابدع اتحد بالابداع فكان لذلك الاتحاد اثرين فيه بالقوة . وانما قلنا أثران لان الابداع والمبدع اثنان واحدهما الابداع والآخر ذات المبدع والابداع والمبدع فان كانا اثنين فهما ايس واحد وهو الكل وهذان الآثران هما في الاول بالقوة ثم انبعث الثاني عن الاول فكان من ذلك الانبعاث اثرين فيه بالفعل احدهما ذات الانبعاث والآخر ذات المنبعث فالأصلان متحدان بالأمر وأحد الاثرين هو الحركة وهو أعلى من الآخر الذي هو السكون كما ان احد الاصلين الذي هو الاول أعلى من الآخر

"We hold that motion and stillness are two effects united in the First and the Second. For when the First was creative, it united with creativity, and for that reason the union had two effects in it by power. We called them two effects because creativity and the creative are two: one is creativity itself and the other is the creator; and creativity and the creator, though two, are a single essence, namely the All. These two effects belong first to the First by power, and then the Second was emitted from the First, and through that emission two actual effects arose in it: one is identical with the emanation, the other identical with the emanated. The two origins are united by the command, and one of the two effects, motion, is higher than the other, stillness, just as one of the two originals, the First, is higher than the other."

3. **The third chapter, in six sections, on the question whether the soul (*al-nafs*) and the spirit (*al-ruh*) are one and the same.** It opens with phrases drawn from *al-Nuṣra*, set among the texts cited from *al-Iṣlāḥ* and arranged in a way that clarifies their original meaning; and here al-Kirmani says that al-Sijistani criticizes al-Razi for something al-Razi never said. The exchange runs:

> وقال صاحب ((الاصلاح)) على ما أثبته صاحب ((النصرة)) ؛ ولا نقول ان الطبيعة والهيولى الظلمانية متولدة من الثاني لتؤكد الثاني من الاول ، لأن ذلك العالم عالم الشرف والفضيلة وهذا العالم هو عالم الظلمة والكدورة ، والثاني وان كان متولداً من الاول فانه شبيه به ، ولا يشبه حاله حال الهيولى ، وقال صاحب ((النصرة)) : ان هذا غلط محض لانه لو كان ذلك العالم نورانياً لا ظلمة فيه وهذا العالم ظلمانياً لا نور فيه لم يقدر ان يقع بين هذا

"The author of *al-Iṣlāḥ*, in reply to what the author of *al-Nuṣra* had advanced, said: We do not say that nature and the dark matter (*al-hayūlā*) are born from the Second in order to confirm that the Second derives from the First. That higher world is the world of honor and virtue, while this world is the world of darkness and gloom. The Second, though born of the First, resembles Him, and its case is unlike the case of *al-hayūlā*. The author of *al-Nuṣra* replies: this is a pure mistake, for if that world were illuminated with no darkness in it, and this world dark with no light in it, no resemblance could fall between them."

4. **The fourth chapter, in eight sections, on whether souls are parts of the First Truths.** Al-Kirmani notes that al-Sijistani alters the meaning of what is stated in *al-Iṣlāḥ* by rearranging its phrases. Al-Kirmani agrees with him on the subject of partial souls, granting that the aggregate of these souls does not constitute the universal soul. Al-Sijistani then returns to speak about this world, alluding to the complete Truths, and says that the human soul belongs to the region between the world of nature and the world of rebirth; it is the compound, known by natural force, and the soul may be raised to the highest world by practicing piety and then by uniting with the Second. He does not stop there, but says that the Intellect is the First Rebirth, while the speaking souls are the Second Rebirth, and whatever occurred in the first rebirth is resurrected in the second as well. Al-Kirmani criticizes this opinion and refers the reader to his own writings, especially to the epistle *al-Wāʿiẓa* (the Preacher) and *al-Muḍīʾa* (the Illuminating), in which he says:

> ان العقل ليس إلا ذات الكلمة والكلمة ليست إلا ذات العقل

"The Intellect is nothing but the Word itself, and the Word is nothing but the Intellect itself." He confirms this by citing the saying of our Master, Imam al-Muʿizz li-Din Allah (ع), in the book *Taʾwīl al-Sharīʿa* (the Interpretation of the Shariʿa), where he speaks of the intellects and indicates that the Word is the Intellect and that these names are properly applied to it. Al-Kirmani adds that he has already explained and discussed the Originator, the Intellect, the Word, and what may be said of the world of creativity and the First Rebirth in the epistle *al-Muḍīʾa* and others, and that these treatises suffice the researcher. Nor does he stop there. He cites the words of al-Razi recorded in *al-Iṣlāḥ*, that motion and stillness are to the soul as the body and the image are to it, and these last two are like the body of the first two, because the soul is gentler than the body and the body is denser than the soul; so motion and stillness are gentler than the substance and the image. Al-Sijistani, it appears, rejects this analysis and supports instead the theory of the author of *al-Maḥṣūl*. Al-Kirmani says that both men erred, and al-Razi especially, regarding motion. Motion is the action of the mover, which we call the soul; stillness, by contrast, is the cessation of the mover's action. Therefore, if the mover is the soul, it cannot resemble motion, which is its own action, and the same applies to stillness. Moreover, motion and stillness cannot be combined into a single thing, because they alternate as opposites, while the structure and the image are inseparable on the mixed plane and form one thing. This topic he has fully explained in the book *Rāḥat al-ʿAql* (the Rest of the Mind).

5. **The fifth chapter, in seven sections, on the theme that the human being is the fruit of the material world.** Al-Razi says so, and holds the human being to belong to the whole world, its foundation, and its roots. *Al-hayūlā* too is the basis of the substrate formed in it by motion, stillness, and certain other factors that lead to the formation of the different souls, such as the region and others. Al-Sijistani calls this philosophy the "world philosophy," which does not acknowledge the existence of the soul after its separation from the body.

I turn now to a group whose position al-Kirmani weighs without giving a fully decisive answer. This group, it is held, follows the school of Democritus and Epicurus, as it was further pursued by Lucretius. Al-Kirmani says that both schools are right on one side and wrong on another. The human being, on this view, is the result of the interaction of natural-planetary and physical action, and the human being is the purpose of the Second and the end of the work of nature. The meaning is this: the human being is created and divided into two parts, one part belonging to lifeless nature and another part belonging to God, and he is therefore the highest of creatures.

Against this we say:

> نقول كيف يصح ما ذكره والنظم والترتيب لا يوجدان الا من العقل ام كيف يصح والأشياء المجملة لا يفضلها الا العقل والنفس ما لا يتأثر فيها بأثاره فهي والبهائم سواء ، ولذلك رفع الله عن النفس القلم الى حين يتأثر فيها آثار العقل ، وقد قلنا فيما تقدم ان القضية في الفاعلين ليست بكلية فليس كل فاعل يطلب بفعله بلوغ ارب او غرض والذي قدره اراده بقوله ذلك ان العقل الأول ذاته ليس بمركبً فيكون اذاً ذا ترتيب ونظم ، وامًا الادراك فالعقل لا يدرك الا ادراكاً حقيقياً

We say: how can what he has said be true, when order and arrangement come only from the Intellect? Or how is it correct, when universals are made preferable only by the Intellect, while the soul, so long as it is unaffected by the Intellect's effects, is on a par with the animals? It is for this reason that God has lifted the pen from the soul until the effects of the Intellect take hold in it. And we have said above that the matter lies in the agents, not in totalities: not every agent seeks, through its action, to reach a goal or purpose decreed for it. By the same reckoning, the First Intellect itself is not a compound, so it is in order and arrangement, and yet the Intellect perceives nothing except true perception.

The sixth chapter, in six sub-chapters, rehearses the theme of motion, stillness, matter, and image. In it al-Kirmani holds that what is reported in the book al-Mahsul (The Harvest), and all that Abu Hatim al-Razi and al-Sijistani transmitted on this score, is incorrect, and he refers the reader to consult his own book al-Masabih (The Lamps). Here the author of al-Nusra proceeds and undertakes to define body and soul:

> وصاحب ((النصرة)) جاء فوعد ان يحد الجسم والروح ، فحد الجسم بقبول الاقدار الثلاثة ثم حدّ الروح بتشابه الاجزاء وأجراها مجرى الماء والنار والهواء التي كل منها متشابه الأجزاء وذلك محال بما تقدم من القول ان الروح ليس يجسم فيلزمه ذلك والروح والنفس اسمان مترادفان على شيء واحد وذلك الشيء ليس يجسم فلو كان جسماً لكان يلزمه من الكيفيات والأعراض التسعة ما يلزم الجسم ولكان يمكن ان يدرك بحس ... لم تبق متوهمة فيه الاعراض التسعة ولا الموجودة فيه ولما كان غير موجود فيه الأعراف الجسمانية بطل ان يكون جسمً، وكتابنا المعروف ((بالمصابيح)) يتضمن من ذلك ما يشفي

The author of al-Nusra undertakes to define body and soul. He defines body by its acceptance of the three dimensions, and then defines the soul as the similarity of its parts, making the soul run the same course as water, fire, and air, each of which is similar in its parts. With this premise it becomes impossible to maintain that the soul is not embodied. Yet spirit (al-ruh) and soul (al-nafs) are two synonymous names for one thing, and that thing is not embodied; for were it a body, it would necessarily carry the nine qualities and accidents that a body requires, and could be apprehended by a sense. Nor would the nine accidents, or even one of them, remain conceivable in it; and since the bodily accidents are not found in it, it cannot be a body. Our well-known book al-Masabih contains what fully heals this question.

The seventh chapter, divided into seven sub-chapters, deals with the classification of the composite materials of the world. In truth it is dedicated entirely to explaining the station of the human being, who forms a class of his own, and his relation to all else. For this reason al-Razi says:

> ان الانسان نبات نام على مواد العالم الكبير

Man is a plant that sleeps upon the substances of the greater world.

Al-Sijistani agrees with him on one side and disagrees on another, since the human body disintegrates or dissolves into such substances. Al-Razi classes the human being and likens him to an animal because he possesses the senses, while al-Sijistani holds that this feeling belongs to the growing vegetative soul. Al-Razi argues that the speaking human soul composes the point of the human being's difference from the animal, and al-Sijistani counters that the animal too possesses the senses. When al-Kirmani weighs the two opinions, he rules that al-Razi is closer to the truth than al-Sijistani. Finally he says:

> وجه الشبه ليس بين الانسان والعالم المادي فقط بل بينه وبين العالم الروحي الموحد في نفسه وان كل شيء سبقه في ميزان الوجود واظهر المشايهات بينه وبين كل شيء في العالمين المادي والروحي

The resemblance is not only between the human being and the material world, but also between him and the spiritual world, the world unified in itself, so that everything preceded him in the scale of existence, and he displays the likenesses between himself and everything in the material and spiritual worlds.

> وفي العالم حركات مختلفات وفي البشر كذلك حركات ستة الى الحركات الست وفي العالم محرك يحركه وفي البشر كذلك محرك يحركه وهو النفس وفي العالم الاستقصات الاربعة وفي البشر كذلك الطبائع الاربعة وفي العالم الانهار والأودية والمياه المختلفة المذاق والجبال والمعادن وفي البشر كذلك كما بينه صاحب ((الاصلاح)) العظام والغضاريف واللحم والدم وما يسيل منه وما يخرج وفي العالم ما ينبت وينمو ولا يحس ، وفي البشر كذلك ما ينبت وينمو ولا يحس مثل الشعر والأظافر وهو يجسمه ينمو ويزيد من صغره الى كبره وفي العالم الحيوان الحساس وكذلك في البشر القوى الحساسة والعالم كله تراكيب وتأليف فقد استكمل بالموجود من ذلك فيه التشبيه بالعالم وان كان ما اوردناه جملة يفصلها كتابنا المعروف ( براحة العقل )

In the world there are various motions, and in the human being likewise there are six motions answering to the six. In the world there is a mover that moves it, and in the human being likewise there is a mover that moves him, namely the soul. In the world there are the four elements, and in the human being likewise the four natures. In the world there are rivers, valleys of differing taste, mountains, and minerals, and in the human being likewise, as the author of al-Islah has shown, there are bones, cartilage, flesh, blood, and what flows from him and issues out of him. In the world there is what grows and increases without sensation, and in the human being likewise there is what grows and increases without sensation, such as hair and nails, and his body itself grows and increases from his youth to his old age. In the world there is the sentient animal as well, and in the human being there are the sentient faculties. The whole world is structures and compositions, and it is complete in what exists of them, and from all this comes the analogy with the world, though what we have set down here is only a summary, which our well-known book Rahat al-Aql (Peace of Mind) lays out in detail.

The eighth chapter is divided into twenty-four sub-chapters, and its subject is qada (القضاء), judgment, and qadar (القدر), destiny. These two expressions are mentioned in the Holy Quran and point to particular synonyms, though both bear the sense of judgment. This topic left its mark on the long scholarly debate that unfolded in the early Islamic era and never reached a decisive result. Thousands of works of Islamic theology have been written upon it, but in truth it is a mistaken and time-wasting matter to return to them.

Al-Razi, the author of al-Islah, says that in view of the statement of al-Mahsul that qada agrees with the First and the Preceding, and qadar with the Second, that is to say the Following, this is entirely wrong, because destiny precedes judgment just as the First precedes the Second. The meaning of qadar is estimation, namely that He made the thing possible, and the meaning of qada is detailing, the separation of the thing or its cutting; and the Second, that is, the manifest qada, plainly cannot be set before the First. Al-Razi adduces several verses of the Holy Quran in support of his reading, and he likens destiny, in its detailing, to the cloth that takes shape in the tailor's imagination before he cuts it; once he has cut it, he has set its form, that is, he has judged it, and after that it can no longer be changed.

Al-Sijistani objects to this explanation in defense of his basic theory, which agrees with what is stated in the book al-Mahsul. He says that qada does not mean detailing, though qadar does mean estimation: the first describes and obliges, while the second, which is the judgment, is an emptying and an end. He adds that the tailor's likeness does not fit the case, because qada is the idea of the garment that results from the tailor's art, which is the sewing, while the second rests upon the soul, and it is the estimation, and so it became destiny. Then al-Kirmani comes and discusses the ideas and the opinions. He says: if qada agrees with the First, and the First with the Preceding, and the Following with the Second, then we must admit that the Speaker suits the Following, as shown in al-Mahsul, even though the Speaker is responsible for defining the Shari'a. Yet it is wrong to join the Speaker and the Following, because the Speaker is concerned with the material world, while the Following, which is the Foundation, is concerned with the spiritual world; and the likeness of the Speaker and the Foundation is like the sun and the moon. Here there is a point worth attention: some of the summoners inclined to set the rank of the Foundation above the rank of the Speaker, saying that spiritual worship is higher than the natural, legal worship.

> قال صاحب ((النصرة)) حكاية عن العلماء: أن الله تعالى لما ابدع العقل فرغ من العالمين لانه مجموع صور العالمين ونقول : ان العقل الذي هو مجموع صور العالمين ليس هو العقل الاول المبدع ولا العقل الثاني الذي هو المنبعث الاول بل هو العقل المرتقي من عالم الطبيعة بالاكتساب عملاً وعلماً الى موازاة الانبعاث الاول فانبعث انبعاثً ثانياً فصار بذلك مجمعاً لصور العالمين على كثرتها وقد تنقش ذاته بصور الموجودات

The author of al-Nusra reports, on the authority of the scholars, that when God Almighty originated the Intellect, He emptied the two worlds into it, so that it might be the sum of the images of the two worlds. We say: the Intellect that is the sum of the images of the two worlds is not the First Originating Intellect, nor the Second Intellect, which is the First Emanated; rather it is the Intellect that ascends from the world of nature by acquisition, in deed and in knowledge, until it parallels the First Emanation, and so it was emanated a second emanation, and thereby became a gathering-place for the images of the two worlds, for all their multitude, and its essence may be engraved with the images of existing things.

The ninth chapter al-Kirmani divides into thirty-three sub-chapters. In it he examines the basis of the origin, the law of Adam, and the laws that came after him, and then proceeds further to speak of the Great Resurrection. He says that the Great Resurrection will come when the doors of teaching are closed, when the call that has been perfected ceases, and when the Qa'im sets aside the ranks in the administration of the call, so that there is then no longer need of them, since a new creation will have come.

In this chapter there are also sub-chapters devoted to the question whether Adam, who is mentioned in the Holy Quran as one of God's interlocutors, conveying revelation to humanity, and as the Speaker, or, in Ismaili idiom, the one who truly holds the rank of the cycle, did indeed proclaim a new law, and to whom, and how. In this chapter we find no concern with purely philosophical conjecture, but only the effort to gather and explain the few symbols and signs scattered through the sacred books, and to combine and reconcile these often contradictory references so as to draw out a convincing theory.

The Christians made of Adam, that simple man mentioned in the Old Testament of the sacred book, the Torah, a towering figure unlike any other. Islam, together with a great range of other views, inherited this new story of Adam, while the remaining groups, the Ismailis among them, established his role with a nearness to the truth greater than the rest, for they held that he stood at the beginning of the world, and it is enough that the angels were commanded to prostrate to him in honor. In the Ismaili system it is affirmed that among the seven speaker-prophets the first is not the last: Adam, the first, and the Qa'im, the last, differ a little in their work and their duties, for Adam did not possess an ʿazima (عزيمة), and the Qa'im will not bring a new law, and for this reason Muhammad is taken as the seal of the messengers and his law as the last.

Al-Kirmani speaks of the Great Resurrection because at that time the call will have been completed and no activity will remain for the ranks, since the task of teaching will have ended and a new creation will have come. This idea, it appears, reverses the aim awaited from the Ismaili call when it reaches its summit, when the whole world becomes united under their authority and holds to a single religious doctrine, which is the true and sound law of the Islamic religion that they proclaim.

The author of al-Nusra, following his interpretation of God's words in the story of the son of Noah, says:

> قال صاحب ((النصرة)) عقب تأويله عما قاله الله حكاية عن ابن نوح : ( سآوي الى جبل يعصمني من الماء) فقد ظهر ههنا بقوله: (( سألتجىء الى لاحق )) ويقول نوح: ((لا عاصم اليوم من أمر الله ))، أي أن الأمر كان قبل فوح من قبل اللواحق ، إذ هو لم يعلم مرتبة الوحي ، ولو كاث لآدم وحي لكان أبنه يعلم ذلك

Following his interpretation of what God related concerning the son of Noah, "I will take refuge on a mountain that will protect me from the water," he says that the son here revealed himself by his words, "I will seek refuge in a subordinate," while Noah says, "There is no protector today from the command of God," meaning that before Noah the command came by way of the subordinate ranks, for the son did not know the rank of revelation; and had there been a revelation for Adam, his son would have known this.

As for the tenth chapter, which is the last of the book, it is a critique of all the passages cited from al-Mahsul that Abu Hatim al-Razi left uncorrected. There are close to nine passages across the fifteen sub-chapters of this section, and we have judged it necessary to present a translation of these basic passages from al-Mahsul so as to give an idea of their style. Al-Kirmani's response in the second passage stands as an example of his method. He says that in truth, when the author of al-Mahsul took up the propagation of the call and set about explaining the foundations and rules of the da'wa, he opened the doors of new departures by composing a number of books for those who stood within it; and the majority accepted his judgment upon al-Mahsul despite the apology of the author of al-Islah. This came as a proof of the error and the tangling of the subject, and it is to be regretted that the reality of the matter is the opposite of what they saw and imagined; for al-Razi's corrections show that he did not impugn the worth of al-Mahsul or of its author, neither by exposing faults nor by passing judgment upon them, since al-Razi was among the men particularly charged with repairing religious knowledge and raising its standing, explaining the truth of the creed, and affirming the duties of the human being toward his God.

From the opinions of al-Kirmani set down in his books, one judges him to be a great author and a writer practiced in the manners and the principles, and from his epistle al-Wa'iza (The Admonition) it appears to us that he could raise an important observation and set it right, where another man would have sent forth abuse and reviling. Here are some of the passages he corrects:

1. The author of al-Islah says, on the subject of the divine unity:

> يقول مؤلف كتاب ((الاصلاح)) في موضوع التوحيد؛ ان الله خالق الاشياء وغير الاشياء فهو : ذكي ، فكري ، منطقي

That God is the Creator of things and other-than-things, that He is the intelligent, the intellectual, and the rational, meaning that such materials or subjects may fall under the rule of such elements or may not. Al-Kirmani replies that this contradicts the theory holding that the Intellect is the foundation of creation, behind which there is nothing but God, and that nothing can be prior to the Intellect in its existence, or alongside the Intellect in its existence; for this theory entails an associating of partners with God (shirk), and it overturns the law of the divine unity. To speak of creating things and other-than-things, the material and the non-material, unless one has confused himself and imagined to his fancy that there is no world other than this one and nothing that negates creation, is impossible; for how can it be said that God is the originating Creator and at the same time this be denied?

2. In the fourth chapter, the author of al-Mahsul says:

> ان الخالق مبدع الاشياء من لا شيء فقط وهو ولا شيء معه، وليس له نهاية ، واذا قلنا فقط هو ولا شيء تكون قد انكرنا الاشياء وغير ألاشياء وجعلنا الاثنين مبدعين

The Creator originates things from nothing alone; He is, and there is nothing with Him, and He has no end; and if we said only "He and nothing," we would have denied both things and other-than-things, and made the two of them originators. We have said that His Self, His very identity, whatever idea it may be, is a simple image, and that He made everything continuous and non-continuous, bringing it into this existence by a caused, bounded, and finite effect, since nothing comes after "the thing," and since the negation of a name is itself a name that comes into being once that name has come to exist, and since the word "no" is not one idea but many ideas, a negation of every idea. This too is a part of shirk and an overturning of the theory of the divine unity. Al-Kirmani refers the student to his own thesis al-Rawda and to the book Tafsir al-Shari'a by the Fatimid Imam al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah.

3. In the eighth chapter, in the section examining the First Originator, the author of al-Mahsul says:

> بإن الله عز وجل ابدع العالم دفعة واحدة وهذا يعني بانه أبدع العقل جملة واحدة ، وبقوته وتقديره اظهر فيه صور الموجودات وافكار العالم وكل شيء يحتويه ، دون ان يدل على اي شيء بمفرده ، فالعقل اذاً له الاقدمية

That God, mighty and exalted, originated the world at a single stroke, meaning that He originated the Intellect in one whole, and by His power and His measuring displayed within it the images of existing things, the ideas of the world, and everything it contains, without any single thing having priority over it, and so the Intellect holds seniority. Al-Kirmani protests against this statement, because it implies and decides that the Intellect works independently, relying upon its own thinking, and this is a grave error, since the Intellect is only a mediator in the divine work, and not another originator.

4. In the ninth chapter, the author of al-Mahsul says:

> ان العقل ازلي ومعلوليته هي وحدة الله ، ووحدة الله أزلية

That the Intellect is eternal, and that its being caused is the unity of God, and the unity of God is eternal. We see al-Kirmani refusing the idea of an "eternal unity," regarding it instead as a mediator between God and the Intellect, the second of which is itself the substance of unity for one primary cause, while at the same time the First is the cause of the act of creating and of the creature, the completion and the complete, the eternal and the everlasting, the existence and the existent.

We come, then, to the last stretch of the argument, where the author of *al-Maḥṣūl* presses his account of the Intellect through its final chapters, and where, at each turn, we watch al-Kirmani measure that account against the belief of those who profess the divine unity.

5. In chapter eleven the author of *al-Maḥṣūl* says: the image is made visible in the intellect by way of the unity of God, so the abstract image becomes eternal within it, because the intellect itself becomes wholly eternal and not partial. Here we see al-Kirmani inclining, as is his habit, to speak of the basic realities even while his thought is upon the manifestations of the physical world; and he suggests that this applies more to the mind of the Speaker, to the Base or to the Imam, than it does to the underlying cause. This is the Nearest Angel, or the Hidden Pen that writes upon the Tablet of existence.

6. In chapter twelve the author of *al-Maḥṣūl* says: since the intellect is caused by the eternity that is called the Word of God (كلمة الله), and since there is no third between them, the intellect becomes similar to it. Al-Kirmani objects to this view, saying that the intellect and the Word are one and the same thing.

7. In chapter thirteen the author of *al-Maḥṣūl* says that the intellect received the name *Tāmm* (تام), the Complete, because its coming-to-be was through the Originator, and because origination was accomplished by the perfect Originator; and so the Originator is complete, and that which was originated, that is, origination itself, can only be complete. We see al-Kirmani objecting to this and saying: God has no attributes, and it is the originator, the creator, who imposes his superiority upon the creature.

8. In chapter fourteen the author of *al-Maḥṣūl* says: the intellect carries the ideas, and the source from which it draws them is the Word exactly, like the sun, which sends out its light by its own brilliance and not by any natural or material friction. If the work of the intellect is the result of the power of the Word and of the Word's capacity to convey it, then it becomes clear that the Word is the cause of everything that proceeds from the intellect, and not the intellect itself; and the second, which is the intellect, is only an intermediary between the Word and the ranks, the *ḥudūd* (الحدود), beneath it. Here too al-Kirmani objects, saying that such an arrangement applies only to the minds of the Speakers.

9. Finally, in chapter fifteen the author of *al-Maḥṣūl* says: the act of origination is a medium between the Originator and the originated, like the effect of an agent upon the matter entrusted to him. He adds that the idea of origination depends upon the influencing agent within the image, which is transmitted from the creature to the Creator. This closing section holds scattered sentences that point to the same subject, gathered from different clues. As one would expect, al-Kirmani rejects these statements, saying that this does not agree with the belief of those who profess the divine unity, since the act of the creature is treated here as independent of the Creator.

In truth, the idea of the divine unity and the understanding of the knowledge of the *ḥudūd* (الحدود) is a hard matter, indeed a very hard one; and it is by reaching this knowledge that souls find their deliverance and their growth, their stability, and their release from the world of matter.

The author of *al-Maḥṣūl* said:

> ( قال صاحب ((المحصول)) : والابداع واسطة بين المبدع والمبدَع ، وهو أثر من المؤثر في المؤثر ، وقال : وصورة الابداع متوسطة بين الفاعل والمفعول ، ووجودها من جهة الفاعل المبدع ، فأثر الصورة يوجد من جهة المبدع في المفعول ، وهذه الصورة أعني الابداع صارت في المبدع من المبدع سبحانه .

Origination is a mediator between the Originator and the originated; it is an effect of the influencer upon the influenced. And he said: the image of origination is intermediate between the doer and the effect, its existence proceeding from the side of the originating doer; so the effect of the image is found on the side of the Originator within the effect, and this image, by which I mean origination, has come to be in the Originator from the Originator, glorified be He.

This is the book *al-Riyāḍ* of the great preacher Ḥamīd al-Dīn Aḥmad al-Kirmānī, and this is the sum of what it sets out: an exposition of the philosophical opinions that were the axis of controversy in his age. Al-Kirmani brought them into the light, drew them with care, refined them, and set them forth in the finest philosophical prose.

In truth, the author of *al-Riyāḍ* has earned the gratitude of the generations and the esteem of the Islamic world, for his book is a fruit among the fruits of thought, a tall tree that grew up in the shade of this vast world.
