# A Map of the Garden

I turn now to the contents of the *Riyāḍ* itself. Al-Kirmani opens the book by praising and glorifying God, the customary opening of all scholars in those eras, and at last he points to the Imam of his ruling time, al-Ḥākim bi-Amr Allah of the Fatimids. He then says:

> لقد رأيت أبا حاتم الرازي قد قوَّم الاخطاء الموجودة في كتاب ((المحصول)) فجاء أبو يعقوب السجستاني فدافع عن مؤلف كتاب ((المحصول)) مؤكداً صواب حجته وقوة نظريته وان الأمور التي ناقشها ليست من الجزئيات او الفروع الفلسفية الاسماعيلية حتى يمكننا التغاضي عنها وفي هذا كله ايذاء للعقيدة وتعطيلاً للمبادىء الاساسية وللأصول . ان أبا يعقوب كما يبدو قد دعم بعض النصوص مخالفاً أبا حاتم الرازي ومهاجماً اياه وفي بعض المواضع قد تكلم بما لا يمت إلى الموضوع بصلة مطلقاً، ومهما يكن من أمر فقد ورد في كتاب ((المحصول)) بعض النصوص التي تعالج التوحيد فكان من الواجب على ابي حاتم ان يصلحها ويناقشها ولكنه لم يفعل ، بل فضل عليها تلك القصص الطويلة التي تعالج مواضيع اقل اهمية وهي البيانات التاريخية الواردة في التوراة ، وقد ملأ كتابه بها فتركها غير مصححة مهملاً اياها وبذلك أوجد نوعاً من الارتباك الفكري لاتباع الدعوة الهادية بتقديمه آراء مختلفة ومتباينة ، وخاصة بعد انكبابهم على قراءة كتاب ((المحصول))، ولقد كان ذلك ذا تأثير على نقاوة فكرة مسالك التوحيد وأفساداً لأصول الشريعة ولترتيب الحدود .

I saw that Abu Hatim al-Razi had taken up the errors in the book *al-Maḥṣūl*. Then Abu Yaʿqub al-Sijistani came and defended the author of *al-Maḥṣūl*, insisting on the soundness of his argument and the strength of his theory, and holding that the matters he discussed were not Ismaili philosophical side-issues or branches that could be passed over, for to pass them over would be an affront to the creed and a disturbance of the basic principles and foundations. It seems that Abu Yaʿqub backed certain texts against Abu Hatim al-Razi, attacking him, and in some places speaking in ways that bore no relation to the topic at all. In any event, certain passages in *al-Maḥṣūl* dealing with monotheism made it obligatory for Abu Hatim to correct and discuss them. He did not. He preferred instead to indulge in long stories on subjects of lesser importance, the historical material contained in the Torah, with which he filled his book, leaving *al-Maḥṣūl* uncorrected and neglected. He thereby produced a kind of intellectual confusion among the followers of the quiet daʿwa by setting different and divergent opinions before them, especially once they had taken to reading *al-Maḥṣūl*. This had an effect on the purity of the idea of the paths of monotheism, and led to corruption of the principles of Shariʿa and of the arrangement of the boundaries (*ḥudūd*).

And so I see him continue:

> ولهذا السبب قررت ان اورد هنا الاقوال الاصلية الواردة في كتاب ((الاصلاح)) مصححاً بعض ما جاء في كتاب ((المحصول)) وما ورد في اقوال ((النصرة)) التي تدعم كتاب ((المحصول)) مقدماً رأيي وتعليقاتي عليها وغرضي من ذلك اظهار الاصول بالمعنى الصحيح والفصل بين الاثنين ، وعندما نصل الى هذا الحد يسهل اظهار الخطأ وتبقى التعاليم الصحيحة بمنجاة من الريب والشك وبعيدة عن كل تباين واضطراب .

For this reason I decided to quote here the original statements contained in the book *al-Iṣlāḥ* that correct some of what is mentioned in *al-Maḥṣūl*, along with what has been mentioned in the statements of *al-Nuṣra* that support *al-Maḥṣūl*, presenting my own opinion and comments. My aim is to display the principles in their correct sense and to separate the two positions. Once we have reached this point it becomes easy to expose the error, so that the correct teachings remain free of doubt and ambiguity, and far removed from every disparity and confusion.

Al-Kirmani divided his *Riyāḍ* into ten chapters, each subdivided into several sections. With his literary and scientific deftness, the strength of his exposition, and his learning, he selected what he saw as important and fundamental from the books *al-Iṣlāḥ* and *al-Nuṣra* and recast it in his own style, clear and concise, a kind of artistry wholly different from al-Razi's.

The first chapter is divided into thirty-eight sections and is devoted to the perfection of the soul. The author of *al-Iṣlāḥ* says:

> ان النفس تامة وهي مصدر تام كامل جاءت من الكمال لان العقل هو الكمال .

The soul is perfect, a perfect source issuing from perfection itself, because the intellect is perfection.

The author of *al-Nuṣra* contradicts this, holding that the author of *al-Iṣlāḥ* does not realize that "the perfect" is higher and more complete than "perfection," because the second, perfection, is an attribute of the first and is therefore predicated of it. The perfect, which bears this attribute, is therefore higher, and the attribute attached to it is named after it. The perfect exists, and when perfection exists it becomes the follower, not the followed.

Here the debate between truth and fancy sharpens, and the author of *Riyāḍ* lifts the cover from the logical error. The author of *al-Iṣlāḥ* did not mean perfection in general, but the highest perfection, namely the intellect, the *ʿaql*, which is the cause of the existence of the perfect soul that has come to be characterized by it. And the effect cannot stand higher than the cause. This view leads us to hold that al-Sijistani's attack on al-Razi was out of place and incorrect.

I then take up al-Razi's opinion, in which he says that although the soul is perfect, its actions are not, since these actions depend on time, whereas the soul is the emanation of the intellect, and the intellect is a being composed in itself of origination, time, and perfection. The soul, unlike the intellect, is emanated within time and depends on the supply that comes from the intellect. This dependence does not impair the soul's substance nor render it base, just as the intellect's dependence on the divine will does not make it inferior. The substance is not the inferior of matter on account of this perfection or imperfection, which are born of the soul and are not its substance. It may be said that everything in the first chapter of *Riyāḍ*, namely these two quotations drawn from *al-Iṣlāḥ*, al-Kirmani presents while strongly criticizing the opinions of *al-Nuṣra*, holding that al-Sijistani misunderstood these theories. He likens him to a newcomer who entered the world of the ideals with no goal but material vanity.

> قال صاحب ((النصرة)) مورداً قول امباذقليس على سبيل الاحتجاج به انما صارت اما لأنه لم يخضع لشيء إلا الباري عز وجل وصارت النفس ناقصة لانها خضعت للطبيعة نقول : ان الفلاسفة المتقدمين منهم وان كانوا فضلاء في زمانهم فقد استمر عليهم سلطان الخطأ في كثير مما تكلموا عليه من العقليات ، ولو كانوا في زماننا ورأوا ينابيع البركات كيف هي فائضة من بيت الوحي بحقائق الأمور على ما هي عليه من الدعوة العلوية لكانوا مع اجتهادهم لانفسهم ينصفون انفسهم ويعبرون عن كثير من اعتقاداتهم وفيما غاب عن الحواس يقفون . ولكانوا لآثار من جعل الله له نوراً في غوامض الامور يقنعون . فانهم في ازمانهم اتبعوا آراءهم وعقولهم فيما نحوه فصار كل منهم يذهب في اعتقاده الى ما تدل عليه مصنفاته على ما هو عليه أهل الظاهر في الملة الحنيفية حين اعتمدوا على عقولهم في سلوك مسالك الديانة فاختلفت آراؤهم وبين أولئك تفاضل فمنهم من صوابه اكثر ومنهم من خطأه اكثر والمعول على ما يشهد به ميزان الديانة الذي هو الآفاق والانفس . وفيما تقدم من الكلام على ذلك والبيان لأن اعتقاد الامر فيه على ما شرحه باطلاً غنياً عن التكلف والتطويل فما المعنى فيما أورده امباذقليس الا ما أورده صاحب النصرة في احتجاجه، وقول امباذقليس انصب للطعن ونسأل الله ان ينور عقولنا ويختم بالسعادة أمورنا والخيرات والاعمال الصالحات يوفقنا بمنه ولطفه وكرمه .

The author of *al-Nuṣra* cited the saying of Empedocles in protest: it, the intellect, became as it is only because it was not subject to anything but the Almighty, and the soul became deficient because it has been subject to nature. We say: the philosophers who preceded them, even if they were virtuous in their time, remained under the sway of error in many of the matters they spoke of from their own minds. Had they been in our time, and had they seen the springs of grace overflowing from the House of Revelation with the facts of things as they are, namely the ʿAlawi daʿwa, they would have used their diligence to do themselves justice, would have expressed many of their beliefs, and would have halted before what is absent from the senses. They would have been content to follow the traces of those for whom Allah set a light in the mysteries of things. In their own time, however, they followed their own opinions and their own minds, each going in his belief in the direction his reason pointed him, resting on the same ground as the Ẓāhirīs of the Hanafi sect when they relied on their own minds in walking the paths of religion. Their opinions diverged, and among them there was a distinction, some closer to right, others closer to wrong, and the reliance is on what the balance of religion testifies to, namely the horizons and the souls. Since the matter of belief in it, as has been explained, is false and free of exaggeration and prolixity, what then is the meaning of what Empedocles mentioned beyond what the author of *al-Nuṣra* mentioned in his protest? The saying of Empedocles is set up only for censure. We ask God to enlighten our minds, to seal our affairs with happiness, and to grant us success in good works through His kindness and generosity.

The second chapter is divided into nine sections and is devoted to the relationship between the intellect and the soul. Al-Sijistani objects to al-Razi for raising the topic of motion, repose, and stillness, the central topic that so often caused difficulty for the ancient philosophers. Here motion and stillness are taken as two effects united in "the First" and the Second. This supports what Abu Hatim al-Razi said, that the First was made originative and has become attached to and responsible for origination, since the truth of His station is a duality, the originator and the originated, and these two effects are present in "the First." When the Second was brought forth from the First, the bringing-forth itself became dualized, the same emanation and the same emanated, and so the two effects are linked to the two basic principles. Motion is the benefaction (*fayḍ*) that the First releases upon the Second and which acts upon all assets and entities, while stillness is the acceptance of the First's command throughout all the basic entities.

Al-Razi says:

> أنا لا اقول ان الحركة والسكون هما متولدان من النفس بفعل القوة المستفادة من العقل ولكني اؤكد بان الاثنين هما أثران من النفس متولدان بفعل القوة المتولدة من العقل في الهيولى والصورة ولهذا السبب فان الهيولى والصورة اصبحتا اساس هذا العالم .

I do not say that motion and stillness are generated from the soul by a power received from the intellect. Rather, I maintain that both are two effects of the soul, generated by the power produced by the intellect within the *hayūlā* (matter) and the form. For this reason, matter and form have become the basis of this world.

Al-Sijistani, however, says in his book *al-Nuṣra*:

> ان هذا الكلام من نوع الهذيان لان الحركة تتولد من قصد خاص ((لطلبها)) كما تهدف حركة الاجسام المادية لتغطية فراغ حركة اللهب الصاعدة الى ما فوق أو حركة الارض لتحت وهكذا دواليك .

This argument is delirious, because motion is generated from a specific intention, to seek something, as the motion of physical bodies is aimed either at filling the void through the upward movement of flame, or through the downward movement of earth, and so on. From this I conclude that al-Razi was correct while al-Sijistani was wrong.

The author of *al-Iṣlāḥ* says:

> ونقول : ان الحركة والسكون هما أثران متحدان بالأول والثاني لان الاول لما ابدع اتحد بالابداع فكان لذلك الاتحاد اثرين فيه بالقوة . وانما قلنا أثران لان الابداع والمبدع اثنان واحدهما الابداع والآخر ذات المبدع والابداع والمبدع فان كانا اثنين فهما ايس واحد وهو الكل وهذان الآثران هما في الاول بالقوة ثم انبعث الثاني عن الاول فكان من ذلك الانبعاث اثرين فيه بالفعل احدهما ذات الانبعاث والآخر ذات المنبعث فالأصلان متحدان بالأمر وأحد الاثرين هو الحركة وهو أعلى من الآخر الذي هو السكون كما ان احد الاصلين الذي هو الاول أعلى من الآخر .

We hold that motion and stillness are two effects united in the First and the Second. For when the First was creative it united with creativity, and by that union there were two effects in it in potency. We called them two effects because creativity and the creator are two, one being the creativity and the other the essence of the creator. Yet creativity and the creator, though two, are a single essence, which is the All. These two effects belong to the First in potency, and then the Second was brought forth from the First, so that from that emanation two actual effects arose in it, one the essence of the emanation and the other the essence of the emanated. The two origins are united in the command. One of the two effects, motion, is higher than the other, stillness, just as one of the two origins, the First, is higher than the other.

The third chapter treats the question whether the soul (*al-nafs*) and the spirit (*al-rūḥ*) are one and the same. It is divided into six sections and opens with phrases drawn from *al-Nuṣra*, set among the texts cited from *al-Iṣlāḥ* and arranged in a way that clarifies their original meaning. Here I note that al-Sijistani criticizes al-Razi for something al-Razi never said.

> وقال صاحب ((الاصلاح)) على ما أثبته صاحب ((النصرة)) : ولا نقول ان الطبيعة والهيولى الظلمانية متولدة من الثاني لتؤكد الثاني من الاول ، لأن ذلك العالم عالم الشرف والفضيلة وهذا العالم هو عالم الظلمة والكدورة ، والثاني وان كان متولداً من الاول فانه شبيه به ، ولا يشبه حاله حال الهيولى ، وقال صاحب ((النصرة)) : ان هذا غلط محض لانه لو كان ذلك العالم نورانياً لا ظلمة فيه وهذا العالم ظلمانياً لا نور فيه لم يقدر ان يقع بين هذا .

The author of *al-Iṣlāḥ*, in reply to what the author of *al-Nuṣra* had advanced, said: we do not say that nature and the dark matter are born from the Second, in order to confirm that the Second derives from the First, because that higher world is the world of honor and virtue, while this world is the world of darkness and gloom. The Second, though born of the First, resembles Him, and its case is unlike the case of the *hayūlā*. The author of *al-Nuṣra* replies: this is a pure mistake, for if that world were luminous with no darkness in it, and this world dark with no light in it, no resemblance could fall between them.

The fourth chapter, in eight sections, treats whether souls are parts of the First Truths. I note that al-Sijistani alters the meaning of what is stated in *al-Iṣlāḥ* by rearranging its phrases. On the subject of partial souls I agree with him, granting that the aggregate of these souls does not constitute the universal soul. Al-Sijistani then returns to speak of this world, alluding to the complete Truths, and says that the human soul belongs to the region between the world of nature and the world of rebirth. It is the compound, known by the natural force, and the soul may be raised to the highest world by practicing piety and then by uniting with the Second. He does not stop there. He says:

> ان العقل هو الانبعاث الاول بينما النفوس الناطقة هي الانبعاث الثاني وما جرى في الانبعاث الاول أيضاً انبعث في الثاني .

The intellect is the First Emanation, while the speaking souls are the Second Emanation, and whatever occurred in the first emanation is emanated in the second as well.

I criticize this opinion and refer the reader to my own writings, especially to the epistles *al-Wāʿiẓa* and *al-Muḍīʾa*, in which I say:

> ان العقل ليس إلا ذات الكلمة والكلمة ليست إلا ذات العقل يصحح ذلك قول مولانا الامام المعز لدين الله صلوات الله عليه في كتاب ((تأويل الشريعة)) حيث يتكلم عن العقليات ويشير بان الكلمة عبارة عن العقل وان هذه الاسماء يستحقها بالاضافة وسبق من شرحنا وكلامنا على المبدع والعقل والكلمة وما يقال على عالم الابداع والانبعاث الاول في الرسالة ((المضيئة)) وغيرها من الرسائل ما يكفي الباحث .

The intellect is nothing but the essence of the Word, and the Word is nothing but the essence of the intellect. This is confirmed by the saying of our Master, Imam al-Muʿizz li-Din Allah (ع), in the book *Taʾwīl al-Sharīʿa*, where he speaks of the intellects and indicates that the Word denotes the intellect and that these names are properly applied to it by attribution. What we have already set out concerning the Originator, the intellect, the Word, and what is said of the world of origination and the First Emanation in the epistle *al-Muḍīʾa* and other epistles is enough for the researcher.

Nor do I stop here. I bring forward the words of al-Razi recorded in *al-Iṣlāḥ*, where he says:

> ان الحركة والسكون هما كروح الهيولى والصورة وهذين الاخيرين كجسد الاثنين الاولين وذلك لان الروح ألطف من الجسد والجسد اكثف من الروح ، والحركة والسكون هما ألطف من الهيولى والصورة .

Motion and stillness are to matter and form as the spirit is to the body, and these last two are as the body of the first two, because the spirit is subtler than the body and the body is denser than the spirit. Motion and stillness are subtler than matter and form.

It appears that al-Sijistani rejects this analysis, supporting instead the theory of the author of *al-Maḥṣūl*. I hold that both men erred, especially al-Razi, regarding motion. Motion is the action of the mover, which we call the soul, and stillness is the cessation of the mover's action. Therefore, if the mover is the soul, it cannot resemble motion, which is its own action, and the same applies to stillness. Moreover, motion and stillness cannot be combined into a single thing, because they alternate as opposites, whereas matter and form are inseparable and form one thing. This topic I have fully explained in the book *Rāḥat al-ʿAql*.

The fifth chapter, in seven sections, treats the theme that the human being is the fruit of the material world, as al-Razi says, and I see him assign it to the whole world, its foundation and its roots. Matter is likewise the basis of the substrate formed within it by motion, stillness, and certain other factors that lead to the formation of the different souls. Al-Sijistani calls this a philosophy of *al-dahr*, one that does not acknowledge the existence of the soul after its separation from the body.

Before this, I traced how the human being stands as the fruit of the material world. I turn now to the shape of the book itself, chamber by chamber, for al-Riyad is a garden laid out in ordered beds, and it is worth walking its paths in sequence.

There remains first a school that al-Kirmani weighs without giving a fully decisive answer. It is held that this group follows the school of Democritus and Epicurus, as further pursued by Lucretius. Al-Kirmani says that both schools are right on one side and wrong on another. Man, they hold, is the result of the interaction of natural-planetary and physical action, and man is the purpose of the Second and the purpose of the work of nature. The meaning is this: man is created and divided into two parts, one part belonging to lifeless nature and another part belonging to God, and therefore he is the highest of creatures.

We say: how can what he has said be true, when order and arrangement come only from the Intellect? Or how is it correct that universals are made preferable only by the Intellect, while the soul is unaffected by its effects, being on a par with the animals? It is for this reason that God has lifted the pen from the soul until the effects of the Intellect take hold in it. And we have said above that the matter lies in the agents, not in totalities; not every agent seeks, through its action, to achieve a goal or purpose decreed for it. By the same token, the First Intellect itself is not a compound, so it is in order and arrangement, though the Intellect perceives only true perception.

The sixth chapter, in six sub-chapters, rehearses the theme of motion, stillness, and the image. It maintains that what is reported in *al-Mahsul (The Harvest)*, and what Abu Hatim al-Razi and al-Sijistani all transmitted on this score, is incorrect, and it refers the reader to consult his own book *al-Masabih (The Lamps)*.

Then the author of *al-Nusra* proceeds and undertakes to define body and soul. He defines body by its acceptance of the three dimensions, and then defines the soul as the similarity of the parts, making the soul of the same course as water, fire, and air, each of which is similar in its parts. With this premise it becomes impossible to say that the soul is not embodied. The soul (al-nafs) and the spirit (al-ruh) are synonymous names of one thing, and that thing is not embodied; for were it a body, it would necessarily possess the nine qualities and accidents that the body requires, and could be apprehended by a sense. Nor would the imagination remain fixed in it as to the nine accidents, or any one of them; and when different physical norms hold in it, it must not be a body. Our well-known book *al-Masabih* contains what fully addresses this.

The seventh chapter, divided into seven sub-chapters, deals with the classification of the composite materials of the world. In truth it is dedicated entirely to explaining the status of the human being, who forms a class of his own, and his relationship to all else. For this reason al-Razi says that man is a plant that sleeps upon the substances of the world. Al-Sijistani agrees with him on one side but disagrees on another: the human body disintegrates or dissolves into such substances, and al-Razi classes the human being and likens him to an animal because he possesses the senses and attributes. Al-Sijistani replies that this feeling belongs to the growing vegetative soul. Al-Razi argues that the very speaking human composes a point of difference from the animal, while al-Sijistani counters that the animal too possesses the senses. When al-Kirmani weighs the two opinions, he rules that al-Razi is closer to the truth than al-Sijistani. Finally he says that the resemblance is not only between man and the material world but also between man and the spiritual world, the one who is united in himself being such that everything preceded him in the scale of existence, and he displays the analogies between himself and everything in the material and spiritual worlds.

In the world there are different motions, and in the human being there are also six motions corresponding to the six. In the world there is a mover that moves it, and in the human being likewise there is a mover that moves him, namely the soul. In the world there are the four investigations, that is, the elements, and in the human being likewise there are the four natures. In the world there are rivers, valleys of different tastes, mountains, and minerals, and in the human being likewise, as the author of *al-Islah* has shown, there are bones, cartilage, flesh, blood, and what flows from him and issues out of him. In the world there are things that grow and increase without sensation, and in the human being likewise there are things that grow and increase without sensation, such as hair and nails; and his body itself grows and increases from his youth to his old age. In the world there is the sentient animal as well, and in the human being there are the sentient faculties. The whole world is structures and compositions, and it is complete in what exists. From all this comes the analogy with the world, though what I have mentioned is only a summary, which our writers detail under the title *Peace of Mind*.

The eighth chapter is divided into twenty-four sub-chapters, and its subject is qada (judgment) and qadar (destiny). These two expressions are mentioned in the Holy Quran and point to distinct synonyms, but both mean judgment. This topic had its impact on the long scientific debate that took place in the early Islamic era and never reached a decisive result. Thousands of works in Islamic theology have been written on it, but in truth it is a wrong and time-wasting matter to revisit.

Al-Razi, the author of *al-Islah*, says: in view of the statement of *al-Mahsul* that qada agrees with the First and the Preceding, and qadar with the Second, that is, the next, this is all wrong, because destiny precedes judgment just as the First precedes the Second. The meaning of qadar is estimation, that He made the thing possible, and the meaning of qada is detail, the separation of the thing or its cutting; and the Second, that is, the manifest qada, cannot be set before the First. Al-Razi cites several verses from the Holy Quran in support of his interpretation and likens destiny to the cloth that is drawn in the tailor's imagination before he cuts it; once he has put it into its form, that is, judged it, it is no longer possible to change it.

Al-Sijistani objects to this explanation in defense of his basic theory, which agrees with what is stated in *al-Mahsul*. He says that qada does not mean detail, although qadar means estimation; the first describes and obliges, while the second, which is the judgment, is a void and an end. He adds that the tailor analogy does not fit the case, because qada is the idea of the dress that results from the art of tailoring, which is the sewing, while the second rests upon the soul as the estimation, and so it became destiny. Then al-Kirmani comes and discusses the ideas and opinions. He says: if qada agrees with the First, and the First with the Preceding, and the Following with the Second, then we must admit that the Speaker is suited to the Following, as shown in *al-Mahsul*. Although the Speaker is responsible for defining the Shari'a, it is wrong to combine the Speaker and the Following, because the Speaker is concerned with the material world while the Following, which is the Foundation, is concerned with the spiritual world. The likeness of the Speaker and the Foundation is like the sun and the moon. And here there is a point worth attention: some preachers tended to set the rank of the Foundation above the rank of the Speaker, saying that spiritual worship is higher than legal and natural worship.

The author of *al-Nusra* reports a story from the scholars: when God Almighty created the Intellect, He emptied the two worlds into it, so that it might be the sum of the images of the two worlds. We say: the Intellect that is the sum of the images of the two worlds is not the First Creative Intellect, nor the Second Intellect, which is the First Emanator; rather it is the Intellect that ascends from the world of nature by acquisition, in work and in knowledge, until it parallels the First Emission, whereupon a Second Emission emerged, and thus it became a collection of the images of the worlds despite their multitude, and it can imprint itself with the images of created things.

The ninth chapter al-Kirmani divides into thirty-three sub-chapters. It treats the basis of the principle and the law of Adam, and the laws that came after him, and then proceeds further. In this connection he speaks of the Great Resurrection, saying that it will come when the doors of teaching are closed, the call that has been perfected ceases, and the Qa'im sets aside the ranks in the administration of the da'wa; then those ranks are no longer needed, for a new creation has come.

In this chapter there are also sub-chapters devoted to the question whether Adam, mentioned in the Holy Quran as one of God's interlocutors conveying revelation to humanity and as the Speaker, or in Ismaili idiom the one who holds the role of the age, truly preached a new law, and to whom and how. Here I find no attachment to purely philosophical conjecture, only an effort to collect and explain the few symbols and signs scattered through the Scriptures, and to combine and reconcile those often contradictory references into a convincing theory.

The Christians turned the Adam of the Old Testament, the simple man named in the Torah of the Bible, into a towering figure unlike any other. Islam, alongside a great variety of other views, has inherited this new story of Adam; and of the various groups the Ismailis came closer to the truth than the rest in defining his role, because they considered him to stand at the very beginning of the world, it being enough that the angels were commanded to prostrate to him in honor. Within the Ismaili system it is acknowledged that the seven speaker-prophets are the first and not the last, so Adam, the first, and the others who follow differ only slightly in their work and their duties: Adam did not bring the closing decree, and the Qa'im will not come with a new law, which is why Muhammad is taken as the seal of the apostles and his law as the last.

Al-Kirmani mentions the Great Resurrection because at that time the call will have been completed and the activity of the hudud, the ranks of the call, will have ceased, the task of education will have ended, and a new creation will have begun. This idea seems to reverse what is expected from the Ismaili call when it reaches its summit, when the whole world becomes united under their authority and follows a single religious doctrine, namely the true Islamic law that they preach.

The author of *al-Nusra*, after his interpretation of God's words in the story of the son of Noah, comments on the verse:

> سآوي الى جبل يعصمني من الماء

"I will take refuge on a mountain that will protect me from the water."

Here, he says, the son of Noah shows his meaning by his words:

> سألتجىء الى لاحق

"I will seek refuge in a subordinate."

Then Noah replies:

> لا عاصم اليوم من أمر الله

"There is no protector today from the command of God."

That is, the matter now was received only through those who bear it, for the son of Noah did not know the rank of revelation, even if it was a subordinate rank. Had Adam possessed a revelation for his son, the son would have known this.

As for the tenth chapter, which is the last of the book, it is a critique of all the passages cited from *al-Mahsul* which Abu Hatim al-Razi had left uncorrected. There are roughly nine passages across the fifteen sub-chapters of this section, and I have considered it necessary to provide a translation of the basic passages of *al-Mahsul* to give an idea of its style. Al-Kirmani's response in the second passage stands as an example of his method. He says: in truth, when the author of *al-Mahsul* undertook the propagation of the call and began explaining the foundations and rules of the da'wa, he opened the doors of new departures by writing a number of books for those within it; and the majority accepted his judgment on *al-Mahsul* despite the apology of the author of *al-Islah*. This came as proof of the error and complexity of the subject, and it is unfortunate to see that the reality is the opposite of what they saw and imagined, for al-Razi's corrections demonstrate that he did not challenge the value of *al-Mahsul* or its author, neither by exposing faults nor by issuing judgments, because al-Razi was among the men particularly concerned with repairing and elevating religious knowledge, explaining the truth of the faith, and affirming the duties of man toward God.

From the opinions of al-Kirmani recorded in his books, I judge him to be a great author and a writer trained in the manners and the principles. From one of his epistles I can see that he could make an important note and set it down with restraint, where another man would have sent insults and abuse. Here are some of the passages he corrects:

1. The author of *al-Islah* says, on the subject of monotheism, that God is the Creator of things and of other-than-things, of the intelligible, the intellectual, and the rational, meaning that such materials or topics may fall under the rule of such elements or may not. Al-Kirmani replies that this contradicts the theory that reason (al-aql) is the foundation of creation, behind which there is no other deity, and that nothing can be prior to the intellect in its existence or alongside it in its existence, for this theory entails an association (shirk) and contradicts the law of monotheism. And to speak of creating things and other-than-things, material and non-material, unless one has confused himself and imagined the illusion that there is no world other than this one and nothing that negates creation: how then is it possible to say that God is the creative Creator and at the same time deny this?

2. In the fourth sub-chapter the author of *al-Mahsul* says: the Creator made things from nothing alone, and there is nothing with Him, and He has no end; and if we said only "He and nothing," we would have denied things and other-than-things and made both ourselves and Him creative. We have said that His Self, His identity, whatever idea it may be, is a simple form (sura), and that He made everything continuous and non-continuous, bringing it into this existence by a caused, limited, and finite effect, because nothing comes after the thing, and because the negation of a name is itself a name that comes into being once that name exists, and because the word "no" is not an idea but is itself one of the ideas, a negation of every idea; and this is part of shirk and a refutation of the theory of monotheism. Al-Kirmani refers the student to his own thesis *al-Rawda* and to the book *Tafsir al-Shari'a* by the Fatimid Imam al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah.

3. In the eighth sub-chapter the author of *al-Mahsul* says, in the section investigating the First Creator, that God Almighty created the world all at once, meaning that He created the intellect with a single utterance, and by His power and design displayed within it the images of existing things, the ideas of the world, and everything it contains, without anything else having priority over it, so that the intellect has seniority. Al-Kirmani protests against this because it implies that the intellect operates independently, relying on its own thinking, and this is a grave error, for the intellect is only a mediator in the divine work and not another creator.

4. In the ninth sub-chapter the author of *al-Mahsul* says that eternity and its causality are the unity of God, and the unity of God is eternal. I see al-Kirmani refusing the idea of an eternal unity, regarding it instead as a mediator between God and reason, the second being itself the substance of unity for one primary cause and at the same time the first cause of the act of creating and of the creature, the perfect and the perfecting, the eternal and the everlasting, the existing and what exists.

5. In the eleventh sub-chapter the author of *al-Mahsul* says that the image is visible in the intellect by way of the unity of God, so that the abstract image becomes eternal in it, because the intellect itself becomes wholly eternal and not partial. And al-Kirmani likewise tends to speak about the basic facts even while thinking about the manifestations of the physical world, and suggests that this applies more to the mind of the Speaker, or the Foundation, or the Imam, than to the underlying cause. This is the Nearest Angel, or the Hidden Pen that writes upon the Tablet of existence.

6. In the twelfth sub-chapter the author of *al-Mahsul* says that, since the intellect is caused by the eternity called the Word of God, and since there is no third between them, the intellect becomes similar to it. Al-Kirmani objects, saying that the intellect and the Word are one and the same thing.

7. In the thirteenth sub-chapter the author of *al-Mahsul* says that the intellect received the name Tamm, the Complete, because its becoming was by the Creator, and because creativity was done by the perfect Creator, so that the creator is thereby perfect, which is itself created; that is, creativity can only be complete. I see al-Kirmani objecting, saying that God has no attributes whereby the creator or the state of being created imposes its superiority over the creature.

8. In the fourteenth sub-chapter the author of *al-Mahsul* says that the intellect carries the ideas, and the source from which it draws is the Word exactly, like the sun which sends out light by its own brilliance and not by any natural friction or material cause. If the work of the intellect is the result of the power of the Word and of the Word's ability to convey it, then it becomes clear that the Word is the cause of everything that proceeds from the intellect, and not the intellect itself; and the second, which is the intellect, is only an intermediary between the Word and the hudud, the ranks beneath it. Here too al-Kirmani objects, saying that such an arrangement applies only to the minds of the speakers.

9. Finally, in the fifteenth sub-chapter the author of *al-Mahsul* says that the act of creativity is a medium between the Creator and the created, and the same is true of the agent entrusted with it. He adds that the idea of creativity depends on the agent who influences the image, which is transmitted from the creature to the Creator. The closing section contains scattered sentences that refer to the same topic, drawn from different clues. As one would expect, al-Kirmani rejects these statements and says that this does not agree with the belief of the monotheists, because the act of the creature here is taken to be independent of the Creator.

In truth the idea of monotheism and the understanding of the knowledge of the hudud is hard, indeed very hard; and it is by reaching this knowledge that souls survive, grow, find stability, and endure beyond the world of matter.

The author of *al-Mahsul* said that creativity is a mediator between the Creator and the created, an effect of the influencer upon the influenced. And he said that the image of creativity is intermediate between the doer and the effect, its existence proceeding from the side of the creative doer; therefore the effect of the image is on the side of the creative in the effect, and this image, I mean creativity, has come into the Creative One from the Creative Almighty.

This is the book *al-Riyad* of the great preacher Hamid al-Din Ahmad al-Kirmani, and the sum of the philosophical opinions that were the subject of controversy in his era. Al-Kirmani brought them into the light, drew them with care, refined them, and produced one of the finest philosophical texts in their exposition. In truth the author of *al-Riyad* deserves the gratitude of generations and the appreciation of the Islamic world, because his book is a fruit of thought, a tall tree that grew up in the shadow of this vast world.
