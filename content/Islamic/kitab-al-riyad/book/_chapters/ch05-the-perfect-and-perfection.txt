# The Perfect and Perfection

The book, I have said, gathers ten chapters. Here I open the first of them, from the book *al-Riyad*, on the ruling between the two disputants, in the chapter on what has been said concerning the soul, which is the first emanator.

Section One.

The author of *al-Islah* said: the soul is complete in itself, because it was emitted from the First Intellect already complete. It is a complete emanation from what is complete, because the Intellect is perfection.

The author of *al-Nusra* said, in rebuttal: he does not know that perfection is better and more complete than the perfect. For perfection is an attribute, while the perfect is what is described; perfection is what is borne, while the perfect is the bearer; and the described and the bearer are better and more complete than the attribute and what is borne. For the perfect is described by perfection and bears it, while perfection is the attribute of the perfect and is predicated upon it. This is what they say.

We say: what the author of *al-Islah* means, in saying that the soul is complete because it is emitted from perfection, is not that perfection is an attribute or is no attribute, nor that the perfect is described or is not described. Rather what he means is that perfection is like the cause from which the existence of the effect comes to be. For he saw that the perfect is the cause of the perfect and precedes it in rank, since were it not for perfection the perfect would not be found. The proof is that if perfection is lifted away in the imagination, the perfect is lifted with its lifting, just as with the verbal roots from which the past act, the future act, the agent, and the object are emitted: if the roots are lifted, then whatever would have come from them under inflection is lifted with their lifting. So he treated the First Intellect, being first in existence and being that from which existents came to be, as the cause, and called it perfection; and he treated what he named the emanating soul as the effect, and called it the perfect. Since this was the intent of the author of *al-Islah*, his saying that the First Intellect is perfection is true. And if it is true, then what the author of *al-Nusra* said in this way is a prejudice against the author of *al-Islah*.

Section Two.

Then the author of *al-Nusra* said: the perfect is better than perfection. But the discourse in this place is about the First Principle, which is the First Effect and the First Emanation, and not about anything else; so to say this is to award the honor to the effect rather than to its cause. For the perfect is perfect only by perfection, without which it would not be perfect, and whatever a thing owes its being to is its cause. If that is so, and the perfect exists by perfection, then perfection is the cause of the perfect, and the perfect is the effect of perfection. Yet the author of *al-Nusra* assigns honor and merit to the perfect over perfection, and by that assignment he makes it necessary that the effect be nobler than its cause, which is impossible. For whatever exists by the existence of another, such that when the other is lifted its own existence is lifted with the lifting, that existence by which it exists is below it in honor and precedence. Since this is impossible, the author of *al-Nusra* in what he brought was prejudiced against the author of *al-Islah*.

Section Three.

The author of *al-Nusra* said, secondly: perfection is an attribute and the perfect is what is described; perfection is what is borne and the perfect is the bearer. This he offered as an indication that the perfect is nobler than perfection, and that the bearer is nobler than what is borne. We say: this is not true without qualification. As regards the world of generation and the second emanation, there is what is borne that is nobler than its bearer. Consider the nutritive faculty, which is the proximate cause of the existence of the growing faculty and is its bearer, while the growing faculty is a form borne within it and is its completion; and the growing faculty is nobler than the nutritive. Consider the growing faculty, which is the proximate cause of the existence of the sensory faculty and is its bearer, while the sensory faculty is a form borne within it and is its completion; and the sensory faculty is nobler than the growing. Consider the sensory faculty, which is the proximate cause of the existence of the rational faculty and is its bearer, while the rational faculty is a form borne within it and is its completion. Yet the nobility of one of these is not like the nobility of another.

Moreover, from the side of origination and the first emanation, it is impossible that the First Intellect should be a thing borne, and the Second Intellect emanating from it a bearer; or that the perfect, which is the First Intellect, should be an attribute of the perfect, which is the Second Intellect. Since this is so, the inference of the author of *al-Nusra* in his rebuttal is a prejudice against the author of *al-Islah*. The meaning of what he said would be true only if it were carried onto the natural things, which are far from what the author of *al-Islah* is speaking of, namely the principles of origination and emanation. So the statement of the author of *al-Islah* is correct with regard to the world of origination and emanation; and the statement of the author of *al-Nusra*, though he is mistaken in charging it against the author of *al-Islah*, is correct with regard to the world of body and its accidents.

Section Four.

After this we say: "Perfection" is a name that falls upon the First Thing, which is the First Cause, and it is the First Perfect, which is the First Effect. Its completion is by the relation of its existence to that from which its existence comes, and its completion is by its relation to that upon which its own essence stands. This is like origination and the Originator on this same order. For origination is not a thing other than the Originator; rather it is that by which the Originator is itself. And what is it? It is a single essence: when it is related to that from which its existence came, it is origination; and when it is related to that which bears upon its essence by the mark of coming-to-be, it is the originated. So the First Intellect is perfection, and it is the perfect in truth. It deserves to be called the perfect because in its kind of existence no other of its kind can exist, for the perfect thing is that outside of which no like of it in its kind is found. This holds even among existents in the natural realm, let alone in origination. Thus "body" is a name falling upon a thing complete in its three dimensions, outside of which nothing like it is found; whatever resembles it belongs to it and is counted within it. Likewise the Sun is complete: nothing exists outside it that is like it in its kind.

In sum, the existence of whatever is below the First Intellect, taken in relation to it, is a deficient existence, yet that deficiency is not in its own essence but in its rank, in relation to what is above it. For all things are complete in their own essences, since their existence is from perfection, which is the First Intellect, and there is no deficiency in them in themselves. Were they deficient in their essences, their existence from perfection would be impossible; and it is impossible too that from the deficient there should come a thing other than it, for the deficient, needing the completion of its own essence, cannot bring another into being. Deficiency attaches to them only when they are related to something above them in rank, not in their essence, since the essences, insofar as they are the essences of one thing, are like the numbers, which are complete in themselves and diminish only by their relation to others by which their existence comes. So it is with all the existents of the world: they are complete, with no deficiency in them insofar as they are bodies, and deficient only with respect to their ranks in the forms, and different when some forms are nobler than others, as the human being, who is the noblest of all the forms, is complete in his own essence like others yet deficient in relation to what is above him. If the matter is thus in the bodily realm, then the way of what is not a body, on this order of ranking, is likewise, like to like. And therefore God, exalted be He, said:

سنريهم آياتنا في الآفاق وفي أنفسهم حتى يتبين لهم أنه الحق

We will show them Our signs in the horizons and within their own selves, until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth.

That is, We will set up for them the Imams and the limits, in the outward and the inward, that they may be guided to the state of the observed bodies and the souls whose acts exist, and to what had been hidden from them of the knowledge of the limits of God, which are the truth, so that the truth of the form may become clear to them.

Section Five.

The author of *al-Islah* said: what stands in need of perfection is the act of the soul, not the essence of the soul, because the act of the soul is completed only in time, and the emanation of the soul is together with time, just as the First Intellect and time are one, since the First Originator and origination and time and perfection are one. So the Creator originated all the originated things at a single stroke; and the soul, though it needs to draw benefit from the First, is not thereby deficient in its essence, just as the First was not deficient though it stood in need of the command. Completion and deficiency appear in the act of the soul, not in its essence.

The author of *al-Nusra* said: he did not know that the Intellect is in need of none of the things in the way the soul needs to draw benefit from the Intellect. The state of the Intellect and the command is not like the state of the soul and the Intellect, for the command, from the side of the Intellect, is not a thing other than the Intellect; their identity is one identity. It is impossible to say that a thing needs what is itself; need is only from one thing to another when the other is distinct from it. This is what they say.

We say: the statement of the author of *al-Nusra* is a reply to what the author of *al-Islah* affirmed, namely the Intellect's need of the command only. And since the discourse is upon the principles that come forth from the world of nature, the statement of the author of *al-Nusra* in this is closer to the truth than the statement of the author of *al-Islah*. For what fittingly may be said to need perfection, and to be completed only in time because it is an emanation from the world of nature and not from outside it, is the souls that do not attain the degree of perfection, that is, the ascent to the second degree of perfection, save in time, and whose existence is an emanated existence. So if the statement of the author of *al-Islah* is carried onto this sense, it is correct.

Section Six.

As for the statement of the author of *al-Islah* that the Intellect and time are one, because the Originator and origination and time and perfection are one, this is not sound. For time is the number of the motions of the degrees of the celestial sphere, the imagined duration between the motion of the first of its degrees and the motion of the degree that follows, until the first is reached again. But origination, which is the Intellect, and its perfection, is prior in existence to the sphere by the motion of whose degrees the existence of time comes about. To join the existence of time to the existence of the First Intellect, and to hold that belief, is contrary to what the law of the guiding Da'wa rests upon. We have already set out, concerning the coming-to-be of time and what precedes it in existence, and the evidence for it from the ordinances of the prophetic Law, in our book known as *Rahat al-Aql* (Peace of the Intellect), what is sufficient for the seekers.

Section Seven.

The author of *al-Nusra* said: were it not that the Intellect denies divinity of itself, the one that follows would have no way to pass its knowledge beyond the rank of the Intellect to the Originator. What deficiency is plainer than this, that it knows the Originator only by way of the First?

We say: that may not be believed of the one that follows. For the power coursing through all the noble existents in the world of unity gives them the knowledge that they have an Originator; and were it not for that power, no thing would have existence or subsistence, and it would come to nothing. For this reason every thing deserves the name of oneness, and though it be manifold in itself, its share of that power is unseen and the justice in it is plain. It is impossible to conceive that a single individual of the human kind does not know it has a Creator, or fancies that it has no Creator, let alone that great and majestic Limit. Rather we say: he apprehends that from which his existence comes as the True Object of worship, though within him there is one who directs. Then, if we apply to the First Intellect what the author of *al-Nusra* mentioned, and to the one that follows what he said, it would be necessary that the Foundation, whose standing is in the world of the body, stand in the place of the one that follows in the world of the Intellect, since without the Speaker one would not know that one has a God. But the matter is not so, for each of them has a portion from God, and the Speaker precedes him in rank only by his nearness to the First and his coming after it. And therefore God said:

لا الشمس ينبغي لها أن تدرك القمر ولا الليل سابق النهار وكل في فلك يسبحون

It is not for the sun to overtake the moon, nor does the night outstrip the day; and each is swimming in an orbit.

They are all one from the side of their circling, and differ by their ranks.

Section Eight.

The author of *al-Nusra* said: enough of the deficiency, too, is this, that all the ancients set the soul in the station of the form and the Intellect in the station of prime matter and matter, and said that forms are forever yearning and longing toward matter, and that matter has no subsistence without forms. We say: his saying that the soul is a form is true. But his saying that the Intellect is in the station of prime matter and matter, when the discourse was upon the First Intellect, was a prejudice against the author of *al-Islah* in addressing him with it. For "intellect" is a shared name, and what he said does not hold of what is separate, namely the first intellects of origination and emanation; it holds only of what is in the world of nature among the other intellects, and of the intellect in potency and the intellect in act. Since the discourse was upon what is separate, to carry onto it what is said of what is natural, and to contend with it, is prejudice.

As for his saying that forms forever long and yearn toward matter, and that matter has no subsistence without forms, this is not correct. For the forms do not yearn toward matter, since their existence is not for the sake of matter, nor are they what acquires matter. Rather it is matter that longs toward the forms, since its existence is for the sake of the forms, and it is matter that acquires the forms; and matter, in relation to the forms, receives both the baser form and the nobler form. Since this is so, and he has run the soul in the course of the form and the Intellect in the course of matter, and has ruled that matter has no subsistence without the forms, it is as though he made it necessary that the existence of the Intellect be by the soul. But how can such a belief hold of that whose existence is an existence of origination? Or how can it be conceived that the soul is the cause of the existence of the Intellect and the soul, when the discourse is upon the separate, which comes forth from the world of nature? Never. It is known that the one that follows has its existence by the one that precedes, not the one that precedes by the one that follows; and this is a plain fallacy, arising only from his carrying onto the world of origination and the first emanation what is said of the natural things. For the affair of the existents of the abode of nature, the intellect and soul that are of the class of the second emanation, is only as he mentioned; and from this side it is correct.
