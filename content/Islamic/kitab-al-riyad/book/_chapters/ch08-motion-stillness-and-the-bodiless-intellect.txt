# Motion, Stillness, and the Bodiless Intellect

This second part of Kitab al-Riyad concerns the adjudication between the two disputants on the question that was raised about the intellect that is the first creation.

Chapter One of Part Two.

The author of al-Islah said:

We say: motion and stillness are two effects united in the first and the second. For when the first became creative, it united with creativity, and that union had two effects upon it in potentiality. We said two effects because creativity and the creative are two, one of them being creativity itself and the other being the creative itself; and creativity and the creative, though they are two, are understood as one essence, which is the whole. These two effects exist in the first in potentiality; then the second is emitted from the first, so the emission has two effects upon the intellect, one being the emission itself and the other being the union of the two origins in the matter. One of the two effects is motion, which is higher than the other, which is stillness, just as one of the two origins, which is the first, is higher than the other, which is the second. The motion of the first is the conveyance to the second of all the essences, and stillness is the second's acceptance of the benefit of the first of all the essences and its inclusion of them within itself. I do not say that motion and stillness are born of the soul by the power learned from the intellect; rather, I say they are two effects of the soul, by the power learned from the intellect, in the image and the form, so that the image and the form became the foundation of this world.

The sum of his statement in this section is that motion and stillness are two effects united in the first and the second, that they exist in the first in potentiality, that one of them, namely motion, is higher than the other, that motion is the benefit of the first and stillness the acceptance by the second of that benefit, that the soul received them by the power learned from the intellect in the image and the form, that the first, when it became creative, united with creativity, and that creativity and the creator are two, even if they are one thing.

The author of al-Nusra said:

This statement contains some confusion, for motion is only for the sake of seeking. As for the motion of bodies, it is a seeking of place, as fire moves upward to seek its place and earth moves downward to seek its place; and the motion of souls is to seek benefit. Wherever seeking is established, need is established there, for seeking arises only from some need, and it is impossible to say that the intellect needs something for which it would move. Even if it were permissible to ascribe motion to the two origins, ascribing it to the soul is more fitting than to the intellect, because the soul needs to benefit from the intellect and moves toward it with the motion of longing, the motion of the effect toward its cause. And if it is established that motion belongs to the soul, then stillness belongs to the intellect, for it is still in what it receives of the virtues and lights bestowed upon it by its cause, not seeking increase, because its cause has already poured upon it of the lights and the radiance a measure beyond which the intellect knows no increase for which it would move in seeking. Yet the intellect has not poured forth upon the soul all that was poured upon it, but only a part, so that the soul might dwell by what was poured upon it, while it withheld a part so that the soul might move in seeking it.

The sum of his statement is that motion is only for need, that the intellect is not in need and so does not move, that ascribing motion to the soul is more fitting than ascribing it to the intellect, that motion belongs to the soul and stillness to the intellect, and that the intellect has not poured forth upon the soul all that it has, but only a part, so as to be the cause of the soul's motion in seeking the rest. This is the whole of what the two of them say.

We say: as for the saying of the author of al-Islah, that motion and stillness are two effects united in the first and the second, existing in the first in potentiality and in the second in actuality, this affirms that the first is at rest and in motion in potentiality, since motion and stillness are in it in potentiality, and that the second is in motion and at rest in actuality, since motion and stillness are in it in actuality; and that is impossible in several respects. And the saying of the author of al-Nusra on this point, that the intellect is not in need and therefore is not in motion, is the correct one. For motion, in all its kinds, is found only in natural bodies, and the first and second intellects, being of the first existence, are not bodies, so that they might be in motion or at rest. The proof is that a body requires that it be matter and form together, and that each of the two needs the other in existence, so that its existence is not from God, may He be glorified, as a first existence; for that whose existence is from God as a first existence is an essence with no otherness in it, being the very act that is the first existence from Him, may He be exalted and magnified, and the essence of the act is one essence, not two. If its existence were not a first existence, it would be necessary that another precede it in existence, and it would be something else. So it follows from the argument that if the intellect were a body, another would precede it in existence, whereas it is void that anything precede the first intellect in its existence, since it is the essence of the act that is the cause and the creation, and behind it there is nothing but God the Creator, the Originator, may He be exalted, from whom is its existence. And if it is void that anything precede it in existence, it is void that it be a body; and if it is void that it be a body, and there is no motion except in what is a body, then it is void that there be motion in the intellect; and if it is void that there be motion in it, then the saying of the author of al-Islah, that the intellect has motion in potentiality, is void.

Chapter Two of Part Two.

The author of al-Islah said:

We said two effects because creativity and the creative are two, one of them being creativity itself and the other the creative itself; and creativity and the creative, though they be two, are nothing but a whole. This is a picture that may not be held as a belief, for the matter of creativity and the creative is contrary to what he mentioned, that they are two. Rather, creativity is the creative and the creative is creativity, and there is nothing there but one. When it is referred to that from which it has its existence, it is a creating, by its being an act of Him and the very essence of the act; and when it is referred to itself, it is a created, by its being an object of another, Him from whom is its existence.

Chapter Three of Part Two.

Concerning what the author of al-Islah said, that motion and stillness are in the first in potentiality and in the second in actuality, we say: that whose existence is a first existence can be nothing but pure act and the reality of act. Were it permissible for motion and stillness to be in the first in potentiality, it would follow that the second is more noble than the first, since the ruling of what subsists in actuality, as regards nobility, is not like the ruling of what subsists in potentiality; and it is impossible that the second precede the first in nobility. What he mentioned is among the attributes of that which is later in existence, which needs, in coming forth, an act and something subsisting in actuality. But the first intellect is intellect in actuality, and so likewise is the second, and what follows them of the intellects that are separate from matter; and it is not permissible to hold as a belief, of what is beyond nature among the high limits, what necessarily belongs to a being of the abode of nature and is proper to it.

Chapter Four of Part Two.

As for the saying of the author of al-Islah, that motion and stillness are two effects of the soul, by the power learned from the intellect, in the image and the form, his meaning is that the bodies, which are the matter and the form together, are such that whatever is moved among them is moved by the natural power whose existence within them is from what is outside them, from the first and second intellects, a motion of longing. To place the two effects, mover and moved, in the matter and the form is impossible; but that this is learned from the intellect, in the sense that its existence is from it, is sound in this respect, and this is the best sense that may be attributed to what he said.

Chapter Five of Part Two.

As for the saying of the author of al-Nusra, that motion belongs to the soul and stillness to the intellect, it is not permissible to hold that as a belief concerning those transcendent limits. We say in summary: the world of origination, comprising the preceding and the following, and the pure intellects, which are the high separate letters distinct from bodies, are too high and too exalted for motion or stillness to be imagined in them. For motion, in that which is in motion, comes only from a mover within it that is other than it, and by the two of them is the existence of its essence; and whatever has the existence of its essence by the existence of differing things is an essence of deficiency and need. And since the following, which he named a soul, is of the world of origination, which is proper to completeness and perfection, and its essence is not an essence of deficiency or need, it is void that there be difference within its essence; and if it is void that there be in it what is other than its essence, it is void that it be in motion, and void that motion be in it. Moreover, motion is that by which the thing in which it resides is found passing from its first state to another, whether in the essence or in its states. And the following, which he named a soul, has an eternal existence, by virtue of its arising, from the side of origination, in the second, and its being intellect; and if it is eternal, it is impossible that it pass from what it was upon at first, so it is void that there be motion in it. The saying concerning stillness is just like the saying concerning motion: the first intellect has no stillness, and the following, which he named a soul, has no motion. The author of al-Nusra, in what he said, intended only those souls and intellects that are in the abode of nature, emitted with a second emission; the being there is moved, according to what we have explained in our book known as براحة العقل, for motion and stillness are proper to the world of the body and what is in it, being among the causes of its perfection, apart from what is proper to the abode of origination. But he transferred the meaning, out of eagerness to satisfy the deficiency he inclined toward in support of his master. May God lodge them in the abode of His generosity, and gather them and us with our pure masters (صلوات الله عليهم اجمعين).

Chapter Six of Part Two.

The author of al-Nusra said that motion is for need, and that it is more fitting to the soul than to the intellect. It has already been made clear, from the words that went before, that motion and stillness befit only what is a body or what is in a body, and that the first and second, of whom the discourse speaks, are not bodies, so the ruling of motion and stillness does not apply to them, as we have shown in what preceded of our discourse in our book known as براحة العقل. His saying holds true for what is natural by way of the second emission, not for what is originated, whose existence is by the first emission. Nor is motion in every thing for need, as he mentioned; motion in a natural thing may be among the causes of its perfection, as with the motion of the spheres, whose motions are not for a need in their own essence to attain what they were not given at the first of their being, but rather are a completion belonging to them. Such too is the motion of the speaking one and his striving in gathering the word upon worship and his exertion in the affairs of governance; that is a completion incumbent upon him, for something is required of him, by which he is completed, after his attaining perfection and his rising to the rank of the speaking one. Since this is so, the saying of the author of al-Nusra without qualification, that motion is for need, is not thorough; his ruling is sound only in some natural things, on the ground that it is not separable from them, as we have mentioned.

Chapter Seven of Part Two.

The author of al-Nusra said that the intellect did not pour forth upon the soul all that it has, but only a part. His meaning in this, that the first is distinguished by a rank of precedence and a loftiness of station by what is not proper to what is below it, which he named a soul, is the best sense that may be attributed to his words by interpretation. Otherwise, were the matter as he described it, and did each of the limits withhold from pouring forth upon another some part of what it has, then it would be conceivable of the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وآله) that the blessings reaching him, as an overflow from the world of holiness, were such that he did not make them clear in their fullness to the legatee and his followers, and did not teach them to anyone, and they came to nothing with the ceasing of any benefit from them, so that the legatee (عليه السلام) would be left doubtful in his perfection and his worthiness and his standing in the place of the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وآله), and the obedience made obligatory toward him would be doubtful in its obligation, held merely as opinion. That is not permissible to hold as a belief, neither of the speaking one, nor of the legatee, nor of any of the limits.

Rather we say: the blessings are poured out and bestowed from the side of all the limits, and the reason that those blessings by which perfection is attained in all beings do not reach a being is not any withholding on the part of the limits, but rather the incapacity of the receivers to accept them. Each attains the blessings according to the strength of the receiver: some receive fully, others receive a part; and this is found among natural things, and among compassionate teachers and eager learners. It is not permissible for any of the limits to withhold benefit from one who would benefit from it, so long as he remains a receiver, standing in his right, when he wills, out of fear of his rising to a rank; for that would be before the spiritual birth. And the speaking one (صلى الله عليه وآله) recited what came to him from God, and said:

ولا تقتلوا اولادكم خشية املاق ومن فعل ذلك فقد أثم وتعدى طوره واسقط نفسه عن مرتبة الفاضلين

Do not kill your children for fear of poverty; and whoever does that has sinned, and exceeded his proper stage, and cast himself down from the rank of the excellent. How, then, could it be permissible to lock up the spiritual wealth, which is the sciences and the luminous blessings, from its owners, when the proof of their worthiness stands, and the Prophet has commanded that it be delivered to them, where He says, exalted is the Speaker:

فان أنستم منهم رشداً فادفعوا اليهم اموالهم ولا تأكلوها اسرافاً وبداراً

If you perceive in them sound judgment, then deliver to them their wealth, and do not consume it wastefully and in haste. No indeed: the blessings are poured out only that they may be conveyed to those who deserve them and settled in their proper place, as we have explained in our letters of interpretation and in our assemblies at Basra and Baghdad. May God make us, and a company of our brethren, among those who render the trust to its owners and fulfill its conditions. And perhaps the author of al-Nusra brought forward what he brought forward by way of analogy.

Chapter Eight of Part Two.

The author of al-Nusra said:

Moreover, action is the substance of encompassing, and by it things are encompassed, and this is not the state of what is in motion, for what is in motion does not encompass things; rather it needs something still, at the utmost of stillness, that from its stillness may encompass things. Were the intellect in motion, it would encompass nothing, and the soul would encompass nothing by benefiting from it. Since we have found it encompassing all things, so that nothing escapes it, and it encompasses whoever attains and acquires of its lights and virtues, we then know that the intellect is at the utmost of stillness, and no motion may be imagined in it; while the soul moves only to reach the inmost of the intellect, and then to become still. It does not move in order to encompass by benefiting from the intellect what it wishes to encompass; and so long as the soul is in motion, it does not encompass, nor does it attain the virtues and the lights, until it becomes still by benefiting from the intellect, and only then does it encompass things. The soul is thus in motion, a motion of subsistence, becoming still by benefiting from the one who is still. This is what he said.

We say: the discourse has already passed, on the point that the intellect is not in motion, in a measure that spares us length. It does not follow that, if the intellect is not in motion, it must be still in the manner in which the state of bodies is found, which are not free of being either still or in motion; for its existence is not like the existence of bodies, which are not free of that. In what preceded of the discourse there is sufficiency and evidence that motion and stillness do not befit it. As for what he made necessary by his saying, that the soul, so long as it is in motion, encompasses nothing and attains no virtues, its meaning is not sound, neither for what is separate, from the second, as we mentioned, nor for what is not separate. For the souls that are in the abode of nature acquire only by being in motion in their bodies, through the use of the senses, and inference, and benefiting; and to hold that the matter is so, when a proof is set up for it, suffices as a text upon the continuance of the error in it.

Chapter Nine of Part Two.

Then his saying, that the soul is in motion, a motion of subsistence, becoming still by benefiting from the one who is still, is true when a meaning is assigned to it, and false when it is carried upon the surface of his words. What makes it true is that one takes, of the soul, the natural souls, and it is said of his saying that the soul is in motion because the natural souls, which subsist in potentiality, are in motion for acquisition; and of his saying a motion of subsistence, because their acquisition drives them toward subsistence; and of his saying it becomes still by benefiting, because it passes from potentiality to actuality by acquiring and benefiting and hunting knowledge and virtues, in knowledge and in action; and of his saying from the one who is still, from the one who subsists in actuality. And what makes it false is that his saying is carried upon its surface, for the second is not in motion, nor is the intellect still, since they are not of the abode of the body, that motion and stillness should be necessary to them. Care must be turned to knowing the ranks of the limits, lest the affair of conceiving things in their realities be disordered, and lead to the corruption of the substance of souls. May God illumine our intellects with their ink, and grant us the happiness of following the pure ones of the stages of the divine sciences and the treasuries of the holy lights (صلوات الله عليهم).
