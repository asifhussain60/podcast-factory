# The Elephant and the Well

Among the traditions that speak to 'Abd al-Muttalib's virtue, honour, and unshakeable trust in his Lord is the following account, concerning the Abyssinian campaign against Yemen. Abraha al-Ashram, the Abyssinian viceroy of Yemen, marched with the intent of destroying the Sacred House of God, bringing with him an elephant named Yaksom.

A Yemeni nobleman named Dhu Nafr rose against him, calling his people and whatever Arabs answered his appeal to wage jihad in defence of the Sacred House. He was one of Yemen's great lords and kings. He engaged Abraha in battle, but Abraha defeated him and his companions, took Dhu Nafr captive, and marched on with him in his train.

Abraha then confronted Nafil ibn Habib al-Khath'ami at the pass of Khath'am, along with two tribes. He routed them and captured Nafil. When Abraha intended to kill him, Nafil said: "Do not kill me — spare me and I will guide you wherever you wish." So Abraha kept him as a guide and marched on.

As Abraha's armies drew near the Haram and approached Mecca, the Quraysh realised they had no means to resist him by force. They withdrew, while 'Abd al-Muttalib stood firm and declared: "By Allah, I shall not abandon the Haram. I seek no glory elsewhere." He remained in the city while the Quraysh dispersed.

'Abd al-Muttalib said:

> *No, let them not think the Ra Yum concerns us —*
> *we shall settle it, so guard what is lawful to you.*
> *Though their prayers beat down and their encampments become your sanctuaries,*
> *do not let them overpower your Qurhan.*

The word rendered "impossible" here is al-Qurrah, as in the Almighty's saying, ﴿ مُسْتَقَرٌّ ﴾ [al-Ra'd: 13].

The Quraysh then departed, and 'Abd al-Muttalib rose greatly in their esteem when they witnessed how firmly he forbade himself to Allah and placed his complete trust in Him — a mark of his sound and penetrating judgment in divine affairs.

When the army of the Elephant drew near the outskirts of Mecca, they struck 'Abd al-Muttalib's camels and drove them off. Word of this reached Abraha, along with news of 'Abd al-Muttalib's standing and his position among his people. Abraha sent for him, and when he came, Abraha marvelled at his bearing, his dignity, and his commanding presence. He honoured him, seated him beside himself, and asked whether he had a need to present. 'Abd al-Muttalib said: "Your army has seized my camels — I ask that you return them to me." Abraha was astonished and said: "You have diminished yourself in my eyes and lowered your standing with me. You come to ask about your camels, yet you say nothing of this House that I have come to demolish — does it mean nothing to you?" 'Abd al-Muttalib replied: "Those camels are mine, and I am their owner. As for the House, it has a Lord who will protect it from you if He wills."

This reply made 'Abd al-Muttalib only greater in Abraha's eyes, and his words carried enormous weight with him. Abraha ordered the camels returned.

Then Allah vindicated the words of 'Abd al-Muttalib:

> ﴿ وَأَرْسَلَ عَلَيْهِمْ طَيْرًا أَبَابِيلَ ﴾ ﴿ تَرْمِيهِم بِحِجَارَةٍ مِّن سِجِّيلٍ ﴾
> "And He sent against them birds in flocks, striking them with stones of baked clay." (al-Fil: 3–4)

It was said that these birds resembled swallows or martins; each bird carried three stones — one in its beak and one in each claw — roughly the size of chickpeas or lentils. Any man struck by one of them perished, though not all were struck. The army broke and fled, scattering along the road back to Yemen.

When Nafil witnessed the divine wrath descending upon them, he cried out:

> *Where is the refuge for one whom God pursues? The victorious has no escape.*

Nafil also said:

> *We greet you with the morning — eyes that saw*
> *what your wing-tips spared us.*
> *I have with me a hedgehog we never saw before;*
> *grieve not for what has befallen us.*
> *Stones were cast upon us*
> *as though the Abyssinians had become our creed —*
> *did you love us? We gave no answer.*
> *We shall dine with you.*
> *Rudeina, if you see or find abundance,*
> *then excuse me and praise my conduct.*
> *I thanked God when I saw the bird,*
> *while all the people ask about the gheeel.*

The army fled, falling in every direction, perishing along every road. Abraha himself was struck in his body; they carried him away as his limbs fell from him piece by piece, each inch dropping away and leaving behind a trail of pus, until they finally brought him into San'a' in a state like a fledgling chick. He did not die until his chest split open.

'Abd al-Muttalib said concerning these events:

> *His heart, as they claim, was in what it harboured.*
> *His building demolishes the lyric —*
> *his fathers rushed toward the House of God,*
> *guided through the age of sin.*
> *You are firm, yet your wealth is not firm;*
> *you change grey hair after youth —*
> *so what do you wear as your alleged garment?*
> *Let your remembrance of the matter of union be forbidden;*
> *you are the one I dream of recalling —*
> *an army that came to you in al-Ashram,*
> *as though they were a people laden with spoils.*
> *The next day they were left like al-Battah,*
> *overwhelmed by what afflicted them.*
> *An elephant they led to the lowest ground —*
> *its Creator honours His close friend.*
> *Where is he who stood in his era?*
> *Their eyes were not shielded from the elephant.*
> *God repented of its destruction —*
> *as though its fountains overflowed with remorse.*
> *The Ababeel birds flew to pelt them;*
> *the stones lodged fast in Hama.*
> *He honoured the people of the sanctuary as the books record,*
> *and despite Abraha's arrogance he was compelled.*
> *With victory from God, the Lord of all servants —*
> *a promise rhyming true with right —*
> *the eagles became the scavengers of their remains,*
> *circling as mourners do.*
> *A land whose Zamzam was dug,*
> *and may God bequeath us the finest of lands.*

'Abd al-Muttalib also has these verses on the same subject:

> *We are the family of God in His town, as it has ever been since the age of Abraham.*
> *Whoever seeks to corrupt it will be silenced.*
> *The House has a God who defends it;*
> *even in the ancient days, it was cast into strife — and was preserved.*
> *I have watched the wicked dragged to their ruin.*

There was indeed a clear sign and a lasting lesson in the destruction of the Companions of the Elephant, through which Allah protected His Sacred House and forbade its violation. He manifested the virtue of its guardian and vindicated His prophet 'Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim — confirming his promise to him and answering his words: "The House has a Lord who protects it." And He answered his supplication.

That same year — the Year of the Elephant — saw the birth of al-Mustafa Muhammad, master of all humanity and seal of the prophets, may God bless him and grant him and his family peace. This was among the blessings heralding his birth, and his religion — peace and blessings be upon him — was thereby blessed.

Then 'Abd al-Muttalib received a vision in his sleep in which he was commanded: "Dig Zamzam — the hidden place of the Grand Sheikh." He awoke and said, "O Allah, make it plain to me." He returned to sleep, and the vision came again, saying: "Dig the troubled woman — none shall be able to withhold her from the people but you." Again he said, "O Allah, make it plain to me," and returned to sleep. The vision came a third time, saying: "Dig Zamzam, between al-Farth and the blood, where the crow alights, hottest above the village of the ants, toward the red monuments."

He rose and went to the Sacred Mosque, where he sat and waited for the sign he had been shown.

A group of people then slaughtered a cow, and it broke free from its tether and bolted into the Sacred Mosque, falling in the very spot of Zamzam. The tether was recognised, and its owner made for that place, slaughtered the cow there, and skinned it. A crow then came and settled until it landed in the manure, where 'Abd al-Muttalib found an anthill. He rose and began digging there for Zamzam.

The Quraysh came to him and said: "O 'Abd al-Muttalib, what do you think you are doing — digging in our mosque without our knowledge?" He replied: "I have been commanded to do this, and He who commanded me will repel anyone who tries to stop me." They left off when they understood that a divine charge had been laid upon him, and they were safe from His ignorance of their affairs. He continued digging until he uncovered swords and shields that had long been buried in Zamzam. When the Quraysh saw this, they said: "Give us a share of what you have found." He replied: "It belongs to the House of God." He dug on until the water welled up; he then contained it so that it would not overflow, and built a basin over it.

Zamzam is the well of Ismail ibn Ibrahim, peace be upon him. It is narrated that during a year of drought, Ismail was suffering from thirst with his mother Hajar; she sought refuge in God and called upon Him, and Allah caused the spring of Zamzam to well up before them. When it later fell into disuse, it was buried, and what had been placed in it was buried with it — until the excavation of 'Abd al-Muttalib.

Concerning 'Abd al-Muttalib's excavation of Zamzam, Khuwaylid ibn Asad ibn 'Abd al-'Uzza says:

> *I say — and what shall I say of him because of this? —*
> *to you, O Ibn Salma: you are the digger of Zamzam.*
> *The excavation of Ibrahim on the day of Hajar's son,*
> *and the running of Jibril in the age of Adam.*

It is said that 'Abd al-Muttalib found a golden gazelle among the buried items and greatly admired it. He cast lots over it with arrows — some for the House and some for himself — and the arrow of the House came out, so he adorned the House with it. It was the first ornament with which the House was ever adorned.

It is also narrated from 'Ali, may Allah's blessings be upon him: when the Quraysh saw what 'Abd al-Muttalib had brought forth from Zamzam, they gathered around him and said: "O 'Abd al-Muttalib, this is the well of our father Ishmael, and we have a right to it — let us share in it and in whatever you find." He said: "I will not do that, for this is a matter that was entrusted to me alone and not to you, and He granted it to me from among you." They refused to accept this unless he shared it with them, saying: "Our judge in this shall be such-and-such." He agreed to take the matter before the soothsayer of Bani Sa'd ibn Hudhaym.

He set out with them, and each tribe of the Quraysh sent one man. The land at that time was waterless; as they made their way through one of those parched stretches between the Hijaz and the Levant, the water ran out for 'Abd al-Muttalib and his companions. He asked the other tribes of Quraysh for water, but they refused, saying: "We are ourselves in want, and we fear for ourselves what has befallen you." Seeing no other course, they dismounted. Each man dug a pit and sat in it, agreeing: "Whoever among us dies first shall be buried by those who remain." Then 'Abd al-Muttalib said: "By Allah, it is weakness and surrender to simply sit here and wait for death without striking the earth in search of water." He rose, mounted his camel, and when it got up from beneath him, water burst forth from under its resting place — a sweet spring. 'Abd al-Muttalib dismounted, gave thanks and drank deeply.

He and his companions drank and filled their water-skins. Then he called to those tribes of Quraysh who were with him and said: "Come to the water — Allah has provided us with water." They came and drank, and then they said: "By Allah, Allah has already given His judgment for you against us, O 'Abd al-Muttalib. We shall never dispute with you again over Zamzam. Whoever gave you water from this camel in the wilderness is your waterman of Zamzam — go back and tend your watering as its rightful master." So he returned, and they returned, and he was set apart from them in this matter.

This is among the virtues that one mentions not to boast, but because it speaks to the truth of his visions — for what he saw concerning Zamzam was a clear sign of Allah's favour and of what Allah had singled him out for. As the Prophet, may God bless him and his family, said: "A true vision is one of twenty-six parts of prophecy."

And what he was then shown — the appearance of the water in Zamzam, the well of Ishmael, peace be upon him — was a blessing for him: Allah provided him with water, caused it to spring from the earth for him, and made it a miracle witnessed by all his people, one that none among them could deny or diminish.

'Abd al-Muttalib was the finest of the Quraysh, their chief, and their master. In him the light of prophecy was visible, along with the majesty of kingship, the awe of virtue, and his qualities are beyond enumeration. He was called "Sayyid al-Wadi" — the Master of the Valley — meaning Mecca and its surroundings.
