## Where this episode picks up

How do you break a document that hangs inside the holiest building in Arabia? In this history it takes two things at once, and they arrive from opposite directions: five men meeting after dark at the head of a pilgrim track, and a small crawling creature nobody saw.

That double answer is the shape of this whole episode. Everything in it happens twice — once at the level of men making arrangements, and once at a level the men in the story cannot reach. The blockade is broken by a conspiracy and by an insect. The obligation of prayer is fixed in heaven and then argued down by a prophet who had already watched a community fail. And the protection that had kept the Prophet alive in Mecca is removed by two ordinary deaths, three days apart.

The previous episode closed with two clans walking into a single valley behind an old man who had never accepted the religion he was defending, and with one relative walking the other way. This one opens three years into that valley and does not leave Mecca until both of the people who had made Mecca survivable are buried.

Three movements. First the blockade itself — what it did to people, who smuggled food through it, and the night it was undone. Then the night journey to Jerusalem and beyond it, which the author places here, in the middle of the pressure, along with the account of how the five daily prayers came to be five. Then the year in which Abu Talib and Khadijah both died, and the Quraysh discovered what they had actually been afraid of all along.

Watch for one thing running through all of it. In this stretch of the history the protections around the Prophet are removed one at a time — first the ability to trade, then the shelter of an uncle, then the household that absorbed his exhaustion — and every removal is answered by something that cannot be taken away in the same manner.

## The document eaten away

The blockade held for three years. Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib lived under it without contracting marriages and without conducting trade, and food reached them only in secret.

Hold on to that last clause, because everything that follows lives inside it. A boycott in a valley that grows nothing is not primarily a legal instrument; it is a supply problem. It works only if every seller in Mecca honors it every day for three years, and it fails the moment enough people decide privately that it is beneath them.

Enough people did. The one the author names is Hakim ibn Hizam ibn Khuwaylid ibn Asad, a man of sound judgment, wealth, standing and protection among the Quraysh — and the nephew of Khadijah bint Khuwaylid. He frequently sent food to Banu Hashim in general and to the Messenger of God in particular, out of regard for his aunt and her bond with him. He would send caravan after caravan by night, and the people of Banu Hashim would come out and meet him and take what he brought.

Note the mechanism, because it explains why the boycott was doomed. Nobody is defying it publicly. It is simply being disregarded in the dark by a man too well protected to touch, on grounds of family that the Quraysh themselves considered sacred.

One night Abu Jahl ibn Hisham intercepted him. "Are you going with food to Banu Hashim? I swear, you and your food shall not pass until I have exposed you before all of Mecca."

That threat tells you what enforcement had come down to: not punishment, but exposure. Abu Jahl cannot seize the goods. His weapon is publicity — he intends to turn Hakim's private decency into a public position, which is the one thing Hakim has been careful to avoid.

Then Abu al-Bakhtari ibn Hisham ibn al-Harith ibn Asad came upon them and asked what was happening. Abu Jahl said: he is carrying food to Banu Hashim. And Abu al-Bakhtari answered with a legal fiction delivered at speed: this is food belonging to his aunt that she had left with him, and he is sending it to her. Are you going to prevent a man from bringing food to his aunt? Get out of this man's way.

He does not dispute the boycott. He re-describes the cargo. The food is not aid to a blockaded clan; it is a woman's own property going back to her — and a man who stands between a woman and her property is not enforcing a pact, he is committing an ordinary theft. In one sentence the enforcer has been moved from prosecutor to defendant.

Abu Jahl leapt at him, and Abu al-Bakhtari struck him with a camel's jawbone, landing a severe blow. Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib witnessed it not long after — and then the detail that says the most about where Mecca had arrived. Abu Jahl hated that the companions of the Messenger of God should come to hear of it. So they kept it quiet, and mocked him in silence.

The man enforcing the boycott takes a beating over a load of smuggled food, and his own side suppresses the story to protect his standing. The pact was already being maintained as a performance.

Meanwhile the campaign against the Prophet himself had changed character. He called his people day and night, openly and in secret, without fearing anyone in it — and the Quraysh, finding that his uncle and his kinsmen of Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib stood between them and their desire to oppress him, fell back on mockery of him, his family and his companions.

Among those who did so were Abu Lahab and his wife Umm Jamil bint Harb ibn Umayyah — the woman the Qur'an describes as the carrier of firewood. It was said that she gathered thorny wood and scattered it along the path where he would walk; it was also said that she spread slander. Then the revelation came concerning them both: "Perish the hands of Abu Lahab, and perish he. His wealth availed him nothing, nor what he earned" (chapter 111, verses 1 and 2), and the rest of the chapter.

When she heard what had been revealed about her, Umm Jamil came to the mosque with a piece of stone in her hand. The Messenger of God was sitting near the Ka'bah with Abu Bakr beside him. She said: O Abu Bakr, I have heard what your companion has said about me — and answered it with a line of her own verse, that they had disobeyed and that his religion was contemptible.

Then the sentence that makes the scene: and I was kept from seeing the Messenger of God. She was standing in front of him, carrying a stone, addressing the man sitting next to him, and she could not see him.

The same pattern governs the insult they had settled on. The Quraysh used to call him by a name of reproach and curse him with it. And he said to his companions: does it not amaze you how God has turned the harm of the Quraysh away from me? They curse and revile a man they call the reproachable one — and I am Muhammad.

Their curse required them to rename him first, because the name he had could not carry the insult; and having renamed him, they were cursing somebody who does not exist.

Abu Talib meanwhile was working on his brother. He addressed verses to Abu Lahab, reproaching him for withdrawing from Banu Hashim: that the good sense of nations had left him in foolishness, and that there was nobody standing close to him in rank now except the envious man who harbors treachery. Then the appeal. Be a man of solidarity and honor, and do not abandon him so long as I live and breathe. A man has come from the finest of the sons of Manaf — do not ride out against him. And the argument at the center of it, put to a brother who had gone over to men bound to him by nothing: if he has kinship, you have a bond of honor. Our cousins, are you too weak?

Then an incident that shows what the appeal was aimed at. Abu Salamah ibn 'Abd al-Asad sought Abu Talib's protection against his own people, Banu Makhzum, who wished to kill him — and Abu Talib granted it. So Banu Makhzum came and put the obvious objection: you have prevented us from your nephew Muhammad, so why do you also prevent us from our own man?

His answer is entirely within the old law and does not mention religion at all. He has sought my protection, and he is my sister's son. If I did not protect my nephew, I would not protect my brother's son.

They argued with him over it. And then Abu Lahab stood up — the one man who had broken from Banu Hashim — and spoke on his brother's side. O assembly of Quraysh, you have burdened this elder beyond measure; you keep pressing on him in the presence of his own people. By God, either step back from this matter or stand with him in what he has done, until he reaches what he seeks.

They withdrew. And the author's comment is dry and exact: they had in that a guardian and a supporter for the Messenger of God. The defector defends the arrangement he defected from, on clan grounds, because the sight of a crowd of Quraysh leaning on one old man offended him more than the religion did. Abu Talib went straight back to verse, urging him to go further and support the Messenger outright — asking where his own counsel had gone, telling him he was not created for helplessness, and cursing 'Abd Shams and Nawfal for scattering the family after friendship and affection.

Then the document came down, and it came down by arrangement.

Hisham ibn 'Amr ibn al-Harith moved first. He was a man of standing among his people, and he was among those who had been keeping watch over Banu Hashim — that is, one of the smugglers — and he had been doing it for a long time. He went to Zuhayr ibn Abi Umayyah of Makhzum. Then to al-Mut'im ibn 'Adi of Nawfal ibn 'Abd Manaf. Then to Abu al-Bakhtari ibn Hisham. Then to Zama'ah ibn al-Aswad.

Look at the spread. Five men, no two of them from the same clan: he is not building a faction, he is building a chorus that cannot be answered by pointing at anyone's kin.

They gathered at the head of the pilgrim track by night and stated the case to each other in the plainest terms. We eat food, we wear clothes, we take wives in marriage — yet the sons of Hashim: nothing is sold to them nor bought from them, they cannot marry nor be given in marriage.

Then they made a pact and agreed to repudiate the document, and Zuhayr said: I will start by speaking and be the first among you.

The next morning Zuhayr circled the Ka'bah seven times, then turned to the people. O people of Mecca — we eat food and wear clothes, while the Banu Hashim perish: nothing is sold to them, nothing is bought from them. I swear I will not sit until this unjust and contemptible document is torn apart.

He circles the House first. The document is hanging inside it, and he performs the sanctuary's own ritual before declaring that what is stored in it is unjust.

Abu Jahl was in a corner of the mosque. You lie — by the House of God, it shall not be torn.

Zama'ah ibn al-Aswad: you, by God, are the liar. We did not approve of this document when it was written.

Abu al-Bakhtari: Zama'ah speaks the truth. We are not satisfied with what is written in it.

Al-Mut'im ibn 'Adi: you both speak the truth, and whoever says otherwise lies. We disavow before God what is written in it.

And Hisham ibn 'Amr said the same.

Four voices in sequence, each certifying the one before. Abu Jahl had one interruption available and had already used it, and what he says next is the sentence of a man who understands exactly what has been done to him and has no answer: this is a matter that was decided overnight — meaning it was arranged without him. He is right, and it does not help.

And into that, from the other direction, comes the announcement.

The Messenger of God said to his uncle: O uncle, my Lord has sent a small creature against the document of the Quraysh, and it has eaten away every name of God that was in it, leaving only the injustice, the severance of ties, and the falsehood in it. God has informed me of this.

Abu Talib took it straight to the assembly gathered at the side of the mosque. O assembly of Quraysh, my nephew has told me such-and-such — so bring out your document. If it is as he has described, then cease your boycott against us.

The people said: we are satisfied. They agreed to that, then examined the document, and found it exactly as he had said.

Two things are worth pausing on. The first is the wager: a claim about the physical condition of an object nobody had looked at, staked in public, checkable in the time it takes to unroll a parchment, with the whole boycott riding on it — and the terms accepted by the other side before it was unrolled. The second is what the creature is reported to have left behind. Not a ruined sheet. Every name of God gone, and the injustice, the severance of kinship and the falsehood all still legible. The document was not destroyed. It was edited down to what it had always been, with the sacred language stripped out of it, and then handed back to the men who wrote it so they could read it that way.

Abu Talib composed verses on the tearing, and they are less triumphant than you would expect. He swears that the men who resolved on it came to the family's own waters for the work of their Lord, and that everything which does not please God is destined to corrupt; that the document has been torn, its knots undone, its binding spell unpicked, and that the magic of the end of ages could not prevail. He blesses the men who had stood at the head of the pilgrim track. And he closes with the family's position rather than its victory: so we fortified ourselves in the heart of Mecca; who is to be driven out of Mecca? We did not yield, and we grew up in it when its people were few.

A second poem drops the imagery and states the case. The document held a lesson. Whoever invents what is not true is a liar, and what they said has been rendered void. He tells his own people not to oppress the family, which has never departed from their counsel — and ends where he always ends, with the same two claims side by side: a hand of Hashim will protect him from us, and God, who is his God, shall help him.

## The night journey and the prayers

The author places the night journey here, in the middle of the pressure, and introduces it with a single clause of timing: when Islam had spread, the Messenger of God was taken by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Al-Aqsa Mosque — that is, Bayt al-Maqdis, which is Aelia.

'Abdullah ibn Mas'ud gave the account of it. The Messenger of God was brought the Buraq, the animal that used to carry the prophets before him, and it placed its hoof at the farthest extent of its reach. It bore him, and God showed him His signs between the heavens and the earth. When he reached Jerusalem he found Abraham, Moses and Jesus there — a company of the prophets gathered for him — and he led them in prayer.

Two details in that carry the weight. The mount is described as the one that used to carry the prophets before him, so the journey is not an innovation but a resumption; and its stride is measured not in distance but by the limit of its own sight. And what happens at the other end is a prayer, with a congregation, in ranks. The claim being made about him is not made in a sentence. It is made by where he is standing in the row.

Abu Sa'id al-Khudri reported what he said about the ascent that followed. When I had finished what I came to do in Jerusalem, a ladder was brought to me — the Mi'raj — and I had never seen anything more beautiful than it. It is what the dying person looks toward when death approaches. My Lord's messenger, Jibril, ascended me through the heavens. And then he ascended to the highest horizon, and he descended.

One clause in that reaches ordinary people and is easy to read past: it is what the dying person looks toward when death approaches. The most exalted object in the account is also the last thing every human being sees. The description of a unique journey turns, in a single sentence, into a description of a universal one.

Then the author places here a family correction on a related matter, and it arrives with visible temper. It was narrated from Ja'far ibn Muhammad, from his father, from his grandfather, from al-Husayn ibn 'Ali ibn Abi Talib, that al-Husayn was asked about the claim people made that the call to prayer originated in a dream seen by 'Abdullah ibn Zayd — that the Prophet was told of the dream and then commanded the call on that basis.

His answer was one sentence long. Revelation descends upon us, and you claim that he took the call to prayer from 'Abdullah ibn Zayd? The call to prayer is the foundation of your religion.

He grew angry, and then gave the household's account, from Ali ibn Abi Talib: that God sent down an angel the like of which had never been seen in the heavens before that time nor after; that the angel gave the call to prayer and intoned it in pairs; and that Gabriel said, O Muhammad, this is how permission to pray has been granted.

What offends him is not the man in the dream. It is the shape of the claim. The daily summons that orders the life of the whole community either came down with the revelation or came out of somebody's sleep, and the difference between those two is the difference between a religion and a custom — which is the same principle about a designated, unbroken source that this history has been arguing from its opening pages.

Two short reports follow on what the ascent yielded. He said: when I was taken up to heaven, it was said to me, where does the highest rank lie? I said, I do not know — inform me. It was said: in performing ablution thoroughly even in hardship, walking to Friday prayers, and waiting for one prayer after another. The question sounds cosmological and the answer is a list of small inconveniences.

Then the obligation itself, narrated from Ja'far ibn Muhammad. God made prayer obligatory: fifty prayers in every day and night. Then God showed mercy to His creation and was gracious to them, and reduced them to five.

The reason is the striking part. On the night journey he passed the prophets, and none of them questioned him until he came to Moses, who did. Moses asked what had happened, and when he was told, he said: return to your Lord and ask Him to lighten the burden upon your community — for I have seen what the Children of Israel were like; even when the commandments were revealed to them, they rejected them.

So he returned and petitioned his Lord, and the number was reduced. When he came back to Moses, Moses said: return. He returned, and it was reduced by five. And Moses continued to send him back, and it was reduced by five each time, until they reached five prayers — at which point the Messenger of God felt too ashamed to go back again.

Then Abu 'Abdallah said: God rewarded Moses on behalf of this community — what a reward.

Read that sequence for what it actually describes. The daily obligation of an entire religion was settled by negotiation, and the negotiator was a prophet of an older community arguing from the record of his own people's failure. He is not claiming that fifty was unjust. He is reporting, as evidence, what he watched human beings do with commandments — and the reduction is granted on the strength of that testimony. And the process ends not with a ruling but with embarrassment: the number stops at five because the man carrying the request could not bring himself to ask a tenth time.

There is one more report from the ascent, and the author gives it in the form of a correction. It was narrated that he said: when I was taken up to heaven, I saw an angel in the likeness of my brother 'Ali ibn Abi Talib. He was asked about this, and he said: no — rather, the angels had longed to behold the face of 'Ali, and so God created this angel; when they longed to look upon him, they looked upon it.

The correction is the content. The resemblance does not run the way a listener assumes. The angel was not the original that Ali resembled; the angel was made afterward, as a remedy for a longing, and the face it was given was his.

Then the return to Mecca, and the reception. When he came out to his people he told them that God had taken him on this journey and had shown him wonders of what he had seen. Some of them accepted him. Some called him a liar. And some turned away from Islam altogether when they heard it.

That third group is why the account is here. Then God revealed, concerning those who apostatized: "And We did not make the vision We showed you except as a trial for the people, and the tree cursed in the Qur'an — We threaten them, but it increases them only in great transgression" (chapter 17, verse 60).

The verse describes the miracle as a trial rather than as a proof. It was not sent to end the argument; it was sent to sort the people hearing it, and the sorting included losses.

Two women's reports close the matter, and both are concerned with the same difficulty: whether this happened to a man or to a sleeper.

'A'isha bint Abi Bakr said that he was taken by night on the Isra' in body, and that they were cleared of the claim otherwise. The question of whether the journey happened in the body or only in vision was live from the very first telling — the verse just quoted calls it a vision — and this is the answer the household gave to it. The distinction is not a technicality. A vision is a private experience that costs nobody anything to accept; a body that left Mecca and returned before dawn is a claim about the world, and it is the kind of claim that can be tested, which is exactly what happened next.

And Umm Hani bint Abi Talib, in whose house he spent that night, gives the domestic frame. He slept after the last of the evening prayer. Before dawn broke he woke them. He prayed the dawn prayer and they prayed with him, and then he told them the account of the Isra'. And when the Quraysh denied him, he came out to them and described the roads of Bayt al-Maqdis, and they recognized that he spoke the truth.

Notice what he offered as evidence. Not a wonder — a road map, of a city on the trade route, checkable by anyone in Mecca who had made the journey the ordinary way.

## The year of sorrow

Through all of this, the author says, he remained steadfast in supplication, earnest in his desire to guide God's servants, and patient under the harm his people inflicted. The Book was being revealed to him, and with every descent new verses and proofs came down to bear witness for him. The polytheists pressed their opposition relentlessly, setting traps, and working to turn away both their own people who might have inclined toward him and the outsiders who had heard of him and come to look at the signs of his prophethood.

Then the author names the three people who made that endurance possible, and it reads like an inventory taken just before a loss.

His uncle Abu Talib stood by him, supported him, and fought on his behalf — so that no serious harm reached him except words of abuse and veiled insinuation. He would find relief with Abu Talib whenever his chest tightened, and be eased of his distress whenever the Quraysh pressed him.

He found in Hamza great support and much benefit; many of the reckless among the Quraysh were kept off him on Hamza's account.

And when he took refuge in his home and told Khadijah what he had endured, he found in her pride in him; she devoted her wealth and everything her hands possessed entirely to him, and she served him herself. He never found another who gave him the way out of what weighed on him as she did, nor a protector like his uncle.

They all died, the author writes, in a single year.

The first of them went slowly enough for a negotiation. When Abu Talib was dying there came to him 'Utbah and Shaybah the sons of Rabi'ah, Abu Jahl ibn Hisham and Umayyah ibn Khalaf, with men of Banu 'Abd Shams and Banu Shaybah and the Quraysh who followed them. Their proposal was a settlement between equals: O Abu Talib, you are one of us in standing and in what you have come to know; you see what is before you, and you know what stands between us and your nephew. If you call him and restrain him from us, and take from him in turn — then let him cease from us and we will cease from him.

So Abu Talib sent for him. And what came back was not a concession but a demand, offered as a gift. Yes — one word: say it, and the Arabs will submit to you and the non-Arabs will be indebted to you.

They said: yes, and your father's word, and ten more.

He said: say there is no god but God, and cast off what you worship beside Him.

They clapped their hands together. Does Muhammad want us to make the gods into a single God? That is indeed a strange thing.

And that sentence, spoken in a dying man's room, went into the revelation as it stood: "Make the gods one God? This is truly a strange thing. And the chiefs among them walked away: walk on and remain steadfast to your gods — this is assuredly a thing contrived. We have not heard of this in the latter religion; this is nothing but an invention" (chapter 38, verses 5 to 7), and the verses that follow.

They had come to buy peace, and the thing they could not do was the smallest thing on the table.

They dispersed. And what happened next in that room is the most carefully hedged passage in this part of the history — the author reports it under the heading of what is claimed regarding Abu Talib's acceptance of Islam, and he leaves it claimed.

The Prophet said: by God, I asked of them nothing unreasonable. Then he turned to his uncle: say this word, uncle, and through it I shall be able to intercede for you before God.

And Abu Talib said: O son of my brother — were it not that it would be said I spoke out of fear as death approached, I would have said it.

That answer is the same answer he had been giving for ten years. It is not about the truth of the word. It is about the reading that would be put on the timing, and about what that reading would do to the value of everything he had done while nobody could accuse him of fear.

Then, they said, when the end drew near his lips began to move, and al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib put his ear close to him — and said: by God, my nephew, Abu Talib has said the word you asked him to say.

And they said that the Prophet said: I did not hear it from him.

The author sets the two sentences down side by side and does not adjudicate between them. A brother with his ear at the mouth says he heard it. A nephew standing further off says he did not. The record keeps both.

His son Ali ibn Abi Talib mourned him in verse — calling him a rain cloud of abundance and a light amid darkness, the shelter of whoever sought refuge; saying that the people of nobility were laid low by his loss; that he had been the finest of uncles to the chosen one, and that he had striven without rest against the polytheists.

Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib mourned his brother at greater length and with the blockade still in his mouth. Suddenly the chief is mourned, the one who gave freely, and this calamity has no successor and no equal; the sons of Hashim are now exposed and besieged. Then the turn outward: so the Quraysh rejoiced at his passing — but they will meet from him a day that cancels their rejoicing. They cherish hopes well greased with their illusions; they hope the prophet will be proven a liar and killed. You lie, by the House of God — we shall make you taste us. And the answer to the exposure he had just named: the neighborhood is not without Muhammad, and I am not alone among the friends of God, for he has among you a helper from God. God named him in the Book: Muhammad. Then the lament closes back down into a private voice — the clouds clear from him and brighten, as though the full moon were the radiance of his face; I lay sleepless at the end of the night, singing, O Abu Talib, shelter of the wanderer.

Khadijah bint Khuwaylid died three days later.

The author gives the two losses their separate functions without ranking them. Abu Talib had been his supporter and guardian in all his affairs, and his defender among the people. Khadijah was the one who comforted him, lifted his sorrow, cared for him herself, and helped him carry his grief.

And it was narrated from Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn 'Ali ibn al-Husayn that the Messenger of God said to Fatimah: Gabriel gave me glad tidings that the house of your mother Khadijah bint Khuwaylid, and the house of Mary bint 'Imran, and the house of Asiya the wife of Pharaoh, are in Paradise — fashioned from a hollow pearl, free of noise and toil.

The consolation is delivered to the daughter, about her mother, and the company it puts her in is chosen: two of the three women named spent their lives holding a faith inside a hostile household. And what the promise specifies is not reward but the absence of the exact thing all three of them lived with — noise, and toil.

He used to say afterward: I was never overwhelmed by grief as I was in the days that saw the deaths of Abu Talib and Khadijah.

After that the practical consequences arrived quickly. His affairs grew weightier and his grief deeper, and the reckless of the Quraysh grew bolder against him once Abu Talib was gone, and began to inflict harm on him directly. The authority of Banu Hashim weakened with him. And the Prophet said to his family the sentence that measures exactly what the old man had been worth: the Quraysh had never visited upon me anything I found hateful — until Abu Talib died.

What had been standing between the words and the hands was one man's protection, and everyone discovered the fact at once when it was withdrawn.

After his death, the author says, there was no one left to shelter him and bring him comfort, no one to watch over his affairs, except Ali ibn Abi Talib — who had grown to manhood under the Prophet's own guardianship, whom the Prophet had raised, and whom God had honored by singling him out as the Prophet's brother and designating him as his successor, exactly as this history has argued from its opening. After his father's death Ali remained the Prophet's companion and stay, the one he leaned on in his affairs.

Meanwhile his enemies multiplied their hostility and their harassment, causing him distress and grief and lying against him. God repelled their evil and foiled their scheming — and still, the author writes plainly, the harm they inflicted reached him.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry forward out of it.

The first is that the boycott was defeated twice over, and the two defeats never touch each other. Five men from five different clans met at night, agreed on an order of speaking, and dismantled the pact in a single morning by refusing it out loud one after another until its author had nobody left to answer. And separately, without any of them knowing, the parchment itself had been reduced to its own contents — every name of God eaten out of it, the injustice and the severed kinship and the falsehood left legible — and the family staked the whole dispute on that being true before anyone unrolled it.

The second is that the night journey is presented here as a burden as much as a vindication. He led the prophets in prayer in Jerusalem and ascended by a ladder that every dying person looks toward; the daily prayers were fixed at five because a prophet of an earlier community kept sending him back to ask for less, on the evidence of what he had watched his own people do; and when he came home and described it, the account cost him followers, and the revelation that followed called the whole thing a trial rather than a proof. The evidence he finally offered the Quraysh was a description of the roads of a city they could check.

The third is that the protection ran out. Within one year the uncle who had made abuse the upper limit of what the Quraysh could do was dead, and the wife who had absorbed what the abuse cost him was dead three days after — one of them still refusing the word to the end for the sake of what refusing it had bought, with a brother swearing he finally said it and a nephew saying he never heard it. What the Quraysh had been afraid of became visible the moment it was gone, and the harm reached him for the first time.

Mecca was no longer survivable, and the next part of this history is the search for somewhere that would be.
