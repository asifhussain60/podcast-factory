## Where this episode picks up

How does a battle report reach a city before any survivor of the battle does?

The previous episode ended in the Levant, with a banner passing from a dead man to a dead man to a dead man exactly as it had been assigned before the army marched. This one opens in Medina, several days' ride away, with the Messenger of God telling his companions who had fallen and in what order — and then telling them something about one of the three that changes how the whole community hears the news.

Three movements. First the mourning: the announcement, the visit to Ja'far's widow, the instruction about who should feed that household, and then the poetry — three named poets and one anonymous returning soldier, most of it damaged past repair, all of it doing the same job. Then the covenant broken at a watering place outside Mecca, and the failed embassy that follows, in which the most powerful man in Quraysh spends a day in Medina being turned down by everyone he asks and goes home with advice that was honestly labelled worthless when it was given. Then the road to Mecca in Ramadan — a letter hidden in a woman's braids, a companion who confesses to writing it, ten thousand men on the march, and a set of rulings the book stops the campaign to argue out.

Listen for how much of this episode is about speech that fails and speech that binds. A treaty spoken over and then broken at night. A protection declared aloud in a mosque by a man who has been told plainly it will not work. A letter that was never delivered. And a verse revealed about the man who wrote it, which does not punish him.

## Mourning Ja'far and his companions

When the army suffered its losses, the Messenger of God said to his companions:

Zayd ibn Haritha took the banner and fought with it until he was killed as a martyr. Then Ja'far ibn Abi Talib took it and held it until he too was killed. Then 'Abd Allah ibn Rawahah took the banner of Zayd ibn Haritha and held it until he too was killed as a martyr.

Note that the order here is not the order the fighting was narrated in, and the book does not reconcile the two. The dispatch and the battle both put Ja'far first; this announcement puts Zayd first. It is left standing as it came down.

Then the Messenger of God fell silent, and the faces of the Ansar changed. They feared that 'Abd Allah ibn Rawahah had done something they would find blameworthy.

Sit inside that pause for a moment, because it is one of the most finely observed things in the volume. Two names have been given with the same formula — took it, held it, was killed as a martyr — and the third has been given the same formula too, and then the speaker stops. The Ansar are reading the silence, not the sentence. Ibn Rawahah is one of theirs. And the fear is specific: not that he died badly, but that he did something they would be ashamed of. A pause of a few seconds is being experienced by a whole section of the community as a verdict withheld.

Then the Prophet said: 'Abd Allah ibn Rawahah took it and bore it until he was killed as a martyr.

Then he said: They were raised up, and I beheld them reclining on couches of gold. I saw the bed of 'Abd Allah ibn Rawahah set apart from the beds of his two companions — and I asked, what is this? I was told: 'Abd Allah had hesitated for a moment, and then went forward.

So the fear was not baseless and it was not correct either. Something is recorded against him, and it is exactly the thing the previous episode showed him doing: he rode forward, stopped, argued with his own soul in verse, and then went. The hesitation is not deleted. It is not treated as a disqualification either — he is on a couch of gold with the other two. The couch is simply set apart. And nobody would have known the difference existed if it had not been reported, which means the report exists to make a distinction that carries no penalty attached to it.

When the news of Ja'far and his companions arrived, the Messenger of God went to Asma' bint 'Umays and said: Bring me the sons of Ja'far.

She brought them to him, and his eyes filled with tears.

She said: By my father and my mother, O Messenger of God — what makes you weep? Have you received news of Ja'far and his companions?

He said: Yes. They were struck down today.

Asma' cried out in grief, and the women gathered around her.

Look at the order of that scene. He does not tell her. He asks for the children first, and it is the sight of them that produces the tears, and it is the tears that produce her question. The news is delivered by her asking for it. And the answer, when it comes, is five words long and includes the day.

Then the Messenger of God went out to his own family and said: Do not neglect the family of Ja'far. Make food for them, for they are occupied with a grief that has overwhelmed them.

That is a practical instruction and it names its own reason. The household is not being fed because feeding the bereaved is a courtesy. It is being fed because grief of that weight consumes the hours in which food would otherwise be made, and somebody has to notice that. The obligation is placed on his own household, immediately, before any public mourning is organised.

And when the news of Ja'far's death came from Mu'tah, the Messenger of God said: He walks among a company of angels, on two white wings with which he flies wherever God wills.

The Prophet was deeply grieved for him, and loved him dearly, and he said: People were created from different trees, but Ja'far and I were created from a single tree.

And in another report: We, the family of 'Abd al-Muttalib, are from one tree. Ja'far and I are from one of its branches — his nature is like my nature, and his character is my character.

The second version is more precise than the first and is doing more work. In the first, the whole of humanity is scattered across many trees and these two men share one. In the second, the tree is the family of 'Abd al-Muttalib — already a small enough grove — and within it these two share a single branch. And what is claimed for that shared branch is not blood, which was never in question, but disposition: his nature is like my nature, his character is my character. The kinship being asserted is the kind you cannot inherit by descent alone.

One deed on the field is recorded by name. Qutbah ibn Qatadah al-'Udhri, who had been stationed on the right wing, charged at Malik ibn Zafilah and killed him while he stood before him. He was among the commanders of the disbelievers on the day of Mu'tah. Qutbah's own verses on it survive damaged — a spear thrust that pierced through and then shattered, a shout at the goodness of the stroke, and a morning after that he calls a market of blessings.

Then the elegies, and this is the largest concentration of them in the volume since Uhud. They have come down badly damaged: whole lines are opaque, names are mangled past repair, and in several places only an image survives with no sentence around it. They are carried here at the level of their surviving sense, with the damage named rather than patched.

Asma' bint 'Umays, weeping for her husband, calls him the pilot, the finest of horsemen on the day of din and clamour, and then turns entirely to what his absence has done to the ground under her feet: you left me to walk a barren road; I had a mountain whose shade I sheltered in; I was a fever, and no life remained in me. Everything in her surviving lines is spatial. He was a height that made shade, and the shade is gone, and what is left is open country with nothing standing on it.

Hassan ibn Thabit's elegy for the three of them is the longest and the worst preserved. What still reads is a night spent awake while others slept, a catalogue of reasons a man may weep, and then the line that everyone remembers: I saw the finest of the Muslims brought low by a death that gave Ja'far two wings. After that the poem widens out from the three dead men to the house they came from — the family of Hashim, the pillars of pride still standing, and a roll of names running Ali, Ahmad al-Mukhtar, Ja'far, his mother's son 'Aqil, Hamza, al-'Abbas. And it closes on a claim rather than a lament: they are the guardians of God, upon whom He sends down His wisdom, and among them is the Purified Book.

Notice what has happened to the poem's subject by the end. It began as a lament for three men killed in the Levant and finished as a statement about which household the revelation belongs in. That is what these elegies are for. They are not decorating the news; they are settling what the news means, and the settlement is that the deaths are losses suffered by a particular family on behalf of everyone.

Ka'b ibn Malik's poem is in similar condition. What survives is a night of returning worry, a long acquaintance with grief, then the advance itself — men who supported him and did not flinch, who were patient in surrendering their souls to God with iron coming down on them, who went forward at the head of the Muslims as though guided by Ja'far and his brigade, until the ranks were overwhelmed and Ja'far among them. Then the same widening: the sun was shy and nearly faded, the moon changed on account of his loss, and the house of Hashim upon whom the Book came down.

And one anonymous voice, a Muslim man who came back from Mu'tah, says the thing none of the professional poets say. Zayd and 'Abd Allah loom larger in sorrow than ever. Enough grief that I returned, while Ja'far did not. I left the calamity behind, covered in dust. Three brave souls stepped forward and advanced.

That is a survivor's poem and it has a different centre of gravity from the other three. The professionals mourn the dead. This man is accounting for himself: it is grief enough that he came home.

Then the roll of the fallen, which the book gives by name, because a battle of this kind is otherwise a number. Among the Emigrants: Ja'far ibn Abi Talib, the Prophet's cousin; Zayd ibn Haritha, his freedman; Mas'ud ibn al-Aswad of Banu 'Adi ibn Ka'b; and Wahb ibn Sa'd ibn Abi Sarh of Banu Malik ibn Hisl. Among the Ansar: 'Abd Allah ibn Rawahah; 'Abbad ibn Qays; al-Harith ibn al-Nu'man; Suraqah ibn 'Amr; and Abu Kulayb and Jabir, sons of 'Amr ibn Zayd, who shared both father and mother — or, the source adds, 'Amr and 'Amir, sons of Sa'd.

These are twelve men in total, the book says. The named list reaches twelve only if that alternative pair is counted alongside the brothers rather than instead of them, and the discrepancy is left standing. Twelve dead, out of an army that had been told a hundred thousand Romans and a hundred thousand Arabs were waiting for it.

## The covenant broken at al-Watir

After the army had been sent to Mu'tah, the Messenger of God remained in Medina through Jumada al-Akhirah and Rajab.

Then the matter arose of nullifying the treaty between him and the Quraysh, because the Banu Bakr ibn 'Abd Manat ibn Kinana had attacked Khuza'ah.

The alignment is the whole of the problem, so hold it clearly. Khuza'ah had entered into a treaty with the Messenger of God and into his covenant. Banu Bakr had entered into the covenant of Quraysh. There had been old feuds and killings between the two tribes from the time before Islam, but Islam had held them in check, and both were now bound inside the truce agreed at al-Hudaybiyah. Two tribes with a blood history are sitting under one document by way of two different guarantors.

Then the Banu al-Dil of Banu Bakr seized upon the truce itself as their opportunity. Khuza'ah were encamped at al-Watir, a watering place of theirs, and Banu al-Dil fell upon them there to take their revenge, wounding men. Fighting broke out and men were killed. Quraysh supplied Banu Bakr with weapons, and some of the Quraysh even joined the fighting themselves, secretly, under cover of night — until one party took a position inside the Sacred Precinct.

The manuscript names Lufl ibn Mu'awiyah al-Dili at this point, and the exchange as it prints it runs: We have entered the Haram — your God, your God. And the answer: There is no god for Him today, O Banu Bakr. Take your revenge. For by my life, do you not steal even within the Haram? Will you not take your revenge there?

The speaker attributions have slipped in transmission, but the argument has not, and it is worth naming exactly. Somebody raises the sanctuary as an objection — we are inside the Haram, remember God. And the answer does not deny that they are in the sanctuary. It concedes the location and attacks the objector's standing to raise it: you steal inside these walls already, so do not discover the sanctity of the place at the moment it would cost you a killing. A boundary that is only invoked when it is convenient is being told it has no authority left. That is the argument, and it wins.

So Khuza'ah took refuge in the house of Budayl ibn Warqa', and in the house of their master, whose name is said to have been Rafi', after Quraysh and Banu Bakr had killed their men.

Then 'Amr ibn Salim al-Khuza'i went to Medina, and found the Messenger of God sitting in his mosque among the Emigrants and the Ansar, and delivered his case in verse. What survives of it is more legible than most of the poetry in this stretch. He calls upon Muhammad by the oath that is bound and firm. He reminds him that his people were the elders and Muhammad's the young ones when the alliance was made, and that they submitted and did not withdraw the hand they had given. He says the Quraysh have betrayed the covenant at the appointed time, have revoked the firmly confirmed agreement, and have killed his people while they were kneeling and prostrating. And he closes: so grant us victory.

That last accusation is the sharpest thing in the poem and it is placed at the end. They killed us while we were kneeling and prostrating. The dead are being identified not as allies of Medina but as people who were at prayer.

He said: O 'Amr ibn Salim, you have been answered.

Then a cloud appeared in the sky, and he said: This cloud heralds the victory of Banu Ka'b.

Then Budayl ibn Warqa' set out with a party of Khuza'ah until they reached the Messenger of God in Medina, and told him what had been done to them and what Quraysh and Banu Bakr had launched against them. They turned back toward Mecca, and the Messenger of God said to those around him: It is as if Abu Sufyan has come to you to tighten the covenant and extend its term.

Budayl and his companions met Abu Sufyan ibn Harb at 'Usfan on their way home — for Quraysh had indeed sent him to reinforce the treaty, extend its term, and reassure those who had broken it. The prediction had been spoken in Medina before the man had finished travelling.

What follows is one of the longest single scenes in this volume, and nothing whatever happens in it. Abu Sufyan comes to Medina to fix a treaty and is refused by every person he speaks to, in a different manner each time.

He came first to his daughter, Umm Habiba bint Abi Sufyan, wife of the Messenger of God. When he moved to sit on the bed of the Messenger of God, she folded it away from beneath him.

He said: O daughter, I do not know whether you found me unworthy of this bed, or the bed unworthy of me.

She said: It is the bed of the Messenger of God, and you are a polytheist and impure. I did not wish you to sit on the bed of the Messenger of God.

He said: By God, evil has come upon you, O daughter, since leaving me.

She had emigrated to Abyssinia with her first husband, who died there — this history has already followed that crossing — and the Messenger of God sent word to the Negus to betroth her, so that she came to him and became one of his wives. What her father meets in Medina is a daughter he last saw in Mecca as a member of his household, who now handles the furniture of the room as a matter of ritual purity, and tells him to his face which side of that line he is on. His reply is the reply of a man who has just discovered the terms have changed everywhere, not only at the negotiating table.

Then he went to the Messenger of God and spoke to him, and received no reply.

So he went to Abu Bakr and asked him to intercede on his behalf. The answer was: I shall not do so.

Then he came to 'Umar ibn al-Khattab and appealed to him in the same way. 'Umar answered: We cannot approach the Messenger of God with what he dislikes. In another telling of the same exchange, 'Umar is harsher: if I had nothing but a seed's worth, I would use it against you.

Then he went out to Ali ibn Abi Talib, who was with Fatima, and al-Hasan ibn Ali was there as a small boy.

He said: O Ali, have mercy on me — yesterday's guest — for I am in need. Let me not return as I came, empty-handed. Intercede for me with the Messenger of God.

Ali said, rubbing his hands: O Abu Sufyan. By God, the Messenger of God has resolved upon something, and nothing we could say will change his mind.

Then Abu Sufyan turned to Fatima and said: O daughter of Muhammad, can you command your son — meaning al-Hasan — to extend his protection to the people, so that he becomes a master of the Arabs until the end of time?

She said: By God, my son has not yet reached the point of extending protection over the people, and no one can intercede against the decision of the Messenger of God.

Weigh that offer, because it is the most revealing sentence Abu Sufyan speaks all day. He is proposing that a small child grant protection to the Quraysh, and he is quite openly naming the price he is willing to pay for it: the boy would become master of the Arabs for all time. He has understood that the currency in this house is not political and is trying to buy in it anyway. And the refusal he gets is given on two separate grounds — the boy is not yet of an age to extend protection, and no intercession stands against that decision — the second of which would hold even if the first did not.

He said: O Abu al-Hasan, I see that matters have grown difficult for me. Give me your counsel.

Ali said: By God, I know of nothing that will serve you. Yet you are the master of Banu Kinana. So stand among the people and extend your protection, and then make your way back to your land.

He said: Do you think this will avail me anything?

He said: No, by God, I do not think so. But I can find nothing else for you.

That exchange is the reason the whole scene is preserved. The advice is given and its worthlessness is stated in the same breath, in advance, without being asked. He is not being tricked. He is being told, by the only man in Medina who bothered to answer him at length, that there is nothing left to do and here is the nothing.

So Abu Sufyan stood up in the mosque among the people and said: O people, I hereby extend my protection among all people. Then he mounted his camel and set off.

When he arrived in Mecca his people asked what news he brought. He said: Muhammad spoke not a word to me, and by God he gave me no answer. Then I went to Ibn al-Khattab and found him the most hostile of enemies. Then I came to Ali and found him the most gentle of the people, and he pointed me toward something I did — though I do not know whether it will avail anything or not.

They said: And what did he order you?

He said: He ordered me to extend my protection among the people, and I did so.

They said: Did Muhammad sanction that? Woe to you. By God, he has done nothing but play with your mind.

He said: By God, I found nothing better than that.

His own people reach the same verdict Ali had already handed him, and read it as mockery. He does not argue. His answer concedes everything: there was nothing better available. The most powerful man in Mecca has been to Medina, been refused by four people, been given one honest piece of useless advice, performed it in public, and come home to be told he was made a fool of — and the only thing he can say in his own defence is that the alternative was nothing at all.

## The rulings on the road

Then the Messenger of God informed the people that he was setting out for Mecca, and ordered them to prepare and make ready. And he prayed: O God, take the news of us from the eyes of Quraysh and those with them, until we come upon them in their own country.

So the people prepared. Hassan ibn Thabit spoke, inciting them and recalling the wounded men of Khuza'ah, in verses that have come down damaged — men of Banu Ka'b in the presence of Mecca, in the hands of men who did not draw their swords, and a warning to Suhayl ibn 'Amr and to Safwan that swords are ready and there is no ground for trusting anyone now.

When the Messenger of God assembled his forces to march on Mecca, Hatib ibn Abi Balta'ah wrote a letter to the Quraysh informing them of what he had resolved to do — namely, to march against them. He entrusted the letter to a woman, said to be a freed slave of some of the Banu 'Abd al-Muttalib, on condition that she deliver it to the Quraysh. She concealed it in her hair, twisting her locks around it, and set out.

Then news of what Hatib had done came to the Messenger of God from heaven. He sent Ali ibn Abi Talib and al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam, saying: Go. You will find a woman who has with her a letter that Hatib ibn Abi Balta'ah wrote to the Quraysh, warning them of what we are gathering against them.

They set out and overtook her at a place known as the grove of Banu Abi Ahmad. They brought her down from her mount, searched her baggage, and found nothing.

Then Ali said to her: By God, I have not lied and I have not been misled. Bring out this letter, or we will strip your clothing.

When she saw the resolve in him she said: I will show it. She turned away, loosened her braids, drew the letter out from within them, and handed it over.

That confrontation turns on a claim about the source of the information, not on a search. The baggage has already come up empty, which in an ordinary interception would end the matter. What he says instead is that he has not lied and has not been misled — an assertion about where the instruction came from, offered as the reason the search will continue regardless of what the baggage held. She does not concede the letter exists; she concedes that he is certain.

He summoned Hatib and said: What drove you to do this?

Hatib said: O Messenger of God, I am a believer in God and His Messenger. I have not changed nor wavered. But I was a man among the Quraysh who had no family or clan there to protect his people and his children, so I sought to earn their favour through this.

'Umar ibn al-Khattab said: O Messenger of God, let me strike his neck. He has committed treason.

The Messenger of God said: And what do you know, O 'Umar? Perhaps God has already looked upon the people of Badr and said: do as you wish, for I have forgiven you.

Consider the defence and the answer separately, because they are not addressing the same thing. Hatib does not deny the act, does not claim the letter was harmless, and does not plead a change of heart. He gives a motive and it is entirely worldly: unlike the other Emigrants from Quraysh, he had no kin in Mecca to shelter his family, so he was buying goodwill from the people who would hold them. And the answer given to 'Umar does not weigh that motive at all. It sets against the act a fact from years earlier — that this man was at Badr — and treats the earlier fact as already having settled a class of question that had not yet arisen. The pardon is not extended because the reasoning was good. It is extended because of where the man was standing in a different year.

Then God revealed concerning Hatib: O you who believe, do not take My enemy and your enemy as allies, offering them affection, when they have disbelieved in what has come to you of the truth, driving out the Messenger and yourselves because you believe in God, your Lord — if you have gone forth to strive in My cause and to seek My pleasure. You confide to them affection, while I know best what you have concealed and what you have revealed. And on to: You have had a good example in Abraham and those with him, when they said to their people: we are innocent of you and of what you worship besides God. We disbelieve in you, and there has appeared between us and you enmity and hatred forever, until you believe in God alone (chapter 60, verses 1 to 4).

The revelation is severe and the man is not punished. Both of those are recorded on the same page, and the book does not resolve the tension between them.

Then the Messenger of God set out to conquer Mecca when ten nights of Ramadan had passed.

And here the history stops the march to settle two questions of law that arise from it, because the campaign is happening in the fasting month and on the road.

On fasting, it is narrated from Ja'far ibn Muhammad al-Sadiq, from his father, from his forefathers: The Messenger of God travelled in the month of Ramadan, and he broke his fast and commanded those with him to break their fast. Some people, however, refused to break their fast, and so they were called the disobedient — because God had commanded them through His Prophet, and they did not obey his command.

That name is the sharpest thing in the section, and it is being applied to men who fasted. They are not called overzealous or mistaken. They are called disobedient, and the reason given makes the logic plain: the command to break the fast was a command, and a command declined is a command disobeyed, whatever the thing declined in favour of looks like from outside.

On shortening the prayer, it is narrated from Ja'far ibn Muhammad, from his father, from Ali ibn Abi Talib, that the Messenger of God said: God Almighty has given my nation a gift He did not give to any nation before them — an honour from Him. They asked: and what is that, O Messenger of God? He said: Breaking the fast and shortening the prayer while travelling. Whoever does not do so has rejected God's gift.

And from Ali: Whoever shortens his prayer when travelling and breaks his fast has accepted the relief God has extended to him.

And Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali al-Baqir said: Whoever completes his prayer while travelling has done wrong.

He was asked about prayer on a journey — how much it is, and how much is required. He said: God the Almighty says, and when you travel in the land, there is no blame on you if you shorten the prayer (chapter 4, verse 101). And he said: Shortening on a journey is obligatory, just as completing the prayer in one's own city is obligatory.

It was said to him: O son of the Messenger of God, God said there is no blame on you, which implies permission, not obligation. How then do you say it is obligatory?

He said: Did not God say, indeed, al-Safa and al-Marwah are among the rituals of God, so whoever performs Hajj or 'Umrah, there is no blame on him if he goes back and forth between them (chapter 2, verse 158)? Do you not see that walking between them is obligatory? For God mentioned them in His Book, and the Messenger of God enacted it as a practice. In the same way, shortening the prayer on a journey was mentioned by God and enacted by the Messenger of God.

That is a real argument and it is worth following, because it turns on the objector's own premise. The objection is that the phrase there is no blame on you grants a permission and cannot create a duty. The answer does not dispute the grammar. It produces a second verse using the identical phrase about a rite everyone in the room already performs as obligatory, and lets the parallel do the work: if that wording did not prevent the walk between al-Safa and al-Marwah from being required, it cannot by itself prevent the shortening from being required either. What settles either case is not the phrase but the fact that God named it in the Book and the Messenger enacted it as practice.

And from Ali: The Messenger of God forbade completing the full prayer while travelling. And Ja'far ibn Muhammad al-Sadiq said: I disown those who pray four cycles on a journey.

The author closes the digression by saying plainly why he opened it: the discussion could go on at length, and it is included here because there is a genuine difference of opinion over whether shortening the prayer on a journey is obligatory — notwithstanding that everyone agrees the Messenger of God shortened his prayer on journeys, and that this is his practice and his example.

Then back to the road. The Messenger of God set out toward Mecca with ten thousand Muslims. With him were the Emigrants and the Ansar, and no one of consequence remained behind. Sulaym and other Arab tribes had embraced Islam, and so the Muslims gathered in full force.

Set that number against the last two campaigns. Three thousand went to Mu'tah. Fourteen hundred went to al-Hudaybiyah and turned back at the edge of Mecca. Ten thousand are now moving on the same city, and the difference is not recruitment from within — it is that whole tribes have come in.

Two men met the Prophet at a waypoint between Medina and Mecca and sought permission to enter his presence: Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib, and 'Abd Allah ibn Abi Umayyah ibn al-Mughirah. He did not grant it.

Umm Salamah spoke to him on their behalf and said: O Messenger of God — your cousin, and the son of your aunt, and your brother-in-law.

He said: I have no need of them. As for my cousin, he violated my honour. And as for the other, he is the one who said what he said in Mecca.

Notice that the refusal answers her plea exactly. She had named three relationships. He does not deny a single one of them; he names what each man did instead, and the second charge is not even described — he is simply the one who said what he said, which everyone present evidently remembers without being told.

When this response reached them, Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith said: By God, he must give me permission, or I will take this son of mine by the hand and we will wander the land until we die of thirst and hunger.

When word of what he had said reached the Messenger of God, he relented and granted them permission to enter. They came before him and greeted him, and he returned their greeting.

The kinship claim was refused and the threat was not. What moved him was not the reminder that these were his relatives but a man announcing he would take a child into the desert and die there.

Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith then composed verses marking his conversion and his remorse for his former conduct. They are damaged, and what survives runs like this: that for the swiftness of Muhammad's horses he will never carry a flag against them again; that he has been guided and shown the way; that the messenger of confusion is the darkest of nights; that by God, who drove away every exile, he was guided, and this changed him and set him right; that he had turned away and kept his distance from Muhammad; and that he seeks now to please the people among whom he had held back from professing his love, and is no scoundrel.

He had been one of the sharpest poets against the community for twenty years, and the instrument does not change when the man does. The verse that attacked is the verse that recants, and it recants in public, which is the only place the attacks had ever been made.

## What this episode lands

The community learned what happened at Mu'tah before any rider brought it, mourned twelve men by name, and heard one of the three commanders marked out for a hesitation that cost him nothing except a couch set slightly apart.

Then the truce came apart at a watering place, in a night raid armed by the men who had guaranteed it — and the answer to it moved through three refusals in Medina, a piece of advice declared useless as it was given, and a declaration in the mosque that fooled nobody in Mecca either.

And on the road out, with ten thousand men marching, the book stops the campaign to argue whether shortening a prayer on a journey is permitted or required. That is what this history is: the largest army the community has ever put in the field is moving, and the page it earns is shorter than the page given to the length of a prayer said on the way.
