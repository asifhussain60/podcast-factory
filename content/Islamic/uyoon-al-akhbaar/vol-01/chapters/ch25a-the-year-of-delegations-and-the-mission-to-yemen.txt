## Where this episode opens

What does a war look like when it stops being fought and starts arriving at your door on its own feet — one tribe at a time, in striped cloth and turbans, asking to be written into something?

The previous episode closed on the northern march that met no army, and on a proclamation recalled from its first bearer and carried into Mecca by the one man who could deliver it. This one opens in the year that followed, and the change of subject is total. There are no battle lines here. The ninth year is the year the Arabian Peninsula came to Medina rather than the other way round, and the Arabs themselves gave it the name it keeps: the Year of Delegations.

Three movements. First the arrivals — dozens of them, most recorded in a sentence, a few in scenes the history slows down for: a delegation that shouted at a closed door and had a verse of the Quran sent down about its manners, and a siege in Yemen described in Medina while it was happening. Then the longest single scene in the chapter: a Christian chief of Tayy who ran from this religion as far as the Levant and came back to test it by watching how its founder sat down. Then the documents — the letters and the covenant that carried Islam into Yemen as written law rather than as an army, and the argument this history builds on them about whose transmission of that law is to be trusted.

Listen for how much of this year is conducted on paper.

## The delegations at the chambers

In the ninth year the delegations came to the Messenger of God from across the Arabian Peninsula, and the Arabs called it the Year of Delegations.

The delegation of Banu Tamim was led by 'Utarid ibn Hajib ibn Zurara al-Tamimi, and they accepted Islam. When they arrived they called out to the Messenger of God from behind his chambers: Come out to us, Muhammad. In response the following was revealed: *Those who call to you from behind the rooms — most of them do not understand* (chapter 49, verse 4).

Nothing bad happens to Banu Tamim; they are Muslims from the moment they arrive and they stay Muslims. What happens is that their manner of shouting through a wall at a private house is preserved in scripture, with a judgement closer to a diagnosis than a rebuke. The verse lands on the delegation that was in the right about everything except how to knock.

'Amir ibn al-Tufayl came as part of the delegation of Banu 'Amir. He said to the Prophet: By God, I will fill this land with horses and men. The Messenger of God replied: God is sufficient for you. As 'Amir set out on his way, God afflicted him with a plague in his neck which killed him in the house of a woman of Banu Salul. He cried out: O Banu 'Amir — a gland like the gland of a camel, and death in the house of a Salulite woman.

The boast is about scale. The death is about smallness and the wrong address: a swelling like the one that takes camels, and an end under a roof belonging to nobody he would have chosen. What he shouts as he dies is not repentance but a man appalled at the shape of his own ending.

Dammam ibn Tha'labah came as the representative of Banu Sa'd ibn Bakr. He accepted Islam, returned to his people, called them to Islam, and they embraced it. Al-Jarud ibn 'Amr ibn Hanash came with his family and his people from Banu 'Abd al-Qays. Al-Ash'ath ibn Qays came with eighty riders from Kindah. 'Amr ibn Ma'ad Yakrab came before the Messenger of God; it is said that he was resistant at first, that war broke out, and that the Commander of the Faithful eventually captured him after a struggle and overpowered him.

Banu Hanifah brought Musaylamah the liar among them. They accepted Islam, but Musaylamah went on to claim prophethood alongside Muhammad. He employed rhymed prose and declared: I am associated with Muhammad. And this is what he produced: God has blessed the pregnant woman, and brought forth from her what He wills, from among the enclosed membrane.

The delegation is not the problem; it submitted like the others. What travelled inside it was a man who had studied the surface of the thing and reproduced it — rhymed prose being the recognised register of oracles and soothsayers, and the sample preserved here exactly that, cadence over a sentence with nothing inside it. The word for the partnership is his own: associated. He is not denying the Prophet; he is proposing a shared office, which is a subtler claim and, on this history's terms, a worse one.

Zayd al-Khayl came in a small delegation; they accepted Islam and their Islam was good. The Messenger of God said: No man was ever described to me with great virtue that, when I finally met him, did not fall short of what had been said — except for Zayd al-Khayl. And he renamed him Zayd al-Khayr — Zayd of the Good, in place of Zayd of the Horses. On his return journey Zayd was struck by fever at one of the watering places of Najd called Farda, and he died there. The verses he spoke when death was certain survive only in fragments: that he leaves behind a house standing alone and furnished while his companions turn east in the morning; that those who did not recover from this sickness will exhaust themselves trying; and a last line asking God, if illness comes again, for a return.

Farwah ibn Masik al-Muradi came having departed from the kings of Kindah and distanced himself from them, and his surviving verses say why: that when he saw those kings he stood exposed, like a man whose own women have outrun him in the race, and that he is turning now toward the rising community of Muhammad in the hope that it will be blessed and flourish. The Messenger of God appointed him over Murad and Zabid and sent a collector of alms with him.

That last clause is the one to notice, and it recurs through the rest of this episode. A delegation is received, the chief keeps his authority over his own people, and a man from Medina goes back with him carrying the alms register. The settlement is not conquest and not merely conversion; it is administration.

Surad ibn 'Abd Allah al-Azdi came at the head of a delegation from Azd and accepted Islam, and the Messenger of God commanded him to wage war against those of his neighbouring people in Yemen who still practised shirk. Surad set out and came down upon Jurash, a fortified city at that time, where tribes of the Yemen had gathered and sealed themselves in when they learned the Muslims were advancing. The siege lasted about a month, and the defenders held. Then Surad withdrew in the direction of a caravan, and when he reached a mountain pass, the people of Jurash — reading the withdrawal as a defeat — came out after him. He turned on them at that pass and cut them down with great slaughter.

Meanwhile the people of Jurash had already sent two men to the Messenger of God. He said to them: In what land is Thankful Mountain? They said: O Messenger of God, there is a mountain in our land that is called that. He said: It is not Sham — it is Thanks. They asked: What is the matter, O Messenger of God? He said: There, the army of God is engaged with your companions at this very hour.

They returned to their people and found that the day on which this was said to them in Medina was the very day Surad killed their companions at that mountain. The people of Jurash then presented themselves and embraced Islam.

The scene is built out of a pun and a date. The correction — it is not Sham, it is Thanks — converts a place-name into a verdict on the outcome before the outcome is known in Medina. What convinces Jurash is not the slaughter, which they already knew about, but that it was described to their own envoys on the day it happened, hundreds of miles away.

The delegation of Hamadan came to the Messenger of God on his return from Tabuk: Malik ibn Namat — Abu Thawr, known as Dhu al-Mash'ar — with Malik ibn Ayfa', Dhammam ibn Malik al-Salmani and 'Amirah ibn Malik al-Kharafi. They arrived in garments of striped cloth and turbans, having ridden swift horses along the route of al-Mays through al-Dawhan and al-Rahbah. Their verse is badly damaged in this manuscript and only its sense survives: that Hamadan is honoured by its markets and its noble lords, that none like them exist in all the worlds, that its place is the high plateau and its men are heroes, that its tables are laid with food generously spread — and, from another of them, lines about setting out across dark stretches of open country through the rains of summer and autumn, straining at fibre ropes. Then Malik rose before the Messenger of God and delivered the delegation's address: that he comes bearing Hamadan, those present and those who could not make the journey, bound by the ropes of Islam; that the tribe should not be held to account for its old quarrels; that on the day of thanksgiving they answered the call and abandoned the gods they had kept, and that their covenant does not break. The Messenger of God wrote out their covenant after they embraced Islam and proved their commitment to it, and their poet made further verses on the road home, of which little more than the place-names and the longing survive.

Rifa'ah ibn Zayd al-Judhami, then al-Dhabi, had been received earlier, during the truce of al-Hudaybiyah and before the conquest of Khaybar, and had given the Messenger of God a slave as a gift.

And one arrival cost the man his life. Farwah ibn 'Amr al-Judhami accepted Islam while serving as an agent of the Romans over Ma'an and the surrounding territories and the regions of the Levant about it. He sent a gift to the Messenger of God — a white mule, which the Prophet accepted. When news of his Islam reached the Romans they sought him out, seized him and imprisoned him. The verses he composed in custody and again at the water called 'Afra in Palestine, where they gathered to crucify him, are among the most damaged in the volume; what survives of them is a man sending word to the Muslims that he is dying, telling those who loved him not to close their eyes on his account, and greeting his Lord at the end. They struck off his head and crucified him at that water.

He is the counterweight this section needs. Everyone else arrives, is received, is written a covenant and goes home with a tax collector. He held an imperial post on the northern frontier, and the moment he took this religion he was inside the wrong empire.

## 'Adi ibn Hatim and the cushion

One arrival in this year is told at length, in the man's own voice, and it is the only account in the chapter that shows the reasoning of someone deciding whether the claim is true.

'Adi ibn Hatim was the son of Hatim of Tayy, the most famous host in Arabia, and he held a religion in his heart — he was a Christian — and he was a ruler among his people. When news of the Messenger of God reached him, he despised him. So he said to a servant boy of his who tended his camels: Prepare for me all my camels. He told him to keep them close, so that if he heard of Muhammad's army advancing he could flee quickly. The boy did so.

Then one day the boy came to him and said: O 'Adi, if you intend to act, then act — I have seen banners. He asked about them and was told: These are the armies of Muhammad. He said: Bring the camels closer to me, and the boy did.

He gathered his family and children and resolved to join the people of his own religion, the Christians, in the Levant. He went to al-Jushiyyah, and he left behind, among those who remained, a daughter of Hatim — his own sister.

That is the pivot of the whole account, and the history leaves it without comment. He kept the camels ready for months for exactly this moment, and when it came he was so quick that a member of his own household was left standing in the camp.

The Messenger of God launched a raid against his territory, and among the captives taken was Hatim's daughter, who was brought to Medina and held in an enclosure at the door of the mosque where the prisoners were kept. The Messenger of God passed by her, and she rose and addressed him: O Messenger of God, my father has perished and my guardian is absent — show me your generosity, and may God show you His.

He said: Who is your guardian? She replied: 'Adi ibn Hatim. He said: The one who fled from God and His Messenger? Then he moved on and left her.

The next day, when he passed by her again, she repeated what she had said. He moved on once more, and she despaired.

Then Ali ibn Abi Talib passed behind the Prophet and pointed to her, indicating that she should rise and speak to the Messenger of God. She approached him and said as she had said before: Who from my people can vouch for me before you?

He replied: I have heard your words — do not be in haste to leave until you find someone from your people whom you trust to escort you to your homeland, then come and let me know.

So she waited until a delegation from her tribe arrived. Then she came and said: O Messenger of God, a group of my people has come — trustworthy people who can accompany me. The Messenger of God provided for her and gave her provisions, and she set out with them until she reached the Levant.

Two refusals and then a release, with a third party's silent prompt between them. What is withheld the first two times is not mercy but transport, and the question underneath is who will answer for her on the road; when a delegation of her own tribe arrives, the request is granted with provisions. The intervention that gets her back to the front of the queue after she has given up is a gesture rather than a speech, and the history records who made it.

'Adi said: By God, I was in my household when I saw her approach — and when she recognised me she was ready to say: What a wretched man. She called out: You abandoned your family and your son, and left the rest of your father's honour exposed. I said: Say only good, for by God I have no excuse to offer — I did what I have mentioned.

Then she came down and stayed with him. She was a firm and resolute woman. He consulted her: What do you make of this man?

She said: By God, I think you should join him without delay. If this man is a prophet, then whoever preceded him to his side will have a distinction; and if his authority is that of a king, you will not be diminished in the glory of Yemen.

He said: By God, this is sound counsel.

Her advice is a wager in two branches, and neither branch requires her to have decided the question. If he is a prophet, arriving early buys precedence; if he is only a king, a chief of Tayy loses nothing by attaching himself to the winning court. She has been a prisoner of these people and freed by them, and her judgement is not sentimental. It is the coldest reasoning in the chapter, and it is what moves him.

So he set out until he came before the Messenger of God in Medina. He entered upon him while he was in the mosque, greeted him, and the Messenger of God said: Who is this man? He replied: 'Adi ibn Hatim.

The Messenger of God rose and took him toward his house. On the way a weak elderly woman stopped him and detained him for a long while, speaking to him about what she needed. 'Adi said to himself: By God, this is no king.

Then the Messenger of God walked with him until they entered his house. He took a leather cushion stuffed with palm fibre and threw it to him, saying: Sit on this. 'Adi said: No, you sit on it. He said: No, you. So 'Adi sat on it, and the Messenger of God sat on the ground. 'Adi said to himself: By God, this is the humility of prophethood, and not the manner of kings.

He arrived to test the question his sister had framed — prophet or king — and he does not test it by asking about doctrine. He tests it twice, by watching what the man does with his own time and his own furniture. An old woman with a private need holds up the head of state in the street for as long as she likes; the one cushion in the house goes to the guest and the host takes the floor. Both observations are made silently, and the second is the one he treats as settled.

Then the Messenger of God said to him: Perhaps, 'Adi, what keeps you from entering this religion is the poverty you see among its followers — but by God, wealth is soon to pour in upon them so abundantly that there will be no one left to receive it. And perhaps what holds you back is the weakness and small numbers you see among them — but by God, you will soon hear that a woman travels alone from the village without fear. And perhaps what holds you back is that you see authority and power in the hands of others — but by God, you will soon hear of the white palaces of Babylon opening to them.

'Adi said: So I accepted Islam and said: O Messenger of God, accept my pledge. He accepted the pledge of allegiance.

Three objections are named for him before he can raise them — they are poor, they are few, they do not hold power — and each is answered with a specific future rather than an argument, in ascending order: money, safety on the roads, empire. It is the only place in this chapter where the settlement being built is described from the far end.

Then the Messenger of God recited to him a verse about the Christians: *They took their rabbis and monks as lords besides God* (chapter 9, verse 31).

'Adi said: O Messenger of God, by God, we never worshipped them or prayed to them.

He said: Did they not make lawful things forbidden for you, so you forbade them; and make forbidden things lawful for you, so you permitted them?

'Adi said: Indeed, that was so.

He said: That is your worship of them.

The exchange redefines the word being disputed. 'Adi hears the accusation as a charge of ritual and denies it truthfully. What is put to him instead is a test of jurisdiction: who decides what is permitted and what is forbidden. On that definition obedience in law is itself the act of worship, and the denial collapses without anybody's account of the facts having changed.

'Adi ibn Hatim was among the finest of those who followed the Commander of the Faithful.

## The covenant carried into Yemen

The rest of the year is conducted in writing, and the history now shifts from arrivals to documents.

A letter from the kings of Himyar was brought to the Messenger of God along with their messenger, conveying their acceptance of Islam. It came from al-Harith ibn 'Abd Kalal, Na'im ibn 'Abd Kalal, al-Nu'man — known as Dhu Ra'in — and from Ma'afir, Hamdan, and Zur'ah Dhu Yazan. When the Rahawi converted to Islam and turned away from shirk and its people, the Messenger of God had his cousin Ali ibn Abi Talib write the following reply:

In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.

From Muhammad, the Messenger of God, to al-Harith ibn 'Abd al-Kalal, to Na'im ibn 'Abd Kalal, and to al-Nu'man — known as Dhu Ra'in — of Ma'afir and Hamadan.

To proceed: I praise God to you, He beside Whom there is no god. Your messenger came to us from the land of Rum and reached us in Medina, conveying what you had sent him with — the news of your affairs, and your embrace of Islam and the killing of the polytheists. We have been informed that God has guided you. If you reform, obey God and His Messenger, establish prayer and pay zakat, you owe from your harvest one-fifth for God, along with the share of the Messenger and what has been prescribed. What the believers owe in charity from land watered by rain or running water is one-tenth, and from land irrigated by effort, one-half of a tenth. For every forty camels, one bint labun is due; for every sixty camels, one ibn labun male; for every five camels, one sheep; for every ten camels, two sheep. For every forty cows, one cow; for every thirty cows, one tabi' — a yearling calf — male or female. For every forty sheep, one sheep, a ewe, offered alone. This is God's obligation imposed upon the believers in charity, and whoever does more good, it is better for him.

Whoever fulfils this and bears witness to his Islam, and stands with the believers against the polytheists, is counted among the believers — sharing their rights and bearing their obligations — and enjoys the protection of God and His Messenger. Whoever is a Jew or a Christian and becomes a believer likewise shares their rights and obligations. Whoever holds fast to his Judaism or Christianity will not be turned away from it, but he must pay what is due. Every adult, male or female, free or slave, owes one dinar of the value of al-Ma'afir cloth, or its equivalent in garments. Whoever fulfils this has the protection of God and His Messenger, and whoever withholds it is an enemy of God and His Messenger.

The Messenger of God also wrote to Zur'ah Dhu Yazan:

I am sending my messengers and commend them to you in the best terms: Mu'adh ibn Jabal, 'Abd Allah ibn Zayd, Malik ibn 'Ubadah, 'Uqbah ibn Nimr, Malik ibn Murrah, and their companions. Collect from your people what is owed in charity and jizyah from your various regions, and deliver it to my messenger. Their leader is Mu'adh ibn Jabal. Do not turn away anyone who comes satisfied to God.

To proceed: Muhammad testifies that there is no god but God and that he is His servant and messenger. Malik ibn Murrah al-Rahawi has informed me that you accepted Islam before the first delegation, and that you renounced the polytheists. I give you glad tidings, and I urge you to do good. Do not betray or deceive. The Messenger of God is the guardian of your rich and your poor alike. Charity is not lawful for Muhammad or for his household; it is zakat for the poor Muslims and the wayfarer. Even so, the good news has reached you and the unseen has been preserved. I have commanded you to do good, and I have sent you from the righteous of my family — guardians of their religion and repositories of their knowledge — and I urge you to treat them well, for they stand as witnesses.

May God's peace, mercy and blessings be upon you.

And when the Messenger of God dispatched Mu'adh ibn Jabal to Yemen he entrusted him with his mission and said to him: Be easy and do not be harsh; give glad tidings and do not drive people away. Some of the People of the Book will ask you: What is the key to Paradise? Say: The testimony that there is no god but God alone, with no partner. Mu'adh then set out with his companions until he arrived in Yemen.

Then Banu al-Harith ibn Ka'b, the people of Najran, accepted Islam, and their delegation came before the Messenger of God. He sent 'Amr ibn Hazm back with them to guide them in their religion, teach them its pillars, and write them a letter, which reads:

In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.

This is a declaration from God and His Messenger: *O you who believe, fulfil your contracts* (chapter 5, verse 1).

A covenant from Muhammad, the Prophet of God, to 'Amr ibn Hazm, upon sending him to Yemen. He commanded him to fear God in all his affairs, for God is with those who fear Him and strive to do good. He commanded him to uphold the truth as God has commanded, to give people glad tidings and encourage them toward good, to teach people the Quran and help them understand it, and to prevent people from touching the Quran unless they are in a state of purity. He is to inform people of their rights and their obligations, to be gentle with people in matters of truth, and to be firm with them against injustice — for God hates injustice and has said: *Is not the curse of God upon the wrongdoers?* (chapter 11, verse 18). He is to preach to people about Paradise and its deeds, and to warn them about the Fire and its deeds, and to keep people composed so that they may understand the religion, and to acquaint people with the rites of Hajj — its obligations and what God has commanded regarding it. The major Hajj is the greater pilgrimage, and the lesser Hajj is 'Umrah. He is to forbid people from praying in a single small garment unless it is one that covers the 'awrah; people must wear garments that reach down over their private parts. He is to forbid that a man gather the hair of his head at the back of his neck. If conflict arises among the people, he is to stop them from calling upon tribes and clans, and their call must be to God alone, with no partner. Whoever does not call upon God but instead calls upon tribes and clans is to be put to the sword, so that their call is directed to God alone. He is to command people to perform ablution — washing their faces and hands to the elbows, their feet to the heels, and wiping their heads as God has commanded them. He is to command them to pray at the appointed times of the day, to bow, to prostrate, and to observe full reverence. He is to lead the dawn prayer in the morning, and the midday prayer when the sun has passed its zenith, and the afternoon prayer while the sun still has height in the sky, and the sunset prayer when night descends — not delaying it until the twilight appears in the sky — and the night prayer at the beginning of the night. He is to call people to prayer when it is called, and to wash when the time comes, and he is commanded to take one-fifth of the spoils for God. What the believers owe in charity from land watered by rain or flowing water is one-tenth; from land irrigated by effort, one-half of a tenth. For every ten camels, two sheep; for every twenty, four sheep; for every forty cows, one cow; for every thirty cows, one tabi' — a yearling calf — male or female. For every forty sheep, one ewe is given alone. This is God's obligation imposed upon the believers in charity, and whoever does more good, it is better for him. Whoever converts from Judaism or Christianity to Islam sincerely and of his own accord is a believer who shares the believers' rights and obligations. Whoever holds fast to his Christianity or Judaism is not to be turned away from it; whether free or slave, he owes a full dinar or its equivalent in garments.

Whoever fulfils this has the protection of God and His Messenger, and whoever withholds it is an enemy of God, His Messenger, and all the believers. God's prayers upon Muhammad, and may God's peace, mercy and blessings be upon him.

Read as a single document, the covenant is startling for how little of it is about belief and how much about conduct that can be checked: the hours of five prayers fixed by the height of the sun, the length of a garment, the way a man wears his hair, the arithmetic of livestock, the terms for those who do not convert. The clause with the sharpest edge is the one about tribal calls — when a quarrel breaks out, the cry that summons a clan to its own is banned outright, on pain of the sword, so that the only name anyone can shout for help is God's. That is the pre-Islamic order legislated out of existence in a sentence, placed mid-paragraph among the manners.

Then the history stops narrating and starts arguing, and the argument is about the numbers.

The letter carried by Mu'adh ibn Jabal, as Ibn Hisham transmitted it, states: For forty camels, one bint labun is due; for thirty camels, one ibn labun.

Al-Qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad narrated from al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad ibn Ali ibn al-Husayn, from his father, from his forefathers, from Ali ibn Abi Talib, that they said: When camels reach one hundred and twenty and exceed that number, then for every forty, one bint labun is due, and for every fifty, one hiqqah — a three-year-old she-camel — and one bint labun. They did not mention the thirty. And they said: If the camels reach twenty-five, one bint makhad is due; if no bint makhad is available, then one ibn labun male is given, up to thirty-five; if the number increases by one, then one bint labun is due.

Should it be said that this ruling derives from the Messenger of God and his household and is therefore binding — we reply: they narrated nothing except from the Messenger of God and his household. Their narration is more firmly established and more correct than the narration of Ibn Hisham, for they are the custodians of the Quran. The Messenger of God said: I am leaving behind two weighty things. If you hold fast to them, you will not go astray: the Book of God and my household. They will not part from each other until they return to me at the Pond. Their word is binding and their doctrine is the soundest.

The same test is then applied to the poll tax. Ibn Hisham's version of the letter has it that every adult, whether Jewish or Christian, male or female, free or slave, owes one dinar. But it was narrated from Ali ibn Abi Talib that the Prophet said: The jizyah is levied on free dhimmis who are adult men — not on their slaves, not on their children, and not on their women. And he said further: It is taken from the wealthy among the dhimmis and those of comparable means, forty-eight dirhams per year from each of them; from those of middle means, twenty-four dirhams; and from those of lesser means, twelve dirhams.

The narration of the Ahl al-Bayt is well-established and is relied upon. Their pronouncements are beyond reproach, for they conveyed nothing in matters of religion except from the Messenger of God and his family.

This is the chapter's real argument, and it is easy to walk past because it arrives dressed as bookkeeping about camels. Two transmissions of the same letter disagree — a flat rate on every head, or a graduated tax on free adult men only; one schedule for camels, or another. The history does not resolve the disagreement by weighing chains of narrators. It resolves it by identity: one transmission comes through the household, and the household's warrant is the saying about the two weighty things that do not separate. Where the versions conflict, the household's is not merely preferred; it is the only one that can bind. The tax table is the occasion; the principle is the point.

The author closes by naming his own restraint: he did not intend to extend the discussion at length, but quoted what the Ahl al-Bayt narrated from the Messenger of God so that no one might suppose he is unmindful of what has been transmitted from them concerning the matters upon which reliance is placed — even if the people of deviation and obstinacy deny it.

## What this episode lands

The ninth year is the one in which the peninsula came to Medina, and most of its delegations are recorded in a line: Banu Tamim shouting through a wall and having their manners entered in the Quran; a chief who promised cavalry across a whole country and died of a swelling in his throat under a stranger's roof; a delegation that submitted honestly while carrying inside it the man who would offer himself as a partner in prophecy; a siege in Yemen narrated in Medina on the day it was fought, by way of a pun on a mountain's name; and one convert on the northern frontier crucified by the empire he worked for as soon as the news travelled.

The one arrival told at length is 'Adi ibn Hatim, told as a test rather than a conversion. He runs to the Levant so fast he leaves his sister in the camp; she is captured, released with provisions after two refusals and a silent prompt from behind the Prophet, and comes back with the coldest advice in the chapter — go early, because it costs nothing if this is only a kingdom and buys precedence if it is not. He settles the question by watching an old woman hold up the head of state in the street and a host give away his only cushion, and is told he will live to see wealth with no one left to take it, a woman travelling the roads alone, and the palaces of Babylon open. Then, when he denies worshipping his priests, the word is redefined under him: the ones who decide what is lawful are the ones being obeyed.

The rest of the year is paperwork, and it carries more of the settlement than any battle in this volume. A reply to the kings of Himyar, written by Ali, fixes the alms rates and the terms on which Jews and Christians keep their religion. A second letter names the men going to Yemen, forbids charity to the Prophet's own household, and describes those being sent as the righteous of his family, guardians of their religion and repositories of their knowledge. A covenant for Najran legislates the hours of prayer by the height of the sun and abolishes the tribal war-cry on pain of the sword. And then the history stops and asks which transmission of those tax tables binds — answering not by counting narrators, but by naming the two weighty things that do not separate until they return to their owner at the Pond.
