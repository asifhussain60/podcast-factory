## Where this episode picks up

What is the smallest thing you could give a proud man to make him carry your safe-conduct through a city that is about to fall — and what happens when he takes it?

The previous episode ended on the road, with the largest force this community had ever assembled moving south in the fasting month while the history it belongs to broke off to argue about the length of a prayer said on a journey. This one opens on the last night of that march, at a halt within sight of Mecca, with a man riding out alone on a borrowed mule because he has worked out that the city is going to be taken by surprise and that surprise will be the end of it.

Three movements. First the night itself: a chance meeting in the scrub, a rescue that is also an arrest, a testimony pronounced with a sword named as the alternative, and a parade staged for an audience of one. Then the entry, and the question it forces — who may be killed today, and who decides. A short list of names is issued, almost everyone on it survives, and men on no list at all are put to death by a commander sent to preach. Then the sanctuary: two keys, a verse that ranks their holders, and an address at the door of the Ka'bah that dissolves the entire inherited system of blood-claims in one clause, keeps two things out of the wreckage, and sends the assembled Quraysh home free.

Listen for how much of this is decided by voices rather than weapons.

## Abu Sufyan at the fires

Al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib met the Muslim army at al-Juhfah. He was emigrating with his family, and he had been the man responsible for supplying water to the pilgrims — an office this history has already followed back to the digging of the well and the vow that went with it. The Messenger of God was pleased with him.

When the Messenger of God halted at Marr al-Zahran, al-'Abbas said to himself: Woe to the Quraysh. By God, if the Messenger of God enters Mecca by force before they come out to him and seek his pledge of security, it will be the destruction of the Quraysh for all time.

Hold that sentence, because everything follows from it. He is not afraid the city will resist and lose. He is afraid it will not get the chance to surrender — and that chance exists only inside a very narrow window, the hours before dawn, and only if somebody inside Mecca learns where the army is.

So he mounted the mule of the Messenger of God and rode out until he reached the Arak trees, saying: Perhaps I will find a woodcutter, or someone with a milk animal, or a pilgrim heading to Mecca who can tell them where the Messenger of God is, so they can go out to him and seek his security before he enters by force.

Anyone at all, provided the news arrives before the army does.

On that same night, Abu Sufyan ibn Harb had come out of Mecca with Hakim ibn Hizam and Badil ibn Warqa', scouting for news and trying to learn what was happening. The information had been kept from Quraysh so completely that nothing whatever had reached them.

Al-'Abbas said: By God, I had not gone far on the mule, seeking what I had gone out for, when I heard the voices of Abu Sufyan and Badil ibn Warqa' as they argued. Abu Sufyan was saying: I have never seen anything like tonight — there is no fire nor army visible.

Badil said to him: This must be Khuza'ah, stirred up by the war.

Abu Sufyan said: Khuza'ah is too few and too lowly to account for all these fires and that army.

He went looking for a woodcutter and found the head of Quraysh, in the dark, mid-argument about the very fires he had hoped to describe to someone. And notice how Abu Sufyan reasons: he has a theory and rejects it himself, on arithmetic. He knows the size of what he is looking at, and not whose it is.

Al-'Abbas said: I recognised Abu Sufyan's voice and called out: O Abu Hanzalah. He recognised me and said: Abu al-Fadl? I said, Yes. He said: What has brought you? May my father and mother be your ransom.

I said: O Abu Sufyan — this is the Messenger of God with his army, and the morning of the Quraysh will be the worst morning they have ever known, by God.

He said: What is to be done? May my father and mother be your ransom.

I told him: By God, if they seize you they will kill you. Ride behind me on this mule and go to the Messenger of God so that I may seek his pledge of security for you.

He mounted behind me, and his two companions turned back.

So the rescue and the capture are the same act, and the safety on offer is a pledge nobody has yet given. He gets on the mule anyway.

Whenever we passed by a fire of the Muslims, they would ask: Who is this? Then they would see the mule of the Messenger of God and me upon it, and they would say: the uncle of the Messenger of God on his mule. And so they let us pass — until we came to the fire of 'Umar ibn al-Khattab, who called out: Who is this? and rose to his feet. When he saw Abu Sufyan on the back of the mule, he rushed forward.

'Umar said: O Messenger of God, this is Abu Sufyan, whom God has delivered into our hands without any covenant or pledge — let me strike his neck.

Al-'Abbas said: I have given him my protection, O Messenger of God. Then I said: 'Umar, if he were a man of Banu 'Uday ibn Ka'b, you would not have said this — but you know well that he is a man of Banu 'Abd Manaf.

That last line is precise about what it accuses. Not bloodthirst — the charge is applying to a man of another clan a standard he would not apply to his own, made by naming both clans out loud in front of everyone at the fire. The mule had carried them through the camp as a passport; the argument before the Messenger runs on the same currency.

The Messenger of God said: Take him to your quarters, O 'Abbas, and if morning comes, bring him to me.

So I took him to my lodging and he spent the night with me. In the morning, I brought him to the Messenger of God. When the Prophet saw him, he said: Woe to you, O Abu Sufyan — do you not yet know that there is no god but God?

He replied: By my father and my mother, how I honour you, revere you, and follow your guidance — yet I confess, by God, that something still lingers in my soul regarding this.

The manuscript stumbles here and repeats the question and the answer with small differences; both passes are carried as they stand rather than merged. He does not refuse and does not accept. He offers three verbs of personal regard — honour, revere, follow — and then admits that on the one point actually asked about, something in him has not moved.

Al-'Abbas said to him: Submit. Accept Islam and testify that there is no god but God, and that he is the Messenger of God — before the sword reaches your neck.

Abu Sufyan pronounced the testimony, finding before him nothing but faith or the sword.

The book states the condition of the profession in the same sentence as the profession. It does not soften it and does not annul it: this is what he said, this is what the alternative was, and the reader is left holding both halves.

Al-'Abbas said: I then said, O Messenger of God, Abu Sufyan is a man who loves distinction — grant him something. He said: Yes. Whoever enters the house of Abu Sufyan is safe, whoever closes his door is safe, and whoever enters the mosque is safe.

The request is frankly cynical — give him something, he likes status — and what is handed over is a clause turning his front door into a sanctuary, with two more named in the same breath that have nothing to do with him.

When Abu Sufyan was about to leave, the Messenger of God said to al-'Abbas: Detain him at the narrow mouth of the valley, at the bend of the pass, until the armies of God have filed past him.

Al-'Abbas said: I carried out what the Messenger of God had commanded. The tribes marched past with their banners, and not one tribe passed without Abu Sufyan asking about them. Each time I named a tribe, he would say: What have they to do with me? What have I to do with them? Then the next tribe would come, and he would ask again: Who are these? I would say: Such-and-such tribe. He would say: What have they to do with me? — and so it continued, tribe after tribe.

This is a parade with an audience of one, staged at a chokepoint so the whole force has to pass a single standing man. His response is the same six words over and over — the response of someone doing arithmetic he does not like. Each tribe he asks about has no quarrel with Quraysh, and every repetition adds to the total.

Until the Messenger of God himself passed at the head of his Green Battalion, comprising the Emigrants and the Helpers, clad in iron so that nothing could be seen of them but their eyes. Abu Sufyan said: Glory be to God, O 'Abbas — who are these? I said: This is the Messenger of God with the Muhajirun and the Ansar. He said: No one before has ever commanded power like this. By God, O Abu al-Fadl, your nephew's kingship has grown mighty today.

I said: It is prophethood, O Abu Sufyan. He said: Yes — then it is prophethood.

There is the disagreement of twenty years, compressed into one correction and one concession. He has been shown a force in iron and reached for the only category he has for what produces such a thing. The correction is a single noun, and the concession is not an argument — it is a man conceding a word because he has lost the ability to hold the other one.

I then said to him: Go quickly and warn your people.

When Abu Sufyan came back to them, he called out at the top of his voice: O assembly of Quraysh. Muhammad has come upon you with something you cannot withstand. Whoever enters the house of Abu Sufyan is safe.

His wife Hind bint 'Utbah leapt up, seized him by his moustache, and cried: Kill this foul fat traitor — what a wretched chieftain of a people.

This is the same woman this history followed onto the field after Uhud, and her reaction to the news is to seize her husband by the face in public and call for his death. She is not arguing with the terms. She is refusing the premise that there are terms.

He said: Woe to you, do not let her deceive you. Whoever enters the house of Abu Sufyan is safe.

They said: What use is your house to us?

He said: Whoever closes his door is safe, and whoever enters the mosque is safe.

So the people dispersed to their homes and to the mosque.

That exchange is why the grant was drafted in three clauses rather than one. He leads with the clause that flatters him, the crowd rejects it on plain practical grounds, and only then does he produce the two that work at the scale of a city. The vanity clause is the delivery mechanism, and it works precisely because he was proud enough to announce it first.

When the Messenger of God arrived at Dhi Tawa, he halted there, bowing his head in humility to God for the honour God had bestowed upon him through this conquest — bowing so low that his head nearly touched the saddle of his mount.

## Blood declared and blood spared

When the Messenger of God marshalled the multitude of Muslims, God had strengthened their victory and made Mecca within their grasp. Its people looked upon what they could not resist and had no power to oppose, and so they submitted, sought pardon, and entered into peace.

He entered Mecca that day at the head of an army the Arabs had never seen the like of — so great in number and so formidable in arms that no man could stare at another from the glare of iron. He placed the Ansar on the right, with their banner carried by Sa'd ibn 'Ubadah, and the Muhajirun on the left, with their banner carried by al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam. He said to each of them: Enter from the place of sorrow.

'Umar ibn al-Khattab heard Sa'd ibn 'Ubadah, banner in hand, as he was about to enter Mecca, saying:

Today is the day of the great battle,
Today the sanctuary shall be made lawful.

'Umar went immediately to the Messenger of God and reported it. The Messenger of God then said to Ali ibn Abi Talib: Go and take the banner from him, and be the one who enters with it. So Ali did so. And in this way Ali was the bearer of the Prophet's banner in war and the standard of his peace.

Two lines of verse cost a man the command of a wing at the gate of the city — not for boasting, but for a legal claim: today the sanctuary is made lawful. That is exactly the statement the Messenger will contradict in his own address a few hours later. The banner moves because the man carrying it announced the wrong rule.

The Messenger of God commanded the leaders of the battalions not to kill anyone except those who fought them — with certain exceptions he named — and he ordered that those exceptions be killed even if they were found sheltering beneath the curtains of the Ka'bah, on account of the grave crimes they had committed.

Many of them, however, were spared by those who encountered them — men who had prior knowledge of them, who cared for them, or who had given and received pledges between themselves — and the Messenger of God accepted their intercession and returned his command concerning those men to them.

Those two paragraphs are the governing rule of the day, and they undo each other by design. Fight nobody who does not fight you — except a short list of named men whose blood is forfeit wherever they are found, including the one place on earth where nothing may be killed. And then the exception dissolves in the next sentence, not by a pardon from above but by whoever happens to run into them.

Among those condemned was 'Abdullah ibn Sa'd, the client-brother of Banu 'Amir ibn Lu'ay. His offence was the gravest of all, and the Messenger of God was more wrathful with him than with any other. He had been the first whose blood was formally declared forfeit, and the Prophet had said: Kill him even if you find him clinging to the curtains of the Ka'bah.

This was because the man had been a Muslim, and the Messenger of God had employed him to write down the Revelation. The Prophet would dictate to him, saying: Forgiving, Merciful, and he would write instead: 'Aziz, Hakim — and the like of that. He then apostatised and joined the polytheists, saying to them: I used to turn our Quran however I wished, and I made it serve myself.

In response, God revealed: And who is more unjust than one who invents a lie about God or says, it has been revealed to me, while nothing has been revealed to him (chapter 6, verse 93).

The crime is substitution rather than invention: he was given two names of God to write and wrote two other names of God, so nothing false went on the page — the wrong true thing did. The boast afterwards is what converts scribal tampering into the offence the verse describes.

'Abdullah then took refuge with 'Uthman ibn 'Affan, who brought him secretly before the Messenger of God and interceded for him. The Messenger of God remained silent and gave no answer. 'Uthman pressed him again and again. At last the Prophet released him, saying: Go — your way is free.

Then he said to the Muslims who were present: I stayed silent for so long so that one of you might rise and strike his neck, as I had commanded — yet none of you did.

They said: O Messenger of God, why did you not signal to us?

He said: A prophet does not kill by signal.

That is the sharpest sentence in the episode. The silence was the standing order, still in force; the men in the room read it as hesitation and did nothing. And when they ask for the small gesture that would have resolved their doubt — a look, a nod — the answer refuses it on principle, because a killing carried out on a signal is a killing nobody has said aloud. The order had been given in words; a wordless supplement to it will not be supplied.

Ali ibn Abi Talib encountered al-Huwayrith ibn Naqidh, who was one of those whose blood the Messenger of God had declared forfeit that day. Al-Huwayrith recognised him, and Ali said to him: Enemy of God — you are here. Al-Huwayrith said: Wait for me, O son of Abi Talib. Ali replied: I will not keep you waiting for God's sake. Ali killed him.

One man on the list meets the one man who will not hand the command back. It is the only clean execution in the whole account, and the exchange takes four lines.

Ali then went to his sister Umm Hani' bint Abi Talib, and he was still carrying his sword. There were with her two men whose blood the Messenger of God had declared forfeit, from Banu Makhzum, and they were under her protection. When Ali saw them, he drew his sword and moved to kill them.

Umm Hani' stood between him and them and said: My brother, I have given them my protection.

He said: The Messenger of God has ordered them to be killed even if they are found beneath the curtains of the Ka'bah.

She seized his hand — and her grip was strong — and pulled the sword from it, then commanded the two men to enter a house, which they did, closing it upon themselves.

She then went to the Messenger of God, and when he saw her, he welcomed her warmly and asked how she was and what had brought her to him. She told him what had happened, and he laughed, and said: We have honoured those you have taken under your protection, O Umm Hani'.

He then sent for Ali, who came, and the Prophet laughed with him and said: Umm Hani' has prevailed over you.

Ali said: O Messenger of God, by Him who sent you with the truth — she did not cease struggling until she wrested the sword from my hand.

The Messenger of God laughed and said: If Abu Talib had filled the world with his children, they would all have been courageous and strong.

The protection stands, and it stands because a woman of the household extended it — the same instrument al-'Abbas used on the mule the night before, working the same way in a doorway. And the verdict passed on the struggle rebukes neither party. It is a compliment paid to both at once, addressed to their father, on the grounds that the strength the sister showed in taking the sword is the strength the brother showed in holding it.

After the conquest and the submission of its people, the Messenger of God sent out several detachments to call the surrounding Arab tribes to God and His Messenger — not to wage war. Among those he sent was Khalid ibn al-Walid.

Bani Judhimah ibn 'Amir came out to meet Khalid's force in arms. He told them: Lay down your weapons, for the people have surrendered and the fighting is over. The Messenger of God has commanded us only to call people to Islam, not to fight anyone. They put down their weapons, but one of them called out defiantly. Khalid replied in kind, and then — by God — once they had laid down their weapons and he had taken them captive, he ordered their necks to be struck. He had them bound and executed.

The survivors protested: O Khalid, do you wish to shed our blood? The people have already converted and laid down their arms. He said: By God, I will not lay down my weapon. They were overpowered, their weapons taken from them, and after they had disarmed, Khalid commanded them to halt — then put a group of them to the sword.

These men are on no list. They are not fighting; they have been talked into disarming by the man who then kills them, with the true statement that the war is over used as the instrument of disarmament. Everything done all day to keep condemned men alive is reversed here on men who were never condemned.

When the news reached the Messenger of God, he rose and lifted his hands toward the sky, saying: O God, I am innocent before You of what Khalid has done.

He then summoned Ali and said: O Ali, go out to these people, assess their situation, and put the ways of the pre-Islamic era under your feet. He provided him with funds and said: Compensate those who were killed, pay the blood-money, and pay for everything that was taken from them and more.

Ali set out and paid the blood-money for those who had been killed and paid compensation for every loss suffered — even, it is said, paying the value of a dog's bowl — so that nothing remained outstanding, neither in blood nor in property. He then asked them: Is there any remaining claim of blood or property that has not been settled? They said: No. He said: Then I shall distribute among you what remains of the funds entrusted to me by the Messenger of God, as a precaution on his behalf and on behalf of his family. He distributed to them everything that remained, and they received it, thanked him, and bade him farewell.

He then returned and reported what had happened. The Prophet said: Well done, Ali — well done.

Then he turned toward the qibla, stood upright, and raised his hands toward the sky until the whiteness of his armpits was visible, and said three times: O God, I am innocent before You of what Khalid ibn al-Walid has done.

Notice the two disavowals. The first is spoken on hearing the news, before anything has been done about it. The second comes after full restitution has been made and confirmed by the injured party themselves — and it is larger, repeated three times, with the arms raised to their limit. Payment did not close the matter: the compensation settled what was owed, and the disavowal settled what it was. And the audit is exhaustive to the point of absurdity — a dog's bowl is priced — before a surplus nobody was owed is handed over on top.

The reason Khalid acted as he did was that Bani Judhimah had killed his uncle al-Fakkah ibn al-Mughirah during the pre-Islamic era.

That fact is placed last, as an explanation and not as an excuse, and it names precisely what was put under Ali's feet. The massacre was an old blood-claim being collected under cover of a mission to preach.

## The sermon at the Ka'bah door

Ali had taken the keys of the Ka'bah at the time of entry and brought them to the Messenger of God. He said: O Messenger of God, here is the key of the Ka'bah. If you wish to unite in our hands both the watering of the pilgrims and the custodianship of the sanctuary, then do so.

The Prophet said: O Ali, I give you something better than that — what God has given us today is a day of righteousness and faithfulness. But I shall give you what you seek, not what you desire. Return the keys to 'Uthman ibn Talha, the brother of Banu Shayba ibn 'Abd al-Dar ibn 'Abd Manaf.

He handed them back to him, and 'Uthman said: O Messenger of God, we are content with whatever you are content with, and we are with you, O Messenger of God.

The middle clause of that answer has come down obscure — what is sought and what is desired are set against each other in a way the manuscript does not unfold — and it is carried as it stands rather than smoothed. The decision is not obscure. The two great offices of the sanctuary have this morning fallen into the household's hands by right of conquest, and the request is simply that they stay there. The keys go back to the family that held them before the city fell.

The Commander of the Faithful, Ali ibn Abi Talib, said:

Al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib, 'Uthman ibn Talha — the brother of Banu Shayba — and I were once in the Sacred Mosque, each boasting of his distinction. 'Uthman ibn Talha said: The Messenger of God gave me al-Sadana — that is, the keys of the Ka'bah. Al-'Abbas said: And the Messenger of God gave me the watering of the pilgrims — that is, the keys of Zamzam. They said: And what did he give you, Ali?

So God revealed: Do you consider the providing of water to the pilgrim and the maintenance of the Sacred Mosque equal to the deeds of one who believes in God and the Last Day and strives in the cause of God? Those who believed and emigrated and strove in the way of God with their wealth and their lives hold the greatest rank in the sight of God, and those are the triumphant. Their Lord gives them glad tidings of mercy from Him, and of His pleasure, and of gardens where they shall have enduring bliss. They will abide therein forever, for God has a great reward (chapter 9, verses 19 to 22).

That is the answer to the request about the keys, delivered in a different register. The offices are not taken from the men who hold them; what gets answered is whether holding them is the distinction they think it is. Both were assigned in the settlement of Mecca this history covered long ago, and both are now publicly outranked by belief and by striving. The man refused the keys is told he holds the thing the keys were being measured against.

Then the Messenger of God stood and addressed the people, saying:

There is no god but God alone, with no partner. He has fulfilled His promise, granted victory to His servant, and routed the confederates by Himself alone. Every claim of blood, wealth, or feudal privilege is hereby placed under my feet — except the custodianship of the Ka'bah and the watering of the pilgrims. Know that unintentional killing carried out with a whip or stick is treated as quasi-deliberate manslaughter, for which the blood-money is severe: forty camels, including those pregnant in their bellies.

O assembly of Quraysh, God has stripped from you the arrogance of the pre-Islamic era and its glorification of ancestry. All people descend from Adam, and Adam was created from dust.

He then recited: O mankind, We have created you from a male and a female and made you peoples and tribes so that you may know one another. Truly, the most honourable of you in the sight of God is the most righteous (chapter 49, verse 13).

He then said: O assembly of Quraysh, what do you think I am about to do with you? They said: Good — a generous brother and the son of a generous brother. He said: Go, for you are free.

Take the address clause by clause, because it does four things that are easy to run together. It opens with what happened, and every human agent is removed: the promise kept, the servant given victory, the confederates routed by God alone.

Then the abolition, and it is total. Every outstanding claim of blood, every claim of wealth, every inherited privilege of office is placed underfoot — the same image used two days earlier when Ali was sent to Bani Judhimah with a purse. What was ordered there for one tribe is here declared for all at once. And out of that wreckage exactly two things are lifted clear: the custody of the Ka'bah and the watering of the pilgrims, the two offices whose keys had been in his hand that morning and which he had already handed back.

Then a piece of law that seems to come from nowhere and does not. Having cancelled the claims, he states the rate at which a claim will be valued in future: a killing with a whip or a stick, neither clearly deliberate nor clearly accidental, counts as quasi-deliberate, and it is expensive — forty camels, the pregnant ones counted with what they carry. The ledger is wiped and reopened on new terms in one breath.

Then the abolition of rank by descent, addressed to Quraysh by name in the city where descent is the whole of their capital, with the verse supplying the replacement measure. Peoples and tribes are not denied; they are given a purpose, recognition, and then ranked by something else entirely. And the answer they give to his closing question comes out of the old vocabulary — a generous brother, the son of a generous brother, a compliment about lineage offered moments after lineage was struck out. He does not correct it. He tells them to go.

He then said: O people, God declared Mecca sacred on the day He created the heavens and the earth — sacred until the Day of Resurrection. It is therefore not lawful for anyone who believes in God and the Last Day to shed blood in it or to uproot a tree from it. It was not made lawful to anyone before me, and it shall not be made lawful to anyone after me. It was made lawful to me only for this hour, out of anger against its people. Now it has returned to the same sanctity it held yesterday. Let those of you who are present convey this to those who are absent. And if anyone claims that the Messenger of God fought here, say to him: God made it lawful to His Messenger and did not make it lawful to you.

This is the sentence that answers the couplet at the gate. The rajaz announced that today the sanctuary is made lawful; here the same claim is made and then fenced on every side. Lawful, yes — for one hour, to one man, for a stated reason, and never before or after, inside a sanctity that runs from the creation of the heavens and the earth to the resurrection. And then it is turned into an instruction about transmission: tell the absent, and when somebody later cites this day as precedent, deny the precedent in these words. A ruling is issued together with the argument that will be needed against its future misuse.

The poets of the conquest come last, and their verse has reached this manuscript in poor condition — whole lines opaque, names mangled past repair, images surviving with no sentence around them. They are carried at the level of what still reads, with the damage named rather than patched.

Hassan ibn Thabit's long poem on the day survives mostly as fragments about an abandoned dwelling and a grief with no cure, and then turns to the matter at hand: that Gabriel is the messenger of God among them and the Holy Spirit does not fall short; that God said, I have sent a servant, and the answer given was, we will not rise and we will not bear witness to him; that every day brings insult, battle or satire, and the verses answer from a distance. Then the two lines the poem is remembered for, both aimed at Abu Sufyan: who among you mocks the Messenger of God — and, of offering Muhammad protection from you, my father, and his father, and my own self are the offering.

Buhayr ibn Zuhayr ibn Abi Salma's verses are in similar condition: a fierce morning, a strike delivered in Mecca on the day of the conquest, a thousand of Banu 'Uthman standing firm, spears called the pillar of discipline. Al-'Abbas ibn Mardas keeps one clear line out of the wreck — their war-cry on the day of the encounter rang out: support the Prophet and witness his days — and around it a plain soaked by a thousand streams, and God enabling him and humiliating his enemy.

And then the poet who had been on the other side. Ibn al-Zuba'ari had taken refuge in Najran, and Hassan ibn Thabit composed a line against him there — do not make a journey darker with the hatred of Najran — and on hearing it he came out to the Messenger of God and accepted Islam. His verses afterwards are an apology: a sick night that prevents sleep, word reaching him that Ahmad had blamed him and the loss of his wits at hearing it, an admission that he lied when he stood in prayer, the seducers whose causes of ruin are extended and lead a man astray — and then, plainly: today Muhammad has accepted my faith, the enmity has passed and its causes are gone.

## What this episode lands

Mecca was taken without a battle because a single sentence reached it in time, in three clauses, and the first was drafted to appeal to the vanity of the man who would have to shout it.

The day's law was announced and then inverted. Fight nobody who does not fight you, except a named few whose blood is forfeit everywhere — and then almost every one of them is spared by whoever finds him, while men on no list at all are disarmed by a promise of peace and executed over a grievance from before Islam, and answered with full restitution, a surplus nobody was owed, and a disavowal made three times.

And at the door of the Ka'bah the whole inherited system of claims was put underfoot in one clause, with two ancient offices lifted clear and handed back to the families that held them — and a verse recited immediately afterwards that ranks both of those offices below belief and striving. The city was declared sacred from the creation of the world to the resurrection, made lawful for exactly one hour, and the argument against anyone who would later cite that hour was supplied in the same address.
