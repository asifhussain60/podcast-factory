## Where this episode opens

What do you do when the request that gets your best men killed is a polite request to come and teach?

Twice in the space of four months, a delegation arrives in Medina asking for instructors. Send us some of your companions, they say, to teach us the Quran and the rulings of Islam. Both times the men are sent. Both times they are surrounded and killed to the last man but one — and in the first case three of them are not killed at all. They are sold, as merchandise, into the hands of Quraysh in Mecca.

That is where this episode starts, and it sets the pattern for the fourth year. Every disaster in the next hour runs through a promise of protection. Six men are guaranteed their lives under an oath sworn by God, and the oath turns out to be a sales tactic. Seventy are sent to Najd under the personal protection of a chieftain who asked for them himself. A whole tribe inside Medina is promised armed support by neighbours who then do not move.

Three movements. First the two disasters, at a place called al-Raji' and at the well of Ma'unah. Then the expulsion of the Banu al-Nadir, which a blood debt out of the second disaster sets in motion. Then the rest of the fourth year: four separate marches in which nobody fought at all, and one grave.

Listen for how the record keeps score here. Not by casualties — by promises made and promises kept. And listen especially for the last few minutes, because the year that produces no battle produces the most doctrinally loaded page in the whole slice.

## The teachers who were sold

After Uhud, a delegation came to the Messenger of God from the Adal and al-Qarah, who are of the Hunn ibn Khuzaymah.

They said: O Messenger of God, send with us some of your companions who will instruct us in religion, teach us the Quran, and acquaint us with the rulings of Islam.

So he sent six of his companions with them: Murtad ibn Abi Murtad al-Ghanawi, an ally of Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib; Khalid ibn al-Bakir al-Laythi, an ally of the Banu 'Adi ibn Ka'b; 'Asim ibn Thabit ibn Abi al-Aqlah of the Banu 'Amr ibn 'Awf of al-Aws; Zayd ibn al-Dinnah of the Banu Bayadah of al-Khazraj; Khubayb ibn 'Adi of the Banu Jahjaba; and 'Abdullah ibn Tariq, ally of the Banu Zafar. And he appointed Murtad over them as commander.

Note who is on that list before it goes wrong. 'Asim ibn Thabit is the man who had killed two of the sons of Talha around the standard at Uhud weeks earlier — you heard his name in the roll of the twenty-two. And Murtad's patron, Hamza, is in a grave on the far side of the same battle. This is not a party of unknowns; it is six senior men sent out on a teaching commission.

They set out with the delegation. When the party reached the region of the Hijaz, their hosts betrayed them. They raised the alarm, and the people of the surrounding area answered the call. The six found themselves met on the march by men with drawn swords, who cornered them, and the companions drew their own swords to fight.

Then their assailants called out: we swear by God, we do not intend to kill you. We only wish to obtain something by delivering you to the people of Mecca, and you have God's covenant and pledge from us that we will not kill you.

Read the shape of that offer. It is an oath sworn in the name of God — the strongest guarantee available in that world — and what it guarantees is that the men will not be killed here, because they are worth more alive at the other end. What is being promised is not their lives but their delivery.

Three of them refused it outright. Murtad ibn Abi Murtad, Khalid ibn al-Bakir and 'Asim ibn Thabit said: by God, we will accept no covenant made with polytheists.

And 'Asim ibn Thabit said in verse: I have no concern for you — I am the wielder of the strung bow, and the bow holds its string within it, less than the span of its stave. Death is a certainty, and life is fleeting. Every soul that God protects must one day descend; one by one they fall like deer. If I do not fight you, let my mother be shamed.

He said also, in a more broken piece: Abu Sulayman, and the feathers of the quiver; if the arrows were scattered, I would not be routed; and a tender shield fashioned from the hide of a barren ox — and he believed in what Muhammad brought.

'Asim was known as Abu Sulayman. He fought the enemy until he was killed, and his companions were killed with him.

And then the strangest passage in this stretch. When 'Asim was slain, a man called Hathil tried to sever his head, to sell it to Sulafa bint Sa'd, whose son had been killed and who had vowed that if his head were brought to her she would drink wine from his skull. But a swarm of bees settled over his body and kept them from reaching him. 'Asim had made a covenant with God that no polytheist, man or woman, would ever touch him.

It was said: the bees protected him, and when night fell we tried to take him, but God sent a flash flood that filled the valley and swept him away.

There are two vows in that paragraph and only one is kept. A woman vows to drink from a man's skull and never gets the skull. A man vows that no idolater's hand will touch him, and the vow is kept by insects and then by weather — and to keep it completely, his body has to be taken away from his own side as well as from the enemy's. Nobody buried 'Asim ibn Thabit. The valley did.

As for Zayd ibn al-Dinnah, Khubayb ibn 'Adi and 'Abdullah ibn Tariq — they were reluctant, and desired to live. They surrendered and were taken captive, and their captors set out with them toward Mecca to sell them there.

That is the whole of the source's comment on the three who accepted the covenant. No condemnation and no excuse: it divides six men into three who fought and three who surrendered, records the reason in five words, and follows both roads to the end.

When they reached Dhahran, 'Abdullah ibn Tariq wrenched his hand free of his bonds and seized his sword. The people drew back from him and pelted him with stones until he was killed, and he was buried there. So the second road forks too: one of the three changes his mind halfway to the market, and is stoned by men who will not close with an armed prisoner.

They arrived in Mecca with Khubayb ibn 'Adi and Zayd ibn al-Dinnah, and sold them to the Quraysh. The Quraysh then killed them, after crucifying them. They roused their tribes and assembled every gathering against them.

And Khubayb said, when they were about to kill him: I am in a trust that has been lost, and I drew near to a tall, ancient trunk. They gathered their sons and their wives, and to God I complain of my grief, then more grief. I do not watch the factions that surround me when I die; they have cut my flesh and I have despaired of my longing. O Throne, grant me patience for what You have willed. This is for the sake of God, and if He wills, I will not close my eyes in fear. They offered me disbelief, and I chose death over it — but beware, Hell is a blazing fire. I have no dread of death, for I am dead on whatever side my death comes in God's path. I hope that if I die a Muslim, I need not fear, for to God is my return.

Notice the line that answers the whole episode: they offered me disbelief, and I chose death over it. The man who surrendered rather than fight, and was carried to Mecca as goods, is given the last word on his own case — and what he says is that the choice was put to him a second time, at the stake, and that this time he took the other option. The lost trust in his opening image is the safe conduct he accepted on the road.

Hassan ibn Thabit composed two poems on what came to be called the Day of Return. Of the first there survives a blessing on those who followed; Ibn al-Bakir leading them, and Khubayb; he died, and then his pledge was written in light; the son of Tariq and the son of al-Dinnah among them, gaining the exalted reward. Of the second: they said on the Day of Return that they were free — we have those who were killed treacherously yet remained loyal. It was not their blood that was a trust broken at this place and at Safa. So to live beneath a dome of treachery and misfortune that boasts — yet its evil never touched them in secret.

Both poems fix the same interpretation, and it is the opposite of the obvious one. These men were not defeated. They were sold under a broken trust, and the treachery adheres to the sellers, not to the sold.

Four months after Uhud, in the month of Safar, it happened again.

The Messenger of God had spent the rest of Shawwal, then Dhu al-Qa'dah and Dhu al-Hijjah, in Medina; the custodianship of that pilgrimage season was in the hands of the polytheists, who held the sacred months inviolable. Then he dispatched the companions of the well of Ma'unah.

It came about because Abu Bara' — 'Amir ibn Malik ibn Ja'far, the man they called the Player of Spears — had come to him in Medina, and the Prophet presented Islam to him. He neither embraced it nor turned away from it outright. He said: O Muhammad, if you were to send men from your companions to the people of Najd to call them to God, I hope they would respond to you.

The Messenger of God replied: I fear for them the people of Najd.

Abu Bara' said: I am their neighbour. Send them, and let them call the people to your message.

Hold onto the Prophet's answer, stated plainly and in advance: I fear for them the people of Najd. The objection is raised, and it is overcome by an offer of personal protection from a man who has not himself accepted the religion whose missionaries he is offering to protect.

So he sent al-Mundhir ibn 'Amr of the Banu Sa'idah in command of forty men — according to al-Bukhari the number was seventy — from among the finest of the Muslims. They marched until they reached the well of Ma'unah, which lies between the land of the Banu 'Amir and the lava fields of the Banu Sulaym.

When they encamped there, they sent one of their number ahead — a man named Haram ibn Milhan — carrying a letter from the Messenger of God to 'Amir ibn Tufayl ibn Malik ibn Ja'far ibn Kilab. When Haram arrived, 'Amir did not so much as glance at the letter before ordering a man to kill him. He was killed.

'Amir then called upon the Banu 'Amir to rise against the Muslim party. They refused to betray the protection Abu Bara' had extended. He called on them again, and they said: we will not break the protection of Abu Bara'.

That refusal is the honourable centre of the episode, and it is delivered twice by men on the wrong side of the fight. 'Amir ibn Tufayl belongs to the Banu 'Amir; Abu Bara' is of the Banu 'Amir; and the Banu 'Amir will not touch men their own chief has covered. The protection worked exactly as promised — against everyone it could bind.

So 'Amir went around it. He roused the tribes of the Banu Sulaym — 'Usayya, Ra'l and Dhakwan — and they answered his call. They closed in on the Muslim encampment and surrounded the companions at their halting place. When the companions saw them coming, they took up their weapons and fought. They were martyred to the last man, save for Ka'b ibn Zayd of the Banu Dinar ibn al-Najjar, whom the attackers left for dead among the fallen. He lay among the slain until the enemy departed, and lived on until he was killed as a martyr on the day of al-Khandaq.

Two of the party had been away from the camp with the mounts: 'Amr ibn Umayya al-Damri and a man of the Ansar. They were alerted to the fate of their companions only by the sight of birds circling overhead. They came to look, and found them lying dead with the enemy horsemen standing over them. The Ansari rushed forward and fought until he was killed, having first said: I will not report back about what has befallen my companions while still alive.

'Amr ibn Umayya was taken prisoner. When 'Amir ibn al-Tufayl learned that he was of the Mudar, he cut off his ear and released him, claiming that this fulfilled a vow he had made to his mother — so the man who ignored a letter and hired a massacre discharges an act of piety with a stranger's ear.

'Amr ibn Umayya set out on the road back. Along the way he came upon two men from the Banu 'Amir and asked them about their tribe, and they told him. They held a treaty of protection with the Messenger of God, though 'Amr did not know of it. He waited until they fell asleep, then attacked and killed them both.

When 'Amr arrived and told him what had happened, the Messenger of God said: I have killed two men whose blood I must answer for. Then he added: this is the work of Abu Bara' — that is why I despised it.

Look at those two sentences, because everything in the second half of this episode comes out of them. The first speaks of the killing as his own act and his own liability: the men were under his treaty, his messenger killed them, and the debt is his — he does not disown 'Amr, who did not know. The second reaches back past the massacre to the offer that caused it, an offer he had accepted against his own stated judgment. Abu Bara' himself was deeply troubled by what 'Amir had done.

Hassan ibn Thabit went to work on that, inciting Abu Bara' against 'Amir ibn al-Tufayl: he was one of the wolves of the people of Nahd — did the Banu Umm al-Banin not want you? And what a deliberate wrong this is. 'Amir mocked Abu Bara' in return: what happened in the two events after me? Your uncle, the noble Hakam ibn Sa'd; your father Abu al-Hurub — Abu Bara'.

And Hassan wept for the dead of the well: with tears from the eye, and no holding back the tear. On the horses of the Prophet they were found the next day. You betrayed their covenant treacherously; destruction struck them, bound by a people. What longing for Mundhir, if he were to tell me.

## The rock and the palm trees

The expulsion of the Banu al-Nadir took place in that same fourth year, and it began with the blood debt.

The Messenger of God went to them seeking help with the blood money owed for the two men of the Banu 'Amir whom 'Amr ibn Umayyah al-Damri had killed. Between the Banu al-Nadir and the Banu 'Amir there was a pact and an oath of allegiance, which is why he came to them and not to someone else.

They said: yes, O Abu al-Qasim, we will help you with what you have asked.

Then they turned to one another in secret and said: you will never find a man like him in such a position again.

That is one of the coldest sentences in this book, and it is worth pausing on the position they mean. He is standing beside the wall of one of their houses, in their quarter, on an errand about money, having just been told yes. The opportunity is not created by them; it is the ordinary vulnerability of a man who has come to ask a favour and been received.

A man was stationed on top of the house, and they said among themselves: let him hurl a rock down upon him, and rid us of him. With the Messenger of God were the Father of Imams and a small group of his companions.

A man of the Banu al-Nadir was assigned the task — it is said his name was 'Amr ibn Jahash ibn Ka'b — and he said: I will kill him. He climbed up to throw the rock down from the top of the wall.

But revelation came to the Prophet from heaven, informing him of what the people had planned. He rose and left, heading back toward the city, and his companions followed. He then told them what the tribe had plotted in its treachery.

Note that nothing was thrown. The plot is defeated at the stage of intention, and the only reason it is on the record is that the intention was reported from above. The whole case against the Banu al-Nadir rests on a plan, not an act.

The Messenger of God commanded his companions to prepare for war and march out. They set out in Rabi' al-Awwal, and he besieged the Banu al-Nadir for six nights. They fortified themselves in their strongholds, and he ordered their palm trees cut down and burned.

'Abdullah ibn Ubayy ibn Salul and a group of the hypocrites had sent word urging them to hold firm and resist, promising them: we will not abandon you. If you are attacked, we will fight alongside you; and if you are expelled, we will go out with you.

That promise has two halves and both are specific — armed support if attacked, exile alongside them if expelled. Both events happened. Neither half was honoured. And this is the second time in two episodes that this man has told people what he would do and then not done it; his walk-out on the road to Uhud with a third of the army is still fresh.

Concerning the hypocrites, verses were then revealed from the chapter of the Gathering. God cast terror into their hearts, and the hypocrites shrank back from what they had promised.

So the Banu al-Nadir asked for safe passage and leave to evacuate. He granted them security on the condition that they take whatever their camels could carry, except weapons. They loaded what they could of their wealth and departed for Khaybar, while some went to al-Sham.

And then the detail nobody would invent. They left with their women and their children, going forth with tambourines and music, with trumpets sounding behind them — a spectacle the like of which had rarely been seen from any community of their time.

Sit with that image, because it is the only one of its kind in this volume. A tribe under eviction orders, having lost a six-night siege and watched its orchards burn, packs what the camels will hold and leaves the city playing instruments. The account does not explain it and does not sneer at it; it records that people had rarely seen anything like it. Whatever else that procession was, it was staged. They were not going to be marched out quietly.

Their remaining property was left to the Messenger of God, and it became his to dispose of as he saw fit. He divided it among the first Muhajirun, to the exclusion of the Ansar. However, Sahl ibn Hunayf and Abu Dajanah — Sammak ibn Kharshah — complained to him of poverty, and so he allotted shares to them as well. Only two men of the Banu al-Nadir embraced Islam, and he therefore spared them and left them to their affairs.

Three things there. This is the first property in this history that falls to the Prophet's sole discretion rather than being divided as spoils by rule. He uses that discretion for the emigrants, who arrived in Medina with nothing, rather than the hosts who already had land. And the rule bends immediately, twice, on a plea of poverty from two of the Ansar — one of them the man who wore the red headband at Uhud.

Ali ibn Abi Talib composed verses recalling the evacuation and the killing of Ka'b ibn al-Ashraf, whose account was given earlier in this history. He had distinguished himself in this campaign with a celebrated stand, a virtue recorded by many.

What survives of his verses runs like this: I knew, and whoever is discerning knows, the tight words from which there is no escape. And truly I understood, and did not encounter any other refuge but God, the Most Merciful, the Most Compassionate. He came not with injustice, nor did he pardon the guilty — O you who broke your promise, you are the fool among them. By God, one who has no fear of Him is faithless; do you not fear even the slightest torment? To wrestle beneath his swords, as Abu Ashraf died, felled at the heel. So he left them, and then pronounced a curse upon them, while they were dwelling in the finest of abodes.

And Ka'b ibn Malik said, recalling the evacuation: indeed, time has a turning that keeps revolving, and she was made sorrowful by her own treachery. There came to them from God, the All-Wise, illuminating verses built upon truth; a truthful warner delivered the Book, and you deny us, O overbearing one. Whoever follows him is guided to every right course, while they turn away from the truth seeking escape. When they drank of treachery and disbelieved, God ruled, and His ruling does not change. So they tasted the folly of their own affairs, deceived, and they turned away. And they evacuated, the deliberate people of Qithqa'.

Both poems date the tribe's fall to the same cause, and it is not the siege. They drank of treachery. She was made sorrowful by her own treachery. You broke your promise. Neither poet claims a military victory. What they claim is that a word was given and withdrawn, and that everything followed from that.

## The year that fought nobody

Then came the affair of Dhu al-Riqa', in the fourth year. The Messenger of God remained in Medina after the campaign against the Banu al-Nadir through Rabi' al-Akhir and part of Jumada al-Awwal, then set out into Najd, intent on the Banu Muharib and the Banu Tha'labah of Ghatafan.

It was called the affair of Dhu al-Riqa' because in it they planted their banners; Dhu al-Riqa' is a tree in that locality, also known as the place of the snake.

A company of Ghatafan advanced toward it, and the two sides drew close to one another — and no fighting broke out. The two forces were wary of each other, until the Messenger of God led the people in the Prayer of Fear, after which they dispersed.

That is the whole of the battle, and it is the first of four in a row that end this way. Two armies come within sight, both hold, and the thing that actually happens on the ground is a prayer.

The description of that prayer is the only piece of law in this episode, and it is transmitted through the household — reported from Ja'far ibn Muhammad, from his father, from his forebears.

The Messenger of God led the Prayer of Fear with his companions at Dhu al-Riqa'. His companions divided into two groups. One group he stationed facing the enemy; the other stood in prayer behind him. He stood and they stood, he recited and they listened, he bowed and they bowed, he prostrated and they prostrated. Then he remained standing while those who had prayed with him completed a cycle on their own, and gave the salutation to one another. They went out to relieve their companions and stood against the enemy, while their companions took their place behind him. These too stood as he stood, listened while he recited, bowed as he bowed, prostrated as he prostrated. He sat and they sat, then he gave the salutation; and they rose and completed a cycle on their own, then gave the salutation to one another and departed.

Notice what that procedure is engineered to protect. The imam prays one complete prayer, once. Half the army faces outward at every moment, including during the prostration, when a formation is most helpless. And what makes it work is the imam holding his position — standing, then sitting — while each half finishes alone. The congregation is split in two and the prayer is not: it stays one act, performed by one man, with two shifts rotating through it under arms.

Then the account of Ghurth ibn al-Harith.

A man of the Banu Muharib — said to be Ghurth — asked his people, Ghatafan and Muharib: shall I not kill Muhammad for you? They said: yes — but how will you do it? He said: I will kill him.

He came close to the Messenger of God while the Prophet was seated, with his own sword lying in his lap. The man said: O Muhammad, look — is this your sword? He said: yes.

The man took hold of it, drew it from its sheath, and began to threaten the Prophet with it. He shook it menacingly — but God restrained him.

Then he said: O Muhammad, are you not afraid of me, with your sword in my hand?

The Messenger of God replied: no. God protects me from you.

And the man put the sword back in its sheath.

Concerning this, God revealed: O you who believe, remember the blessing of God upon you when a people resolved to stretch out their hands against you, but He restrained their hands from you. And fear God — and in God let the believers place their trust (chapter 5, verse 11).

That is the second assassination in this episode defeated at the stage of intention. In the first, a man climbed a wall with a rock and never threw it because the plan was reported from heaven. In the second, a man had the weapon in his hand at arm's length from a seated target, asked his victim to be afraid, and got back a flat sentence with no bravado in it. The verse that follows names neither episode. It generalises: hands were raised against you and held.

He then returned to Medina, where he remained for the rest of Jumada al-Awwal and Jumada al-Akhirah.

Then came the affair of the second Badr, in Sha'ban of the fourth year. Abu Sufyan and the disbelievers of Quraysh had appointed Badr as the place for a rematch, and the Messenger of God set out in answer to the challenge. He reached the market of Badr and stayed eight nights, waiting for him.

Abu Sufyan marched out with the people of Mecca until he halted at Dhahran — some say at 'Asfan. He looked at his companions, saw in them a lack of resolve, and turned back. He said: O people of Quraysh, nothing will serve you but a year of plenty, in which you can graze your animals on the trees and drink their milk. This year of yours is a lean one, and I am turning back — so turn back.

The people of Mecca turned back, and they were afterwards called the army of the barley drink. It was said of them: you went out only to drink your barley. The Messenger of God waited until it was clear Abu Sufyan had turned back, and then returned to Medina.

'Abdullah ibn Rawahah said of it: he gave his word honestly, yet did not keep it — Abu Sufyan promised us Badr, and we did not fall short. I swear, if you meet us, you will find us ready. We left him the limbs of 'Utbah and his son, and 'Umar; Abu Jahl we left at Thawiyya. You disobeyed the Messenger of God, following your own religion, and your wretched counsel led you astray. A ransom for the Messenger of God — my family and my wealth. A blazing star in the darkness of night: we obeyed him, and nothing in us was altered but through him.

That is a mocking poem about a battle that did not happen, and the joke it lands is a nickname. An army marches out, loses its nerve at the halfway point, is told by its own commander that the grazing is poor this year, and goes home to be known ever after by the drink it went out to have. The insult is not cowardice. It is triviality.

The account then restates its own bookkeeping: he had previously concluded a treaty with the Banu al-Nadir; Islam had spread; he had established himself in Medina after going out to Badr for the appointed meeting. A full year had passed and another had begun.

And when he returned to Medina he remained there some months, until Dhu al-Hijjah had passed. The polytheists were the guardians of the Sacred House that year. Then he set out — but turned back before reaching it, and he received no rebuke for doing so.

That is the fourth march of the year, and it is one sentence long. He goes out; he comes back; nothing is held against him. Four times in a year an expedition sets off and no battle is fought, and the record keeps all four rather than quietly dropping the ones with nothing in them.

So what did the fourth year actually produce? A form of prayer for men under threat. A verse about hands raised and held. A nickname that made a great tribe ridiculous. And one grave.

In the fourth year of the emigration, Fatima bint Asad died — the mother of the Father of Imams, Fatima bint Asad ibn Hashim ibn 'Abd Manaf.

Al-Waqidi said she was the first woman to embrace Islam after Khadijah al-Kubra, and the first woman to migrate from Mecca to Medina on foot. Ibn 'Abbas said the Messenger of God used to visit her and rest in her home, and that she was devoted to him.

And a report reaches us from her own mouth. I heard Fatima bint Asad say that the Messenger of God once remarked: on the Day of Resurrection, people will be gathered naked. She said: how shameful that would be. He said: I ask God, exalted be He, to clothe you. And she heard him mention the torment of the grave, and said: how feeble I am before that. And he said: I ask God to spare you that.

Mujahid said she was the first Hashimite woman born to a Hashimite father and a Hashimite mother. 'Ikrimah said she used to circle the Ka'bah while pregnant with the Father of Imams; her labour overtook her, the door of the Ka'bah was opened for her, and she gave birth inside it — and this history has already told that story in its place.

Al-Waqidi said: the Messenger of God attended her funeral. He ordered that she be shrouded in his own shirt. And he went down into her grave and lay in it, saying: I wanted to ease this for her, and so that it might benefit her as it benefited Abu Talib.

Take those two acts together before the last report. She is buried in the Prophet's own shirt, and he climbs into the grave and lies down in it first — and the reason he gives is not symbolic. He says it is to make the place easier for her, and that he has done it before, for the man who raised him. The same physical intercession, performed twice, for the two people who took him in.

And it was also narrated that when he saw her in her grave he said: your son, your son — Ali, Ali. He was asked to explain, and he said: the two angels asked her, who is your Lord? She said, God. They said, who is your prophet? She said, Muhammad is the Messenger of God. They said to her, who is your Imam? She hesitated — and I said to her, your son, your son. She said: 'Aqil. And it was said to her: Ali, Ali.

That is where this episode ends, and it is the doctrinal centre of the whole slice. The interrogation at the grave has three questions, not two. The first two are answered instantly and without help. The third — who is your Imam — is where she falters, and it is not a trick question for her: the answer is one of her own sons. She names the wrong one and has to be corrected. This history has already established that the succession never lapses and that authority passes by designation. What this scene adds is what that doctrine costs at the moment it is examined: the third question is asked of everybody, it is asked last, and the mother of the man himself needed prompting to get it right.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry forward.

The first is that every catastrophe here came through a guarantee. Six teachers were given God's own covenant that they would not be killed, and the covenant meant only that they would be sold alive. Seventy went to Najd under a chief's protection, and it bound everyone it could bind — the Banu 'Amir refused twice to break it — so the man who wanted them dead hired a different tribe. A besieged community in Medina was promised armed help and shared exile by neighbours who then did not move. And the record scores this year by promises rather than casualties: what the poets say of the men killed on the Day of Return is not that they lost, but that the treachery adhered to the sellers and never touched the sold.

The second is the chain of consequence, which this history lays out without ever announcing it. A survivor walking home from a massacre kills two sleeping travellers he does not know are under treaty. The Prophet accepts the debt as his own and goes to ask a neighbouring tribe for help paying it. Standing at their wall on that errand, he is nearly killed with a rock. Six nights of siege and a burnt orchard later, that tribe leaves Medina for Khaybar playing tambourines, and its land becomes the first property in this history to fall to the Prophet's personal discretion. Every link is somebody else's decision, and none of it was aimed at the outcome it produced.

The third is that a year with no battle in it is where the doctrine surfaces. Four expeditions marched and none fought. What the year yielded instead was a prayer designed so that the imam prays once and half the army always faces outward; a verse about hands raised and restrained; a mocking nickname for Quraysh; and the death of the first woman to accept Islam after Khadijah, buried in the Prophet's shirt in a grave he had lain down in himself. And in that grave, three questions — the third about the Imam, asked last, and the one she could not answer without help.
