## Where this episode picks up

Why would a monk who had spent years watching Meccan caravans pass his hermitage without once coming down to speak to them break that habit for a single boy travelling with his uncle?

He did, and the answer he gave — privately, taking the uncle aside — was a warning rather than a congratulation: take him home, and be careful who sees him. That pattern repeats through this whole episode. Every person in it who recognises what the boy is responds by trying to get hold of him, protect him, marry him, or kill him. Nobody who works it out stays neutral.

The previous episode closed with an old man dying in Mecca with his grandson in his lap, having already put the care of the child in writing, in verse, to the son he trusted above the others. This episode is what that son did with the charge. The boy is eight when it begins and past fifty when it ends, and in between the record moves from a private family arrangement to a public one: a journey north, a stone lifted onto a cloak, a war fought in the sacred months, a marriage proposal that came from the woman, and finally a revelation and the first three people on earth to accept it.

Three movements. The making of a reputation — guardianship, the monk at Busra, and the name the Quraysh gave him before he claimed anything. Then a formal marriage address, delivered by Abu Talib in front of the leading men of Mecca, which is recited here whole because it is the one set piece in this stretch and the only place where the family states its own standing in its own words. Then the household that formed around that marriage, the cousin raised inside it, and the two days in Rajab on which everything the rest of this book is about begins.

Watch for one thing in particular. The author is building toward a claim that will be contested for centuries — that the second person to accept Islam was a boy in the house — and he knows it. When he gets there he stops narrating and starts arguing, naming the objection, quoting the Qur'an back at it, and conceding nothing.

## The monk and the Trustworthy

When 'Abd al-Muttalib died the Messenger of God was eight years old, and Abu Talib did exactly what he had been asked to do. He took charge of the boy, sheltered him, singled him out with special care, adopted him as his own, and kept him close. The author's summary is not a formality: of all Abu Talib's children, this was the one he was most devoted to and most loving toward.

The first test of that came when Abu Talib decided to travel to the Levant with a party of Quraysh merchants.

He was assembling provisions and equipment when he noticed something on the boy's face — signs of profound sadness — and asked what was wrong. What he got back was grief at being left behind and how deeply the separation was already being felt. The boy asked to be taken along. Abu Talib relented and took him.

That small domestic scene is the reason everything else in this section happened. A merchant changed his packing list, and a caravan carried a child north.

They stopped near the hermitage of a monk in the land of Busra, a man called Bahira. Quraysh caravans had passed and rested there often, and the author is careful with what he says about the monk's habit: he never came down to them, and he never spoke to any of them. Not reserve exactly — a settled practice, years long.

This time he came down, and asked after their well-being.

He was a learned man. He looked intently at the boy, studied him carefully, and then began asking questions about his situation and his story. He was told. And having confirmed that the child was in fact travelling with the party, he drew Abu Talib aside and spoke to him privately.

"This is the affair of your nephew — he will be a prophet, and this is his time. His sign is clear and his proof established. Beware of the Jews on his account, and return — out of love for him — to your land, for I fear for him from them."

Four claims in three sentences, and only the first is what people usually remember. He will be a prophet; the time is now; the evidence is already legible to anyone trained to read it; and precisely because it is legible, he is in danger from others who can read it. The monk does not offer to help. He tells the uncle to leave.

And while he was still speaking, some Jews arrived. They saw Bahira with Abu Talib, looked closely at the boy, and recognised in him the signs of prophethood. They grasped it — and Bahira turned them away from him, warned them against the Quraysh, and told them plainly that they would not be able to harm him. Abu Talib took his nephew back to Mecca.

The author then gives a second version of the same encounter without harmonising it, which is his habit throughout this book. In this account Bahira first saw the boy with a cloud of light above him, shading him and moving with him wherever he went. That is what brought him down from the hermitage. He prepared food, invited the whole caravan, and gathered them together. Three Jews opposed him over it — named as Driss, Tamam and Zarir — intending to lie in wait for the child. Bahira took a different course.

What that different course was survives mainly in Abu Talib's own verse, and the verse is badly damaged in transmission, so it has to be read for its shape rather than its lines. It recalls the uncle's anxiety on the road and the boy reaching for the reins; a meal to which everyone was summoned and from which no one was excluded; a shading cloud over a company that sat together while the rest of the people could not sleep; and then the confrontation. Driss and Tamam came with the intention of killing the child, and the monk turned them away — not by force but by argument, and specifically by an argument from their own scripture. He tells them they are not unjust men, and settles the matter by the interpretation of the Torah so that they may be certain. Another of Abu Talib's poems on the same journey adds a last image: the monk weeping in dread as the caravan left with the boy, as if he did not expect to see them return.

A man who had refused to speak to these caravans for years came down, fed them, argued three would-be assassins out of it using their own books, and then wept when they left. That is the whole of what the record gives us about Bahira, and this author does not embroider it.

Back in Mecca, the relationship deepened into something the author describes in the language of rank rather than affection. The Messenger of God was with his uncle like the most beloved of his own children — honoured and esteemed — and the reason given is evidential: on account of the signs Abu Talib had witnessed in him, his virtue, the marks of his prophecy and message, and his father's bequest concerning him. Abu Talib was not being sentimental about an orphan. He was acting on what he had seen.

And the traffic ran both ways. The author lists what was in the boy — nobility of character, purity of lineage, forbearance, truthfulness, sound reason, piety, asceticism, virtue — and then makes a point of the conduct rather than the qualities: he was the most righteous of people in his dealings with his uncle, and held with him an elevated and firmly established standing. Fatima bint Asad, Abu Talib's wife, preferred him above all her own children, honouring him, serving him, and glorifying him.

He was, in other words, a nephew whom his aunt ranked above her own sons. Remember that, because one of those sons is coming.

The public turn came with a building project. The Ka'bah had been demolished, and the Quraysh set about rebuilding it. He was a young man by then, raised — the author says — upon the purity and noble character of the prophets, and the people already called him al-Amin, the Trustworthy.

The work went well until they reached the place of the Black Stone. Every clan of the Quraysh wanted the honour of carrying it to its position, and they fell into dispute and then into quarrel. What they eventually agreed on is a measure of how bad it had become: they would submit the matter to the judgment of whoever walked in front of them next.

He walked in.

"The Trustworthy man has come," they said — and they said it before they knew what he would rule. They put the matter to him. He took his garment, laid the stone on it, and said: let one man from each clan of the Quraysh take hold of the hem of the garment, and let them all lift it together.

They were pleased with the ruling and accepted it, and they carried it that way to its place. Then he set the stone into its position with his own hand.

It is worth being precise about what he did there, because it is easy to admire it for the wrong reason. He did not divide the honour or find a compromise between the claimants. He changed the unit of action: the thing that carried the stone was no longer a clan but the whole of Quraysh, holding one cloth. The dispute did not have to be settled because it could no longer be stated. And the Quraysh called him al-Amin, the author says, because his grace, honesty and chastity had become manifest to them — a title conferred by the people who would later oppose him, on the basis of nothing but observed conduct.

The other public event of these years cut the opposite way. When he reached the age of fourteen and entered his fifteenth year, the War of the Ungodly broke out — the Quraysh and their allies from Kinana on one side, Qays ibn Aylan on the other. The name is a verdict, and the author explains it: it was called the War of the Ungodly because neither party held sacred what was sacred. Prohibitions that both sides recognised were violated between them.

He was present at some of those engagements when he was twenty. His own description of what he did there is careful: "I was — on that day — defending my uncles," meaning that he returned upon the enemy the arrows they had shot. He did not open the exchange; he sent back what came. And the Quraysh and Kinana gained the upper hand when he came out with them — they knew it, and they would ask Abu Talib to bring him along.

So by his early twenties he had two reputations running at once in the same town. He was the man both sides of a deadlocked quarrel would accept as judge sight unseen, and he was the fighter one side asked for by name. The author summarises the war briefly and moves on, which tells you which of the two he thinks matters.

It is worth pausing on how little of this is doctrine. Nothing in this movement is a claim about God. A boy asks to go on a business trip; a monk breaks a habit; a stone is moved on a cloak; arrows are returned in a war nobody should have been fighting. The author is assembling a record of public conduct, witnessed by people who had no theological stake in it — and much of it witnessed by people who would later be on the other side. That is the point of putting it here. When the claim finally comes, the town cannot say it had no idea who was making it.

## Abu Talib's marriage address

Khadijah bint Khuwaylid worked it out for herself, and the author is careful about how.

Her lineage is given in full — Khuwaylid ibn Asad ibn 'Abd al-'Uzza ibn Qusayy ibn Kilab ibn Murrah ibn Ka'b ibn Lu'ayy ibn Ghalib ibn Fihr — which places her on the same trunk as her future husband, a few branches over. What she beheld was his virtue, and what shone from his signs and was evident in the marks of his prophethood and the glad tidings of it.

But she had a second, independent source. Her slave Maysarah had accompanied him on some of his trading journeys, and had seen the cloud shade him. Monks on those routes had spoken of his virtue and told Maysarah that his mission was near.

So the same testimony that reached Abu Talib on the road to Busra reached Khadijah in Mecca, by way of an employee. She was, the author says, a resolute woman held in the highest esteem among her people, and she acted on the information.

She sent word to him: "O my cousin, I have desired you on account of your kinship, your standing among your people, your honesty, your good character, and the truthfulness of your speech." And then she offered herself to him in marriage.

Five reasons, and not one of them is the cloud or the monks or the signs. She had the private evidence and she made the public case, and the public case was character. At that time she was the foremost woman of the Quraysh in honour and nobility of lineage.

Abu Talib stepped forward and conducted the marriage on his nephew's behalf. He attended the contract along with the leading men of Banu Hashim and the notables of the Quraysh, and when the Messenger of God was wed, Abu Talib stood and spoke. This is the address, whole:

"Praise be to Allah, who made us the descendants of Abraham, peace be upon him, and planted from Ishmael a flourishing root, an honoured lineage. He made him from the heart of His house, and placed in him reverence, and made it a sanctuary of refuge for us, a safe haven, and He made it a gathering place for people.

And this nephew of mine, Muhammad ibn Abdallah — no man is weighed against him in merit but that he surpasses him in vision and grace, in what is permitted and what is sound, in piety and in nobility — though when it comes to wealth, say: wealth is a fleeting shadow, a transient thing. And Muhammad is a man whose lineage you know, and whose kinship, truthfulness, and honesty are plain to all. He seeks the hand of Khadijah bint Khuwaylid, and she has agreed with what he has put forward and deferred from my wealth, the amount being such-and-such — and by Allah, after this he shall have great standing and momentous affairs."

Two paragraphs, and they are doing different jobs.

The first is a genealogy compressed into a single sentence about a place. Descent from Abraham, a root planted from Ishmael, the House at the centre of it, and the sanctuary that follows — refuge, safety, a gathering place. That is the whole argument of the earlier episodes of this volume stated as a preamble, and Abu Talib states it not as history but as the family's standing warrant for the transaction about to be conducted. He is saying who is at the table.

The second paragraph is a valuation, and its most interesting move is the concession. Abu Talib runs through the qualities on which his nephew outweighs any man — vision, grace, lawfulness, soundness, piety, nobility — and then, at exactly the point where a marriage negotiation would normally name assets, he stops and says what everyone in the room already knows: on wealth, there is nothing to say, because wealth is a fleeting shadow. He is marrying a young man with no money to the richest woman in Mecca and he does not pretend otherwise. He simply declines to accept that it counts.

Then the operative clause. The dowry is being deferred out of Abu Talib's own property, and the amount is left unstated in the transmitted text. And the address closes not with a blessing but with a prediction: by God, after this he shall have great standing and momentous affairs. The uncle who had heard the monk at Busra is telling a room full of Quraysh notables, at a wedding, that something is coming — without saying what.

It was said the dowry was twenty she-camels, ten waqi of gold, a male slave and a female slave. Note whose property that was. The bride was the wealthiest woman in Mecca; the dowry came, deferred, out of the uncle's estate. The family that had nothing to declare at the start of the address still paid.

There is one more thing the address does, and it is easy to miss because it is a matter of who is speaking. The proposal came from Khadijah. She identified him, sent word, and offered herself. Yet the contract is conducted by Abu Talib on his nephew's behalf, in front of Banu Hashim and the notables of Quraysh, in the form of a public declaration of the young man's worth. The initiative and the formalities run in opposite directions, and the author records both without treating either as a concession.

What the marriage became is stated in unusually strong terms. Khadijah was the best of his wives; he did not marry another woman while she lived, out of reverence for her rank, elevation of her status, and honour of her memory. No woman among his wives could equal her in virtue or surpass her. And all of his children were from her, except Ibrahim, who was from Maria the Copt. The sons she bore him were al-Qasim — the name that gave the Prophet his kunya — then al-Tayyib, then al-Tahir. His daughters were Ruqayyah, Zaynab, Umm Kulthum, and Fatimah.

Her role after the marriage is the last thing this section carries, and it is the beginning of a chain. She went on observing signs of prophethood and marks of the message in her husband, and she recounted them to her cousin Waraqah ibn Nawfal.

Waraqah would hear her gladly and say: "By Allah, he is the awaited Prophet."

He was a poet, and whenever Khadijah told him what she and her husband had witnessed, he would be moved, and he would say: when he is raised up, I will believe in him and follow him. His verse on the matter survives in fragments, and what runs through the surviving lines is waiting. He speaks of long remembrance and longing, of description upon description brought to him from Khadijah, and of how long he has waited. He tells her that her words show him a way through. He weighs what he has found in the sayings of monks who refuse to be led astray, and in the words of a shepherd, against his own hope. He looks ahead: those who greet the man will find welcome and those who fight him will be beaten; he hopes for what the Quraysh once despised; he foresees that Muhammad will prevail and the light of justice appear throughout the land.

And then the author records the one fact that turns all of it into tragedy. Waraqah ibn Nawfal died before the Messenger of God was sent.

He was, on this account, entirely convinced. He said what he would do when the call came. And the call did not come in his lifetime.

## The first to answer

Two households have now been established — Abu Talib's, and the new one Khadijah's marriage created — and the rest of this episode is about a boy who was born in the first and raised in the second.

When Fatima bint Asad was pregnant with Ali ibn Abi Talib, she saw something in a dream. A pillar of iron was wrenched out of the earth; then a light blazed up into the sky until it reached the heavens, came back down, stood for a time, and rose again from her feet.

She asked what it was, and she was answered. This is the destroyer of the people of disbelief, the bearer of the Charter of Victory, strong in his troops — God's aid to His Prophet and His support against His enemy. He is the beloved and the pious, a parable in the exalted heavens. The earth is set by him, and the majestic mountains, the vast seas, and the shining stars.

That is a designation delivered before birth, in the same register as the one Amina received, and it is worth noticing what it names him as: not a prophet, but a support to one. The two announcements are shaped to fit together.

When labour came, Abu Talib told her to circumambulate the Ka'bah, to wipe her hands along its interior, and to make as many supplications as she was able. She did, and she gave birth inside the Ka'bah. When she wanted to leave she gathered the child quickly, took him home, and began to raise him.

Then God placed the love of him in the heart of His Prophet — who at that time was in the fullness of his youth and his splendour — and he said to Fatima bint Asad, addressing her as he always did, "O his mother: place his cradle beside mine." He took charge of much of the child's upbringing until the boy grew strong, his guidance became clear, and he was rightly directed.

The formal arrangement came later and had a custom behind it. Among Arabs of standing, when a man had a son and wanted him properly disciplined and cultivated, he would entrust the child to a respected elder of his people, who would raise him and employ him in his household so that the boy might develop and find his role. Abu Talib had four sons — Talib, Aqeel, Ja'far and Ali, with roughly ten years between each of them — and he distributed them accordingly. Aqeel he gave to al-'Aas, Ja'far to Hamza, and Ali to the Messenger of God. On another account he kept Aqeel himself and told his nephew: take whichever you wish. Ali came to live with him.

So Ali grew up in that household. The author's description of the upbringing is absolute: he never worshipped an idol, never committed a sin, was raised entirely in purity and piety, and kept away from vice and immorality. His character was moulded by the Prophet's character and his conduct followed the Prophet's way of life. And Khadijah, who knew what her husband felt for the boy, honoured him and held him in the highest esteem.

Then the revelation came.

The author states it as a choosing: God chose His Prophet Muhammad — Seal of the Covenant, best of the sons of Adam — and sent him as a Prophet and a Guide. When the revelation descended and Gabriel brought him the message, he called Khadijah to Islam that same day. It was the twenty-seventh of the month of Rajab, and she accepted at that moment, bearing witness to the Oneness of God and affirming his prophethood. That was a Monday.

The next day — Tuesday — he called Ali, told him what God had assigned to him and what He had called him to, and that he had been sent to all people.

Ali's reply is the reply of someone who wants more of it: "By my father and my mother, tell me more every hour." And the answer he got was a caution about secrecy as much as a promise: "I will tell you as you wish; but what I have told you in truth is not yet known to anyone else."

Then comes the moment itself, and the author gives it in Ali's own words with the hesitation left in. "I wished to put this before my father first" — and even as he was saying that, he says, he declared: I bear witness that there is no god but Allah and that you are the Messenger of Allah.

He intended to consult Abu Talib, and the testimony came out of his mouth mid-sentence.

The prophethood came on Monday and the Islam of Ali came on Tuesday, the second day, which makes him one of the forerunners and the very first of the true believers. The author says this is a point of consensus across the entire community, uncontested and unopposed — and then, having said it, he immediately turns to the people who contest it. That is the tell. He knows this is where his book will be argued with.

The objection he names is not that it did not happen. It is that it did not count: his Islam came too early, he had not reached the age of legal accountability, so it should not be reckoned.

His reply is a single move, made twice. If youth disqualifies a person from responsibility before God, then the Qur'an disqualifies two of its own prophets. Of Yahya ibn Zakariyya it says: "O Yahya, hold fast to the Book with strength — and We gave him wisdom as a child" (chapter 19, verse 12). Of the Messiah it says: "He will speak to the people in the cradle" (chapter 3, verse 46). Both of those are in the context of prophethood, which is the highest station there is. So, the author argues, if a child can be given the Book with strength and can speak to people from the cradle, how much more can a child hold Islam and faith — which stand below prophethood and are lesser than that station?

The argument is from the greater to the lesser, and it is hard to answer without saying something about Yahya and Jesus that the objector does not want to say.

The author does not stop at logic. Their attitude, he says, is ingratitude toward the virtue of his guardianship and a denial of God's grace — they have rejected what is plain to the eye and turned away from the proof. Then he adds a point from the other side's own material: it is reported from 'Abdullah ibn 'Umar ibn al-Khattab, one of the people they themselves narrate from, that when a person reaches the age of seven, his faith and his disbelief are recorded.

And then he stops, deliberately. The arguments on this matter are extensive and wide-ranging and go beyond the scope of the book; whoever wishes to investigate will find them in numerous books and compiled refutations. A little is enough, he says, for one who governs himself by reason, is fair to himself, and listens to the word of God — one who is not driven by whims or blinded by hatred of those whom God has chosen.

What follows the argument is the quietest stretch in the episode and the most striking. Ali remained for years praying alongside the Messenger of God. They would go out together and pray in the valleys of Mecca, and they continued that way for eight years. And during all that time, the author says, no one on the face of the earth had entered Islam except the two of them and Khadijah bint Khuwaylid.

A religion of three people, praying in ravines outside the town, for the better part of a decade.

Abu Talib came upon them one day and asked: "What is this you are doing, nephew?" And the answer he got was an invitation, framed by kinship: "O uncle, this is the religion of God with which He has sent me. This is the faith of our forefather Abraham. You, uncle, are the most deserving person before whom I should call aloud and invite to the religion of God, and you are the most deserving of those who should answer and support Him."

Abu Talib's reply splits cleanly in two, and the split is the whole of his position. "As for abandoning what I follow, I find no way to do that. But as for supporting you — by Allah, no one shall harm you with something you hate, as long as I remain."

He declines the first request and gives an unlimited guarantee on the second. Then he turns to his son and settles the boy's future in two sentences: "Follow what Muhammad says to you. Do not leave him and do not forsake him, for he has called you to nothing but the religion of God."

The man who would not convert instructed his son to stay.

A second report puts the number of years at seven rather than eight. It is narrated from Abu Ayyub al-Ansari that he heard the Messenger of God say: Ali ibn Abi Talib and I worshipped God for seven years before anyone else believed in Him. And he attached a verse to it — the one about those who bear the Throne and those around it, who glorify their Lord, believe in Him, and seek forgiveness — reading it as the heavenly counterpart of what was happening on earth. That, he said, was for me, Ali and Khadijah bint Khuwaylid; and then came those who believed afterward.

The third person to pray was recruited by the same uncle who had refused to join. Abu Talib passed by while the Messenger of God and Ali were praying, with his son Ja'far standing beside him. He turned to Ja'far and said: go and join your cousin's wing. Ja'far went, accepted Islam, and prayed with them — and that, the author notes, was the first congregational prayer the Messenger of God led.

Abu Talib built the first row of that prayer out of his own sons while standing outside it himself.

The episode closes with the moment the private religion was first put to the wider family. When God revealed to His Messenger the command to warn his nearest kinsmen (chapter 26, verse 214), he gathered the men of Banu 'Abd al-Muttalib with a haunch of mutton and a cup of milk. They numbered forty, and Ali's account is dry about what forty such men could eat: not one of them could not finish an entire haunch and drink a full skin on his own. They all ate until they were full and drank until they were satisfied.

Then he put the proposition to them. "O sons of 'Abd al-Muttalib, hear me out. Be the kings and rulers of the earth, for Allah does not send a messenger except that He appoints for him a guardian, a minister, an heir, and a brother. So which of you will be my brother, my guardian, my minister, and my heir?"

Not one of them spoke up.

"There was no one left among them except me," Ali said, "and I was the youngest of them on that day — but I stepped forward and offered myself." The matter was presented to him and he answered: yes, I am, O Messenger of God. And the answer came back: yes — you are my brother, my guardian, my minister, and my heir. Then, to the room: listen to him and obey him.

That is the designation this volume has been building toward since its first pages, and the author lets the reaction stand as the last word. When they had gone, Abu Lahab said to them: if your companion had not bewitched you, you would not believe what you saw — that he brought you a sheep's haunch and a cup of milk and filled you all. And they mocked, and said to Abu Talib: your son has been appointed over you today.

They understood exactly what had been done. They just refused it — and they refused it in the form of a joke about seniority.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry forward from here.

The first is that the recognition arrived from outside before it arrived from inside. A monk on the Syrian road who had never spoken to a Meccan caravan came down for one child in one, told the uncle the boy would be a prophet, and warned him to go home because others could read the same signs. Khadijah heard it from a slave who had travelled the same routes; Waraqah ibn Nawfal heard it from Khadijah and said flatly that this was the awaited Prophet, and then died before the call came. None of these people were persuaded by an argument. They were reporting an observation.

The second is the standing that was built in Mecca before any claim was made. Two quarrelling clans accepted the judgment of the next man through the door and were relieved when it was him; his ruling did not divide the honour of setting the Black Stone but abolished the question by making all of Quraysh lift one cloth together. They called him the Trustworthy on the evidence of his conduct. And at his marriage his uncle stood before the notables of the town, traced the family to Abraham and Ishmael and the sanctuary, conceded without embarrassment that his nephew had no money, and closed with a prediction that great affairs were coming.

The third is what happened in the ravines outside Mecca. For seven or eight years the religion consisted of three people — a man, his wife, and the cousin he had raised from the cradle — praying where they could not be seen. The author knows the second of those three is the disputed one, so he stops narrating and argues: if a child could be given the Book with strength and could speak from the cradle, a child can hold faith. And when the call was finally taken to the wider family over a haunch of mutton, forty men sat silent, the youngest in the room stood up, and he was answered in the terms he had accepted — brother, guardian, minister, heir — with an instruction to the rest of them to listen and obey.

They laughed. That laugh is where the next part of the history begins.
