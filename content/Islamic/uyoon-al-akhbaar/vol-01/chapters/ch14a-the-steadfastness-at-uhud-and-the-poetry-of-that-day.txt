## Where this episode picks up

A woman on the losing side of Badr cut the liver out of the Prophet's uncle, chewed it, and found she could not get it down. The verse that came down afterwards was not addressed to her. It was addressed to the men who wanted to do the same thing back.

That is the centre of this episode, and it tells you what kind of episode it is. We pick up in the middle of the afternoon at Uhud, with the fighting still on the ground and the Prophet standing wounded on a rock. There is almost no tactics in this whole hour of the book. What there is instead is bodies: a wound sucked clean by a man's mouth, two metal rings pulled out of a cheek with another man's teeth, a liver, and two swords handed to a young woman at the door of a house to be washed.

Three movements. First the last men standing — who held the ground beside him, what was said over that from above, and the names of the three men who broke his face. Then Hamza: the mutilation, the vow of revenge that the Prophet swore and revelation cancelled, the poems the women of both sides threw at each other over the corpse, and the burial. Then the pursuit north the next morning, the accounting of the dead on both sides, and the poets.

That last third is the largest stretch of verse in this volume so far, and it needs a warning. The poetry of Uhud has come down to us badly damaged: whole lines are unrecoverable, proper names are garbled beyond repair, and in places only an image survives with no sentence around it. Where a poem is broken you will hear it carried at the level of its surviving sense, with the damage named rather than patched.

One thread to hold. Watch how often somebody here is working out a verdict that was announced before the deed. A man is told in Mecca that he will be killed and dies of a wound nobody can find. Another is named as belonging to the Fire before the battle starts, fights better than anyone on the field, and then explains himself out of his reward in a single sentence.

## The last men standing

Ali ibn Abi Talib killed Shaybah ibn Malik, brother of the Banu 'Amir ibn Lu'ayy, in that encounter.

And when Gabriel saw what Ali was doing in defence of the Messenger of God, he said: O Muhammad, this is true loyalty.

The Prophet said: he is of me, O Gabriel, and I am of him.

And Gabriel replied: and I am of you both, O Muhammad.

Thus the angels were called Hashimite.

Sit with the direction that formula runs. The clan name does not descend from the Prophet's house onto the angels as a courtesy; it is claimed by an angel, on his own initiative, as membership. Gabriel does not say Ali is like one of us. He asks to be counted as one of them.

Then a voice was heard calling out from heaven: there is no sword but Dhu al-Fiqar, and there is no young man but Ali.

When Ali held firm, the Muslims came back and rallied around the Messenger of God. Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas also took a stand beneath him.

Then Malik ibn Sinan — the father of Abu Sa'id al-Khudri — came and sucked the blood out of the wound of the Messenger of God, and swallowed it.

And the Messenger of God said: whoever's blood mingles with mine, the Fire shall not touch him.

Notice what that promise is about. A man performs an act of first aid that is also an act of intimacy, and what he is given in return has nothing to do with courage or with the battle. It is about the Fire, and the ground of it is that his blood and the Prophet's are now in the same body.

The two rings of the helmet had been driven into the cheek of the Messenger of God, and Abu 'Ubaydah ibn al-Jarrah drew them out with his teeth. Two of his own teeth fell out in doing it.

By that point the Messenger of God had cast aside his bow, having used it until it was no longer of any use.

Both details are physical bookkeeping and both are precise. A man extracts metal from another man's face with his mouth and pays two teeth for it. A bow is not put down as a decision; it is used until it breaks — and that is the only fact the account gives us about what the Prophet himself did with a weapon that day.

Then Ubayy ibn Khalaf came forward, calling out across the ground: where is Muhammad? I shall not survive if he survives.

Ali ibn Abi Talib said: O Messenger of God, this is Ubayy ibn Khalaf — shall I go to him?

And the Messenger of God said: I will deal with him myself.

Those around him tried to hold him back. He broke free from their hands, snatched a spear from one of them, and went out to meet Ubayy. He struck him such a blow that Ubayy nearly came off his horse, and Ubayy fled.

Now the back-story, which the account supplies immediately afterwards, and which is the whole reason the Prophet refused Ali's offer. The two men had met before, in Mecca. Ubayy had said: Muhammad, if you do not finish me off, I will kill you. And the Messenger of God had replied: I will kill you, God willing.

So when Ubayy rejoined his companions he was writhing in agony, and they could not find anything wrong with him. There is nothing seriously wrong with you, they told him — why this state?

He said: he told me in Mecca that he would kill me. Had he but spat upon me, it would have killed me.

And the enemy of God died of that wound while they were carrying him back toward Mecca.

Read the sequence again in order. Ali offers to take the man and is refused, and this is the only point in the Uhud narrative where the Prophet insists on going out himself. The reason is not that the man was dangerous, but that a sentence had been spoken in Mecca years earlier and had named who would carry it out. The wound was survivable — his own people examined him and said so. What killed him was the sentence, and he is the one who says so out loud.

It is related that on that day the Messenger of God prayed the noon prayer with his companions sitting down, because of the pain of his injuries and his inability to stand. And he ordered those praying behind him to pray sitting as well.

That is a ruling, not an anecdote, delivered without any of the language of ruling. The congregation may not stand above an imam who cannot rise; whatever posture he is reduced to, the rows behind him take it too.

It is also said that the one who broke the facial bones of the Messenger of God was 'Utbah ibn Abi Waqqas, who struck him with a stone and hit him. 'Abd Allah ibn Shihab al-Zuhri stabbed him in the forehead. And Ibn Qumi'ah al-Harithi inflicted the wound on his cheek. He took these blows from the volley the polytheists unleashed against him.

The polytheists were then repelled and driven back — but the Muslims had already retreated. It was a day of trial and severe testing.

Hold those two clauses together, because they are the strangest pair of facts about Uhud and the source sets them side by side without comment. The enemy was pushed off the ground — and the ground was already empty, because the men posted on the high slope had gone down after the plunder and everything came apart behind them. The field was cleared and the day was lost anyway. Notice too that the three men who broke the Prophet's face are named. This history does not deal in an anonymous mob; it writes down which man threw the stone, which one used a blade on the forehead, and which one opened the cheek.

## Hamza, Hind, and the burial

Hamza was killed, and then he was mutilated.

Hind tore out his liver, chewed it, and found that it would not go down.

When the Messenger of God saw what had been done to him he was deeply grieved, and he said: if God grants me victory over them, I will mutilate seventy of them in return.

And God then revealed to him: and if you retaliate, retaliate with the like of that with which you were afflicted; but if you are patient, it is better for those who are patient (chapter 16, verse 126).

Watch what that verse does to the vow. It does not forbid retaliation; it sets a ceiling on it — like for like, and no more — which by itself already cancels seventy for one. Then, having granted the permission, it recommends against using it. The vow was sworn aloud in grief over a mutilated body, answered within the same paragraph, and never carried out.

Then a second correction, aimed the other way. The Muslims were also gladdened by what they had inflicted on the enemy. So God revealed: when a disaster struck you, though you had struck twice as much, you said, how is this? Say, it is from yourselves, for God is over all things competent (chapter 3, verse 165).

And the source glosses that verse itself, in plain terms: meaning, because they had disobeyed the Messenger of God when they left Medina, and then abandoned the archers' posts.

That is this book's own verdict on Uhud, and it is not a soft one. The community is caught taking comfort in the enemy's casualties, and the answer is arithmetic — you struck twice as much as you took — followed by a diagnosis: the cause was internal. And the gloss names the two acts precisely, the decision to fight outside the city and the abandonment of the high ground. Both were narrated in the last episode. Here they come back as the official cause of death.

The Messenger of God then ordered that the martyrs be buried where they had fallen.

And now the women take over the record, on both sides.

Hind said, in verse, about the killing of Hamza and what she did to him: I satisfied myself on Hamza when his liver was drawn from his belly. Then it left me — I could not keep it down — from the piercing grief that had seized hold of me.

She said also: we repaid you for the day of Badr, for war follows war, blow for blow. I could not bear the loss of my father, nor my brother, my uncle, and my firstborn. I have healed myself and fulfilled my vow, and mended my fierce, unyielding heart. A wild gratitude remains in my life until my dearest joins me in the grave.

Two things in that. The atrocity is corroborated by the perpetrator in her own composed verse, including the part that failed — she could not keep it down, and she says so in poetry. And listen to the vocabulary: healed, fulfilled my vow, mended. She is not describing revenge as an outrage but as medicine, and as an obligation discharged. The four dead she is paying for are her father, her brother, her uncle and her eldest son, all from one afternoon at Badr.

Then Hind bint 'Utbah ibn 'Abbad ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib ibn 'Abd al-Manaf replied to her — and the reply comes from a woman of the Prophet's own clan carrying the same first name, which is worth flagging plainly rather than tidying away: the account gives us two women called Hind on opposite sides of the same argument.

Her poem is damaged and what survives runs like this. She was shamed by Badr and by what came before it, and tells the other woman that the depth of that disgrace is immense. She calls a blessing down on the morning after that dawn at the hands of the towering Hashimites in full bloom. In every quarter, she says, a blade was running them through — Hamza the lion, and Ali the hawk. If your lord and your father were treacherous, the outskirts of the sacrifice were shaken by it. And she closes by warning her of the evil of the vow, of a hardened conscience, of a brutal assault behind a veil of concealment.

That last line is the point of the reply: she takes the other woman's proudest word — the vow, fulfilled — and turns it into the charge.

It is related that Hind would have mutilated herself as she mutilated Hamza, had he been taken alive. She was among the women who had held the tambourines.

Hassan ibn Thabit then composed verses of his own about her, and they are the coarsest thing in this volume: the poem opens with an obscenity about her body, curses her and her husband together, throws back at her that she produced a dancing girl at Badr on the day her own father and son were killed there, and taunts the young men of Mecca for covering nobody. The history sets it down without softening it, which is a fair measure of the temperature of that month in Medina.

It is related that the Messenger of God had never stood in any position that filled him with more grief than the moment he saw what they had done to Hamza. He said: I have never stood in a position that has made me angrier than this.

Then Gabriel came down and said: O Muhammad, it is written among the people of the seven heavens that Hamza is the lion of God and the lion of His Messenger.

The Messenger of God then ordered that a cloak be brought, and he prayed over Hamza with seven pronouncements of God's greatness. Then the other martyrs were brought and laid beside Hamza, and he prayed over them — each group laid beside Hamza — until he had prayed over him seventy-two times in all.

That number is not a decree but a by-product of a procedure, and the arithmetic habit of this history is what exposes it. Hamza was made the fixed point of the funeral: the bodies came to him in batches, every batch was prayed over where he lay, and so his own count rose with each group. Nobody legislated seventy-two prayers for Hamza. He got them because he was never moved.

Then the Messenger of God was told: Safiyyah bint 'Abd al-Muttalib has come to look upon her brother Hamza.

He said to al-Zubayr: go to her and turn her back. Do not let her see what has been done to her brother, for she will be greatly distressed.

Al-Zubayr went and said: O my mother, the Messenger of God commands you to return.

And she said: why? I have been told that my brother was mutilated, and that this was in the cause of God. I am content with it, and I shall be patient, God willing.

The Messenger of God said: let her go to him.

So she came, and looked upon him, and prayed over him, and sought forgiveness for him, and then returned.

She is the only person in this episode given an instruction by the Prophet who refuses it and has the refusal granted. And the ground she gives is not grief and not defiance. It is doctrine: the mutilation happened in the cause of God, therefore it is acceptable to her, therefore the protection is unnecessary. The order was issued to spare her a shock she had already absorbed.

The Messenger of God then commanded that Hamza and the rest of the martyrs be buried. He ordered that they be buried where they had fallen, each in the spot where he lay. And he said: I bear witness over these people. Every Muslim who is wounded in the path of God — his wound shall come on the Day of Resurrection as it was: its colour the colour of blood, its scent the scent of musk.

Then he returned to Medina. As he entered the city and passed by the houses of the Ansar, he could hear their women weeping for their dead. Tears came to his own eyes, and he said: but there is no one weeping for Hamza.

The Ansar heard it and ordered their women to go to the mosque and weep for Hamza. They did so, and the Messenger of God came out and wept for Hamza. Then he had the door of the mosque closed and said to them: return home — may God have mercy on you — for you have done what was right. But lamentation is forbidden.

That paragraph gives you a permission and a prohibition in five sentences and does not reconcile them. He notes the absence of mourning for his uncle; the mourning is organised; he joins it and weeps; then he shuts the door and rules the practice out. The women are told in the same breath that they did right and that what they did is not allowed. The distinction being protected is between grief, which he shared, and lamentation as a performance, which he ended — at the exact moment it was being done for his own family, which is the only moment at which ending it costs him something.

When the Messenger of God entered his house, Fatima came out to receive him. He handed her his sword and said: wash the blood from this, O my daughter — it has served me faithfully today.

And Ali gave her his sword, Dhu al-Fiqar, saying to her — and his verse survives better than most in this stretch: I have not been moved by wealth or any price, but by obedience to a merciful and devoted servant. For my life, she declared her love for Ahmad; may he be rewarded from every rebel and hard of heart. My sword alone, like a shooting star, sufficed — I drove on until God broke up their ranks. And even the breath of every clement soul found healing. Fatima, this sword bears no reproach.

The worst day this community had ever had ends at a doorway, with two men handing their weapons to one woman to be cleaned, and both of them saying something over the handover. The Prophet praises the blade for its service. Ali defends his motive before anyone has questioned it — not wealth, not payment, obedience — and then makes a claim about the sword itself: it has nothing to be ashamed of. That is the second time in two episodes that this blade's honour is treated as having a standing of its own.

## The pursuit and the poets

On the following day — Sunday, the sixteenth of Shawwal — the Messenger of God called upon the people to pursue the enemy. He stipulated that only those who had gone out on the first day should march with him, and none who had stayed behind.

That condition deserves a moment. The men invited to chase a victorious army are exactly the men it beat the day before, several of them wounded, and the men who kept out of the first fight are barred from the second. It is a rule about who has earned the right to be at risk.

He advanced with them as far as Hamra' al-Asad, eight miles from Medina. He stayed there Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday, and then went back to the city.

Along the way he was met by the Banu Ibn Abi — a tribe divided between polytheists and Muslims, who acted as his advisors. One of them said that day: O Muhammad, by God, what has befallen your companions has grieved us deeply. And they marched alongside him after the enemy.

Meanwhile, Abu Sufyan and those with him had been unanimous about turning round and coming back. We have struck down their companions, their nobles and their leaders, they said — let us go back and finish them off before they recover.

Then Ma'bad reached them. What news do you bring, Ma'bad, Abu Sufyan asked.

Ma'bad said: Muhammad has gone out with his companions to seek you, in a gathering the likes of which I have never seen. They burn with fury against you, and those who had stayed behind have now joined them.

Woe to you, Abu Sufyan said. What are you saying?

Ma'bad said: by God, I think you should not leave until you see the hooves of the horses.

Abu Sufyan said: by God, we had resolved to fall upon them again and finish off the rest of them.

Ma'bad said: I am warning you. By God, what I witnessed drove me to compose verses about it.

And what did you say, Abu Sufyan asked. Ma'bad's answer was a poem, and it is one of the most damaged pieces in this chapter. What can still be read of it is an earth filled with cloud, a voice he nearly lost, a warning that whoever meets them comes back to a generous lion, a suspicion that the ground itself had tilted, and a direct address: woe to Ibn Harb from meeting you.

Abu Sufyan and those with him turned back toward Mecca.

When word of it reached the Prophet he said: by the One in whose hand my breath rests, I had intended to pursue them. Had they pressed forward, they would have been like yesterday.

So the last act of Uhud was a poem. An army that has just won, and has agreed among itself to come back and finish the job, is turned around by one man's account of the road — and the account was true. The Prophet's own comment supplies the counterfactual: if they had come on, the previous day would have repeated itself with the sides reversed.

On the way, the Messenger of God came upon Mu'awiya ibn al-Mughirah ibn Abi al-'As ibn Umayya ibn 'Abd Shams — who was the grandfather of 'Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan through his father, and of 'A'isha bint Mu'awiya — and said: O Zubayr, strike his neck. And al-Zubayr struck his neck.

It was said that 'Uthman had wanted to grant the man protection, whereupon the Messenger of God ordered two of his companions, Zayd ibn Haritha and 'Ammar ibn Yasir, to strike his neck. And God knows best.

Two things are being done at once there. The genealogy is not decoration — the man executed on the road from Uhud is traced forward to the grandfather of a caliph who would rule sixty years later, which is this history telling you where a dynasty comes from. And the account carries two versions of the killing, one involving a companion's attempt to shield him, then closes with the formula that leaves the matter unsettled.

The Messenger of God returned to Medina. Seventy men had been martyred on that Sunday, four of them from the Muhajirun, and among those was Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib — the Master of the Martyrs.

Twenty-two men were killed among the polytheists, and the roll is kept by killer, not by tribe.

The Messenger of God himself slew 'Amr ibn 'Abdullah ibn Umayra of the Banu Jumah, and Ubayy ibn Khalaf, also of the Banu Jumah.

Ali ibn Abi Talib killed Talha ibn Abi Talha and Abu Sa'id ibn Abi Talha — though it was also said that Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas killed the second of those — both of the Banu 'Abd al-Dar. He killed an Abyssinian slave. He killed Abu Ummah ibn Abi Hudhayfah ibn al-Mughirah of the Banu Makhzum, 'Abdullah ibn Humayd ibn Zuhayr of the Banu Asad, and Abu al-Hakam ibn al-Akhnas of the Banu Zuhra ibn Kilab.

Hamza killed 'Uthman ibn Abi Talha of the Banu 'Abd al-Dar, Artah ibn 'Abd Sharhabil of the same clan, and Saba' ibn 'Abd al-'Uzza, an ally of theirs from Khuza'a.

'Asim ibn Thabit ibn Abi al-Aqlah killed Musafi' and al-Jallas, the sons of Talha. Al-Harith ibn Talha was killed by a man of the Ansar. Kilab ibn Talha was killed by 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf. Al-Qasit ibn Shurayh of the Banu 'Abd al-Dar and Khalid ibn al-A'lam were killed by Abu Dajanah al-Ansari. 'Ubaydah ibn Jabir was killed by 'Abdullah ibn Mas'ud. And three men — Abu Zayd ibn 'Umayr ibn Hashim, Hisham ibn Abi Umayya ibn al-Mughirah, and Shayba ibn Malik — were killed by Qarman, an ally of the Banu al-Najjar among the Ansar.

Look at that ledger before we go on. Seventy dead on one side and twenty-two on the other, which is the shape of a defeat and not of a rout. Nine of the twenty-two are attributed to two men of the Prophet's own house. Four are brothers or sons of one family, the Banu 'Abd al-Dar, who had promised Abu Sufyan the night before to take charge of the standard and not repeat Badr — and kept the promise by dying one after another around it. And three belong to a man the book is about to tell us was already condemned.

Because there was a man among them whose identity was uncertain, and it was said his name was Qarman. Of him the Messenger of God had said: Qarman is one of the people of the Fire.

On that Sunday, Qarman fought the polytheists with extraordinary ferocity, killing eight or seven of them single-handedly. He was struck by many wounds and was carried to the house of the Banu Zafar. Some of the Muslims came to him and said: by God, you have worn yourself out today, O Qarman — rejoice.

He said: what should I rejoice about? I fought only for the sake of my people, and for no other reason.

And when his wounds grew severe, he drew an arrow from his quiver and killed himself with it.

That is the hardest paragraph in the chapter and the book puts it in the middle of the casualty list, where you cannot avoid it. The verdict came first. The performance was, by any visible measure, the best on the field — more kills than anyone else the roll names. And what reconciles the two is supplied by the man himself, unprompted, to well-wishers trying to congratulate him: he was not fighting for what they thought. He fought for his people. Then he finished the job on himself. Nobody in the account argues with the verdict, and nobody needed to.

And then the poets. Ali composed two pieces about that Sunday, and both have come down heavily broken.

Of the first, what survives opens in praise of God the steadfast Lord, whose wisdom no one shares, and who made the disbelievers known by their disbelief. Talha, he says, they left hunched over. There are fires between them and plates of iron. The birds and the eagles kept feeding on the fallen. The believers will mourn their own with what they were promised. There is a reproach to a boaster — if you pronounce proudly, I do not care — and a demand: where are the noses, where is the branch and the number. Mus'ab is named, left behind in haste. Hell's doors are under watch. And it ends on the line the whole poem exists for: they are not like the dead of the unbelievers.

Of the second, what survives is this. He saw the polytheists rebelling against them and resorting to temptation. Hamza is in the upper chambers. A thin blade, a courtyard swept clear, something like an agate in shadow, salt mixed in. They said, we are the greater when we are estranged; if they sell, and take pride against us.

Then Hassan ibn Thabit, commemorating several of the companions who fell on that Sunday. This is the longest poem in the chapter and the worst preserved. What can still be followed is a night of unsleeping worry, an illness inside a muffled thing, a hurt heart, oppression and broken bones, and a cry — O my people, does one kill a man like me? He refuses to be told to stop mourning her. He observes that youth does not last, that the sun of that day was not destroyed by anything, and then runs through a catalogue of hands and houses: my father and Waqid released for me, every hand has a share, every house has a great father, and I have forgotten what melted away from them.

It was said that Hassan composed that poem one night at the hour of the evening prayer, calling his people together, and declaring: I feared that my fate would overtake me before I could tell them. It was said to be the most eloquent of his verse.

That aside is the most human thing in the poetry section. A poet wakes his household at night because he is afraid he will die before the poem is out of him — and what he was in a hurry to hand over was the record.

Hassan also composed an elegy for Hamza, and what remains of it is imagery rather than argument: mourners like bearers of reverence, uncovered faces, tears running as though dyed, women shaking out their hair in open country like the tails of horses, a heart struck where it lives. Then the questions — I mean the two events, the ones we had hoped for; who was our knight and our protector if he sent out the messengers. Then the oath: Hamza, no, I swear I will not forget you. And the losses named as a climate: orphans, guests, widows. And the line about the season: when the time comes round on behalf of war for war, and it is bad.

He wept for Hamza in a second poem too, and that one is better preserved in places. After you, he says, one asks along the downward road — do you know the house, chaste and marked? Between al-Saradeh and the well at Hail I asked her about it, and she was bewildered and did not know what the questioner wanted. Then: weep for Hamza, the one of noble gifts. Radiance at the summit of Hashim — he never passed by without upholding truth over falsehood. In every matter our young men go out, and we used to look upon Hamza as our guardian. He was neither weak nor among the losers, and in Islam this stands as a landmark. Then, directly across the field: do not rejoice, O Hind, and do not be pleased, for the sword waits beneath the barrier. And at the end, the claim that outranks everything else said about the man — he was the minister of the noble knight, and the following day, Gabriel was his minister.

Al-Hajjaj ibn Alaat al-Sulami praised Ali ibn Abi Talib, remembering that he had killed Talha ibn Abi Talha, the standard-bearer of the polytheists. He addresses him as the son of Fatima. Your hands went before him in haste, he says; I left a patch for a shaved forehead; by dragging, when they are dear, I turn my brothers; the intensity of the brave man intensified and left them exposed.

And Ka'b ibn Malik lamented Hamza. His poem opens on fear — that the young men were armed, that love had gone, that he had been asking for the sedition to be refuted and it went too far, that he had threatened the loss of that redness, and that the daughters of the edges kept thundering from it. Then the surviving middle: at Uhud, an enemy managed to penetrate the ranks of the Banu Hashim, and the shame of that endures whenever it is remembered. Then the uncle of the Prophet Muhammad, and those who stood by the Prophet, and the martyr among them. He passed on as a teacher within his family, to die within a branch that gives no shade. Then the claim that answers the whole day: from what we became, in the necklace of its people, Gabriel was under our banner and Muhammad was before us, and Badr cut them down as it turned their faces back. And the accounting: two fates, those He wills to be slain and those expelled — seventy at their threshold, and lions among them.

Notice what the poets are for. They are not decorating the prose; they are fixing its meaning. The narrative says Hamza was killed and mutilated. The poetry says he was the lion of Hashim, our guardian, minister to the noble knight, and that Gabriel took over that office the next day. And Ali's first poem states the doctrinal claim baldly, in the one line of it that came through undamaged: the dead of Uhud are not to be counted as the dead of the unbelievers are counted.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry forward.

The first is that this history gives Uhud an internal cause and prints the verdict in the text. The field was cleared of the enemy and the day was still lost, and when the community took comfort in the enemy's casualties it was answered with arithmetic and a diagnosis: it is from yourselves. Nothing supernatural is offered to explain the defeat. The supernatural in this episode is all on the other side of the ledger — an angel asking to be counted as a Hashimite, a voice from the sky naming a sword and a young man, and the seven heavens keeping a record of who Hamza was.

The second is that the vow of seventy did not survive contact with revelation, and the mourning did not survive the doorway of the mosque. Between the mutilation and the burial, the Prophet swears to mutilate seventy and is told in the same breath that like for like is the limit and patience is better; so he buries his dead where they fell instead, and tells them their wounds will come up on the last day with the colour of blood and the scent of musk. Then he notices that nobody is weeping for his uncle, allows the weeping to be organised, weeps himself — and closes the door and forbids the practice, at the one moment when forbidding it costs him personally.

The third is that the day sorted people by verdicts announced in advance, and the two that landed hardest went in opposite directions. Ubayy ibn Khalaf was told in Mecca that he would be killed, took a survivable wound, and died of it on the road home saying out loud that the sentence was what killed him. Qarman was named as belonging to the Fire before the fighting started, killed more men than anyone else on the roll, and then told the people congratulating him that he had fought for his tribe and nothing else, before finishing himself with his own arrow. Between those two, the men the heavens named were Hamza — the lion of God — and the young man of whom a voice said that there is no sword but Dhu al-Fiqar and no young man but Ali.

And the account does not rest. The pursuit stops at Hamra' al-Asad on a Wednesday, Abu Sufyan turns for Mecca on the word of a poet, and the fourth year opens the way the third one did — with a polite delegation arriving in Medina to ask for something.
