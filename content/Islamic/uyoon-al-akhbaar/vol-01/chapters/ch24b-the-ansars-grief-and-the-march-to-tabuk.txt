## Where this episode picks up

What do you say to men who gave you their city, their houses and their protection when nobody else would, and who have just watched you hand a hundred camels apiece to the men who spent twenty years trying to kill you — and nothing at all to them?

The previous episode ended inside that distribution, with the policy stated aloud: the great gifts were a price paid for men who had to be bought, and the man given nothing was the man who could be trusted without payment. That is a coherent principle, and it is cold comfort if you are the one who receives nothing. This episode opens with the people who received nothing working out what it means, and it is the only place in the volume where the Messenger of God assembles one section of the community alone, behind a fence, and answers them.

Three movements. First that assembly and the address given in it, carried here whole — followed by the smaller sequel, in which a pardoned poet recites the most famous ode of the era in the Prophet's presence and again leaves the Ansar out of it. Then the northern march to Tabuk, the longest expedition of the Prophet's life and the one with no battle in it, which is therefore made almost entirely of incidents on a road: men who wept because there was nothing to carry them, a lost camel, a man walking alone in the desert, a Christian king caught hunting, a rock that gave water, a mosque pulled down, and three men who offered no excuse at all. Then the thread this history cares about most, gathered from three separate moments and set together at the close.

Listen for what a person is given when he is given nothing.

## The Ansar's portion

When the Messenger of God gave generously to the Quraysh and the Arab tribes, and the Ansar received nothing, Hassan ibn Thabit composed verses reproaching it. His poem has come down in the same wrecked state as most of the verse of this campaign, and only its surviving sense is given: that the Ansar gave up their homes and gave victory; that when the Prophet came they answered; that God named them Helpers for their aid; that they had turned back Badr, and were his soldiers on the day of Uhud when they stumbled and all the people stumbled — and then a line of pure grievance, that the people crowd around you for what is not ours. It closes on a question left open: so what did we say, and what did we imagine, and what were we told?

The Ansar felt it keenly when the Messenger of God distributed the spoils among others and left them with nothing, until many of them were saying: The Messenger of God has found his own people.

That sentence is the injury in five words, and it is not an accusation of injustice. Nobody says the division was unlawful. What they say is that he has been reunited with his tribe, and the implication underneath it is that the arrangement they entered into — a city that took in a man his own people had driven out — was a stage he has now passed through. It is a fear about status rather than about property.

So Sa'd ibn 'Ubadah al-Ansari went in to the Messenger of God and said: O Messenger of God, a number of the Ansar have taken it hard that you did not include them in what you divided among your people — the great gifts you bestowed on the tribes of the Arabs — while the Ansar received nothing of it.

The Messenger of God said to him: And where do you stand in all of this, Sa'd?

He said: O Messenger of God, I am only a man of my people.

The question is the sharpest thing in the exchange and it is asked before anything is conceded. Sa'd has come as a reporter of other men's feelings, which is the safe position; the question refuses him it and asks whether he holds the grievance himself. His answer neither denies it nor claims it. He says he is one of them, which is both an admission and a plea of ordinary membership.

The Prophet said: Gather your people for me in this enclosure.

When they had gathered, Sa'd came to the Messenger of God and said: The Ansar have assembled. The Messenger of God came to them, praised God and gave Him His due, then said:

O community of the Ansar, what is this talk that has reached me from you — this grievance you feel toward me? Did I not find you astray and God guided you? Were you not poor and God enriched you? Were you not enemies to one another and God made your hearts one?

They said: Yes — to God and His Messenger belong grace and bounty.

Then he said: Will you not answer me, O community of the Ansar?

They said: And what shall we say in answer to you, O Messenger of God? To God and His Messenger belong grace and credit.

He said: By God, if you wished, you could say — and you would be telling the truth, and you would be believed — You came to us rejected, and we believed in you; you came to us abandoned, and we supported you; you came to us driven out, and we sheltered you; you came to us destitute, and we brought you comfort. We believed in you, we supported you, we struggled alongside you, and we brought you joy.

You have felt it, O community of the Ansar, over a portion of the world by which I bound certain men so that they might submit, while I entrusted you to your Islam. Are you not content that the people should depart with sheep and camels while you return with the Messenger of God? By Him in whose hand Muhammad's soul rests, had it not been for the emigration I would have been a man of the Ansar. If the people took one path and the Ansar took another, I would follow the path of the Ansar.

O God, have mercy on the Ansar, the sons of the Ansar, and the sons of the sons of the Ansar.

The Ansar wept until their beards were wet, and they said: We are content with the Messenger of God as our lot and our fortune. And they parted from him.

There is a manoeuvre in the middle of that address which is easy to miss because it goes by quickly, and it is the reason the whole thing works. He begins with three questions that put the entire account on his side of the ledger: you were astray, poor, at war with each other, and God changed all three. They agree, which is the only honest answer. Then, rather than resting on the agreement he has just extracted, he argues their case for them — better than they had argued it, in four parallel clauses, and with the explicit statement that if they said it they would be telling the truth and would be believed. He has taken the grievance out of their mouths, sharpened it, and spoken it aloud himself.

And only then does he reach the point, which is the same principle the previous episode ended on, now applied to them and named as a compliment rather than an exclusion: the men who were given camels were being bound; you were entrusted to your Islam. It is the sentence spoken about Ja'il ibn Suraqah, enlarged from one man to a whole community. What follows is not an argument at all. It is the statement that if the migration had not happened he would have been one of them, and that if the community ever divided he would walk on their side — and then a prayer that runs down three generations.

The Messenger of God departed from al-Ji'ranah to perform the lesser pilgrimage. He ordered the remainder of the collected spoils to be held at Majannah, in the region of Marr al-Zahran. When he had completed the pilgrimage he set out for Medina, appointing 'Attab ibn Usayd as his deputy over Mecca, and leaving Mu'adh ibn Jabal with him to teach the people the Quran and instruct them in religion. He provided them with the remainder of the collected spoils. His pilgrimage was in Dhu al-Qa'dah, and he arrived in Medina during the nights of Dhu al-Qa'dah. That year the people were led on the pilgrimage by 'Attab ibn Usayd.

The Ansar's portion then produces a sequel that nobody planned, and it comes from an unlikely direction.

Ka'b ibn Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma was brought before the Messenger of God after the departure from Ta'if. The Messenger of God had previously been subjected to his mockery and abuse. After the conquest of Mecca, Ka'b had written to his brother Bujayr ibn Zuhayr — the poet whose verses on Hunayn and Ta'if closed the previous episode — in lines that survive only in fragments, reproaching him for having drunk with the Trustworthy a cup of resolution and gone over to guidance.

His brother Bujayr wrote back urging him to fear God and directing him toward Islam, and telling him: The Messenger of God does not kill those who come to him as Muslims. So Ka'b took heart and presented himself to the Prophet. Bujayr's own reply survives in pieces: that Ka'b will be saved and delivered if he goes to God; that among the people none survives except the pure-hearted Muslim.

He then recited his celebrated poem, whose surviving lines are almost all about the same thing — a man asking to be judged on what he did rather than on what was said about him. Forgiveness is hoped for from the Messenger of God. The Messenger of God came, and I came apologising; the excuse is accepted by the Messenger of God. Do not take me to account for what the whisperer said — why should I be seized for something I did not do? I see and hear things that, if the elephant heard them, would be unbearable were they not from the Messenger of God. Until I placed my right hand on what I disputed. And then the line the poem is remembered for: the Messenger is a light to be illuminated by, a drawn sword from the swords of God.

Ka'b ibn Zuhayr opened it with the line that made it famous — Su'ad appeared, and my heart today is a captive, bound and helpless.

The Ansar were displeased, saying: He praised his brothers among the Emigrants but did not praise us.

It is transmitted from 'Abd al-Malik ibn Hisham that the Messenger of God said, when Ka'b recited that opening: Had he not mentioned the Ansar well, they would have been worthy of it.

Twice now in a matter of weeks, the same community has been left off a list and has said so out loud. The first time the list was of camels, and the answer was an address behind a fence. The second time the list is a poem, and the answer is one line from the man being praised in it, redirecting the praise. Ka'b took the point and composed a second poem for them. It has survived worse than the first, but its subject is unmistakable in what remains: the favoured of the Ansar; those who turn people away from their religions on the day of tumult; those who are fed when they are hungry and who strike for the neighbour's entitlement; those who do not lament death when it descends upon them; the noble ones, the sons of the good.

## The road to Tabuk

The Messenger of God remained in Medina from the month of Dhu al-Hijjah until Rajab. Then came the expedition to Tabuk, and the Messenger of God ordered preparations for a march into the land of Rum.

When the Messenger of God made his determination known, seven men of the Ansar and others came to him: Salim ibn 'Umayr; 'Ulbah ibn Zayd, the brother of Banu Harithah; Abu Layla 'Abd al-Rahman ibn Ka'b, the brother of Banu Mazin ibn al-Najjar; 'Amr ibn al-Hammam ibn al-Jamuh, the brother of Banu Salamah; 'Abd Allah — it is said, 'Abd Allah ibn 'Amr al-Muzani; Harami ibn 'Abd Allah, the brother of Banu Waqif; and 'Irbad ibn Sariyah al-Fazari. They presented themselves in a state of need, and he said: I cannot find anything to carry you on. They turned away, their eyes overflowing with tears of grief that they had nothing to spend. They are those about whom the verse was revealed.

Seven names are given, with their clans, for men who did not go. That is more care than this volume spends on most of the men who did. They came asking to be mounted, were told there were no mounts, and left in tears — and the account fixes their identities as precisely as it fixes the identity of a commander or a martyr, because the campaign is about to be measured by who stayed behind and why, and these are the first entries on that ledger.

The Messenger of God appointed Ali ibn Abi Talib as his deputy in Medina and placed him in charge of affairs there. This sat heavily with the hypocrites, and they said: He was left behind only because he is a burden to him.

That sentence is left standing here, and its answer comes at the close of this episode. What matters in the run of the march is that the slander did its work: it travelled fast enough that the man it concerned rode out of Medina to catch the army.

While the Prophet was travelling on a certain road, his camel went astray, and his companions set out to search for it. Zayd ibn al-Lasit al-Qaynuqa'i, who was a hypocrite, said: Muhammad claims that revelation comes to him from the heavens, yet he does not know where his own camel is.

The Messenger of God then announced: A man from the hypocrites has said such and such. I know nothing except what God has taught me, and God has now shown me where the camel is. It is in this valley, in a place belonging to such and such people, held fast by a tree that has caught its rein.

They set out and found the camel exactly as he had described. The Muslims asked who among the hypocrites had made that remark and were directed to him. He happened to be in the company of 'Ammarah ibn Hazm, one of the Ansar of al-'Aqabah and a veteran of Badr, who brought Ibn al-Lasit forward with his hands bound, saying: O company of Muslims, I present him to you — I sensed his duplicity and could not ignore it.

The taunt and the answer are built on exactly the same claim and that is why the answer lands. The mocker's point is that a man who receives knowledge from above should not mislay livestock. The reply concedes the whole of it — I know nothing except what God has taught me — and then applies the concession to the camel. He does not say he knew where it was. He says he has now been shown.

The Messenger of God continued on his march, and one man after another was left behind. Each time, someone would say: O Messenger of God, so-and-so has fallen behind. He would reply: Call him back. If there is good in him, God will reunite him with you; and if there is not, God has spared you from him.

Then it was said: O Messenger of God, Abu Dharr has fallen behind — his camel has slowed him down. He said: Leave him. If there is good in him, he will catch up with you.

Abu Dharr's camel had indeed slowed, so he unloaded his belongings, carried them on his back, and walked on.

The Messenger of God had stopped at one of his halting places when someone said: O Messenger of God, there is a man walking alone on the road. The Messenger of God said: Let it be Abu Dharr. When the people looked, they said: It is Abu Dharr, O Messenger of God.

The Messenger of God said: May God have mercy on Abu Dharr — he walks alone, dies alone, and will be resurrected alone.

And it came to pass exactly as he foretold. Abu Dharr died alone at al-Rabadhah, after 'Uthman ibn 'Affan had exiled him there. It is also related from the Messenger of God that he said: The sky has not shaded, nor has the earth supported, anyone more truthful than Abu Dharr.

The whole force of that saying is in its tense. The first clause describes what everyone is watching at that moment — a man far back on the road, on foot, under his own baggage. The next two clauses take the same word and run it forward past the end of his life. And this history, writing long afterwards, closes the loop by naming the place and the man who sent him there, so that the reader can see that the sentence was a description of a character before it was a prediction about a death.

When the Messenger of God reached Tabuk, Yuhanna ibn Ru'bah, the ruler of Ayla, came to him. The Messenger of God made peace with him and granted him a covenant of protection. The people of Jarba' and Adhruh also came, paid the tribute, and he wrote them a document of protection.

The Messenger of God sent Khalid ibn al-Walid to take Ukaydir of Dumah captive, telling him: You will find him out hunting wild cows. And so it was. Ukaydir was Ukaydir ibn 'Abd al-Malik, a man of Kindah who ruled as a king and was a Christian. When he was brought before the Messenger of God, he spared his life and settled terms with him on the basis of the tribute.

On this occasion Bajir ibn Bajrah al-Ta'i composed verses about the words you will find him hunting wild cows, of which two lines still read clearly: I saw that God guided all of this — blessed be the one who drives the cow before me; and, who goes astray after Tabuk?

The Messenger of God remained in Tabuk for several nights, then set out with his caravan toward Medina.

That is the whole of what the northern march achieved at its furthest point, and it is worth stating plainly because the expedition is remembered as an army going out against Rum. No Roman force is met. A port ruler comes in and is given protection; two towns come in and pay tribute and receive a written guarantee; a Christian king is taken while hunting and is left alive on terms. Three of the four are settled by document rather than by arms, and the fourth is a capture made possible by knowing a man's hunting habits. An army marched the length of the peninsula in the heat and came back with paperwork.

Along the way there was a small trickle of water seeping from a rock, barely enough for two or three travellers, in a valley called Wadi al-Mashqaq. The Messenger of God said: Whoever reaches that valley before us must not draw any water from it. A group of hypocrites rushed ahead of him and drew from it. When he arrived he found no water and asked: Who preceded us to this water? They named them one by one, and the Messenger of God cursed them.

He then went down, placed his hand beneath the rock, and poured into his hand whatever God caused to flow from that trickle; he wiped it across the rock and called upon God. A witness present described the rock as sounding like thunder from what poured out of it. The people drank and took all the water they needed.

The Messenger of God then said: If you — or those of you who remain — live long enough, you will hear of this valley again: it will be one of the most fertile valleys in the land, and all that lies before it and behind it.

The order that was broken was not a prohibition on drinking; it was a prohibition on drinking first. A seep that fills a cup in an hour is a resource for a whole column only if nobody helps himself on arrival, which is why the instruction was issued in advance and why breaking it emptied the rock for everyone behind. And the answer to it makes the point permanent: not a rebuke, but water in a quantity the valley could not produce, and then a sentence putting the valley's future far past the lifetime of most of the men standing there.

The Messenger of God continued until he halted a short distance from Medina, where he found a mosque that the hypocrites had built as a place to gather. The Messenger of God ordered it demolished, and it was torn down. Concerning it God revealed: And those who took a mosque in opposition and disbelief, and to cause division among the believers, and as an outpost for those who had previously made war on God and His Messenger (chapter 9, verse 107).

Three men stayed behind from the Messenger of God who were not hypocrites: Ka'b ibn Malik, Mararah ibn al-Rabi', and Hilal ibn Umayyah. When they came before the Prophet they said: We have no excuse for our staying behind except to ask God to forgive us, O Messenger of God.

God then revealed: And upon the three who were left behind — until the earth, vast as it is, closed in upon them, and their own souls became too narrow for them, and they were certain that there was no refuge from God except in Him. Then He relented toward them so that they might repent. Indeed, God is the Relenting, the Merciful (chapter 9, verse 118).

Set those two demolitions side by side, because the march is bracketed by them. A building put up by men with excuses is pulled to the ground. Three men with no excuse at all say so in one sentence, and a verse comes down for them that describes the earth closing in and then opens again. The distinction the campaign draws is not between those who went and those who stayed. It is between those who produced a reason and those who did not.

The Messenger of God returned to Medina.

## The position of Aaron

It was asked why he witnessed no direct combat on this campaign, and the answer this history gives runs back to the man who was not on the march at all.

So Ali ibn Abi Talib took his weapons and set out until he reached the Messenger of God at his encampment. He said: O Messenger of God, the hypocrites claim that you appointed me as your deputy only because I am a burden to you.

He replied: They have lied. I left you behind as my deputy. Return, for you are my successor among my family. Are you not content to stand to me as Aaron stood to Moses — except that there is no prophet after me?

So Ali ibn Abi Talib returned to Medina, and the Messenger of God continued on his journey.

The saying is cited in these terms by Ibn Hisham in his biography, and the book makes its reading of it explicit rather than leaving it to be inferred. Ali was not a brother to the Messenger of God by lineage. What the comparison indicates, rather, is that he is the guardian of Muhammad and his successor after him — just as God said in the story of Moses, Succeed me among my people (chapter 7, verse 142). And if the successor of the Messenger of God stood in that relation to his people, then is not one who defies him like the Samaritan who defied Moses and took to the calf?

The comparison itself is not new to this history — it was spoken once before, at the gate of Khaybar, inside a much longer saying and with the same clause fencing it. What is new here is the occasion and the exposition. At Khaybar it was said after a feat of arms, to a man standing in front of the speaker; here it is said to send a man back, and it is attached to an absence rather than to a victory. And this is the one place where the book stops to say what it takes the comparison to mean: not a compliment but an office, evidenced by a verse in which the delegation is spoken aloud — Succeed me among my people — and by what Aaron actually did with it, which was hold a people for a fixed interval. The exception clause names in advance the single thing that is not transferred, which is precisely why everything else in the relation can be left standing.

And then the reading is pushed one step further, into a warning, by naming who the opposing party was in the Moses story: not an enemy army but the community itself, in the interval, around an object of its own manufacture.

The Prophet said: You will assuredly follow the ways of those who came before you, span by span and cubit by cubit — even if they entered the burrow of a sand lizard, you would enter it.

His words are true, and his promise is certain.

Then the answer to the question about the fighting. Some of the narrators among the scholars mentioned that a large force of the people of Rum had gathered, and a fierce battle broke out between them and the Messenger of God. He called out to his guardian Ali ibn Abi Talib, who was in Medina. Ali heard his voice in his heart and hastened to him, witnessed that fighting, and drove the polytheists away from the Messenger of God with his own hand, by God's leave. Then he returned to Medina.

This episode is not in conflict with the established virtue of the Messenger of God, nor with the merit of Ali — on the contrary, the honour belongs to both of them.

The book knows exactly what objection this report invites, and it does not wait to be asked. Those who cite the account of 'Umar calling out to Sariyah, a commander on one of the expeditions, saying O mountain, O mountain, argue that the Muslims were routed, that God cast his words into Sariyah's ears, and that Sariyah took shelter at the mountain while the Muslims fortified themselves there.

Yet the call of 'Umar is not comparable to the call of the Prophet, nor is it as credible as what is transmitted from Ali ibn Abi Talib. God knows best about these matters. The miracles God grants His prophets in the service of their guardians and successors are immense, and His bounty to the servants of their Lord is generous.

That is an argument conducted entirely on the opponent's ground, and it is worth noticing how it is built. The report of a voice crossing a desert is, on its face, the hardest thing in this episode to accept. So the author does not defend it directly. He names a parallel report that his opponents accept — a caliph's shout heard by a general hundreds of miles away — and asks which of the two has the stronger claim: a call made by a prophet, or a call made by a man who is not one. The disputed miracle is not being proved. It is being priced against one already on the other side's books.

The Messenger of God arrived in Medina in the month of Ramadan. That same month a delegation of the people of Ta'if came to him and accepted Islam.

There is the answer to the taunt thrown at al-Lat at the start of the previous episode, and to the prayer made on the road away from the failed siege. The city that shut its gates and killed twelve men beneath its wall sent a delegation, in the month after the northern march, and it came on its own feet.

The Messenger of God said: O people of Ta'if, establish the prayer and pay the alms — otherwise I will send upon you a man who is as I am, and he will put you to the sword. He then took Ali's hand and raised it, at which 'Umar said: Deflect, deflect — as if trying to divert this distinction from him.

Two gestures are recorded in that scene and only one of them is speech. The warning to Ta'if names a successor without naming him: a man who is as I am, who will come with the sword if the two obligations are not kept. Then the hand is taken and raised, which is not an argument and cannot be answered as one. What is answered is the raising itself, by a bystander telling him to turn it aside — and this history records the interruption rather than the reply, because the interruption is the evidence that everyone present understood what the raised hand meant.

The delegation of Thaqif remained with the Prophet until the end of Ramadan. The Messenger of God commanded al-Mughirah ibn Shu'bah and Abu Sufyan ibn Harb to demolish al-Lat, the idol of Thaqif, which was also known as al-Taghiyah. Al-Mughirah demolished it, while Abu Sufyan grieved over it and cried out: Alas — your father's ruin.

The Messenger of God sent the declaration of immunity with Abu Bakr. When Abu Bakr had set out on the road, Gabriel descended upon the Prophet and said: None shall deliver this but a man of your own people. The Messenger of God then dispatched Ali, who caught up with Abu Bakr, took the chapter from him, and proceeded with it. Abu Bakr returned in distress and said: O Messenger of God, has something been revealed against me? He replied: No — only that Gabriel came down to say that none should deliver it but Ali.

Those of the Shi'a who argue from this point have said: How can someone be fit to succeed the Messenger of God when he was not trusted to deliver a chapter that the Messenger of God had already assigned to him?

Ibn Hisham stated that this event took place in the ninth year — namely, the affair of the chapter of Immunity. It has also been said that it occurred before the conquest of Mecca.

It is narrated by Ibn Salam, with a chain going back to Ali, who said: When the Messenger of God set out to conquer Mecca, he wished to reason with them and call them to God just as he had called them at the outset. He wrote them a letter warning them and alerting them to God's punishment, while promising them forgiveness. He included within it the opening of the chapter of Immunity, to be recited before them. He then delegated Abu Bakr to take it to them and direct them to it. Gabriel descended and said: O Muhammad, none shall represent you in this matter except a man from among you. The Messenger of God informed me of this and directed me to go after Abu Bakr. I set out after him, caught him on the road, took the chapter from him, and came to the people of Mecca.

At that time there was not a man among them who had not sworn an oath to exert himself against me with his own person, his family, his wealth, and his children. I conveyed to them the letter of the Messenger of God and recited to them: A declaration of immunity from God and His Messenger to those polytheists with whom you had made a treaty — travel freely through the land for four months (chapter 9, verses 1 to 2). He also declared that no one should circumambulate the House unclothed, and that no polytheist, man or woman, should do so. Every man I met, whether threatening or peaceable, whether showing me hostility or bringing accusations — not one of them gave me any trouble. I carried out what the Messenger of God had commanded me.

Read the last line of that account against the first. He arrives in a city where, by his own count, every man has sworn a personal oath against him, to deliver the one announcement guaranteed to be received worst, alone. And the whole of what he reports about the reception is a negative: not one of them gave me any trouble. It is the flattest possible sentence, and it is the point of the report.

## What this episode lands

The Ansar received nothing at al-Ji'ranah and said the Messenger of God had found his own people. What they got instead was an assembly behind a fence, in which their own case was put back to them better than they had made it, and then the principle from the distribution restated as a compliment: the men given camels were being bound, and you were entrusted to your Islam. It closed with a declaration that but for the emigration he would have been one of them, and a prayer for their grandchildren. Weeks later a pardoned poet praised the Emigrants and left them out again, and the correction came in a single line from the man the poem was about.

The march to Tabuk fought nobody and is therefore made of what happened on the road: seven men who wept because there was nothing to carry them, a mocked camel found under a tree, a man walking alone under his own baggage and told what his death would look like, a Christian king caught hunting, a rock made to give water to a column after the order about it was broken, and a hypocrites' mosque pulled to the ground — set against three men who offered no excuse whatever and had a verse of relenting sent down for them.

And the thread this history follows is gathered from three moments and set side by side: a man riding out to the camp to answer a slander and being told he stands to the Prophet as Aaron stood to Moses; a report of a voice heard across a desert, defended not by proof but by weighing it against a miracle the other side already accepts; and a chapter of the Quran taken back off its first messenger on the road and given to the one man who could deliver it — who carried it into a city where every man had sworn against him, and reported afterwards only that nobody troubled him.
