## Where this episode picks up

What do you do when the man you have been ordered to obey builds a fire and orders you to walk into it?

The previous episode ended on an argument rather than an event: a single administrative detail from one expedition — who stood in front when the men prayed — turned into a claim about who was fit to stand in front of the whole community afterwards. This episode opens on the same problem from the other end. Not who is owed obedience, but how far obedience reaches, and what happens to a commander who tests it.

Then the history moves, and the change is abrupt. After nine years of marches, raids, sieges and dispatched detachments, it arrives at a journey with no enemy in it at all. In the tenth year the Messenger of God announced that he intended to make the pilgrimage, and the announcement itself was the point: he sent word ahead so that the community could come and learn the rites from him while he performed them. What follows in this history is therefore not a campaign narrative. It is a walking curriculum — where you put on the garment, what you say when you put it on, which direction you turn, how many circuits you make, which of them you jog, what you may not hunt or cut or pick up inside the sanctuary — recorded step by step because this was the one time the whole practice was performed by the man who had been given it.

Three movements. First the limits of a command: an officer who ordered his men into a fire, and a group of guests who repaid hospitality with murder. Then the campaigns into Najran and Yemen under the command of Ali ibn Abi Talib, the stream of complaints that came back from them, and the sayings the Messenger of God issued each time a complaint arrived. Then the pilgrimage itself, from the announcement in Medina to the seventh circuit of the House.

Listen for how much of this episode is a man being defended by someone who never once asks whether the complaint is true.

## Obedience and its limits

Abu Sa'id al-Khudri said:

The Messenger of God dispatched 'Alqamah ibn Mahziz at the head of an army, and I was among them. At one point along the road, he gave a group of the army leave to depart. With him was 'Abd Allah ibn Hudhafah al-Sahmi, one of the companions of the Messenger of God. At some point along the way, 'Abd Allah grew angry with those under his command and ordered a fire to be lit. He then said to the men: Am I not owed your hearing and your obedience? They said: Yes. He said: Is there anything I might command you that you would not carry out? They said: Yes. He said: Then I charge you, by your obedience to me, to throw yourselves into this fire.

At that, some of them rose as though to leap into it, but he stopped them. Some of them said: We fled to the Messenger of God precisely to escape the Fire. They held their ground until the man's anger cooled and the fire died out.

When they rejoined the Messenger of God and told him what had happened, he said: Had you entered it, you would never have come out of it until the Day of Judgement. Obedience is due only in what is good.

And Ali said: There is no obedience to a creature in disobedience to the Creator.

It is worth noticing what the men who refused actually said, because it is not what a modern ear expects. They did not argue that the order was disproportionate, or that their commander was in a temper, or that a serving officer has no right to spend his men's lives on a point of pride. They said that fleeing the Fire was the entire reason they were there. The whole of their case is a pun that turns out not to be a pun: the fire in front of them and the Fire they had converted to escape are the same word, and obeying the first would have delivered them to the second. That is why the ruling that comes back is phrased as it is. The Messenger of God does not say that the order was excessive or that the officer exceeded his authority. He says that had they gone in, they would have stayed in until the Day of Judgement — the punishment following the obedience, not the disobedience — and then he states the rule in six words that fit any case anyone could bring: obedience is due only in what is good.

The line placed beside it is a general statement of the same limit, and it is placed there because the history wants the limit stated twice. There is no obedience to a creature in disobedience to the Creator. A command has authority only as far as its own source reaches; past that point the man giving it is a creature like the man receiving it, and the chain of obligation simply stops.

The second account in this stretch tests the other side of the same boundary — not what a subordinate owes a commander, but what a guest owes a host.

It was narrated from Ja'far ibn Muhammad, from his father, from his forefathers, that Ali said:

A people from Banu Dha came to the Messenger of God. The Messenger of God said to them: Stay with me — if I cannot settle your debts, you may dispose of your goods privately. So they settled in the city. He then led them out to the camels of the charitable endowment and ordered them to drink from their milk and to use their urine as a remedy. When they had recovered and grown strong, they killed three men who had been tending the camels, and they drove the camels off and fled with them back toward their homelands.

The Prophet was informed, and he sent me in pursuit of them. I tracked them to the edge of Yemen, where they had entered one of the valleys and found themselves unable to get out of it. I took them and brought them before the Messenger of God. He recited to them this verse: Indeed, the penalty for those who wage war against God and His Messenger and strive to cause corruption in the land is none but that they be killed or crucified (chapter 5, verse 33), to the end of the verse. Then he said: Cut. And so their hands and feet were cut off on opposite sides, as a penalty.

Ibn Hisham adds one clarifying detail: the men who were killed had been left in charge of the camels belonging to the Prophet, and it was those herdsmen the guests murdered before driving the animals away.

Set the two accounts side by side and the shape of the section becomes clear. In the first, a man with lawful authority issues an unlawful order and the men under him are told they would have been destroyed for carrying it out. In the second, men who arrived as debtors in need of relief are given shelter, treated at public expense, restored to health from a charitable herd, and then repay it by killing the men who had tended them. Both accounts are about the point at which a relationship of obligation runs out, and in both the answer arrives as a rule rather than as a judgement of character. Neither the angry officer nor the sick guests are described. We are told what they did and what may be done about it, and that is all.

The detail about the pursuit is small and easy to pass over, but it is the hinge into everything that follows. The Messenger of God did not send an army after the camel-thieves. He sent one man, who tracked them the length of the peninsula to the edge of Yemen and brought them back. It is the first of a run of missions in this stretch of the history where the same commander is sent south, and where the difficulty on his return is never the mission.

## From me, and of me

The Messenger of God sent Ali ibn Abi Talib to Najran. He collected the alms due from the Muslims there, while the Christians and polytheists became flustered; he slew their warriors, and they paid him the tribute submissively and under compulsion. He returned to the Messenger of God bearing their charitable dues, their gifts, and the tribute from the protected subjects among them.

The Messenger of God dispatched Ali to Yemen on two occasions. Ibn Hisham narrates that on one of them Khalid ibn al-Walid was present in a separate contingent, yet Ali ibn Abi Talib was appointed commander over all. Indeed, whenever the Messenger of God sent Ali on any campaign, he invariably placed him in command — Ali was the prince over all the companions.

'Abd Allah ibn Buraydah narrated from his father, Buraydah al-Aslami, who said:

The Messenger of God dispatched us to Yemen under Ali ibn Abi Talib, and sent with him a detachment that included Khalid ibn al-Walid. He said: If you come together under one command, Ali shall be over all; if you divide, each shall command his own men. We met the enemy, fought them, killed their fighters, and took captives. Ali chose for himself a slave girl from among the captives.

Khalid ibn al-Walid then wrote to the Messenger of God reporting what Ali had done, and ordered me to carry the letter. I was among those assigned to him. I brought the letter to the Messenger of God and delivered it. I said: O Messenger of God, you sent me with a man whom you commanded me to obey, and he has now sent me to you to complain against him — and this matter is one you are best placed to judge.

The Messenger of God said to me: O messenger, do not speak against Ali — for Ali is from me and I am from him, and he is your guardian after me.

Read the exchange as the man carrying the letter experienced it. Buraydah is not a partisan on either side; he is a courier who has been handed an awkward errand and says so plainly. He was placed under a commander he was ordered to obey, and that same commander's colleague has now made him the instrument of a complaint against him. He does not endorse the complaint. He hands it over and says, in effect, that this is above his pay. And the answer he receives does not adjudicate the complaint at all. It does not ask what Ali took, or whether a commander may select from the captives before the division, or whether Khalid's account is accurate. It tells the courier not to speak against the man, gives a reason that is a statement of identity rather than of conduct — Ali is from me and I am from him — and then adds a clause that has nothing to do with Yemen or with captives: and he is your guardian after me.

That pattern repeats. It was narrated from Abu Rafi' that he said:

The Messenger of God sent Ali as commander, and when 'Amr ibn Shas returned from the campaign he began to speak ill of Ali and to complain about him. Word of this reached the Prophet, who summoned him. When 'Amr came before him, the Prophet said: Tell me about Ali — did you find any injustice in his judgements, or any unfairness in his conduct? He said: No. The Prophet said: Then why do you speak against him, saying what has reached me?

The Prophet grew angry until his colour changed, and we could see the anger plainly in his face. Then he said: Whoever claims to love me yet hates Ali has hated me, and whoever hates me has hated God. And whoever loves Ali has loved me, and whoever loves me has loved God.

And in another narration, from 'Amr ibn Shas himself:

The Messenger of God said: Whoever harms Ali has wounded my very ear.

He said: That was when I went out with Ali to Yemen. I felt some resentment toward him, and when I returned to the city I began to voice complaints about him to those who would sit with me. One day I entered the mosque and saw the Messenger of God looking at me steadily until I took my seat. When I had settled, he said: By God, O 'Amr ibn Shas, you have hurt me. I said: I seek refuge in God and in Islam from ever hurting the Messenger of God. He said: Indeed — whoever harms Ali has hurt me. I said: By God, I will never harm him.

The two versions of 'Amr's story are worth keeping side by side, because they were told from opposite ends and neither is flattering to the teller. In the first, someone else reports the scene: the summons, the question, the flat admission that there was nothing wrong with the man's judgements or his conduct, and then the anger visible in the Prophet's face. In the second, 'Amr tells it himself, and what he confesses is the thing the first version leaves out — that the grievance was never about a ruling. He felt resentment. He got home and started talking. The complaint had no content, and both narrators say so.

Notice also that the question asked is a narrow one and it is asked first. Did you find any injustice in his judgements, or any unfairness in his conduct? That is the only ground on which a complaint against a commander could stand, and once it is answered in the negative the conversation moves off conduct entirely and onto attachment: love and hatred, harm and hurt, and a chain that runs from the man complained about to the Prophet to God without a break in it. The image in the second narration is the most physical statement of that chain in the volume. Whoever harms Ali has wounded my very ear — not his reputation, not his household, but a part of his own body.

The history then places one more account here, and its function is to answer the complaints with an event rather than a saying.

On one of the campaigns, Khalid ibn al-Walid found himself isolated in a valley of Tihama in which there lived a formidable warrior known as Muhlhal ibn al-Fayyad. Khalid called him to Islam and to bearing witness that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is the Messenger of God. But Ibn al-Fayyad refused outright — the pull of the old order overtook him — and he called out to his people. They came to him in successive waves, gathering around Khalid ibn al-Walid until they surrounded him, took him captive, bound him in restraints, and strung him up with palm fronds. He then wrote to the Commander of the Faithful.

The verses of that letter are badly damaged in this manuscript and are carried here only at the level of surviving sense; no line is reconstructed. What survives is a man describing his own humiliation and asking to be seen in it: a rope tied at his neck along with his sons, his body suspended, a whip brought down into the palm of a hand, a captor amusing himself at his expense, and an oath that the disasters that have overtaken him are enough — followed by a call for the army to come, and to raise him up under a Yemeni banner.

The Commander of the Faithful set out at once, struck down the warriors opposing him, and drove the men from their positions. He reached Khalid, unbound him, and set him free.

The placement is doing the argument. Khalid is the man who wrote the letter of complaint from Yemen; Khalid is the man hanging in palm fronds in a valley of Tihama; and Khalid is the man who writes to Ali because there is no one else to write to. The history does not comment on the sequence. It does not need to.

## The rites of the pilgrimage

The Messenger of God performed the Farewell Pilgrimage in the tenth year, after he had informed the people that he intended to perform the pilgrimage, so that the community would learn the rites and what God had prescribed for them. He departed from Medina in the sacred month of Dhu al-Qi'dah, five nights into the month.

This pilgrimage is called the Farewell because it was the last pilgrimage of the Messenger of God, and it is called the Pilgrimage of Conveyance — al-Balagh — on account of what was revealed to him during it: O Messenger, convey what has been revealed to you from your Lord; if you do not, you will not have conveyed His message (chapter 5, verse 67). The history states plainly that it will hold that hadith back until it reaches the end of the pilgrimage. That withholding is deliberate, and it is the reason this episode stops where it stops.

The Messenger of God travelled with his family. When he had finished at Sarf, he instructed the people to perform the lesser pilgrimage, except for those who had brought a sacrificial animal with them.

From Ja'far ibn Muhammad, from his fathers, they said:

Hajj is superior to 'umrah — that is, the hajj with which the Quran was revealed. The Messenger of God had brought a sacrificial animal. When he reached Mecca, he circumambulated the House and walked between al-Safa and al-Marwah. What was then revealed to him led him to say: Had I known in advance what has now been shown to me, I would not have brought a sacrificial animal and would instead have performed 'umrah. So whoever has no sacrificial animal, let him dissolve his ihram.

The people then dissolved their ihram and converted it to 'umrah, except for those who had a sacrificial animal with them. These were then re-consecrated for hajj on the day of watering at the Grand Mosque.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad said: This is the basis for tamattu' — combining 'umrah with hajj — for those who were not among the people of the sanctuary, as God said: For those who were not residents of the Sacred Mosque (chapter 2, verse 196). For the people of the sanctuary are able to perform 'umrah whenever they wish. But God extended this blessing to those who came from afar, granting them both a hajj and an 'umrah in a single journey — a mercy from God to His creation and a kindness bestowed upon them.

The reasoning there is worth slowing down for, because it explains a concession by explaining who does not need it. A resident of Mecca can walk to the House on any morning of the year; the lesser pilgrimage costs him nothing but the walk. A man who has crossed a desert to get there cannot come back next month. So the dispensation that lets the distant pilgrim take both rites out of one journey is not a relaxation of the law but a correction for distance — and the verse is read as saying exactly that, since it defines the people it applies to by where they do not live.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad said:

When someone who is entering ihram intends to do so, he should make his intention explicit and declare what is forbidden to him, whether it be hajj combined with 'umrah, hajj alone, or 'umrah alone. He says: O God, I intend tamattu' — 'umrah followed by hajj. Or he says: O God, I intend qiran — combining hajj with 'umrah, if he has a sacrificial animal. Or he says: O God, I intend hajj, if he is performing hajj alone. Or he says: O God, I intend 'umrah, if it is 'umrah alone — upon Your Book and the example of Your Prophet. O God, keep me in that state, and if You have decreed something against me by Your power, then let that be what You will for me, and accept it from me, and release me from it.

Then he should pray as he wishes, even if his full intention is to perform what he intends — whether hajj or 'umrah — without his limbs uttering it aloud.

Ali ibn Abi Talib made his way back to the Messenger of God from Najran, and the Messenger of God sent word to him. They met in Mecca. The report traced by Ibn Hisham goes back by way of Yazid ibn Talha ibn Yazid ibn Rukanah, who said:

When Ali came from Yemen to meet the Messenger of God in Mecca, he hurried ahead to greet him and left a man in charge of the soldiers with him. That man then dressed the soldiers, and each man among them was clothed in a garment. When the army came out to meet the people, Ali said to the man in charge: What is this? He replied: I clothed the men so that they might appear presentable before the people. Ali said: Woe to you! Take the clothes off them before you bring them to the Messenger of God. So he took back the garments and returned them to where they had been stored. The army then came forward, and its members voiced their complaint about what had been done to them.

It was narrated from Abu Sa'id al-Khudri that he said: The people complained about Ali, and the Messenger of God rose to address them. I heard him say: O people, do not reproach Ali, for he is among the most rigorous in the way of God — or he said: for the sake of God — to ever be complained against.

That is the third complaint in this episode and the flimsiest of the three: men annoyed that they were made to hand back new clothes before a parade. The point of the objection, from the commander's side, is that the garments were not his to issue — the army was carrying property that had not yet been divided, and a deputy had dressed the men in it to look impressive. The point of the answer is the same as it was for Buraydah and for 'Amr ibn Shas. The complaint is not investigated. The man is defended by what he is, and the defence is stated as a general rule about him: he is too exacting in this matter to be a fit subject of complaint at all.

Ibn Hisham said: Ali had entered upon Fatimah after his arrival from his journey. He found that she had adorned herself and prepared. He asked: What is this, O daughter of the Messenger of God? She said: The Messenger of God has commanded us to dissolve our ihram, and we have done so.

He said: Then the Messenger of God came. When he had finished, he said to Ali: Circumambulate the House and then dissolve your ihram as your companions have done. Ali said: O Messenger of God, I have entered ihram with the same qualification as you. He said: Go back and dissolve it as your companions have done. He said: O Messenger of God, when I entered ihram I said: O God, I enter ihram with what Your Prophet, Your servant and Your Messenger Muhammad has entered ihram with. He said: Do you have a sacrificial animal with you? He said: No. So the Messenger of God shared his sacrificial animal with him.

From al-Sadiq, from his father, from his fathers: The Messenger of God had associated Ali with his sacrificial animal. There were one hundred animals in all, so the Messenger of God slaughtered sixty-three of them and ordered Ali to slaughter the rest.

Al-Sadiq said: The Messenger of God slaughtered his sacrificial animal at Mina and said: This one is for all those who are unable to sacrifice. He then commanded the people to slaughter and offer their sacrifices during their days at Mina.

The exchange over the ihram is a small piece of legal reasoning conducted at speed and it deserves unpacking. Ali is told to do what everyone else has done: circumambulate, dissolve, convert to the lesser pilgrimage. He objects that he cannot, because his consecration was not made in the ordinary form. He had not declared a specific rite at all; he had bound himself to whatever the Prophet had bound himself to, sight unseen, from a thousand miles away. That formula makes his state a copy of another man's state, and it cannot be dissolved independently of it. The question that resolves it is the practical one — do you have an animal — and the resolution is to make the copy literal: the Prophet takes him into his own offering, and the hundred animals become a joint sacrifice, sixty-three cut by one hand and thirty-seven by the other.

It was narrated from Ali that he said: The Messenger of God looked upon the throngs of pilgrims and said: For every sandal they lift, a good deed is recorded for them, and for every sandal they set down, another good deed is written. When they complete their rites, it is said to them: You have built an edifice that will never be torn down. As for what has gone before, it is forgiven — so do well in what lies ahead.

And it was narrated from him that the Messenger of God said: Whoever desires this world or the next, let him make for this House. No servant comes to it asking for the things of this world but that he is given some of them. And no one asks for the Hereafter but that God saves him from his sins. O people, make hajj and 'umrah an obligation upon yourselves, and perform them one after another, for they wash away sins as water washes away grime, and they repel poverty as fire burns away the dross of iron.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad said: The miqat — the prescribed station for entering ihram — is one of five places that the Messenger of God designated: the miqat for the people of Medina is Dhu al-Hulayfa, which is the Mosque of the Tree; for the people of the Levant, it is al-Juhfa; for the people of Yemen, it is Yalamlam; for the people of Ta'if, it is Qarn al-Manazil; and for the people of Najd, it is al-'Aqiq. Al-Sadiq said: These stations serve as the miqat for the people of those regions and for all who approach Mecca from those directions, regardless of where they come from.

That last clause converts a tribal map into a geometric one. The five stations are named after the peoples who would naturally arrive through them, and then the rule is immediately detached from the peoples: a Yemeni approaching from the north uses the northern station, and a Syrian approaching from the south uses the southern one. Where you are from stops mattering at the moment you choose a road.

From al-Sadiq, from his father, from his fathers: When the Messenger of God performed the Farewell Pilgrimage, he departed. Upon reaching the tree, he instructed the people to shave their underarms, trim their pubic hair, and wash. He then stripped off his ordinary clothing and put on the ihram garment — a robe and a wrap, one of which was fastened at the waist and the other draped over his back. Whoever intends to enter ihram should trim his moustache, clip his nails, and remove any cause for concern before entering ihram. If he can do so after the sun has passed its zenith, that is preferable. The miqat stations are prescribed for ihram, and no time — day or night — is forbidden for it.

Al-Sadiq said, and from him from his fathers: When the Messenger of God came within sight of the people of al-Bayda', he said: O God, here I am. Here I am. You have no partner. Here I am. Praise is Yours, and grace is Yours, and the dominion is Yours. You have no partner.

From him, from his fathers: The Messenger of God forbade hunting in Mecca, cutting down its trees, and carrying off its lost property — except to announce it publicly on behalf of its owner.

From Ali: When the Messenger of God entered the Sacred Mosque during the Farewell Pilgrimage, he began at the corner — touching it — and then proceeded with the circumambulation.

Al-Baqir Muhammad ibn Ali said: When the Messenger of God entered the Sacred Mosque, he began by moving to his right with the House to his left, and he completed seven circuits — jogging three of them and walking the remaining four.

From al-Sadiq: The Messenger of God used to touch both pillars — the pillar in which the Black Stone is set, and the Yemeni corner — each time he passed them during the circumambulation.

The last ruling in this stretch is the one where the reasoning is exposed most fully, and it turns on the exact wording of a verse rather than on its plain sense.

Al-Baqir said, regarding the words of God: Indeed, al-Safa and al-Marwah are among the rites of God. So whoever performs hajj to the House or 'umrah, there is no blame on him for walking between them (chapter 2, verse 158). Abu Ja'far said: The walk between them is an obligatory duty, and the words of God are a declaration of that. Had there been a dispensation to abandon the walk between them, He would have said: There is no blame on one who does not walk between them. But when He said: There is no blame on him for walking between them, it becomes clear that people used to regard it as blameworthy to walk between them — and so it was.

The Ansar used to celebrate Manat — a great idol — and they were therefore reluctant to walk between al-Safa and al-Marwah. When Islam came, they asked the Messenger of God about it, and God revealed: Indeed, al-Safa and al-Marwah are among the rites of God. Whoever performs hajj to the House or 'umrah, there is no blame on him for walking between them.

That is a careful argument and it is easy to get backwards. Read casually, no blame on him for walking between them sounds like permission, and permission sounds like the thing is optional. The reading offered here reverses it by asking who a permission is addressed to. You do not tell people they are not blamed for doing something unless they already believe they are blamed for it — and if the verse had been lifting an obligation it would have run the other way, absolving the man who stays away rather than the man who goes. So the wording preserves the memory of the scruple it was sent to remove: an idol at the coast that the Ansar had honoured, a walk between two hills they had therefore avoided, and a verse that removes the embarrassment without ever conceding that the walk was optional.

## What this episode lands

The first half of this episode is three complaints and three answers, and none of the answers is about the complaint. A courier arrives from Yemen carrying a written objection about a captive and is told not to speak against the man, because Ali is from me and I am from him, and he is your guardian after me. A soldier who has been grumbling in the mosque is asked the only question that could have justified the grumbling — was there injustice in his judgements, or unfairness in his conduct — answers no, and hears that hating this man is hating the Prophet and hating the Prophet is hating God. An army annoyed at losing a set of new clothes is told that the man who took the clothes back is too exacting in the way of God to be complained about at all. And then, without a word of comment, the history reports that the officer who wrote the first complaint ended up hanging in palm fronds in a valley of Tihama, and that the man he had complained about came and cut him down.

The second half changes register completely. There is no enemy in it, no dispute, and almost no narrative — only a man performing a set of actions in front of a crowd that has been summoned specifically to watch him do it. The announcement went out ahead of the journey so that people could learn the rites; the record that follows reads like the notes of the people who came to learn. Where you consecrate yourself and what you say when you do. What you take off and what you put on, and what you cut before you put it on. That the intention has to be specific, and that it does not have to be spoken. What may not be hunted, felled or picked up inside the sanctuary. Which shoulder faces the House, how many circuits, which three you jog. Which corners are touched on every pass. And why a walk between two hills that an old idol had made awkward is an obligation and not a concession, on the strength of which way round a single verse is phrased.

Two threads are left deliberately open. The pilgrimage has a second name in this history — the Pilgrimage of Conveyance — and the verse that gives it that name has been quoted already: convey what has been revealed to you from your Lord; if you do not, you will not have conveyed His message. The history quotes it, names it, and then states outright that it is holding the account of it back until the end. And the consecration of Ali ibn Abi Talib, made in a formula that bound him to whatever the Prophet had bound himself to before he knew what that was, has just been resolved by sharing a single offering of a hundred animals between two hands.

Both threads run to the same place. The next episode goes out to Arafah.
