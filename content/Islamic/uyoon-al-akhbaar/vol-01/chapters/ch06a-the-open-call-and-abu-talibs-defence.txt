## Where this episode picks up

What is a new religion worth when the one man keeping it alive says plainly, in public, that he does not follow it?

That is the arrangement this episode describes, and the author is not embarrassed by it. For the whole stretch covered here, the survival of Islam in Mecca rests on an old man who declines to convert, states his reasons for declining, keeps declining when the price of declining rises, and never once wavers on the protection. Three separate delegations of Quraysh leaders come to him to break that arrangement. They try complaint. They try the threat of war. They try offering him a replacement son. He turns all three away, and the author's judgment on him — delivered as an argument, not as an aside — is that the refusal to convert was itself the service.

The previous episode ended with a room of forty men laughing at a designation they had just watched happen, and one of them turning the whole thing into a joke about a nephew now outranking his elders. This episode begins when the private religion of that room stops being private.

Three movements. The delegations to Abu Talib, and what he was actually doing when he refused them. Then the season when the leaders of Mecca sat down together to agree on a single word for what Muhammad was — and threw out every word their language gave them. Then a sermon of the Commander of the Faithful, recited here whole, in which a man standing at the Prophet's right hand describes what he saw a tree do, and what the people who watched it did with what they saw.

Watch for one pattern in particular. Every group in this episode is offered evidence, and every group has to decide what to do with it. The uncle who refuses the religion says openly that the religion is the best of religions. The chief who rejects every accusation against the Qur'an ends by inventing one. The men who watch a tree walk call it sorcery before it has finished moving. Nobody in this episode fails to see. They fail at what comes after seeing.

## The three delegations

The instruction that opens this chapter is a single verse, and it is worth noticing what it does not say. "So proclaim what you have been commanded and turn away from the polytheists" (chapter 15, verse 94). It does not say argue, and it does not say persuade. It says say it out loud and then let it stand.

So he came forward and invited the Quraysh and the people of Mecca to Islam. He told them what he had been sent with. He warned them of God's punishment. And he spoke against their idols and against what they attributed to them in place of God — which is the part that turned a family curiosity into a civic emergency. His uncle stood up to defend him against those who moved to deny and obstruct him, and forbade the Quraysh from harming him. But the mission grew mightier the more it was opposed, because what it challenged was not a policy but the things the town held dearest.

The leaders who took the lead in the opposition are named. They are worth hearing as a list, because most of them will still be standing on the other side twenty years later. Abu Sufyan, who is Sakhr ibn Harb ibn Umayyah ibn Abd Shams ibn Abd Manaf, and his kinsmen from Banu Umayyah. Utbah and Shayba, the sons of Rabi'a ibn Abd Shams. Abu al-Bakhtari, who is al-'Aas ibn Hisham. Al-Walid ibn al-Mughira. Al-Aswad ibn Abd al-Muttalib. Al-'Aas ibn Wa'il. Al-Hakam ibn Abi al-'Aas. And Abu Jahl ibn Hisham. The author describes them the way you would describe a standing committee: men who stood up and sat down together in opposition and deliberation.

They deliberated, and concluded that the correct address for their grievance was not the Prophet but his guardian.

The first delegation is almost polite. "O Abu Talib, your nephew Muhammad has insulted our forefathers, denounced our gods, declared our fathers to have been misguided, and you have shielded him from us. Either you restrain him, or leave us to deal with him." Three charges and an ultimatum, and the ultimatum is really a request for permission. They are not asking Abu Talib to agree with them. They are asking him to step aside.

He replied to them gently and with dignity, and they departed. The author gives no content for the reply, which is itself the point: there was nothing to answer, and Abu Talib declined to open a negotiation.

Then the situation changed under them. The Messenger of God went on doing exactly what he had been doing — calling to God, conveying what he had been sent with — and many of the Quraysh accepted Islam. That is what brought the second delegation, and the author is precise about who felt it: it weighed heavily on Banu Umayyah and on those among the Quraysh who followed them. Conversions inside their own town were a loss of standing, and they came back with a different tone.

"O Abu Talib, we have come to you before and thanked you for what you granted us, and you hold honor and standing among us. We have endured what your nephew has brought upon us — the cursing of our gods, the disparagement of our fathers, the dismissal of our dreams as foolishness — and now a group of Quraysh have gone over to him. Either you stop him from us, or we shall make war on him and on you and those who stand with him, until one of the two sides is destroyed."

That is not an ultimatum about a nephew. It is a declaration that they are prepared to burn down the clan structure of Mecca to end this, and they say so in the language of extermination: until one of the two sides is destroyed.

Abu Talib answered them fairly and in measured terms and said: "I am considering what is best in this, God willing." Then he called his nephew privately and told him what his people had resolved — that they had decided it was easier to strike first than to wait — and made the only request he ever made. "O nephew, your people have come to me with what you have heard. Spare me and spare yourself, and do not burden me with more than I can bear."

Read that sentence carefully, because everything hangs on how it was heard. It is not a withdrawal of protection. It is a man telling his nephew what the actual weight is.

But it was heard as wavering, and the answer it produced is the most famous sentence in this part of the book. "O uncle, if you wish, do not be overwhelmed. But I, by God — even if they placed the sun on my right hand and the moon on my left — I would not abandon this matter until God caused it to prevail or I perished in its pursuit."

Notice the structure of the offer. The first clause releases the uncle. Whatever you decide, do not be crushed by this. Only then comes the refusal, and the refusal is not conditional on the protection continuing. The two men are each trying to take the weight off the other, and only one of them is being asked to die for it.

At that, Abu Talib stood firm, called him back, and closed the question permanently: "O son of my brother, go and say what you love and do whatever you will — for by God, I will never abandon you for anything."

He then put it into verse, and the verse is the first place where his own position becomes legible. He swears they will not reach the boy while he himself lies buried in the dirt, even if they gather every force they have. He tells him to preach, and not to be diminished by it, and to work at his affair without anger. And then he says the thing that a man protecting a nephew out of family loyalty would have no reason to say: you believed, and you were truthful, and you called, claiming to be a sincere adviser. Then he goes further. The best of the religions of the wilderness is our religion — and yet a religion has been offered that must inevitably come. And then the line that sits under everything else: were it not for blame, and my fear of reproach, I would have found myself free to follow it.

The author does not let that pass as poetry. This too, he says, is testimony from Abu Talib to the truthfulness of the Messenger of God, and that his religion is the finest of religions — and what he endured in suppressing his own acceptance of that religion is the greatest proof of his conviction.

That is an argument, and it is the doctrinal spine of this whole section. The claim is not that Abu Talib secretly believed and we should be charitable about it. The claim is that the suppression was the costly act, and that a man who conceals a conviction he is paying to defend has demonstrated the conviction more strongly than a man who announces one.

The third delegation tests it in the most direct way available. When the Quraysh learned that he would not abandon his nephew and would not surrender him, they came back with an offer. "O Abu Talib, this is 'Amarah ibn al-Walid — the finest and most vigorous young man among the Quraysh. We offer him to you: take him as your son, for he is yours. Hand over to us your nephew, who has opposed your religion and the religion of your fathers, sown division among your people, and made a mockery of their cherished beliefs — for he is but a man like any man."

It is a swap, calibrated for a man of exactly Abu Talib's circumstances: an aging chief, rich in honor and poor in everything else, offered the best young man in Mecca as a son. His answer takes the transaction apart in one sentence. "By God, what a terrible bargain you offer me — that you give me your son to clothe and feed, while I hand over my son to be killed! That, by God, will never happen."

He does not argue about religion. He prices the trade, and names what each side is actually receiving.

Then al-Mut'im ibn 'Adi ibn Nawfal ibn 'Abd Manaf — a kinsman, not an enemy — put the establishment's case: "Your people have been fair to you, O Abu Talib, and have gone to great lengths to spare you what you dislike. Yet I see you unwilling to accept anything from them." And the reply is the moment the alliance breaks. "By God, they have not been fair to me — rather you have all abandoned me and united against me. Do then whatever seems right to you."

The "you" there is plural, and it includes al-Mut'im. Abu Talib has just told his own extended kin that they are on the other side.

The poetry that follows is his answer to that abandonment, and it reaches us badly damaged — legible in outline, not line by line. He tells 'Amr and al-Walid and al-Mut'im not to imagine he would ever seek their protection. He accuses Banu 'Abd Shams of casting out their own — of throwing them off the way a fire rejects its own embers — and of bringing men of uncertain lineage into a matter that was not theirs. He names Taym and Makhzum and Zuhrah as cisterns he never filled. He says their reason and their forbearance have gone quiet. And he closes by looking at the kin who share his own mother and father and finding them the most comfortably settled people alive.

Then he turned to the clan that had not abandoned him. The hearts of Banu Hashim and Banu 'Abd al-Muttalib had turned fully to supporting the Messenger of God, with none of them holding back except Abu Lahab — a name to keep, because he will come back at the end of the next episode. Banu al-Muttalib and Banu Hashim, as the author has already established, had been effectively one house since ancient times. Abu Talib's verse to them asks how long Hashim must stand in fear and isolation while the rest of the people stand as a single party, and tells them to protect their own multitude, the finest inheritance from the finest.

And when he saw the two clans close around him, his confidence returned and he went public. The declaration to Banu 'Abd Shams and the bellies of Quraysh standing with them says outright that we have protected the Prophet, the messenger of the King; that he has thrown himself between them and the man; and that he has not abandoned the Prophet at any point, least of all when things grew hot. One line in it is worth hearing for what it names as his assets: 'Ali and Ja'far are my trust. He is telling the opposition that the two sons he has already given away are the security on his pledge.

A last poem in this sequence praises his own people rather than threatening the others — recalling their ancestors, placing Hashim's honor and antiquity at the center of Quraysh's pride, and setting his nephew's standing inside that inheritance. The author says why it was composed: so that some among them might strengthen their resolve and commit unanimously. It is a poem written to hold a coalition together, not to win an argument.

And then the author steps out of the narrative to state the doctrine directly, because he knows what people say about Abu Talib.

His statement, the author writes, is like that of the believer of Pharaoh's household, who concealed his faith. And the evidence is not a claim about the inside of a man's heart; it is a list of acts. What he said. What he did. His struggle on behalf of the Messenger of God. His protection of the Prophet's family. His shielding of him from his enemies. His striving without respite. His testimony to the prophethood and the truthfulness. All of it, the author says, because of the signs that had become clear to him and the virtues he had come to know.

Then comes the argument from consequence, and it is the sharpest thing in the section. Had Abu Talib openly declared his Islam, he would have found no supporter among his people. He would have become merely one more man who accepted Islam while his people rejected it — one protected person instead of the protector. But God supported His Messenger through him and established His religion by means of him.

That is a claim about what the position was for. The concealment was not weakness of faith; it was the only configuration in which the faith survived. A convert has no standing to forbid the Quraysh anything. A chief of Hashim who is known not to have converted has all of it.

And the author closes the section by showing the effect running outward. When the religion began to appear openly, Abu Talib's poetry spread among the Arabs. The rise of Banu Hashim through him checked the Arabs who had been drawn away from the Messenger of God; some submitted, most denied. The people of Yathrib remembered what the Jews among them had been saying since before the Prophet appeared — that a messenger had been sent — and their eyes turned toward him. And when the polytheists of Quraysh tried to rouse the tribes who had promised them help, what stopped those tribes was the sanctity of the sanctuary, which Abu Talib's poetry had put back in front of them. What was in their hands was let go, because their plot found no justification and their plan could not be carried out.

The uncle's weapon, in other words, was not the sword. It was a reminder, delivered in verse, of what Mecca was supposed to be.

## The word for it

The next attempt is a media strategy, and it is run by a man who is honest to the point of self-destruction.

Al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah — one of the leaders of Quraysh, already named among the standing opposition — gathered a group around him as the pilgrimage season approached. His reasoning is entirely practical. "O assembly of Quraysh, the season has come and the Arab delegations will arrive before you. They have heard of this matter concerning your companion, so agree on one position — do not differ, lest you give the lie to one another and your words contradict each other."

He is not asking what Muhammad is. He is asking what they should all say he is, which is a different question, and he knows the difference. Then he does something a committee chairman almost never does: he refuses to speak first. "No — you speak first, and I will listen to what you say."

What follows is a four-round elimination, and each round is decided by expertise rather than by preference.

They said: we shall say he is a soothsayer. He said: by God, he is no soothsayer — we have seen soothsayers, and what he says bears no resemblance to their murmuring or their manner.

They said: we shall say he is mad. He said: we have seen madness and we know it well; what he does has nothing of madness's interference or its whispering.

They said: we shall say he is a poet. He said: we have known all manner of poetry — its metres, its forms, its recitation — and what he brings is not poetry.

They said: we shall say he is a sorcerer. He said: we have seen sorcerers and their sorcery; what he does has nothing of their breath or their knotted cords.

Four categories, and every one of them is thrown out by the same argument: we are experienced people, we know what that thing looks like, and this is not it. Al-Walid is not defending the Qur'an. He is defending Quraysh's reputation for competence. If they stand up in front of visiting Arabs and call it poetry, the visitors will hear it and know they are lying.

So they asked him directly: then what shall we say, O Abu 'Abd Shams? And here is his answer, in full, with the concession at the front of it.

"By God, his speech is sweet — and none of what you have said fits it. The closest thing you can say is this: he is a sorcerer who has come with a magic that divides a man from his father, from his wife, and from his brother and his clan."

Every part of that is worth taking slowly. He begins by admitting the quality of the thing he is about to smear. He states plainly that all four available accusations are false. And then he selects the least false of the four and repairs it by attaching an observable effect: this splits families. That part was true, and everyone listening had seen it happen. So the charge would survive contact with the visiting delegations in a way that "poet" would not.

It is the invention of a talking point, and it is done in front of witnesses by a man who has just finished explaining why it is not true. They dispersed in agreement on it and stationed themselves along the roads to warn people about him.

The Qur'an answered him by name — not with a rebuttal of the charge, but with a description of the process that produced it. Concerning al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah it was revealed: "Leave Me with the one I created alone, and gave wealth extended, and sons present beside him, and smoothed his way for him — smoothly. Then he desires that I should add more. No: he has been stubborn toward Our signs. I shall burden him with a mounting ascent. He reflected, and he calculated. So may he perish — how he calculated! Then may he perish — how he calculated! Then he looked. Then he frowned and scowled. Then he turned away and grew arrogant, and said: this is nothing but sorcery handed down; this is nothing but the speech of a mortal. I shall roast him in Saqar. And what will make you know what Saqar is? It leaves nothing and it spares nothing" (chapter 74, verses 11 through 29).

The verses do not say he was stupid or that he lacked evidence. They walk through the deliberation step by step — he reflected, he calculated, he looked, he frowned, he turned away — and the verdict falls on the calculation itself. What is condemned is exactly the meeting we have just read: a man weighing which accusation would hold up in public.

Abu Talib's response to the roadside campaign was to raise the stakes of the location. Fearing what the Arab delegations would be provoked into, he composed the long poem that made him famous — the one in which he takes refuge in the sanctity of Mecca and in his own standing inside it, appeals to the nobles of his people, and states again, as in his other poems, that he was not a Muslim in the sense of formally following the Messenger of God, and that he would nonetheless never abandon him for anything, even unto death.

The poem survives in poor condition and must be read for its shape. What is legible is a sustained inventory of the sacred: the House in the belly of Mecca, the sacrifices and the rites, the Black Stone and the wiping of it, the standing barefoot without sandals, the footprints of Abraham in the open ground, the running between the tracks at Safa, the pilgrims from every road, the night of the gathering and the houses at Mina. Against that inventory he sets the conduct of his opponents: they have cut every bond and every means, they bite their fingers in rage behind his back, they have made an alliance against their own. And twice he stops the catalogue to swear at them directly — you lie, by the House of God, we do not abandon Mecca; you lie, by the House of God, at the disgracing of Muhammad. Later the private line surfaces again: by my life, I have been committed to Ahmad. And near the close, plainly: I stood with him and protected him.

That is a man arguing that the sanctuary itself forbids what Quraysh is planning — and doing it in the one medium the Arabs would carry home and repeat.

He was not the only poet to try it. Abu Qays ibn al-Aslat was connected to the Quraysh by marriage; he had a wife from among them, would bring her to Mecca and stay among her people for stretches at a time, and was on close terms with them. He sent them a poem urging them to stop the war, exhorting them to follow the Messenger of God, and reminding them how God had turned away the army of the elephant and its cunning stratagem. That is the event this volume has already told at length, and it is used here as an argument rather than retold: the House defended itself once within living memory, and you are the people who watched it happen.

His verse is as damaged as the rest, but its drift holds. God rules by His wisdom; war severs a soul and destroys a nation; do they understand what war actually is. He calls them the navel of the valley, a polished lineage without flaw, keepers of ancient dignities — and then tells them to arise, single out their Lord, and remember the day of the elephant's master. Establish for us a true religion, he says, for you are the trustworthy.

The campaign failed at the level it was designed for. When the Quraysh were discouraged from rallying the Arabs to their side, they gave up on organized opposition and fell back on the only thing left to them — harming him with their tongues, while his uncle went on standing in front of him. And the Messenger of God went on calling them to God and reciting to them the verses of the Book as they came down, while they persisted in their denial and stubbornly rejected the signs they were shown.

Which raises the question the next section is about. What signs?

## The tree and the sealed hearts

The answer comes from a witness who was standing at his right hand, and it is preserved as a sermon of the Commander of the Faithful. It is recited here whole, in his own words, before anything is said about it.

"I was with him when the Quraysh came to him. They said: 'O Muhammad, you have made a great claim that neither your fathers nor any of your kinsmen ever made. We will put a matter to you, and if you show it to us and answer it, we will know that you are a prophet and messenger. If you cannot, we will know that you are a lying sorcerer.'

He said to them: 'What do you ask?'

They said: 'Call this tree to come to you, so that it uproots itself and stands before you.'

He said: 'God is Able over all things. If God does this for you, will you believe and bear witness to the truth?'

They said: Yes.

He said: 'I will show you what you ask, and I know full well that you will not turn to what is good — for among you are those in whose hearts it has been sealed, and those who are partisan.'

Then he said: 'O tree, if you believe in God and the Last Day, and you know that I am the Messenger of God, then uproot yourself and stand before me.'

By Him who sent him with the truth, it uprooted itself with a great rushing sound, thundering like the beating wings of a bird, until it stood in the hands of the Messenger of God, and flung its upper branch toward him, while one of its branches grazed my forehead — for I was standing at his right hand.

When the people saw this, they said: 'Arrogance and sorcery!'

They asked that half of it return. He commanded it, and half of it came back as though it had never moved. Then it drew closer still until it nearly encircled the Messenger of God, and they said: command it to return the other half as well. So he commanded it, and it returned.

I said: 'There is no god but God. I am the first to believe in you, O Messenger of God, and the first to acknowledge that the tree did what it did by the command of God, exalted be He, as a testament to your prophethood and in reverence for your word.'

Yet all the people said: 'He is a lying sorcerer — and sorcery is well-suited to his kind. Is there no one who will believe you except someone like this?' — meaning me.

And I am among those people whom you do not blame regarding God, especially the righteous — those whose words are the words of the righteous, who spend their nights in worship and their days in journey, holding fast to the rope of the Qur'an. They follow the way of God and the way of His Messenger; they are neither arrogant nor conceited nor haughty. Their hearts are tender and their bodies are at work."

That is the saying of the Commander of the Faithful, and there are three things in it that the narration itself insists on.

The first is that the sign was requested with a contract attached, and the contract was witnessed. They named the test. They set the two possible verdicts — prophet, or lying sorcerer. They were asked directly whether they would believe if it happened. They said yes. Nobody was ambushed.

The second is the warning delivered before the sign, which is the strangest part of the whole account. He tells them he will do it, and in the same breath tells them it will not work: I know full well that you will not turn to what is good. He even says why — among you are those in whose hearts it has been sealed, and those who are partisan. Two different failures. One is a condition of the heart; the other is simply belonging to a side. And the sign is performed anyway, with the outcome announced in advance.

The third is what the crowd actually did, and the sequence is worth following because it is not a single refusal. First they call it arrogance and sorcery — while it is still moving. Then they ask for half of it to be sent back, and it goes. Then, when the tree comes closer and nearly encircles him, they ask for the other half, and it goes. They are running the experiment twice more, on their own terms, after they have already declared the verdict. And when it is over and one man has testified, they do not answer the testimony. They attack the witness: is there nobody to believe you except someone like this?

The narration closes by answering that sneer, and it does not answer it with rank. It answers it with a description of the people the speaker belongs to — those who spend their nights in worship and their days in travel, who hold to the rope of the Qur'an, who are not arrogant or conceited or haughty, whose hearts are tender and whose bodies are at work. The reply to "only someone like him would believe you" is a portrait of what someone like him is.

And the pattern held after the tree. The author says the Messenger of God showed them mighty signs — among them the splitting of the moon, and other things beyond the power of all humankind — and they only grew more arrogant, and their denial and disbelief only deepened.

Then he widens the frame past Mecca. The Messenger of God reached out to the people of the book across the Arab lands — Jews and Christians, their scholars, their monks, their rabbis — and they came to him to hear his arguments. Some recognized in him the evidence of prophethood from what they found in their own books, and submitted. But most of them, when they went back, spoke of him only under the shadow of envy and enmity, and that is what kept them from acknowledging what they had recognized.

That is the same failure as the tree, in a different profession. The recognition happens. The acknowledgment does not.

Abu Talib's warning to the Quraysh at this stage takes the register of the Qur'an's own arguments about vanished nations. Wake up, Banu Ghalib, and be forewarned, he tells them — outstanding men from your own house will stand against you, and otherwise I fear what may come. He puts their ancestors in front of them as evidence: Thamud and 'Ad, and what remains of them. He recalls the she-camel given water and hamstrung the next morning, and the blow out of a clear sky, and the wrath that descended. Wonders laid bare in the desert, he says, for those who are patient and truthful and God-fearing. And then he names what awes him more than any of it: the hand of a man who has risen through hardship in spite of this foolish opposition.

The chief who would not convert is warning Mecca with the stories of destroyed nations, and telling them that what frightens him is not the punishment but the person.

The episode's last scene is what happened when one of them stopped talking and picked something up. Once the Messenger of God had made his command firm and was openly worshipping his Lord, he would come to the House and pray there. Abu Jahl ibn Hisham al-Makhzumi came upon him one day mid-prayer, stood, and took up a stone — filling both hands with it. He advanced while the Prophet was prostrating, meaning to bring it down on his head. The disbelievers of Quraysh had incited him to it, and they sat and watched to see whether he would do it.

He got close, and stopped. His face drained of color and he threw the stone out of his hands. The people came at him: what is the matter with you, Abu al-Hakam?

"When I drew near him, a great stallion camel loomed before me, opening its mouth — and I fled from it."

And the men who had sent him drew the correct conclusion, in the plainest words anyone in this episode manages: by God, Muhammad has been safeguarded against us, as no man among a people before him was ever safeguarded.

They understood the situation exactly. It changed nothing about what they did next.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry into what follows.

The first is the shape of Abu Talib's position, now fully stated. He refuses the religion in public, testifies to its truth in verse, and pays for the protection out of his own standing — and the author argues, as doctrine rather than as sympathy, that this was the only arrangement in which the religion survived. A convert can be persecuted like any other convert. A chief of Hashim who has not converted can forbid the whole of Quraysh. Three delegations came to break that position — with a complaint, with a threat of war until one side was destroyed, and with the finest young man in Mecca offered as a replacement son — and each of them left with less than it came with, until the last one ended with Abu Talib telling his own kinsmen they had abandoned him and to do whatever they liked.

The second is what happened when the leaders of Mecca tried to name what they were facing. They convened before the pilgrimage season to agree on one story, and their own chairman knocked down every category they proposed — soothsayer, madman, poet, sorcerer — on the grounds that they were experienced men who knew what each of those things looked like. Then he assembled a charge out of the one fact he could not deny: that this was splitting families. The Qur'an answered him by describing the deliberation itself, step by step, and letting the calculation be the crime.

The third is the pattern under all of it. A tree was called and it came, and the people who had set the terms called it sorcery before it stopped moving, then asked for it to be sent back in halves, then answered the one man who testified by insulting him. Scholars of the older scriptures recognized him from their own books and went home and said nothing, held back by envy. A man came to the sanctuary with a stone in both hands, saw something, and dropped it — and the men who had sent him said out loud that this man was protected as no man before him had been.

Every one of them was given the evidence. The next episode is about what happens when the people being crushed stop waiting for Mecca to accept it, and take the message to a king who will.
