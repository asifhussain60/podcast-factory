## Where this episode picks up

How does a document that concedes nearly everything come to be called an opening?

This episode ends with fourteen hundred men turning around within a day's march of Mecca, having been refused entry, having agreed in writing to hand back anyone who escapes to them while getting nobody back in return, and having watched a young man in shackles dragged away from the negotiating table by his own father. On the road home, the chapter of the Opening is revealed about it. Nothing in the terms explains that. Everything that happens after them does.

Three movements. First a campaign against a tribe gathering for war at a watering place near the coast, and the captive woman whose settlement of her own emancipation contract frees a hundred households of her people in an afternoon. Then the march home, which is a run of speech and nothing else — a shout at the water that nearly splits the community in two, a boast about who will drive out whom, a messenger's false dispatch that nearly launches an expedition against people who had ridden out to welcome him, and a comparison drawn at the door of the mosque that the hypocrites turn into a sneer. Then the journey to the Sacred House that becomes the truce of al-Hudaybiyah.

Listen for what keeps being tested here, which is not courage but the reliability of a word. A shout, a rumour, a false report, a written clause, a pledge under a tree. In three of those cases a verse of the Quran comes down about it, and in one case a treaty is honoured against the visible interest of everybody standing there.

## Banu al-Mustaliq and Juwayriyah

The Messenger of God had spent part of Jumada in Medina when the Banu al-Mustaliq of Khuza'ah mounted their campaign, in Sha'ban of the sixth year after the emigration. Word had reached him that they were gathering to make war against him. Their commander was al-Harith ibn Abi Dirar — the father of Juwayriyah bint al-Harith, who later became a wife of the Messenger of God.

Hold onto that parenthesis. The record introduces the enemy commander by naming his daughter and telling you in advance where she ends up, before it has told you there will be captives at all. Everything in this section is arranged around a relationship that has not happened yet.

When the Messenger of God heard the news, he marched out from Medina toward them and engaged them at a watering place that was one of their own sources of water, known as al-Muraysi', lying toward the coast in the direction of Qadid. He struck them there. Many were killed and many others taken captive.

The battle cry of the Muslims on the day of the Banu al-Mustaliq was: O Mansur, slay.

Ali ibn Abi Talib charged against them, broke their ranks, and killed Malik and his son, striking off their heads. The Muslims then pursued the rest, killing many of them. The Messenger of God distributed a great number of the captives among the Muslims.

That last sentence is the ordinary law of the thing, and it is what makes the next paragraph remarkable. The captives are allotted. They are now the property of individual men, scattered across a whole army, and there is no mechanism in the account by which anyone can take them back.

Juwayriyah was the daughter of al-Harith ibn Abi Dirar. She had fallen by lot to Thabit ibn al-Dahdah, or to his cousin. She drew up a contract of emancipation with him, and then went to seek the help of the Messenger of God.

Notice the order of her two acts, because the order is the whole point. She does not go to the Prophet as a captive asking to be released. She first uses the instrument the law already gives a captive: a written agreement with her holder, by which she buys her own freedom for an agreed sum. Having put herself into that contract, she goes for help with the payment. She arrives, in other words, as a party to an agreement rather than as an item in a distribution.

When she had finished settling her contract of emancipation, the Messenger of God married her.

News of the marriage spread among the people that the Messenger of God had taken her as a wife, and the Muslims said: she is now the in-law of the Messenger of God. So they released those captives who were in their hands.

The narrators said: a hundred people from the households of the Banu al-Mustaliq were freed because of her. She was thus the most blessed of women for her own people.

Consider what actually released those hundred households, because it was not a command and it was not a ransom. No order was given. No compensation was paid. What changed was a category. The captives of an army become the relatives-by-marriage of the man that army follows, and holding them is now a thing nobody wishes to be seen doing. A single marriage reclassified an entire tribe overnight, and the release moved through the camp the way a piece of news moves, one owner at a time.

And the phrase the account chooses to close on is worth weighing. It does not say she was the most fortunate of women, or the most honoured. It says she was the most blessed of women for her own people — the blessing is measured in what it did for the hundred households, not in what it did for her.

One other thing happened on that day, and it will not stay finished.

A Muslim man of the Banu Kalb ibn 'Awf ibn 'Amir ibn Laith ibn Bakr was killed on the day of the Banu al-Mustaliq. He was known as Hisham ibn Sababa. A man of the Ansar who worshipped alongside Ibn al-Samt struck him, mistaking him for a polytheist, and killed him in error.

A man on the right side, killed by mistake by a man on the right side, in the confusion of a pursuit. The account names him, names the manner of it, and moves on. His brother does not.

## Words on the return march

At the watering place of the Banu al-Mustaliq, as the people were returning, a hired hand of 'Umar ibn al-Khattab from the Banu Ghifar — known as Jahjah ibn Mas'ud — was leading his horse. He jostled a man from Juhayna who was an ally of the Banu 'Awf ibn al-Khazraj at the water, and a quarrel broke out.

The man of Juhayna called out: O people of the Ansar.

And Jahjah called out: O people of the Muhajirun.

This ignited a heated exchange between 'Umar and 'Abd Allah ibn Ubayy ibn Salul, along with a faction of the hypocrites, and they came close to driving a wedge between the Muhajirun and the Ansar.

Two men have collided at a water trough over the right of way for a horse. Neither shouts his own name, and neither shouts for his family. Each reaches past his own person to the largest available category and calls it down onto a private shoving match. In the space of two words the incident stops being about a horse and becomes about the two halves of the community — the emigrants who arrived with nothing and the hosts who took them in.

The Messenger of God then set out with the people without halting. He kept them marching through the day and into the night until they were exhausted by the long march and worn down by the heat of the sun, and only then did he give the order to camp. When they reached the ground they fell asleep at once — and this was precisely his intention: to distract the people from the dispute that had erupted among them.

That is a deliberate remedy applied to a quarrel, and it is physical rather than verbal. No adjudication is offered, no side is named as wrong, no one is punished. The people are marched until argument becomes impossible, and then allowed to sleep. What the treatment is aimed at is not the merits of the case but the heat in it.

Meanwhile 'Abd Allah ibn Ubayy ibn Salul had said: when we return to Medina, the mightier will drive out the humbler — they have come into our own land.

When the Messenger of God finished the march and dismounted, Usayd ibn Hudayr came to greet him, and his tone carried displeasure. He said: O Messenger of God, you set out at an hour I cannot think you would normally have chosen.

The Messenger of God said to him: did you not hear what Ibn Ubayy ibn Salul said?

He replied: what did he say, O Messenger of God?

He said: he claimed that when he returns to Medina, the mightier will drive out the humbler.

Usayd said: O Messenger of God, by God, it is you who are the mighty and he who is the humble — and if you will it, God will drive him out. O Messenger of God, deal gently with him, for God has brought him to you. His own people had been preparing beads to crown him as their king, and he now sees that you have supplanted him in that.

That last sentence is the most useful piece of political explanation in this episode, and it comes from a man of Medina about a man of Medina. It supplies the motive the record has never bothered to supply for this man's long record of promises made and not kept. His own tribe had been stringing the beads for a crown when the emigrants arrived. He is not opposing a doctrine; he is watching a coronation not happen. And the counsel Usayd draws from his own diagnosis is not to punish him but to go gently with him.

Then the brother of the man killed by mistake came north.

Muqees ibn Sababa came from Mecca after his brother had been killed by a Muslim who took him for a polytheist, and he pretended to have embraced Islam. He said: O Messenger of God, I have come to seek the blood money for my brother.

The Messenger of God ordered the blood money to be paid to him. He then stayed a few days before turning on his brother's killer and slaying him, after which he fled to Mecca as an apostate.

He said in verse, of which this survives: he was struck by a blow that reached her and left her paralysed, from the soaking of the blades that rise above it and prevail; do not trust the sons of Bakr if they wrong you — I said it, and death is sustained by those who seek it.

He took the settlement and then took the revenge as well. The law's remedy for an accidental killing was paid out to him in full, in public, and he treated it as a payment on account rather than a settlement — and then removed himself from the community whose law had just compensated him. False profession of the faith is what got him into Medina and a false acceptance of blood money is what kept him there long enough.

Then a false report almost started a war.

In the days after the campaign, the Banu al-Mustaliq accepted Islam, and the Messenger of God sent a messenger to them: al-Walid ibn 'Uqbah. When the Banu al-Mustaliq heard of his coming, they rode out to receive him and to welcome him with the good news of their Islam, exactly as the Messenger of God had dispatched him to find.

However, al-Walid turned back and claimed that the tribe had rebelled, that they were planning to kill him, and that they had refused to hand over the charity due from them — to the point that the Messenger of God was on the verge of sending an expedition against them.

While that was being prepared, their delegation arrived and said: O Messenger of God, we remain firm in our Islam. We heard that your messenger was coming, so we rode out to receive and honour him and to hand over to him whatever charity we owed. He turned back and then claimed we had gone out to kill him — by God, we intended no such thing.

Then God revealed, concerning al-Walid ibn 'Uqbah: O you who believe, if a wicked person comes to you with news, verify it, lest you harm a people out of ignorance and then regret what you have done; and know that among you is the Messenger of God — were he to obey you in many matters, you would surely suffer (chapter 49, verse 6).

The margin in that story is a single delegation arriving in time. A tribe that had just entered the religion rode out in welcome, and the man who saw them coming turned his horse around and reported the welcome as an ambush. The verse that follows does not merely condemn him. It legislates: a report from an unreliable source is to be verified before it is acted on, and the reason given is the harm done to people out of ignorance and the regret that follows it.

Two more reports belong to this stretch, and the record handles them very differently.

A'isha bint Abi Bakr was with the Messenger of God on this journey. She fell behind; it was said that she had gone out to relieve herself; the litter was lifted and she was not in it, and what was said was said. It is reported that a verse was revealed to the Prophet declaring her innocence.

The account is deliberately brief and it does not repeat the accusation. What was said was said — and then the verdict, which is that a revelation came down clearing her. On a page whose subject is the damage done by unverified speech, the history declines to transmit the speech itself.

And then, at the door of the mosque, a comparison.

Salman al-Farisi said: when the Messenger of God departed from the battle of the Banu al-Mustaliq, he placed himself at the head of the people and commanded Ali to bring up the rear. When the Messenger of God reached Medina and came to the door of the mosque, he sat and waited for Ali. He did not enter his house. He wiped the sweat from his forehead, and then he said — gesturing with his hand toward some of the ranks — in this very hour, from among these people, there will emerge a man who resembles the Messiah in his standing among people. He is the best of people after me on the Day of Resurrection, and the first to enter Paradise.

He then had us watch the people. And the first to emerge was Ali ibn Abi Talib. When he came to the Messenger of God, he rose and embraced him, kissed him between the eyes, and they entered together.

Some of the hypocrites said: he likens his cousin to the Messiah and equates him with him — are our gods, whom we used to worship, better, or is Ali?

Then God revealed: and when the son of Mary was cited as an example, your people immediately raised an uproar, and they said, are our gods better, or is he? They cited him only to argue — indeed, they are a contentious people (chapter 43, verses 57–58).

That is the doctrinal centre of this episode, and every element of it is staged. The commander places himself at the front and puts Ali at the rear, which is the position that guarantees he will be the last man in. Then, at the city gate, he refuses to go home, sits down in the road, and announces in advance what the next man through will be. The prophecy is made before the man appears and the witnesses are told to watch. And the objection it draws is not that the claim is false; it is that the comparison is offensive — and the verse that answers it identifies that objection, by name, as argument for its own sake.

## The truce at al-Hudaybiyah

The Messenger of God spent Ramadan and Shawwal in Medina, then set out in Dhu al-Qi'dah as a man intending the lesser pilgrimage, and not war.

He called upon the Arab tribes around him, but many of them held back. He proceeded with those who accompanied him from the Muhajirun, the Ansar, and the Arabs who had pledged themselves to him. He brought the sacrificial animals with him and entered the state of consecration, so that people would know he had come only as a visitor to the Sacred House — a great mark of honour for it. The animals he sent numbered seventy head.

Jabir ibn 'Abd Allah al-Ansari used to say: we, the companions of al-Hudaybiyah, were fourteen hundred.

He entered consecration at Dhu al-Hulayfah. His companions followed him into it, and they garlanded the sacrificial animals and marked them so that they would be recognised.

Every element of that departure is a declaration made in a language the whole peninsula could read at a glance. Consecrated men may not fight. Garlanded animals are for slaughter at the House. Seventy head of them, moving on the road with fourteen hundred unarmoured pilgrims, is a statement of intent that requires no herald.

When they reached 'Usfan, Bishr ibn Sufyan al-Ka'bi — also called Busr — met him and said: O Messenger of God, Quraysh has heard of your march. They have gone out with their women, wearing animal skins, and have sworn a covenant with God that you will never enter upon them. Khalid ibn al-Walid is now at the head of their cavalry, encamped at the shepherd's plateau.

The Messenger of God said: woe to Quraysh — war has consumed them. What would it cost them to leave me to the rest of the Arabs? If they were to strike me down, that would be what they sought. And if God were to grant me victory over them, they would enter Islam in great numbers. And if they do not, they will fight and be destroyed. What do Quraysh think? By God, I will never cease striving for what God has sent me to accomplish until He causes it to prevail — or until this neck is severed. And he pointed to the front of his neck.

Then he said: is there anyone who can lead us by another road, so that we avoid encountering Quraysh?

The two halves of that speech are worth holding apart. The first half is an argument addressed to his enemies' own interest: every branch of the tree ends better for them than fighting, and the only thing war can buy them is destruction. The second half concedes nothing at all — the striving continues until it prevails or the neck is cut. And the very next sentence out of his mouth is a request for a detour, so that the men wearing the pilgrim's cloth do not have to meet Khalid's cavalry on the road.

A man who had accepted Islam said: I am the one, O Messenger of God. He led them along a rough and difficult road.

When they reached the descent toward al-Hudaybiyah, his she-camel knelt and refused to move. His companions said: she is spent, O Messenger of God.

He said: she is not spent, nor is that her nature. What has restrained her is the same force that restrained the Elephant from Mecca. Today I will not refuse any plan by which Quraysh asks me to honour the ties of kinship.

That is a reading of an animal's behaviour as a sign, and it names its precedent exactly. The elephant that would not advance on Mecca in the year of the Prophet's birth is a story this history told at the beginning of the volume. What he draws from the repetition is not that Mecca is protected against him; it is a policy — that he will accept any proposal Quraysh makes to him in the name of kinship. The concessions that fill the rest of this section are announced here, before a single Qurayshi envoy has arrived.

Then he said to them: dismount.

They replied: O Messenger of God, there is no water in this valley on which we can camp.

He took an arrow from his quiver and gave it to one of his companions. The man plunged it into the bed of that hollow, digging it into the earth — and the water welled up. The people drank and watered their animals.

Envoys then moved back and forth between the Messenger of God and Quraysh. Quraysh sent 'Urwah ibn Mas'ud al-Thaqafi to speak with him, and the Messenger of God told him that he had not come to fight, but only to honour the Sacred House.

'Urwah returned to Quraysh having watched how the companions treated him: whenever he performed ablution they rushed for the water he had used; whenever he spat they caught it; and whenever a hair fell from him they took it.

He went back and said: O people, I have seen Khosrow in his kingdom, and Caesar in his kingdom, and the Negus in his kingdom — and by God, I have never seen any king among his people as Muhammad is among his companions. Do not provoke them.

The report Quraysh commissioned is an intelligence assessment, and it comes back inverted. The envoy was sent to weigh a threat and he came back weighing an attachment. His three points of comparison are the three greatest thrones he knows of, and the man he is describing has no throne, no guard and no city — and stands, in his judgment, above all three of them in the only measure 'Urwah found worth reporting.

Then Quraysh sent fifty men to circle the camp and take prisoners. They were themselves captured. The Messenger of God pardoned them and released them. Yet they pelted his camp with arrows and stones. He sent envoys to Quraysh, who imprisoned them and acted in bad faith.

When the Messenger of God learned that his envoys had been seized and killed, his companions pledged allegiance to him. This is the Pledge of al-Ridwan.

Some say he took their pledge unto death. But Jabir ibn 'Abd Allah used to say: the Messenger of God did not take our pledge unto death; he took our pledge that we would not flee.

The history preserves both readings and does not choose between them, but it gives the correction to a man who was standing there. The difference is not small. A pledge unto death commits fourteen hundred consecrated men to be killed where they stand. A pledge not to flee commits them only to hold the ground — which is what a body of pilgrims outside a hostile city actually needs, and what makes the negotiation that follows possible rather than pointless.

Quraysh eventually sent Suhayl ibn 'Amr, of the Banu 'Amir ibn Lu'ayy, to treat with him. When the Messenger of God saw him coming he said: the people want peace.

The two men negotiated and came to terms with relative ease, and a settlement was near. Nothing remained but the written document.

At that point 'Umar ibn al-Khattab rose in protest and addressed the Messenger of God: O Messenger of God, are you not the Messenger of God?

He said: yes.

He said: are we not Muslims?

He said: yes.

He said: are they not polytheists?

He said: yes.

He said: then why should we concede anything of our religion?

The Messenger of God said: I am the servant of God and His Messenger. I will not disobey His command, and He will not forsake me.

That is a syllogism answered by a station. The three premises are each conceded without hesitation, and the conclusion drawn from them is simply not addressed. What is offered instead is a description of the speaker's own position — servant, messenger, under command, not abandoned — which locates the decision somewhere the argument cannot reach.

Then the Messenger of God called on Ali ibn Abi Talib to write the document. He said: write — in the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.

Suhayl said: I do not recognise this. Write instead: in Your name, O God.

Ali wrote: in the name of God. Then he wrote: this is what Muhammad, the Messenger of God, has agreed upon with Suhayl ibn 'Amr.

Suhayl objected: if I acknowledged you as the Messenger of God, I would not have fought you. Write your name and your father's name instead.

The Prophet said to Ali: write — this is what Muhammad ibn 'Abd Allah has agreed upon.

The Commander of the Faithful said: O Messenger of God, I cannot erase your name.

He replied: write it. You will one day be called to do something similar.

That day came in the time of Mu'awiyah, and this history says it will recount it in its place.

Two erasures are demanded and both are granted — the opening formula and the title. And into the second of them is set a prophecy about the scribe rather than about the treaty. The man refusing to strike out the words the Messenger of God is told that he will be asked to accept the same erasure of his own title, and the history stops there and promises the rest for later. What the reader is being given is not an anecdote about a document. It is the first half of a pattern.

Then Ali wrote, at the dictation of the Messenger of God: in the name of God. This is what Muhammad ibn 'Abd Allah and Suhayl ibn 'Amr have agreed upon. They have agreed to lay down the state of war between the two peoples for ten years, during which all people shall be safe and refrain from attacking one another. Whoever comes to Muhammad from Quraysh without his guardian's permission shall be returned to them. Whoever comes to Quraysh from those with Muhammad shall not be returned. There shall be no treachery and no breach of faith. Whoever wishes to enter into the covenant and alliance of Muhammad may do so, and whoever wishes to enter into the covenant and alliance of Quraysh may do so.

Khuza'ah stepped forward and declared: we are in the covenant and alliance of Muhammad. The Banu Bakr likewise stepped forward and declared: we are in the covenant and alliance of Quraysh.

Then Quraysh said: you must turn back from us this year and not enter Mecca. But when a year has passed, we shall withdraw, and you shall enter with your companions and remain there for three days. You may carry only the weapons of a traveller — swords in their scabbards — and bring nothing more.

Read the return clause on its own, because it is the clause that makes the treaty look like a defeat. It runs one way. A man who escapes to Medina goes back; a man who leaves Medina for Mecca stays. It was drafted that way and accepted that way, and the very next thing that happens is a demonstration of what it costs.

The document was almost finished when Abu Jandal ibn Suhayl ibn 'Amr appeared, shuffling toward them in his shackles. He had accepted Islam and his father had imprisoned him for it. He had now escaped and made his way to the Messenger of God.

His father said: has not the agreement between us already been settled, before this man arrived?

The Prophet said: yes.

Suhayl then rose, seized his son, and dragged him back toward Quraysh — while Abu Jandal cried out at the top of his voice: O community of Muslims, am I to be handed back to the polytheists, who will persecute me for my religion?

The Muslims were deeply shaken.

The Messenger of God said: O Abu Jandal, be patient and persevere. God will surely provide relief and a way out for you and for those like you who are oppressed. We have made peace with these people and have given them the covenant of God. We cannot act in bad faith toward them.

When the document was completed, several Muslim men witnessed the reconciliation.

The same thing happened again, in Medina, and the answer did not change. Abu Basir — whose full name was 'Utbah ibn Usayd ibn Jariya, and who was of Thaqif — had been one of those imprisoned in Mecca. He came to the Prophet, brought by a polytheist carrying a letter from Quraysh that said: did you not promise us that anyone who came to you from our side would be returned?

The Prophet said: yes. And then he said: O Abu Basir, we have given these people a binding pledge, and treachery has no place in our religion. God will grant you and those like you relief and a way out.

Abu Basir was escorted back with the polytheists. When they reached Dhu al-Hulayfa, he killed one of his two escorts. He then made his way until he encamped at al-'Is, on the shore of the sea, on the road that Quraysh used to travel to the Levant.

When news of this reached the Muslims who were still imprisoned in Mecca, they began slipping away one by one to join him. The Messenger of God had said of him: woe to his mother — what a kindler of war he would be, if only he had men behind him. And some seventy men eventually gathered around him at al-'Is. They waged relentless war on Quraysh, letting no caravan pass without attacking it and killing those they could.

This continued until Quraysh wrote to the Messenger of God, appealing to him by the ties of kinship to grant Abu Basir and his companions shelter in Medina — saying that they no longer had any need of the treaty clause requiring their return. The Messenger of God granted them shelter, and they came to him in Medina.

That is what the relief and the way out turned out to be, and it arrived by exactly the route the clause had opened. The men the treaty would not let into Medina were not covered by it anywhere else. Seventy of them on the Syrian road did to Quraysh's commerce what an army at Medina could not have done without breaking the peace, and it was Quraysh who asked for the clause to be dropped — in a letter appealing to kinship, which is the one appeal he had said at the descent to al-Hudaybiyah he would never refuse.

One clause was cancelled by revelation rather than by request. Around that time Kulthum bint 'Uqbah ibn Abi Mu'ayt emigrated to the Messenger of God, and her brothers 'Amara and al-Walid came after her, asking for her return. Then God revealed: O you who believe, when believing women come to you as emigrants, examine them — God knows best concerning their faith. If you find them to be believers, do not return them to the disbelievers. They are not lawful for the disbelievers, nor are the disbelievers lawful for them. Give the disbelievers what they have spent, and there is no blame on you if you marry them when you have given them their bridal gifts. And do not hold to the marriage bonds of disbelieving women (chapter 60, verse 10).

So God commanded that these women not be returned to the disbelievers.

And at al-Hudaybiyah itself, the pilgrimage had to be ended without being performed.

The Messenger of God was troubled. He went to his sacrificial animal and slaughtered it, then sat down and shaved his head. When the Muslims saw that he had slaughtered and shaved, they rose and did the same — some shaving their heads completely, others cutting their hair short.

The Messenger of God said: may God have mercy on those who shave.

They said: and those who cut short, O Messenger of God?

He said: may God have mercy on those who shave.

They said again: and those who cut short, O Messenger of God?

He said: may God have mercy on those who shave — and on those who cut short. On the third asking, he included them.

He returned with his caravan, and while he was between Mecca and Medina, the chapter of the Opening was revealed to him.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry forward.

The first is that a change of category can do what no order could. A hundred households of the Banu al-Mustaliq were not ransomed, bought back or released by decree. Their captors were scattered through an army and each held lawful property. What freed them was that one woman settled her own emancipation contract, married the Prophet, and made her whole tribe the in-laws of the man they all followed — after which holding her people became a thing nobody wished to be seen doing. The account measures her blessing in the hundred households, not in the marriage.

The second is that the whole middle of this episode is about the reliability of speech, and it ends with the Quran legislating on it. Two men shout tribal slogans over a horse at a water trough and nearly split the community; the remedy is a forced march that makes argument physically impossible. A messenger reports a welcoming party as an assassination attempt and nearly starts a war against new Muslims; the verse that comes down does not merely condemn him but establishes a rule — verify a report from an unreliable source before acting, because of the harm done in ignorance and the regret that follows. And on the same page, when a slander is raised against the Prophet's wife, this history declines to repeat the words at all and gives only the verdict.

The third is that al-Hudaybiyah is where a written word is honoured against every visible interest of the people who gave it. The terms were one-sided on their face: turn back this year, hand over anyone who escapes to you, get nobody back. The scribe was made to erase both the opening formula and the title from the document he was writing, and was told he would one day be asked to erase his own. A young man in chains was dragged back to his persecutors in front of fourteen hundred witnesses, and the answer given to him was that a covenant of God had been given and could not be broken. Then the clause emptied itself: seventy escapees who were not allowed into Medina camped on the Syrian road instead, and Quraysh wrote asking for the clause to be lifted. On the way home, with nothing gained that anyone could point to, the chapter of the Opening came down.
