## Where this episode opens

What if nearly everything the world believed about a religious community for the better part of a thousand years had been written down by the people who wanted that community erased? Not misremembered, not exaggerated in the retelling — composed deliberately, in ink, by hostile hands, and then copied forward so faithfully and for so long that the copies quietly became the record. That is not a thought experiment. Until the middle of the twentieth century, that was the actual condition of Ismaili history. The books scholars consulted, in Europe and in the Muslim world alike, were overwhelmingly books written by the Ismailis' opponents, and a considerable portion of the evidence inside them had been manufactured for the purpose.

This episode is about how that situation came apart, and about the one Arabic work that stands at the centre of the reversal. The book we are beginning is called *Uyun al-Akhbar wa Funun al-Athar* — a title we can render roughly as the choicest of reports and the varieties of traditions — and it was written in fifteenth-century Yemen by a man named Idris Imad al-Din. It runs to seven volumes. It traces the history of the Ismaili Imams from the earliest times down to the closing years of the Fatimid period. And in the entire surviving Ismaili corpus, it is the only comprehensive history of the Imams written by an Ismaili author. Not the best of several. The only one.

That single fact is worth pausing on before we go any further, because it explains why this series exists and why the first volume — the volume on the life of the Prophet — is not simply another biography of the Prophet among the hundreds already available. It is a biography written from inside a tradition that, for most of its existence, could not afford to write history at all.

So before we open the book and let the author speak in his own voice, we need three things settled. We need to know why Ismaili history had to be recovered rather than simply read — why the archive was so thin and the hostile testimony so thick. We need to know who Idris Imad al-Din was, and, more to the point, what office he held that put a lost library within his reach. And we need to know how this book is built: its seven parts, the sources it draws on, and — because an honest introduction owes a listener this — where its own scholarly readers say it should be treated with caution.

Answer those three, and the rest of the series stops being a sequence of episodes about events and becomes something better: a record you know how to read.

## A history written by its enemies

Begin with the archive, because everything else follows from it. Until the mid-twentieth century, the study of Ismailism rested almost entirely on evidence gathered — and very often fabricated — by the Ismailis' adversaries. The consequence was not a few scattered errors. It was an entire body of myth and misconception about this Shi'ite Muslim community, circulating with equal confidence in Muslim societies and in the West, concerning both what the Ismailis taught and what they were supposed to do. The community had a reputation long before it had a readable literature, and the reputation had been assembled by people with no interest in accuracy.

The turning point came from a single change in method: the recovery and systematic study of original Ismaili texts. Those texts existed. They had never stopped existing. They were held in large numbers of private collections — in Yemen, in Syria, in Persia, in Central Asia, in South Asia, and elsewhere — passed down within families and within the institutions of the da'wa, the Ismaili mission. What they had not been was accessible. A small collection of manuscripts found in Syria surfaced in Paris during the twentieth century. Another body of material, preserved in Yemen and in Central Asia, was recovered during the first decades of the same century. And yet as late as 1933, when a bibliographic survey of Ismaili writings was undertaken — building on the earlier work of Louis Massignon, who lived from 1883 to 1962 — the information available in European libraries, and known to the wider research community, remained very limited indeed.

The real breakthrough came not in Europe but in India. In the 1930s, new research in Ismaili studies was effectively launched there, in the region where important collections of Ismaili manuscripts had been preserved by the Ismaili Bohra community. The credit belongs primarily to the pioneering work of Vladimir Ivanov, who lived from 1886 to 1970, and to a small number of Bohra scholars — most notably Asif Ali Asghar Faydi, Hussein ibn Faydullah al-Hamdani, who lived from 1901 to 1962, and Zahid Ali, who lived from 1888 to 1958. What distinguishes these men is that they built original scholarship on manuscripts held by their own families. The archive and the scholar were, in the first instance, the same household.

And then, decisively, those collections stopped being private. In time they were made available to researchers everywhere: Professor Faydi gave his manuscripts to a university library in Bombay; Hussein al-Hamdani likewise gave part of his family's collection to the same library, while his son, Professor Abbas Hamdani, gifted another portion to a research library in London in 2006. In 1977 the Zahid Ali Collection — two hundred and twenty-six Ismaili manuscripts in Arabic — was donated to the Institute of Ismaili Studies. That is a small procession of acts of scholarly generosity, and it is the mechanism by which a hidden tradition became a readable one.

The recovery gathered institutional weight as it went. In 1946 the founding of the Ismaili Society in Bombay gave the work a home; Ivanov played a key part in establishing it, and the Society assembled a large collection of Arabic and Persian manuscripts, which were later transferred to the Institute of Ismaili Studies in the early 1980s. What had been scattered across households in half a dozen countries became, over roughly fifty years, a consultable archive in two or three places. That is the unglamorous machinery behind the phrase "Ismaili studies": not a discovery but a migration, carried out by families willing to give away what their ancestors had guarded.

The scale of what emerged is worth stating plainly, because it measures how badly the old picture had failed. Ivanov's 1933 survey identified some seven hundred distinct Ismaili titles and demonstrated, for the first time, a richness and diversity in Ismaili literature and intellectual heritage that nobody outside the tradition had suspected. By 1963, when he published a revised version of that survey, many new sources had come to light and considerable progress had been made in editing and studying the texts. Later still, Professor Poonawala's bibliography listed around thirteen hundred titles by more than two hundred authors, and a further bibliographic study assembled roughly five thousand primary texts and scholarly studies published in the field. Seven hundred, then thirteen hundred, then five thousand. A literature the world had been confidently describing without reading.

But now the harder question, and the one that gives this episode its spine. If the Ismailis produced this enormous body of writing, why did they produce so little history? Why does a tradition with thirteen hundred recorded titles yield exactly one comprehensive history of its own Imams?

The answer lies in two conditions that shaped Ismaili writing from the beginning. The first is persecution. The Ismailis were frequently persecuted in regions outside the territories they themselves controlled, and that pressure forced them into strict adherence to the Shi'ite principle of taqiyya — protective dissimulation, the concealment of one's belief and affiliation when disclosure would bring destruction. A community practising taqiyya does not keep public chronicles of its leadership. A written history naming names, places, and dates is precisely the document that must not exist. Think about what that costs over centuries. Every generation that protects itself by not writing is a generation the next one cannot read about. The silence is not neglect; it is a survival strategy working exactly as intended, and its price is paid by everybody who comes afterward and wants to know what happened.

The second condition is a matter of vocation. Ismaili authors and preachers were, for the most part, jurists and religious scholars. Given the education they received and the pressing necessity of secrecy in their activities, they had little inclination toward historical writing in the first place. Their training pointed elsewhere, and their circumstances made the elsewhere safer. The evidence of this shows up starkly in the recovered corpus: an enormous volume of Ismaili texts, and among them a very small number of historical works.

What those texts contain instead tells you what the tradition valued. Legal compendia. Biographies. Poetry. Treatises on the Shi'ite faith, running from the centrality of the Imamate out to complex metaphysical theses organized around the Ismaili system of esoteric truths — a system with a cyclical view of history, a universal hierarchical order, and its own doctrines concerning the Hereafter and salvation. From the very outset, a large portion of Ismaili writing was devoted to ta'wil: the interpretation, the esoteric exegesis, of Qur'anic texts, religious injunctions, and ritual observances. Several preachers and scholars who lived in Iran — among them Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani, who died after the year 361 of the Islamic calendar; Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani, who died after 411, corresponding to 1020; and Nasir-i Khusraw, who died after 462, corresponding to 1070 — established out of this material a distinctive Shi'ite intellectual tradition grounded in theology and in a range of philosophical doctrines. That is where the energy went. Not into chronicles.

There were, however, two periods when the Ismailis did turn sustained attention to historical writing, and the reason is the same in both cases: they had a state. During the Fatimid era, from 297 to 567 of the Islamic calendar — 909 to 1171 — and again during the Alamut period, from 483 to 654, which is 1090 to 1256, the Ismailis possessed states and ruling dynasties whose reigns and accomplishments needed to be reliably recorded. A community in hiding writes no history; a community that governs must. During the Fatimid caliphate, and especially after the transfer of the Fatimid centre from North Africa, in what is now Tunisia, to Egypt in 362, corresponding to 973, a substantial number of historical books about the dynasty and the Fatimid state were written — by contemporary Ismaili and non-Ismaili historians alike. These were, in the fullest sense, official histories.

And then both archives were destroyed, in almost identical fashion, a little under a century apart.

Most Fatimid historical works did not survive the fall of the Fatimid dynasty in 567, which is 1171, when the Ayyubids who replaced the Fatimids carried out a systematic destruction of the celebrated Fatimid libraries in Cairo. Note the adjective: systematic. This was not the incidental loss that attends any change of regime. It was the deliberate unmaking of a record. The same fate befell the works recording the events of the Ismaili state in Persia during the Alamut period, which were destroyed along with other Ismaili writings by the Mongol armies that swept through Persia in 654, corresponding to 1256.

So the shape of the problem is now complete, and it has three layers. For most of their history the Ismailis could not safely write history. During the two periods when they could, they did — and those writings were destroyed by the powers that succeeded them. And into the vacuum left behind flowed the accounts of their opponents, which were preserved precisely because their preservers had won.

Despite all of this, a number of Ismaili preachers did manage to compile historical works that survived. Among that handful, the writings of Idris Imad al-Din — and above all his *Uyun al-Akhbar* — occupy a place that no amount of later scholarship can supply from elsewhere. The seven-volume *Uyun al-Akhbar* is the only comprehensive history of the Ismaili Imams, from earliest times to the late Fatimid period, written by an Ismaili author. It is not a good source among many. Where it speaks, it is very often the only voice from inside the tradition still speaking at all.

## The nineteenth absolute da'i

Which raises the obvious question. If the archive was destroyed in Cairo in 1171 and again in Persia in 1256, how could a man writing in Yemen in the 1430s produce a comprehensive history of the Ismaili Imams from the earliest times to the late Fatimid period? Where did he get his material?

The answer is that the archive had moved, and that he happened to hold the office that kept the key.

His full name is Imad al-Din Idris ibn al-Hasan ibn Abdullah ibn Ali ibn al-Walid al-Anf. He belonged to the al-Walid family, a house of Qurashi descent settled in Yemen, which had led the Tayyibi Ismaili da'wa for more than three centuries, beginning early in the seventh Islamic century — the thirteenth of the common era. He was born in the year 794, corresponding to 1392, at the fortress of Shibam, set on a high peak of Mount Haraz, one of the Ismaili strongholds of Yemen.

No entry on him appears in the standard biographical dictionaries — itself a small reminder of how a tradition practising concealment shows up in the reference works, which is to say, largely by absence. What we have instead is a biographical notice written centuries later by an Indian da'i, Qutb al-Din Sulayman ibn Hasan al-Burhanpuri, who died in 1241 of the Islamic calendar, corresponding to 1826. Al-Burhanpuri devoted a notice to him in a work called *Muntaza' al-Akhbar fi Akhbar al-Du'at al-Akhyar*, and he composed it by drawing on Idris's own later books, the *Nuzhat al-Afkar* and the *Rawdat al-Akhbar*. Al-Burhanpuri did not record the year of Idris's birth; the scholar Ismail Qurban Poonawala established it as 794, corresponding to 1392.

Now the office, because it is the whole point. Idris was the nineteenth absolute da'i in the line of Tayyibi preachers during the period of the second concealment. Those words carry a great deal, so let us unpack them slowly.

A da'i is a summoner — the head of the Ismaili mission, the da'wa, in a given territory. The absolute da'i, the da'i mutlaq, is the leader of the whole mission in a period when the Imam himself is not manifest. That period is called satr, concealment. In the Tayyibi tradition, the second concealment began after the occultation of the Imam who administers the ordinances of God, whose son, Imam al-Tayyib ibn al-Amir, went into hiding. From that moment the community was led not by a visible Imam but by a succession of absolute preachers acting on the hidden Imam's behalf. Idris was the nineteenth of them. His family had supplied that leadership for three centuries.

He came to the office by testament. His uncle, Ali ibn Abdullah ibn Ali ibn al-Walid, the eighteenth absolute da'i, designated him and then died on the third of Safar in the year 832 — the thirteenth of November, 1428. Of Idris's life before that designation we know essentially nothing. He appears in the record already carrying the office.

He assumed leadership of a concealed mission during a violent period, marked by fierce wars among the rulers and imams of Yemen. Following the policy of his predecessors, he allied himself with the Rasulid rulers of Zabid in the lowlands and fought a series of battles against the Zaydis of northern Yemen; when the Rasulid king al-Zahir, who ruled from 831 to 842 — 1428 to 1439 — joined forces with him, Idris campaigned against the Zaydi Imam al-Mansur Ali. Out of those confrontations he acquired several castles and successfully defended numerous forts and strongholds. Later he enjoyed the support and the friendship of the two Tahirid brothers, Ali and Umar, who in 858, corresponding to 1454, took control of Aden, Zabid, and Halay, succeeding the Rasulids as lords of lower Yemen. He was, in other words, not merely a scholar who happened to hold a religious title. He was a political actor with fortresses and allies, in a country at war with itself.

Then, in 840 — 1436 — a plague struck the region of Mount Haraz and carried off many supporters of the da'wa. Among the dead were Izz al-Din Ma'ad, son of the da'i Abdullah ibn Ali ibn al-Walid, and his uncle Muhammad ibn Ali; most of them died in the month of Safar of that year. The calamity forced Idris to withdraw to Shibam. And here is the detail that gives the man a face rather than a title: he did not return to Haraz — the principal stronghold of the Ismaili mission in Yemen — for thirteen years. He came back only in Sha'ban of the year 853, which is November 1449. Of that homecoming he wrote afterwards: "The people of the da'wa welcomed my return as though a father long absent had come home after a lengthy journey."

Thirteen years away from the centre of the mission he led. It is worth holding that sentence beside the *Uyun al-Akhbar*, because those were also the years in which the writing was underway.

His other great labour was at a distance. Idris took a particular interest in the affairs of the Tayyibi Ismaili mission in western India, and across a leadership of nearly forty years he contributed substantially to the success of the Tayyibi da'wa and of the Bohra community in Gujarat. In doing so he laid the groundwork for something that would only become visible long after his death: the eventual transfer of the centre of gravity of the Tayyibi mission from Yemen to the Indian subcontinent. The books he wrote in Yemen would be preserved, copied, and studied in India — which is, in the end, a large part of why we have them.

When he sensed that his end was near, he designated his son, Hasan ibn Idris Badr al-Din, as his successor in the leadership of the mission, in accordance with the principle of nass — designation by explicit appointment — as his community understood it. He strengthened his son's position by pairing him with his cousin, Abdullah ibn Ali ibn al-Hasan Fakhr al-Din. Idris died on the nineteenth of Dhu al-Qa'dah in the year 872, corresponding to the tenth of June, 1468, at Shibam, having held the leadership of the Yemeni mission for more than forty years.

So: a fortress-born administrator, a wartime ally of lowland kings, a builder of a distant community in Gujarat, and a man who spent thirteen years exiled by plague from the mountain he governed. All of that matters. But none of it is the reason his history could be written.

The reason is custodial. Idris Imad al-Din is regarded as the most important Ismaili historian, and his significance derives from a fact of position rather than of talent: as senior Tayyibi leader in Yemen, he had access to the entire literary heritage of the Ismailis then available in that country — much of which has since been lost. He was not reconstructing the Fatimid past from hostile summaries. He was reading it.

How did that heritage reach Yemen at all? Gradually, and by a route the destruction of the Cairo libraries did not reach. From the second half of the fifth Islamic century onward — the eleventh century of the common era — a large portion of the Ismaili texts dating from the Fatimid era and earlier was transferred to Yemen, carried there by the close ties between the Fatimid missionaries in Yemen, who acknowledged Fatimid sovereignty and led the mission in the southern Arabian Peninsula, and the Fatimid centre in Cairo. The heritage was brought during the mission of al-Qadi Lamak ibn Malik al-Hammadi in the middle of that century. After the occultation of Imam al-Tayyib ibn al-Amir in the year 524, corresponding to 1130, that literary inheritance passed into the custody of the Tayyibi preachers of Yemen — the institution of the da'wa itself — and it was that institution which Idris came to head in 832, corresponding to 1428.

Follow the chain and the survival of this history stops looking accidental. Books travelled from Cairo to Yemen while the Fatimid state still stood. The Cairo libraries were destroyed in 1171; the Yemeni copies were not. The da'wa institution held them through three centuries of concealment. In 1428 a scholar inherited both the institution and its library. Drawing on those original sources — many of which have not reached us in any other form — he produced numerous historical and doctrinal works. And the books he wrote were then carried to India and copied there.

Every link in that chain had to hold. This is what the title of this episode means by survival.

## The seven parts and their sources

Now the book itself, and its own architecture is the first thing to notice.

Its full title is *Uyun al-Akhbar wa Funun al-Athar fi Dhikr al-Nabi al-Mukhtar al-Mustafa wa Wasiyyihi Ali ibn Abi Talib Qatil al-Kuffar wa al-A'imma al-Athar, Alayhim Salawat Allah al-Malik al-Ghaffar* — a long title in the classical manner, which names its own subject matter: the Chosen Prophet, his appointed legatee Ali ibn Abi Talib, and the pure Imams. It is the most important work chronicling the Ismaili mission from its origins through the second half of the sixth Islamic century, the twelfth of the common era.

The work is divided into seven parts, and the author does not call them volumes or books. He calls each one a *sab'* — a "seven". The choice is deliberate and doctrinal rather than editorial. The number carries deep significance for the Ismailis, who are also known as the Seveners after their seventh Imam, Muhammad ibn Isma'il ibn Ja'far al-Sadiq. A history of the Imams built in seven parts is already making an argument before its first sentence: the shape of the container is meant to echo the shape of the cycle it describes. When we speak in this series of "volume one", it is worth remembering that the author would have said the first seven.

Here is what the seven parts cover.

The first part gives the virtues and the biography of the Prophet, the marriage of Fatima and Ali, and the events of the Prophet's life through to his death. That is the volume this series begins with, and it carries a particular importance: it presents the life of the Prophet from the Ismaili doctrinal perspective, which is not a thing the general reader has ready access to elsewhere.

The second and third parts give the life of the Commander of the Faithful, Ali ibn Abi Talib, the events of his battles, and two descriptive sections — again setting out the Ismaili view of a figure whose story is told very differently by different traditions. These first three parts are appearing here in a complete critical edition for the first time.

The fourth part gives an account of the Imams from al-Hasan ibn Ali ibn Abi Talib — who died in the year 61, corresponding to 680 — through the end of the era of the hidden Imams, closing with Husayn ibn Ahmad, the last concealed Imam of the early Ismailis during the period of concealment, and the beginning of the appearance of the Mahdi Abdullah.

The fifth part covers the establishment of the Fatimid state in Ifriqiyya, in 297, corresponding to 909, and the first three caliph-imams who ruled in succession: al-Mahdi, from 297 to 322 — 909 to 934; al-Qa'im, from 322 to 334 — 934 to 946; and al-Mansur, from 334 to 341 — 946 to 953.

The sixth part takes up the next four caliph-imams and the transfer of the Fatimid caliphate to Egypt: al-Mu'izz, from 341 to 365 — 953 to 975; al-Aziz, from 365 to 386 — 975 to 996; al-Hakim, from 386 to 411 — 996 to 1021; and al-Zahir, from 411 to 427 — 1021 to 1036; together with the early years of the reign of al-Mustansir, who ruled from 427 to 487, corresponding to 1036 to 1094.

The seventh and final part is the longest in reach and the richest in detail. It covers the remaining period of al-Mustansir's reign, the rise of the Sulayhid dynasty in Yemen, the schism that followed al-Mustansir's death in 487, corresponding to 1094, and the reigns of the subsequent Fatimid caliph-imams recognized by the Musta'li Ismailis — al-Musta'li, from 487 to 495, which is 1094 to 1101, and al-Amir, from 495 to 524, which is 1101 to 1130 — along with the beginnings of the Tayyibi mission in Yemen and the eventual collapse of the Fatimids in Egypt. It also carries important details about a range of Yemeni preachers, and it remains a major source for the history of the Ismaili mission in Yemen during the Crusader period.

It appears that Idris began working on the *Uyun al-Akhbar* immediately after completing an earlier work, the *Zahr al-Ma'ani*, in the year 838, corresponding to 1434 — which places the start of the writing four years before the plague that drove him to Shibam.

What did he build it out of? Partly out of memory that had never been written down: he preserved the oral heritage of the Tayyibi mission, material that existed nowhere but in the transmission of the community itself. Beyond that, he drew on Ismaili sources, among them the writings of al-Qadi al-Nu'man, who died in 363, corresponding to 974; the biography of al-Da'i al-Mu'ayyad fi al-Din al-Shirazi, who died in 470, corresponding to 1078, and who is regarded as the godfather of the Yemeni mission; and other Ismaili biographical works, including the life of Imam al-Mahdi and the life of Ibn Hawshab Mansur al-Yaman — the latter a text whose authorship remains unknown and which has not come down to us independently at all. Sit with that: a book preserved only inside another book.

He also relied on a range of non-Ismaili sources, Yemeni and otherwise: the history of Ibn al-Zulaq, who died in 386, corresponding to 996; the history of the judge Muhammad ibn Salama al-Quda'i, who died in 454, corresponding to 1062; and the history of Umara al-Yamani, who died in 569, corresponding to 1174. Several of these, likewise, have not survived directly. And beyond narrative sources he had documents — decrees, letters, registers, and other archival materials of the Fatimid movement — which no longer survive in full, but which shed light on the workings of the Ismaili mission in Yemen and on the relations between the Fatimids and the Sulayhids. All of this is what makes the *Uyun al-Akhbar* an invaluable reference for Ismaili history in the medieval period. It is a container of lost libraries.

The *Uyun al-Akhbar* was the first of three historical works. The second, the *Nuzhat al-Afkar*, in two volumes, treats Ismaili history in Yemen, particularly the period following the decline of the Sulayhid dynasty down to the year 853, corresponding to 1449. It is the most important source for the history of the Tayyibi mission in Yemen across the three centuries after the Sulayhids, whose effective rule ended in 532, corresponding to 1138, with the death of the Lady Queen Arwa. Queen Arwa is worth a sentence of her own: besides being the Sulayhid sovereign, she had been appointed by Imam al-Mustansir to the highest rank in the Fatimid mission's organization, the rank of hujja. The book divides into two parts — the first tracing the mission in Yemen from the days of Mansur al-Yaman, that is Ibn Hawshab, down to the appointment of al-Dhu'ayb ibn Musa al-Wada'i as the first absolute preacher of the era of concealment, followed by brief memoir-like notices on a succession of preachers, ending with the seventeenth da'i, Abdullah ibn Ali ibn Muhammad ibn Hatim, who was the author's own grandfather; and the second devoted entirely to the remainder of that seventeenth preacher's life and to the biography of the eighteenth, one of the author's immediate ancestors.

The third work, the *Rawdat al-Akhbar*, picks up where the second leaves off, covering events of the author's own lifetime from 854, corresponding to 1450, to 870, corresponding to 1465. It is an important source for the history of the Tahirid rulers who governed Yemen after the Rasulids — precisely because Idris was allied with them — and it is the principal source for Idris's own life and for the part he played as head of the concealed mission in Yemen.

He wrote a great deal besides. He was a poet, and his unpublished collection of verse carries historical information alongside panegyrics in praise of the Imams and the preachers. His major theological work, divided into twenty-one chapters, is the most important elaboration of *al-haqa'iq* — the esoteric system of Ismaili thought — that the Tayyibi mission in Yemen ever produced. And he wrote a number of polemical works refuting Sunni, Mu'tazili, and Zaydi doctrines. Most of his writings have come down to us, preserved in private and institutional collections, including the large libraries of the Tayyibi Bohra mission at Surat.

And here is the detail that ties the survival of this history to a daily discipline rather than to luck. Because of the book's importance to the history of the Ismaili mission, every student at the Ismaili university at Surat, in India, was required to make a complete and faithful copy of the manuscript by hand — reviewed and corrected by the university's professors — with all such copies retained in the university library. A text that must be copied entirely, by every student, under supervision, does not go missing. That requirement is a preservation system disguised as a curriculum, and it is one of the reasons this seven-volume history outlived the libraries that burned.

An honest introduction owes one more thing, and this book's own scholarly readers supply it. Hussein al-Hamdani was the first modern scholar to recognize the historical importance of Idris's writings and to draw on them extensively. But those writings are not free of bias or inconsistency. An excess of partisanship, and a deep-seated animosity toward certain parties, sometimes leads to the suppression of truth, the distortion of facts, and the omission of events — a pattern that becomes visible when the account is compared against other historical sources. And the author does not clearly distinguish between Ismaili sources and sources hostile to Ismailism, nor does he identify them specifically. That is a real limitation and we will meet it in the volumes ahead.

There is also a matter of position that a listener should know from the start. Idris's account of the origins of Tayyibi Ismailism diverges from the narrative of the Hafizi Ismailis and of other later groups who recognized the later Fatimid caliphs as their imams after al-Amir, who died in 524, corresponding to 1130, and who did not continue as a distinct community after the fall of the Fatimid state in 567, corresponding to 1171. The same applies to his account of the Nizari–Musta'li schism, which reflects the official position of the Tayyibi Ismailis — the only Musta'li group to survive, in Yemen and elsewhere. That view the Nizari Ismaili tradition rejects insofar as it concerns the succession after al-Mustansir. The Nizaris uphold the right of Nizar, who died in 488, corresponding to 1095, al-Mustansir's eldest son, who had been designated heir apparent during his father's lifetime but was forcibly set aside in favour of his younger brother al-Musta'li, who died in 495, corresponding to 1101, by the Fatimid vizier al-Afdal.

None of that diminishes the work. It locates it. This is a history written from a particular place in a divided tradition, by a man who held office in that tradition, using an archive that tradition had rescued. Read that way, its partisanship is not a defect to be apologized for but a piece of evidence in its own right: it tells us what the Tayyibi mission of fifteenth-century Yemen believed about its own past, in its own words, at a moment when almost no other Ismaili voice was still audible.

## What this episode lands

Three things are now in place, and they will carry through the whole series.

The first is why this book is rare. A community that spent most of its history practising concealment did not write chronicles; the two periods when it did write them ended with its archives systematically destroyed, in Cairo in 1171 and in Persia in 1256. What filled the gap was the testimony of its opponents, much of it fabricated, and that testimony held the field until the manuscripts themselves were recovered in the twentieth century.

The second is why this particular man could write it. Idris Imad al-Din was the nineteenth absolute da'i of the Tayyibi mission in Yemen, and the office carried custody of a library that had travelled out of Fatimid Cairo before the burning and had been safeguarded in Yemen for three centuries afterward. He was reading sources that no longer exist anywhere else. Much of what we will hear in the volumes ahead survives only because it passed through his hands and into his pages.

The third is how to listen. The seven parts are called sevens for a reason, and the shape is doctrinal. The sources behind them are a mixture the author does not always label. The partisanship is real and has been named by the scholars who value the work most. So we read it as what it is: the one surviving comprehensive account of the Imams from inside the tradition, written by a leader of that tradition, out of an archive that only just survived.

With that settled, we can open the first of the sevens. It begins where the author himself chooses to begin — not with the Prophet's birth, but with the purpose of the work and the line of descent that leads to it.
