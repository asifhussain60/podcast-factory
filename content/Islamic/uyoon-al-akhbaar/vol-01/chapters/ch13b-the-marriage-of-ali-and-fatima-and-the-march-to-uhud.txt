## Where this episode opens

Between a battle won and a battle lost, this history stops to count the dishes at a wedding. One ram, some fat, some flour, some dates — and sixteen hundred people fed from it, counted as they ate.

That is where this episode starts, and the placement is the author's, not a convenience. He sets the marriage of Ali ibn Abi Talib to Fatima immediately after Badr, notes that some hold it was after Uhud instead, tells you which of the two views has the weight of numbers behind it, and then marches straight out to the second battle. So the wedding sits in the seam. On one side of it is the victory that made the community's reputation; on the other is the defeat that nearly ended it.

Three movements. First the marriage — how it was proposed, who proposed it, what the dowry was and which items of it the bridegroom was ordered to keep. Then the road to Uhud: an enemy war chest, a war council with the women brought along on purpose, a dream that named the price in advance, and a vote that overruled the Prophet's own military judgment. Then the battle, which the Muslims won for most of an afternoon and lost in about the time it takes fifty men to walk off a hill.

Listen for one thread running through all three. In this episode almost every disaster and every deliverance turns on somebody being told exactly what would happen and doing something else anyway.

## The wedding between two battles

After the affair of Badr came the marriage of the Father of Imams, Ali ibn Abi Talib, to Fatima, the daughter of the Messenger of God.

Both Abu Bakr and 'Umar had sought her hand, and the Messenger of God declined them both. He would hear her virtue mentioned and would say: Fatima is a branch of me.

The proposal, when it finally came, did not come from the family. A group of his companions talked among themselves and said: have you not noticed that the Messenger of God has held back from marrying his daughter Fatima to anyone?

'Ammar ibn Yasir said: we see no one more fitting for her than his cousin Ali ibn Abi Talib. He had not yet married. So 'Ammar, al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam and Sa'd ibn 'Ubadah agreed among themselves to raise it with him.

They came to him and 'Ammar said: we see no one who emigrated to Medina who was not granted a child after his emigration — except you.

They pressed him on the practical question, and 'Ammar put his own wealth at his disposal: tell me what you have in hand, and take from mine what you like. Ali answered that the matter was already provided for — what God granted me at Badr, he said, is what I set aside for this.

Then they said: if you would mention it to the Messenger of God — his daughter Fatima. We believe he has held back from marrying her to any of his companions, and we do not think he has left her for anyone else.

And Ali said: Fatima belongs to the Imams of God, and her affair is in the hand of God. My tongue turns around wishing to mention it to the Messenger of God, and I feel shy before him.

They said: and who is more deserving of it than you? We will mention it to the Messenger of God ourselves.

So they went back to him and put it to him. And at that point, the account says, Gabriel descended to the Prophet and said: God the Almighty commands you to respond to what they have asked of you — for I have married Fatima to Ali in the same hour that Eve was married to Adam, and this was in My judgment, and I had already made it known in what is better than My creation.

Sit with what that does to the story. Three companions have a conversation about a young man who has no children and no wife and can barely raise the subject himself, and they carry it to the Prophet as a suggestion. The answer they get is that the marriage was contracted before there was a Mecca to hold it in, at the hour the first marriage in creation was made. The delegation was not persuading anyone of anything. It was the last step in something already settled, and the settlement is dated — not to a decree in general, but to a specific hour, and to a decision already published somewhere better than the created world.

The Messenger of God smiled at this and said: God the Almighty has answered your request. Go, O 'Ammar, and bring me my son-in-law.

'Ammar says: so I came to Ali. He was bowing and prostrating, making supplication to God about Fatima. I said: O my lord, I bring you glad tidings that will heal your soul, delight your eyes, and fulfil the response of the Messenger of God.

He had been praying for exactly this while the answer was being given elsewhere. That is a small piece of narrative architecture and the author does not point at it.

So Ali came to the Messenger of God. When he entered, the Prophet said: O Ali, do you know what Sa'd and 'Ammar have said? He said: yes, O Messenger of God. And he said: O Ali, Gabriel has come and told me that God has given you Fatima, and has joined her to you in marriage, and has granted you a gift. What dowry do you have?

Ali said: I have set aside for that twenty ounces of gold and silver, two black slaves, a horse for battle, and a shield to face my enemy.

And the Messenger of God edited the list. O Ali, he said, do not let the horse go from your hand, for it is for riding and raiding, nor the two black slaves. Do not give up your horse, and do not surrender any of your weapons. But bring whatever gold and silver you have.

That is the hinge of the whole episode, and it is easy to walk past. A dowry is offered in four parts and cut down to the metal. Everything with a military use is ordered back into the bridegroom's hands — keep the horse, it is for riding and raiding; keep the weapons; hand over the coin. Within thirteen months, that horse and those weapons are on the field at Uhud, and the man holding them is the last one standing beside the Prophet.

Then the Messenger of God contracted the marriage. He gave orders and a ram was bought, with fat, flour and 'Ajwa dates, and food was prepared. Then he ordered a crier to go through the quarters of the city announcing that people should come to the Messenger of God. And they came.

Those who ate were counted, and they numbered one thousand six hundred, and the food remained as it was, not diminished in the least, by the blessing and grace of the Messenger of God.

Note that the number is a count, not an estimate — they were counted. This history's habit of arithmetic follows it indoors.

Then the drum. The Messenger of God heard the sound of a tambourine and said: what is this? Asma' bint Umays said: O Messenger of God, I wanted to bring joy to Fatima, for I feared she might say to herself, my mother has died and there is no one left to delight me.

The reason she gives is a dead mother. Khadijah's death was mourned in full earlier in this history, in the year that took her and Abu Talib both; here it surfaces as the thing a bride would be thinking on the one night nobody wants her thinking it, and as the reason a woman picked up a tambourine.

He said: O Asma', what are you saying? She said: I do not know what to say, O Messenger of God. And he said: say — we have come to you, we have come to you, so greet us — say the like of that, and do not say farewell. Then he said: may God bring you joy, O Asma', as I have brought joy to my son.

The correction is not to the drum. It is to the words of the wedding song: keep the greeting, drop the leave-taking. Nobody is being sent away tonight.

Then the Messenger of God gave instructions that Fatima be brought to Ali.

It is reported from Ibn 'Abbas that when Fatima was brought to Ali, the Messenger of God walked in front of her, with Gabriel on her right and Michael on her left, and seventy thousand angels went with her from behind, glorifying God the Almighty and sanctifying Him until the break of dawn.

Asma' bint Umays says: I was among those who accompanied Fatima to Ali. When she entered her house, the Messenger of God came forward until he entered upon her. He called for water, the name of God was invoked over it, he drank from it and she drank from it. Then he sprinkled water between Fatima's veil and her body, and likewise between Ali's garment and his body. Then he said: O God, protect the people of this house, bless them, and bestow Your blessings upon them wherever they may be. Then he said: may God reward Asma' and those like her with good.

That prayer is spoken over a single room on a wedding night, and it is spoken over the people of that house as a standing category — wherever they may be. This history has been building toward the existence of that household from its opening pages.

Anas ibn Malik asked his mother to describe Fatima, and she said: white as an egg, as though she were the moon on the night of its fullness, and as the sun when it emerges from behind clouds.

The virtues of Ali and Fatima are many and well known. It is narrated from the Prophet that when Ali married Fatima he said: today faith has been doubled and Islam has been made complete.

And then the dating, handled the way this author handles every disputed fact. The marriage of Ali took place after the affair of Badr; it has also been said that it was after the affair of Uhud, though the first is the view of the majority. He gives you both readings and tells you which way the weight falls, and he does not pretend to have settled it.

## The council and the march out

The battle of Uhud came about on a Saturday in the month of Shawwal — and it is worth saying at the outset that the account will twice call the day of the fighting a Sunday. Both are in the book. Neither is corrected.

It began with money. When Quraysh suffered their losses at Badr, Abu Sufyan ibn Harb stirred them up to make war and commanded them to seek revenge. O assembly of Quraysh, he said, Muhammad has left and killed your finest men — so support us with this wealth to make war against him. He meant the wealth that had been saved out of the caravan on the day of Badr.

They agreed to it, and in response God revealed: the disbelievers spend their wealth to turn people away from the path of God. They will continue to spend it, but then it will become a source of regret for them, and then they will be overcome. And those who disbelieve will be gathered to Hell. (chapter 8, verse 36)

The caravan that started the first war becomes the war chest for the second. The whole point of Badr, from Quraysh's side, had been to get that caravan through; it got through, and the profit on it is now being spent on the army.

So Quraysh gathered to fight. The people of the caravan and its surrounding districts joined them, and so did Kinanah and the people of Tihama who answered their call. They set out with their cavalry, their weapons and their allies — and they brought their women along with them to prevent the men from fleeing.

Read that clause again. The women are a military measure. An army that broke and ran at Badr is being kept in the field by placing its own households behind it, where the men will be seen.

Abu Sufyan, who commanded the army, went out with Hind bint 'Utbah. 'Ikrimah ibn Abi Jahl went out with his mother, Hakim bint al-Harith ibn Hisham ibn al-Mughirah. Al-Harith ibn Hisham ibn al-Mughirah went out with Fatima bint al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah — and that is the man the poet of Medina had publicly shamed for getting away from Badr alive. Hardly any woman of Quraysh stayed behind. They marched until they came down at a place of springs, on a mountain in the belly of the Sabkhah, beside a canal at the edge of the valley, facing the city.

When the Messenger of God was informed of their approach and the Muslims had come down, he told them: I have seen a vision. I saw a cow; I saw something in the flat of my sword; and I saw that I put my hand into a fortified coat of mail, and I interpreted that as the city.

Then he read the first two out. As for the cow, it represents people from among my companions who will be killed. And what I saw in the flat of my sword is a man from my household who will be killed.

He has told them, before anyone marches, that there will be many dead among the companions and one death inside his own family. The notch in the blade is a man of the house. Everything that happens on the field happens to people who have heard that.

Then his counsel. My view is that you remain in Medina and let them come to where they have encamped. If they hold their ground, they will be in an unfavourable position; and if they enter upon us, we will fight them inside it.

The opinion of 'Abd Allah ibn Ubayy ibn Salul agreed with the opinion of the Messenger of God — do not go out to them. That is the same man whose plea had lately been granted over Banu Qaynuqa', and it is the last time in this account he is on the right side of anything.

But some of them said: we should march out and fight them where our women and children cannot see us and be terrified. They refused to heed the words of the Messenger of God, so he withdrew from them in anger and ordered them to march out.

When they saw his displeasure they tried to take it back: O Messenger of God, we fear we have angered you against your own judgment — return, and do as you see fit.

And he said: once a prophet has armed himself for his community and taken up his weapon, he does not turn back until he has fought.

Both halves of that matter equally. The community overruled his military judgment, on the argument that a battle inside the town would be fought in front of the women — the mirror image of the reasoning Quraysh used to bring their own women along. And when they lost their nerve and offered him the decision back, it was already too late, because of a rule about arms once taken up. There was a window in which the thing could have been undone, and it closed while they were arguing.

He set out with a thousand of his companions. And when they had reached the ground between Medina and Uhud, 'Abd Allah ibn Ubayy ibn Salul withdrew with a third of the force, saying: he obeyed them and disobeyed me. I do not know why we are throwing ourselves to our deaths here, O people. He turned back, taking with him those of his people who were hypocrites and waverers.

His justification for leaving is that he had been right. He had agreed with the Prophet's plan; the plan was overruled; and he treats being overruled as release. A thousand men are now something over six hundred, and the enemy is three thousand.

Then a small thing happened that he chose to read well. He went on until he passed the quarter of the Banu Haritha, where a horse struck its sword against something and bolted. The Messenger of God took an omen from the sword and said: draw your sword — I see that swords will delight you today.

And he raised a sword and said: who will take this sword and give it its right?

Al-Zubayr said: I will, O Messenger of God.

Abu Dajanah al-Ansari asked a different question. And what is its right, O Messenger of God? He said: that you stand by him at the end of the ranks and strike the enemy with it until it bends. The man said: I will take it, O Messenger of God. And Abu Dajanah al-Ansari — Sammak ibn Kharsha, brother of the Banu Sa'ida — took it.

The sword went to the man who asked the price, not to the man who asked for the sword. Remember that al-Zubayr had asked first; he is going to spend the battle thinking about it.

Abu Dajanah brought out a red headband and tied it round his head, and the Ansar said: Abu Dajanah has put on his death-band — whenever he did that, this is what followed. Then he strode out swaggering between the two lines, reciting: I am the one my close friend made a promise to, and we are at the foot of the palm trees — shall I not rise in the days of battle? Strike with the sword of God and the Prophet.

And when the Messenger of God saw him walking like that he said: that is a gait God hates — except in a place such as this.

On the other side, Abu Sufyan was working on his standard-bearers, the Banu 'Abd al-Dar. You abandoned the standard on the day of Badr, he told them, and you brought us what you have seen. Either you take full charge of our standard, or leave it to us and let us attend to our own affair. They took him at his word and pledged themselves: tomorrow you will know whether we have fulfilled our obligation.

Then the ground. The Messenger of God advanced until the people came down at Uhud, positioning themselves in the valley with their backs to the mountain, and said: let no one among you fight until he is ordered to fight. Quraysh spread their cavalry and infantry through the cultivated fields running alongside a canal, and a man of the Ansar called out: are you going to let them trample the crops of the Banu Qila — why are we not fighting?

He arrayed seven hundred men for battle. And he placed 'Abd Allah ibn Jubayr, brother of the Banu 'Amr ibn 'Awf, in command of fifty archers, with an instruction that has to be quoted exactly: keep the horses away from us — let them not come at us from behind, whether the battle goes in our favour or against us. Do not leave your positions.

Whether it goes in our favour or against us. He has ruled out in advance the one circumstance in which they will actually leave.

He himself appeared between two shields. Quraysh were drawn up three thousand strong, with two hundred horses.

And Hind bint 'Utbah and the women with her beat their tambourines to drive the fighters on. She cried out among the polytheists: we are the daughters of Tariq, we walk upon the cushioned litters — if you advance, we embrace you, and spread soft carpets beneath you; but if you turn and flee, we part from you, a bitter parting, a parting that leaves no love behind, and shame upon you ever after. And she said: be steadfast, O Banu 'Abd al-Dar, protectors of those who guard the rear — strike with all the force of vengeance.

That is the device the women were brought for, spoken aloud. The battle-cry of the companions of the Messenger of God that day was: O Mansur, put them to death — meaning, be relentless and press on.

## The turn at Uhud

The people fought until the battle was fully joined, and Abu Dajanah fought until he was the wonder of everyone who watched.

One of the people watching was al-Zubayr, and he says why. I resented the fact that I had asked the Messenger of God for the sword and he gave it to Abu Dajanah instead of me — and I am the son of Safiyya, his aunt, and I am of Quraysh. By God, let me watch what Abu Dajanah does. So I followed him.

He met no one without killing him. And among the polytheists there was a man who spared no wounded Muslim — he finished off every one he came across. I prayed to God to bring the two of them together, al-Zubayr says, and they met. They traded two blows: the polytheist struck Abu Dajanah, who caught it on his shield, and the sword bit into it; then Abu Dajanah struck the polytheist and killed him.

Then I saw him raise the sword over the head of Hind bint 'Utbah — and he held back, and pulled away.

Afterwards Abu Dajanah explained it: I deemed it beneath the honour of the sword of the Messenger of God to use it on a woman. The woman whose chanting was holding the enemy line together was inside his reach, and the thing that saved her was the reputation of the blade.

Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib was fighting elsewhere in the line. He killed Arta'ah ibn 'Abd Sharhabil ibn Hashim ibn 'Abd Manaf ibn 'Abd al-Dar, one of the standard-bearers. Then Saba' ibn 'Abd al-'Azza al-Ghubshani, who was called Abu Nayyar, passed by him, and Hamza called out to him: come to me, O son of the one who mutilates eyes — his mother having practised that mutilation. They met, and Hamza struck him and killed him.

The fighting raged, and Ali and Hamza wrought havoc among the polytheists.

Then the standard-bearer of the polytheists, Talha ibn Abi Talha, called out across the ground: O companions of Muhammad! You claim that whoever among us is killed goes to the Fire, and whoever among you is killed goes to Paradise. Which of you will come forward to hasten me to the Fire and hasten himself to Paradise?

Ali ibn Abi Talib stepped forward and said: by God, I will not leave you until I send you to the Fire, God willing.

They clashed, and when the shield slipped from Talha's leg, Ali struck him with the sword and severed the leg, and stood over him meaning to finish him. But Talha called upon God and pleaded for mercy. And Ali said: when he invoked God and pleaded for mercy, I was too ashamed to kill him, so I left him — knowing he would not survive. Talha died of that blow.

He was challenged inside a theological wager and answered inside its terms, word for word. And then the sparing is reported without any softening: it was not clemency about the man's life, because the man was already dead on his feet, and Ali says so plainly. What he would not do was strike a man in the act of calling on God.

After Talha the standard went to Abu Sa'id ibn Abi Talha, and Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas killed him. Then 'Uthman ibn Abi Talha took it, and Hamza killed him. Then Musafi' ibn Talha took it, and 'Asim ibn Thabit ibn Abi al-Aqla killed him. The pledge the Banu 'Abd al-Dar had given Abu Sufyan the night before was being paid out one brother at a time.

And the Muslims were winning. They struck the polytheists with killing blows and left them wounded and routed.

Then the battle turned, and here is the whole of why. When the polytheists saw their own defeat, the archers whom the Messenger of God had ordered to hold their positions abandoned them, chasing the enemy after the spoils, and saying: we missed the plunder on the day of Badr. When the archers had left the Messenger of God exposed, Abu Sufyan, Khalid ibn al-Walid and the polytheists seized the moment.

The reason fifty men walked off the only ground that mattered is a bookkeeping grievance from the previous battle. They had been given an order framed to cover this exact situation — whether it goes in our favour or against us — and they left because they felt short-changed thirteen months earlier.

What came through the gap took Hamza first.

Wahshi, the slave of Jubayr ibn Mut'im, had been watching him roar through the battle like a lion; no polytheist who came near him was left alive. And Jubayr had told Wahshi: if you kill Hamza, the uncle of Muhammad, in exchange for my uncle Tu'ayma ibn 'Adi, you are free. Hind, for her part, had promised Wahshi a reward for the same killing.

So Wahshi lay in wait for him behind a tree. Just then Saba' ibn 'Abd al-'Azza came at Hamza, and Hamza leapt on him and killed him — and in that moment Wahshi hurled his spear, and it struck Hamza in the lower body. Hamza moved toward him, but he was overcome, and he fell.

There were two prices on that man: a slave's freedom, offered life for life by a nephew of the dead at Badr, and a woman's private reward. Neither was offered by the army's commander. The killing of the Prophet's uncle was arranged as a transaction between individuals, and it was carried out from behind a tree by a man buying himself out of slavery.

The polytheists also killed Mus'ab ibn 'Umayr, as he was holding the banner of the Ansar for the Messenger of God.

Hanzala ibn Abi 'Amir met Abu Sufyan himself and was raising his sword to cut him down when Shaddad ibn al-Aswad, a man of the Shu'ub, killed him before he could land the blow. Hanzala was called al-Ghusl — the washed one — because the Messenger of God said: I saw the angels washing Hanzala ibn Abi 'Amir. He had gone out to fight without having purified himself.

And when Hanzala was killed, Abu Sufyan cried out: up, O Hubal! And he called: Hanzala for Hanzala — meaning his own son, who had been killed at Badr by Ali ibn Abi Talib. The Messenger of God answered him: God is most high and most mighty. Are our slain in Paradise while your slain are in the Fire?

That exchange is the two theologies of the day shouted across a field: one man settling a ledger between two men with the same name, the other refusing the ledger and naming the two destinations instead.

Then the standard again. When the standard of the polytheists fell with its bearer killed, 'Umra bint 'Alqama al-Harithiyya took it up and raised it again for Quraysh. And after her it passed to a slave of Quraysh, who fought on with it until he was killed, crying out: O God, have I acquitted myself — meaning, have I been excused?

Hassan ibn Thabit answered both of them in verse, and the verse is damaged; what survives of the first is that a standard was boasted of and it was a vile boast, that they placed their pride in a slave and that is no right thing, that they supposed they could sell red shame in Mecca, and that when the swords met them the boaster admitted his hands were bound. And of the second, on 'Umra raising the standard: grief from blows raining in from every side, stab upon stab, and were it not for the brigade of the Haritha they would have come to ruin.

Notice what Quraysh's standard was carried by once its men were dead: a woman, and then a slave. Those are precisely the two categories their own code of honour excluded from the field. And the poet of Medina attacks them for it rather than crediting either — you placed your pride in a slave. The slave died asking whether he had done enough to be excused.

Meanwhile, with the archers gone, the polytheists rallied and the Muslims were routed. And a voice cried out — the voice of Satan, from whom we seek refuge in God — Muhammad has been killed.

That sentence did more damage than the cavalry. When 'Amr was routed, Anas ibn al-Nadir met him and was told that the Messenger of God had been killed. And Anas said: what use is life to you after him? Go back and die for what the Messenger of God and his family died for. Then he turned toward the enemy and fought on until he was killed, God have mercy on him.

He is acting on the report as though it were true, and drawing the only conclusion he can see in it.

Meanwhile the Messenger of God had been left very nearly alone. The polytheists cut down those around him and wounded him: they broke his lower right tooth, cut his lip, shattered the egg of his helmet on his head, and landed blows of great severity on him. He had been standing between two shields.

Ali came to him. The Messenger of God stumbled into a pit, and Ali took his hand and helped him to his feet. He stood up on a rock, and Ali stayed alone at his side.

When the people kept pressing in, the Messenger of God said: go forward, O Ali.

And he replied: where shall I go, O Messenger of God? I will not turn back a disbeliever after having become a Muslim.

He is not refusing an order to attack. He is refusing to read it as permission to leave. And so when the polytheists came at them, the Messenger of God turned him on them instead: bear down on these others, O Ali. And Ali would drive back whoever came at them, inflicting losses on them before they withdrew. He was counted among the bravest of those present.

The last account of the day comes from Abu Rafi' — the same freedman whose eyes we borrowed in Mecca on the morning the news of Badr arrived. On a Sunday, he says, when the battle was at its fiercest and the polytheists were pressing hard against the Messenger of God, Ali ibn Abi Talib stood beside him. The Messenger of God said: go, O Ali. Ali replied: where shall I go while I am with you?

At that moment Ali spotted a battalion advancing, and the Messenger of God said to him: O Ali, charge that battalion. So he charged it and broke through it, killing Hisham ibn Umayya al-Makhzumi, and drove it off. Then a second battalion came forward, and the Prophet said: charge it. So he charged it, and killed 'Amr ibn 'Abd Allah al-Jumahi, and scattered it. Then a third battalion advanced, and the Prophet said: charge it. And he charged that one too.

There was no young man like Ali on the day of Uhud.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry forward, and the battle itself does not finish here — the account breaks off with the Prophet standing on a rock, the field still in confusion, and one man being sent at a third battalion.

The first is that the wedding and the war are one story, and the dowry proves it. The marriage is presented as ratification of something contracted at the hour Adam and Eve were joined, announced to a Prophet who had already turned down two of his closest companions for the same hand. And then, in the middle of that, the practical instruction: keep the horse, keep the weapons, hand over only the gold. Thirteen months later the horse and the weapons are what is left standing beside him on the field.

The second is that Uhud was lost by men who had been told. The Prophet named the cost in advance — many companions dead, and a man of his own household — and named the safer plan, and was outvoted. The archers were given an order written to cover the precise moment they broke it. The one man whose judgment matched the Prophet's took a third of the army home on the grounds that he had been ignored. And the thing that turned a rout into a collapse was not a charge but a sentence: a voice shouting that the Prophet was dead, and at least one man believing it and going out to die on the strength of it.

The third is that this day sorts everybody by what they did in about an hour. Abu Dajanah asked what a sword's right was before he took it, and then would not use it on a woman. Hamza was killed for a slave's manumission and a woman's private bounty, from behind a tree. Quraysh's standard ended in the hands of a woman and then a slave, and the slave died asking whether he had been excused. And when the Prophet was left almost alone, wounded in the mouth and the head, standing on a rock, the man who would not treat an order to advance as leave to withdraw was the one who had married his daughter and been told to keep his weapons.
