## Where this episode opens

Which is the harder sentence for an army to hear at the foot of a wall it has been battering for three weeks — that the wall will not fall, or that permission to bring it down was never given?

The previous episode closed with the captives and the collected herds of Hunayn held under guard at al-Ji'ranah, waiting on a decision nobody had yet made. This one begins with the army marching straight past that camp and up into the highlands, to the walled city the defeated had run to, and it ends with the same guarded herds being counted out into named hands while a man in the crowd tells the Messenger of God to his face that the count is dishonest.

Three movements. First the siege of Ta'if: a road with a mosque built on it, the first killing in Islam brought before a judge, a catapult, a covered ram, a burning iron beam, twenty nights, and a withdrawal ordered on the grounds of permission rather than of failure. Then a long private conversation held in the middle of that siege, in full view of an army with nothing to do, and the argument this history builds out of it — an argument that runs through a verse of charity that only one man is recorded as having obeyed. Then al-Ji'ranah, where six thousand captives are handed back for nothing and a hundred camels at a time are handed out to men who fought on the other side a month earlier, and where the difference between those two transactions is stated aloud.

Listen for the arithmetic of the giving. Almost everything contested in this episode turns on the gap between what a thing is worth and what it is being used to buy.

## The twenty nights at Ta'if

Before the march begins the poets are still working over the battle that ended it. Malik ibn 'Awf al-Nasri, who had brought the families to Awtas and lost them, composed lines excusing his own flight, and they have reached this manuscript in the condition that most of this stretch's verse has reached it — whole passages opaque, names collapsed, single images floating loose. Only what still reads is given here. He speaks of stretches of road that prevent sleep, of a battalion he clothed in armour, of being abandoned by those he fought beside — one and another are not equal — and of bracing himself for the weight of the spears.

Verses were also recounted among Hawazin about their own journey to the Messenger of God with Malik after his acceptance of Islam, and out of a badly damaged poem a few lines still stand: that they had strength and bodies and resolve, that they met hardship when hardship met them, that the people scattered until none could be seen — and then a line that concedes the whole case, that Jibril descended with their victory. A second fragment adds the counter-boast from the other side: from us, and even if it were not Jibril fighting beside us, our swords are liberation.

Then came the Battle of Ta'if.

The Messenger of God marched to Ta'if after returning from Hunayn. Thaqif, on the arrival of the Prophet at Ta'if, shut the city gates against him and prepared their fortifications for battle.

Ka'b ibn Malik composed verses when the Messenger of God assembled his forces for the march. His poem is long and mostly unreadable now, but its offer survives intact and it is the clearest statement in the stretch of what a besieger was proposing: in the courtyard of your house stand thousands of us; obey us and obey God, and make yourself a support and a refuge for us; if they offer us peace, we will accept, and we show no weakness or trembling; but if you refuse, we will fight you with patience until you submit. The rest is fragments — horses eager for battle, a lined wall surrounded, and the closing claim that the religion is moderate and true.

Shaddad ibn 'Arid al-Jashmi said, commemorating the journey to Ta'if, lines of which three still read: how can he be aided who finds no victory; do not support al-Lat, for God will destroy it; and, addressed to the goddess herself, when will the Prophet come down to your land?

That last line is worth holding on to. It is asked here as a taunt, at the start of a siege that is about to fail, and this history will answer it before the source chapter after next is finished.

The Messenger of God marched along the Palm of Yemen, then along a pass, then along the salt flats, then across the sea of foam from Layyah, where he built a mosque — one that remained a seasonal landmark.

At that time a man from Banu Layth who had killed a man from Hudhayl was brought before him, and this was the first blood case adjudicated in Islam.

One sentence, no verdict recorded, and it is easy to read past. A homicide between two Arab clans would ordinarily have been settled by the clans themselves, by retaliation or by a negotiated payment, and the settlement would have belonged to them. Here it is brought to a man on the march, in the open, between one halt and the next, and the book marks it as a first. What changed is not the killing and not the penalty. It is the address: the case now goes somewhere.

The Messenger of God ordered the fortress of Malik ibn 'Awf to be demolished, and the march continued until the army descended near a Sidra tree. There was a property there belonging to a man of Thaqif, and the Messenger of God sent word to him: either surrender, or we will tear down your wall. He refused to accept Islam, and so the Messenger of God ordered his wall to be destroyed.

The Messenger of God then advanced until he camped near Ta'if. He positioned his army there, and men who were with him fell, because the soldiers of Thaqif shot down from the wall of Ta'if and their arrows reached the camp. When the fallen were among them, he moved his army back to where his mosque in Ta'if stands today.

The Messenger of God besieged them for twenty nights — some say seventeen.

The Messenger of God had two of his wives with him. One of them, Umm Salamah, was sheltered between two tent poles, and he prayed between the two intervals. When Thaqif later embraced Islam they built a mosque over the place where the Messenger of God had prayed.

That detail is dated by its own tail. The people building the mosque are the people behind the wall, and they are building it over the spot where the man outside the wall put his forehead down while their arrows were coming over. The book records the prayer and the monument in the same breath, and the interval between them is the whole outcome of this campaign, deferred.

The Messenger of God fought fierce battles against them throughout the days of the siege, and they found themselves worn down by it.

The Messenger of God ordered a catapult to be constructed, and stones were hurled at the people of Ta'if. When the stones began to pile up and break through part of the wall, a group of the Companions of the Messenger of God crept beneath a protective tank and pushed it toward the wall of Ta'if in order to breach it. Thaqif responded by hurling down upon them an iron beam wrapped in fire. The men scrambled out from beneath the tank, and Thaqif killed a number of them. The Messenger of God then commanded, alongside his forces, that the grape vineyards of Thaqif be cut down, and the Muslims carried this out.

This is the most technical passage of warfare in the volume, and it is a catalogue of siege engineering met and beaten at every point. A catapult opens a gap. A covered ram is walked up to the gap. The defenders drop a heated iron bar onto the roof of the shelter, the shelter empties, and the men who ran out of it are killed in the open. Then the vines come down, which is not an assault at all but the standard pressure applied to a city that cannot be entered: destroy the year's income and wait.

The Messenger of God said, while the siege still lay heavy upon him: Permission has not been granted to conquer Ta'if. When this became known to the Muslims, they questioned him about it, and he confirmed it. They said: O Messenger of God, then give the order to withdraw. And so the Messenger of God commanded the herald to call it out, and the call for withdrawal was made.

Take the shape of that seriously. The army does not lose heart and it is not driven off; the wall is already cracked. What is announced is that the objective has not been authorised, and the army's own response is to ask for the order to leave. In a history that has spent this volume recording who ran and who stood, here is the one withdrawal that is not a rout and carries no reproach at all, and the reason given for it is not military.

'Uyaynah ibn Husn al-Fazari said: Thaqif is a glorious and honourable people. Some of the Muslims said to him: You praise the polytheists, and you came here to support the Messenger of God. He replied: I did not come to fight Thaqif alongside you. What I wanted was for Muhammad to open Ta'if, so that Thaqif might give up a slave girl whom I could take, that she might bear me a son — for they are a people of noble stock.

He is answering a charge of disloyalty by admitting something much worse and not appearing to notice. He is not a sympathiser. He joined a siege in the hope that it would succeed, so that he could select a woman out of the wreckage for breeding purposes, on the grounds that the stock is good. Remember him: he reappears twice before this episode ends, and both times money is involved.

While the Messenger of God was besieging Ta'if, some of the enslaved people came down out of the city to him, and the Messenger of God freed them.

Set that against everything else on the ledger of these twenty nights. Twelve Muslims were killed — seven from Quraysh, four from the Ansar, and one from Banu Layth. A catapult was built, a wall was cracked, an orchard was destroyed, and the city was not taken. The only thing the siege produced that lasted is a number of people who walked out of a besieged city on their own feet and were let go free at the gate of the camp that was besieging it.

After the Messenger of God departed from Ta'if, Bujayr ibn Zuhayr composed verses recalling Hunayn and Ta'if together. They too survive badly, and the legible remainder is mostly the shape of the two engagements laid side by side: Awtas and the day of lightning, a wound in the heart of Hunayn; Hawazin gathered and then scattered like a torn bird; and then the change of ground — except their wall and the belly of the trench, they did not hold us back from a single position; so they fortified themselves behind a closed door, and we were left to draw them out.

In the open field nothing held; against a shut door everything did.

The Messenger of God departed from Ta'if, and it was said to him: Pray against Thaqif, O Messenger of God.

He said: O God, guide Thaqif and bring them to us.

## The long private counsel

In the middle of those twenty nights, with an army camped in front of a wall it could not enter and very little to do between assaults, something happened in full view that this history treats as more consequential than the siege around it.

Isma'il ibn Aban narrated, from Jabir ibn 'Abd Allah al-Ansari, who said: The Messenger of God withdrew into his chamber while he was besieging Ta'if, and he prolonged his private counsel with Ali, while the people looked on. Many envied me for what he had singled him out with.

Note the grammar of that last sentence as the narrator gives it, because it is the sentence of a man reporting a crowd's mood and losing hold of his own position inside it. The envy is general. It is the length of the conversation that is the provocation — not that a consultation happened, but that it went on, and on, with an army watching the flap of the tent.

Abu Bakr and 'Umar said: O Messenger of God, you have prolonged your private counsel with Ali today.

The Messenger of God said: It was not I who chose him — it was God who chose him.

The answer refuses the premise of the complaint. The complaint assumes a decision was made by the man being complained to, which is why it is put to him. The reply says the selection is not his and therefore the objection has no addressee. Nothing is said about the content of the counsel, then or later.

All the people came to know the distinction that the Messenger of God and his God had accorded to his guardian Ali ibn Abi Talib, drawing him close above all other Companions, such that none among them had been granted his favour during the lifetime of the Messenger of God.

The book then does something it has done only rarely in this volume: it stops narrating and argues. It reaches for a verse, and for the one recorded case of anyone obeying it.

In the chain of Abu Ghassan, from Ali, he said: When God revealed, O you who believe, when you consult the Messenger in private, offer charity before your consultation (chapter 58, verse 12), Ali said: I possessed a dinar, which I exchanged for ten dirhams. Whenever I wished to consult the Messenger of God, I would give a dirham in charity before doing so, until it was spent. No other Muslim did this. Then God revealed: Are you fearful of having to give charity before your consultation? Then when you do not, and God has pardoned you (chapter 58, verse 12), to the end of the verse. No one fulfilled the verse of private counsel other than me.

This is agreed upon among Muslims, and both al-Zamakhshari and al-Tha'labi mention in their commentary on this verse that no one among the Muslims acted upon the verse of private counsel except Ali ibn Abi Talib.

Look at what the verse actually asks and how small the sum is. A dinar broken into ten dirhams is not a fortune; it is pocket money, deliberately so, because the commandment is a filter rather than a tax. It puts a price — any price — on access, and it stands only until it is withdrawn. The whole recorded history of that commandment being kept, in this account, is one man spending ten coins, one consultation at a time, until the coins run out. Then the requirement is lifted, and the lifting is quoted here too, so the reader can see that the window was brief and that nobody else got through it while it was open.

His virtues are many and well known, and those who deny his virtue have rejected most of them. Neither Abu Bakr, nor 'Umar, nor any other Companion is cited in connection with the verse of private counsel, nor is there any mention of them in it.

Ali ibn Abi Talib said: The Messenger of God placed his mouth to my ear and opened to me a thousand doors of knowledge, and from each of those doors a thousand more doors were opened to me.

None of the Companions received anything like this.

Then the book sets two exhibits beside that claim, and the way it sets them is worth watching, because it does not caricature either man.

The first is a sentence of Abu Bakr's, quoted flatly and without comment: I am your guardian, and I am not the best among you.

The second is longer and is a scene rather than a saying. 'Umar said: Do not exaggerate in the dowries of women — the most the Messenger of God ever gave to any of his wives was ten ounces.

A woman who was not among the most learned stood up and said to him: O Commander of the Faithful, have you not prevented us from a right that God has obligated for us? He said: And if you wish to take one wife in place of another, and you have given one of them a heap of gold, take nothing from it (chapter 4, verse 20), to the end of the verse.

'Umar ibn al-Khattab felt ashamed and turned to those around him, saying: You hear such a thing from me and do not object — until a woman who knows little corrects me?

Read that scene on its own terms first, because it is more interesting than the use the book makes of it. A ruling is announced from the pulpit capping what a bride may be given. A woman with no scholarly standing stands up and quotes the verse that speaks of giving a wife a heap of gold and forbids taking any of it back, and the ruling collapses on the spot. What 'Umar says next is not a defence and not an apology to her. It is a rebuke aimed at the men around him, and its content is that they let it get that far. He is the source of the report of his own correction, which is why the report exists at all.

The book's point in placing it here is narrower. Its argument is about who was equipped to answer a question of law without being corrected, and it has just quoted a man who says a thousand doors were opened to him and, beside him, a man overruled from the floor by someone he himself describes as knowing little.

Many such instances could be cited had we not already exceeded what we set out to recount, but they are mentioned here in connection with the private counsel of the Messenger of God with his cousin, and his distinction above others. The denial of those who deny this amounts to a challenge to the Prophet himself, in that they dare to prefer over him one whom he preferred.

That is the author closing his own digression and naming its purpose. It is also, in a volume that spends most of its length reporting marches and raids in the third person, one of the few places where the writer steps forward and tells you what he is doing and why he stopped.

## The division at al-Ji'ranah

When the Messenger of God reached al-Ji'ranah, the people of Hawazin came to him and asked him to return their wives and children. They said: O Messenger of God, among the captives are your aunts, your maternal relations, and the women who nursed and raised you. Even if we were al-Harith ibn Abi Shimr or al-Nu'man ibn al-Mundhir, and he then came down to us with what he had received, we would beg for his generosity — and you are the best of those who are generous. We have come to you as Muslims.

Every clause of that speech is doing work. They open on kinship, which is a claim on him personally rather than on his office. They name two kings of the border states as the harshest imaginable creditors and say they would still have appealed to them, which concedes that they have no rights here and are asking anyway. And they close by announcing a conversion, which changes who they are but does not change the ownership of anything already taken.

The Messenger of God said: Which is dearer to you — your wealth, or your women and children?

They replied: O Messenger of God, you have given us a choice between our wealth and our lineage. Our wives and our children are dearer to us.

Hawazin had been taken captive to the number of six thousand, comprising women and children, with camels and sheep beyond counting.

The Messenger of God said: As for what belongs to me and to the family of 'Abd al-Muttalib, it is yours. And when I lead the people in the noon prayer, stand and say: We ask the intercession of the Messenger of God with the Muslims, and of the Muslims with the Messenger of God, for our women and our children.

The people stood and said what he had commanded them, and the Messenger of God said: Whatever belonged to me and to the sons of 'Abd al-Muttalib is yours.

The Muhajirun and the Ansar said: Whatever belonged to us is for the Messenger of God.

Al-Aqra' ibn Habis said: As for me and Banu Tamim, no. 'Uyaynah ibn Husn said: As for me and Banu Fazarah, no. 'Abbas ibn Mardas said: As for me and Banu Sulaym, no. But the Banu Sulaym said: Yes — whatever was ours belongs to the Messenger of God. At that, 'Abbas ibn Mardas said to his own people of Banu Sulaym: And you are content with this?

The procedure is the interesting part. He does not order the captives released, and he is not asked to. He gives away his own share and his clan's outright, and then he coaches the petitioners in exactly what to say, at the exact moment to say it, in front of the assembled army after the noon prayer — so that the request is public, addressed to everyone with a stake, and framed as a mutual intercession rather than as a confiscation. Then he lets the answer come back tribe by tribe. Three commanders refuse in a row, in identical form, and one of them is immediately overruled by his own men in public, which is the only reversal of the day and is done to him rather than by him.

The Messenger of God said: Whoever holds fast to his share of these captives shall receive six shares from the first spoils I acquire as compensation.

The people had no wives or children among the captives except 'Uyaynah ibn Husn, who had taken an old woman. He said: I count her of noble lineage in her clan, and perhaps a great ransom will come for her.

Abu Surad Zuhayr ibn Surad said to him: Give her up — by God, her breast gives no milk, her belly bears no child, her husband is gone, and her youth is spent.

So he returned her in exchange for six shares of compensation, once Zuhayr had said what he said. It is claimed that 'Uyaynah later regretted it.

Here is the man from the wall of Ta'if again, and the shape of him has not changed at all. He held a woman not as a captive but as an asset with an expected yield, and he let her go only when someone itemised, in four clauses, why the yield would never arrive. And then he regretted the trade. Zuhayr's speech is brutal and it is aimed with precision at the only faculty in 'Uyaynah that responds.

Al-Aqra' ibn Habis complained about the arrangement and said: By God, you have not given to a man of standing, nor to one of influence.

The Messenger of God said: Tell Malik that if he comes to me as a Muslim, his family and his wealth will be returned to him, and I will give him a hundred camels.

Malik did so. He came out from Ta'if — he had feared that the people of Ta'if would learn of it and detain him, so he slipped away under cover of night — and he came to the Messenger of God and met him at al-Ji'ranah, or at Mecca, and there he submitted. The Messenger of God restored his family and his wealth to him and gave him a hundred camels.

Malik ibn 'Awf said, upon becoming Muslim, verses of which the opening survives clearly: Who among all people is like Muhammad? Never have I seen or heard of anyone like him. The rest is broken — a man who delivers and gives to the lowly, a polished spear striking every keen blade, scribes who could not count their ranks, and a figure standing watch in the midst of confusion as though guarding his cubs.

The Messenger of God then appointed Malik over those of his people who had accepted Islam, from the surrounding tribes. These tribes had caused persistent trouble, and he fought them hard, never ceasing to raid them until they were subdued.

That is the same commander who a month earlier had massed Hawazin and Thaqif, brought the families to Awtas against advice, and lost everything. He is bought back with the return of his household and a hundred camels, and then handed a command against the tribes he used to lead. Verses of Abu Muhajin ibn Habib al-Thaqafi survive from the other side of that turn, damaged but plain enough in their grievance: that a people broke their covenant and violated what was sacred, that we were the first to rebuke them, and still they came against our homes.

When the Messenger of God had finished returning the captives, he rode on. The people called out: O Messenger of God, divide among us the spoils — the camels and the sheep. They pressed him so urgently that his robe was pulled from him as he was backed against a tree.

He said: Return my robe to me, O people. By God, if you had as many camels as the trees of Tihamah, I would divide them all among you, and you would not find me miserly, cowardly, or a liar.

He then went to a camel, took a tuft of hair from its hump, held it up between his fingers and said: O people, by God, I have nothing of your spoils — not even this thread — that is not returned to you. So hand over even the needle and thread, for those who steal from the spoils will carry their shame before their families on the Day of Resurrection.

The three words he denies of himself are chosen against the three things a crowd in that position suspects. Miserly answers the charge of holding back; cowardly answers the suspicion that he is yielding to pressure; a liar answers the possibility that the whole accounting is false. And the demonstration he gives is deliberately the smallest object on the field — a single hair pinched off a camel's hump and held up so it can be seen — because the rule he is stating has to hold at that size or it holds nowhere.

It is related that 'Aqil ibn Abi Talib came home on the day of Hunayn to his wife Fatimah bint Shaybah ibn Rabi'ah, his sword still stained with blood. She said: I know you fought bravely — so what did you bring from the spoils of the polytheists? He said: I have nothing but this needle. Then he heard the Messenger of God proclaim that whoever had taken anything must return it, and that even a needle and thread must be given back. 'Aqil said to his wife: I think you have lost your needle. He then took it from her and went and threw it back into the spoils.

The story is comic and it is the proof of the rule. A man comes home from a battle with one needle, gives it to his wife, hears the announcement, and takes it back off her with a line that is almost an apology. The book keeps it because the announcement would be a slogan without it.

Then the other transaction. The Messenger of God gave generously to those whose hearts were to be won over — people who had submitted out of worldly desire and ambition, and whom he hoped to draw closer by winning them with gifts.

He gave Abu Sufyan a hundred camels, and his son Mu'awiyah a hundred camels. Hakim ibn Hizam received a hundred, and a son of al-Harith ibn Kaldah received a hundred. A man from Banu 'Abd al-Dar also received a hundred; it was said he was Nusayr ibn al-Harith. Al-Harith ibn Hisham received a hundred, Suhayl ibn 'Amr a hundred, Huwaytib ibn 'Abd al-'Uzza a hundred, and al-'Ala' ibn Jariyah al-Thaqafi, an ally of Banu Zahra, a hundred. 'Uyaynah ibn Husn received a hundred, al-Aqra' ibn Habis al-Tamimi a hundred, Malik ibn 'Awf al-Nasri a hundred, and Safwan ibn Umayyah a hundred. Men of Quraysh below that rank received less.

Run down that list once for what it is. Nearly every name on it commanded, financed, or spoke for the other side within the last two years, and several within the last two months. The man who conceded at the bend of the pass gets a hundred and so does his son. The man who lent a hundred shields under a guarantee gets a hundred camels. The two commanders who refused to give up their share of the captives are on it. So is the enemy general.

'Abbas ibn Mardas received less than a hundred and was aggrieved. He reproached the Messenger of God in verses that survive in poor condition, though the complaint in them is unmistakable: between 'Uyaynah and al-Aqra' I have grown weary while my game was plundered; I was given nothing, nor was I withheld, yet I was present in the war; they have surpassed my elder in the assembly, though he was neither a stronghold nor a prisoner; whoever is diminished today will not rise again.

The Prophet said: Go to him and silence his tongue with gifts. So they gave him until he was satisfied.

The instruction is not disguised. The poet's complaint is that the market rate was misapplied to him, and the remedy applied is to pay the market rate. The same phrase would be an accusation in an enemy's mouth; here it is the policy, stated in four words by the man carrying it out.

It was said to the Messenger of God: Al-Aqra' ibn Habis was given a hundred camels, and 'Uyaynah ibn Husn was given a hundred camels, while Ja'il ibn Suraqah al-Damri was passed over.

The Messenger of God said: By Him in whose hand is the soul of Muhammad, Ja'il ibn Suraqah is better than the whole earth filled with the likes of 'Uyaynah ibn Husn and al-Aqra' ibn Habis. But I gave to those two in order to draw them toward surrender, while I entrusted Ja'il ibn Suraqah to his Islam.

That is the whole policy stated once, and it inverts the ledger completely. The hundred camels are not a reward and they are not a ranking; they are the price of a man who has to be bought. The man who is given nothing is given nothing because he does not have to be bought, and the sentence says so in the plainest available terms: I entrusted him to his Islam. Every name on the list above is, on this accounting, a purchase; the one name off the list is the only one described as being trusted.

Dhu al-Khuwaysirah, who was from Banu Tamim, came to the Messenger of God while he was distributing the spoils among the people and said: O Muhammad, you have not been just in what you have divided.

The Messenger of God grew angry and said: Woe to you — if I am not just, then who is?

The Messenger of God then said: From his followers will come a group who immerse themselves so deeply in religion that they pass right through it, just as an arrow passes clean through its target — nothing remains on the arrowhead, nothing on the shaft, nothing on the fletching. They will have gone beyond even the blood.

The Messenger of God said: They are the worst of creation, and the best of creation will be those who kill them.

And those are the Khawarij who rose up against the Commander of the Faithful, and whom he killed at Nahrawan.

The image repays slowing down. An arrow that passes clean through a body is not stopped by it and takes nothing away with it. The shaft is dry; the head is dry; even the feathers at the back come out clean, and the last clause insists on it — they will have gone beyond even the blood. What is being described is not a weak passage through religion but too fast a one, so that a man who has gone all the way through can be, from the outside, indistinguishable from a man who has gone deepest in.

And the seed of it, in this account, is a complaint about the fairness of a distribution — made in the exact hour when the distribution was being run on a principle its author had just explained aloud, and made by a man who had evidently not heard the explanation or had not accepted it. This history names where that line ended: at Nahrawan, decades later, against the man who had been kept in private counsel outside the wall of Ta'if.

## What this episode lands

Ta'if is the objective this campaign did not take, and the reason recorded for leaving is not that the wall held. A catapult cracked it, a covered ram reached it, a burning beam drove the men out from under the ram, and the vines came down; and then the army was told that permission had not been granted, and asked for the order to withdraw. What the twenty nights produced that lasted is a road with a mosque on it, the first homicide in Islam brought before a judge instead of settled between clans, a handful of enslaved people who walked out of the city and were freed at the gate, and a prayer for the guidance of the people who had shut the gate.

Inside that siege, a conversation held too long in front of a watching army produced the sentence that it was not the Messenger who chose him. The book stops narrating at that point and argues — through a verse that priced access to the Prophet in single coins and was obeyed, on this account, by one man until his ten coins ran out, and then lifted; through a claim of a thousand doors opened at the ear; and through two exhibits set beside it, including a caliph corrected from the floor by a woman quoting a verse he had overlooked, who reported his own correction himself.

And at al-Ji'ranah two transactions ran side by side and were never confused. Six thousand captives went home for nothing, beginning with the share of the Prophet's own clan, on a request he scripted for the petitioners and had them make in public. Meanwhile hundreds of camels went out in blocks of a hundred to men who had commanded the other side, a poet was quieted with a payment on instruction, and a man who received nothing at all was named better than the earth's fill of two who received the most — because those two were being bought and he was being trusted. Between the two stood a single hair from a camel's hump, held up between two fingers, and a needle taken back off a wife and thrown into the pile.
