## Where this episode picks up

What does a defeat sound like in the city that lost? In Mecca it sounded like an old man sitting down with his back against a slave's back in the room beside the well, asking his nephew how the fighting had gone — and getting an answer that no one in that room could use.

The previous episode ended on the field. This one begins on the road home and then crosses to the losing side, because that is where the author takes us next. He does three things in this stretch, and they are not the three things you would expect from a war chronicle.

First he brings the captives back and lets one of them be argued into belief by a piece of household accounting. Then he stops the narrative almost entirely and gives over page after page to poetry — nine poems and fragments from both sides, including an elegy by a woman and a poem by a man who could not choose which side to grieve for. Then he resumes and walks through the whole year between the two great battles as a list: four marches, two settlements of accounts inside Medina, and one killing carried out at night by a man's own milk-brother.

One warning about the middle stretch. The verse in this part of the book has come down badly damaged. Names have been mangled in transmission, whole lines have gone opaque, and in several poems only the sense survives. That is how it will be given here: the sense, honestly, with no line reconstructed and no garbled name repaired into the one it probably was. What the poems are for is not in doubt even where the words are. Listen for what each poet thinks the battle proved.

## The captives and the news

He came back to Medina with the captives under guard, and some of them were ordered to ransom themselves.

Two of the prisoners were his own family. Al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib, his uncle, had been taken, and so had 'Aqil ibn Abi Talib. Quraysh had compelled both of them to march out against their will, and before the fighting the Messenger of God had given a standing order about exactly this: whoever among the Banu Hashim you are able to capture, do not kill them, for they were brought out against their will.

Notice what that order does. It draws a line through an enemy army between the men who chose the war and the men who were marched into it, and it draws that line by clan. It is given in advance, when nobody yet knows who will be standing where. And it does not exempt anyone from ransom — only from being killed. Al-'Abbas and 'Aqil were captured alive because of it, and then presented with a bill.

The Messenger of God said to al-'Abbas: ransom yourself and your nephew, for he has no money. Al-'Abbas said: I have nothing.

The reply is the whole scene. Where is the money you left with Umm al-Fadl on the day you departed from Mecca? You said to her: if I am killed, then such-and-such goes to 'Abdullah, and such-and-such to al-Fadl. And he described to him exactly what he had said to her.

Al-'Abbas said: no one knew of this except God, and so I bear witness that there is no god but God and that you are the Messenger of God. 'Aqil testified to the same. Al-'Abbas then ransomed himself and 'Aqil both.

The argument that moves him is not doctrinal. It is a private conversation with his wife about the disposal of his estate, repeated back to him word for word by a man three hundred miles away who could not have heard it. Two named beneficiaries and two amounts. That is the evidence, and he accepts it on the spot.

The author then lets a second account sit beside that one without smoothing the two together, and it is worth noticing that he does. The next narration comes from Abu Rafi', a freedman of the Messenger of God who had been al-'Abbas's slave, and Abu Rafi' says that Islam had already entered that household well before Badr: al-'Abbas had embraced it, Umm al-Fadl had become Muslim, but al-'Abbas feared his people and was unwilling to oppose them openly, and so he concealed it. He had considerable wealth spread out among his kin. So the same chapter records a man professing faith as a captive at Badr and records his house as quietly Muslim already. The author prints both and reconciles neither.

Abu Rafi's account is the only eyewitness view in this book from inside Mecca on the day the news arrived, and it is worth hearing in full.

Abu Lahab had stayed behind from Badr and sent al-'Asi ibn Hisham ibn al-Mughirah in his place. When word came of Quraysh's defeat, Abu Rafi' says, God humbled Abu Lahab and shamed him, and we felt a sense of strength and pride within ourselves.

He was carving goblets in the room of Zamzam — the well the earlier episodes of this history recovered from beneath the sand — and Umm al-Fadl, the wife of al-'Abbas, was there with him. The two of them were delighted by what they had heard. Then Abu Lahab came dragging his feet and sat down on the edge of the room with his back against Abu Rafi's back.

People outside said: here comes Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib. Abu Lahab called out to him: come to me — you have news. The man sat down; the others stood around him. And Abu Lahab said: O nephew, tell me how it went.

The answer, from a man who had been in the line: by God, we had barely met the enemy when they turned on us and did with us as they pleased. They cut us down; they led us captive at will. And yet, I tell you, between heaven and earth we saw white horsemen, and there was nothing we could do against them. Nothing could be done against them, and nothing availed.

That is a commander's report and a commander's excuse in one breath. It concedes the whole defeat and then explains it by something no army could have fought.

Abu Rafi' says he raised the support-pole of the room with his hand and said: those, by God, were angels.

He was a slave and he had just contradicted the master of the house in front of a crowd, by taking the excuse and converting it into a doctrinal claim. Abu Lahab raised his hand and struck him hard across the face. Abu Rafi' fought back; Abu Lahab overpowered him, threw him to the ground and knelt on his chest. And then Umm al-Fadl seized one of the room's pillars, struck Abu Lahab with it and split his head open, saying: you bully him because his master is away?

He got up and left. He lived only seven more nights before God struck him with a pestilence that killed him, and the people were rid of him.

Hold the shape of that scene, because the author has chosen it deliberately. The greatest reversal in the history so far is narrated from the losing city, in a storeroom, by a slave — and the three people in the room are a slave, a woman, and the last of the Prophet's Meccan enemies from the early chapters, who dies of plague within the week and is spoken of by everyone as a relief.

## The poets take the field

Then the author stops. For the length of several pages he gives up narrating and simply prints poems, and there are more words of verse about Badr in this book than there are words about the fighting.

That is not a digression. Among the Arabs a battle was fought twice — once in the field and once in verse afterwards — and the second fight settled what the first one had meant. So the poems are the argument about Badr, conducted in public by both sides, and the author collects them the way he collects casualty lists.

Ali ibn Abi Talib recited on the day of Badr. What survives says: did you not see that God has fulfilled His Messenger's promise, when the disbelievers were brought low in humiliation and found awaiting them captivity and slaughter? The Messenger of God was sent with justice, and the Messenger of God was granted his victory. What signs these are for those who possess understanding. He brought a house from God, and people believed in that and were guided, and they were reunited — praise be to God — while their false one lies in ruin before the Throne, and the nations that deny have broken hearts.

A second fragment follows in the same place, and it is in worse condition. Its sense is that the reasons for the matter have now become plain; that they grew angry and did what they thought best, holding on to what they clung to, and were met with clarity and resolve; that young men were left behind crying out, with an old man among them; that the eyes of the mourning women linger over the dead, turning with wailing and lamentation; that an old man is mourned, and Abu Jahl is mourned; that these were people tested in wars, whose mighty bonds of kinship were dissolved. Several of the proper names in it cannot be recovered at all. It is printed here as it stands in the book, damaged, rather than mended.

Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib also spoke on the day of Badr. They marched against us, he says, and we met at a fated hour, and we would have sought no other outcome; now they lie in disobedience and disbelief, mortgaged to destruction from Badr; on the eve of their departure they gathered toward Badr, and we had no recourse but to strike with the dark blade; we left off their pride and displayed the colour of manifest proof; and an old man lies among the dead, dragged into the pits.

Then Hamza adds a second piece, and this one carries a claim. I fear God's punishment, he says; I see what you do not see, and what the people have not yet heard is news. They became a thousand and we gathered, they in one place, and then the matter clarified. Among us are the soldiers of God, when He grants us a trial in which their wishes are tested. So Gabriel led them beneath our banner. And those others are the ones who err in their transgression — a misguided brigade, led by Satan to his own.

Put that beside what Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith said in the room at Zamzam and you have the most interesting agreement in this episode. The losing officer says there were white horsemen between heaven and earth and nothing could be done about them. The winning poet says the soldiers of God were in the field and Gabriel led them beneath the banner. Neither side denies that something was there. They disagree only about whose side it was on, and the slave in Mecca had already supplied the name for it.

'Ubaydah ibn al-Harith ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib composed as he was being carried away from Badr, wounded. The people of Mecca have been informed of a calamity, he says, and those far from it felt it keenly. I ask from it a life from God, close at hand, with the highest of heaven for those who were noble. I struck until the lowest were lost, and sold that life, and came to know clarity, with a garment of Islam that covered all alike. If my leg is cut off, then I am still a Muslim, among the sincere, like a swaying palm. The Merciful has honoured me with His bounty, and it was no hardship to fight them. He did not falter when they asked the Prophet about us — the three of us — until we came to the one who called; we found them like lions guarding a thicket; we fight in the cause of the Merciful against the disobedient; the three of us, until the fates came to them, with our feet still planted in our place.

He is one of the three men called out by name to single combat in the previous episode, and he is writing this with a severed leg, from the litter, on the road. The line about the leg is not a metaphor he chose for effect. It is a description of his own body, made while the wound that would kill him was still open.

Ka'b ibn Malik, of Banu Salamah, wrote at greater length and in better repair. I obeyed the command of God, he begins, and God is capable of whatever He wills — God is not powerless. The day of Badr overtook you as you met a tithe of corruption, and the ways of corruption among the people are unjust. They came from a people whose gathering grew and multiplied; they assembled and sought out those who came after them; they marched in harm and soaked themselves in revolt. And the Messenger of God had the Aws around him and his companions standing firm. When we met them, all fighters together, and the Messenger of God with the truth made plain, we testified that there is no god but God — a testimony that adorns the eyes of those who witness it. I was exposed to waves of spears as though they were blossoms, and now one would discover who was true. Abu Jahl was shouting in its face, and none of them but the Lord of the Throne shall judge. Shayba and Taymi were left in the valley, and every disbeliever shall remain in Hell; they stoked the fuel of the fire in its place, iron and stone together.

And then the last turn of that poem, which reaches back years. The Messenger of God was called a sorcerer, Ka'b says, and yet he acted not but by the command of God's guardian; God willed that they perish through it. The charge of sorcery belongs to the Meccan chapters of this history, when it was the standard accusation in the streets. Here it comes back as something a victory has answered.

Hassan ibn Thabit's poem on Badr is the strangest inclusion in the chapter, because for its first half it is not about Badr at all. It opens the way a classical ode opens — with a woman. There is a cold bright smile that heals, and marble set upon cotton, and a soft and graceful form too languid to rise from her bed; the night distributes his dreams through her and by day he will not mention her; he swears to forget her and leave off her name, and rebukes those who blame him for his foolishness. Only then does he turn, and when he turns it is onto a named man: the fate of al-Harith ibn Hisham came to pass, and he survived, with the head of resolve and courage — were it not for God and his flight, the poet says, he would have left him mired in turmoil. Captives with their bonds drawn tight. Banners vanishing from sight. Shame and disgrace laid bare.

That is the form working exactly as it should. The poet of Medina answers a military catastrophe by beginning with a love-prelude and then wheeling round to name a survivor and reproach him for surviving. Remember al-Harith ibn Hisham. He is mocked here for getting away from Badr alive, and he will be consoled by name in a poem later in this same episode by the enemy's poet.

Ka'b ibn Malik also wrote the elegy for 'Ubaydah ibn al-Harith, who had died of that leg. Weep with your tears truly, and hold nothing back; O eye, do not be sparing. A scene of nobility and generosity, over the master of this fate now sealed. A bold commander, steadfast, bearing arms, a crusher of enemies. I would not deny our nakedness or our loss: 'Ubaydah, yesterday — and we have not yet rested from grieving him.

Hind bint Athathah ibn 'Abbad ibn al-Muttalib lamented him too, and hers is the one woman's voice in the poetry of this chapter. A true vision that floods the heart and mind, she says; a widow brought low, trembling; weep, O 'Ubaydah, and cry for those who die in exile. If the sky's horizons turn red from the blaze, he was a shelter for nations through every winter. The youth of the pot, as long as it froths and boils. Weeping for orphans while the wind howls. He used to feed them with firewood — but when the one who kindled the fire dies, his throat grows cold.

Look at what she praises him for. Not one line about the fighting, and he died of a battle wound. The whole elegy is hospitality: the cooking pot on the boil, the firewood carried in, the orphans fed, the cold hearth after. That vocabulary of generosity is older than Islam by centuries, and it is what a lament for a dead man was made of. The new faith has changed who is mourned and not yet changed how.

The last poem is the one that cannot choose. Talib ibn Abi Talib, Ali's eldest brother, praised the Messenger of God and mourned the wounded of Quraysh in the same poem. She weeps over a heel, he says; have my eyes not already shed their tears? Would that I knew — might I yet see closeness to them? They give promises, and their neighbour shall not be oppressed by force; they are my brothers, whose tongue shall speak no more. Were it not for God's defence there would be nothing at all, except that our protector is better than the one who stepped down and faltered. When we were born into Quraysh, greatness was already there. And then, turning to the clans directly: O our brothers of 'Abd Shams and Nawfal, a ransom for you both — send no war between us. These are reports over which all of you lament the catastrophe. Do not, after friendship and familiarity, forget that war was not something noble men desired. I swear by God I will not keep my soul in sorrow.

There is even an image in it borrowed from the oldest story in this book: the army of Abu Yaksum when they filled the land, and now you have no squadron left. The host that came with the elephant, invoked by a Meccan poet against Mecca's own dead.

Every other poem in this chapter knows which side it is on. Talib's does not, and the author prints it anyway, at length, in a book whose whole purpose is to establish a line of authority. His own uncle's son is allowed to stand in the record grieving the enemy dead by clan name and asking his kinsmen to stop the war.

## The year of marches and reckonings

The battle is over in the second month of the second year. Uhud is thirteen months away. What fills the gap is a list, and the author keeps every item on it even when the item is that nothing happened.

He was back in Medina only seven nights before setting out again, this time against Bani Sulaym at al-Kadir. He led it himself, reached a watering place of theirs called al-Kadar, camped there three nights and returned to Medina without any fighting. Then he stayed in Medina through Shawwal and Dhu al-Qa'dah, and what occupied those two months was administrative: overseeing the ransom of the Qurayshi captives.

Then came the expedition they called al-Suwaiq, and it begins with an oath. When Quraysh got back to Mecca from Badr, Abu Sufyan ibn Harb swore that water would not touch his head from ritual impurity until he had raided Muhammad.

He set out with two hundred horsemen, bearing right toward the Najdiyyah road, and descended at the mouth of a valley near a mountain called Tayb, about a post from the city. He travelled on by night and reached Banu al-Nadir after dark. He went to Huyayy ibn Akhtab and knocked on his door, and Huyayy would not open it, fearing him, so Abu Sufyan left. He went instead to Sallam ibn Mishkam, who held the highest standing among Banu al-Nadir, and Sallam let him in, received him hospitably, gave him water to drink, and told him the news of the people.

That is the detail to hold onto out of the whole raid. An enemy commander walked into a settlement inside the city's own confederacy at night and was briefed on the state of Medina over a cup of water. Nothing is made of it here. It is the fact that later expulsions will turn on.

Abu Sufyan slipped out again in the last part of the night, rejoined his men, and at dawn they rode to a place called al-'Arid, where they burned the palm enclosures along its edges. They came upon two Ansari men working their land and killed them both, and then retreated. The pursuit went out after them, and the Messenger of God chased them as far as Qarqarat al-Kudr before turning back, having missed Abu Sufyan and his companions. The raiders had thrown their provisions away as they ran, and most of what they dropped was sawiq flour. That is why the expedition is called al-Suwaiq.

An oath about not washing, discharged by burning a hedge and killing two farmers, and the campaign is named after the food the raiders abandoned while getting away. The name is the verdict, and both sides used it.

Then Dhu Amr. He marched out toward Najd meaning to strike at Ghatafan, camped at Najd al-Safra, and returned to Medina without encountering the enemy. It took the whole of Rabi' al-Awwal, or most of it.

Then Buhran. He set out in search of Quraysh as far as the mines of Buhran in the Hijaz, stayed there through Jumada al-Akhirah and part of Jumada al-Ula, and returned without fighting.

Three of those four marches ended with nobody met and everybody home, and the author records each one with its route, its water, and its month. He did the same for the year before Badr. This is not a chronicler padding; it is a chronicler who thinks an expedition that found nothing is still a fact about the year.

Between them sit two reckonings inside Medina itself, and both are messier than the marches.

The first is Banu Qaynuqa'. They were Jews, and there was a covenant between them and the Messenger of God. After Badr he gathered them in the market of Banu Qaynuqa' and said: O assembly of Jews, beware lest God bring down upon you the like of what He brought down upon Quraysh. Embrace Islam, for you know well that I am a prophet who has been sent — you will find this in your own scripture, and God has bound you to it.

They answered: O Muhammad, do not be deceived by having defeated a people who had no knowledge of war and who gave you an opening against them. By God, if we were to fight you, you would know that we are truly a people.

It is said that it was concerning them that God revealed: say to those who disbelieve, you will be overcome and gathered into Hell — and evil is the resting place. There was already a sign for you in the two forces that met: one fighting in the cause of God and another that disbelieved; they saw them with their own eyes as twice their number. And God strengthens with His victory whom He wills — in that is a lesson for those who have sight. (chapter 3, verses 12 and 13)

There is something worth noticing inside that verse, in a book this preoccupied with counting. The claim is not that the smaller army was larger. It is that the losing side saw it as twice the size it was. The number that decided the day was a number in somebody's eye.

The trouble itself started in a marketplace. A woman went into their market and suffered an indignity there at the hands of a Jew; a Muslim man leapt on him and killed him; the Jews fell on the Muslim and killed him. The community cried out, and the Messenger of God came forth and besieged them for fifteen nights. Ali ibn Abi Talib said: O Messenger of God, let us force our way in against them.

They were brought to submit to his judgment and God delivered them into his power. And then 'Abd Allah ibn Ubayy ibn Salul objected on their behalf — a man this history names as one of the chiefs of the hypocrites. He stood in front of the Messenger of God and said: I ask you to spare them. They are four hundred men in armour and three hundred without, who have protected me against every foe, Arab and non-Arab alike. And the Messenger of God said: they are yours.

A confrontation with a whole quarter of the city turns on a market insult, escalates to a fifteen-night siege, and then ends with the plea of a man the author has already labelled a hypocrite being granted outright. Note the figure he quotes — seven hundred fighting men who owe him. And note his name, because in the next episode he takes a third of an army off the road to Uhud.

The second reckoning is a raid and then a killing.

Zayd ibn Haritha was sent out on a raiding mission and struck the caravan of al-'Ayr. Abu Sufyan ibn Harb was in charge of it, and it was carrying a great quantity of silver. The men fled and came to the Messenger of God in disgrace. What had happened was this: after Badr, Quraysh were afraid to travel their usual route to the Levant, so they had taken the road of Iraq instead — and it was on that road that Zayd intercepted them.

Hassan ibn Thabit reproached them for the choice. Let the farmers of Damascus stand in their way, he says — a blow like the edge of a blade, at the hands of men who migrated to their Lord, with His supporters and the hands of angels. If you walk in the valley of the sands of that land, tell them there is no passage there.

That is Badr's real economic result, stated by a poet: a trade route abandoned, a detour attempted, and the detour cut too.

Then came the killing of Ka'b ibn al-Ashraf. He was a man of Tayyi', of the clan of Banu Nabhan, and his mother was of Banu al-Nadir. When the casualties of Badr reached him he said: can it be that Muhammad has killed the noblest of the Arabs and the kings of men? By God, if Muhammad has truly struck down these people, the belly of the earth is better than its back.

He acted on it. He went to Mecca and stirred up hostility there, composing verse that worked directly on the grief of the bereaved families. The mill of Badr ground for your time and your kinsmen, he told them, and for what Badr meant you deserve to weep and mourn; do not stand apart while kings are struggling. How many men of white-robed nobility were struck down. They feared to kill Abu al-Hakim and they held back — all the sons of al-Mughirah — and none came to the aid of the doomed, nor did the sons of Rabi'ah come to him. And there, in the middle of it, a line that embraces al-Harith ibn Hisham by name. The same man Hassan ibn Thabit had shamed for escaping is comforted by the poet of the other side. Hassan then answered Ka'b, as he answered everyone.

Back in Medina, Ka'b began composing poems designed to seduce the women of the Muslims. And the Messenger of God said: who will deal with Ibn al-Ashraf for me?

Muhammad ibn Maslamah al-Ansari said: I will, O Messenger of God — I will kill him. He replied: do so, if you are able. Muhammad ibn Maslamah said: O Messenger of God, we will need to say things. And he replied: say whatever seems necessary to you, and I grant you leave in that.

That exchange is a permission, asked for and given in advance, and it is what the rest of the account runs on.

He enlisted Salkan ibn Salama of Banu 'Abd al-Ashhal — who was Ka'b's own milk-brother, and who is also called Abu Nayla — together with 'Abbad ibn Bishr, al-Harith ibn Aws, others of Banu 'Abd al-Ashhal, and Abu 'Abs ibn Jabr of Banu Haritha.

Salkan went ahead alone. He presented himself, and the two of them talked for a while and traded poetry, as milk-brothers might. Then Salkan said: O Ibn al-Ashraf, I have seen what this man has brought upon us, and what a calamity it has been. The Arabs have grown hostile to us, our trade routes have been cut off, and even our children have gone without. Ka'b, convinced, grew alarmed and said: by God, I have told you, O Ibn Salamah, it will be exactly as I said.

Then the trap. Salkan asked to buy food and to leave a pledge for it. Ka'b said: will you pledge your children? Salkan said: you want to disgrace us among the Arabs. I have companions with me like myself, and I will bring them to you, so sell to them and do well by me; we pledge you in the transaction that there will be fulfilment. What they actually wanted was to be able to come armed without being questioned. Ka'b said: if that is the case, then it is a pledge of fulfilment.

Two of them went back and told the others to take up arms, and they did. Then Abu Nayla called out. Ka'b was in his fortress, and he came down. They spoke about what had been arranged and said: Muhammad has become a source of hostility between us and the Arabs. He listened. Then they said: can you come out with us for a walk among the people? We will talk through the rest of the night. He said: if you wish. And they went out walking for an hour.

Then Abu Nayla put his hand on Ka'b's head, and sniffed his hand, and said: I have never smelled anything more fragrant. Ka'b had married recently and was still close to his wedding, so the perfume was his. Abu Nayla moved away. Then he came back and did the same thing again, and again, until Ka'b was entirely at ease with a hand in his hair. And then he came back once more, took hold of his head, and said to his companions: strike the enemy of God.

They killed him. He let out a great cry as he died, and not one of the Jewish fortresses was left without a fire lit on it. Al-Harith ibn Aws ibn Mu'adh was wounded in the dark when his own side's swords caught him by mistake. They carried him and got away, and reached the Messenger of God while he was standing in prayer. When he had finished and given the salutation he came out to them, and they told him the enemy of God was dead. He praised them and commended them greatly. And from that point on, the Jews of the city were gripped by a severe and lasting fear.

Ka'b ibn Malik commemorated it in verse, and his poem does not soften the method. After his death the harm was brought low, he says, and a piercing cry came back from them; on the palms of hands his sickness was met with our hands, a celebrated man; by the command of Muhammad, who strikes at night. So he is cunning, and I bring him down by cunning.

The poet states the technique as the achievement. And notice how the whole affair is bracketed. It begins with poems — the ones Ka'b wrote in Mecca to reopen the grief of Badr, and the ones he wrote in Medina about other men's wives — and it ends with a poem about killing him for it. In this chapter, verse is not decoration around the events. It is one of the weapons in use.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry out of this stretch.

The first is that Badr's real consequences were commercial and domestic before they were military. A trade route to the Levant was abandoned and the substitute route was cut. Captives became a revenue stream that occupied two whole months of the calendar. A ransom negotiation turned into a profession of faith because a man's instructions to his wife about his estate were quoted back to him. And in the losing city, the news was absorbed in a storeroom by a slave, a woman, and a dying man.

The second is that the poets, not the chronicler, are where the meaning of the battle was fought out — and the author knows it, which is why he gives them more room than the fighting. The two sides agree on the eeriest fact of the day and disagree only about whose it was. An elegy for a man killed in action praises him entirely for his cooking pot. And the one poem in the chapter that refuses to take a side is by the Prophet's own cousin, and is printed in full.

The third is that the year between the two great battles is mostly marches that met nobody, and the two things that did happen in it happened at home. A market quarrel became a fifteen-night siege that ended in a hypocrite's successful plea. A poet was killed at night by his milk-brother, after a permission had been asked for and granted, using his own wedding perfume as the signal. Both were settled inside the city, and both left something unfinished.

Quraysh, meanwhile, had been spending the money rescued from the caravan. What they bought with it, and the wedding the history stops for on the way there, is where this account goes next.
