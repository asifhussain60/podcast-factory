## Where this episode picks up

If a man is about to raid your camp and you kill him with an arrow, that is war — but what happens when he says, in the second before the blow lands, the one sentence that makes him one of you?

The previous episode closed on documents: letters and a covenant that carried this religion into Yemen as written law, and an argument about whose transmission of that law is the one that binds. This one turns to the other half of the same years, and to a different kind of record entirely. The history gathers here the small detachments — the raiding parties and the secret missions sent out under other men's command, kept separate from the campaigns the Prophet led in person. Most are two or three lines long. Several are told by the man who went, in the first person, and they are among the plainest pieces of narration in the volume: a scout lying still while arrows go into him, a man walking beside his target until the moment comes, three men in a cave outside Mecca deciding whether the horseman on the path has seen them.

Three movements. First the detachments themselves, one after another, as the source lists them. Then the cases where something went wrong and a rule came out of it — a man killed after declaring the faith, a killing avenged from before Islam, a set of standing orders given to a commander with a turban tied on his head, and a captive released before he converted. Then the last section, which is not narrative at all: a single detail about who led the prayer on one of these expeditions, and the argument this history builds on it about who was fit to lead the community afterwards.

Listen for how often the rule arrives after the damage.

## The detachments

As for the military expeditions dispatched by the Messenger of God, those are distinct from what has already been mentioned regarding the campaigns led by the commanders. They include the following.

That sentence is the source's own filing note, and it explains the change of texture. What follows is a list of small operations entered by the name of the man who led each one, most a paragraph long, a handful expanded because someone who was there left an account.

The raid of Ghalib ibn 'Abd Allah al-Laythi against Banu al-Mulawwih. The Messenger of God ordered him to raid them. When he reached al-Kadid at sunset and was resting in the valley, he sent Jundub ibn Mukayth al-Juhani ahead as a scout.

Jundub said: I went out until I came to a vantage point overlooking the encampment, and I leaned against it and observed them. I was keeping watch from the hill when one of them came out of his tent and said to his wife: I can see on the hill a dark shape that I did not see before. Check your vessels — have you lost anything? The dogs have not been pulling at each other. She looked and said: No, by God, I have lost nothing. He said: Then hand me my bow and arrows. She gave them to him, and he shot an arrow. God did not let it miss its mark — it struck me, and I pinned it down and held firm. Then he shot another arrow that lodged in my arm. He said to his wife: If it were a living thing it would have moved — my arrows have found it. Then he went back inside.

We waited until they were reassured and fell asleep, and then, when the night was at its darkest, we launched our raid. We cut them down, and the cries of the people rang out. They came at us, but the valley of Qadid lay between us and them, and God sent a torrent through that valley from wherever He willed, without a cloud or rain in sight. It came with a force that no one had the power to cross or withstand, and it stood between us and them, protecting us. We drove our gains ahead of us and pressed on until we came to the Messenger of God.

Two arrows go into a man who has to lie still or the operation is lost, and what saves him is ordinary reasoning by the man shooting: a living thing would have flinched. The torrent is given the same flat treatment as the arrows — no cloud, no rain, a valley in flood, an enemy who cannot cross it.

The raid of Ali ibn Abi Talib against Banu 'Abd Allah ibn Sa'd, the people of Fadak. When Gabriel came to the Messenger of God with the command concerning Fadak, he gathered his company and set out by night with Ali and a body of men. When they reached Fadak they found its people on guard and keeping their distance, and the Messenger of God ordered Ali to take the road into Fadak. When he advanced, the people of Fadak came out to fight him and he engaged them; it is said that he killed eighteen of them. They fled, and they submitted themselves to the Prophet, and he accepted their wealth as ransom — giving his daughter Fatimah the land as her redemption, with Umm Ayman among the witnesses. The Messenger of God took a fifth of the spoils and whatever he saw fit, and divided the remainder among the Muslims.

The conquest of Zayd ibn Haritha at Jadhab, which took place before Mu'tah. Zayd struck some of those who had embraced Islam and was wounded; he brought back captives among them and delivered a group of them to the Messenger of God. Rifa'ah ibn Zayd al-Judhami was presented to him with a letter the Messenger of God had written. The Messenger of God sent word, and the Commander of the Faithful was given his sword and ordered to ride out on this mission — and whenever he encountered anyone from Zayd's army he took what they had and returned it to its people, so that everything they held was given back. The martyrdom of Zayd ibn Haritha has already been recorded in this history; this expedition is set down here because it had not been mentioned before.

The history does not draw attention to it, but a commander here raids a group who turn out to have already accepted Islam, and the correction is not a punishment but a recovery: a second detachment rides the same road and takes the plunder back off the raiders, item by item, until the people wrongly raided have everything again.

The conquest of Zayd ibn Haritha, also, against Banu Fazarah. A number of his companions were wounded, and Zayd swore an oath over his fallen dead that he would not wash himself of a major impurity until he had raided them. The Messenger of God sent him, and he killed them in the valley of the villages.

The secret mission of Zayd ibn Haritha to Madyan, before Mu'tah. The Messenger of God sent with him the freed slave of Ali ibn Abi Talib and his brother. They were taken captive by the people of that coastal region, and the captives were sold and separated. The Messenger of God went out weeping. When it was said to him, distinguish between them, he said: Do not pursue them except all together.

The conquest of 'Abd Allah ibn Rawahah at Khaybar, before Khaybar was opened. He was accompanied by a group of companions, among them 'Abd Allah ibn Anis. The verses attached to this notice are badly damaged; what survives of them names a warrior worth the lord of Nahd, a ransom offered in every quarter, and a commander seen the next day in their own dwelling with a company of Jews about him.

The raid of 'Abd Allah ibn Anis. The Messenger of God summoned him and said: It has been reported to me that Khalid ibn Sufyan al-Hudhali is mustering men to attack me. He is at a certain palm grove. Go to him and kill him.

'Abd Allah ibn Anis said: When I came to him the man said: Who are you? I said: A man from the Arabs, who heard that you have mustered men against this man, and I have come to you for that purpose. He said: Yes, I am doing exactly that. So I walked with him until I found an opportunity, and then I killed him with the sword.

Then I went back to the Messenger of God and told him, and he handed me a staff and said: This will be a sign between me and you on the Day of Resurrection. 'Abd Allah ibn Anis tied the staff to his sword, and it remained with him until he died; he left instructions that it be buried with him, and so it was buried alongside him.

The verses he made about it are damaged, and what survives is boast rather than report: a blade bright from the water of iron, a man left fallen among his own people, a burst of flame, and the line that carries the whole of it — that he told him, while the sword was at his head, to take a blow that no one before him had ever struck with tongue and hand together.

The staff is the detail worth keeping. He is given no reward that can be spent — only an object whose whole value is a promise about a day that has not happened, and he treats it as exactly that: tied to his weapon, kept for life, put in the ground with him.

The conquest of 'Uyaynah ibn Hisn against Banu al-'Anbar. The Messenger of God dispatched him against them and he raided them. Among those killed from Banu al-'Anbar were 'Abd Allah and his brothers, the sons of Wahb, and Hanzalah ibn Darim. Among the women taken captive were Asma' bint Malik, Nahra bint Nahd, Jumay'ah bint Qays and 'Amrah bint Matar; the Messenger of God redeemed some of them. The verses of Salma bint 'Attab on that day, and the lines al-Farazdaq later made about it, survive only in fragments — hers a lament for a woman who lost her pride and her forefather and was surrounded by enemies on every side, his a boast about necks bound in ropes and prisoners released at the price of redemption.

The expedition of Abu 'Ubaydah ibn al-Jarrah to the shore of the sea. The Messenger of God sent him in command of a company and provisioned them with a sack of dates. When their supplies ran out and they were exhausted, God brought forth for them a creature from the sea; they struck it and fed on its flesh and its fat, remaining there for twenty nights until they grew stout. 'Ubadah ibn al-Samit said: We took one of its ribs and stood it upright along the road, then rode beneath it on one of the largest-bodied mounts we had, and the rider passed clean under that rib. When we returned to the Messenger of God and told him the news, he said: That is provision which God the Almighty has sent forth for you.

The expedition of 'Amr ibn Umayyah al-Damri, sent to kill Abu Sufyan ibn Harb before the conquest, with Jabbar ibn Sakhr al-Ansari dispatched alongside him. They came to Mecca, tethered their camels among a group of people, and entered the city by night. Jabbar said: What if we entered the mosque and prayed two rak'ahs? 'Amr said: The people will be alert. Jabbar said: No, by God, we shall. So we went to the mosque, prayed, and then set out in search of Abu Sufyan. But as we were walking through Mecca a man of the city caught sight of me and recognised me. He said: 'Amr ibn Umayyah — by God, this can only mean evil. I said to my companion: Run.

So we went out and climbed a mountain, and they came out in pursuit. When they had almost reached us they lost our trail. We turned back, entered a cave in the mountain, and blocked its entrance with stones we gathered around us. In the morning a man of Quraysh came leading his horse. We held still inside, and I said: If he sees us he will raise the alarm. I had a dagger I had prepared for Abu Sufyan; I went out to him and struck him a blow, and he cried out so loudly that I heard the people of Mecca stir. I went back to my place. He was found with people gathering around him, drawing his last breath. They said: Who struck you? He said: 'Amr ibn Umayyah. Death overcame him and he died there, without revealing where we were, and they carried him off. I said to my companion: We are saved.

We walked to Dahnan, took refuge in a mountain and entered a cave. An elderly one-eyed man of Banu al-Dil came in upon us with his provisions and said: Who are you? I said: From Banu Bakr. He raised his head and said: I am not a Muslim, and I will never embrace nor praise the religion of the Muslims. So I waited until he fell asleep, and then I took my bow, fitted an arrow, and drove it into his eye. Then we left. On our descent we encountered two men whom Quraysh had sent to watch the city and gather intelligence. I said: Surrender as prisoners. I shot one of them with an arrow and killed him; the other I took captive, bound him, and brought him to Medina.

Two men are sent to kill the most protected man in Mecca and kill nobody they were sent for. The account is made of the argument about praying in the mosque, the wrong face in the street, the stones piled at a cave mouth, and a run of improvisations on the way home. The mission fails and the report is preserved in full anyway, which is characteristic of this list.

The expedition of Salim ibn 'Umayr of Banu 'Amr ibn 'Awf, and the expedition of 'Umayr ibn 'Adi al-Khatmi, are recorded here as killings ordered against two people whose verses had turned against the community after the death of al-Harith ibn Suwayd ibn al-Samit — Abu 'Afak, an old man of Medina, and Asma' bint Marwan, the wife of a man of Banu Khatmah. Both sets of verses, and the answering lines attributed to Hassan ibn Thabit and to Umamah al-Muzarriyyah, are among the most damaged in the manuscript and survive only as fragments of taunt and counter-taunt.

And the expedition of 'Alqamah ibn Mujazziz, sent out after the people who had killed Waqqas ibn Mujazziz al-Mudliji on the day of Dhu Qarad — a day already recorded earlier in this history — so that he might exact the reckoning for his brother.

## The limits set on the sword

Several of these operations produced a ruling rather than a result, and the history keeps them in the list rather than gathering them into a chapter on law. The rule and the wreckage stay in the same paragraph.

The raid of Ghalib ibn 'Abd Allah in the land of Banu Murrah. Mirdas ibn Nuhayk, an ally of theirs from the Harqah clan of Juhaynah, was killed by Usamah ibn Zayd, even though he had declared: I bear witness that there is no god but God. When the Messenger of God learned of this, he said that those who say there is no god but God are not to be fought.

That is the shortest entry with the largest consequence. A man is killed by a raiding party in the field after saying the sentence that admits him, and the ruling that comes back from Medina does not treat the declaration as a possible ruse to be assessed by the man holding the spear. It removes the assessment. The words are spoken; the fighting stops. What the speaker may have meant by them is not the raider's question to answer.

The expedition of Ibn Abi Hadrad into the valley of Idham, before the conquest. When a group of Muslims reached that valley, 'Amir ibn al-Adbat al-Ashja'i came out toward them, and as he passed them he greeted them with the greeting of Islam. Muhallim ibn Jathamah set upon him and killed him, on account of something between them from the days of ignorance.

Concerning this, God revealed to His Prophet: *And do not say to one who greets you with peace, You are not a believer, seeking the goods of this worldly life* (chapter 4, verse 94).

After the Battle of Hunayn, al-Aqra' ibn Habis and 'Uyaynah ibn Hisn rose and disputed with the Messenger of God over the blood of 'Amir ibn al-Adbat. 'Uyaynah demanded blood for 'Amir's death, being at that time the chief of Ghatafan, while al-Aqra' stood on the side of Muhallim ibn Jathamah by virtue of his standing among Khandaf. The Messenger of God ruled that blood money be paid for 'Amir ibn al-Adbat, and it was accepted.

As for Muhallim ibn Jathamah, he came to the Messenger of God seeking forgiveness. The Messenger of God raised his hands and said: O God, do not forgive Muhallim ibn Jathamah. He repeated it three times. Muhallim did not survive beyond seven days before he died.

Al-Hasan al-Basri said: The earth rejected him, and they returned him. Then the earth rejected him again, and they returned him once more, and the earth expelled him. His people then placed him between two shields and pressed him between them, and heaped stones upon him until they covered him. The Messenger of God said: By God, the earth accepts those far more wicked than him, but God wished to make an example for you in the sanctity of what I have forbidden you.

Look at what the case actually settles, because it settles three different things and they do not all point the same way. The greeting of peace given by a man walking past a war party is a claim of membership, and killing him over a grudge from before Islam is a killing of a Muslim; the verse says so, and says why it happens — for the goods of this worldly life. Then a tribal dispute is brought to Medina by two chiefs who each represent a side, and it is settled with money, which is the ordinary remedy. And then, on top of the ordinary remedy, there is a prayer against the man that is not ordinary at all, and a death seven days later. The last word given to the affair is a correction of the obvious inference: this is not a verdict on how wicked he was, since the earth takes worse men every day. It is a demonstration staged for everyone watching.

The expedition of Ibn Abi Hadrad, also, to kill Rifa'ah ibn Qays al-Jushami in the forest, is the one entry in this list that begins with a domestic problem.

Ibn Abi Hadrad said: I had married a woman and pledged two hundred dirhams as her dowry. I came to the Messenger of God and asked him to help me with the marriage. He said: How much? I told him, and he said: Glory be to God — if you were to take dirhams from the belly of a valley you could not have increased more. By God, I have nothing with which to assist you.

So I went to the mosque, and I became aware of a man of Banu Jusham ibn Mu'awiyah known as Rifa'ah ibn Qays, who had come down with his people into a forest, intending to gather Qays against the Messenger of God. So the Messenger of God called me and two men from among the Muslims and said: Go out to this man so that you may bring us intelligence and news of him.

We set out with our swords, spears and bows as the sun set, and approached their people after dark had fallen. I took cover in one direction and ordered my two companions to conceal themselves in another, and told them: When I make my move, hold firm and close in with me. We waited in ambush as the night grew dark around them. They had a shepherd who was late returning, and they grew anxious for him. Then their leader, Rifa'ah ibn Qays, stood up, strapped his sword around his neck and said: By God, I will go after our shepherd's tracks. Some of his people said: We are enough for you. But he turned away from them and said: By God, none of you shall follow me.

He went out alone, and as he passed by me I shot him with my arrow and struck him in the heart. He said nothing; he fell fixed to the ground. I took his head and secured it, then rallied my companions. We closed in, and not one of them had any chance of escape save what they could manage — their women and children, and whatever property they could carry off. They left behind great camels and many sheep. We brought these before the Messenger of God, and I came to him bearing the head of Rifa'ah. The Messenger of God helped me from those camels with thirteen for my marriage, and I was united with my wife.

The account opens with a man being told plainly that there is no money — the dowry he has promised is judged extravagant and the treasury is empty — and it closes with him paid, out of the spoils of the operation he was sent on for reasons that had nothing to do with his marriage. Nothing in the middle is about the dowry. It is a reconnaissance that turns into a raid because a chief insists on walking out alone into the dark.

The expedition of 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf to Dumat al-Jandal is prefaced by a scene in Medina. A young man of the Ansar came, greeted the Messenger of God, sat down, and asked: O Messenger of God, which of the believers is best? He said: The best of them in character. He asked: Which of the believers is most discerning? He said: Those who most remember death and are best prepared for it before it comes to them. Then the young man fell silent.

The Messenger of God turned to his companions and said: O company of the Emigrants, five things — if they befall you, I seek refuge in God that you should ever see them. Immorality never spreads openly among a people until they flaunt it, but plague appears among them, and diseases that were unknown to their ancestors who had gone before. Nor do a people withhold the measure and the balance without being seized by years of drought, grinding hardship, and the injustice of their ruler. Nor do they withhold the zakat due on their wealth without the rain of the sky being withheld from them — and were it not for the animals, no rain would fall at all. Nor do they violate the covenant of God and the covenant of His Messenger without an enemy from beyond being given power over them, taking from them what was in their hands. And whenever their rulers do not govern by the Book of God and choose from what God has revealed, God sets them against one another.

The Prophet then commanded 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf to prepare for the secret mission to Dumat al-Jandal. The Messenger of God bound a turban on his head and said: Go forth, Ibn 'Awf. Lead them all in the ways of God, in the name of God. Fight those who disbelieve in God. Do not be treacherous. Do not mutilate. Do not kill children. This is the covenant of God and the way of His Prophet with you. So 'Abd al-Rahman took command of the detachment and set out for Dumat al-Jandal.

The five warnings and the four orders are placed back to back on purpose. The warnings are a description of how a community destroys itself from the inside — by flaunting what it once hid, by cheating in the market, by keeping what is owed to the poor, by breaking its word, and by rulers who legislate around the revelation. The orders given to the commander a moment later are the same discipline applied to the one activity in which a man can most easily tell himself the rules are suspended. No treachery, no mutilation, no killing of children — spoken while the turban is being tied, before he has left the city.

The list ends with a captive. A cavalry force went out and, before they knew it, had captured a man of Banu Hanifah. When the Messenger of God was informed, he said: Do you know whom you have captured? This is Thumamah ibn Athal al-Hanafi — release him from his bonds. He then returned to his family and said: Bring together whatever food you have and send it to him, and he ordered that his she-camel be milked for him. Yet Thumamah remained unmoved, and nothing offered to him prompted him to eat. Eventually he embraced Islam, and from that point he ate so little that the Muslims marvelled at it. The Messenger of God said: What do you marvel at? A man eats at the start of his day with the appetite of a disbeliever, then eats again — for the intestines of a disbeliever eat in seven portions, while the Muslim eats in one.

After his conversion Thumamah travelled to Mecca to perform 'Umrah. He entered the city to see to his needs, and the Quraysh seized him. They were troubled over what to do with him, fearing that if they harmed him it would cut off their food supply and the goodwill of their neighbours. When he reached al-Yamamah he forbade the Quraysh the mira, the grain supply, until they wrote to the Prophet: You command the bonds of kinship, and yet you have severed ours. The Messenger of God wrote to Thumamah instructing him to let the supply through to them and restore relations.

That is the last of the detachments and the only one that ends with an embargo being lifted. A man is captured by accident, released before he believes anything, fed from the household's own stores while he refuses to eat, and converts anyway. Then he applies what he has learned as a weapon of his own — starving Mecca of grain — and is overruled by the man on whose behalf he thought he was acting, on the grounds that Quraysh's complaint is correct.

## The question of precedence

One entry in the list carries a single administrative detail, and the history stops the whole chapter on it.

The conquest of 'Amr ibn al-'As with the expedition of Chains, in the land of Banu 'Udhrah. The Messenger of God dispatched him, and he arrived at a watering place known as al-Silsil. Abu Bakr, 'Umar and Abu 'Ubaydah ibn al-Jarrah were among those with him. 'Amr ibn al-'As led them all in prayer throughout, and they served under his command.

On this basis the Shi'a scholars challenged those who had elevated Abu Bakr, arguing against what they claimed — that the Messenger of God had commanded the people to pray behind him. The Shi'a scholars said: 'Amr ibn al-'As has a stronger claim to the leadership than Abu Bakr by that reasoning, for the Messenger of God appointed 'Amr to lead them in prayer. And the Messenger of God never appointed anyone over Ali ibn Abi Talib, and no one led prayer over him save the Messenger of God himself.

The argument is a piece of pure logic and it costs the other side nothing to concede the facts, which is what makes it awkward. The claim being answered is that leading the prayer in the Prophet's lifetime is a designation of the man who leads the community afterwards. Very well, the reply runs: then take the same rule and apply it to this expedition, where a young commander led the prayer for weeks with two of the three most senior men in Islam standing behind him in the rows. Either leading the prayer designates a successor, in which case the succession belongs to 'Amr ibn al-'As, which nobody has ever proposed; or it designates nothing, in which case the argument for Abu Bakr has to be made on some other ground. And then the second half, which does not depend on the first: the man this history follows was never once placed under another man's command, and no one but the Prophet ever stood in front of him to lead a prayer.

The history then gives two smaller reports from the same expeditions, and both of them are about Abu Bakr in his own words.

Abu Bakr had a Fadaki cloak that he used to wear on those expeditions, and when the commanders were appointed some said: By God, do not pledge allegiance to the man of the cloak.

And Rafi' ibn Abi Rafi' al-Ta'i, who is Rafi' ibn 'Umayrah, said: I accompanied Abu Bakr on those expeditions and he would regularly counsel me. He said: I advise you never to take command over two Muslim men.

Then, when he became my guardian and the people chose him, I came to him and said: Abu Bakr, did you not release me from commanding two Muslim men? He said: Yes, and now I am weary of that. I said to him: What made you comply with the people's command? He said: I find no justification for it, but I feared division for the community of Muhammad.

This is the narration of Ibn Abi Rafi': that Abu Bakr commanded him but he did not obey, and advised him but did not take his own advice.

The report is preserved because of its shape, not its content. A man is given a piece of personal counsel on campaign — never take charge of even two Muslims — and years later he is standing in front of the same man, who now has charge of all of them, and he simply asks the question. The answer he gets does not dispute the premise. It concedes there is no justification and names a fear instead.

And the history answers the fear directly. As for Abu Bakr's claim that he feared division for the community of Muhammad — by my life, he brought about the very division he feared. Had he left the guardianship and the command to the one whom the Messenger of God had appointed over the believers, saying, You are to me as Aaron was to Moses, no two people would have disagreed.

The saying invoked there was expounded in full earlier in this history, when the deputyship over Medina was defended during the northern march; it is named here only as the appointment already on the record, and the argument built on it is new. The claim is not that division was bad and unity good. It is that the division actually feared — a community that splinters — is precisely what the attempt to avert it produced, because the one arrangement that nobody could have disputed was the one already made and set aside.

But the Prophet said: You will follow the paths of the Children of Israel. And in another place: You will follow the ways of the nations before you, span by span and cubit by cubit and rung by rung — even if they had entered the burrow of a lizard, you would have entered it.

The Prophet, in saying this, was referring to their disputing with the guardians and their claiming an authority other than the one the prophets had commanded.

That is the frame the whole chapter has been building toward, and it is worth seeing what it does to the material around it. This is not a complaint about one succession. It is a claim that the pattern is old, that it has happened to every community given a guardian, and that it is so exact it can be measured — span by span, cubit by cubit, into a hole a lizard would fit in. Set beside the first two sections of this episode, the effect is deliberate. Those sections are full of rules arriving after the damage: a man killed for saying the words that protected him, and a ruling issued afterwards; a grudge from before Islam carried into a greeting on a road, and a verse sent down afterwards; a commander sent out with four prohibitions tied on with his turban because the fifth thing that goes wrong in war is always the thing nobody forbade. This last section applies the same shape to the succession itself — and argues that here, uniquely, the rule was given first and the damage was done anyway.

## What this episode lands

The detachments are the least ceremonious material in the volume and the most concretely told. A scout takes two arrows and does not move because moving would prove he was alive; a valley floods with no cloud in the sky and separates a raiding party from its pursuers; a raid on people who had already become Muslims is repaired by a second party sent to take the plunder back and hand it over item by item; a man who kills to order is paid in a staff whose only value is a promise about the Day of Resurrection, and is buried with it; a company at the shore lives twenty nights on a creature the sea gave them and rides a horse under its rib; and two men sent to assassinate the chief of Mecca fail entirely and have their failure written down in full.

The rules in this episode nearly all arrive after the harm. A man declares that there is no god but God and is killed anyway, and the ruling that follows takes the judgement out of the raider's hands altogether. A man returning a greeting of peace is killed over a quarrel from the days of ignorance, and the verse names both the act and the motive — the goods of this world — while the tribal claim behind it is settled with blood money by two chiefs who each had a side. A commander is given four prohibitions while his turban is being tied, immediately after five warnings about how a community rots from the inside. And a captive taken by mistake is fed before he believes, converts on his own, blockades Mecca's grain in the Prophet's name, and is told to lift it because the people he is starving are right about kinship.

Then the chapter stops on one detail from one expedition: a young commander leading the prayer for weeks with Abu Bakr, 'Umar and Abu 'Ubaydah in the rows behind him. If leading the prayer names the next leader, it names 'Amr ibn al-'As — and nobody says that. Two reports follow, both in Abu Bakr's own voice: the counsel never to take charge of two Muslims, and, years later, the admission that he could find no justification but feared the community would split. This history's answer is that the split came anyway, that the appointment which would have prevented it had already been made and set aside, and that the pattern was named in advance — span by span and cubit by cubit, all the way into the lizard's burrow.
