## Where this episode picks up

What is the key to the holiest building in Arabia worth? Not as a symbol — as a transaction, in a market town, between two men. The answer this history records is one skin of wine. That is the price the custodian of the Ka'bah accepted for the key of it, and the man who handed over the wine was the great-great-grandfather of the Prophet. The sale started a war, produced a proverb the Arabs were still repeating centuries later, and put the custody of the Sacred House into the family this entire volume is about.

The previous episode traced a line: from Abraham's division of his household, through Ishmael settled at the House, down the generations of the northern Arabs to the tribe called Quraysh, and finally to a single household. This episode is that household's story, and it covers roughly a century and a half in four lives — Qusay, his son 'Abd Manaf, his grandson Hashim, and his great-grandson 'Abd al-Muttalib. It ends in the year the Prophet was born.

What holds the four together is an office, and it is stranger than it sounds: they held the custody of a shrine surrounded, for most of this period, by people worshipping idols in it. The author's persistent claim — the reason he tells any of this — is that what they kept in that setting was Abraham's own religion, inherited as a family charge.

Three movements, then. How the custody was won and organised, by a man whose own name is a record of childhood exile. What one of his grandsons did with it — a sermon delivered annually with his back to the door of the Ka'bah, and a trade compact that turned the sanctuary's prestige into safe passage for caravans. And an invading army with an elephant, an emptied city, one man who refused to leave, and the sentence he said to the general who had come to demolish the building he was charged with.

Watch the names as we go, because in this family they are not decorative: one of these men is called "far away", one "the breaker", and one carries for life a name meaning he was once mistaken for a slave.

## The Gatherer of Mecca

Qusay was not born to the office, and how he came back to it is the first thing the author tells us about him. His father, Kilab ibn Murrah, died while Qusay was still a small child. His older brother Zuhrah was already grown; their mother was Fatima, daughter of Sa'd ibn Sa'il. After Kilab's death a man named Rabi'ah ibn Haram — of the Quda'a, from the north — came to Mecca, married the widow, and took her back to his own people, the Banu 'Udhra. She took the small boy with her. Zuhrah, being a man by then, stayed behind with his father's people.

So a child of the Meccan house was raised in his stepfather's tribe, hundreds of miles away — and his mother marked it in what she called him. His name had been Zayd; she named him Qusay, which means "far away", because he had been carried far from his own country. It is a hard name to give a boy: a permanent, public reminder of what he had lost.

He grew up in Rabi'ah's household, and the author says he yearned to return and follow his origins. When the pilgrims of Quda'a set out for Mecca he went with them, and when he arrived he simply stayed.

The author gives him a portrait rather than a résumé: handsome, slender, composed, wise, commanding reverence, chaste, and generous. Then the note he never omits when a link in the chain comes into focus — when God honoured Qusay by making him the forefather of His Messenger, and caused the good lineage to flow through his descendants, He brought the man's qualities to their fullest perfection in virtue and honour. The character is not the cause of the selection; it accompanies it.

His descendants were remembered in verse for exactly that — al-Harith ibn Zalim praised the Banu Qusay as the honourable among all living people, generous in lineage and free of folly. And the offices they held are worth listing, because they are what custody of the House actually consisted of: the doorkeeping of the shrine, the watering of the pilgrims, the provisioning that fed them, the leadership of the tribe, and the war-banner carried when it fought. Five functions — and the author is explicit about the terms on which the family understood them. They upheld by inheritance the religion of their fathers Abraham and Ishmael: welcoming guests, providing for the pilgrim, honouring the sanctuary, protecting it from injustice and impiety, suppressing the oppressor, and defending the oppressed. That is a job description with a moral content.

But the custody had to be taken from somebody, and here we arrive at the wine.

The key of the Ka'bah was in the hands of Abu Ghabshan al-Khuza'i, who was then the House's custodian. Qusay met him at Ta'if and bought the key from him for a skin of wine. Then he went to his own people and presented it in an entirely different register: "This is the key of your father Ishmael, which Allah has returned to you without injustice or treachery." Two framings, side by side. In the market it was a purchase from a man who wanted a drink more than he wanted the office; in Mecca it was a restoration — an inheritance returning to its line, and returning cleanly.

Not everyone accepted the sale. A woman came forward and blamed her brother, saying: "I had pledged it to him — I did not sell it." A pledge is security against a debt and can be redeemed; a sale is final. On her account the key had been pawned, not sold. War broke out over exactly that.

The Arabs remembered the transaction in a proverb — "more regrettable than the deal of Abu Ghabshan" — and in verse that blamed Khuza'a's own elder rather than the buyer: if your elder sold what he was holding, the poet says, blame him, and do not press Qusay over what he pursued.

The war itself was not one-sided. Qusay fought Khuza'a and Banu Bakr, and the striking detail is what his own tribe did when he called them: the Quraysh hesitated and held back. His support came from the family that had raised him. His brother Razah ibn Rabi'ah — the son of the stepfather who took him north — gathered his own people and those who obeyed him among the northern tribes and marched to Mecca to fight for him. Razah put the campaign into verse afterwards, and the surviving lines carry its shape: a summons answered without delay, a night march pressed until morning, tribes gathered from every direction, the arrival at Mecca, and the fighting, where the swords decided the matter. Only when the northerners had moved did the Quraysh come in behind them.

So the man raised far away won Mecca with the help of the people who had raised him. What he did with the victory was clear the sanctuary: he expelled Khuza'a from the sacred precinct for belittling its sanctity and working destruction inside it.

The author also gives a second account of how the custody came to him, without harmonising it with the first. Qusay became engaged to Hubba, daughter of Halil ibn Habashiyyah ibn Salul al-Khuza'i. Halil had himself been the Ka'bah's custodian and the commander of Mecca, and when he recognised Qusay's stature he gave him his daughter in marriage. She bore him four sons: 'Abd al-Dar, 'Abd Manaf, 'Abd al-'Uzza, and 'Abd. A purchase, a war, and a marriage into the outgoing custodian's own house — the record keeps all three.

What followed was administration, the least glamorous and most consequential part of his life. He was the first to command Mecca and to oversee the pilgrims, taking charge of the doorkeeping, the watering, and every other affair of the town. He received the pilgrims, fed and watered them, and honoured them with generosity — bread and meat, and in the verse the Arabs remembered him by, pure milk and crumbled bread, from a man described as never wearying, his eyes always open. He gave his son 'Abd Manaf the watering rights, the provisioning, and the council house. He built that council house — the Dar al-Nadwa — with its door facing the Ka'bah, so the town's deliberations happened in sight of the building they were about. Then he divided Mecca into four quarters among the clans of Quraysh.

A shrine with a staffed administration, a food and water system for visitors, a permanent chamber for decisions, a surveyed town: that is the founding of a settled polity. He was called "the Gatherer", and the poet explains the title exactly — your father Qusay was called the Gatherer, and through him God assembled the clans of Fihr.

Muhammad ibn Jubayr, who traced his own descent from this family, records the scale of it: Qusay ibn Kilab, he said, was the first to reign as a king among the sons of Ka'b ibn Lu'ayy. And with all that glory, the author adds, the distinction passed to 'Abd Manaf ibn Qusay and became his — because God bestows His bounty on whom He wills, and He placed the seed of prophethood in Qusay's line and caused it to take root through his heirs.

'Abd Manaf was, the author says, the finest of the four brothers, for God had singled him out to be the Prophet's forefather. The tradition preserves a saying attached to his name, reported as an inscription commanding the fear of God, and the author's interest in it is narrow: what matters is that this man commanded only the fear of God, which he takes as one more proof of the fathers' great station. The poets add the rest — more honourable than 'Abd Manaf in lineage, one of them says, no mother ever bore a child of finer stock.

The succession did not pass quietly. Because the offices had been split among Qusay's sons, the Banu 'Abd Manaf and the Banu 'Abd al-Dar held different parts of the same inheritance, and the Banu 'Abd Manaf — known as the good ones — sought to take the war-banner and the doorkeeping of the shrine from their cousins. The Banu 'Abd al-Dar refused and formed a pact with other clans to block them.

Both sides then swore their alliance with a gesture. On one side a woman of the family, Umm Hakim, daughter of 'Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim, brought perfume to the shrine, set it out at the stone and put her hand into it, declaring that whoever had shown kindness to her kinsman was one of them. Those who dipped their hands with her — Banu 'Abd Manaf, Banu Asad, Banu Zuhrah, Banu Taym, and Banu al-Harith — were called the Perfumed. Banu Sahm answered with the opposite image: they slaughtered a camel, plunged their hands into its blood, and said that whoever licked the blood was one of them. Those who did — Banu Sahm, Banu 'Adi, Banu 'Abd al-Dar, Banu Jumah, and Banu Makhzum — were called the Confederates.

Perfume against blood, in front of the same building. And then, the author reports, nothing further passed between them: two blocs now existed inside Quraysh, each knowing exactly who was in it, and neither fought. That standoff is the political map the Prophet would be born into.

The poets also associate 'Abd Manaf's house with the two great trade journeys, the winter one and the summer one, and with a covenant that made them possible — the family, one of them says, who took the covenant across the horizons. What that covenant was the verses do not say. It is explained by what 'Abd Manaf's son did next.

## Hashim's charge to Quraysh

The honour of 'Abd Manaf passed, by God's grace, to his son — and the son's name was not Hashim. It was 'Amr, and he was called 'Amr the Exalted on account of his high standing. Hashim is a nickname meaning "the breaker", earned because he was the first to break bread, crumble it into broth, and feed it to the people: a man remembered by history under the name of a kitchen action.

Before turning to his life, the author inserts what looks like a digression and is in fact the doctrinal centre of this episode — a long tradition of the Prophet. He says plainly why he put it here: because it is through Hashim's lineage that the best of all households descends, an undeniable virtue established on sound authority. The tradition is his proof that this family line was not one distinguished house among several.

It opens with a piece of stagecraft. The Prophet ordered Bilal ibn Hamamah to call the noon prayer one day before its time, in the month of Rajab, thirteen nights in. Bilal did so, and the people stirred, saying, "Something has happened." They gathered in the mosque. The Messenger of God came walking until he reached one of the gates and took hold of both its sides. Then he called out: "Do you hear, O people of al-Saddah?" They answered, "We hear and we obey, O Messenger of Allah." He said, "Do you wish to know?" And then he said:

"Allah the Almighty created the creation and divided it into two parts, and I was placed in the better of the two parts. This is what He said: the Companions of the Right Hand, how excellent are the Companions of the Right Hand (chapter 56, verse 27). I am of the people of the right, and I am the best among the people of the right hand. Then He divided those two parts into three, and I was placed among the best of them. This is what He said: the Companions of the Right Hand, how excellent are the Companions of the Right Hand; and the Companions of the Left Hand, what of the Companions of the Left Hand; and the foremost, the foremost (chapter 56, verses 8 to 10). I am of the foremost, and I am the best of the foremost. Then He divided the third into tribes, and I was placed among the best of them. This is what He said: O mankind, We created you from a male and a female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another (chapter 49, verse 13). My tribe is the best of all tribes. I hold them in awe before Allah the Almighty, and I am the master of the sons of Adam, and I say this without pride. Then He divided the tribes into clans, and He placed me in the best of them. This is what He said: Allah only wishes to remove impurity from you, O People of the House, and to purify you thoroughly (chapter 33, verse 33)."

The structure is a narrowing funnel, each stage fastened to a verse: creation into two, the two into three, the third into tribes, the tribes into households. Four divisions, and the same claim at every one — placed in the better part. The descent traced in the previous episode was not a genealogist's curiosity; it was a series of selections.

Then the tradition turns from the abstract to a night in the open. God, the Prophet continues, singled out three from his household — and he is the master of all three, and holds them in awe before God, without pride. He was chosen above Ali, Hamza, and Ja'far. They had been resting at al-Abtah, each of them with his garment drawn over his face: Ali on his right, Ja'far on his left, Hamza at his feet. What roused him from sleep was the rustling of angels' wings and a coolness on his chest from Ali's arm. He looked up and found Gabriel standing before three angels. One of them asked: "O Gabriel, to which of these four have you been sent?" Gabriel nudged him with his foot and said, "To this one." The angel asked, "And who is this?" He replied, "This is Muhammad, the master of the messengers." Then he gestured toward Ali and said, "This is the master of the guardians." He gestured toward Hamza and said, "This is the master of the martyrs." He gestured toward Ja'far and said, "This one — in Paradise he has a door with two wings, two gilded wings with which he flies."

Four sleeping men, and an angel identifying each by office. The word used of Ali ibn Abi Talib is the one the last episode kept returning to — the master of the guardians, head of the line of those who carry what a prophet brought. What the author argued abstractly there, from wills and from the impossibility of a community appointing its own foundation, is delivered here instead by an angel, over a group of men asleep in a hollow outside Mecca.

Now, having established the household, back to the man. Hashim held the guardianship of his father's rights in the watering and the provisioning, and he held the presidency in Mecca above all his father's other sons. And once a year, on the first of Dhu al-Hijjah, as the pilgrimage season opened, he would address the Quraysh with his back to the door of the Ka'bah, commanding them to do good and exhorting them to noble character. This is what he said:

"O gathering of Quraysh, you are the masters of the Arabs, the finest of them in face and the greatest of them in forbearance. You are the heartland of the Arabs in lineage and the closest to them in bonds of kinship. O gathering of Quraysh, you are the neighbors of the House of Allah the Almighty — may Allah honor you with its guardianship and single you out from among the sons of Ishmael for nearness to it, and may He preserve in you the best that any neighbor has preserved. Honor the guests of Allah and the visitors of His House, for they come to you travel-worn and dusty from every land. As for my wealth, if all of it were sufficient for you I would give it freely — but I am a steward of my wealth, lawfully directing it, so long as no kinship tie is severed, nothing is taken unjustly, and nothing forbidden is mixed into it.

"And whoever among you wishes to give from his own wealth what he is able to give — I ask you by the sanctity of this House that none of you hold back from providing for the dignity and sustenance of the House's visitors, so long as what is given has not been taken unjustly and no kinship tie has been severed in acquiring it."

It is a short address, built with care. It gives his listeners their honours, then names what those honours rest on: they are neighbours of the House, and their standing is a function of their nearness to something not theirs. The demand follows from the honour rather than overriding it. Then he puts his own money in first and attaches conditions to his own giving before asking anything of anybody — he is a steward of his wealth, not its owner — and only then asks them to give, repeating those same conditions over their contributions. It is an appeal for charity that refuses tainted money on the way in, sworn by the sanctity of the building he is standing against.

The Banu Ka'b ibn Lu'ayy answered earnestly and spent freely. Hashim collected the contributions and stored them in the council house until the pilgrims arrived, and then he fed them. He brought large waterskins from beside Zamzam, filled them, and set them out for the pilgrims to drink. He fed them in Mecca before the day of watering, and on the day of watering itself he fed them at Mina, at 'Arafah, and at the gathering-place: bread distributed to them along with meat, clarified butter, parched barley meal, and dates. He watered them with milk and crumbled the bread for them — and that, the author repeats, is why he was called Hashim. He was also called "the Moon", for his bearing and his beauty. Matrud al-Khuza'i's couplet fixes it: the men of Mecca were gaunt and lean, until 'Amr broke bread for his people.

The second half of his life starts from a problem. He was the first to make the roads of Mecca safe. The Quraysh were a mercantile people, yet their trade had never extended beyond Mecca itself, because the roads out belonged to tribes who would rob them. A merchant town with no safe routes is a market of one.

So Hashim rode to the Levant and presented himself before Caesar, using the method his family had made its trade: every day he slaughtered a sheep, prepared food, and invited everyone around him to eat. Word of his generosity and his bearing reached the emperor, who summoned him, honoured him with special distinction, and showed him great favour. Then Hashim made his request, framed entirely in terms of the emperor's own advantage: "O king, my people are the merchants of the Arabs. If you see fit to write them a letter of safe passage, they will bring you the finest goods from across the Hijaz." Caesar agreed and wrote the letter.

What he did with it solved the actual problem, which was never Byzantium — it was the tribes in between. Whenever he passed through an Arab tribe's territory on the Levant road, he would stop and tell them the Quraysh were carrying goods on their behalf; he would arrange for those goods to be sold and delivered, and return the tribe's capital along with its share of the profit. Every tribe on the route became an investor in the caravan instead of a predator on it. That is the covenant the poets were praising — the compact of safe passage he secured for everyone between Mecca and the Levant.

He returned to Mecca with more blessings than his people had ever known, and they set out with a great caravan. Hashim led them, and they went on benefiting from the compact all the way to the Levant. He died on that journey, at Gaza. Matrud al-Khuza'i mourned him in verses that begin with the plain fact and then refuse it: a grave in Gaza holds Hashim — and he does not pass away. His absence, the poet says, is a loss to everyone who lived in his yard, and the instruction to those left behind is to hold firm to his memory with tongue and with hand.

## The House has a Lord

'Abd al-Muttalib inherited his father's bounty, his generosity, and his noble qualities. He was the Prophet's grandfather. And like everyone else in this family, he is known by a name that records an accident.

His given name was Shaybah al-Hamd — Shaybah, meaning a streak of white, because he was born with a streak of white hair on his head. The circumstances of that birth explain the rest. Hashim had married Salma, daughter of 'Amr, of the Banu al-Najjar of the Khazraj in Yathrib, and brought her to Mecca — but her father had made a condition of the marriage: if she conceived, she would return to give birth among her own people. She conceived in Mecca, and when the pregnancy grew heavy Hashim took her north to Yathrib on the very journey that was carrying him onward to the Levant. He died on that journey. So the boy was born in his mother's town, into his mother's family, with his father already dead somewhere on the road to Gaza.

He grew into a youth of striking bearing. A man of the Banu al-Harith ibn 'Abd Manaf, passing through Yathrib, saw him wrestling two other boys — the most handsome of the three and the most formidable — and noticed that each time he threw an opponent he called out: "I am the son of Hashim! I am the son of the master of al-Batha'!" A fatherless boy in his mother's town, announcing his Meccan paternity every time he won. The traveller, struck by it, told Hashim's brother al-Muttalib ibn 'Abd Manaf when he got back to Mecca.

Al-Muttalib went to Yathrib and brought the boy home, and here the story turns comic, then permanent. When people saw them arriving they asked where the boy had come from, and he said, "From Yathrib." They asked al-Muttalib who this was, and he answered: "A slave I purchased." Then he took him home, dressed him in a new garment, and presented him to the Banu 'Abd Manaf. They asked who this was. He said: "He is the son of your nephew Hashim." He told them the boy's story and described his generosity, and they said: "This one whom we called your slave just yesterday!"

And so the name stuck. 'Abd al-Muttalib means al-Muttalib's slave: the grandfather of the Prophet carried for the whole of his long life a nickname derived from his uncle's evasive answer to a question at the edge of town. Al-Muttalib himself later died in Yemen, at a place said to be Radman, and Matrud al-Khuza'i mourned him and his brothers in an elegy that ends on the bare geography of it — yesterday in Radman he was an exile, and today he is here no more.

'Abd al-Muttalib was raised on noble character, generosity and goodness, and given the title Shaybah al-Hamd, the white streak of praise. Hudhayfah ibn Ghanem praised his house in verses about a night shining bright as the full moon over an elder with a radiant face — generous to pilgrims, walking his father's path, pardoning the offence that other men avenge.

Those verses had an occasion, reported from Ja'far ibn Muhammad ibn Ali ibn al-Husayn on the authority of his forebears. Two lepers had fled their colony and kidnapped a man from the households of Mecca; the man was Hudhayfah, bound and being marched away. 'Abd al-Muttalib met them on his way back from Ta'if. He had lost his sight by then, and his son Abu Lahab was leading him. When Hudhayfah cried out, 'Abd al-Muttalib ordered Abu Lahab to ransom him on the spot, with twenty ounces of gold, ten camels, and a mare. A blind old man, led by the hand, buying a stranger's freedom from kidnappers at the roadside, at a price he set himself. The poem is the receipt.

Which brings us to the account for which he is chiefly remembered, and which the author offers as evidence of his virtue, his honour, and his unshakeable trust in his Lord.

Abraha al-Ashram, the Abyssinian viceroy of Yemen, marched north with the declared intention of destroying the Sacred House of God, bringing with him an elephant named Yaksom. He met resistance twice on the way and defeated it twice. First a Yemeni nobleman, Dhu Nafr — one of Yemen's great lords and kings — called his own people and whatever Arabs would answer him to fight in defence of the House; Abraha beat him, took him captive, and marched on with him in his train. Then, at a mountain pass, Abraha confronted Nafil ibn Habib al-Khath'ami with two tribes, routed them, and captured Nafil too. When Abraha meant to kill him, Nafil made an offer: "Do not kill me — spare me and I will guide you wherever you wish." So the man who had come out to fight the invasion became its guide.

As the armies neared the sanctuary and approached Mecca, the Quraysh made a clear-eyed assessment: they had no means of resisting by force. So they withdrew from the town. All of them but one. 'Abd al-Muttalib stood firm and said: "By Allah, I shall not abandon the Haram. I seek no glory elsewhere." He remained in the city while his people dispersed into the hills, and he put his refusal into verse as well as into speech.

The author records the effect on his standing. When the Quraysh saw how completely he had forbidden himself to God and placed his trust in Him, he rose greatly in their esteem — and the author reads the decision not as bravado but as a mark of sound and penetrating judgment in divine affairs. He was not staying because he thought he could win.

Then the army, camped on the outskirts, seized his camels and drove them off. Word of the seizure reached Abraha, along with reports of who this man was and what he counted for among his people. Abraha sent for him. When he arrived, the general marvelled at his bearing, his dignity and his commanding presence; he honoured him, seated him beside himself, and asked whether he had a request to present.

And 'Abd al-Muttalib said: "Your army has seized my camels — I ask that you return them to me."

Abraha was astonished, and his reply is the sharpest line in the whole account: "You have diminished yourself in my eyes and lowered your standing with me. You come to ask about your camels, yet you say nothing of this House that I have come to demolish — does it mean nothing to you?"

The answer came back: "Those camels are mine, and I am their owner. As for the House, it has a Lord who will protect it from you if He wills."

It reads at first like the most disappointing sentence a custodian of a shrine could produce, and Abraha heard it that way. It is not indifference but a division of jurisdiction. The camels are his property and he will petition for them, because that is his business. The House is not his: he keeps its keys, feeds its visitors and defends its dignity, but he does not own it, and its defence is not finally his to manage. Standing alone in the emptied town and speaking that line in the tent are the same position twice — he will not abandon his post, and he will not pretend the outcome depends on him.

The reply made him greater in Abraha's eyes rather than smaller, and his words carried enormous weight with the general. Abraha ordered the camels returned.

Then God vindicated the words of 'Abd al-Muttalib: "And He sent against them birds in flocks, striking them with stones of baked clay" (chapter 105, verses 3 and 4).

The tradition preserves the detail. The birds resembled swallows or martins. Each carried three stones — one in its beak and one in each claw — roughly the size of chickpeas or lentils. Any man struck by one perished, though not every man was struck. The army broke and fled, scattering down the road back to Yemen. Nafil, the captured guide, watched the wrath descend and cried out a single line: "Where is the refuge for one whom God pursues? The victorious has no escape." His other verses are those of a man marched north against his will who cannot believe what he is seeing: he greets the morning, marvels at what his own party was spared, and thanks God for the sight of the birds.

Abraha himself was struck in his body. They carried him away as his limbs came off him piece by piece, each inch dropping away and leaving a trail behind it, until they brought him into San'a' in a state like a fledgling chick. He did not die until his chest split open.

'Abd al-Muttalib composed verses on the events, and the clearest of them states the claim his family had been making for four generations: we are the family of God in His town, as it has ever been since the age of Abraham; whoever seeks to corrupt it will be silenced; the House has a God who defends it — even in the ancient days it was cast into strife, and it was preserved. The rest describes the army left overwhelmed the next morning, the elephant led down to the lowest ground, the stones lodging fast, the eagles scavenging the remains and circling as mourners circle — and ends on the ground itself, a land whose well of Zamzam was dug, with a prayer that God bequeath them the finest of lands.

The author's conclusion is plain. There was a clear sign and a lasting lesson in the destruction of the Companions of the Elephant, by which God protected His Sacred House and forbade its violation. He manifested the virtue of its guardian, and He vindicated 'Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim — confirming His promise to him and answering the words he had spoken, that the House has a Lord who protects it. And He answered his supplication.

And then the sentence the episode has been travelling toward, set down almost without ceremony. That same year — the Year of the Elephant — saw the birth of Muhammad al-Mustafa, master of all humanity and seal of the prophets. This, he says, was among the blessings heralding his birth.

Four generations of custody, and then in the same twelve months the House is defended by its Lord and the child is born in the town it stands in. For this author those are not two coincident events but one event described from two directions.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry forward.

The first is that the custody of the Sacred House became a working institution, built by a man who had been carried out of Mecca as a small boy and named for the distance. Qusay came back with a pilgrim caravan, bought a key for a skin of wine, fought a war with his stepfather's people because his own tribe hesitated, and then did the unglamorous work: doorkeeping, watering, provisioning, a council house facing the shrine, a town in quarters. Those offices are what his descendants inherit, and the author insists on what they were for — Abraham's charge to feed the visitor, honour the sanctuary, restrain the oppressor and defend the oppressed.

The second is what Hashim did with that inheritance. Once a year, with his back to the door of the Ka'bah, he told the Quraysh their honour was a function of their nearness to something not their own, and asked them to pay for the pilgrims' dignity — refusing in advance any money from a severed kinship tie or an unjust taking. Then he took the same instinct onto the northern road, secured a letter of safe passage from Caesar, and turned every tribe between Mecca and the Levant from a threat into a partner with a share of the profit.

The third is a sentence spoken in a general's tent by a blind old man who had refused to leave an emptied city. Asked why he petitioned for camels when the building he guarded was about to be demolished, he distinguished what was his from what was not: the camels are mine, and the House has a Lord who will protect it. Within days the army was scattering down the road to Yemen, and within the year the child was born whose life this volume is about.

Which is where the history proper begins. The line has been traced, the custody established and defended, and the year has arrived. What comes next is the birth itself, and the childhood of a boy who lost his father before he was born and was raised, for a time, by the man who stood alone in front of the elephant.
