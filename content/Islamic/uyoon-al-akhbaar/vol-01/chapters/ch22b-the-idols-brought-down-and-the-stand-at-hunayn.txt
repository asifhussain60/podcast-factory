## Where this episode opens

How long does it take a community that has just watched the idols of Mecca shatter on the ground to ask for a sacred tree of its own?

The previous episode closed at the door of the Ka'bah, with an address that put every inherited claim of blood and rank underfoot and sent the assembled Quraysh home free. This one opens the next morning, with the building itself still ringed by the objects the address had made unthinkable, and with a man being lifted onto prophetic shoulders to clear the roof.

Three movements. First the clearing and what pulled against it: idols hurled from the roof, a shrine in the hills pulled down — and then, within days, two returns of the old appetite. A confederation marching to defend the shrines, and a group of new Muslims asking, on the road, for a tree they can hang their weapons on. Then the valley of Hunayn, where the largest army this community has ever assembled is ambushed in the dark and breaks as one man, and where a verse afterwards names the cause of the collapse as the pleasure the army took in its own size. Then the aftermath, which is where this history puts its clearest rulings: who may not be killed, what is owed to a woman with a bite mark on her back, and what a defeated enemy's counsellor says to the young man who does not recognise him.

Listen for how the recovery is achieved. Not by a charge and not by a manoeuvre, but by a voice calling people back by the names of things they had once stood inside.

## The idols and the tree

The next morning, the Messenger of God approached the Ka'bah, which was ringed with idols, and he began to break and destroy them.

Then he saw idols mounted atop the Ka'bah, and said to Ali ibn Abi Talib: O Ali, place me upon your shoulders so that I may climb up and break those idols on the roof of the Ka'bah.

Ali replied: No, O Messenger of God — rather, you mount my shoulders.

The Prophet said: O Ali, even if all the people of the earth were gathered and tried to carry me for as long as they could, they could not do what I command you to do.

The refusal is ordinary deference. The answer does not argue with it but states a fact about capacity: the whole population of the earth, working together for as long as they could stand it, would not accomplish what is being asked of one man now. And what is being asked is only that he stand still.

Ali said: I mounted the shoulders of the Prophet, and he raised me up to such a height that, had I wished, I could have reached the sky and touched its stars — and I did what I was commanded.

When he climbed onto the roof of the Ka'bah, he hurled the idols down to the earth and they shattered. Then, feeling abashed before the Messenger of God, he leapt from the roof to the ground and landed upright. The Messenger of God smiled at him.

The Messenger of God said: Why do you smile, O Ali?

He said: O Messenger of God, I did not expect to land so well from such a height.

The Prophet said: You stood upon the shoulder of Muhammad, and I descended upon the shoulder of Jibril.

That last sentence relocates the entire scene. Everything before it has been physical: a lift, a height, a throw, and a good landing that surprised the man who made it. The sentence takes the jump and puts it in a series. If the ascent was made on the shoulders of the Messenger, the descent was made on the shoulders of the one who brings the revelation — so the landing was never a matter of the man's own body, and the smile was at something he had not yet been told the shape of.

The Messenger of God then ordered that every idol in the vicinity of Mecca be destroyed. Among them was a shrine held in great veneration by the Quraysh and Banu Kinana and all who were associated with it. Its custodians and its veil belonged to the Bani Shayban of Banu Sulaym, who were allies of Banu Hashim.

When the guardian of the shrine heard what was coming, he hung his sword upon it and leaned against the mountain in which it stood, saying verses that have come down damaged: O al-'Uzza, however fierce the trial that Khalid brings upon you — I cast off my cloak and throw it aside. My father rushes forward, or you are a Christian. Oh — if you do not kill a man, you live forever.

Khalid ibn al-Walid was among those sent to demolish it, and when they arrived they tore it down.

Those lines are broken past mending in this manuscript, and only what still reads is given here; nothing has been reconstructed to fill the holes. The gesture around them is unambiguous: the man who keeps the shrine hangs his own sword on the goddess and stands aside against the rock. He arms it and watches. It is the last recorded act of that custodianship, and it consists of asking the idol to defend itself.

The Conquest of Mecca took place with ten nights remaining in the month of Ramadan, in the eighth year of the Hijrah. The Messenger of God stayed in Mecca for fifteen nights.

When the Hawazin tribes heard what had happened, and what God had granted him in the conquest of Mecca, Malik ibn 'Awf al-Nasri rallied them. He gathered the Hawazin, all of Thaqif, all of Nasr and Jushm, Sa'd ibn Bakr, and a few from Banu Hilal. Of all the clans of Qays 'Aylan, only these few took part; the rest were absent. The Hawazin brought with them Ka'b and Kilab, though none of those clans distinguished themselves by name.

Among Banu Jushm was a great elder, Duraid ibn al-Sammah — a man whose only remaining value was his judgment and experience in warfare, and who was carried in a litter. Among Banu Thaqif there were Qarib ibn al-Aswad ibn Mas'ud ibn Mu'athib, and among Banu Malik there were Dhu al-Khimar Subay' ibn al-Harith ibn Malik and his brother Ahmar ibn al-Harith. Overall command fell to Malik ibn 'Awf al-Nasri.

When Malik resolved to march, he led the people out along with their wealth, their wives, and their children. When he descended upon Awtas, the people gathered around him, including Duraid, who was carried in his litter to that place.

Duraid asked: In what valley are you camped? They said: In Awtas. He said: Good terrain for horses — neither rocky enough to injure hooves nor so soft as to swallow them. But what is this I do not hear? No lowing of camels, no braying of donkeys, no crying of children, no wailing of women?

They told him that Malik ibn 'Awf had led the people out with their wealth, their wives, and their children.

An old man who cannot see the camp is reading it by ear, and what he notices is an absence. The terrain he assesses from a description; the disaster he detects from a silence — livestock and families not where they should be, which here means they are somewhere much worse.

He said: Bring him to me. When Malik came before him, Duraid said: O Malik, you have become the leader of your people, and this day will have days after it. Why do I hear no lowing of camels, no braying of donkeys, no crying of children, no wailing of women?

Malik replied: I brought the people along with their wealth, their children, and their wives.

Duraid said: And why is that?

Malik said: I wanted every man to feel that his family and his wealth were behind him, so that he would fight to defend them.

Duraid said: By God, you think like a shepherd of sheep. Does a routed man ever save anything? In the end, nothing helps a man but his own sword and spear. If the battle goes against you, you will have exposed your family and your wealth.

The argument is complete in four sentences and it is entirely about what happens after a defeat. Malik's reasoning concerns morale before the fighting. Duraid does not dispute that a man will fight harder; he points out that the calculation only holds if the battle cannot be lost, and that losing converts a defeat into the loss of everything the tribe owns and everyone it loves. There is no answer to it.

Then he asked: And what of the Kilab and Ka'b — did they witness this gathering? They said: None of them came. He said: The men of resolve and seriousness were absent. Had this been the day of al-'Ala' and Rifa'a, neither Kilab nor Ka'b would have stayed away. Who from Amir's people is with you? They said: 'Amr ibn 'Amir and 'Awf ibn 'Amir. He said: Those two branches of Amir will neither help nor harm.

O Malik, you have accomplished nothing by bringing the women and wealth before the horses. Send them back to the highlands and put the fighting men on horseback. If you win, what lies behind you is yours; if you lose, at least you have preserved your family and your wealth.

Malik said: By God, I will not do that. You have grown old and your mind has weakened with your age.

Then Malik called out: Obey me, O assembly of Hawazin. Or I will fall upon this sword until it comes out through my back.

They disliked that Duraid ibn al-Sammah should have any say or influence in the matter, and so they said: We obey you.

Notice what actually decides it, because it is not the argument. The argument was won by the man in the litter. What settles the question is a threat of suicide from the commander and, behind it, a preference among the men themselves: they did not want the old man to have influence. So they sided with the man whose reasoning had just been dismantled in front of them, for reasons that have nothing to do with the reasoning.

Duraid ibn al-Sammah said: This is a day I have not witnessed before, and it has not stirred wonder in me. His verses on it survive only as fragments — a wish for a trunk to hide within, a leading and an extinguishing of the impulse, a crack.

Then Malik said to the people: When you see them, break off the hilts of your swords, then charge them as one man.

When the Messenger of God was preparing to march against Hawazin, he was reminded that Safwan ibn Umayyah possessed shields and weapons. He sent to Safwan, who was at that time still a polytheist, and said: O Safwan, lend us what you have of weapons so that we may meet our enemy tomorrow.

Safwan said: O Muhammad, is this by compulsion?

The Prophet said: It is a guaranteed loan, to be returned to you in full.

Safwan said: In that case, there is no objection.

It is said that he borrowed from him a hundred shields, along with the weapons and equipment to carry them.

Two questions and two answers, and the transaction is settled. Safwan asks the only thing that matters to a man in his position — whether this is a request or a requisition dressed as one — and the answer names the legal form of the transfer and adds nothing: no appeal to the new order, no reminder of who now holds the city. He is a polytheist arming the army that took Mecca from his own people, on the strength of a stated term.

When word came of Malik ibn 'Awf's movements, the Messenger of God departed Mecca on the twelfth — with ten thousand men, plus two thousand from the people of Mecca and ten thousand of his companions who had marched with him to the conquest. The arithmetic as the manuscript prints it does not resolve, and it is left standing rather than corrected into a clean total.

As the Messenger of God marched with the Muslims, 'Abbas ibn Mirdas al-Sulami composed verses that survive in poor condition: from me, a letter of counsel, plainly set forth — carry it to Hawazin, to great and small alike; his army fills the breadth of earth, its farthest corners; I say the Messenger of God shall meet you in the morning. And a closing line that still reads clearly: the earth near trembled beneath his awe.

When the Muslims reached a place called Dhat Anwat — a tree the Quraysh had venerated in the time of ignorance, coming to it every year, hanging their weapons upon it, slaughtering animals beside it, and staying near it for a day — some of those who had recently embraced Islam called out: O Messenger of God, make for us a Dhat Anwat as they have one.

The Messenger of God said: God is greater. By the One in whose hand is the soul of Muhammad, you have said what the people of Moses said to Moses: Make for us a god as they have gods. He said: You are a people who are ignorant (chapter 7, verse 138). It is the same pattern — you would follow the ways of those who came before you.

Set that request beside the roof of the Ka'bah, days earlier and a short ride away. What surfaces on the road is not a wish for those idols back; it is a wish for the same practice under new management — a tree of our own, for our weapons, on our calendar. And the answer does not treat it as a beginner's error to be gently corrected. It reaches for the sharpest available parallel: a people just brought through the sea who asked for a god because other people had gods, and were told they were ignorant. The request has a shape, and the shape is older than any object it attaches to.

## The stand at Hunayn

When the Messenger of God descended into the valley of Hunayn, he moved through a wide, hollow valley of the Tihama region in the darkness before dawn. The Hawazin and those with them had arrived before him and lay in ambush in the ravines, bends, and narrow passages of the valley. They had gathered, drawn up their ranks, and made ready.

The Muslims, advancing through that valley in loose order, were suddenly overwhelmed and broke into flight as one man; the men turned and retreated, and no one could be blamed.

Read that last clause carefully, because it is the book's own verdict and it is unusual. This history has recorded elsewhere who ran and who stood; here it declines to. Twelve thousand men are ambushed in the dark in a defile, in loose column, by an enemy already deployed, and the judgment offered is that the rout was not a moral event.

The Messenger of God turned to the right and called out: Come to me. Where are you going? I am the Messenger of God. I am Muhammad ibn 'Abdullah.

The people fled in defeat. Some of the hypocrites revealed what lay in their hearts. Abu Sufyan said: This is a rout that will not be stopped until it reaches the sea. And another cried aloud: Is not the magic defeated today?

That first line is spoken by the man who, days before, stood at the bend of the pass counting tribes and conceded the word prophethood. He is now inside the army he conceded to, watching it break, and what comes out of him is a prediction offered by a professional rather than an accusation. The second voice does something worse — it treats the defeat as a verdict on the claim, which is the argument the community had been hearing from outside for twenty years, now heard from inside its own column.

Five men of the Banu 'Abd al-Muttalib stood firm with the Messenger of God: Ali ibn Abi Talib, sword in hand, shielding and striking for him; al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib, who held the bridle of the Messenger's mule, and the Prophet was riding his white mule; Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib; al-Fadl ibn al-'Abbas; and al-'Abbas himself. It was also said that Qatham was not among them, but rather Ja'far ibn Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith.

The enumeration does not add cleanly to five, and the variant reading is carried beside it rather than resolved. What the list establishes is the composition: of twelve thousand men, the ones still standing at the centre are one household, and among them the man with the sword out is named first.

The Messenger of God said to al-'Abbas, who was a man with a powerful voice: Call to the people and let them know where I am.

The people had been routed, as God has described in His words: Indeed, God has given you victory on many fields — and on the day of Hunayn, when your great numbers pleased you but availed you nothing, and the earth, vast as it is, seemed to close in upon you; then you turned back in flight. Then God sent down His tranquillity upon His Messenger and upon the believers (chapter 9, verses 25 to 26) — meaning those who remained steadfast with the Messenger of God when the people fled.

This is the only battle in this volume where the community's own strength is named as the cause of what went wrong. Not the terrain, not the ambush, not the loose order — the pleasure taken in the numbers. Every earlier engagement was fought at a disadvantage reported as a disadvantage. The first fight entered with an overwhelming advantage is the first that opens with a rout, and the verse makes the two facts one fact.

Shayba ibn 'Uthman ibn Abi Talha had resolved to kill the Messenger of God. He said: Today I shall avenge my father — his father having been killed on the day of Uhud. He said: I drew close to the Messenger of God, but my heart was overwhelmed — it was as though something closed over me and held me back.

The account is his own, told afterwards, and it is a failure he cannot explain by anything outside himself. Nobody stopped him; he got close. What he reports is an interior closing, and the word he reaches for is a covering.

Ali ibn Abi Talib looked and saw the man carrying the polytheists' standard — mounted on a camel, banner in hand — driving into the Muslims and pressing them hard as they fell back before Hawazin, with the enemy behind him. Ali charged him, struck the camel's leg with his sword, felled the beast, and split the man's head open. The fallen camel became a barrier between the Muslims and the polytheists.

Al-'Abbas called out at the top of his voice: O assembly of Muslims. O assembly of the Muhajirun. O assembly of the Ansar. O people of the Tree. O people of the Pledge of al-Ridwan — come to your Prophet.

From every direction came the reply: Labbaik. Labbaik.

Those who had fled were already beginning to realise that the Messenger of God had not been killed and had not abandoned those who had turned back. Each man who wished to wheel his horse or camel around found he could not do so in the press and crush of the crowd, so he would take his weapons, cast himself from his mount, and make his way on foot to the Messenger of God.

The call is the turning point of the battle, and it is worth noticing what it is made of. Five vocatives moving from the general to the particular: Muslims, then the two great divisions of the community, then two names that are not organisational at all — the people of the Tree, and the people of the pledge given under it, the oath this history followed at al-Hudaybiyah. He is not summoning an army. He is naming aloud the occasions on which these men have already committed themselves, and asking them to be the same men.

And the physical detail underneath it is the evidence that it worked. The press is so tight that a mounted man cannot turn his animal around, so men throw themselves off and come back on foot, through a crowd going the other way.

When the polytheist standard-bearer was struck down, the enemy could no longer hold their position. Their formation collapsed, their order dissolved, and their affairs fell into disarray. God struck them in their faces and supported His Messenger with a humiliation of the enemy the like of which had not been seen before, just as He has described in His Book. The last of those who had fled did not return until the captives were already sitting in the hands of the Messenger of God, and the spoils had been collected and were present.

Ali ibn Abi Talib had on that day of tribulation a role that no one else matched. When the Ansar returned to the Messenger of God they stood well, and the Muhajirun were patient. The Messenger of God oversaw the men from his mount, watching as they fought, and said: Now the furnace burns hot.

The Messenger of God turned to Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib during the rout, while he was holding the Prophet's mule, and asked: Who is this? He said: I am your cousin, O Messenger of God. And he held firm at that hour, and his Islam proved good when he had embraced it.

This is the cousin refused an audience on the road to Mecca in the previous episode, admitted only after threatening to walk into the desert with his son. He is now holding the bridle in the one place nobody else has stayed, and the answer he gives is the same claim that was rejected when it was made on his behalf. It is accepted here without a word, because of where he is standing when he makes it.

The Prophet saw Umm Sulaym bint Milhan. She was with her husband Abu Talha, standing firm in the midst of the battle, pregnant with 'Abdullah ibn Abi Talhah, and Abu Talha's mount was with her. Afraid that the camel might throw her off, she gripped its head and plunged her hand into the halter, and the Messenger of God spoke to her.

She said: Yes, by my father and mother, O Messenger of God — kill those who flee from you, as you kill those who fight you, for they deserve it.

The Messenger of God said: O mother, God is sufficient.

It is said she had a dagger with her. Abu Talha asked her: What is that dagger for, O Umm Sulaym? She said: I took it so that if any polytheist came near me, I would plunge it into him. Abu Talha said: Do you not hear, O Messenger of God, what Umm Sulaym al-Rumaysa' is saying? — the name referring to the dirt that collects in the corner of the eye.

Her sentence is the harshest thing anyone says in the episode, and it is aimed at her own side: a proposal to treat the rout as a capital matter, made by a pregnant woman holding a camel steady in the middle of it, and refused in four words that do not argue with her. The book has already given its verdict on the flight — no one could be blamed — and here is the alternative, spoken aloud on the field and declined.

## After the rout

When the people were routed, Malik ibn 'Awf said, struck by what had befallen him, verses that survive only in fragments: this was a day of denial — a man like me, a man like you, shield and shield; if the company loses one day and the burden falls, then I drove back ranks upon ranks; when the nobles howl and flee from the thrust of the spear; O Zayd, son of their concern — where do you flee; the white banners have yielded to the long red lances. Banu Sulaym had joined the march under al-Dahhak ibn Sufyan al-Kilabi.

Abu Qatadah al-Ansari said: On the day of Hunayn, I saw two men fighting — one a Muslim and the other a polytheist — and a man from among the polytheists was trying to get his companion the upper hand over the Muslim. So I went at him, struck his hand, and cut it off. He then seized me with his other hand and held me so tightly that I feared he would kill me, but the bleeding weakened him until he fell, and I struck him and killed him.

When Hawazin was defeated, Thaqif suffered losses at Banu Malik, with seventy men killed under their banner, among them 'Uthman ibn 'Abd Allah ibn Rabi'ah ibn al-Harith ibn Habib. Their banner had been carried by Dhu al-Khimar, and when he was killed, 'Uthman ibn 'Abd Allah took it up and fought under it until he too was killed. When he fell, the Messenger of God said: May God keep him away — he hated the Quraysh. One of the Muslim women remarked on this.

That is a man dying under a banner he picked up from a dead man, and the word said over him is a dismissal with a reason attached — not that he fought, but that he hated a tribe. The note that a Muslim woman had something to say about it is left as unspecified as the manuscript leaves it.

The banner of the allied forces was held by the son of Ibn al-Aswad. When the people were routed, he propped it against a tree, and then he, his cousins, and his kinsmen fled from the alliance. Of the allied forces, only two men were killed: a man known as Wahb, and another from Banu Kubbah known as al-Jalah. When word reached the Messenger of God that al-Jalah had been killed, he said: Today the master of the youth of Thaqif was killed — except for the son of Hunaida, meaning al-Harith ibn Uwais.

When the polytheists were routed, they fell back to al-Ta'if with Malik ibn 'Awf. Some fled in haste while others headed toward a valley, and the cavalry of the Messenger of God pursued them, pressing through the palm groves rather than following the well-worn path of the passes.

Rabi'ah ibn Rafi' al-Sulami caught up with Duraid ibn al-Sammah, taking hold of the halter of his camel. He mistook Duraid for a woman, since he was riding in a litter, and intended to take him captive. But the man was an aged elder, and Rabi'ah did not recognise him.

Duraid said to him: What do you want, young man? He replied: I will kill you. Duraid said: And who are you? He said: I am Rabi'ah ibn Rafi' al-Sulami.

He struck him with his sword, but it did nothing.

Duraid told him: Your mother has given you a poor weapon. Take that sword from behind the litter, strike with it, raise it above the bones, and bring it down on the brain — for that is how I used to strike men. But if you have lost your wits, then go back to your mother and tell her you have killed Duraid ibn al-Sammah, and there will come a day when you will have kept your women from harm.

When Duraid fell and was exposed, his thighs were like parchment from a lifetime in the saddle.

When Rabi'ah returned to his mother and told her he had killed Duraid, she said: By God, he freed three of your mothers.

Take that sequence slowly, because it is the strangest thing in the episode. A young man who does not know who he has caught tries to kill him with a sword that will not do the job, and the old man — told all campaign that his judgment is worthless — corrects his technique with the authority of a man describing his own practice. Then the instruction about what to tell his mother, the only part of the exchange that concerns Duraid himself. And her answer converts the whole thing into a debt: three women of your own family were freed by that man. He had ransomed them in some earlier year, and the son has just killed him without knowing.

The Messenger of God sent Abu 'Amir al-Ash'ari in pursuit of those who had fled toward Awtas. He caught up with some of the defeated fighters and they battled him. Abu 'Amir was struck by an arrow and killed. Abu Musa al-Ash'ari then took up the banner and fought them.

The Messenger of God passed by a woman who had been killed by Khalid ibn al-Walid, with a crowd gathered around her. He said: Who killed this? They said: Khalid ibn al-Walid. He sent word to Khalid through one of those with him, saying: The Messenger of God forbids the killing of a child, a woman, or a hired labourer. And al-Asif means the hired man.

Al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam fought fiercely that day. Malik ibn 'Awf positioned himself and those with him at a mountain pass to cover the retreat of their vulnerable, so that they might get away safely.

Two things sit side by side there and the book does not connect them. The prohibition is issued because of a body on the ground, over the same commander disavowed twice after Bani Judhimah — and the ruling that comes out of it is general, permanent, and covers three categories: children, women, and the hired man, who is on the field for wages rather than for the cause. And in the next line the defeated commander, whose insistence on bringing the families put them in danger, is holding a pass open so they can get out.

The Prophet said regarding Bajad, a man from Banu Sa'd ibn Bakr: If you are able to find him, do not let him escape you. The Muslims captured him along with his family, and with them was al-Shayma' bint al-Harith ibn 'Abd al-'Uzza, the foster sister of the Messenger of God.

Those who captured her treated her roughly during the march, and she said: I am your companion's foster sister. They did not believe her.

When she was brought before the Messenger of God, she said: I am your sister from breastfeeding. He said: And what is the sign of that? She said: A bite you gave me on my back when I was carrying you.

The Messenger of God spread out his robe for her, seated her upon it, treated her with kindness, and said: If you wish, you may stay with me in honour and love, and I shall hold you dear — or if you wish, I will provide for you and return you to your people.

She said: Rather, provide for me and return me to my people. And she chose that, and the Messenger of God returned her to her people.

The claim was made twice and only heard the second time, and what makes the difference is evidence nobody could have fabricated and nobody else could have checked: a mark left by an infant on the body of the woman who carried him. She is asked for a sign and produces the one thing only the two of them could know.

And the choice she is then offered is a real one, with both halves described. She takes the second, the record says twice that this is what she chose, and it is done.

The martyrs of the day among the Muslims were few. From the Quraysh: Ayman ibn Ubayd; and Yazid ibn Zama'ah ibn al-Aswad ibn al-Muttalib ibn Asad ibn 'Abd al-'Uzza, who had a mare called The Wing, and was killed by her. From the Ansar: Saraqa ibn al-Harith ibn 'Adi, from Banu al-'Ajlan. From the Ash'aris: Abu 'Amir al-Ash'ari.

The captives of Hunayn were brought together before the Messenger of God, and he ordered that the captives and the spoils be held at al-Ji'ranah, and they were kept there.

The poets close the chapter, and their verse has reached this manuscript in the worst condition of anything in the volume — whole passages opaque, names collapsed past recognition, single images floating loose. Only the legible remainder is given.

Al-'Abbas ibn Mardas al-Sulami satirised the flight of Ibn al-Aswad's son and the imprisonment of his people, and out of a long and heavily damaged poem a few lines still stand: by God, who neither strays nor leads astray, Muhammad is the servant and the Messenger; we found him a prophet like Moses; and, of the man who abandoned the banner, that Dhu al-Khimar was no leader of a people. His second poem on the day is in the same state and keeps a handful of clear lines: I am a minister of the Prophet; so we are with a thousand of Sulaym upon them; before the Messenger of God, who towers above us; but the religion of God is the religion of Muhammad, and God's command has no hindrance.

Bujayr ibn Zuhayr ibn Abi Salma's verses survive mostly as one contrast: God honoured us and made our religion plain, and honoured us through worship of the Most Merciful — and humiliated them through worship of Satan.

And the chapter ends where the battle turned, in a line some narrators added: when the uncle of your Prophet and his guardian stood and called — O battalion of faith, where are those who answered their God on the day of al-Ara'id and al-Ridwan?

That closing sentence names two men by title rather than by name: the uncle, and the guardian. The uncle is the voice that turned the battle. The guardian is the sword, held out in front of the mule for as long as the rest of the field was empty. The last word of a chapter about twelve thousand men is about two.

## What this episode lands

The idols came off the roof of the Ka'bah, thrown by a man raised on prophetic shoulders and told afterwards that his landing had been made on the shoulders of the one who brings the revelation. And within days of watching them shatter, men of that same community asked on the road for a tree of their own, and were answered with the sharpest parallel available.

At Hunayn the largest force this community had ever fielded was ambushed in the dark and broke as one man — and the verse revealed about it names the cause as the pleasure the army took in its own numbers, the first time in this history that strength rather than weakness is charged against it. What stood was one household, and what turned it was a voice calling men back not by their units but by the oaths they had already sworn.

And the rulings come out of the wreckage rather than out of the victory: no child, no woman, no hired labourer is to be killed, issued over a body on the ground; an enemy's ransomed prisoners surfacing as a debt in the mouth of the killer's own mother; and a foster sister recognised by a bite mark, offered a place in the household, and given instead exactly what she asked for.
