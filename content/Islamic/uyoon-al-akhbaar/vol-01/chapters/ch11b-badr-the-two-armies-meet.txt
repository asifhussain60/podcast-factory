## Where this episode picks up

How do you work out the size of an army you cannot see? At Badr the answer came from two frightened slaves who genuinely did not know the number, and it was extracted from them by asking about dinner.

That question sits in the middle of this episode, and it is a fair sample of the whole. What comes before it is a run of expeditions in which almost nothing happens — marches out, camps made, enemies missed, everyone home. What comes after it is the first full battle of Islam, and the account of it is built out of small verifiable things: a count of camels slaughtered per day, a helmet that would not fit, a basin of water somebody swore an oath about, and a list of names with a killer attached to each one.

Three movements. First the raiding year — the first banner ever raised, four expeditions that ended without fighting, and one small patrol at a palm grove that killed a man in the wrong month and handed Quraysh a grievance they could take to war. Then the two armies converging on a well, with a dream in Mecca three days ahead of the messenger and a scouting report that nobody acted on. Then the fighting itself: three duels, a handful of pebbles, and the roll of the dead.

Watch what the author counts. He gives the strength of both sides, the number of camels, the ages of the duellists, the tally of the slain and the tally of the captives, and where his sources disagree on a number he prints both. This is a history that expects to be checked.

## The road to Badr

Mecca had by now understood what had been assembled in the north, and wanted the man dead without being able to get at him. The first move across the gap between the two cities was his, not theirs.

He dispatched his uncle Hamza to the coastal region near al-'Is with thirty riders from among the Muhajirun, and Hamza established a covenant with the people there. The first banner ever raised in Islam was that of Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib, the uncle of the Messenger of God.

When the news reached Quraysh they dispatched three hundred riders against him under the command of Abu Jahl ibn Hisham. The two forces nearly met in battle, but Majdi ibn 'Amr al-Juhani stepped between them as an intermediary and the two sides parted without fighting. Hamza and those with him withdrew.

Hamza put it into verse afterwards. What survives of the poem is damaged, and only the sense of it can be given: a reproach to his own people for the weakness of their judgment and their reason — are they not my people who dream idle dreams? — that they had been commanded to accept Islam and refused; that the Messenger of God gave the first command and God is too noble to countenance anything but the finest deed; that thirty travellers stood against them and Abu Jahl rose there like a stray and was disappointed, God turning back his schemes; and a warning to the sons of Lu'ayy not to yield to temptation but to come to Islam and the easy path.

Three hundred against thirty, and no fighting. Hold that ratio, because it recurs — and note who commanded on each side. The first two men to face each other in the field are the Prophet's uncle and the man whose head will be carried off the field at Badr.

Then the Messenger of God led a raid against Quraysh after a quarter of a month. It was the first expedition in which both the Muhajirun and the Ansar went out with him. He reached Buwat, in the vicinity of Radwa, then returned to Medina without engaging the enemy. The remainder of that month was Rabi' al-Akhir, and some of it fell in Jumada al-Awwal.

He then led a raid against Quraysh until he reached the tribe at the heart of Yanbu', and encamped among them for a time — this was during the nights of Jumada al-Akhirah. He then returned to the city without encountering any fighting.

During that expedition he came upon Ali and 'Ammar ibn Yasir sleeping on a mound of dust, their bodies covered in it. He said to Ali: what is the matter with you, O Abu Turab? Then he said: shall I not tell you who is the most wretched of all people? He said: Uhaymir Thamud, who hamstrung the camel, and the most wicked of the later ones, who will strike you, O Ali, on this — and he placed his hand on his shoulder — until this becomes wet from it. And he took him by the beard.

That is the moment the name Abu Turab enters the record — father of dust, given as a joke to a man asleep in a dust-heap — and it arrives in the same breath as the other thing. In one sentence the man who hamstrung the she-camel of Thamud, the standing example of the worst deed in the pre-Islamic memory of the Arabs, is paired with a man not yet born, and the identification is made by touching the place where the blow will land and the beard the blood will run into. The whole exchange happens on a raid where nothing occurred. That is the second thing to hold about this stretch: almost nothing happens on any of these expeditions, and the author keeps every one of them anyway.

He dispatched Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas with eight of the Muhajirun; they set out until they reached al-Kharrar in the land of the Hijaz, then turned back without any fighting. He went out in pursuit of Kurz ibn Jabir al-Fihri, who had raided the grazing lands of Medina, and pressed on until he reached Badr, but Kurz had already escaped. He returned to Medina, and that expedition became known as the First Battle of Badr.

Then the patrol that changed the terms. He dispatched 'Abdullah ibn Jahsh with eight men and said to him: travel until you descend upon a palm grove between Mecca and Ta'if, and keep watch over Quraysh. When he had travelled two days, 'Abdullah told his companions that he was going to disclose what he had been charged with — whoever wishes to be a witness and aspires to it, let him be glad; and whoever is unwilling may return. So Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas and 'Utbah ibn Ghazwan turned back, and 'Abdullah marched on with those who remained. Ibn Hisham reports it differently: that the two lost their way while following him and so fell behind.

When he descended upon the palm grove where he had been commanded, he came upon a caravan of Quraysh carrying goods. 'Abdullah and his companions fell upon those who were with it. From among the polytheists they took captive 'Uthman ibn 'Abd Allah and al-Hakam ibn Kaysan; they killed 'Amr ibn al-Hadrami, seized the caravan, and brought it to the Messenger of God — who held back both the caravan and the captives.

Quraysh sent to ransom the two men. Al-Hakam ibn Kaysan accepted Islam and remained with the Messenger of God until the day he was killed at the well of Ma'unah. 'Uthman ibn 'Abd Allah returned to Mecca and died a disbeliever. The Messenger of God allocated to 'Abdullah ibn Jahsh what remained after taking the fifth, and distributed four-fifths of that gain among the people of that expedition.

'Amr ibn al-Hadrami was the first man killed by the Muslims, and it occurred during the Sacred Month, concerning which God says: "They ask you about the Sacred Month — about fighting during it" (chapter 2, verse 217).

The Quraysh said: Muhammad and his companions have violated the sanctity of the Sacred Month and shed blood in it.

'Abdullah ibn Jahsh answered in verse, and the surviving sense of it is this: you count it a grave sin during the forbidden month, and pass your judgments on what Muhammad says, yet he disbelieved in him and God is ever-watchful — that he would keep out of the House those who prostrate to God and expel from the mosque of God its own people; tremble at Islam and be consumed by envy; even if you shame us with a killing, we watered our spears from the son of al-Hadrami, and 'Uthman the son of 'Abd Allah is our prize.

Notice the shape of that answer. It is not a denial. Nobody disputes that a man was killed in a month in which killing is forbidden. The reply is a comparison of offences — you object to one death in a sacred month, from people who have barred worshippers from the sanctuary and driven its own people out of it. Quraysh had a real charge and got a counter-charge, and the question was serious enough to be put to the Prophet and answered in the Book. The record here is small — one man dead, two captives, one caravan — and it is the point at which raiding stops being a nuisance and becomes a war with a legal argument attached. The next thing in the account is the largest caravan of the year.

## Two armies at the water

The Messenger of God received word that Abu Sufyan ibn Harb was returning from the Levant with a great merchant caravan belonging to Quraysh, carrying their wealth and trade goods, accompanied by thirty or forty men. He urged his companions forward: this is the caravan of Quraysh with their wealth — go out against it, and perhaps God will grant it to you as spoils. The people readied themselves, though some were reluctant, supposing that he would not actually face a battle.

Everyone leaving Medina understood the errand as an interception. That assumption is the reason the force is the size it is, and it is worth carrying all the way to the end of this episode.

Abu Sufyan got word that Muhammad had roused his companions against his caravan. He hired Damdam ibn 'Amr al-Ghifari, sent him to Mecca, and ordered him to call Quraysh to arms in defence of their property.

'Atikah bint 'Abd al-Muttalib had a vision three days before Damdam arrived, and it terrified her. She saw a rider approaching on a camel until he halted, then called out at the top of his voice: hasten to your fallen warriors — in three days. He then entered the mosque, and the people followed him. He made his camel kneel against the back of the Ka'bah and cried out the same words. Then he led his camel to the summit of Abu Qubays, called out as before, took a rock and hurled it from the mountain. It crashed to the ground below, and as it rolled to the foot of the mountain it shattered, until there was not a single house or dwelling in Mecca that was not struck by a fragment of that rock.

She told her brother al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib, and al-'Abbas mentioned it to al-Walid ibn 'Utbah ibn Rabi'ah, who spread the news. Abu Jahl said: since when has this prophetess arisen among you? Is it not enough that your men lay claim to prophethood — must your women do so as well? Al-'Abbas denied that she had seen anything, and the Quraysh weighed in heavily on it, none more so than Abu Jahl.

Al-'Abbas told the rest himself. The women of Banu 'Abd al-Muttalib said to me: Abu Jahl is belittling your men with insults, and will not be satisfied until he turns to insulting your women. So I went out in anger, intending to confront him with something he would detest. I entered the mosque and saw him, and moved toward him to expose him. He retreated from some of what he had been saying, and I cornered him — for he was a lean man with a face like iron, a tongue like iron, and a gaze like iron. When he moved toward the door of the mosque, I pressed him harder. I said to myself: what does the curse of God matter — would this change anything for me if I were to curse him?

Then something distracted me from him: the voice of Damdam ibn 'Amr al-Ghifari shouting from the belly of the valley, standing on his camel with his shirt torn, saying — O people of Quraysh, the caravan, the caravan! Your wealth with Abu Sufyan has been intercepted by Muhammad and his companions — rescue it, rescue it! And so that drew me away from Abu Jahl, and it drew him away from the matter between us.

The vision is given three days of lead time and spends all of it being argued about, and the argument is not over whether the warning is accurate. It is over whether a woman is allowed to have one. The image at the centre of it is a single rock breaking into fragments, with one fragment reaching every house in the city — a picture of a loss with no exemptions in it — and the messenger who confirms it arrives mid-quarrel, in a torn shirt, shouting about property.

The people prepared swiftly, saying: does Muhammad and his companions think this will be like the affair of Ibn al-Hadrami? No, by God, it will not. Not one of the nobles of Quraysh stayed behind, except that Abu Lahab remained and sent al-'As ibn Hisham ibn al-Mughirah in his place.

Quraysh took out al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib, Talib ibn Abi Talib and 'Aqil ibn Abi Talib with them to fight the Messenger of God, though they went out reluctantly. A dispute arose between them and a companion of theirs, and they turned Talib back, saying: we know that your heart is with Muhammad. So Talib returned, and composed lines on it whose sense is a prayer against his own army — O Lord, if they have gone forth in a league of sworn warriors, then make them the losers and not the victors, and let them be the despoiled and not the ones who despoil. The author notes that Talib had in fact accepted Islam, and cites the verses he composed on the Messenger of God: that the glory of Banu Hashim has come, and the pure of Banu Hashim, Ahmad; bold of heart and clear of voice, the light of the land; pure in conduct inwardly and outwardly, chaste, pious, immaculate in character; and that he is like a lion, relentless and unspent.

An army marches out of Mecca with a man in it praying for its defeat, and it marches because a grievance from the palm grove is fresher than a caravan is safe.

The Messenger of God set out in the early nights of Ramadan with his companions — it is said on the eighth. He had two black banners: one he gave to Ali ibn Abi Talib, and the other to the Ansar. His companions had seventy camels, which they rode in turns. He and Ali shared one camel between them in turn, and he walked alongside the Muhajirun and the Ansar until they descended upon Sajjaj, which is the well of al-Ruh, then continued until, drawing close to al-Safra', he sent two men ahead to gather news of Abu Sufyan.

Seventy camels for the whole force, ridden in shifts, with the commander taking his turn on foot. Set that beside what is coming over the dune.

He pressed on until he came to a valley called Dhafran, where he made camp. There the news reached him that Quraysh had marched out to defend their honour. He consulted the people, and they responded well and gave good counsel. He said: advance and be of good cheer, for God has promised me one of the two parties, and by God, it is as though I can see right now where the enemy will fall.

One of the two parties. That sentence is the hinge of the campaign — it converts an interception into a choice between a caravan and an army, and it is spoken before anybody knows which one they are going to get.

He departed from Dhafran and descended upon Badr. When he had nearly arrived he sent Ali ibn Abi Talib with a group of Muslims to scout for news of Quraysh at the waters of Badr. They found two servants of Quraysh there — Aslam, a slave of Banu al-Hajjaj, and 'Arid, the slave of Abu Yasar of Banu al-'As ibn Sa'id — captured them, and brought them in.

Tell me about Quraysh. They said: they are behind that great dune you see, the Dune of 'Aqanqal. How many are the people? Many. What is their number? We do not know their count. How many camels do they slaughter each day? Nine one day and ten another. Then the people number between nine hundred and a thousand. And he asked: who among them are the nobles?

Two prisoners who are not lying and genuinely do not know are asked a question they can answer, and the number falls out of the catering. It is the most quoted piece of reasoning in the whole battle and it is a kitchen calculation — and it produces the ratio the day will be fought at, roughly three to one, the same odds Hamza faced on the coast.

As for Abu Sufyan, he turned his camel away from the road and left Badr on his left. He learned that the caravan was safe, and sent word to Quraysh ordering them to turn back, but they refused. The Messenger of God said to his companions: this is Mecca — she has cast her liver-pieces before you.

He moved until he reached the nearest water of the people and descended there, then commanded that a basin be built at the water source and filled. When the Quraysh drew near, they were let through. And when he saw them advancing from al-'Aqqul, and from the dune through which they entered the valley, he said: O God, here are the Quraysh — they have come in their arrogance and deceit, to challenge You and to give the lie to Your Messenger. O God, grant me the victory You have promised me. I am the one who stands against them.

He caught sight of 'Utbah ibn Rabi'ah among the ranks and said: if there is any good in any one of those people, it is in the rider of the red camel — if they follow him, they will be rightly guided. Yet 'Utbah was among those slain at Badr, and no good came of it. He spoke truly, the author says, for God says: "Nor does he speak from his own inclination" (chapter 53, verse 3). Rather, it was with those who were of his children — the Imams of the family of the Messenger of God — that the guidance lay.

When the Quraysh had settled their position they sent 'Umayr ibn Wahb al-Jumahi to assess Muhammad's companions. He rode his horse around the Muslim army, returned, and said: about three hundred men, give or take — but let me look more carefully to see whether they have an ambush or reinforcements waiting. He swept through the valley as far as he could, found nothing, and came back. I found nothing, he said, but I have seen, O assembly of Quraysh, calamities that carry their fates. Yathrib has sent you soaking death: a people who have no shield and no refuge except their swords. By God, I do not see that a single one of them will be killed without first killing one of you in return. If they kill among you a number equal to their own, what good is life after that?

'Utbah ibn Rabi'ah then rose, pulled away from the Quraysh, and called on them to abandon the fighting. But Abu Jahl said: he has swelled with cowardice, by God — his resolve has bewitched him. And when he saw Muhammad and his companions he said: no, by God, we shall not turn back until God judges between us and Muhammad. When word of 'Utbah's speech reached him he replied: he who is yellow with cowardice — that is he, not I. He will know which of us is swollen with magic. Then 'Utbah sought a helmet to place on his head, but could find none to fit his large skull; seeing this, he wrapped his head with a cloth.

The reconnaissance is accurate, the most senior man present agrees with it, the caravan they came for is already safe, and the assembly follows the man who called them cowards. The last detail before the fighting starts is a commander who cannot find a helmet that fits.

## The single combat and the rout

Al-Aswad ibn 'Abd al-Asad al-Makhzumi came forward and declared: I will drink from Muhammad's basin, or I will destroy it, or die without having done so. Hamza went out to meet him. When they clashed Hamza struck him and severed half his leg, cutting it clean, and he fell on his back with the leg streaming blood. He then dragged himself toward the basin and plunged into it, intending to fulfil his oath, as he claimed. Hamza followed him and struck him again in the basin until he was killed.

The first man to die at Badr dies keeping a promise about a water trough. He had bound himself to three outcomes, and having lost the use of one leg he crawls to the only one still available.

Then 'Utbah ibn Rabi'ah came forward, flanked by his brother Shaybah and his son al-Walid, and called for single combat. Three men came out to meet them — 'Awf and Mu'awwidh, the sons of al-Harith, their mother being 'Afra', and a third whom some say was 'Abdullah ibn Rawaha. The Quraysh asked who they were. Men of the Ansar, they said. We have no need of you, said the Quraysh. A spokesman called out: O Muhammad, send out to us men who are our equals from among our own people.

The Messenger of God said: rise, O 'Ubaydah ibn al-Harith; rise, O Hamzah; rise, O Ali. When they rose and were asked who they were, they identified themselves and were recognized as worthy and honourable opponents.

'Ubaydah, the eldest of the three, faced 'Utbah ibn Rabi'ah. Hamza faced Shaybah. And Ali faced al-Walid ibn 'Utbah. Ali was the youngest of the three, eighteen years old; some say he had not yet reached twenty. Al-Walid wasted no time before Ali killed him, and Hamza wasted no time before he killed Shaybah. As for 'Ubaydah, he and 'Utbah exchanged two blows, each man striking the other severely, and 'Ubaydah had his leg severed. Ali and Hamza then turned their swords on 'Utbah and finished him, then carried 'Ubaydah back to the Muslim army.

The demand was about rank — send us our equals — and it is a demand Quraysh had refused to make of the Ansar. What it got in reply was three men of one house, two of whom finish their opponents instantly.

Then the two sides advanced and engaged. The Messenger of God commanded his companions not to charge until he gave the order, saying: if the enemy surrounds you, drive them back with arrows. The Muslims left him in the field shelter, saying: if we are victorious, we will remain by your side. Abu Bakr stayed with him and did not fight.

It is narrated from al-Baqir Muhammad ibn Ali that the Messenger of God arranged the ranks of his companions on the day of Badr and ordered them that no one should leave his position; if anyone was pressed by the polytheists, he was to fall back to his place and regroup with his fellows. And he said to them: rejoice, for here is Gabriel, leading a horse by the halter, fully armed.

He went among the people and urged them on: by Him in Whose hand is Muhammad's soul, no man who fights them this day, killing patiently in God's cause, will fail to enter Paradise.

The Muslims held their positions and stood firm. Then he took a handful of pebbles, flung them toward the Quraysh, and said: may those faces be disfigured. Then he gave the order to advance — press forward — and the rout came.

The handful is the same gesture that opened the previous episode. In Mecca a fistful of dust was thrown into the faces of men who had come to kill him, and it covered an escape. At Badr a fistful of pebbles is thrown into the faces of men who have come to kill him, and it opens an attack.

Ali and Hamza fought magnificently. The people witnessed Ali enduring hardship and difficulty and holding firm against adversity in a manner they could scarcely believe came from any human being, given his age. The polytheists were routed, and God granted the Muslims victory through slaying and capture.

Then the author does what he has done at every turn of this history: he names everybody. Ali ibn Abi Talib slew al-'As ibn Sa'id ibn al-'As ibn Umayyah; 'Uqbah ibn Abi Mu'ayt of Banu Umayyah ibn 'Abd Shams; 'Amir ibn 'Abd Allah, an ally of theirs; Ta'imah ibn 'Adi ibn Nawfal; Nawfal ibn Khuwaylid ibn Asad — the man who had bound Abu Bakr ibn Abi Quhafa and Talhah ibn 'Ubayd Allah together with a rope when they became Muslims, and one of the devils of Quraysh; al-'Arr ibn al-Harith of Banu 'Abd al-Dar, killed in single combat before the Messenger of God; and 'Umayr ibn 'Uthman ibn Tayyim ibn Murrah. Also Harmalah ibn 'Amr, an ally of Banu Makhzum; Mas'ud ibn Abi Umayya ibn al-Mughirah al-Makhzumi; Abu Qays ibn al-Fakah ibn al-Mughirah; 'Abd Allah ibn al-Mundhir ibn Abi Rifa'ah; Hajib ibn al-Sa'ib; Abu al-'As ibn Umayya ibn al-Hajjaj al-Sahmi; Aws ibn Mu'ir ibn Luthan ibn Jumah; and Mu'awiya ibn 'Amir of 'Abd al-Qays, an ally of theirs. Al-Walid ibn 'Utbah, slain in single combat, is counted among those killed by Ali.

Ali and Hamza shared in the killing of 'Utbah ibn Rabi'ah together with 'Ubaydah ibn al-Harith; the two of them and Zayd ibn Haritha shared in the killing of Hanzalah ibn Abi Sufyan; and Zama'ah ibn al-Aswad ibn al-Muttalib, and 'Aqil ibn al-Aswad, each fell to them jointly as well. Shaybah ibn Rabi'ah was killed by Hamza in single combat. Hamza also killed Abu Qays ibn al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah — some say Ali did — and al-Aswad al-Makhzumi, and Nabih ibn al-Hajjaj ibn 'Amir, with some saying Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas shared in that one, and al-'As ibn Munabbih ibn al-Hajjaj, with Sa'd sharing.

'Ammar ibn Yasir killed al-Harith ibn al-Hadrami, al-Harith ibn Zama'ah ibn al-Aswad, Yazid ibn 'Abd Allah — an ally of Banu Makhzum known for his bravery — and 'Ali ibn Umayya, the son of Umayya ibn Khalaf. Salim, the client of Abu Hudhayfa, killed 'Umayr ibn Abi 'Umayr and his son, two clients of Banu 'Abd Shams. Al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam killed 'Ubaydah ibn Sa'id ibn al-'As ibn Umayyah and al-Sa'ib ibn Abi al-Sa'ib al-Makhzumi. Khubaib ibn Isaf, of Banu al-Harith ibn al-Khazraj, killed al-Harith ibn 'Amir ibn Nawfal ibn 'Abd Manaf. Abu al-Bakhtari, who was al-'As ibn Hisham, was killed by al-Mujdhir ibn Dhiyad al-Balawi. Uzayd ibn Malis was killed by Bilal ibn Rabah, along with Zayd, an ally of Banu 'Abd al-Dar — some say al-Miqdad ibn al-Aswad killed him. Suhayb ibn Sinan killed 'Uthman ibn Malik ibn Ka'b.

Abu Jahl ibn Hisham — whose name was 'Amr ibn Hisham ibn al-Mughirah ibn 'Abd Allah ibn 'Amr ibn Makhzum — was killed jointly by Mu'adh ibn 'Amr ibn al-Jamuh, Mu'awwidh ibn 'Afra', and 'Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud. Al-'As ibn Hisham was killed by 'Umar ibn al-Khattab. Abu Musafi' al-Ash'ari was killed by Abu Dujana al-Sa'idi. Rifa'ah ibn Abi Rifa'ah al-Makhzumi was killed by Sa'd ibn al-Rabi'; al-Mundhir ibn Abi Rifa'ah by Ma'n ibn 'Adi; 'Uwaymar ibn al-Sa'ib by al-Nu'man ibn Malik al-Qawqali; 'Amr ibn Sufyan by Yazid ibn Ruqaysh; Jabir ibn Sufyan by Abu Burdah ibn Nayyar; Munabbih ibn al-Hajjaj and 'Asim ibn Abi 'Awf ibn Dhubayrah both by Abu al-Yusr, of Banu Salamah; and Abu al-'As ibn Qays ibn 'Adi ibn Sa'd ibn Sahm by Ali, though some say by al-Nu'man ibn Malik and some by Abu Dujana. Umayya ibn Khalaf ibn Wahb of Banu Jumah was killed by Mu'adh ibn 'Afra', with Kharijah ibn Zayd and Khubaib ibn Isaf sharing in it. Abu Ma'bad ibn Wahb, an ally from Banu Kalb, was killed by Khalid and Iyas ibn al-Bukayr, though some say Abu Dujana.

The total count of those killed at Badr from among the polytheists was fifty men. Some say the dead were seventy, and the captives likewise seventy — this is the opinion of Ibn 'Abbas and Sa'id ibn al-Musayyib. God says in His Book: "Why, when a calamity struck you, though you had already struck them with one twice as great" (chapter 3, verse 165). Ka'b ibn Malik sang of it: seventy of them were struck down by the stinging snake, and the lions.

Two figures for the same battle, printed side by side with the names of the men who hold each. That is how this history behaves whenever it does not know.

On the day of Badr fourteen Muslims were martyred, six of them Muhajirun. The source names 'Ubaydah ibn al-Harith, whose leg was severed and who died of the wound; 'Amir ibn Abi Waqqas, brother of Sa'd; Dhu al-Shamalayn, 'Umayr ibn 'Abd 'Amr ibn Nadlah, an ally of Banu Zahra; and from Banu 'Adi ibn Ka'b ibn Lu'ay, 'Aqil ibn Abi al-Bakir, their ally and the freedman of 'Umar ibn al-Khattab; and Safwan ibn Bayda of Banu Fihr. Eight of the Ansar fell: from Banu 'Amr ibn 'Awf, Sa'd ibn Khaythama and Mubashir ibn 'Abd al-Mundhir ibn Zanbar; from Banu al-Harith ibn al-Khazraj, Yazid ibn al-Harith and 'Umayr ibn al-Hammam; and Rafi' ibn al-Mu'alla, Haritha ibn Suraqa, and 'Awf and Mu'awwidh, the sons of al-Harith ibn Rifa'ah — the sons of 'Afra'.

After the battle the Messenger of God distributed the spoils among his companions and entrusted the captives to a guard. When he reached al-Safra' he went to 'Ubaydah ibn al-Harith ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim ibn 'Abd Manaf, who had been struck before this and was in great pain. He asked about the wound to his leg. 'Ubaydah said: O Messenger of God, Abu Talib believed — and he cited a line of his verse, about handing him over only when they had pressed close around him, and about their sons and what is lawful. If Abu Talib had seen this, he said, he would have kept it to himself. The Messenger of God praised him, and Ali was with him. 'Ubaydah died of his wound.

The dispute over Abu Talib's faith was argued out in full earlier in this history. Here it returns in the only form that matters at that moment: a dying man with his leg gone quoting his uncle's poetry, and saying that if the man who wrote it had lived to see this day he would have kept the sight to himself.

The Prophet ordered the execution of al-Nadr ibn al-Harith. And at the valley of the doe he ordered the execution of 'Uqbah ibn Abi Mu'ayt. When the order was given, 'Uqbah said: who will look after my children? He said: the Fire. So Ali struck his neck; it was also said that the one who struck him was 'Asim ibn Thabit ibn Abi al-Aqlah.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry forward from Badr.

The first is that the war began by accident and was legalized afterwards. Four expeditions produced no fighting at all; the fifth, a patrol of eight men at a palm grove, killed one man in a forbidden month, and that single death — not the caravan, not the migration — is the grievance Quraysh named as they mobilized. The answer given to it was never a denial. It was a comparison of offences, and the question was serious enough to be answered in the Book.

The second is how much of this battle is arithmetic. Nine or ten camels a day gives nine hundred to a thousand men. Seventy camels ridden in shifts carries a force of about three hundred, with the commander walking his turn. A scout reports that each of those three hundred will take one of yours with him, and asks what life would be worth afterwards — and is ignored by an assembly that had already been told, three days in advance, in a dream nobody was willing to credit because a woman had it.

The third is that the day was decided in front of everyone, by name. Three men were called out of the Muslim line by name and two of the three duels ended instantly. Then the ranks, the order to hold, the handful of pebbles, and the rout. And afterwards the author counts: every man killed with the man who killed him, both figures for the dead where his sources disagree, fourteen martyrs listed by clan, and a wounded man dying at al-Safra' with an argument about somebody else's faith on his lips.

Mecca had lost its leading men in an afternoon. What that news did when it reached the city is where this history goes next.
