## Where this episode opens

While the succession to the Messenger of God was being settled in a hall on the other side of the city, where was his cousin? Washing the body. That is not an arrangement invented by a later partisan — it is how this history reports the two events, side by side, inside a single sentence, and a good deal of what follows depends on taking that sentence literally.

The previous episode ended in a room full of argument and with a pen that was never brought. This one begins a few days after that, at the point where the illness stops being an illness. It carries the last conversations, the death itself, then the sequence of things done to a body over the following day and night — the washing, the wrapping, the choice of where to dig — and finally the two kinds of speech the community produced once it was over: an address delivered at the graveside that stakes a claim in ascending order of controversy, and four elegies by a poet who had spent twenty years working for the man he was now burying.

Three movements, and they are very different in texture. The first is intimate and crowded: grandchildren carried in and carried out again, a companion asked to lean forward and then displaced, a direct question about guardians receiving a direct answer, and a charge repeated three times by a man who could barely speak. The second is procedural, and it is procedural on purpose — this history is not only recording what was done to the body, it is deriving from it the law of how every Muslim is washed, wrapped and buried, which is why the number of washings and the composition of the shroud are given with the flatness of a manual. And the third is what was said over him, first in prose by one man standing at the grave, and then in verse that reaches us in pieces.

This is also the end of the volume. Everything after the poetry is the copyist's own record of when and where he finished writing, and it is left where it belongs, outside the history.

Listen, in the first section, for who is told to make room for whom.

## The last hours

It is narrated that when he was dying, al-Hasan and al-Husayn were called to him. He embraced them and drew them close, until he fell into a faint, at which point they were moved away from him. When he opened his eyes, he said to Ali: Let them take pleasure in me and I in them — though my passing will weigh heavily on them.

That is a short report and it is easy to read past. Two children are brought to a dying man, he holds them, he loses consciousness while holding them, and they are lifted away by whoever is in the room. What he says when he comes round is not about himself. It is a wish that the pleasure between him and them should be mutual while it lasts, followed by a flat prediction about what his death is going to cost them in particular. The history has spent a great deal of this volume on those two boys — on who their father is, who their mother is, what their uncles and aunts were — and it records this without commentary of its own.

The illness was not one long unconsciousness. It came in waves, and between them he could get up. The history records that he later sat in the mosque, after Abu Bakr had led the people in prayer, and addressed them, warning them of the discord to come:

O people, do not cling to me. I make lawful only what God has made lawful in the Quran, and I forbid only what the Quran has forbidden. O Safiyyah bint 'Abd al-Muttalib, aunt of the Messenger of God; O Fatimah bint Muhammad — work for what is with God, for I cannot spare you anything from God.

That is among the hardest sentences in the volume, and it is delivered from a pulpit, in public, to two women named aloud — one his aunt, one his daughter. It withdraws in advance the thing a dying man's household might most reasonably expect to be left: a claim on him. And it withdraws it by giving a reason rather than by scolding anyone. The first half of the address has already narrowed his own authority to the point of vanishing — he permits nothing the Book has not permitted and forbids nothing it has not forbidden, which makes him a conduit rather than a source. The second half simply applies that to his relatives. If he is not the origin of the rules, then proximity to him is not a shortcut around them, and the people closest to him are told to work for what is with God exactly as everyone else must.

It is worth being precise about what that passage does not say, because the volume has spent the previous episode arguing something quite different. It is not a statement about the succession. It is a statement about intercession and privilege, and it removes them. The two claims sit side by side without contradiction: an office can be transmitted by designation, and still no amount of kinship shortens anybody's account. The history places this address immediately after the account of a man carried out between two supporters to take back a prayer, so the two limits land together — the office does not pass by standing in for someone, and nearness does not buy anything.

The most detailed account of the sickroom itself comes from Abu Dharr.

I was with the Messenger of God and his family during his illness. He was confined to his bed, and he said: Come close, O Abu Dharr — lean on me. So I leaned against him, and he rested against my chest, until Ali entered and said: Rise, O Abu Dharr — Ali has more right to this than you. The Commander of the Faithful then sat, and the Prophet leaned upon him. Then he said to me: Come here, O Abu Dharr, take my hand. I sat holding his hand, and he said to me:

Hold fast to this contract: whoever seals his final moments with the testimony that there is no god but God will enter Paradise; whoever seals them by feeding a poor person will enter Paradise; whoever seals them by a valid excuse will enter Paradise; whoever seals them with a lesser pilgrimage will enter Paradise; and whoever seals them with struggle in the way of God — even if it amounts to no more than a brief moment — will enter Paradise.

Two things happen in that report at once, and the history does not pull them apart. One is a physical rearrangement of the room. A man is holding the Prophet up, and is asked to move so that another man can. The words used to move him are the plainest possible form of a precedence claim — has more right to this — and what they are claiming precedence over is not an office or a title but a chest to lean against. The history has spent a whole episode assembling declarations made in front of crowds; here the same thing is asserted in a bedroom, about a physical task, by the person who wants to perform it.

The other thing is the teaching, and it deserves attention on its own terms rather than as scenery. It is about how a life ends, and it is being dictated by a man whose life is ending, to a companion who is holding his hand while he says it. Five different final acts are named, and the striking feature of the list is how small four of them are. A single sentence of testimony. A meal handed to someone who needs it. A valid reason accepted on your behalf. A short pilgrimage. And then the fifth, struggle in the way of God, is immediately cut down to size by the qualifier attached to it: even if it amounts to no more than a brief moment. Nothing on that list requires a long life, wealth, health, or an audience. The word the report uses for what is being handed over is a contract — something to be held to, not merely remembered.

Salman al-Farsi asked the question that everything in the previous episode had been circling.

O Messenger of God, every prophet has had a guardian — who is yours? He said: My guardian, my confidant, and my successor over my family, and the best of those I leave behind, who will uphold my religion and fulfil my trust, is Ali ibn Abi Talib.

The form of the question is as significant as the answer. Salman does not ask whether there is a guardian; he treats the existence of one as a standard feature of prophethood, established by every case before this one, and asks only for the name. That framing is the same argument the history made at length in the previous episode, arriving here from the other direction and in a single clause. And the answer comes back as a list of functions with the name at the end of it — guardian, confidant, successor over the household, the best of those left behind, upholder of the religion, discharger of the trust. Five descriptions, then a name. The order matters: the office is described before the person is identified, which is the reverse of how a favour would be granted.

And then the death itself, in two reports, both from Ali.

The first is transmitted by way of al-Tabari: The Messenger of God charged me at the time of his death, while I was his support. He said to me: O Ali, I commend the Arabs to your good care — he said this three times — and then he breathed his last in my arms.

The second is shorter: The Messenger of God clasped my head, placed my hand in his, and wiped my face with it.

Three repetitions is the form used for something that must not be lost, and what is repeated here is not a doctrine, a name or a title. It is a responsibility for a people, given as a commendation rather than an order — take good care of them. After an entire episode of designations, the last instruction recorded is about the governed rather than the government.

The second report closes a circle the previous episode opened. A hand had once been passed over a face at a doorway in Madinah, and the man it was done to had wept and asked what had been revealed, because nothing in twenty years had prepared him for it. Here it is done again, and this time there is no question and no explanation. The two reports also disagree about nothing while describing the same minutes from two angles: in one he is the support and the man dies against him; in the other his head is being held and his hand taken.

## The washing and the grave

When the Messenger of God passed away, the history says, Ali had no time for anything else. He was wholly occupied with what had fallen to him — washing the body, shrouding it, preparing it for burial, and consoling a family overwhelmed by what had happened to them — while Abu Bakr, 'Umar and 'Uthman gathered the people at the hall of the Bani Sa'idah, seeking, in this history's words, to move the succession away from the people it belonged to.

The objection is then put in a single conditional, and the conditional is the sharpest part of it. Even had that meeting been a proper one, the history says, it ought to have been held in the mosque of the Messenger of God, in the presence of his family, at the time of his funeral — and its business ought to have been to pray that God would guide them to obey the rightful successor. Notice what is being contested there. Not, in the first place, the outcome. The room, the hour, and who was not in it. A decision of that magnitude taken in a tribal meeting hall, during a funeral, with the household of the deceased unable to attend because they were performing it, is being challenged on procedure before it is challenged on substance. The history then says it will return to the matter — and that return is where the next volume of this work begins.

Before the washing, one report of consolation. Ja'far ibn Muhammad said that when the Prophet passed away a voice was heard, its speaker unseen, saying:

Peace be upon you, O People of the House, and the mercy and blessings of God. Every soul shall taste death, and on the Day of Resurrection you shall receive your full reward. Whoever is kept away from the Fire and admitted to Paradise has truly triumphed, and the life of this world is nothing but the enjoyment of delusion (chapter 3, verse 185). In God there is consolation for every calamity, and a successor to every perishing thing. So be patient, and know that the one who bears affliction with patience will find his reward preserved, and the mercy of God is upon you.

He was asked who the speaker was, and answered: We understood it to be Gabriel.

That consolation is built out of ordinary material. It quotes a verse that applies to every human being without exception, and it offers two general principles — that God is a consolation for any calamity and a replacement for anything that perishes — to a household that has just lost the one thing it might have thought irreplaceable. Nothing in it is addressed to their particular position. What is addressed to their position is only the first line, the greeting, which names them as the People of the House before saying anything else.

The washing follows, and it is narrated by the man who performed it, transmitted down through his descendants.

When the Messenger of God instructed me to wash him, and that someone should wash him alongside me, I said: O Messenger of God, you are a large man — I cannot turn you over alone. He said to me: Gabriel will be with you to wash him. I said: Who will pour the water? He said: Al-Fadl ibn al-'Abbas will pour it, but tell him to cover his eyes — for no one who looks upon my nakedness except you will retain his sight.

And in a second account of the same event: When I began to wash him, I heard a voice from the side of the house saying: Do not remove his shirt. So I washed him in his shirt. As I washed him, I felt a hand passing alongside my hand — whenever I wished to turn him, it would turn him; and when I wanted to wash his front, it would position him, and I would wash his back. A voice called out: Do not tire yourself. So I turned him to his side and washed his back.

Al-Baqir, Muhammad ibn Ali, supplied the arrangement: al-Fadl ibn al-'Abbas poured the water with his eyes covered, while Gabriel washed him. He was washed three times — once with water and lotus leaves, once with water mixed with ground spices and camphor, and once with pure water, this last being the third.

Read that sequence for what it is doing, because it is doing two jobs at once. As narrative it is a report about permission. One man is authorised to see and to touch; a second is required, because the work cannot be done alone, and is present with his eyes covered under a warning about what looking would cost him; and the labour itself turns out to be shared with something the washer can feel but not see, to the point where he is told to stop exhausting himself. The practical objection he raises at the start — that the body is too heavy for one man — is answered not by assigning him a helper of the ordinary kind but by telling him who the helper will be.

As law it is a specification, and this is the part that has outlived everyone in the story. Three washings, in a fixed order, with three named preparations, ending in plain water. That paragraph is the ground of how Muslims have washed their dead ever since, which is why its incidental details are recorded with such care. The instruction about the shirt is the clearest case: it is preserved not as a pious touch but as a prohibition heard aloud and obeyed, and it is what makes this washing different from the rule it otherwise establishes.

The shrouding is given the same way, first as event and then as rule. From al-Sadiq: on completing the washing, Ali shrouded him in three garments — two Yemeni garments and a shirt. And a turban. And from Ja'far ibn Muhammad, stated as a general rule: the shroud consists of three garments — a shirt that is not buttoned or sealed, wrapped as a full winding sheet with a cloth. He added: My father charged me to shroud him in three garments, one of which was a garment he used to wear on Fridays, another garment, and a shirt.

Then the funeral prayer, and the choice of where to dig, which was contested.

Al-Sadiq mentioned the death of the Messenger of God and said: When Ali had washed and shrouded him, he came out and was told: O Ali, when the people gather to pray over the Messenger of God, they will propose that he be buried at al-Baqi' with one of them leading the prayer — go out and address them. So Ali went out and said: O people, the Messenger of God was a living and present Imam, and no prophet has ever been taken except that he was buried in the very place where he died. They said: Do as you see fit. So Ali stood at the door of the house and led the funeral prayer over the Messenger of God, with the people entering in groups of ten to pray over him and then departing.

That is the second contest of the day and it is settled in one sentence without an argument about rank. Look at what the proposal on the table would have produced. A public cemetery, and one man leading the prayer over the Prophet in front of the whole community — which would have made the funeral itself the demonstration of who was now in charge, in the most visible way available and with no possibility of a rematch. What is offered against it is not a counter-claim to that position. It is a precedent about prophets in general, stated as a fact rather than as an argument: prophets are buried where they die. And the response to it is immediate consent.

The arrangement that follows then quietly dissolves the question the proposal existed to answer. Nobody leads the community in that prayer, because there is no assembled community to lead: people are admitted ten at a time to a doorway, they pray, and they leave. The man standing at the door does not move for any of it. A single office that could have been claimed once, in front of everyone, has been replaced by the same act performed dozens of times in front of ten people.

The details of the grave are recorded with the same instructional flatness, and for the same reason — each is establishing a rule. A niche was cut for him, and the history glosses the term as it goes, since the gloss is the point: a recess carved into the side of the grave facing the direction of prayer, along the grave's wall, cut for the deceased in the middle of it. A mat of woven palm fronds was laid down, because the ground was damp. And when he was buried, a quarter of the grave was filled.

There is no scene there and no speech in it. This history has spent an entire volume on the man — his ancestry back to Ishmael, the year he was born, every campaign, every delegation, every ruling — and the last physical facts it gives about him are the drainage of the soil and how much earth went back into the hole.

The precedent about where prophets are buried also has a consequence that the report does not spell out and does not need to. If he is buried in the room he died in, then the grave is inside a dwelling attached to the mosque rather than out at the cemetery with everyone else, and it stays there permanently. The community that assembled ten at a time to pray over him would go on walking past the place every day for the rest of their lives. Whatever else was being decided in the hall across the city that afternoon, the geography of this decision was fixed within a day of the death and could not be revisited.

## What was said over him

Abu Rafi' narrated that when the Messenger of God passed away, there was no one among the people more bereaved than the Commander of the Faithful — and that he now rose to address them. He praised and glorified God, invoked blessings upon the Prophet, and spoke of the favours God had shown them in sending a Messenger from among themselves, who had lifted abomination from them and purified them. Then he said:

I am the cousin of the Messenger of God — no one can say this truthfully but me. I accepted Islam and prayed with him before all the people, and I am his guardian and his successor after him, and the husband of his daughter, the mistress of the women of all the worlds. We are the People of the House of Mercy, through whom God guided you out of error and gave sight to your blindness. So fear God, that He may preserve His grace upon you.

Every clause of that is a claim, and they are ordered from the one nobody in the crowd could dispute to the one every one of them was about to. Kinship comes first, and it is stated in a form that makes it exclusive by simple fact rather than by argument: of all the men standing there, only one can say that sentence and have it be true. Then priority in time — belief and prayer before the rest of them, which is a matter of dates, and equally hard to contest. Only then the two positions the previous episode spent five thousand words assembling evidence for, guardian and successor, and they are placed third in a list rather than at the head of it. Then the marriage, and the description of the woman he married.

And then the address changes person. It stops being a man's claim and becomes a household's function: We are the People of the House of Mercy, through whom God guided you out of error and gave sight to your blindness. The subject shifts from I to we, and the object shifts from the speaker's standing to the audience's condition. The last line asks for nothing at all. It does not demand allegiance, name an opponent, or mention the meeting taking place elsewhere. It instructs them to fear God so that what they have already been given is not taken back from them.

The opening of the address is worth noticing too, because it is the part with no claim in it. Before any of that, he praises God, invokes blessings on the dead man, and reminds the crowd of a favour — that God sent them a Messenger from among themselves, who lifted defilement from them and made them clean. That is the standard opening of a formal address, and it is doing the ordinary work such openings do: it puts the speaker and the audience on the same side of something before it asks them to consider anything. The favour named is one they all received equally. Only after that does the speech begin listing the things that distinguish one man in the crowd from the rest of it.

The community's other answer was poetry, and the history closes the volume with four elegies by Hassan ibn Thabit — the poet who had spent the Medinan years answering, in verse, whatever the Prophet's opponents composed against him. This volume has quoted him repeatedly: at Badr, at Uhud, on the day of the trench, on the day of the designation. He is the last voice in it.

All four elegies reach this manuscript badly damaged. Lines are transposed, names are garbled, and in places the sense of a couplet has gone entirely. Nothing is reconstructed below. What follows is what can still be read with confidence, and where the damage has taken something, that is said rather than filled in.

The first is a poem about places. The poet says he still recognises the image of the Prophet and the marks of his time, and that the traces have not been erased from the house of sanctity — the quarter of the mosque, the prayer ground, the pulpit the Guide used to climb, and other landmarks that have outlasted him. He describes the chambers the Prophet lived in as worn down by time and yet renewed by longing, and features that the passing years have not effaced. Then he sees him buried, lying in the earth as in a niche. He says he went on weeping for the Prophet while others rejoiced, remembering the banners and everything he had witnessed, and the surviving line that closes it is the plainest in the poem: that this is a catastrophe, and that Ahmad has departed.

The second is longer and the damage in it is heavier. What survives is a blessing pronounced on the grave in which Ahmad lies, and the claim that the heavens themselves wept on the day Muhammad died, and that the people fell silent when the earth wept for him. It says the house of revelation was cut off from them, and that the light was departing and fading. Much of the middle of the poem is a portrait of what was lost, and it is a portrait of a manner rather than of an office — a guide whose method was gentleness, who did not make things strict, who was eager that his people be upright and guided and who grieved that they might turn away, who called them to an obedience that would make them happy, and who led them firmly to the truth. Toward the end the poet says the counsel of the Muslims has been lost with him, hopes for him the Garden of Eternity, and says that on the day of resurrection he strives and exerts himself for one thing only: to stand beside the Chosen One.

The third is the most personal of the four, and the damage there has left the direct address almost intact. It opens on an eye that will not sleep, as though its water were kohl upon kohl. Then it turns and speaks to him: O best of those who ever trod on pebbles, do not depart. He offers his own face as a shield against the dust of the grave, and wishes it could be. He speaks of remaining before him at Baqi' al-Gharqad, of the day the people lamented the Prophet, and of his father and mother as the price of those who witnessed his death. Then the wishes turn violent in their grief — that he had never been born, that he had become the venom of lions — and he says that after his death he remained prostrate, and that he will go on living in the city among them. The surviving close asks God to bring them together with good intention, and to write Paradise for them.

The fourth is the shortest and the most broken. What can be read is a statement that God has left them no one after him, that neither female nor male carries such a burden as this, and that God does not create another from his like. He says the necks of all the Banu al-Najjar were humbled by it. He asks who it is that causes his departure and his grief, and observes that after God the loss is hearing and sight themselves. There was light, he says, and light followed it. In the last surviving line he has become like a solitary man, and he addresses him one final time as the best of people.

The history then leaves the poetry where it stands and stops. It has already said, of the material on this subject, that if everything transmitted were set down no single book would hold it, and it does not repeat the apology at the end.

That ending is a choice, and it is worth registering what it declines to do. A volume that has just spent an episode assembling proofs — sayings, incidents, verses, an argument from the four demonstrated obligations — could have closed on the argument, or on the meeting at the hall, or on a summary of the case. Instead the last thing it puts in front of the reader is four broken poems about a man's furniture and his walking pace and the mosque quarter where he used to stand. The doctrinal quarrel is deferred to the next volume, in one sentence. What is allowed to end this one is grief, in the words of someone who had known him for twenty years and whose only skill was verse.

## What this episode lands

A volume that began with a lineage running down from Ishmael to Quraysh ends with three days of practical work.

The last hours are recorded as a series of small physical arrangements, and each one carries something. Two boys are brought in and moved away again when their grandfather loses consciousness. A man propping him up is told to make room, in the language of a better claim, over the use of a chest to lean on. A hand is held while a list of five reachable ways to finish a life is dictated — a sentence, a meal, an excuse, a short pilgrimage, and a moment of struggle — by someone who is finishing his own. A question that assumes every prophet has a guardian is answered with five job descriptions and then a name. And from a pulpit, in the same days, the two women closest to him are told in public that he cannot spare them anything before God. Then a responsibility for a whole people is commended three times over, and he dies against the man he commends it to.

The middle of the episode has no drama in it at all, and that is what it is for. A body is washed three times in a fixed order with named preparations, in a shirt a voice forbade removing, by one man permitted to look and another present with his eyes covered and his objection about the weight already answered. It is wrapped in three garments, one of them a shirt that is not sewn shut. A proposal to bury it in the public cemetery, with one man leading the prayer over it in front of everyone, is met not with a rival claim to that position but with a precedent about prophets — and the alternative arrangement then dissolves the office the proposal would have created, admitting the community ten at a time to a doorway where the same man stands throughout. A recess is cut on the side facing the direction of prayer, palm fronds are laid because the ground is wet, and a quarter of the grave is filled.

Around all of it the history keeps one fact in the frame: that while this was being done, a meeting was under way elsewhere in the city, and the people doing it were not there. Its objection is not that the wrong side won. It is that a gathering of that kind belonged in the mosque, at the hour of the funeral, with the family present — and the promise that the matter will be taken up is the last thing this volume says about it.

What remains is speech. A man stands at the grave and states his claims in ascending order of controversy, beginning with a fact about kinship that only he can say truthfully, ending by asking the crowd to fear God so that what they have already been given is not withdrawn. And a poet who had spent two decades defending him in verse writes four elegies, all of which reach us in pieces, and which turn out to be about the quarter of the mosque, the pulpit, the worn chambers, the damp grave, an eye that will not close, and a way of leading people that consisted mainly of not making things hard for them.
