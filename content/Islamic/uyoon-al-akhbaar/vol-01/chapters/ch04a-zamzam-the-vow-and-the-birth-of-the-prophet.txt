## Where this episode picks up

The father of the Prophet came within one throw of a divining arrow of being sacrificed as a boy, by his own father, in front of the Ka'bah, to keep a promise. Everything this volume goes on to narrate — the birth, the message, the line that runs out of it — hangs on what happened in the few minutes after he was taken by the hand and led out.

That is where this episode arrives. It begins somewhere stranger: with the same father, years earlier, being told in his sleep to dig a hole in the floor of the sanctuary he was charged with keeping.

The last episode carried the custody of the Sacred House down four generations and left it with a blind old man who stayed behind after the city was emptied in front of an invading army. He is the central figure here too — 'Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim — and this episode covers the remainder of his life and the beginning of another one. A vision repeated three nights running and a well recovered from beneath a mosque floor. A vow made in a moment of want that came due years later on the wrong child. And then a birth, a wet-nurse in the desert, two mothers lost early, and a grandfather who kept a small boy beside him on a mattress that nobody else in the family was permitted to sit on.

Three movements, then: the well, the ransom, and the child.

Two things are worth watching as we go. The first is how this author handles a dream. He does not treat visions as decoration; he treats them as evidence, and he says so explicitly — twice in this stretch he stops the narrative to argue that a true dream is a fragment of prophecy and therefore a mark of divine selection. The second is a piece of imagery he returns to again and again, and which does more work in this chapter than any argument: a light. It sits between a man's eyes, it moves from a father to a son, a woman sees it and wants it, and when it has passed on she can tell at a glance that it is gone.

## The well beneath the mosque

The vision came to him three times, and each time it gave him an instruction he could not yet act on.

The first said: "Dig Zamzam — the hidden place of the Grand Sheikh." He woke and said, "O Allah, make it plain to me." He went back to sleep. The second said: "Dig the troubled woman — none shall be able to withhold her from the people but you." Again he said, "O Allah, make it plain to me," and slept again. The third came with directions attached, and they are the strangest sentence in this part of the book: "Dig Zamzam, between al-Farth and the blood, where the crow alights, hottest above the village of the ants, toward the red monuments."

Notice what changes across the three nights. The command itself never changes. What changes is how much detail it carries — and the detail, when it finally arrives, is not the kind a surveyor would recognise. Between the stomach contents and the blood is a place where an animal has been butchered. Where the crow alights is a bird landing. Above the village of the ants is an anthill. None of these are features of the ground. They are events. They have to happen before they can be looked for.

So he did the only thing the instruction allowed. He went to the Sacred Mosque, sat down, and waited for the sign to assemble itself in front of him.

It assembled. A group of people slaughtering a cow lost hold of it; the animal broke its tether, bolted into the Sacred Mosque, and dropped in the exact spot where Zamzam lay. The tether was recognised, the owner came for his cow, and rather than drag it out he slaughtered and skinned it where it had fallen. That produced the stomach contents and the blood. A crow then came down and settled in the manure. And where the crow settled, 'Abd al-Muttalib found an anthill.

He got up and began to dig.

The Quraysh came at him immediately. "O 'Abd al-Muttalib, what do you think you are doing — digging in our mosque without our knowledge?" His answer is the whole of his defence, and he offers no other: "I have been commanded to do this, and He who commanded me will repel anyone who tries to stop me." They backed off. The author's account of why they backed off is precise and a little cold: they understood that a divine charge had been laid on him, and they were safe from His ignorance of their affairs. Nothing was proven to them. They simply recognised the kind of claim it was and decided not to be on the other side of it.

He kept digging, and the hole began giving things up. First swords and shields, buried in the well long ago. The Quraysh, watching, changed their argument: "Give us a share of what you have found." He replied: "It belongs to the House of God." Not to me — and, by implication, not to you either. Then the water came up. He contained it so that it would not overflow, and built a basin over it.

At this point the author stops to explain what has actually been recovered, because it is not a new well. Zamzam is the well of Ishmael son of Abraham. In a year of drought Ishmael was suffering with thirst alongside his mother Hajar; she took refuge in God and called on Him, and God caused the spring to well up in front of them. Later it fell out of use, and when it was abandoned it was buried, and whatever had been placed in it was buried with it. What 'Abd al-Muttalib dug out of the mosque floor was the sanctuary's own history, sealed under the ground since before Quraysh existed. The line from Ishmael down to this family was traced in an earlier episode; here it turns up literally, as water and buried metal.

Khuwaylid ibn Asad ibn 'Abd al-'Uzza put the excavation into verse, and what survives of it does the same thing the author has been doing: it collapses the distance between the generations. He addresses 'Abd al-Muttalib directly as the digger of Zamzam, and sets that act beside two much older ones — Abraham's excavation on the day of Hajar's son, and Gabriel's running in the age of Adam. The claim in the poetry is that this is not a new act at all. It is the same act, done again, by the man whose turn it was.

Among the buried objects he found a golden gazelle, and admired it greatly. What he did next tells you how the custody worked in practice. He did not keep it and he did not simply donate it. He cast lots with arrows — some arrows for the House, some for himself — and the arrow of the House came out. So he used the gazelle to adorn the Ka'bah, and the author notes the consequence in one line: it was the first ornament with which the House was ever adorned. A man who has just been told a share of the find belongs to God submits his own share to a draw, and loses it.

The dispute with the Quraysh, though, was not finished, and the second round of it is reported from Ali ibn Abi Talib.

When the Quraysh saw what had come out of Zamzam they gathered again and put their case on the strongest ground available: "O 'Abd al-Muttalib, this is the well of our father Ishmael, and we have a right to it — let us share in it and in whatever you find." It is a good argument. They are Ishmael's descendants too. His refusal does not dispute the genealogy; it disputes what follows from it: "I will not do that, for this is a matter that was entrusted to me alone and not to you, and He granted it to me from among you." The inheritance is common; the charge is not.

They would not accept it, and proposed arbitration — a soothsayer of Bani Sa'd ibn Hudhaym. He agreed. He set out with them, one man from each tribe of the Quraysh, across a waterless country between the Hijaz and the Levant.

And in one of those parched stretches, his water ran out.

He asked the other Quraysh parties for water. They refused, and their refusal was reasonable: "We are ourselves in want, and we fear for ourselves what has befallen you." Seeing no other course, 'Abd al-Muttalib and his companions dismounted. Each man dug himself a pit and sat down in it, and they made an agreement — whoever dies first shall be buried by those who remain. They were digging graves and drawing lots on who would fill them.

Then 'Abd al-Muttalib said the sentence the whole episode is built on: "By Allah, it is weakness and surrender to simply sit here and wait for death without striking the earth in search of water."

He got up, mounted his camel, and when the animal rose from where it had been resting, water burst from underneath it — a sweet spring. He dismounted, gave thanks, and drank deeply. He and his companions drank and filled their water-skins.

And then he did the thing that settled the case. He called out to the very Quraysh parties who had just refused him: "Come to the water — Allah has provided us with water." They came, and they drank. And having drunk, they gave up the claim on the spot: "By Allah, Allah has already given His judgment for you against us, O 'Abd al-Muttalib. We shall never dispute with you again over Zamzam. Whoever gave you water from this camel in the wilderness is your waterman of Zamzam — go back and tend your watering as its rightful master." So he returned, and they returned, and he was set apart from them in the matter. The arbitration never happened. They never reached the soothsayer.

The author then breaks off the narrative to say why he has told it, and the disclaimer is worth hearing, because he makes it more than once in this book. This is among the virtues that one mentions not to boast, but because it speaks to the truth of his visions. The point of the story is not that 'Abd al-Muttalib was resourceful in a drought. The point is that a man was told something in his sleep, acted on it against the objection of his entire tribe, and was vindicated twice — once in the mosque floor and once in the open desert. And for that the author reaches for a saying of the Prophet: "A true vision is one of twenty-six parts of prophecy."

That is the doctrinal hinge. A true dream is a fraction of prophecy — not prophecy, but a piece of the same substance. So when 'Abd al-Muttalib is shown Zamzam, what he receives is a blessing with a public face: God provided him with water, caused it to spring from the earth for him, and made it a miracle witnessed by all his people, one that none among them could deny or diminish. The witnesses are part of the evidence. A private vision that stayed private would prove nothing.

The portrait that closes this movement is unstinting. 'Abd al-Muttalib was the finest of the Quraysh, their chief, and their master. In him the light of prophecy was visible, along with the majesty of kingship and the awe of virtue, and the author says outright that his qualities are beyond enumeration. He was called Sayyid al-Wadi — the Master of the Valley — meaning Mecca and everything around it.

He had ten children. The best of them were 'Abd Allah and Abu Talib. Abu Talib was the eldest by birth, raised on generosity and noble conduct, and it was clear, the author says, that these two were superior to their brothers.

Hold on to that number. Ten children is not a genealogical detail here. It is the trigger for everything in the next movement.

## The vow and the hundred camels

When he had nine children, 'Abd al-Muttalib made a vow: if a tenth son were born to him, he would sacrifice him.

The author says many narrations have been transmitted on this matter, and he does not pretend to reconcile them. He selects. The most authentic report, he says — transmitted from the Ahl al-Bayt, and given here on the authority of al-Qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad — begins not with the knife but with a hesitation.

'Abd Allah grew up. He was still young, and his father held him very dear. And when the day came, what stopped the sacrifice first was not an argument. It was something God cast into 'Abd al-Muttalib's own heart — a reluctance, and one that was felt by everybody else who looked at the boy. That detail matters for how the whole episode is read. The reprieve does not arrive from outside as a rescue. It starts inside the man holding the knife.

But he went anyway. He took 'Abd Allah by the hand and set out to slaughter him, and the Quraysh rose to stop him.

Their objection is not what you would expect. They do not tell him the vow was wicked, or that God does not want this. They tell him what it will cost the town: "If you slaughter him, men among us will follow your example with their own sons." They are worried about precedent. The most respected man in Mecca is about to establish that a vow of this kind can be honoured, and they can see where that ends.

His reply is the reply of a man genuinely trapped: "What am I to do? I have vowed to do this."

And their counter-proposal is the sentence that unlocks the entire scene: "Stand by your vow, yet find another way."

Not break it. Satisfy it differently. That single instruction is the doctrinal content of this section, and everything that follows is the working out of it.

His brothers from Banu Makhzum rose in the matter as well — 'Abd Allah's mother's people. What survives of al-Ghayrah ibn 'Abd Allah ibn 'Umar ibn Makhzum's verse on the scene is fragmentary, but its charge is legible: it speaks of love, and of a boy dressed in rags being slaughtered as though he were a statue of gold. The verse attributed to the family in this stretch is arguing about worth — what our son is among people who love him, what it would mean to ransom him with property instead, that property is there to be spent and a son is not.

Abu Talib defended his brother, and so did another brother, al-Zubayr ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib. Abu Talib's lines on it survive in better shape than most of the verse here, and they swear by the Lord of the House: by every kinsman of the House, every pilgrim who comes to God's House beneath its covering — 'Abd Allah was not to be slaughtered easily, not among young men of high lineage, not among women of noble descent. He casts himself as caught between hostile rivals and his own people of ancestry and standing, and tells his father plainly that this is not what he looks like in the eyes of the Lords.

Then 'Abd al-Muttalib answered them in verse himself, and it is the clearest thing anyone says in the whole scene:

O God, my God, I have made a vow, and I fear God if I disobey His command. This is my son — I meant to slaughter him. If He delays him and accepts his excuse, I bind myself to thank the Almighty God.

Read that carefully. He is not asking to be released. He is asking whether God will accept a postponement and an excuse — and he is pre-committing to gratitude if the answer is yes. He has moved the question from what he owes to what God will accept.

So he brought out ten camels and said: cast the lots, and slaughter these in his place, then release him. But he added a condition on himself. "I do not know whether Allah is pleased with me or whether my vow is fulfilled — I will keep casting until He is."

He cast. The arrow fell to 'Abd Allah.

He added ten more camels and cast again. The arrow fell to 'Abd Allah.

He kept adding, ten at a time, and the arrow kept falling to the boy, until the total reached a hundred. At a hundred, the arrow fell to the camels.

And then he refused his own result. "No, by Allah, I will not accept this once — the arrow must fall on the camels three times." He cast again: the camels. A second time: the camels. A third time: the camels.

"Now I know that my Lord is pleased."

He slaughtered them and said to the people, "Take." And they rushed to take the meat — which is the last thing in the scene and easy to skip past. A hundred camels is an enormous quantity of food, and it goes to the town. The ransom did not disappear into a rite. It was eaten.

The author's reading of the whole affair is that 'Abd al-Muttalib's intention to sacrifice his son was an inspiration from God, following the example of Abraham. He grounds it in the Qur'an, where Abraham says to his son: "O my son, I see in a dream that I am slaughtering you, so tell me what you think." And the son answers: "Father, do what you are commanded; you will find me, God willing, among the patient." (chapter 37, verse 102) 'Abd Allah, the author says, was ransomed with a hundred camels just as Ishmael was ransomed with the great sacrifice — God following that act of grace toward Ishmael with this one, and bestowing His bounty on whomever He wills.

So the structure repeats: a father, a son, a command understood as binding, and a substitute accepted at the last moment. What was true of the ancestor is true again of the descendant. The author has already argued in an earlier episode that the Prophet's forebears held to Abraham's religion and never worshipped idols, and he simply notes it again here in passing rather than restating the case — these are men keeping a law, not improvising a rite.

And then he shifts from the theology to the face of the boy who survived it.

'Abd Allah ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib was raised on the finest moral character, on purity, generosity, prosperity, and honour. He was beautiful and handsome. And between his eyes there shone a radiant light, which the author names for exactly what it is: the clear sign of the imminent birth of the Messenger of God.

That light is about to become a plot device, and the story that follows is one of the strangest in the volume.

'Abd Allah married Amina bint Wahb. And a priestess of Khatam named Fatima bint Murr, one of the people of Tabala, looked at him. She had studied the scriptures. She could see the light in his face, and she knew what it meant.

So she made him an offer. She called him to herself, and he refused. She offered him a hundred camels — the same number that had bought his life — and he refused again, in verse: as for what is forbidden, death stands in front of it; and what is lawful has not come in a form you could soften. A generous man guards his honour and his religion. How could I do what you are asking?

Then she asked him to marry her, properly. And here the story turns. He went first to his wife Amina; afterwards he intended to marry Fatima in the lawful manner, according to the religion of Abraham. And it was she who refused.

Her explanation is the whole point of the episode: "I saw a light in your face, and I know of a prophet who is to be sent near the time of his birth. I had hoped that I might be the one, but now I see that light has departed from you."

She had not wanted him. She had wanted what he was carrying, and between one meeting and the next it had been transferred. He told her what had passed with his family, and her verse on it — damaged in transmission, but clear in outline — describes a dazzling flash like the shimmer of lightning, a light in his face spreading around him like the light of the full moon, and her asking to be given him. She turns then to Banu Hashim and to Amina: what the boy contains, a lamp gone out, a father bereaved, and what she lost for her own sake. There is nothing bitter in it. She had read the scriptures accurately, identified the right man, made her bid, and been an evening too late.

Amina conceived, and God preserved that light, founded as it was on purity. The author gives her lineage in full, running her back through 'Abd Manaf ibn Zuhra to Kilab, Murrah, Ka'b, Lu'ayy, Ghalib, Fihr, Malik, al-Nadr and Kinana — the same trunk as her husband's, met from another branch.

Then 'Abd Allah died. On one report he died two months after the Prophet was born; on another he died while Amina was still pregnant. The author does not choose between them, and this book rarely does.

The laments for him are the most damaged verse in the chapter, and what remains of them is grief rather than argument. Amina's lines mourn a master among his people, a man mourned aloud in the open, whose loss came like clouds that gather and do not fall. Another lament sets him among the generous line and turns on the one thing the mourner cannot get past: that had he lived, everything would have been known — he would have been a guiding light in the darkness. He was buried by companions who bore him tenderly, and his brother Abu Talib mourned him too.

The man whose life had been bought for a hundred camels died young enough that his son would never remember him.

## The mattress in the Ka'bah's shade

While Amina was pregnant, someone came to her in her sleep and told her two things. First, what she was carrying: "You are pregnant with the master of this nation." Second, what to say when she delivered him — a short formula of refuge, committing the child to the One, from the evil of every envious person, and naming him Muhammad.

She was given his name before he was born.

She also saw a light come out of her, and it illuminated the palaces in his sight. She told her husband 'Abd Allah; he told his father 'Abd al-Muttalib; and the old man waited for the birth with an eagerness the author makes a point of recording.

When she gave birth she said what she had been instructed to say. 'Abd al-Muttalib came, received the child with joy, carried him to the Ka'bah, and prayed over him there. His prayer survives as verse: praise to God who gave him this boy, the remedy of the distressed; a boy who has already prevailed in the cradle over other boys; a request that he be brought back to the house of the corners until the grandfather can see him fully grown; and a seeking of refuge from every evil. Then he went out to the child's mother and gave him back to her.

The date is given precisely. The Messenger of God was born on a Monday, ten days into the month of Rabi' al-Awwal, in the Year of the Elephant — the year the previous episode ended on, when an army came to demolish the House and did not. The author reads the two events as one act of protection with two faces: what God granted the Quraysh that year was His shielding, so that no enemy could appear against them while the Prophet was still a child, or before he was born at all. The elephant was turned back for the sake of a boy who had not yet been conceived.

He was nursed by a woman of Banu Sa'd ibn Abi Bakr — Halima bint Abi Dhu'ayb. Her husband, in whose household the nursing took place, was al-Harith ibn 'Abd al-'Uzza ibn Rifa'a, of the same tribe. They kept seeing in the child the marks of virtue and the signs of prophecy; they were blessed through him, and they witnessed things that testified to his distinction.

What happened out there among Banu Sa'd is told, unusually, by the Prophet himself. Some of his companions asked him: "O Messenger of Allah, tell us about yourself." And his answer begins by placing himself in a line rather than describing his character: "I am the answer to the prayer of my father Abraham, and I am the good news brought by my brother Jesus." He is the granting of one request and the fulfilment of one announcement — an inheritance claim on both sides.

Then he tells them about his mother's vision, from her side: she saw, when she conceived him, a light come out of her that lit the palaces of Syria. And then he tells them what happened in the desert.

"I was nursed among Banu Sa'd ibn Bakr, and while I was with a brother of mine tending our flock, two men came to me wearing white garments, carrying a vessel filled with snow. They split open my belly, extracted my heart and split it," and from it they took out a black clot, washed his belly, and sealed it with that ice until he was satisfied.

Then one of them said: weigh him against ten of his community. He outweighed all ten. Weigh him against a hundred. He outweighed them. Weigh him against a thousand. He outweighed them too. And then: leave him — for even if you weighed him against his entire community, he would outweigh them all.

Two operations in one scene. Something is taken out of him, and then he is put on a scale. The first is a purification; the second is a ranking, and the ranking is unbounded — the last line closes off the possibility that any quantity of his own people could balance him.

He also used to say to his companions, on this same period of his life: "I am the most eloquent of you — I am of Quraysh, and I was nursed among Bani Sa'd ibn Bakr." The claim is about language. The town gave him one thing and the desert gave him another, and the combination is what he names.

The people of the biographies explain why Halima brought him back. After the weaning, when she came back with him, people saw the child with her and gathered around to look at him. They asked her about him, examined him closely, and said: let us take this boy to our king and our land — this is a child of great importance. She only escaped them with difficulty. She brought him to Mecca and returned him to his mother and to his grandfather.

She gave him up because keeping him had become dangerous. Recognition, in this book, is repeatedly the thing that puts him at risk.

His mother Amina died when he was four. It was also said she died when he was six. Either way, he had lost both parents before he was old enough to have chosen anything.

So the Messenger of God lived in the care of his grandfather, and the author's picture of that household is the warmest thing in the chapter.

'Abd al-Muttalib used to have a mattress laid for him in the shade of the Ka'bah, and his sons would sit around it waiting for him to come out. None of them would sit on it. That was the rule of the house — the mattress was the old man's, and grown men, chiefs in their own right, sat on the ground beside it.

The Messenger of God would come as a small boy and sit straight down on it. His uncles would try to move him off. And every time 'Abd al-Muttalib saw it happening he would say: "Leave my son — Allah has a great matter destined for him." Then he would sit the child next to him on the mattress, pat him on the back, and look at him with great affection.

The men who could not sit on it watched a child be lifted onto it. And the reason given was not indulgence but expectation: something is coming for this boy, so let him sit.

When 'Abd al-Muttalib died, the Messenger of God was in his lap.

He had already made arrangements. He entrusted the child's care to his son Abu Talib, and composed verses about the charge. What survives of them is an instruction and a plea. He commends the boy to Abu Talib for the time after he himself is gone and beneath the earth; he asks him to be to the child what a mother is in her tenderness, lowering him from her own entrails and liver, however long the span of care turns out to be. He commends the family to him. He asks that no hatred come upon him, since none of this is by design. And he reports the boy's own standing back to his son: my nephew, he says, and the saying is trustworthy — I have not lived among Ma'ad except as the most devoted of my sons in affection. That this boy is the master of the people.

That is a will, delivered in verse, with a child in the lap of the man delivering it.

As death drew near, 'Abd al-Muttalib gathered his daughters together and told them: let each one of you recite to me her lament before I die. Each of them composed verses, and the biographies record them. He wanted to hear his own elegies while he could still hear.

When he died, Abu Talib mourned him. His elegy calls him the elder of the House of religion and generosity, weeps with tears falling like pearls, and credits him with virtues that surpassed the masters of nations — the one who relieved distress in hardship and vengeance, who protected those who sought his protection, a leader no one could rival in character, the light that dispelled the darkness of oppression, the guardian of the House of God who filled it with the honour of a proud people, and the lord of the assembly, generous to his guests. The author pauses on that last item to connect it backwards: the assembly prepared for 'Abd al-Muttalib went back to the time of Qusay ibn Kilab, for him and for the great men among his sons. The office was still the office.

Hudhayfa ibn Ghanem, of Banu 'Adi ibn Ka'b ibn Lu'ayy, wept for him too, and his long lament does something the family elegies do not: it uses the death to run back up the line. What remains of it mourns a man whose loss no age can replace, the pride of Quraysh and its shield, the arbiter and the defender, the spring of Lu'ayy in hardship and in plenty — and then keeps going, back through 'Abd Manaf, through the bread of Hashim, through the family of Qusay, naming them as the keepers of dignity and the link of the House of God in ease and hardship.

The lament for one custodian turns into a roll-call of all of them, and it is the last time in this volume that the line will be recited as a line of custodians rather than as the ancestry of a prophet.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry forward.

The first is a rule about how this author reasons. A dream, in his hands, is evidence — a fraction of prophecy, as the Prophet's own saying has it — and its truth is established by what happens afterwards in public. 'Abd al-Muttalib was told three times to dig, waited for a cow, a crow and an anthill to arrange themselves, dug in the middle of somebody else's mosque, and came up with the buried well of Ishmael. Then, contesting the ownership of it, he was given water in a waterless country and handed it to the men who had refused him. He never won the argument. The argument stopped being available.

The second is the shape the family's crisis takes and how it resolves. A vow that a tenth son would be sacrificed came due on the boy the town could least afford to lose, and the way out was not a repudiation but a substitution — stand by your vow and find another way. A hundred camels, an arrow that had to fall three times before the man holding it would believe his own result, and a father who asked only whether God would accept a postponement. The author reads it as Abraham's trial repeating in the descendant, one ransom answering another, and the boy who walked away from it carried a light in his face that a scripture-reading priestess recognised on sight and lost by a single night.

The third is a small boy on a mattress in the shade of the Ka'bah that nobody else in his family was allowed to touch. He was named in a dream before he was born, delivered in the Year of the Elephant, nursed in the desert, opened and weighed by two men in white, given up by a foster-mother who found him too easily recognised to keep safe, and orphaned twice before he was six. And when the grandfather who had raised him died with the child in his lap, he had already put the care of him in writing, in verse, to the son he trusted most.

That son is Abu Talib, and the next episode is his. The boy is eight years old, the guardianship is about to begin, and the first thing that happens under it is a journey north — where a monk who had spent years ignoring the Meccan caravans comes down off his hermitage to look at one child in it.
