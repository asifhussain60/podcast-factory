## Where this episode opens

What does it mean that the man who opened Khaybar was the third man sent to open it?

The previous episode ended with a written settlement that gave away almost everything and was named an opening anyway. This one begins two months later, with an army marching north out of Medina toward the strongest fortress complex in the Hijaz — and its centre is a sentence spoken not before the fighting but after it, to a man standing there with a torn-out gate still in his hands.

Three movements. First the march on Khaybar and what happened at its walls: a supplication said over every settlement he ever approached, a chanter killed by his own sword, and a banner given out and returned twice before being given a third time with a promise attached. Then the reckoning of Khaybar — what was taken, forbidden and contracted, a roasted foreleg that carried poison, a Muslim who lied to Quraysh with permission and emptied their purses doing it, and a verse revealed a year later that looks back and calls Khaybar a near victory. Then Mu'tah, where two hundred thousand men met three thousand.

Listen for what the account keeps doing with hands. A blow deflected on a shield. A gate torn out of a wall by one man and not lifted afterward by eight. A hand cut off and a banner moved to the other. And a pair of arms clasping a standard to a chest when there are no hands left to hold it with.

## The banner given three times

After returning from al-Hudaybiyah, the Messenger of God remained in Medina through Dhu al-Hijjah and into part of Muharram — and during that stretch the polytheists still held custody of the sacred pilgrimage. Then, in the remaining days of Muharram of the seventh year, he set out for Khaybar with the Muhajirun, the Ansar, and the most distinguished of his companions. He gave the banner to Ali ibn Abi Talib, and it was white.

Hold that sentence: the account will contradict it within a page and keep both versions.

Ibn al-Akwa' went out ahead of the Messenger of God, striking the ground with his feet and chanting. The verses ask God for the one thing an army setting out cannot supply itself: had it not been for God we would not have been guided, nor given charity, nor performed our prayers; send down upon us steadfastness, and make our feet firm when we meet them.

The Messenger of God said: May God have mercy on you.

He was killed on the day of Khaybar as a martyr. His own sword glanced back against him while he was fighting fiercely, and he died of the wound.

And then a thing the account does not soften. The Muslims fell into doubt and began saying he had killed himself with his own weapon — until the Messenger of God declared: He is a martyr. Then they prayed over him.

A man dies by his own blade in a battle he had chanted his way into, and the army's instinct is not grief but suspicion. The question is legal — whether a man killed by his own weapon has fallen in God's cause or out of it — and the visible facts are identical either way. Note the sequence: doubt first, ruling second, prayer only after the ruling.

Earlier, arriving within sight of Khaybar, he said to his companions: Halt. Then:

O God, Lord of the seven heavens and all they shade, Lord of the seven earths and all they carry, Lord of the devils and those they lead astray, Lord of the winds and all they scatter — we ask You for the good of this settlement, the good of its people, and the good of what lies within it. We seek refuge in You from its evil, the evil of its people, and the evil of what lies within it. In the name of God.

He would say this upon approaching every settlement he intended to enter.

That last line converts a supplication into a habit. He stands outside a hostile fortified town he has marched an army to reduce, and the words ask for its good before they ask protection from its evil — and the same words were said outside every town he ever entered, so they carry no assessment of Khaybar at all.

The halting places are given. Departing Medina he stopped at 'Asr, where he had a mosque, then at al-Sahba', and then pressed on until he descended into a valley known as al-Raja', between the lands of Khaybar and Ghatafan — and the account gives the reason without dressing it up: to cut off any support from Ghatafan. The march is not a straight line to the target. It is a wedge driven between the target and its allies before the target knows an army is coming.

He arrived at Khaybar under cover of night, so that by morning the Muslims were mounted and in formation around him. The people of Khaybar came out carrying their tools for the day's work, saw the army, and cried out: Muhammad! Muhammad and his army! They turned and fled.

The Messenger of God said: God is greatest. Khaybar is destroyed. When we descend into the courtyard of a people, wretched is the morning of those who have been warned.

Farmers walking out to work with implements in their hands, and a formed army standing in their fields. The night march and the wedge at al-Raja' were aimed at exactly that moment.

Now the contradiction. The Messenger of God first gave the banner to Abu Bakr — it was white — and sent him against the people of Khaybar. He returned without victory. The next day he sent 'Umar ibn al-Khattab, who also returned without conquering anything.

Then the Prophet said: Tomorrow I shall give the banner to a man who loves God and His Messenger, and whom God and His Messenger love. He does not flee. God will open Khaybar at his hands.

Everyone eagerly hoped to be that man.

Consider the shape of that. A man is described by four properties and the name is withheld. The description is issued as a public criterion, in front of an army that has just watched two attempts fail, and the whole camp spends a night measuring itself against it. The naming, when it comes, is heard as a verdict rather than an appointment.

When morning came, the Messenger of God said: Where is my brother Ali ibn Abi Talib?

He was summoned. The Prophet spat into his eyes and said: O God, remove from him the discomfort of heat and cold. From that day Ali was never again troubled by either. Then he gave him the banner: Go forward with it, until God opens victory at your hands.

He set out at a swift pace, the Muslims behind him, until he planted the banner against a rock at the foot of the fortress. A Jew looked down from the top of the fortification and called: Who are you?

He replied: I am Ali ibn Abi Talib.

The Jew said to those around him: By what was revealed to Moses son of 'Imran, you have been defeated.

That is the enemy's own sentry supplying the assessment, and swearing it on his own scripture. No blow has landed. A name was called up a wall, and the man on the wall told his own people the siege was over.

A Jewish warrior came out from the fortress, fully armed and trembling with battle-fury, and chanted: Khaybar knows well that I am Marhab — a proven warrior, fully armed, a hero of battle; sometimes I thrust with the spear, sometimes I strike with the sword, when the young man steps forward to fight; none may come near my protected ground.

Ali answered him: I am the one whose mother named him Haydara. I shall feed you the blade of the sword, full measure.

Two challenges, and they are not symmetrical. Marhab's is a resume — reputation, equipment, repertoire of strokes, boundary. Ali's is two lines with no inventory at all: a name his mother gave him, and a promise about what the other man is about to be fed.

Marhab launched his attack against the Commander of the Faithful, and Ali met the blow with his shield and deflected it. Then Ali seized a door from the fortress gate and used it as a shield. Swinging it as a weapon, he struck Marhab, shattered his shield, and brought down a blow of mighty, resounding force. He fought on with that door in his hand until he had slain a number of their champions.

Then God opened his hands, and he flung the door away — after which eight men gathered together to move it. It was said there were more. However many they were, they turned the door over and could not lift it.

The account does not say the door was heavy; it stages a measurement. A number of men — eight, or more, the source will not commit — get it rolled over and cannot raise it. And the phrase used for the release is not that he dropped it: God opened his hands, and the door fell.

Ali's own account of the day is preserved, and it is worth hearing in his words rather than the narrator's.

When the Messenger of God led the campaign against Khaybar, its people met us like mountains of horses and weapons. They held the most fortified of strongholds and were the greatest in number, each man calling out to challenge anyone who would face him, and not one of the Muslims who approached them was left unkilled.

Until I pressed forward, drawn into combat, every man occupied with himself. The Messenger of God and his family came toward their position, and not one of their men appeared before me without my killing him, and no horseman among them stood against me without my running him through. Then I drove at them with the full ferocity of a lion upon its prey, and thrust them deep into their city until they scattered among themselves.

I came to the city gate and found it shut against them, so I tore it out with my hands and entered their city alone, killing every man of theirs who appeared before me and taking captive every woman I found among their wives. I opened it alone, and I had no helper except God alone.

Notice the last phrase. I had no helper except God alone is simultaneously a boast and its own cancellation: a man who says he acted alone and then names God as his only assistance has not claimed the thing he appears to be claiming.

When Ali came back to the Messenger of God having opened the gate, this was said to him:

Were it not that a group among my community would say of you what the Christians say of Christ, I would have said to you today a word such that you would not pass by a gathering of people without them taking from the dust under your feet and the water of your purification as a means of healing.

But it is enough for you that you stand to me as Aaron stood to Moses — except that there is no prophet after me. You will acquit my obligations, and you will fight for my Sunnah. In the Hereafter you will be the closest of all people to me, and you will be at the basin as my successor. You will be the first to come to me at the basin, because you were the first to believe in me, the first to be clothed by me, and the first of my community to enter Paradise.

And when your Shia stand on pulpits of radiant light, their faces bright, I will intercede for them so that they may be my neighbours in the Garden. Whoever fights you fights me, and whoever makes peace with you makes peace with me. Your bond of kinship is as my bond of kinship. You are born of my son and fulfil my obligations. And if the truth is on your tongue and in your heart, before your hands and before your eyes, and faith is mingled with your flesh and blood just as it is mingled with mine, then no one who hates you will reach the basin, and no lover of yours will fail to find you there with him.

Take the first sentence on its own, because everything after it depends on it. He does not say the word. He says there is a word he is not going to say, names the harm that would follow — that a party of his own community would treat this man the way Christians treat Christ — and then describes, in physical detail, the veneration that would result. The withholding is not modesty about the man. It is a precaution about the crowd.

What he says instead is a list of positions rather than praises. A standing borrowed from Aaron and Moses and immediately fenced: except that there is no prophet after me. A discharge of obligations, a defence of the Sunnah, a place at the basin held as successor — and the reason given for that place is chronological priority in belief: first to believe, first to be clothed, first into the Garden. Then the four load-bearing clauses: whoever fights you fights me, whoever makes peace with you makes peace with me, your kinship is my kinship, faith is mingled in your flesh as in mine. Each collapses a distance. And the last sentence turns the whole into a test applying to the listener rather than to the man addressed.

At that, Ali prostrated and said: Praise be to God, who blessed me with Islam, taught me the Quran, made me beloved to the best of creation — the phrase is damaged in transmission and is given at the level of its surviving sense — and set upon me the seal of the Prophets and the Messengers, as a grace from Him to me and a distinction He conferred upon me.

And the Messenger of God said: Were it not for you, O Ali, the believers would not have recognised me after me.

That is a claim about knowledge, not rank. It does not say the believers would have lacked a leader. It says they would not have recognised him — that the Messenger would have become unrecognisable to his own community once he was gone, and that the thing preventing it is this man.

## The reckoning of Khaybar

The Messenger of God then opened Khaybar and seized its fortresses. He put to death whomever he willed among the men, granted safety to whomever he willed, took captives from their descendants, and chose for himself Safiyyah bint Huyayy ibn Akhtab — daughter of the man this history already showed going fortress to fortress turning allies against the community during the siege of Medina.

Then the prohibitions, which are the first thing the account records after the fall. He forbade four things on the day of Khaybar: taking ropes from captives; eating domestic donkey; eating any creature possessing a tusk from among the predators; and selling spoils before they are divided. Jabir ibn 'Abd Allah reported that he forbade the meat of donkeys and permitted the meat of horses.

And he delivered a sermon at Khaybar. The substance of it was this:

It is not lawful for a man who believes in God and the Last Day to water his crops using the irrigation of another man — meaning the tied labour of the captives. It is not lawful for a man who believes in God and the Last Day to approach a captive woman until she has been declared free. It is not lawful for a woman who believes in God and the Last Day to sell her share until she has taken her oath and been released. Nor is it lawful for a man who believes in God and the Last Day to ride a mount belonging to the Muslims and then return it worn out and ruined. And it is not lawful for a man who believes in God and the Last Day to wear a garment belonging to the Muslims and return it to them threadbare and destroyed.

Five clauses, and every one opens with the same formula: it is not lawful for a man — or a woman — who believes in God and the Last Day. The repetition is the argument. Each prohibition is anchored not in the commander's authority but in the listener's own profession of faith, so it reads not as an order but as a description of what belief already excludes.

And look at what is prohibited on the morning after a victory, when the whole camp is rich. Three of the five concern property belonging to the community rather than to you, and the injury each names is not theft but wear: you gave it back, and you gave it back destroyed. This is a sermon about what victorious men do to things that are not quite theirs.

Then the terms. The Messenger of God made peace with the people of Khaybar, of Fadak, and of al-'Awali, on the condition that they cultivate the land and retain half of whatever it produced, and that whenever he willed he could expel them from it. A conquered population is left on its own soil, keeping half the yield — with the right of removal held in reserve. They keep everything except security.

When the Messenger of God had settled and felt secure, Zaynab the daughter of al-Harith, wife of Salam ibn Mishkam, presented him with a gift: a roasted sheep. She had first inquired which part was most beloved to him, and was told: the foreleg. She brought it and set it before him.

He took the foreleg and chewed it, but could not swallow it, and called out: I have been poisoned. Bishr ibn al-Bara' ibn Ma'rur had also eaten from it. He spat his out and said: This bone is telling me that it is poisoned.

He summoned her, and she confessed. He said: What drove you to do this?

She said: If you are a king, we shall be rid of you; and if you are a prophet, you will be told.

The Messenger of God pardoned her. The man who had eaten from it died of what he had consumed.

Her answer is the most economical piece of reasoning in the chapter, and it is spoken by a woman who has just been caught. She has not built a plot; she has built an experiment, and says so to the man she has poisoned. If he is a king, the poison works. If he is a prophet, it is disclosed to him. And by the time she speaks, the experiment has returned its result: he stopped chewing before anyone said a word, and named what was in the meat.

The pardon is left without explanation. And the account attaches a coda that lands years later. Umm Bishr, the sister of the man who died, came to the Messenger of God during his final illness, and he said to her: O mother of Bishr, this is the time — I find in it the severing effect of that food which I ate with your brother at Khaybar.

The gate and the meal are placed in the same chapter deliberately. One took a fortress in a morning. The other is still working at the end of a life.

Then a piece of wartime deception carried out with permission, narrated with obvious relish. When Khaybar was conquered, al-Hajjaj ibn 'Ilat al-Sulami came and said: O Messenger of God, I have money in Mecca with my wife Umm Shayba bint Abi Talha, and wealth scattered among its merchants. Grant me leave. He was given leave.

Mecca was agitated by rumour, stopping travellers and asking about a march they had heard was aimed at Khaybar — which they knew to be the great stronghold of the Hijaz. When al-Hajjaj arrived they said: this is the man with the news. They did not know he had become a Muslim.

He said: I have news that will please you. He suffered a defeat the like of which you have never heard. His companions were slaughtered as never before. And Muhammad himself was taken captive.

They said: We shall not kill him until we send word to the people of Mecca so they may kill him too. And they stood and shouted it through the streets.

Then he asked them a favour. Help me collect my wealth in Mecca and among my assets — I wish to go to Khaybar, for the people of Muhammad and his companions may have got there ahead of me as merchants. So they gathered everything he had for him.

The lie does two jobs at once. It gives Mecca the defeat it wants to hear, and the celebration is the cover under which a man calls in every debt owed him and is handed his money by people helping him reach the sale early.

When al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib heard the news he came to al-Hajjaj and asked what it was. He said: Guard what I entrust to you, Abu al-Fadl, for I gathered their money using it, and I fear the consequences three times over. Al-'Abbas said: I shall. And then he was told the truth: by God, you have left your nephew as a groom to the daughter of their king.

Three days after al-Hajjaj's departure, al-'Abbas put on his finest garment, took up his staff, went out to the Ka'bah and circumambulated it. When they saw him they said: Abu al-Fadl, this is how one bears a calamity.

He said: No, by God. Muhammad conquered it by force, and took the daughter of their king Huyayy ibn Akhtab as his bride, and their wealth and all that it contained became his and his companions'.

They said: Who brought you this news?

He said: The same one who brought you what he brought you — and he has now become a Muslim, so he took his wealth and set out to join Muhammad and his companions.

They said: O servants of God! The enemy of God has slipped away. And it was not long before the news reached them by other roads.

The city learned its celebration had been a fiction only after the man who sold it to them was out of reach with their money — and learned it in its most public place, from a man dressed for a festival, walking around the House.

When the Messenger of God had finished at Khaybar he proceeded to Wadi al-Qura, besieged its people for several nights, and returned to Medina. The poetry composed on the conquest — by Ibn Luqaym al-'Absi, by Hassan ibn Thabit, by Ka'b ibn Malik — has come down badly damaged, with whole lines opaque and proper names mangled past repair, and is carried here only at the level of what still reads: fortresses and the horses that came against them, the flight of Khaybar's defenders into open country, and the martyrs counted on the other side of the ledger.

He remained in Medina through the two months of Rabi', the two Jumadas, Rajab, Sha'ban, Ramadan and Shawwal. Then, in Dhu al-Qa'dah of the seventh year, he set out to perform the pilgrimage the polytheists had turned him away from.

The Muslims went out with him, and when the people of Mecca heard of his approach they withdrew from his path. Quraysh said among themselves that Muhammad and his companions were worn down by hardship, and gathered at the arcade of the sanctuary to watch them come in.

When the Messenger of God entered, he wrapped his robe under his right arm, baring it, and said: May God have mercy on the man who shows them his strength today. He touched the corner and set off at a brisk trot, his companions alongside him, until the House stood between him and the watching crowd; then he took hold of the Yemeni corner and walked the rest. He ran the first three circuits so, and walked the remaining ones.

The Muslims understood that he had done this only because Quraysh were watching, wishing to display strength. And then, at the Farewell Pilgrimage, he did it again — and it became an established practice.

A gesture is improvised for a single audience on a single morning, aimed at men leaning on a wall; its whole meaning is local. Then it is repeated at a pilgrimage where those men are no longer the audience, and by that repetition it becomes a rite pilgrims still perform. Notice too that the trot stops when the House comes between him and the crowd. He was not running for the sanctuary. He was running for the spectators, and he walked where they could not see.

'Abd Allah ibn Rawahah took hold of his camel's halter as he entered and chanted — the lines are damaged, and what survives is a command to clear the way, a declaration that all good is in his Messenger, and a profession of belief in him. He remained in Mecca three days, after which Quraysh urged him to depart.

And during that pilgrimage this was revealed: God has fulfilled the vision of His Messenger in truth: you shall enter the Sacred Mosque, God willing, in security, with your heads shaved and hair cut short, without fear — for He knew what you did not know, and He has appointed a near victory besides that (chapter 48, verse 27).

The near victory mentioned there, the account says, is the conquest of Khaybar.

So the verse arrives a full year after the fortress fell and reaches backwards to name it. At the time, Khaybar was a campaign against a fortified settlement to the north. Read from inside this pilgrimage, it is the smaller opening granted while the larger one was still being waited for.

## The three commanders at Mu'tah

Ja'far ibn Abi Talib arrived on the day Khaybar was conquered — back from Abyssinia at last, with his wife Asma' bint 'Umays, his son 'Abd Allah who had been born in that country, and a company of the Muslims who had emigrated there. This history has already told how that crossing was made and what Ja'far said before the Negus; what matters here is that the exile ended on the morning of a conquest.

And the Messenger of God said: I do not know which of the two brings me greater joy — the opening of Khaybar, or the coming of Ja'far.

Weigh that against everything the last section described — fortresses, champions killed, a gate torn from its hinges, a stronghold the whole Hijaz thought impregnable — set on a balance against one man walking back into Medina with his family, and the balance will not tip. Remember the sentence. In fifteen months this man will be dead.

After the pilgrimage he remained in Medina through Dhu al-Hijjah — the polytheists again overseeing that season — and through Muharram, Safar and the months of Rabi'.

The battle of Mu'tah took place in Jumada al-Awwal of the eighth year. The Messenger of God dispatched an army to Mu'tah and appointed his cousin Ja'far ibn Abi Talib to command it, saying: If Ja'far is struck down, then Zayd ibn Haritha shall lead the people; if Zayd is struck down, then 'Abd Allah ibn Rawahah shall lead.

Stop on the order of dispatch, because nothing else in the volume looks like it. At the moment of sending an army out, the commander names not one leader but three, each conditional on the death of the man above him. The chain of command is announced in advance as a chain of deaths, and every man in the ranks marches out knowing who takes the banner when the first commander falls, and who takes it when the second does. No expectation is stated aloud. The structure states it.

'Abd Allah ibn Rawahah composed farewell verses to the Prophet, and what survives is fragmentary: that he is the Prophet, and who could forbid his prayers; that among the messengers he is a victory like those who triumphed; a prayer that God grant him all the good that came to him; and a face whose beholder is blessed. And when the Messenger of God came out to see Ja'far and his companions off, Ibn Rawahah spoke again — a blessing left behind on a man he calls the finest and most radiant of men.

They marched until they halted at Ma'an, in the land of the Levant. There news reached them that Heraclius had descended upon Ma'ab, in the land of Balqa', with a hundred thousand Romans, and that he had been joined by a hundred thousand men from the Arab tribes of Lakhm, Judham, Bahra' and Bali.

The Muslims remained at Ma'an two nights deliberating. Some said: let us write to the Messenger of God and inform him of the numbers they face, so that he may either send us reinforcements or give us his command, and we shall follow it.

That is a reasonable proposal and the account does not mock it. It is not retreat; it is to report accurately and ask.

But Ja'far ibn Abi Talib and 'Abd Allah ibn Rawahah encouraged the people, saying: We did not march out seeking martyrdom in order to shy away from it when the enemy outnumbers us. We do not fight by force of numbers, nor by strength, nor by equipment — we fight with this religion by which God has honoured us. So advance, for one of two good outcomes awaits us: either victory or martyrdom.

And the people set out.

That is not an appeal to courage. It begins by naming what the expedition was for: if the errand was martyrdom, a report that the enemy is overwhelming is not bad news about the errand — it is the errand arriving. Then the middle clause disqualifies the basis on which the numbers were being counted. Once numbers, strength and equipment are set aside, two hundred thousand is not a larger figure than three thousand; it is a figure in a currency that has stopped being used. Then the close, where a soldier's decision is actually made: one of two good outcomes, with no third branch and no bad one. Ibn Rawahah's own verses on the march survive only in fragments — horses brought up, two nights at Ma'an, Arabs and Romans waiting beyond.

The army pressed forward until they were near Balqa', where the forces of Heraclius met them in one of its villages, said to be called Masharif. The enemy drew near, the Muslims withdrew to a village called Mu'tah and formed their ranks, placing on the right wing a man of Banu 'Udhra known as Qutbah ibn Qatadah, and on the left a man of the Ansar known as 'Abayah ibn Malik.

The armies met and battle was joined. Ja'far ibn Abi Talib took command of the troops — it is also said that he took the banner — and when the fighting grew fierce around him and he feared for his horse, he dismounted and hamstrung it, and fought on foot.

That is a deliberate act and it is not about the horse. A mounted man can leave. Hamstringing your own mount in front of your own line removes the last physical possibility of withdrawal, and removes it visibly, in a way every man behind can see. The commander has made his own retreat impossible before asking anyone else to hold.

As he fought he recited: How good is Paradise drawing near, cold its drink and sweet its fragrance, while Rome faces punishment — a nation of unbelievers, far removed.

He fought on, bearing the standard in his right hand, until that hand was cut off. He took it up with his left, until that too was cut off. Then he clasped the banner to his chest with his arms, and held it so until he fell as a martyr. He was thirty-three years old, and God rewarded him with two wings, with which he flies in Paradise as He wills. It is also said that a man of Rome struck him a single blow and cut him in two.

Read that sequence as the account structures it. Right hand, left hand, chest. The standard never touches the ground while he is alive, and the last stage of holding it is not a grip at all. And the compensation named for it is the loss reversed in kind: the man who lost both hands holding a banner is given two wings.

When Ja'far was martyred, Zayd ibn Haritha took up the banner and fought with it, until the spears of the enemy pressed in upon him and he was killed.

When Zayd fell, 'Abd Allah ibn Rawahah took it up. He rode forward on his horse — and then hesitated for a moment.

Then he said: I swear to you, O soul, you shall not be disgraced. Dismount, or what you hate will come. The people have tightened their reins for battle; I see you loathe nothing but the cut of a blade. How long have you dwelt at ease in this frame? Are you not merely a drop in its vessel? O soul — unless you are killed, you die.

And he said also: This is the dove of death — I have wooed her, and what I longed for I have been given. If you do as they did, you shall be guided — meaning his companions Zayd and Ja'far.

Then he pressed forward and fought until he was killed.

That hesitation is the most human moment in the chapter, and the account refuses to hide it. The third commander in the chain, who knows the chain, watches both men above him die and then stops. And what he does with the pause is argue with himself out loud, in verse, in front of an army — addressing his own soul and closing with a line that removes the option he was weighing: unless you are killed, you die. He did not go forward because he had stopped being afraid. He went forward having said the fear aloud.

Thabit ibn Akram, of the Banu 'Ajlan, then took the banner and handed it to Khalid ibn al-Walid, who accepted it and rallied the troops until the enemy withdrew.

## What this episode lands

Khaybar was opened at the hands of a man named the night before by four properties and not by his name, and what was said to him afterwards was withheld before it was given — a word not spoken because a community would misuse it, and in its place a standing borrowed from Aaron, a place at the basin, and four sentences equating him with the man speaking. Then the ordinary business of victory: prohibitions on how victorious men treat what is not theirs, a contract for half the produce of the fields, a poisoned foreleg that stayed in a body for years, and a lie told to Quraysh with permission that cost them their purses and their celebration together.

And at Mu'tah, the chain of command was announced as a chain of deaths before the army left Medina, and it ran to the end of its length exactly as announced. The next episode begins with how that news arrived in Medina — which was not by messenger.
