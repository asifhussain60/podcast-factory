## Where this episode opens

How do you kill a man so that nobody can be made to answer for it? The council of Mecca worked on that problem for an evening, and its answer was not a better assassin. It was an accounting trick: spread the guilt across every clan in the city so thin that no one could ever collect on it.

The previous episode ended with a town four hundred miles to the north having promised, in the dark on a hillside, to defend a man it had barely met. This one begins with the consequences of that promise arriving in Mecca — the emigrants leaving in groups, the houses emptying, and the leadership of Quraysh working out that the window for solving the problem locally was closing.

Three movements. The night of the plot, and the young man who lay down in the bed the swords were meant for. Then the cave, and the argument the author builds out of a single verse about who was in it — followed by the arrival, the camel that chose the site, and the doors of a new mosque. Then the first two sermons preached in that town, given here whole, and the pact-making that turned an assortment of refugees and hosts into one population.

Watch for the author's habit throughout. Every event here arrives attached to a claim about rank. He is not smuggling that in: he states it, argues it from the Book, and tells you when he is being brief.

## The night of the swords

Islam had by now taken firm root among the Ansar, and the Prophet gave his companions permission to migrate to Medina. They emigrated in groups and settled in the homes of the Ansar, who sheltered them, welcomed them, shared their dwellings with them, and gave them preference over themselves. It was concerning the hosts that God revealed: "Those who had settled in the city and embraced faith before them love those who emigrated to them, finding in their own hearts no desire for what has been given to the emigrants, but preferring them over themselves even when they are in need — whoever is saved from the greed of his own soul, it is they who are the prosperous" (chapter 59, verse 9).

The praise in that verse is not for belief and not for courage. It is for an absence — no desire in the heart for what the newcomers were given. The hosts are commended for what they did not feel about property.

The Messenger of God remained in Mecca after his companions had gone. Nobody was left there except those who were imprisoned or otherwise unable to leave — apart from his cousin Ali ibn Abi Talib and Abu Bakr ibn Abi Quhafa.

When the disbelievers of Quraysh saw this, they understood that he had gained a party and supporters, and they feared his authority would grow formidable against them. So they assembled in the house of the council, Dar al-Nadwa. Present among them, according to some accounts, was the Devil in the form of an elderly shaykh. They were suspicious of him and asked who he was. He said: I am a man from the people of Najd. I have been informed of what you have assembled to discuss and I have come to attend with you; you will not lose any counsel from me that may benefit you.

They conferred. The people of Yathrib have believed Muhammad, they said, and they have drawn to themselves those who have come to them. The companions had migrated to join him. They feared his standing would grow, his authority increase, his supporters multiply. They worried that he would fight them with those who had already followed him, and they doubted the Arabs would resist him the way the people of Yathrib had not. So they asked one another: what should be done?

Some of them said: arrest him, bind him, and throw him in prison. The shaykh of Najd said: what kind of opinion is that? If you do so, his people will come to free him — you know them well. They agreed the shaykh was right.

Another said: banish him from our midst, let him go wherever he will, and we shall have peace. The shaykh said: that would only scatter his influence further and set you to chasing after him.

Then another said: we kill him. The shaykh said: now that is the best counsel you have offered. But here is how it must be done. Take one man from each clan and arm each with a sword. Let them strike him together as one, so that his blood falls on every branch of Quraysh alike. Then the Banu Hashim cannot demand satisfaction from any single tribe — the blood-guilt would be too diffuse to pursue. They said: this is the plan. They agreed upon it and readied themselves for the night they would come to him.

Look at what the intelligence in that room is spent on. Not on overcoming his defenders — the two rejected proposals show they had already priced that. Not on secrecy; the plan is deliberately public, one man per clan. In a society where a killing creates a debt owed by a named family to a named family, the only way to make one survivable is to make the debt unassignable. They were not designing a murder. They were designing an immunity.

Jibril came to the Prophet and informed him of the plot. He then instructed Ali — having told him what Quraysh had conspired — to wrap himself in the Prophet's cloak and sleep in his bed, so that anyone who came would see what appeared to be the Prophet still at rest. He informed Ali of his emigration, told him to remain behind with the family, and charged him to discharge the trusts and debts that people had left with him, and then to follow.

That charge deserves a moment. The men assembling swords outside are, many of them, the same men whose property is sitting inside that house for safekeeping. The instruction is not to take it, not to hold it as leverage — it is to stay behind in a hostile city long enough to give it back to the people who tried to have him killed.

He also instructed Ali to remain composed even as the men surrounded the house. Outside, someone was working the crowd: Muhammad claims that if you follow him you will rule the Arabs and the non-Arabs alike, and that when you die you will enter a garden like the Garden of Jordan — but if you refuse and he kills you, you go to a fire and burn in it. So hasten and be done with it.

That speech is worth hearing twice, because it is not a distortion. It is a fair summary of the message, delivered as the reason to end the messenger. The crowd is being told what he teaches and asked to find it intolerable.

When the Messenger of God heard the commotion outside, he commanded Ali to lie down on his bed and drew the green Hadrami mantle — the one in which he slept — over him. Then he recited the chapter of Ya-Sin, took a handful of dust, scattered it in the faces of those who had gathered, and walked out among them unseen, for God had veiled their sight from him.

He then went to the cave, having first arranged with Abu Bakr, 'Amir ibn Fahira and 'Abdullah ibn Arqat to accompany him and guide him to Medina, carrying what he needed for the journey.

Meanwhile the watchers peered through the open door and saw a figure lying in the bed, wrapped in the cold cloak. They had no doubt it was him. When the full group had assembled and steeled themselves to act, a man who had not been with them from the start came up and asked what they were waiting for.

We mean to kill Muhammad, they said.

God has confounded you, he said. By God, Muhammad has already passed out among you — not one of those present when he left has been spared seeing him — and the dust has already settled on your heads.

They reached up and found dust on the heads of most of them. Then they looked toward Ali through the gap of the door and saw him lying there in the cloak, as if he were the Messenger of God himself at rest. That is Muhammad, they said. They burst in with their swords, and when Ali sensed them he seized the swords out of their hands. When they saw who it was and recognized him, they held back. We have no quarrel with you, O son of Abi Talib, they said. And among themselves: killing him would bring us nothing but blood-feud. So they left.

The plan is defeated by the plan. That council's whole architecture rested on one calculation — a death is only actionable if it can be charged to somebody — and the moment the men in the room find a different man in the bed, they run it again and it comes out the other way. They do not spare him out of scruple. They spare him because they cannot afford him.

Note too what Ali does before they recognize him. A man woken by armed men in a dark room takes their weapons off them. From that the author draws his conclusion directly: this was the first act of jihad Ali carried out in the service of the Messenger of God — offering himself as a shield against death without the Prophet present, facing a large company of men who sought the Prophet's life, at a young age and in the earliest days of his standing in Islam.

And then the history hands the same night to a second kind of witness. Muhammad ibn 'Isa al-Nakha'i narrated with his chain from Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali ibn al-Husayn, who said:

When God commanded His Prophet to emigrate, and informed him of what the polytheists had conspired in order to kill him, He commanded Ali to lie down in his bed, and he did so. Then God revealed to Gabriel and Mika'il: "I have established a brotherhood between you, and I shall take the soul of one of you. Choose which of you it shall be." Both loved life and were reluctant to face death. So God revealed to them: "Neither of you shows the same selfless devotion to his companion that Ali has shown to Muhammad. Go, therefore, and guard him from every harm of My enemy until dawn."

So they descended, and one sat at his head and the other at his feet, and they said: well done, O Ali — the beloved one who gives himself as ransom.

The narration is a controlled comparison, and the comparison is its whole content. Two beings not liable to the ordinary weaknesses are put to precisely the question that was put to the man in the bed — one of you will die for the other, choose — and they hesitate. Nothing is said against them for it; the reluctance is reported plainly, as the natural response of anything alive, and placed on the record so that the absence of hesitation in a house in Mecca has something to be measured against. The assignment that follows is not a punishment but a posting: the two who paused are sent to guard the one who did not, until dawn.

## The cave and the closed doors

The Messenger of God entered the cave, and Abu Bakr entered with him. The polytheists searched until they reached the cave's mouth, but God turned them away. Abu Bakr grew frightened when the searchers drew near the entrance, and the Messenger of God calmed his fear and reassured him of what God had promised concerning His victory and the completion of His command.

This episode is cited by some as a virtue of Abu Bakr — that the Prophet took him as a companion in the cave. The scholars of the Shia, the author says, have held that it points more toward a failing than a virtue, for he did not bear the situation with patience alongside the Prophet, nor did he place trust in what God had promised to send down. Rather he was distressed and anxious. And God rebuked him when He said, "When he said to his companion, 'Do not grieve, for God is with us,'" and then, "God sent down His tranquility upon him and supported him with soldiers you could not see" (chapter 9, verse 40). The Messenger of God and his family, the author says, were the ones upon whom tranquility truly rested.

Then the second argument, which is about a word rather than a pronoun. If the honour of being granted tranquility were a virtue belonging to Abu Bakr, as it is to the believers in another verse — where God says, "Then God sent down His tranquility upon His Messenger and upon the believers, and bound them to the word of piety, for they were most deserving of it and worthy of it" (chapter 48, verse 26) — then mere companionship proves nothing on its own. A believer may travel alongside a disbeliever, a righteous man alongside a sinner. Consider, he says, what God has said: "His companion said to him, while he was speaking with him, 'Do you disbelieve in the One who created you from dust, then from a drop of fluid, then fashioned you into a man? But as for me — He is God, my Lord, and I associate no one with my Lord'" (chapter 18, verses 37 and 38).

That last citation is the load-bearing one. In it the word rendered companion describes a man arguing with a disbeliever. The word carries no rank in it at all; it reports proximity, not standing. Whatever else the cave establishes, the author's claim is that the vocabulary of the verse does not establish it.

And then he closes the file. The true distinction, he says, belongs to the one who ransomed the Prophet with his own life — who slept in his bed and made himself a willing sacrifice for the sake of God, and waged jihad in the path of the Prophet while the Prophet was absent from him. The discussion on this point is long and wide-ranging, but we have aimed for brevity.

Notice what he does not do. He does not deny that Abu Bakr was in the cave, or that the fear was human. He takes the most-cited proof of another party's standing, leaves every fact of it in place, and argues that the facts do not support the weight put on them — then tells you in one sentence that he is stopping early.

The Messenger of God then migrated to Medina. He entered it on a Monday, twelve nights into the month of Rabi' al-Awwal. He was fifty-three years of age, having resided in Mecca for thirteen years after God sent him on a Monday, when he was forty years old.

Ali remained in Mecca for three days after the Prophet's departure, fulfilling his obligations, then followed him to Medina and joined him at the home of Kulthum ibn Haddam in Quba'. And the Messenger of God did not set out from there until Ali arrived, which was one or two nights after his own arrival. The stay at Quba' was Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday. Then on Friday he led the prayer at Bani Salim ibn 'Awf — the first Friday prayer he led in Medina.

The waiting is the detail. He is four days from a city that has been preparing for him a year, with a congregation already assembled, and he stops until the man who stayed behind to hand back other people's property catches up.

As he traveled, groups of men would gather around him and say: O Messenger of God, stay with us — we are many and strong. They would try to halt his camel, and he would say: leave her and do not hold her back, she is under command. So they would step aside and she would move on. Whenever he passed by the Ansar they invited him to stop, and they would seize the camel's halter, and he would say: let her go, she is commanded.

Every one of those invitations is an offer of alliance, and accepting one is a decision against all the others. He is arriving in a town whose defining feature, by the account of the men who invited him, was that its clans could not stop fighting each other. To settle in one clan's quarter on the first day would be to take a side before hearing a case. A camel under orders is the only arbitrator in the valley nobody can accuse of favouritism.

She went on until she reached the area of Bani Malik ibn al-Najjar, and there she knelt at the door of what is now his mosque — a plot belonging to two young orphan boys of Banu al-Najjar, kept as a threshing floor in the stonefield of Mu'adh ibn 'Afra'. When she knelt there he did not dismount immediately. She rose and walked a short distance, then he turned her around and she came back to the spot where she had first knelt, lowered herself there once more, settled firmly, and he dismounted.

He ordered that his belongings be carried into the house of Khalid ibn Zayd — that is, Abu Ayyub — and lodged with him until the mosque was finished. He then asked about the plot of land where the camel had knelt, learned whose it was, and bought it from the orphans.

He bought it. The site was already his by every convention available: chosen by a sign, owned by minors, in a town competing to give him land for nothing. The first act of property in the new community is a purchase from two children who could not have refused.

The mosque was built on that site. He worked on it with his own hands, and the Muhajirun and the Ansar worked alongside him. One of the Muslims composed the lines: if we sit while the one who works toils on, then we have no part in the work of prayer. And the Muslims laboured cheerfully as they built, chanting: there is no life but the life of the Hereafter — O God, have mercy on the Ansar and the Muhajirun. And the Messenger of God took up the words and said: there is no true life but the life of the Hereafter. O God, have mercy on the emigrants and the Ansar.

The chant goes around and comes back with the order reversed. The hosts, digging, ask mercy for themselves and then for their guests; he repeats it and puts the guests first — the same arithmetic the verse at the top of this episode was praising.

Ali and his companions were the most diligent of all who worked. He laboured on and chanted: the one who builds mosques and toils within them, standing and sitting alike, is not the same as one who merely passes by, turning aside from the dust — while the dust still clings, barren and unsettled. Then 'Ammar ibn Yasir took up the chant and worked alongside him, exerting himself tremendously.

'Uthman ibn 'Affan stood to one side. When he saw what was being asked of him in the way of labour he said to 'Ammar, with a stick in his hand: O son of Sumayyah, I will press this stick against your nose.

The Messenger of God heard it and was angered. He said: their wealth calls them to Paradise, and they call him to Hell. And he said: 'Ammar's place is between my eyes and my nose — meaning that 'Ammar was as dear to him as his own face. These are your companions, he said; go with them if you wish.

Work continued until the building was complete. He then ordered his houses to be built nearby, and his companions settled around the mosque. Each of them was given a door opening directly into it, and they lived that way for years — in the house of every one of the companions there was a door leading into the mosque. Then God revealed to His Prophet that he should command them to close their doors, all except the door of Ali.

The account of what that was like comes from Ali himself. Some people came to him and said: tell us of your greatest virtues. He said: I prefer my distinction to be one I did not make for myself. They asked what that was, and he told them.

When the Messenger of God arrived in Medina, he ordered the construction of the mosque. No one was left who had not been assigned a door opening into it — until Gabriel came and commanded him to close all those doors except mine. So he sent a messenger to them. He sent Mu'adh ibn Jabal to Abu Bakr and ordered him to block his door, and Abu Bakr said: I hear and obey, and his door was closed. Then he sent word to 'Umar and ordered him to close his door, and 'Umar came to the Messenger of God and said: O Messenger of God, let me keep it open just enough that I may look at you with my eyes. The Messenger of God refused, and his door was closed. Then he sent word to Talhah, al-Zubayr, 'Uthman, 'Abd al-Rahman, Sa'd, Hamzah and al-'Abbas, ordering them all to close their doors, and they heard and obeyed. Yet Hamzah and some others said: he orders us to close our doors while he leaves the door of Ali open.

The Messenger of God said: I have been told what you said about the closing of the doors. By God, I did not do this of my own accord — God did it. God revealed to Moses to take a house of purification in which none would dwell except him, Aaron, and his sons.

The meaning, as the report explains it, is that no one else was to enter it. And God revealed to me likewise: to take this house as pure, so that none may dwell in it save me, Ali, al-Hasan and al-Husayn. By God, I did not order your doors closed, nor did I leave Ali's door open — it was God who commanded me to do so.

Three things in that. Who objected: Hamza, his own uncle, objecting openly and answered openly, with no penalty and no change to the order. The shape of the answer, which is not a defence of the decision but a denial of authorship — the Prophet does not argue that Ali deserves the open door, he anchors it in an earlier prophet's household rather than in anything Ali had done. And Ali's own framing: asked for his greatest distinction, he selects the one he had no hand in producing, which is the claim this book has been making about designation from its first pages, arriving here as a door left open in a wall.

## The first sermons and the brotherhood

The first sermon the Messenger of God delivered was at that first Friday gathering in Medina. As Ibn Hisham narrated it, he praised God and extolled Him as He deserves, and then said:

"As for what follows — O people, send forth provision for yourselves. By God, one of you will surely be struck unconscious with no shepherd and no interpreter and no intercessor between him and his Lord. Then his Lord will say to him: 'Did My Messenger not come to you and convey the message? I gave you wealth and favoured you — what have you put forward for yourself?' He will look to his right and see nothing, then to his left and see nothing, then before him and see nothing but the Fire. Whoever can shield his face from the Fire, even with half a date, let him do so. And whoever cannot find even that, let him do so with a good word, for a good deed is rewarded ten times over — up to seven hundred times over. And peace be upon you, and the mercy and blessings of God."

He then delivered another sermon, in which he said:

"In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful. Praise be to God — we praise Him, seek His help, and take refuge in God from the evils of our own souls and the evils of our deeds. Whoever God guides, none can lead astray, and whoever He leaves to stray, none can guide. I testify that there is no god but God alone, with no partner. The finest of speech is the Book of God, blessed and exalted is He. Whoever God has adorned with it in his heart, and who has entered Islam after disbelief, choosing it over all other discourse, has found the best of guidance. Love what God loves, and love God with all your hearts. Do not grow weary of the word of God and His remembrance, and do not let your hearts harden against Him, for God selects from all things the best of deeds, the best of servants, and the most righteous of speech. Observe what God has made lawful and forbidden for people; worship God and associate nothing with Him; fear God as He truly deserves to be feared; be sincere to God in what you profess with your mouths; love one another in the spirit of God. God is angered when His covenant is broken. And peace be upon you."

Set those two beside the situation that produced them. He has been in the town a matter of days, arriving from a city that spent a night trying to kill him, facing a population made of hosts, refugees, recent converts and old enemies of each other. And in the first address he gives there is no mention of Mecca, no mention of the journey, no mention of who he is, no programme and no law.

What there is instead is one scene, in the second person, of a man alone at the moment of dying with no interpreter and no intercessor, being asked what he did with what he was given — and then the smallest imaginable answer to it. Half a date. If not that, a good word. The first practical instruction issued in Medina is priced so low that nobody in the audience can claim to be exempt.

The second sermon is about speech and about hardening: the best of speech is the Book, hearts can grow weary of remembrance, and the commands come in a rush at the end. It closes with a warning that has nothing to do with Mecca either — God is angered when His covenant is broken. In a town about to be built entirely out of covenants, that is the sentence to hold.

Then he set about the covenants themselves. He concluded a pact with the Jews, made peace between the Aws and the Khazraj, and abolished the blood feuds of the pre-Islamic era.

One sentence, three settlements, and the third is the mechanism the council at Dar al-Nadwa had built its entire plot around. The vendetta accounting that made the murder plan thinkable — and that, running the other way, made it unthinkable to touch the man they found in the bed — is cancelled by decree in his first weeks of governing anything.

He then established bonds of brotherhood among his companions. He made Hamzah and Zayd ibn Haritha brothers. Ja'far ibn Abi Talib, who was at that time in the land of Abyssinia, was paired with Mu'adh ibn Jabal. He made Abu Bakr and 'Umar brothers, and in the same way established brotherhood between all of his companions, pairing each man with another.

Then he took the hand of Ali ibn Abi Talib and said: this is my brother, my guardian and the fulfilment of my pledge, and my successor after me.

The pairing exercise manufactures kinship, on the spot, between men who have no blood between them and in several cases a history of killing each other. Absence is no exemption — Ja'far, in Abyssinia and years from returning, is assigned a brother anyway, because the point is the register, not the acquaintance. And note the shape of the last pairing. Every other pair is two companions; the last one is the Prophet himself, and brother is only the first of four words. The other three — guardian, the fulfilment of my pledge, successor after me — describe an office rather than a friendship.

When the Messenger of God had settled, and the religion had spread and taken root in the city of his dwelling, a man of the Banu 'Adi ibn al-Najjar, Abu Fais Sarmah ibn Abi Anas, composed verses on the honour God had bestowed on his people through Islam. The lines have come down damaged, and are carried here only at the level of their surviving sense: that he had found few supporters among Quraysh, and none to shelter him or heed his call though he declared himself openly at the pilgrim seasons; that he arrived content and was received gladly, and God made His religion manifest when he came; that he tells them what Moses said when he answered the caller and what Noah said to his people; and that they gave him their wealth and their own selves in hardship, knowing that there is no god but God.

The account of Salman al-Farsi belongs here too. He reached the Prophet and embraced Islam. He was from Persia. He had come to love the Christian religion and had abandoned his father's faith, for his father followed the religion of the Magi. He travelled with caravans to the Levant and served righteous monks one after another. The last of them, as he was dying, counselled him and said: you will find that a prophet sent by God — the best of prophets — whose time has now come is near at hand; he accepts gifts but does not accept charity, and he bears the seal of prophethood between his shoulders.

Salman had been sold by compulsion into the ownership of a Jewish man. He came to the Prophet and put him to the test. He brought him charity, and the Prophet did not eat from it but distributed it among his companions. Then Salman brought him a gift, and the Prophet ate from it and shared it with them. Then Salman looked at the seal of prophethood between his shoulders, recognized the signs, and submitted.

That is a conversion conducted as an experiment, with the criteria fixed in advance by a third party who is dead before the test is run — and two of the three are about money, the same food offered under two different names, with the whole question resting on which one he will eat.

Salman went on to become one of the closest companions to the Prophet, who would greet him warmly, draw him near, and speak highly of him. He said of him: Salman is of us, the people of the house. He would pray for him and send him greetings.

And Salman was among the Shi'a of Ali. Ali had followers during the lifetime of the Prophet who were known for their belief in his guardianship and were celebrated for it. Among them were Salman al-Farsi, 'Ammar, Abu Dhar, al-Miqdad, 'Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud, 'Abd Allah ibn 'Abbas and others. The Messenger of God would remind people of their virtue and call them the Shi'a of Ali. This, the author says, is a well-known matter, familiar to both the learned and the general public, and the narrations that have come down concerning it are too numerous to be contained within this book.

Then the other thing that grew in the new town. Many Jews converted to Islam, as did the remaining members of the Aws and the Khazraj — some of them sincerely, while others concealed hypocrisy when they saw that Islam had grown strong and was taking hold. Among the hypocrites were 'Abd Allah ibn Ubayy ibn Salul and others, many in number and well known by name. They would say one thing to the believers and another among themselves: "And when they meet those who believe they say, 'We believe'; but when they are alone with their devils they say, 'We are with you; we were only mocking'" (chapter 2, verse 14). The Prophet accepted from them what they outwardly displayed of Islam and left them to themselves, because verses had already been revealed exposing their hypocrisy and making clear that even if they said they believed with their mouths, their hearts had not believed (chapter 5, verse 41).

That is a policy, and an unexpected one: told in revelation exactly who is lying, he governs on the statement rather than the knowledge. The town runs on declared allegiance.

Which leaves the practical question of how anyone else was to tell. The Ansar's answer is preserved: we used to recognize the hypocrites at the time of the Messenger of God by their hatred of Ali ibn Abi Talib. And it is confirmed, the author says, by the Prophet's own words to Ali: no one hates you, O Ali, except a hypocrite — or, in some narrations, except one of illegitimate birth, or except one whose mother conceived him while she was menstruating, or except one who was conceived in transgression.

Meanwhile, in Mecca, news reached the polytheists of Quraysh of the unity between the Muhajirun and the Ansar and of the growing strength of the community. When the religion became manifest, it filled them with revulsion and overwhelming grief. They yearned for the death of the Messenger of God, and found themselves powerless to bring it about.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry forward.

The first is that the plot in Mecca was a legal instrument rather than a military one, and it failed on its own terms twice over. It was designed so that no clan could be charged for the killing, and it was abandoned in the doorway because the man they actually found could not be killed without a charge falling on someone. Between those two moments a young man lay down in a marked bed, was left with instructions to hand back the property of the men holding the swords, and disarmed the room when it came.

The second is that the author does not let one of these events pass without extracting the claim about rank inside it. The cave gets a full argument, run off a pronoun and off the ordinary meaning of the word companion, conceding every fact while denying the conclusion. The open door in the mosque wall is answered by the Prophet with a denial that he chose it. And Ali, asked to name his greatest distinction, picks the one he had no part in making.

The third is that a town was assembled out of nothing in a matter of weeks: ground bought from two orphans, a mosque built by hand, two sermons that mention neither Mecca nor the migration and price the first act of obedience at half a date, a pact with the Jews, peace between two warring clans, the abolition of the blood feud, and a register of manufactured brothers ending in a designation.

Mecca now had a rival it could not reach and could not ignore. What follows is the first attempt on it.
