## Where this episode picks up

What does an army do when it is told, at midday, with its armour already off and its dust barely settled, that the war it has just finished is not finished?

The previous episode ended with a coalition of ten thousand dissolving without a battle and riding home in a windstorm. This one begins a few hours later, in Medina, when the weapons have been stacked and the men have gone to rest — and a visitor arrives to say that the angels have not stood down, and that the campaign has one more address to go: the fortress of the tribe that tore up its treaty while the ditch was being held.

Three movements. First the march itself, and the twenty-five nights of siege that follow it — including the strangest conversation in this volume, in which the leader of the besieged tells his own people that he knows the man outside is a prophet described in their scripture, and is refused three times on three separate principles. Then the surrender, and the judgment: not delivered by the Prophet, but by an arbiter the besieged chose for themselves, carried into the assembly on a donkey because he is dying of an arrow wound taken at the trench. Then the following six months, and two expeditions in which almost nothing goes as planned.

Listen for who holds the pen at each stage. This is an episode about jurisdiction — about who is asked, who agrees to be bound, and what happens to a ruling once a whole community has sworn in advance to accept whatever it turns out to be.

## The command at midday

When the Messenger of God returned from the trench to the city, with the Muslims alongside him, and had set down his arms, Gabriel came to him at midday.

Gabriel said: have you laid down your weapons, O Muhammad?

He said: yes.

Gabriel replied: the angels have not yet laid down their weapons. You have come back only from the direction of the people who sought you out. God commands you to march against the Banu Qurayza — I am heading toward them now, and I will shake their strongholds.

Notice how that message is put. It is not a report of an unfinished task and it is not an accusation of negligence. It is a comparison between two armies. One of them has stacked its weapons because the enemy in front of it has gone home; the other has not, because it is already on the road to somewhere else. And the reason given for the difference is a matter of direction: you have come back only from the direction of the people who sought you out. The siege ended when the besiegers left. The campaign, on this account, did not.

The Messenger of God then ordered his muezzin to call out to the people: hear and obey — no one shall pray the afternoon prayer except at Banu Qurayza.

That order is worth reading as an instruction and not as a slogan. It fixes a place for the next obligatory prayer, and by fixing the place it fixes the marching order, the pace and the deadline in a single sentence. Nobody is told the objective, the strength of the enemy or the plan. They are told where they will be standing in a few hours when the sun is halfway down, and everything else follows from that.

The Messenger of God sent Ali ibn Abi Talib ahead to the Banu Qurayza. Ali carried the standard forward and the people followed.

As he drew near their fortresses, he heard them speaking about the Messenger of God in vile terms. When the Messenger of God himself approached, Ali went out to meet him and urged him to turn back, wishing to spare him from hearing what they had said.

But the Messenger of God pressed on, and when he reached them he said: O brothers of apes — shall God not shame you and bring His wrath down upon you?

The whole exchange takes four lines and it has a shape that is easy to miss. The standard-bearer's instinct is protective and domestic: he wants his commander not to hear the insult. It is refused. The man goes forward specifically to hear it, and answers it himself, in the same register in which it was offered. There is no council of war in this account and no order given between the two moments. The first thing that happens at the walls of the Banu Qurayza is an argument.

The phrase he used to them is one the Quran itself applies to a community that was punished for transgressing the Sabbath — and it will matter in a few paragraphs, when the Sabbath is the third of the three things these people refuse to set aside. The insult and the refusal are drawn from the same page of their history.

The Messenger of God encamped near one of their own wells. The account pauses to note that the well was their own property and was known by a particular name, and by another name besides — the kind of detail a record keeps when the people who first told it could still walk to the spot.

He besieged them for twenty-five nights, and God cast terror into their hearts.

Twenty-five nights is longer than the siege that had just been lifted from Medina. The city that had spent three weeks behind a ditch waiting for ten thousand men to leave now spends twenty-five nights outside a wall of its own, and the account gives the two figures without drawing the comparison. What it does say is what decided it, and it is not the wall: God cast terror into their hearts.

Huyayy ibn Akhtab had gone into their fortress alongside them after Quraysh and Ghatafan turned away — fulfilling to Ka'b ibn Asad exactly what he had promised him. That promise is the phrase you heard in the last episode carried into this fortress in place of an argument, and this is the man delivering on it: not with troops, not with terms, but by coming inside and sharing the outcome.

The Messenger of God did not relent until their situation had been decided.

And then Ka'b ibn Asad said to them: O community of Jews, you see the situation you are in. I put three options before you — take whichever you will.

They said: and what are they?

He said: follow this man and his command. For by God, you know that he is a prophet sent to you — he is the one you find described in your scripture — and so protect your lives, your wealth, your children and your women.

They said: we will never do that. We will never abandon the ruling of the Torah.

Sit with that first exchange, because nothing else in this episode is as strange. The proposal is made by the holder of their compact, the man who negotiated with the Prophet in the first place and then broke with him. He does not present the case as a calculation of odds. He presents it as a fact they already possess: you know that he is a prophet sent to you, and he is described in your own book. And the refusal does not contradict him. Nobody says he is not a prophet. Nobody says the description is not there. The answer is that they will not abandon the ruling of the Torah — which is to say, the refusal is offered as an act of fidelity to scripture, in reply to a claim that scripture itself names the man outside.

He said: then let us kill our own sons and our women, and go out to Muhammad and his companions with our swords, leaving nothing behind us — either the victory is ours or it is his. If we prevail, we can find women and children again.

They said: shall we kill these poor souls? What is life worth after them?

He said: if you refuse that too, then tonight is the Sabbath night, and perhaps Muhammad and his companions have relaxed their guard. Let us descend on them and perhaps catch them off guard.

They said: shall we violate our Sabbath and bring upon ourselves what came upon those before us, who transgressed on the Sabbath?

He said: not one man among you has had a night's firm resolve since the day his mother bore him.

The three proposals also form a descending scale, and their leader put them in that order deliberately. The first asks them to change what they believe. The second asks them to give up everything they love and die attacking. The third asks only that they break one day's observance to attempt a night raid — the smallest concession of the three, and the one they refuse most indignantly. A man who has watched his own council refuse the greatest thing, then the middle thing, then the least, is entitled to the sentence he ends on.

Three refusals, and this is what makes the passage a teaching rather than a scene. Not one of them is a refusal on grounds of impossibility. Each is a refusal on a principle, and the principles are drawn from three different registers. The first is loyalty to law. The second is love of their own children. The third is observance of the Sabbath. Any of the three, taken alone, would be creditable. Taken together, at the twenty-fifth night of a siege, they are what their own leader calls them: a settled inability to resolve on anything at all. The account gives him the last word and does not soften it.

## The arbiter carried on a donkey

When the siege had worn them down, and God had cast terror into their hearts, they asked to be placed under the judgment of the Messenger of God.

They said: O Messenger of God, make us loyal allies as you did with the Khazraj — as you did with them yesterday, and as I understand it. They meant the Banu Qaynuqa', in whose case 'Abd Allah ibn Ubayy ibn Salul had interceded with the Messenger of God.

That is the precedent they reach for, and it is precise. They are not asking for mercy in the abstract. They are naming a case from a few years earlier in which another Jewish tribe of Medina, condemned, was spared because a chief of the Khazraj stood up and asked for them. What they want is not a different verdict; it is a different intercessor.

The Messenger of God said: O community of the Aws — do you agree that a man from among you should rule over them?

They said yes.

He said: then let it be Sa'd ibn Mu'adh.

His people came to him and carried him on a donkey, over which they had put a leather cushion — for he was a large and handsome man, and he was suffering from the arrow wound he had taken at the trench. They brought Sa'd to the Messenger of God and to the Muslims, and the Messenger of God said: rise for your master.

They rose for him, and they said to him: O Abu 'Amr, your clients have placed their fate in your hands.

Look at how the jurisdiction was constructed before a word of the ruling was spoken. The condemned asked for the intercession of an ally. The answer was to convert intercession into arbitration: a man of the Aws, the tribe that had been the Banu Qurayza's confederates, will decide the case. The tribe that would have pleaded for them is instead made to judge them. And then the man chosen arrives dying, on a borrowed donkey, from the wound taken in the siege their treachery helped bring on.

Sa'd said: O community of the Ansar — do you covenant by God and His pledge to accept my judgment?

They said: yes.

He said: O Messenger of God, I decree that the men be killed, the women and the children taken captive, and the property divided.

The Messenger of God said to Sa'd: you have judged among them by the judgment of God from above seven heavens.

That endorsement does something the ruling by itself could not. Sa'd had derived his authority from two human sources — the Prophet's appointment and the Ansar's sworn undertaking to abide by whatever he said — and both of those could in principle be argued about afterwards by the people who gave them. The sentence spoken over the verdict removes it from that ground entirely. It is not called a sound judgment, or a just one, or the right call in the circumstances. It is called the judgment of God, located above seven heavens, and delivered by a dying man on a donkey who had been summoned to the assembly an hour earlier.

There is a report about why they accepted his arbitration at all. Ibn Hisham said that some of those he trusts among the people of knowledge told him: the Banu Qurayza did not surrender until Ali ibn Abi Talib approached their fortresses carrying the standard and called out — O battalion of faith, advance. And he said: by God, I will not turn back until God opens this for His Messenger, or until I taste what my uncle Hamza tasted, or until their fortress is opened. When they saw this they said: O Muhammad, we surrender to the ruling of Sa'd ibn Mu'adh.

The oath in that sentence is the same oath the last episode watched a man keep at the trench, and its terms are worth noticing: victory or the death of Hamza, with no third outcome offered and no time limit set. The surrender is timed to it.

When that had come to pass and Sa'd had delivered his ruling, the Messenger of God had them confined in Medina, in the house of a woman of the Banu al-Najjar.

Then he went out to the market of Medina and had trenches dug in it. The word is the same one this history has used for three weeks about the defence of the city, and the account uses it without remark: the campaign that began with a ditch dug to keep an army out ends with ditches dug in a marketplace. The men were brought out and their necks were struck over those trenches. Among those brought out were the enemy of God, Huyayy ibn Akhtab, and Ka'b ibn Asad, the chieftain of the people. They numbered six hundred or seven hundred; it was also said they were between eight hundred and nine hundred.

The record does not resolve that count and does not editorialise around it. It gives two ranges from two transmissions and leaves both standing, exactly as it has left every other doubled figure in this history standing.

When Huyayy ibn Akhtab was brought forward he was wearing a patterned garment that had been torn all around its edges, each tear about a hand's breadth, so that it could not be taken as spoils. His hands were bound to his neck with a rope.

When he looked at the Messenger of God he said: by God, I do not blame myself for having opposed you — but whoever forsakes God shall himself be forsaken.

Then he turned to the people and said: O people, the command of God is a decree and a fate inscribed for the Children of Israel. Then he sat down, and his neck was struck.

Two acts and two sentences, and they are all of a piece. A man cuts his own coat to pieces so that no one will profit by it, refuses to say he was wrong, and describes what is happening to him as something written rather than something lost. Jabal ibn Jawwal al-Tha'labi made a poem of it, of which this survives: whoever forsakes him, may God forsake him; you never blamed a son who gave himself; and worry seeks shame in every anxious one, to strive until the soul finds its excuse.

Then the arbiter died.

It is related that when the Messenger of God had finished the campaign against the Banu Qurayza, the wound of Sa'd ibn Mu'adh burst open, and he died a martyr.

The Messenger of God said: the gates of heaven were opened for Sa'd, and the Throne trembled for him.

When Sa'd was buried, the Messenger of God praised him. The people then noticed that he was walking and moving more briskly than before, and they said: O Messenger of God, why did you walk like that?

He said: the angels had crowded around him so thickly that I feared being jostled, until God granted him relief.

And one of the Ansar said in his honour: did the Throne of God tremble at the death of a mortal? Such is what we heard, by the Lord of Sa'd ibn Abi 'Amr.

Take the sequence whole, because the book has arranged it and not merely reported it. The same man is sent to verify whether the covenant had been broken; is wounded during the siege that breach helped to bring on; is chosen by the condemned themselves as arbiter; takes an oath from his own tribe binding them to whatever he says; rules; is told his ruling came from above seven heavens; and dies of that wound within days, with the Throne trembling and the angels so thick around the grave that the Prophet had to watch his step. The judgment and the judge's death are one paragraph in this history, not two.

Then the Messenger of God sent Sa'd ibn Zayd, of the brothers of the Banu 'Abd al-Ashhal, with some of the captives of the Banu Qurayza, to purchase horses and weapons. And for himself the Prophet chose, from among the captive women, Rayhana bint 'Amr ibn Hanafa, one of the women of the Banu Qurayza.

That first sentence is how the third clause of the ruling was actually carried out. Sa'd's judgment had said the property was to be divided; what the account records is captives converted into cavalry and arms. A community that had just held a ditch for three weeks because it could not meet an enemy in the open converts the outcome of the siege directly into the thing it had been short of. The history states it in one line and does not soften it, exactly as it stated the two competing counts of the dead without choosing between them.

The poetry that follows is severely damaged in transmission, and much of it survives only as isolated lines whose connections are gone. What it is doing, however, is legible.

Ka'b ibn Malik of the Banu Salamah composed on the day of the trench and the killing of the Banu Qurayza together: if you witnessed our patience in what we endured, then trust us; we all plunged into it together while they watched us with enmity; with blows delivered in haste; with them we healed every sore, and in our right hands were gleaming blades; at the gate of the two trenches, like lions against the enemies; our knights, when they rode out early, were loyal servants of our deliverer; and the people of Mecca know how they marched.

Sa'd ibn Mu'adh's own verses survive in the same broken condition: a questioner asks what we have found — our patience sees no injustice before God; and the Prophet was a true leader for us; hasten them, if they rise against us, and you will see us bring ruin upon them; and God is the guardian of the believers, God is the best of those who judge; either you kill Sa'd the fierce, and he enters paradise with good deeds, or you grant our good a place to stand.

Hassan ibn Thabit answered in the same vein, and his surviving lines run through the confederates' march and their scattering: even when they came to Medina and made it tremble; they turned back on their heels in anger, yet were met with hands capable of more; the winds of a storm that scattered their gathering, and the soldiers of your Lord are the masters; God suffices for the believers as their protector — after they had despaired, He gathered them.

And on the other side, two men wept for the champion killed at the crossing. Musafi' ibn 'Abd Manaf ibn Wahab ibn Hudhafah ibn Jumah lamented 'Amr ibn 'Abd Wad and his killing at the hands of Ali ibn Abi Talib: 'Amr ibn 'Abd was the foremost knight; the contest was over, and the knight was taken; he sought to fight with a sword that troubled him not; the noble gave himself freely, once for all; for a slave's son among them could not have done it; at dawn I found no dilemma like it — so come to me, for I will not find his equal. Hubayrah ibn Abi Wahb wept for the same man in verses now almost entirely lost.

Hassan ibn Thabit also spoke of Ka'b ibn al-Ashraf, whose killing this history has already recounted in its place, in reference to the killing of Ibn Abi al-Haqiq: O son of al-Haqiq, and you son of al-Ashraf — I found a band bold as lions in their den; they pressed upon you, they struck you, even in the heart of your own land; contemptuous of every trifling thing, clairvoyants, upholding the religion of their prophet.

The conquest of the Banu Qurayza took place in Dhu al-Qa'dah and the opening days of Dhu al-Hijjah, and the Messenger of God returned to Medina in Dhu al-Hijjah.

## Banu Lahyan and Dhu Qarad

Then came six months in which two expeditions went out, and neither of them found what it was looking for.

The Messenger of God remained in Medina through Dhu al-Hijjah, Muharram, Safar and the two months of Rabi'. He then launched his campaign against the Banu Lahyan in Jumada al-Ula, six months after the conquest of the Banu Qurayza. He went to avenge the companions of Khubayb ibn 'Adi and those who had died with him — the teaching party this history followed to its end two episodes ago, sold on the road and killed at the other end of the sale.

He let it be known that he intended to head toward the Levant, in order to take the people by surprise. He moved swiftly until he came down upon Gharran, which was the territory of the Banu Lahyan.

He found them forewarned and sheltering in the heights of the mountains.

When the Messenger of God descended and found that he had missed the opportunity he had hoped for, he said: had I descended upon 'Usfan, the people of Mecca would have seen it.

So he marched out with two hundred of his companions until he reached 'Usfan, then sent two horsemen ahead until they reached the level grazing ground, and then returned.

Read what that second march is for. The first objective failed: the enemy had warning and went up into the rocks, and there was nothing to do. So a column of two hundred men walks to a place within sight of Mecca, sends two riders a little further, and turns around. Nothing is taken and nobody is fought. The purpose is to be seen — to put it on the record in Mecca that the road is open and the force can use it. Having lost the surprise he wanted, he spends the march buying a different result.

Jabir ibn 'Abd Allah said: I heard the Messenger of God, when he returned, say — we are returning, repentant, if God wills; our Lord is praiseworthy. I seek refuge in God from the hardships of travel, from distressing sights along the way, and from finding family and wealth in a bad state.

That supplication is the only thing the expedition brought home, and it is worth hearing for what it asks protection from. Not from enemies and not from defeat. From the road itself, from what a man may have to look at while he is on it, and from what he may find waiting when he gets back.

Ka'b ibn Malik composed on the campaign, and what survives runs: they found among them a believer of steadfast nerve, had the sons of Lahyan been made to answer; before them a host like a rushing galaxy; they quickly found the squadron tireless and wondrous; the strongholds of the Hijaz yield not in agreement — but they were righteous, and they followed.

Then came the day of Dhu Qarad, and this time the enemy came to them.

'Uyaynah ibn Husn ibn Hudhayfah ibn Badr al-Fazari raided with cavalry from Ghatafan and struck the herds of the Messenger of God in the forest. Among those present were a man of the Banu Ghifar and a woman of his household. They killed the man, took the woman captive, and drove off the camels.

The first Muslim to give chase was Salamah ibn 'Amr ibn al-Akwa'. He was heading toward the forest, carrying his bow and his quiver. He shouted at the raiders, drove them into confusion against one another, and shot at them, saying as he loosed his arrows: take that — I am the son of al-Akwa'; today is a day of slaughter for the young.

That is one man with a bow against a mounted raiding party, and what he does first is shout. The alarm matters more than the arrows, and the rest of this account is the machinery that shout sets going.

The Messenger of God heard the cry of Ibn al-Akwa' and commanded that the call be made throughout the city. The horsemen rode out to him, and the first of the knights to reach him was al-Miqdad ibn al-Aswad. The Messenger of God ordered al-Miqdad to lead eight horsemen in pursuit of the raiders, and he placed Sa'd ibn Zayd in command of them. The rest of the people set out after them, together with Ali ibn Abi Talib and the other Muslims.

The first Muslim to overtake the raiders was Muhriz ibn Nadhlah of the Banu Asad ibn Khuzaymah, who was also called al-Akhram. He confronted them and called out: stand, O sons of al-Lakiya, until those behind you of the Muhajirun and the Ansar can reach you. He was then overwhelmed, and a man of Ghatafan killed him. The raiders scattered but he could not be saved, and the people returned to Medina. It was said that Waqqas ibn Mujazz al-Madlahi was killed alongside him.

Then Abu Qatadah al-Harith ibn Rabi'i, of the Banu Salamah, caught up with Habib ibn 'Uyaynah ibn Husn al-Fazari — the son of the man who had led the raid — killed him, beheaded him, and rejoined the people.

And here the account produces one of its small, exact human moments. When the Muslims came up with the Messenger of God, they found Habib al-Fazari smeared with the blood of Abu Qatadah's camel, and they assumed that Abu Qatadah had been killed.

The Messenger of God said: it is not Abu Qatadah. He is the slain enemy of Abu Qatadah, covered in the camel's blood so that it might be known whose he is.

A dead man has been marked with the blood of the mount of the man who killed him, as one marks property. The company reads the blood as a casualty and mourns the wrong person; the sign is corrected in a sentence. The whole exchange is about a convention of ownership being misread as a report of death.

'Ukasha ibn Muhsin of the Banu Asad ibn Khuzaymah caught up with 'Ubara and his son 'Amr ibn 'Ubara of Ghatafan, pinned them both with a single spear, killed them both, and recovered some of the Muslims' camels.

The Messenger of God marched on until he came down from the heights of Dhu Qarad. The people urged him: O Messenger of God, if only we could strike the people.

He replied: by now they are already in Ghatafan.

That is a refusal, and it is given as an estimate of distance rather than as a decision about risk. The men want to press the pursuit; the answer is that there is nothing left within reach to press it against. The raiders had a night's start, mounted, on their own ground, and the eight riders sent after them had already recovered what could be recovered. Two men of the Muslims were dead, four of the raiders were dead, and some of the camels were back. The account keeps the arithmetic and does not call it a victory.

He distributed the recovered camels among his companions, allotting a share to every hundred men. Then he and those with him departed in a caravan until they reached the outskirts of Medina.

And then the last thing in this episode, which is a woman with a camel and a vow.

The wife of the man of Ghifar, whom Ghatafan had taken along with the camels, had seized one of the camels belonging to the Messenger of God and ridden it back to him. She told him what had happened, and she said: O Messenger of God, I vowed to God that if He brought me safely through on it, I would slaughter it.

The Messenger of God smiled, and then he said: that is a poor reward. God carried you to safety on it — and then you slaughter it. A vow made in disobedience to God is not binding, and you have no right over what is not yours. It is a camel of mine, so return home in the blessing of God.

That answer carries two separate rulings and a joke, and the joke comes first. The absurdity is named before the law is: the animal that saved her is to be killed as thanks for saving her. Then the law, in two clauses. A vow that commits a person to an act of disobedience creates no obligation — the words do not bind merely because they were addressed to God. And a person cannot vow away what does not belong to them; the camel was never hers to promise. She had escaped captivity, brought back stolen property, and arrived owing a debt she had invented on the road. She is sent home owing nothing.

Hassan ibn Thabit and Ka'b ibn Malik both composed on Dhu Qarad, and their verses are among the most damaged in this volume. What holds together in Hassan's is the praise of the eight riders — the protectors of truth, noble in lineage; we found peace in the aftermath thanks to the knights of al-Miqdad; we were eight, and they were confounded; the days of Dhu Qarad revealed the faces of servants. And in Ka'b's: do you imagine, O sons of the free, that we on horseback are not their match in cavalry? We do not flinch at the trampled shafts of spears; we are a people who do not regard killing as a reproach; we honour the guest and ward off every oppression; they defend their rights and their children.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry forward.

The first is that the judgment on the Banu Qurayza was built as a jurisdiction before it was delivered as a sentence. The condemned asked for an intercessor and were given an arbiter instead. The arbiter was drawn from the very tribe that had been their confederates and would have pleaded for them. He took a binding oath from his own people before he opened his mouth, so that the ruling could not afterwards be disowned by the men most likely to want it softened. And he ruled while dying of a wound taken in the siege the broken covenant had helped bring on. Every step of that is arranged, and the history reports the arrangement as carefully as it reports the verdict.

The second is that this record keeps the losing side's own reasoning intact, including where it is most damaging to them and where it is most creditable. Ka'b ibn Asad tells his people, on the twenty-fifth night, that he knows the man outside the wall is a prophet described in their own scripture — and they refuse him without disputing it. They refuse his second proposal out of love for their children and his third out of reverence for the Sabbath. Huyayy ibn Akhtab goes to his death refusing to say he was wrong and cutting up his coat so no one can profit by it. None of that is edited out to make the outcome easier to read.

The third is that the six months afterwards produced almost no fighting and one clear ruling. An expedition lost its surprise and turned itself into a demonstration march within sight of Mecca. A raid on the herds was chased down by eight riders, and the man who started the pursuit did it with a shout. And the episode ends not on a battle but on a woman who came home riding a stolen camel she had promised to kill, and was told that a vow to do wrong binds nobody, and that no one can pledge what was never theirs.
