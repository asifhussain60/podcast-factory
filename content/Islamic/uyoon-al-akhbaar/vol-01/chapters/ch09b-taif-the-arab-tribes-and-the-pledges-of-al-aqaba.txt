## Where this episode picks up

What does a man do the week after the two people who made his life survivable are buried? In this history he leaves town to look for a replacement — and comes home from that journey worse off than he set out, with a mob at his back and exactly one adherent, a slave who was told to stay where he was.

That is where this stretch of the history begins, and it is the low point of the whole Meccan period. The previous episode closed on an inventory of losses: a blockade beaten, an uncle in the ground, a wife in the ground three days later, and a town that noticed the protection was gone. What follows is a search for somewhere else to stand — conducted in the open, at the pilgrimage seasons, in front of everyone.

Three movements. First the journey to Ta'if, which fails completely and produces the most famous supplication of the Meccan years, recited here whole. Then the seasons of presenting the call to the Arab tribes — including one delegation that answers with a strategic map of the region and a conditional yes. Then al-Aqaba, where six men from a town four hundred miles north sit down with him for the wrong reason and change everything.

Watch for what actually makes the difference in the end. It is not eloquence — the tribes are met by the most eloquent man available and it produces nothing. It is not power; the strongest tribe he approaches gives the most carefully reasoned refusal in the episode. What works is a town with a problem that only an outsider could solve, and people who came looking for an arbiter before they knew they were looking for a prophet.

## The wall at Ta'if

He traveled to the Thaqif at al-Ta'if to seek their support. When he arrived he sat with them in their gathering place and called them to God, speaking to them about what had been sent down to him, urging them to support Islam and to stand with him against those among his own people who opposed it.

Notice how the ask is put, because it is the same ask he will make of every tribe for the rest of this episode and it has two parts. There is the call to God, which is the message. And there is a request for something concrete: that they stand with him against the people of his own town. He is asking a second power to take a position in a quarrel that until now has been internal to Mecca. That is not a conversion appeal with a political attachment; it is a single proposition in which the two halves cannot be separated, and every group he approaches from here on will weigh both.

They gave him a response he and his family found deeply painful. He despaired of any good from them — and then made a second, much smaller request: that they at least keep his mission secret, so that his own people could not use the rejection to humiliate him further.

They refused even that. Instead they provoked the foolish and the slaves against him, who jeered and shouted at him until crowds gathered around him. He took refuge by the wall of a garden belonging to the sons of Rabi'ah — Shaybah and his brother — having retreated from the mob of Thaqif's fools who had pursued him.

The second request is the one worth pausing on. He did not ask them to reconsider; he asked them to say nothing. That is the request of a man calculating what the news will do when it reaches home, and it tells you the errand was already understood as a gamble — one whose failure could be absorbed if it stayed quiet and could not be absorbed if it did not. In Mecca the argument had been running for years on the question of whether anyone outside the town would take this seriously. A public rejection by the nearest town of any consequence would answer that question for them.

And the refusal was not merely a no. It was a no designed to travel. Notice also that the leaders of Thaqif did not lay a hand on him themselves. They provoked the foolish and the slaves — the two categories of person in any town whose actions nobody is held responsible for — and then let the crowd do what a crowd does. It is deniable, it costs them nothing, and it works.

So the sequence of that day runs: an appeal, a painful answer, a small request for mercy, the refusal of the small request, a mob, and a wall. The author gives the whole of it in a few lines and spends no words on how it felt. What he gives instead is the words the man said when he stopped running, and they turn out to be the most complete account of his own position anywhere in this volume.

Against the wall of a stranger's garden, he said this.

"O God, to You I complain of my weakness, my lack of resourcefulness, and my humiliation before the people. O Most Merciful of the merciful, You are the Lord of the weak, and You are my Lord. To whom do You entrust me? To one far away who will treat me with contempt? Or to an enemy whom You have given power over my affair? If You are not angry with me, I do not care, but Your well-being is far more ample for me. I seek refuge in the light of Your face — by which the darkness is lit and the affairs of this world and the next are set right — lest You bring Your anger down upon me or Your wrath descend upon me. I will continue to seek Your forgiveness until You are pleased. There is no power except in You."

Read it again for what it does not contain. There is no request that Ta'if be punished. There is no request that the mission be made easier, or that the road be smoothed, or that anyone be brought to belief. There is no complaint about the Thaqif at all.

What it complains of is a condition rather than an injury: weakness, lack of resourcefulness, humiliation in front of people. And the question in the middle of it is a real question with two bad answers — to whom do You entrust me, a distant man who will hold me in contempt, or an enemy given power over my affair? Those are the only two options a man in that position has, and he names both.

Then the pivot, and everything in the supplication turns on it: if You are not angry with me, I do not care. The entire disaster is reduced to a single variable, and the variable is not the outcome. Every other consideration — the mob, the refusal, the news going back to Mecca — is set aside as not bearing on the question at all. And then, immediately, the sentence is qualified rather than left standing: but Your well-being is far more ample for me. He does not pretend the indifference is complete. He states the principle and then admits what he would prefer.

The line between those two is the reason this supplication has been repeated for fourteen centuries. It is not the speech of a man who has risen above his situation. It is the speech of a man who is in it, who names his own weakness twice in the first sentence, and who then locates the one thing that would make the situation intolerable and finds it is not present.

And the close is not a plea for rescue. It is a request for forgiveness held open indefinitely — I will continue to seek Your forgiveness until You are pleased — followed by an assertion about where power is located. Read as a whole, the supplication asks for exactly one thing, and it is not deliverance from Ta'if.

What came out of the wall was one man, and he arrived carrying fruit.

When the sons of Rabi'ah saw him resting there and the state he was in, compassion stirred in them. They sent a Christian slave known as Addas, telling him: take a bunch of these grapes, put them on a plate, carry them to that man, and tell him to eat.

Addas did so. He came forward and placed the grapes in the hands of the Messenger of God. He reached out his hand and said, in the name of God, and then ate.

Addas looked at his face and said: by God, these are words the people of this land do not know.

The Messenger of God said: and what land are you from, Addas? And what is your religion?

He said: I am a Christian, a man from the people of Nineveh.

He said: from the village of the righteous man Yunus ibn Matta?

Addas said: and what do you know of Yunus ibn Matta?

He said: he is my brother — he was a prophet, and I am a prophet.

At that Addas fell upon his feet, kissing them, and kissed his head, his hands and his feet.

One of the sons of Rabi'ah said to his brother: he has already corrupted your slave. And when Addas came back they said: woe to you, Addas — why did you kiss that man's head and hands and feet? He said: O my master, there is nothing on this earth better than him. He has told me something that only prophets know. They said: Addas, do not let him draw you away from your religion.

The exchange turns on two things, and neither is an argument. The first is a formula — a man who names God before he eats, which a Christian from the north recognized instantly and the people of that valley did not. Nothing was preached to Addas. He was sent to deliver fruit to a beaten stranger, and what identified the stranger was four words said over food.

The second is a place name. Addas is from Nineveh, a town with exactly one thing in it worth knowing, and the man against the wall knows it, knows whose town it was, and does not treat the knowledge as a credential. He is my brother — he was a prophet, and I am a prophet. In one sentence a Christian slave is told that the righteous man his own town is remembered for and the man resting against this wall belong to the same order of person. Addas takes about as long to accept it as it takes to fall to his knees, and what he says about it afterward is a statement of fact rather than of belief: he has told me something that only prophets know.

And the frame around the conversion is left standing without comment. The men who sent the grapes did it out of pity, from the garden he was sheltering against; their entire reaction to what happened is that a piece of their property has been damaged; and their instruction to Addas is to stay exactly where he is. The journey to Ta'if produced one adherent, who was a slave, and who was told to keep his religion to himself. That is the yield.

## The tribes at the pilgrim seasons

He then went to the Arabs, presenting Islam to them, seeking their support during the pilgrimage seasons and urging people toward their lands.

The account of what that was like comes down through a chain to Ali ibn Abi Talib, who was there. When the Messenger of God was instructed to present himself to the Arab tribes during the seasons, he set out for it and ordered me to accompany him, so I went out with him. Abu Bakr, a man of deep knowledge of lineage, went out with him too.

They came to a gathering and Abu Bakr stepped forward to address it — and what follows is not a religious appeal at all.

Who are these people? They said: from Rabi'ah. Are you from her nobles, or from those who were defeated by her? From her nobles. And who among her nobles are you? The mightiest of the mighty.

Then the audit began. Are you the strength of 'Awf ibn Muhallim, who used to say, is there no generosity in the valley of 'Awf? No. Are you Bistam ibn Qays, the magnanimous one, the pride of the living? No. Are you Jassas ibn Murrah, the defender of honor and the protector of the neighbor? No. Are you the son of Shurayk, the slayer of kings and the one who put them to the test? No. Are you the one who bears the unique crown? No. Are you the uncles of the kings from Kindah? No. Are you the sons-in-law of the kings? No.

Then you are not the eldest wonder, nor the wonder of the youngest.

He has taken a gathering that opened by calling itself the mightiest of the mighty and emptied the claim in front of everyone, item by item, before a word has been said about God. Whatever the intention, it is a demonstration of standing rather than an invitation.

And a man from Banu Shayban of lowly bearing, known as Daghfal, rose to face him and turned the instrument around. The burden we neither know nor carry, he said — our questioner must ask elsewhere. You there: you have questioned us while we put our questions to you. Do not conceal it from us — who is this man?

From Quraysh. A fine people of honor and leadership. And from which branch of Quraysh? From Banu Taym ibn Murrah. I swear by God, you come from the very same threshing floor.

Then Daghfal ran the identical list back at him. Do you know that Qusay ibn Kilab ibn Murrah gathered the tribes of Fihr and was called the Gatherer? No. Are you Hashim, who crumbled bread for his people and fed the pilgrims? No. Are you Shaybah al-Hamd, 'Abd al-Muttalib, whose face shone like a moon in the deep darkness? No. Who are you then, from among the overflowing people? Are you from the people of the council? From the people of al-Rifadah? From the people of the veil? From the people of the watering? No, and no, and no, and no.

Every honor in that list was established in the earlier episodes of this history — the gathering of Mecca, the feeding of the pilgrims, the offices of the sanctuary — so there is no need to teach them again. What matters is who is being described. Daghfal has just recited, item by item, the ancestry and the sacred offices of the man standing silently at the back of that delegation, in order to prove that the man doing the talking holds none of them. He is unknowingly reading out the credentials of the person he has not been introduced to.

At that Abu Bakr pulled the reins of his camel and turned back toward the Messenger of God. The man called after him — something to the effect that the flood had been diverted and pushed aside — and said: by God, if I had stood my ground I would have told you that you are one of the fools of Quraysh.

Ali said: when the Messenger of God heard this, he smiled. I said to Abu Bakr: I have found that you fell upon a fool among the Arabs. He said: yes, O Abu al-Hasan — for every affliction there is a greater affliction, and the affliction of wit is entrusted to logic.

Then they rode on to a second gathering, with composure and dignity, and everything about the encounter changes. Abu Bakr went forward to greet them and they returned his greeting. Who are these people? From Shayban ibn Rabi'ah. And Abu Bakr turned to the Messenger of God and said: by my father and my mother, there is no greater glory to be found among their people.

Among them were Mafruq ibn 'Amr, Hani' ibn Qabisah, al-Muthanna ibn Haritha and al-Nu'man ibn Shurayk. Mafruq was the most commanding of them in speech and the most eloquent of tongue; he had two locks of hair that fell onto his chest, and the ears of everyone in that gathering were turned toward Abu Bakr.

The questions this time are about capability, and the answers are the answers of professionals. How many are you? We number in the thousands, and a thousand are not overwhelmed by a small force. How strong is your defense? We spare no effort, and every people has its mettle. How do you fare in war against your enemy? We are the most fierce in the press of battle; when we are stirred to anger we drive our horses over our children, preferring the weapon above all else. Victory comes from God — sometimes for us and sometimes against us.

And then Mafruq turned the conversation himself: yet perhaps you are the brother of Quraysh? Abu Bakr said: if the Messenger of God has reached you — and he pointed to him — this is he. Mafruq said: we have heard that this is what he says.

He drew near and asked directly: to what do you call, O brother of Quraysh?

The answer states both halves of the proposition without softening either. I call you to bear witness that there is no god but God and that I, Muhammad, am the Messenger of God; and to protect me and defend me and support me. The Quraysh have set themselves against the command of God, denied His Messenger, and clung to falsehood over truth — except those whom God has protected and guided to His religion. God is the Protector and the Sufficient.

There is a creed in that sentence and a request for armed protection in the same breath, and he does not put a gap between them. He is asking a tribe of thousands to adopt a religion and to go to war with Quraysh over it, and he says so first.

To what else do you call? — the sense being that if these words came from among their own kind, they would have recognized them. So he recited: "Say: Come, I will recite what your Lord has forbidden to you," through to the words, "This He has enjoined upon you, so that you may use reason" (chapter 6, verse 151).

And what else do you call for? And he recited: "Indeed, God commands justice and good conduct and giving to relatives, and forbids immorality and bad conduct and oppression. He admonishes you, that perhaps you will take heed" (chapter 16, verse 90).

Mafruq's verdict was immediate: you call to the noblest of character and the finest of deeds. Yet some people have come out against you and denied you. Then he handed the matter on — this is Hani' ibn Qabisah, our elder and the custodian of our religion.

Hani' gave the most careful refusal in this episode, and it is not a refusal of the message. O brother of Quraysh, we have heard your words. For us to abandon our religion and embrace yours in a single sitting — a sitting in which you have not fully set out your case — and without seeing what the outcome of what you invite us to will bring, would be a slip of judgment, a hasty conclusion, and a misfortune for those behind us, who would hate to have a binding agreement made without their counsel. Let us return and let you return, and we will reflect and consider.

Then he handed on in turn: this is al-Muthanna ibn Haritha, our elder, our chief, and the master of our wars. And al-Muthanna said that as for leaving their religion and embracing his, that would be a fine answer — but that they sat between two streams.

The Messenger of God asked him: what are these two streams?

He said: the waters of the Arabs, and the rivers of Khusraw. As for what lies along the waters of the Arabs, the sin of one's companion there is forgiven and his excuse accepted; but as for what lies along the rivers of Khusraw, the fault rests entirely with its owner, who is neither forgiven nor excused. We went down there under a covenant we made with Khusraw: we cause no disturbance and we take no independent action. Yet what you call us to is what kings despise. If you wish us to shelter you and support you in it, then after the waters of the Arabs, we will give you victory and aid.

That is a conditional yes with a map attached, and its honesty is the point. He is not declining the religion. He is declining to open a war with the Persian empire on a first acquaintance, and he specifies precisely which half of his own territory he is free to risk.

The reply grants it. You have done no wrong in your answer, since you spoke truthfully — only one who embraces the religion of God in all its dimensions truly fulfills it. And then a question that looks past the refusal entirely: but tell me — if God grants you their wealth, their homes and their women, will you praise and glorify Him? He said: that would be for you, O Messenger of God.

Then he recited to them a verse from the chapter of al-Ahzab: we have sent you as a witness, a bearer of glad tidings, and a warner, and a caller to God by His permission, and a shining light.

He rose, took Ali's hand, and said: O Ali — these are the dreams of the pre-Islamic era in which God responds, one bolstering the other. And they will contend against it in this world?

Three delegates, three registers — flattery, procedural caution, and a strategic account of the map — and not one of them disputed the message. In this entire sequence the refusals are never doctrinal. They are about cost, about timing, and about who else has to be consulted first.

The author sets a marker at this point in the account: Abu Talib died three years before the Hijra, and during that time the Prophet began to hear more harm from his people, and to see them holding back from his message, keeping the Arabs away from it, and repelling them from following him. That is the condition under which every one of these approaches was made.

## The two pledges of al-Aqaba

Then, when God willed to strengthen His religion and fulfill His promise, the ordinary routine produced an extraordinary result. He would go out during some of the pilgrimage seasons and present himself to the Arab tribes, reciting to them the signs of his Lord, as he had done every season. And while he was at al-Aqaba he encountered a group from the Khazraj, for whom God intended good.

Who are you? From the Khazraj. The allies of the Jews? Yes. Will you not sit with me so we may speak? Yes.

The second question is the one that shows he knew where he was. He does not ask where they are from; he asks whose allies they are, and having established it, he sits them down.

They sat with him, and he called them to God, presented Islam to them, and recited the Qur'an. There were Jews nearby, and those Jews had told the Ansar that a prophet was about to arise at that time. So when he spoke to these men and called them, they said to one another: this is the very prophet those Jews told us of. Let us not allow the Jews to reach him before us.

The most consequential sentence in this episode is a competitive one, and the author does not tidy it. They accepted partly because they believed someone else was about to, and they said so out loud.

They believed in the Prophet and in God his Lord, then departed — he bidding them farewell — and returned to their land, where they brought others to belief. They were six men of the Khazraj: As'ad ibn Zurara, known as Abu Umama, of Banu al-Najjar; 'Awf ibn al-Harith ibn Rifa'ah, the son of Afra'; Rafi' ibn Malik ibn al-'Ajlan of Banu Zurayq; Qutbah ibn 'Amir ibn Hudaydah and 'Uqbah ibn 'Amir ibn Nabi of Banu Salamah; and Jabir ibn 'Abd Allah ibn Ri'ab.

And what they said when they embraced Islam is the hinge of everything that follows. O Messenger of God, we have left behind a people among whom there is no greater evil and enmity than that which exists between them. May God unite them through you; if God unites them around you, there is no man more honored than you.

They are not offering shelter. They are offering an appointment. These are men from a town at war with itself who have identified the person in front of them as the arbitration their town needs — and that is a far sturdier foundation than sympathy. It is also the answer to why this approach succeeded where Ta'if and every tribe at every season had failed. Yathrib had a problem that a prophet could solve, and it knew it.

When the following year came, during the pilgrimage season, twelve men came to him at al-Aqaba and pledged allegiance to him after believing in God and accepting him. That was the first pledge — likened to the pledge of women — in which fighting was not mentioned.

They were As'ad ibn Zurara, and 'Awf and Mu'adh the sons of al-Harith ibn Rifa'ah, of Banu al-Najjar; Rafi' ibn Malik ibn al-'Ajlan and Dhakwan ibn 'Abd Qays of Banu Zurayq; 'Ubadah ibn al-Samit of Banu 'Awf ibn al-Khazraj, and Abu 'Abd al-Rahman, who is Yazid ibn Tha'labah, of Banu Ghusayna of Bali, as their ally; al-'Abbas ibn 'Ubadah ibn Nadlah of Banu Salim; 'Uqbah ibn 'Amir ibn Nabi and Qutbah ibn 'Amir of Banu Salamah; and from al-Aws, Abu al-Haytham ibn al-Tayhan, whose name is Malik, of Banu 'Abd al-Ashhal, and 'Uwaym ibn Sa'idah of Banu 'Amr ibn 'Awf.

The clause about fighting is the one to hold. At this stage nobody has been asked to defend anybody. It is a pledge of belief and conduct, and its significance is chronological — it marks the last moment before the arrangement acquires a military dimension.

When the delegation departed he sent Mus'ab ibn 'Umayr back with them, instructing him to recite the Qur'an to them, to teach them Islam, and to instruct them in the religion. He became the first man to be called one of the reciters in Medina. He would lead the Ansar in prayer and call them to God and His Messenger; and it was through his hands that Sa'd ibn Mu'adh and Usayd ibn Hudayr came to Islam — and through those two, all of Banu 'Abd al-Ashhal, after which not a single man or woman remained in that household except as a Muslim.

The teacher goes before the leader. A congregation was assembled, a religion taught and two clan chiefs converted in a town the man they had pledged to had never entered. Everything about the migration when it comes is shaped by this: he does not arrive as a fugitive looking for asylum, he arrives at a community that has been running for a year.

Then Mus'ab returned to Mecca, and the Ansar who had become Muslims set out for the pilgrimage season along with the polytheists of their own people. The Messenger of God appointed a meeting with them at al-Aqaba during the middle of the days of Tashreeq. With him were Ali ibn Abi Talib and his uncles Hamza and al-'Abbas, the sons of 'Abd al-Muttalib.

Al-'Abbas spoke first, and he spoke against his own side's interest. O assembly of the Khazraj, Muhammad is from us, as you well know, and we have protected him from our people. He lives in honor among his people and in security in his land. He has, however, resolved to join you and attach himself to you. If you are prepared to be faithful to what you promise and to stand firm against those who oppose him, then so be it. But if you think you may abandon him and surrender him after he has come to you, then leave him now — for he is in a position of strength and protection among his people and his country.

Two things are happening in that speech at once. It is an honest warning: do not take this on unless you can carry it, because abandoning him later is worse than declining him now. And it is also a negotiating posture, insisting that the man is in no difficulty and has alternatives — which the previous episode makes very hard to sustain. The family is protecting the price as well as the person.

The Ansar answered: we have heard what you said. Speak now, O Messenger of God, and take for yourself what you wish.

He spoke, recited the Qur'an and called to God, and then stated his terms in the plainest domestic language available: I take your pledge on the condition that you protect me from what you protect your women and children.

Al-Bara' ibn Ma'rur, one of the masters of the Ansar, took his hand. Yes — by the One who sent you with the truth, we will protect you from all that we protect ourselves from. So take our pledge, O Messenger of God. By God, we are the sons of war and the people of the shield; we have inherited that from one generation to the next.

Then they pledged allegiance to him at al-Aqaba on the terms of Islam: that they would protect him as they protected their women and children, and that they would stand firmly by him.

Set the two pledges side by side. The first mentioned no fighting at all. The second is a defense pact, and the man accepting it enlarges it in the act of accepting: not what you protect your family from, but all that we protect ourselves from — which is everything, including the thing a household is not normally asked to face.

He then appointed twelve chieftains from among them: As'ad ibn Zurara, 'Abdullah ibn Rawaha, Sa'd ibn al-Rabi', Rafi' ibn Malik, al-Bara' ibn Ma'rur, 'Abdullah ibn 'Amr ibn Haram, 'Ubadah ibn al-Samit, Sa'd ibn 'Ubadah, al-Mundhir ibn 'Amr, Usayd ibn Hudayr, Sa'd ibn Khaythama, and Abu al-Haytham ibn al-Tayhan.

And he said to them: you are the guarantors over your people — as the disciples of 'Isa ibn Maryam were guarantors for him — and I am the guarantor for those among my own people who have believed in me. They said: yes, O Messenger of God. Then they set out for the city.

The comparison is exact rather than decorative. Twelve men made answerable for a whole community, on the model of the twelve made answerable for another one. And the structure is reciprocal: having imposed that liability on their side, he assumes the identical liability for his own.

Ka'b ibn Malik put the twelve into verse, remembering them and addressing the polytheists of Quraysh directly. The poem tells them that what has happened is now a fact and cannot be crowded out of the place it has taken; that they should gather whatever force they are able to gather; and that the covenants with them are broken and they should know it. Then it goes through the guarantors one at a time. Ibn Rawaha will not yield it, but will honor it and raise its standing. Ibn al-Rabi' strives to fulfill what he has bound himself to, meeting every pledge he has given. Abu al-Haytham fulfilled his pledge in full. Ibn Hudayr was tested and the wounds did not weigh on him. Sa'd, the brother of 'Amr ibn 'Awf, is a man around whom the night closes in on you. And then al-Bara' and Ibn 'Amr, and al-Mundhir, and al-Qawqali ibn Samit — fulfillment personified.

The poem is a roll call, and that is its function. Twelve names read out in public, attached to a commitment, addressed to the people who would have to come and take them. The pledge was given at night on a hillside; the poem is the part that makes it impossible to deny afterward.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry forward.

The first is that the search for a new protector failed everywhere it was tried on the old terms. Ta'if refused, refused the follow-up request for silence, and set its slaves and its fools on him — and the whole journey yielded a Christian slave who recognized a formula and a place name, and was told by his owners to stay where he was. The supplication that came out of that wall asks for nothing to be fixed. It complains of weakness and humiliation, names the two bad options available, and then sets the entire disaster aside on one condition: if You are not angry with me, I do not care.

The second is that the tribes were approached at the height of Arab eloquence and answered with reasons rather than denials. A gathering of Rabi'ah was audited on its ancestry and answered by auditing back — and unknowingly recited the offices and lineage of the man it had not been introduced to. Banu Shayban listened, praised the message as the noblest of character and the finest of deeds, and then explained, with a map, that the half of their territory bound by covenant to a Persian king was not theirs to risk on a first meeting. Nobody in this episode says the message is false. They say it is expensive.

The third is that the thing which finally worked was not offered as shelter at all. Six men from a town tearing itself apart accepted him partly to get ahead of their Jewish neighbors, and asked him to unite a people among whom the enmity was worse than anywhere. Twelve pledged the next year without any mention of fighting. A teacher was sent ahead and converted two clan chiefs and their entire household before the man himself had ever entered the town. And when the second pledge came, it was a defense agreement measured against what a man protects his own family from — with twelve guarantors named in public, and a poem written to make sure Quraysh knew every name.

He now had a city. The next part of this history is how he got there.
