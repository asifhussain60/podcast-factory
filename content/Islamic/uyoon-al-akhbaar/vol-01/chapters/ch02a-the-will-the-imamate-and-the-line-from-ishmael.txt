## Where this episode opens

What do you make of a Prophet who forbade his followers to let two nights pass without a written will beside the bed — and who then, on the account most of his community came to accept, died having named nobody at all? Either the instruction did not apply to him, which is a strange thing to say about the man who gave it, or it did apply and something is missing from the record. That question is where the author of this history chooses to begin, and he does not treat it as a puzzle. He treats it as the hinge on which everything after it turns.

This is the first episode in which the author speaks for himself. The previous one was scaffolding — who he was, what office he held, how the library he wrote from survived when comparable libraries did not. From here on we are inside his own sentences, and the first thing to notice is where he starts. He does not start with the Prophet's birth. He starts with two arguments, and he places them before the narrative deliberately, because without them the narrative would mean something else entirely.

The first argument is about authority. Guidance, he says, has never been left to a community's own arrangement; God has always paired the one who is sent with the one who carries it forward, and the sealing of prophethood did not repeal that pairing. The second argument is about ancestry. His opponents held that the Prophet's forefathers were idolaters, and the author regards that as a slander — not merely an insult to a family, but an attack on the claim that the line itself was prepared. So he sets out to establish that the fathers of the Prophet were monotheists, and then he walks the line down from Ishmael, generation by generation, until it reaches the tribe of Quraysh and stops at the doorstep of a man named Qusay.

Three things, then, in this episode. Why the leadership of the community was never the community's to choose, and what the Prophet's own instruction about wills has to do with it. Why the author insists that the Prophet's ancestors were not polytheists, and what evidence he brings. And how the descent runs from Abraham's eldest son to the tribe that would receive the revelation — including a detour, borrowed from a philosopher, on why that tribe spoke the finest Arabic of any of them.

Hold on to one image as we go, because the author returns to it constantly and it does a great deal of work for him. He speaks of a mark, or a light, passing along a line — visible in a face, recognised by a cousin, handed forward from father to son across centuries. It is his way of saying that the ancestry was not incidental to what happened later. It was the vessel being prepared.

## The succession that never lapses

Begin with the author's own opening testimony, because his doctrine is already fully present in it before he has made a single argument.

He praises God as Lord of the worlds and Creator of all mankind, who raised the heavens without support and chose His Messenger Muhammad from among the messengers. Then he bears witness that there is no god but God, the One and Only — and immediately, in the same breath, he adds a second clause that is the whole of his position in miniature. God, he says, chose His prophets and His guardians to be guides, and through them delivered His servants from destruction and rebellion. Prophets and guardians. Not prophets alone. The pairing is there in the confession of faith itself, at the point where a different author would have stopped.

He also sets out, in that same testimony, what the guidance was sent into — and the picture matters, because the second half of this episode depends on it. Before the Prophet, he says, humanity had been wandering in misguidance, in association of partners with God, and in ignorance, scattered like broken rays of light: bowing to idols in place of the Most Merciful, bowing to something other than the Creator of mankind and jinn, disobeying God and obeying the devil. Against that background he sets the succession of guidance itself — through the Torah, through the Gospel, and then through the Prophet who was sent last of all. It is a compressed history of revelation in a single sentence, and its shape is worth noting: not one intervention but a series, each arriving into a world that had drifted since the last one.

From there he moves to the Prophet, sent as the Seal of the Messengers, a guide, a bearer of glad tidings, a warner, and a shining lamp. And then to the designation. God, he writes, appointed Ali ibn Abi Talib as his helper, his supporter, his partner, his vizier and his brother — as Aaron was to Moses. That comparison is not decorative. Aaron was not a second prophet competing with Moses; he was the one Moses was given so that the mission would not rest on a single pair of shoulders, and the one who held the people while Moses was on the mountain. The author reaches for it because it names a relationship the Arabic word for guardian also names: not a rival to the one who is sent, but the one who carries what he brought.

He gathers the household into the claim by way of two verses. The first is the verse of purification: "Allah only wishes to remove impurity from you, O People of the House, and to purify you thoroughly" (chapter 33, verse 33). The second describes them feeding others at their own cost: "They feed the poor, the orphan, and the captive out of love for Him" (chapter 76, verse 8). Then the two heirs by name — Abu Muhammad al-Hasan and Abu Abdallah al-Husayn — and then the sentence that opens out into all seven volumes of this history: the pure Imams from the descendants of al-Husayn, whom God has placed in every age, each chosen in his own time for the community of his grandfather, upholding the law of that grandfather without deviation, inheriting the station of the Imamate generation after generation.

Read that slowly, because it contains a claim and also a difficulty, and the author states the difficulty himself rather than hiding it. Among these Imams, he says, are those through whom God has made the light of His clear proof manifest — and some who have concealed themselves as the sun is hidden in the darkness of night, patient under whatever afflicted them. A tradition that believes in an unbroken line of guides has to explain the long stretches when no guide was publicly visible, and the author's answer is that concealment is not absence. The sun behind the night sky has not gone out. The previous episode described what concealment cost this community as a practice of survival; here we meet it as a doctrine about the Imamate itself, applied to the office rather than to the believer. And the line does not end: the word of the Imamate, he says, will be carried to the Last Day.

That is the testimony. Now the argument, and it is tighter than the testimony because he knows he is being disputed.

When God sent His prophets and messengers, the author says, He did not rest His religion on the strength of the sent one alone. In His wisdom, justice, mercy and virtue, He appointed guardians, successors and guides to follow each prophet — figures God Himself selected and chose. That establishes the general rule from the prophets before. Then the specific case: the community of Muhammad was likewise provided with those who would guide them and succeed him after God took him. And here is the pressure point, which the author meets head-on. God had sealed prophethood with this Prophet and made him the last of His messengers. Does that not close the whole arrangement?

No, he answers, because the sealing of prophethood ended the sending of prophets; it did not end the need for guidance. What passed at that moment was the interruption of prophethood, and it passed to successors — successors who themselves know the way, who stand upright in accordance with the purified sacred law and its method, and who are needed precisely because the community still requires guiding after revelation has stopped arriving. The need did not close when the door of prophecy closed. If anything it sharpened, because there would be no further correction from above.

Then the negative half of the argument, which is the part that bites. This, he says, was not something the community could determine on its own. It does not fall to them to make or to settle matters in the religion of God. The Imamate is the foundation of religion, the thing by which religion is upheld, and it is through the Imams of truth that obedience to the Living, the Self-Sustaining, is made valid. Notice the order of the claim: he is not arguing that a community-chosen leader would be a worse administrator. He is arguing that the office is load-bearing in the structure of the religion itself, and that a load-bearing element cannot be supplied by the people standing on the floor it holds up.

He then presses the practical impossibility, and his sentences here are almost impatient. It was not possible for the community to converge on a single person by their own accord. Nor could such a matter be known through reports alone, without a clear trace. And who would determine it, and from where? Four questions with no available answer. If the appointment is not made from above and marked plainly, then the community faces a decision it has no instrument to make: no procedure, no criterion, no authority to arbitrate between candidates, and no way to check the result against anything.

And the author does not leave that as a thought experiment. He points at what happened. Disagreement arose among the Companions after their Prophet, and they fought one another in grief. Whatever else one believes about the first generation, that sentence is not controversial; the wars of the first century are in every chronicle there is. His reading of them is that they are the predicted consequence of a gap — the visible result of an unmade decision, not an unlucky accident of temperament.

He reinforces it with a saying of the Prophet that he reports as soundly transmitted:

"The nation of my brother Moses divided into seventy-one sects, of which one band survived and the rest are in the Fire. The nation of my brother 'Isa divided into seventy-two sects, of which one company survived and the rest are in the Fire. My nation will divide into seventy-three sects, of which one division will survive and the rest are in the Fire."

Set beside the argument, the saying does two things at once. It tells the listener that fragmentation was foreseen rather than surprising. And it makes the identification of the surviving group into the most urgent question a believer can ask — which is exactly the question the author says only a designated line can answer, since a fragmented community cannot adjudicate its own fragmentation.

Now the argument from the will, which is the sharpest thing in this whole opening and the reason it lends its name to the episode. The author reports that the Prophet forbade a certain kind of negligence in the plainest terms: "A Muslim should not sleep two nights without his will written at his bedside." That is an instruction about ordinary property — camels, debts, a house, the care of dependants. Its logic is that no believer should leave the people who depend on him to guess.

Then the inference. A Prophet who legislated that for every Muslim's household would not have left the community itself in a disordered and neglected state. He would not have failed to entrust those who would manage its affairs and raise a guiding banner for the people of religion. And what applies to him applies onward: nor would his guardian and the Imams of truth after him have abandoned the community to wander with nobody raised up to guide creation. The argument is a single move and it is elegant: measure the largest claim by the smallest rule. If two nights without a written will is culpable neglect for a man with a flock of sheep, what would it be for the man entrusted with the religion itself?

So the guardianship, he concludes, extended from the Prophet to the Commander of the Faithful, Ali ibn Abi Talib, and from him to one guardian after another, in accordance with clear designations, as is well known. The phrase "as is well known" is worth hearing for the confidence in it. He is not presenting a discovery. He is presenting something he takes to be public, and then explaining why the public did not act on it: many people, generally and specifically, denied it, and most of the community rejected it out of rebelliousness.

That is a heavy accusation, and he supports it with a verse about a people who knew better than they behaved: "They denied it, though their own souls were convinced of its truth, out of wrongdoing and arrogance" (chapter 27, verse 14). It is the strongest form his claim can take, and also the most difficult: he is not saying the community was mistaken but that it was unwilling. Whether a listener accepts that or not, it is important to see that his history is written from inside it. Every volume that follows is the record of a designated line as understood by someone who believes the denial of it was deliberate.

He notes, almost in passing, that righteous scholars have written so much on this subject that the classifications could not be counted in a month of sustained effort, and that he intends to enter that field anyway, with full determination and an acknowledgement of his own shortcomings. As for the shape of what he means to write — the Prophet's life, then the guardian and what befell him after the Prophet, then the Imams of guidance, those who appeared by God's command and those who remained hidden, those who drew their swords and those who took refuge in piety, those afflicted and patient and those who disputed them — the architecture of the work was set out in the previous episode, and one line of the author's own is enough to stand for the rest: he will not take the path of excessive length, since the best of speech is that which conveys its meaning with precision.

## The purity of the fathers

Then the subject changes, and the change can look abrupt until you see what is driving it.

Having established that guidance descends by designation, the author turns to the family it descended through — and specifically to a charge laid against them. There were those who held that the Prophet's forefathers were idol-worshippers like the rest of pre-Islamic Mecca. The author's response is not defensive. He calls it a grave slander and a calumny, says that whoever repeats it deserves punishment unless he purifies himself through repentance, and treats the charge as an attack on the Prophet rather than on a set of dead ancestors.

Why so severe? Because of what the Prophet himself is reported to have said about that ancestry, in a saying the author claims all of Islam converges on:

"You and I, O Ali ibn Abi Talib, have been carried from the noble loins to the purified wombs."

Two words in that sentence are doing the work: noble, and purified. And the author's method here is to take both words at their Qur'anic value rather than as compliments. On nobility he cites the verse "Surely the most honored of you in the sight of Allah is the most pious" (chapter 49, verse 13), and beside it the Prophet's own definition: "Nobility is piety." So the Prophet's testimony that his fathers were noble is not a statement about their social standing among the Arabs. By the definition the Prophet himself supplied, it is a statement about their piety.

Then purity, and here the argument closes like a trap. The Qur'an says of idolaters: "Indeed, the polytheists are impure" (chapter 9, verse 28). Put the two together. If polytheists are impure, and the Prophet testified that the line he came through was purified and noble, then those through whom he came were not polytheists. The conclusion is forced by the premises, and both premises come from sources the opponent also accepts. That is why the author treats the charge as a slander against the Prophet: to say the fathers were idolaters is to say the Prophet described impure loins as purified.

He then reaches back to Abraham to show that the arrangement was intentional from the start, quoting the prayer Abraham made when he left part of his family in a barren valley: "Our Lord, I have settled some of my offspring in a valley with no vegetation, near Your Sacred House, our Lord, so that they may establish prayer. So make the hearts of people incline toward them and provide for them from fruits" (chapter 14, verse 37). The author draws a specific inference from it. A prayer answered by God means the Sacred House has never been empty of descendants of the Friend of God who deserved that honour. Not one continuous community of the faithful in Mecca — that is not the claim — but never nobody. In every generation, someone at that House who held to what Abraham held.

And then a detail that ought to stop a listener, because it takes the argument somewhere unexpected. What about the generations when the people around the House were visibly worshipping idols? The author's answer is that some among them concealed their faith — as the believer in the household of Pharaoh concealed his, while showing outwardly the praiseworthy qualities and good character that others lacked. The Qur'anic figure he chooses is precise: a man of faith inside the court of the age's great tyrant, whose belief was real and whose disclosure of it would have been fatal. The previous episode described a community centuries later keeping its beliefs unspoken in order to survive. Here the author places the same posture in the deep ancestry of the Prophet — not as a concession, but as the ordinary condition of a believer surrounded by power that would kill him for speaking.

It is worth being exact about what the author says the Prophet's testimony discloses, because he is precise about it himself. The testimony is not a compliment paid to relatives. It discloses, he writes, that they stood upon piety and purity and that they were free of associating partners with God — and that this is what the Prophet made plain. Which is why the slander is a slander in his eyes rather than merely an error: a person who says the fathers were idolaters is not disagreeing with a genealogist, he is contradicting a sentence the Prophet spoke, and repeating it after the correction has been supplied is what earns the author's harshest language. He then closes the argument the way a man closes a case he considers won: this, he says, is amply sufficient as clarification, as proof, and as demonstration of what these men were distinguished with.

He is careful, though, not to overclaim. He grants freely that idolaters were mixed among that lineage — people who worshipped images while taking pride in descent from Abraham. That combination is worth pausing on, because it names a distinction the whole argument rests on. Pride in an ancestor is not the same thing as keeping his religion, and a family can preserve the memory of Abraham while abandoning everything he stood for. The author's claim is not that everyone who could recite the descent was faithful. It is the narrower and harder claim that within that crowd of proud idolaters, the actual thread of Abraham's religion was still being carried by somebody, generation after generation, whether or not it could be seen from outside. He describes the pre-Islamic period without softening it: ignorant people more astray than camels, polytheists among Quraysh and other Arabs, a society where the believer could walk beside the disbeliever and the righteous beside the wicked. He even reaches for the Qur'an's own picture of that mixture, the account of a believing man arguing with his disbelieving companion, who asks him how anyone can disbelieve in the God who created him from dust (chapter 18, verse 37). The point is not that the ancestral line was a pure enclave. The point is that a mixed field is exactly the condition in which a concealed thread can run unbroken without anybody outside it noticing.

So what does the author finally claim for these men? Something more modest than sainthood and more consequential than respectability. It is sufficient as a source of pride for them, he writes, that they were among those through whom the light of pure lineage was transmitted, and that they were singled out for the birth of the Seal of the Messengers from their pure origins. Their significance is custodial. They carried something forward, and the honour is in the carrying.

Which brings him to the division of Abraham's household, and to the choice of which son's line would carry it. God made Moses, Jesus, and the other prophets heirs of Isaac — the author says so plainly, and it is worth noticing that he does not diminish that branch in order to elevate the other. The prophetic tradition of Israel is granted its full weight. And then he singles out Ishmael, whom he identifies as the son designated for sacrifice, for the Prophet Muhammad to descend from his noble line and from the best of his noble sons.

Two lines, then, from one father: one that produced the prophets of the Holy Land, and one that produced the Prophet of the Sacred House. The author's whole ancestral argument depends on holding both without embarrassment, because the honour he claims for the Meccan line is not that Abraham's other descendants failed. It is that this branch was kept for something specific, and that the keeping is visible in the record if you know what to look for.

## From Ishmael to Quraysh

The division of the household was, on this account, arranged by Abraham himself. He commended to his two sons that Isaac and his descendants dwell in the Holy Land, while Ishmael and his family settle at the Sacred House of God, around Zamzam and the Standing Place. That House was Abraham's own direction of prayer, the place he turned toward, the place he summoned people to make pilgrimage to, and the place he made known — and he was, in the author's phrase, the father of its custodians. The custody of the House starts as a family charge, and it starts here.

Of Ishmael the author says something that quietly connects the two halves of this episode. Ishmael, he writes, was the guardian of Abraham and his eldest son. It is the same word he used for Ali ibn Abi Talib's relation to the Prophet. Whatever else the pattern is, it is at least consistent: in this book, the one who is sent is always accompanied by the one who carries it, and the pattern is as old as Abraham.

He supports Ishmael's standing with the Qur'an's own description: "And mention in the Book Ishmael — he was true to his promise, and he was a Messenger, a Prophet. He used to enjoin his family to prayer and zakat, and he was pleasing to his Lord" (chapter 19, verses 54 and 55). Then the transmission begins. Ishmael's sons followed him in uprightness in the religion of their ancestor Abraham, and the mark of distinction passed from one to another until it reached Muhammad. The children of Ishmael were twelve, the best of whom was Qaythar, whose mother was Ra'la, daughter of Madad ibn 'Amr al-Jarhami. And it is said that when Ishmael died he was buried in Mecca beside his mother Hagar — a small detail that does a lot of quiet work, since it plants both the mother and the son permanently at the site the family is charged with.

Then comes the moment the author clearly relishes, because it makes his abstract mark of distinction into something a person could see. Qaythar grew up, and the mark upon him was evident — evident enough that his cousin Jacob, son of Isaac, son of Abraham, recognised it. Jacob saw in his face the light of prophethood, and gave him the glad tidings that the Prophet Muhammad would be among his descendants, telling him that the beginnings of that light were already visible in the contours of his face.

Notice who is doing the recognising. Jacob belongs to the other branch, the one that produced Moses and Jesus. So the earliest witness the author can produce for the Meccan line's destiny is a patriarch of the Israelite line, identifying it in the face of a young cousin and saying so out loud. Whether one reads that as history or as the tradition's way of stating a conviction, it is a well-chosen story: the two branches are not rivals in it, and the testimony comes from the side that had nothing to gain.

From Qaythar the mark continued to pass along the line of Ishmael's sons while other branches spread away from it, and the author moves through the generations at speed. The line ran on until it reached Adnan, who lived in the time of Moses son of Imran — a figure whose story, he says, is well known and whose virtue is great. He mentions in passing a tradition that Shu'ayb, Moses, and al-Khidr met one another in that era, and that from this came a kind of preparation and readiness. He touches on tribal quarrels of Adnan's descendants and then explicitly declines to pursue them: those are not the people he wishes to examine, he says, because his subject is the fathers of the Prophet. It is a useful glimpse of his method — he is working through a genealogical thicket and pruning it to a single thread.

That thread runs on: through Ma'ad, whose sons included Nizar, their chief; and through Nizar's sons, from whom the great northern Arab branches descend, each carrying a nickname whose origin story the tradition preserves — one branch remembered as the red, another remembered by way of Nizar's apportioning of his bequest, in which a horse and a donkey went to one son and were still being talked about generations later. The author is not detained by any of it. His interest is in one line only, and he states it as a principle: honour never ceased to be tied to that line, and goodness never ceased to be transmitted through it, running through the fathers of the Prophet until it culminated in the man called Quraysh, to whom the whole tribe is attributed.

Now, why that name? The author gives the popular etymology and then sets it aside, which tells you something about him. It has been said, he reports, that the name derives from the word for a shark — a sea creature that devours the smallest of whales and gathers them into the deep — and he quotes a Yemeni poet, al-Mushmarj ibn 'Amr al-Humayri, whose verses play on exactly that image of a creature in the sea that eats what it gathers. Then his own verdict: rather, the name comes from the fact that they prevail over the other Arab tribes and hold authority over them. He prefers the derivation that describes a position among people to the one that describes an appetite.

And having said that Quraysh held authority, he does something a listener might not expect from a religious historian. He calls a philosopher as a witness on a question of language.

Abu Nasr al-Farabi, in the first book of his work on words and letters, is quoted at length. "The Quraysh were the finest of the Arabs in their command of words," al-Farabi writes — "easiest on the tongue when pronounced, most pleasing to the ear, most precise in conveying what is held in the soul." Three criteria, and they are not sentimental ones: ease of articulation, pleasantness to hear, and precision in carrying what a person means. That last one is the serious claim. A dialect that transmits an inner meaning with the least distortion is, for a philosopher of language, the better instrument.

Al-Farabi then names the tribes from whom Arabic was actually taken and imitated: Qays, Tamim, and Asad — the sources of the greater part of the language, its grammar and its inflection — with certain metaphors and usages taken from a few others besides, and nothing at all from the remaining tribes. And then, at much greater length, he explains the exclusions, because the reasoning behind them is the whole point.

Nothing was taken from city dwellers, nor from those in the deep wilderness, nor from those in border regions adjacent to foreign nations. Not from Lakhm or Judham, who were neighbours of the people of Mudar. Not from Ghassan or Iyad, who bordered the Levant and were in close contact with its people, most of them Christians who recited their prayers in a tongue other than their own. Not from those who bordered the Greeks. Not from those adjacent to the Persians and the Nabataeans and in constant contact with them. Not from 'Abd al-Qays, who inhabited Bahrain and dealt with Persia and India. Not from the people of Yemen, because of their contact with India and Abyssinia and the mingling that followed. Not from Banu Hanifah and the people of al-Yamamah, nor from Thaqif, nor from the people of Ta'if, because all of them mixed with the nations of the world. And not from the Hijaz region, because those who first set about recording the Arabic tongue found that its people had already come into contact with foreign nations and that their speech had been altered by it.

One principle runs through every exclusion: contact corrupts. A tongue is preserved by isolation and thinned by traffic — with Persia, with Byzantium, with India, with Abyssinia, and above all with liturgies recited in a borrowed language. Al-Farabi's Arabic is not the language of the cosmopolitan centres. It is the language of the people who were left alone.

He closes with a portrait of the tribes he trusted, and it is a portrait of hardness. Their occupations were hunting, herding, and raiding. They were the strongest of souls, the hardest of hearts, the most resolute in spirit, the most resistant to being subjugated, the most devoted to those they loved, the least willing to submit to kings, the most upright in character, and the least prone to abjectness and humiliation. That is a description of a people who would not be governed, and therefore would not be linguistically colonised either. The author quotes all of it and then simply says: this is what al-Farabi said.

He leaves the inference to us, and it is not hard to draw. This history has spent an entire episode arguing that a line was prepared — a lineage kept, a custody handed down, a mark visible in a face. The passage on language extends the same argument to a different kind of instrument. The tribe at the end of that line spoke, on a philosopher's technical judgment, the version of Arabic best able to carry meaning without distorting it. For an author about to describe a revelation delivered in Arabic to a man of Quraysh, the observation does not need to be underlined.

And with the tribe established, he narrows once more to a household. The lineage of the fathers of the Messenger of God converges, he writes, noble upon noble, all the way back to Qusay ibn Kilab — a name we have not yet met, attached to a man who was carried out of Mecca as a small child and came back to take charge of it.

## What this episode lands

Three things are now in place, and the rest of the volume rests on them.

The first is the author's case that leadership of the community was never the community's to arrange. Guidance has always come in pairs — the one who is sent and the one who carries it forward — and the sealing of prophethood ended the sending, not the need. A body of people cannot supply the element the whole structure rests on, they had no procedure by which to try, and the wars of the first generation are what the attempt produced. His sharpest proof is the smallest rule he could have chosen: a Prophet who forbade any believer to let two nights pass without a written will did not leave the community itself without one.

The second is the purity of the ancestral line, argued from premises his opponents share. The Prophet testified that he came through noble loins and purified wombs; the Prophet defined nobility as piety; the Qur'an calls idolaters impure. Therefore the fathers were not idolaters — and where they lived among idolaters, some of them kept their faith unspoken, as a believer in Pharaoh's own household did. The claim made for them is custodial rather than saintly: they carried something, and the honour is in having carried it.

The third is the line itself, from Abraham's division of his household through Ishmael at the Sacred House, to Qaythar with the light already visible in his face, to Adnan in the age of Moses, and down through the northern Arab branches to the man whose name the tribe of Quraysh still carries. Along the way, a philosopher's verdict that this tribe's speech was the clearest instrument any of the Arabs had — a claim about language that lands, in this book, as a claim about preparation.

The line has now arrived at a single household, and the next episode belongs to four men in it. One of them bought the key of the Ka'bah for a skin of wine. One of them talked a Byzantine emperor into a trade guarantee and turned the sanctuary's prestige into a caravan route. And one of them stood alone in an emptied city in front of an invading army, was offered his own camels back, and said something about the House that his family repeated for generations afterwards.
