## Where this episode opens

How do you lose a war you are never allowed to fight?

Ten thousand men came for Medina in the month of Shawwal in the fifth year after the emigration, and they went home without a battle. There was no line of engagement, no charge, no rout in the open field. What stopped them was a ditch — a foreign idea, dug in haste by hungry men, which the Arabs looked at and said plainly had never been done before. After the ditch came a rumour. After the rumour came a wind.

That shape means the decisive events of this episode are almost all off the battlefield. Three movements carry it. First the digging itself, and the run of wonders that attends it — every one of them about food or about stone, not one of them about a weapon. Then the covenant that collapses behind the defenders' backs while the enemy stands in front of them, and the fear that follows it into the camp. Then the single moment when a man actually crosses the ditch, and everything that comes apart from his death.

Listen for what this record counts as the act that turned it. It is not the trench, though the trench held. It is a fight that lasted a few minutes, and the history says so twice in plain words — once through a verse of the Quran recited a particular way, and once in the mouth of a companion asked to defend the claim to a sceptical room.

## The trench and its signs

The war began as a diplomatic errand.

Some of the Jews of the Hijaz had grown embittered — among them Salam ibn Abi al-Haqiq al-Nadhari, Ibn Akhtab al-Nadari, Kanana ibn al-Rabi' ibn Abi al-Haqiq al-Nahri, Hudha ibn Qays al-Wa'ili and Abu 'Ammar al-Wa'ili, along with a company of the Banu al-Nadir and the Banu Wa'il. The Banu al-Nadir are the tribe this history watched load its camels and leave Medina for Khaybar in the previous year, and they have not stopped moving since.

They set out until they reached Quraysh in Mecca and called them to war against the Messenger of God, telling them: we will stand with you in this until we uproot him.

Quraysh did not accept at once. They put a question first. O community of Jews, you are the people of the First Book, and you know well what we and Muhammad are at odds over — tell us: are we on the side of right, or is he?

They said: your religion is better than his, and you are more deserving than he.

Read what has just been asked and answered. Quraysh, who keep three hundred and sixty idols around the House, have gone to men with a revealed scripture and asked them to adjudicate between idolatry and the message of one God — and have been told that the idolaters are in the right. The delegation gets what it came for by giving Mecca a certificate no one in Mecca could have issued for itself.

When they had said this to Quraysh and secretly secured their agreement to make war — telling them, gather yourselves for this and make ready — those same leaders went out to Ghatafan of Qays 'Aylan and invited them too. They told Ghatafan that they would stand with them and that Quraysh had already agreed to join. Ghatafan fell in with them as well.

So Quraysh marched out under their commander Abu Sufyan ibn Harb, and Ghatafan marched out under their own leaders: 'Uyayna ibn Husn ibn Hudhayfah ibn Badr at the head of the Banu Fazara; al-Harith ibn 'Awf ibn Abi Haritha al-Marri at the head of the Banu Murrah; and Muqarr ibn Rukhilah leading those who followed him from the people of Ashja' ibn Rayth ibn Ghatafan.

When the Messenger of God received word of their advance, and the command came to him, he ordered a trench dug around the city.

He worked on it himself, alongside his God-given duty, in order to make the Muslims exert themselves. The Muslims worked with him. Ali and those who followed him were among the most energetic and the hardest working; the hypocrites were slack and negligent in what they did.

That sentence about the hypocrites is the first appearance of a group who will be quoted three more times before this episode is done, and it is worth noticing that the record first identifies them not by anything they say but by how much earth they move. Before the theology, the shovel.

The workers were severely hampered by the ground, and they complained of it to the Messenger of God. He called for a vessel with water in it, spat into the vessel, made whatever supplication God willed, and then sprinkled that water on the rock.

Jabir ibn 'Abd Allah al-Ansari said, in what has been narrated from him, that the Prophet struck it, and it crumbled away like a dune — and after that neither axe nor pick was turned back from it.

Then two accounts of food, and they run one after the other.

The first is told by the sister of al-Nu'man ibn Bashir. My mother called me, she said, gave me a handful of dates, and said: my daughter, take this to your father and your uncle 'Abd Allah ibn Rawaha as their lunch. As she passed by the Messenger of God he said to her: come here, O daughter — what do you have with you? So she poured the dates into his palms, and they did not even fill them.

He called for a garment to be spread out, then called for the dates to be scattered on it, and then he commanded his caller to announce to everyone in the trench that they should come and eat. They came and they ate until they had had their fill — and the dates only increased, until they were spilling over the edges of the garment.

The second is told by Jabir ibn 'Abd Allah. I ordered my wife to slaughter a sheep and prepare food, he said, and then I went to the Messenger of God to invite him to come with me alone. He came — and the Messenger of God arrived with the food and the sheep. He blessed it, and the blessing of God came upon it. Then he ate, and the people came to it. No sooner had one group finished and departed than another arrived, until all the people of the trench had eaten their fill.

Both stories are a private meal turned public against the wishes of the man who cooked it. A girl is carrying lunch to two named relatives. A householder invites one guest and specifies that he wants him alone. In each case the food is taken out of the hands that were carrying it, spread where everyone can see it, and announced. The miracle is not only that the quantity holds. It is that the quantity holds after the portion has been made common.

Then Salman al-Farisi struck the ground.

I struck the pickaxe against a section of the trench, he said, and a rock resisted me. The Messenger of God took the pickaxe from my hand and struck three times. I saw three flashes of light inside it, and I said: what is this, O Messenger of God?

He said — and I confirmed, yes, by my father and my mother — as for the first flash, God has granted me the opening of Yemen; as for the second, God has granted me the opening of the Levant and the Maghreb; and as for the third, God has granted me the opening of the East.

Set that against where the man saying it is standing. He is in a ditch outside a town of three thousand fighting men who are about to be short of food, waiting for an army more than three times their size, and the sign that comes up out of the rock is not deliverance from the siege. It is Yemen, Syria, North Africa and Persia. The wonders in this stretch divide neatly in two: the ones that concern the next few weeks are all about eating, and the one that concerns the future skips the siege entirely and shows him three continents.

The Messenger of God had not yet finished the trench when Quraysh came down and camped at the settlement of al-Asiyyals near Ruma, between the escarpment and al-Ghaba, with ten thousand of their forces together with their allies from the Banu Kinana and the people of Tihama. Ghatafan and the people of Najd who were with them advanced likewise, until they descended in overwhelming numbers near Uhud — the same ground where this community had been broken two years earlier.

The Messenger of God marched out with three thousand Muslims, set them with their backs to the heights, placed his army there, and put the trench between himself and the enemy. He commanded them to encamp and dig in. They settled into their positions.

## The covenant broken behind them

The line held in front. It failed behind.

Huyayy ibn Akhtab, the hostile Jew, went out until he came to Ka'b ibn Asad al-Qurazi, the holder of the compact and covenant of the Banu Qurayza — a man who had kept faith with the Messenger of God, toward his own people and toward the covenant he had made.

Huyayy said to him: I have come to you with glory undying.

He would not relent until Ka'b answered him, and the Banu Qurayza broke the treaty they had with the Messenger of God and with the protected community.

That is the whole account of the breach, and it contains no threat, no bribe named, and no argument recorded. One man walks into a fortress with a phrase — glory undying — and stays there until the answer changes. The record is careful to say what Ka'b was before that conversation: a man who had kept faith. It does not offer a reason for the reversal. It offers a visitor and a duration.

The Messenger of God summoned four men: Sa'd ibn Mu'adh, master of al-Aws; Sa'd ibn 'Ubada of al-Khazraj; 'Abd Allah ibn Rawaha; and Khawwat ibn Jubayr, of the brothers of the Banu 'Amr ibn 'Awf. He said to them: go and see — is what has been reported to us about these people true or not?

They went out to them and found them in the worst of states. The Banu Qurayza said: who is the Messenger of God? There is no covenant between us and Muhammad, and no compact. They answered with abuse toward the Messenger of God, and Sa'd ibn Mu'adh cursed them.

The companions returned and reported the news. And the Messenger of God said: God is greatest — give glad tidings, O community of Muslims.

Stop on that reply, because it is the strangest sentence in this episode. Four senior men have just come back to a besieged camp to confirm that the tribe holding the southern approaches has torn up its treaty, that the city's rear is now open, and that the messengers were insulted to their faces. The answer is congratulation. Nothing in the account explains it. What follows immediately, in the very next lines, is the opposite reaction from everybody else.

The calamity grew greater, fear intensified, the enemy pressed from above and from below, and the stars of the sky appeared. Some of the hypocrites said: Muhammad used to promise us that we would eat the treasures of Chosroes and Caesar — yet now not one of us can be sure he will even make it to the latrine.

Others went to the Prophet and said: our houses are exposed to the enemy.

The believers were shaken by a violent trembling, as God describes it in the chapter of the Confederates: there the believers were afflicted and a severe earthquake struck (chapter 33, verse 11).

Put the hypocrites' complaint beside Salman's three flashes and you have the two halves of the same argument standing four paragraphs apart. One man is told, over a rock, that Yemen and Syria and Persia are opening. Another man says out loud that he cannot be confident of reaching the latrine. Both sentences are about the distance between a promise and a position, and the record does not soften either.

The polytheists laid siege for more than twenty nights, and the fighting between the two sides was only skirmishing and archery across the trench. The polytheists looked at the ditch and said: this is a trick the Arabs have never known before.

That is the whole military history of three weeks. Nobody closed. The most powerful coalition the peninsula had assembled sat in front of a hole in the ground it had no method for, and shot arrows across it.

Meanwhile the danger the broken covenant had opened up was working its way toward the women.

Safiyyah bint 'Abd al-Muttalib was in the fortress of Fara', where she had taken refuge with the women and the children, and Hassan ibn Thabit was with them. A Jewish man came to the fortress, circled it, and began to probe its defences — for the Banu Qurayza had cut communication between the fortress and the Messenger of God and the Muslims, who were occupied facing their enemy.

Safiyyah said: I said to Hassan — this Jew, as you can see, is prowling around the fortress, and I fear he will expose our position from behind us to the Jews, while the Messenger of God and the Muslims cannot reach us. Go down and deal with him.

He said: O daughter of 'Abd al-Muttalib, you know I am not capable of this.

She said: when he told me that, and I saw nothing of use in him, I took a tent pole, climbed down from the fortress, and struck the man with it until I had killed him. When I had finished I went back up and said: O Hassan, go down and strip him of his weapons, for it is not seemly for me to strip a man.

He said: I fear there may still be fight left in him.

The account gives that exchange without a word of comment, and it does not need one. The man who will compose the victory poetry for this campaign — whose verses about the enemy's cowardice you will hear before this episode is over — is the man who would not go down the wall, and would not go down afterwards either. The aunt of the Prophet did both, with a tent pole, and then had to explain why she was not going to do the third thing.

And during those weeks an arrow came across the ditch.

Sa'd ibn Mu'adh al-Ansari was struck by an arrow that severed the vein of his arm. The man who shot it was of the Banu 'Amir ibn Lu'ay. Sa'd did not die at once; he died after the Battle of the Trench and after the affair of the Banu Qurayza, from that wound.

Hold onto him. The master of al-Aws is one of the four men sent to find out whether the Banu Qurayza had broken their word. He now carries a wound taken in the siege that breach helped cause. What he does between that wound and his death is the whole of the next episode.

## All faith against all disbelief

Then one man decided the ditch was not going to hold him.

A champion was sent out from among the polytheists: 'Amr ibn 'Abd Wudd ibn Abi Qays, of the Banu 'Amir ibn Lu'ayy. He was one of the most formidable fighters among them and one of the greatest, a man renowned for his prowess. He had fought at Badr on the side of the polytheists and had taken wounds so severe that he was counted among the dead, and yet he had survived. From that time he had fought in no battle at all — and now he wished to make a show of himself before Quraysh and demonstrate his valour. So he put on a distinguishing mark, so that he might be recognised.

With him went 'Ikrima ibn Abi Jahl, Hubayra ibn Abi Wahab al-Makhzumi, and Dirar ibn al-Khattab ibn Mardas, of the brothers of the Banu Muharib ibn Fihr. They armed themselves, made ready, and rode out until they drew up before the Banu Kinana and called: prepare to fight, O Banu Kinana — today you will see true horsemen.

Then they urged their horses forward until they reached a narrow point of the trench. Their horses leapt it, and the riders spread out in the salt flat between the goods-storage and the ditch.

'Amr ibn 'Abd Wudd was the mightiest of them, and he rode ahead as their leader, calling for single combat. What survives of the verse he called it in runs: calling them to gather — is there any swordsman? I have escaped the frost of age. At the station of great deeds across the ages I stood when they called me, rushing toward the shaker. Yes, I am still undefeated; and valour is among the finest of traits — courage in a man.

Ali ibn Abi Talib rose and sought permission from the Messenger of God to go out to him. Permission was granted, and he went out.

When the Prophet sent him toward the man, he said: he is 'Amr ibn 'Abd al-Wad. Ali replied: I gladly accept — by God, O Messenger of God.

The Messenger of God gave him permission and handed him his own sword. Then he raised his hands toward the sky and said: O God, protect him from what is before him and behind him, from his right and his left, from above him and from below him.

And then he said: all of faith has gone forth to meet all of disbelief.

That is the sentence the whole episode turns on, and it is worth being precise about what it does. It does not say that a brave man has gone out to meet a dangerous one. It removes both men from the category of individuals. One of them is now the entirety of the religion and the other is the entirety of its refusal, and whatever happens in the salt flat happens to those two things and not to two fighters. Everything the rest of this episode claims about the day rests on this line having been said before the fight, not after it.

Ali advanced, reciting: when you called the lover, your voice was never helpless, and sincerity clears a path past every rival. You must mourn, you criminals, for the sting of the bees will reach you. I hope you will prove strong.

'Amr said to him: who are you?

He said: I am Ali ibn Abi Talib.

'Amr said: a worthy man of noble rank — yet I have no wish to kill you.

Ali said: but I have been told about you. O 'Amr, I put to you two choices, and you must answer one of them.

He said: then put them to me.

He said: I invite you to God, to His Messenger, and to Islam.

He said: I want none of that.

He said: then I invite you to single combat.

'Amr said: your father Abu Talib was a dear friend to me, and I hate to kill you.

Ali said: as for me, I would gladly kill you, since you have refused Islam.

Notice the order of the offer, because it is the same order this history has recorded at every earlier confrontation and it is never reversed. The invitation to the religion comes first. Combat is what is left when it is declined, and it is offered second, by name, as a choice the other man is asked to make himself. And notice what stands between the two men when the choice is put: a friendship with Abu Talib, the man who protected the Prophet in Mecca for four decades. 'Amr's reluctance to strike is a debt to a dead guardian, and it is the son of that guardian who tells him it will not be reciprocated.

'Amr dismounted from his horse and charged at him, striking at his face. He came at Ali, who gave ground and sidestepped. A fierce struggle broke out between them, and no one could see them through the dust, and the Muslims feared for Ali. 'Amr struck him on the head, splitting his helmet. He then struck at his flank — and Ali delivered a blow that severed his head from his body.

The dust settled to reveal Ali wiping his sword on his garment.

What survives of the verses he spoke there runs: against them I did not leave my friends without support, while the knights broke apart before me like this. I upheld the Lord of Muhammad with my righteousness — a victory wrested from the folly of his arrogance. Like the bond between your brother and my narrator, issued the moment I left him boasting. I was the one whom he called to challenge, and I shamed his deputies, though I did so alone. And O Prophet, O gathering of the confederates — do not imagine that God would abandon His religion.

'Amr's cavalry fled in rout until they plunged back across the trench. 'Ikrimah ibn Abi Jahl hurled his spear away as he fled, and Hassan ibn Thabit made a poem of it: was it not you, 'Ikrimah, who fell short? You barely escaped from the ditch. He fled and left his spear behind us. And it was said also: would you not count yourself among the enemy of darkness? Or has your back grown tame — is your back like the back of a snake?

Ali killed, together with 'Amr ibn 'Abd Wad, his son Hasal ibn 'Amr, and Nuqal ibn 'Abd Allah al-Makhrumi, who had crossed the trench alongside 'Amr and become entangled in the fighting with him.

Then the polytheists asked the Messenger of God to let them ransom 'Amr's body. He said: we have no need of his body or its price. And he left them to it.

Because of what Ali achieved that day, God opened the way for the Muslims. When He opened it, their hearts were strengthened, and they knew that the polytheists had lost all hope of forcing the trench. Despair and dread came down on the besieging army.

And here the history sets down a reading of the Quran, because the claim it is making needs a text under it. In the recitation of 'Abd Allah ibn Mas'ud: and God has sufficed the believers in fighting — with Ali — and God is Strong and Mighty (chapter 33, verse 25). The reading is transmitted from Ibn Mas'ud through Fadl ibn al-Qasim, and Ibn Makula records it in his Ikmal.

The verse in its ordinary reading says that God spared the believers a battle. This reading names the instrument by which He spared them, inside the verse itself, in two words. Whatever a reader makes of the variant, its function in this book is exact: the escape of the community at the trench and the act of one man are being identified as the same event.

Then a Ghatafani came across in the dark.

Na'im ibn Mas'ud ibn 'Amir ibn Anif, a man of Ghatafan who had been among the polytheists, came to the Messenger of God and said: O Messenger of God, I have accepted Islam, and my people do not yet know of it. I have come to you — employ me however you see fit.

The Messenger of God said: you are but one man among us, a single voice. But go to your people and sow discord among them if you can, for war is deception.

He went out on that errand, and he ran it twice.

He was a friend of the Banu Qurayza, so he went to them as a visitor. They welcomed him and drew him close. After he had greeted them he said: you know my affection for you, and I have come to give you counsel, if you will accept it.

They said: may God reward you well. We do not suspect you, and we trust your affection. Give us your advice.

He said: you have taken a course of action without thinking carefully about yourselves. You have given your backing to Muhammad's enemies, Quraysh and Ghatafan — yet your situation is not like theirs. Quraysh and Ghatafan came only to fight Muhammad and his companions on soil that is not their own. If they get what they want from him, well and good; but if the tide turns, they will ride away and leave you behind with him. And you know you cannot stand against Muhammad and his companions alone, if he turns on you. Your confederates have been worn down by failure and disagreement; their momentum has slackened and their resolve is diminished. You saw what became of 'Amr ibn 'Abd Wad and his companions. He was the man they depended on, and you saw what came of him when he stormed the trench. Now, with despair gripping people's hearts, a few more days and any shock will send them home — and when they go, they will return to their own lands and leave you to face Muhammad alone.

They said: you have spoken truthfully and counselled us well. But what stratagem is there, after all this?

He said: the stratagem is not to fight alongside these people until you take hostages from them — nobles of their men, held in your hands — so that they cannot simply abandon you while you hold their kinsmen.

They said: you have pointed us to sound counsel, and you have done well. May God reward you.

Then he went to 'Uyaynah ibn Husn and to Abu Sufyan, separately, and told each of them the same thing: between me and the Banu Qurayza there is something you have heard of, and I have met with them and learned their position.

They said: and what is it?

He said: the people regret having broken their covenant with Muhammad, now that they see where we stand. They have looked at the outcome of 'Amr ibn 'Abd Wad and his companions, and they fear that we may withdraw and leave them behind — and then Muhammad will reach out to them, and they will send to him wishing to reconcile and expressing regret for what they did. They are proposing to demand hostages from both tribes, nobles from among our men, which they would then hand over to Muhammad to have their necks struck, or to deal with as he sees fit. After that they would join him against whatever is left of us. Do not be taken in by the Jews, or they will hand one of you over to the enemy.

Now watch the two halves of the errand close on each other.

Abu Sufyan sent 'Uyaynah ibn Husn and 'Ikrimah ibn Abi Jahl to the Banu Qurayza, with a contingent of Quraysh and Ghatafan, to enquire into this and to summon them to battle. They said: we cannot remain here any longer. The horses are exhausted, the provisions have run out, and Abu Muhammad and his companions are blocking us with that trench of theirs. You know the terrain — come out with your forces, let us finish off Muhammad and his companions, and let us storm the trench together.

When the leaders of the Banu Qurayza received the appeal they replied: we were bound to Muhammad by a covenant, and we did not break what stood between us and him without reason. We fear that when the tide of war turns against us you will withdraw and the war will have moved away from you. We will not fight alongside you unless you give us hostages from among your leading men, to hold as a guarantee.

When word of this reached Abu Sufyan and 'Uyaynah, they recognised that it was exactly what Na'im ibn Mas'ud had told them to expect — and they refused to give anyone as a hostage.

And the Banu Qurayza said: this confirms precisely what Na'im ibn Mas'ud told us.

Each side now had its suspicion confirmed by the other side's behaviour, and both confirmations were manufactured by the same man in the same week. Nobody lied. Na'im told the Jews a true thing about their allies' reliability and told the allies a true thing about the Jews' fear, and put a demand for hostages between them that each side could only read as proof of the other's treachery.

The confederates held their positions, each faction distrustful of the other, their hearts estranged, and they had no course left but to withdraw to their own lands.

Then God sent a fierce wind upon them that left them with no vessel upright, no cooking pot standing, and no shelter intact. Abu Sufyan rose when the night had grown long and said: O people of Quraysh — by God, this is no place to remain. The horses and the camels are perishing, and we have left the Banu Qurayza behind us having gained nothing. Add to that the ferocity of this wind you see. So depart.

And they departed. He went to his own mount, which had been hobbled and was kneeling, sat on it and spurred it, then struck it and set it off at a trot — and he did not release its headband until he was standing in the open.

Quraysh and Ghatafan departed, each to their own lands, and not one of them reached Medina. God delivered the Muslims through the blessing of His Messenger and the struggle of Ali ibn Abi Talib.

That final sentence names two causes and no third. And because the second of them is the kind of claim that gets argued about, this history closes the account by putting it into a conversation where it is challenged.

Rabi'ah al-Sa'di said: I came to Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman and said, O Abu 'Abd Allah, relate to us something about Ali and his virtues — for the people of Basra tell us that we exaggerate concerning him and his merits. Will you not tell me a hadith about Ali?

Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman said to me: O Rabi'ah, what do you ask me about a man who — if the deeds of all the companions of Muhammad were placed together on one pan of the scale, and the deeds of Ali were placed on the other — his deeds would outweigh theirs?

Rabi'ah said: I said, even Abu Bakr and 'Umar?

He said: yes, and all the rest.

I said: even Abu Bakr and 'Umar?

He said: O you — how could it be otherwise? Where were Abu Bakr and 'Umar, may your mother mourn you, and all the companions of Muhammad, on the day of 'Amr ibn 'Abd al-Wad, when he stormed the trench and called for a champion? Every single one of them held back — except Ali, who stepped forward and killed him with his own hand, and thereby caused all the confederate forces to scatter. By Him in whose hand my soul rests, his deed on that day outweighs in reward the deeds of all the followers of Muhammad until the Day of Judgment.

## What this episode lands

Three things carry forward.

The first is the arithmetic of the day, which this history states three separate ways and never softens. A verse of the Quran, in one recitation, names Ali inside the clause about God sufficing the believers in fighting. A closing sentence gives two causes for the deliverance of Medina and no others. And a companion, asked to defend the claim to people who think it is exaggeration, does not retreat an inch: he answers the challenge by asking where everyone else was standing while one man walked out to the salt flat. The strongest version of the claim is the one the book puts in the mouth of the man being cross-examined about it.

The second is that this siege was decided by things said, not by things thrown. A phrase carried into a fortress — glory undying — turned an ally into an enemy without a threat being recorded. A sentence spoken before a duel — all of faith has gone forth to meet all of disbelief — decided what the duel would mean before it was fought. And one man telling two true things to two frightened parties, with a demand for hostages set between them, dissolved a coalition of ten thousand without a single further casualty. In three weeks the only fighting was archery across a ditch.

The third is what the wonders of the trench were actually for. A rock crumbles like a dune. A handful of dates feeds an army and spills over the edges of the garment. One sheep goes round in relays until everyone has eaten. These are not weapons and they are not victories; they are a besieged town's daily problems solved one at a time. The single sign that looks past the siege — the three flashes in the stone — does not promise that Medina will hold. It promises Yemen, the Levant, the Maghreb and the East, to men who at that moment could not be sure of reaching the latrine.
