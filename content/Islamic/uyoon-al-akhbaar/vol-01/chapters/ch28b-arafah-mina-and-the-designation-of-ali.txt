## Where this episode picks up

A man tells a crowd of tens of thousands that he does not expect to see them again, and then spends the rest of the hour cancelling debts. What is he actually doing?

The previous episode ended in the middle of a pilgrimage and with a verse held deliberately in reserve — the one commanding that something be conveyed, on pain of the whole message counting as undelivered. It taught the rites up to the seventh circuit of the House and then stopped, because the history itself announces that it is saving the account of that verse for the end. This episode is the end. It walks the remaining ground of the pilgrimage, and then, on the road home, arrives at the thing the whole journey has been carrying.

Three movements. First the day of Arafah, and the sermon delivered there: the fullest single address in this volume, given from the back of a camel in a valley floor with the community gathered in front of it, and given whole here rather than in pieces. Second, the ground between Arafah and Mecca — the departure at sunset, the night at Muzdalifah, the stoning, the slaughter, the shaving, the days at Mina — recorded in the same instructional detail as the rites of consecration, because the point of the journey was that people could watch it being done. And third, the designation itself: what was proclaimed at a watering place called Ghadir Khumm eight days after the sacrifice, why this history says the Messenger of God was afraid to proclaim it, and the argument it builds on the fact that he did.

Listen, in the first section, for how much of the sermon is about property.

## The sermon at Arafah

We have narrated from the Messenger of God: Tomorrow is the day of Arafah. From Mina, after the sun has risen, I prayed the noon prayer.

And from Ali: The Messenger of God descended from Arafah to Nimrah and stayed there. When the sun went down, he commanded that al-Qaswa be brought out, and he rode her. He continued until he came to the valley floor, where he stood to address the people. Then Bilal gave the call to prayer, and he prayed the noon prayer, then the afternoon prayer, with nothing between them. Then he rode until the station came.

The sermon of the Messenger of God, after he had praised God and glorified Him, included the following:

O people, listen to me, for I do not know — I may never meet you again after this year in this place.

O people, your blood and your wealth are forbidden to one another until you meet your Lord, just as the sanctity of this day of yours and the sanctity of this month of yours are inviolable. You will meet your Lord, and He will ask you about your deeds. It has been conveyed to you, so whoever holds a trust, let him return it to the one who entrusted it to him.

I have left among you something by which, if you hold fast to it, you will never go astray after me: the Book of God and my family. They will not part from one another until they return to the Basin.

O people, listen to what I say and take it to heart, for every Muslim is a brother to every Muslim. The Muslims are brothers. It is not permissible for a man to take from his brother anything except what that brother willingly gives him.

Every form of usury is annulled, but your principal is yours — you will not wrong, and you will not be wronged (chapter 2, verse 279). God has decreed that there is no usury, and the usury of 'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib is all annulled. Every blood debt from the days of ignorance is annulled. The first blood debt I begin with from the blood of the age of ignorance is the blood of the son of Rabi'ah ibn al-Harith ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib — he was nursed among Banu Layth and was killed by Hudhayl — for he is the first claim I set aside from the blood of that age.

Yet, O people, Satan despairs of ever being worshipped in this land of yours again, but if he is obeyed in anything less than that, he will be satisfied with what you regard as trifling among your deeds. So guard your religion against him.

O people, the intercalation is an increase in disbelief, by which those who disbelieve are led astray. They make it lawful one year and forbid it another year, so as to agree with the number that God has made sacred, thereby making lawful what God has forbidden (chapter 9, verse 37).

Indeed, time has turned full circle as it was on the day God created the heavens and the earth. The number of months with God is twelve months (chapter 9, verse 36). Of these, four are sacred — three consecutive, and Rajab of Mudar, which falls between Jumada and Sha'ban.

Now then, O people: you have rights over your wives, and they have rights over you. Your right over them is that they not allow onto your beds anyone you dislike, and that they not commit a manifest indecency (chapter 4, verse 19). If they do so, God has given you permission to desert them in their beds and to strike them without severity or humiliation. Their right over you is their provision and their clothing in a fitting manner (chapter 2, verse 233). Treat women well, for they are helpers to you, not independent agents. You have taken them in the covenant of God and made their intimacy lawful through the word of God.

Take heed, O people: God has appointed to every rightful heir his right. It is not permissible to make a bequest in favour of an heir. A child belongs to the marriage bed, and the one who claims otherwise is subject to stoning. Whoever claims a father other than his own, or claims a guardian other than his rightful one, the curse of God, of the angels, and of all mankind is upon him, and God will accept from him neither an obligatory act nor a voluntary one.

By obligatory he meant the obligatory duties; by voluntary he meant the supererogatory prayers.

Now look at what that address actually does, because its shape is not the shape of a farewell. It opens by saying that this may be the last meeting, and then it spends most of its length dismantling outstanding claims. Blood and wealth are declared inviolable in the same breath and by the same comparison — as sacred as the day and the month everyone is standing in. Trusts are to be returned to whoever left them. Interest is cancelled while the principal is left intact, which is a precise instruction rather than a slogan: no one loses what he lent, and no one collects what he charged. Then, having stated the rule, the speaker applies it first to his own uncle, whose lending is named aloud in front of the whole gathering. The same move is made again with blood. Every outstanding vendetta from the old order is annulled, and the first case struck out is a killing inside his own clan — a child of his own house, fostered with one tribe and killed by another. In both cases the rule is proved by being applied where it costs the speaker's family first.

Then a warning about scale — Satan has given up on being worshipped outright here, and will settle for obedience in the things you consider too small to count — and then a correction to the calendar itself: sliding a month to suit a season is named as an increase in disbelief, and the year is snapped back to the shape it had at creation, twelve months, four of them sacred, the last named so exactly that no one can mistake which Rajab is meant.

And then the household. Rights are stated in both directions in the same sentence, maintenance and clothing are put on the record as a debt rather than a favour, and the instruction to treat women well is given a reason drawn from the covenant that made the marriage lawful. The section on inheritance closes the same argument from the other side: shares are fixed by God and cannot be redirected by will, a child belongs to the bed he was born in, and a man who attaches himself to a father or a guardian not his own is cursed by God, the angels and all people, and has neither his obligatory nor his voluntary acts accepted. It is the sharpest sentence in the sermon, and it is about claiming a line of descent or a relationship of authority that is not yours.

Set beside that, the sentence that sits early in the address and is passed over without emphasis: I have left among you something by which, if you hold fast to it, you will never go astray after me — the Book of God and my family. They will not part from one another until they return to the Basin.

The same day carries a second address, and this one is reported through Sa'd al-Khafaf on the authority of Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, who said:

The Messenger of God came out to us on the day of Arafah and said: O people, God has honoured you on this day — the angels have sought forgiveness for you in general, and have sought forgiveness for Ali in particular. As for the general forgiveness: it is for whoever among you does not violate his covenant after me, in accordance with the word of God: Whoever breaks his oath breaks it only against himself (chapter 48, verse 10). As for the particular: it is obedience to him. Whoever obeys him obeys me, and whoever disobeys him disobeys me.

Then he said to Ali: Rise. So Ali stood, and the Messenger of God took his hand, then said: O people, I am the Messenger of God to you all, and obedience to me is obligatory. Indeed, I harbour no fear of my people, nor do I seek refuge in my kinship with them. I am only the Messenger of God — and the Messenger has no duty but to convey clearly (chapter 24, verse 54).

This is Gabriel informing me on behalf of the Almighty: The fortunate one is truly the one who loved Ali in his lifetime and after his death. The wretched one is truly the one who harboured hatred for him in his lifetime or after his death.

Two things in that address point forward. The first is the gesture — a man told to stand up, and a hand taken and held in front of a crowd — which will be repeated eight days later in almost the same words and with the arm raised instead. The second is the disclaimer, and it is unusual enough to be worth reading twice: I harbour no fear of my people, nor do I seek refuge in my kinship with them. That is a denial of a motive nobody in the crowd had voiced. It answers the accusation that a man who elevates a member of his own household is doing family business. And the answer given is that he is a messenger, and a messenger's sole duty is to deliver what he was given.

## From Arafah to Mina

Al-Sadiq said, regarding the word of God: Then overflow from where the people overflow (chapter 2, verse 199) — he said: The Quraysh used to depart from Muzdalifah in the days of ignorance, saying: We are more entitled to this than other people. So God commanded them to depart from Arafah, as all other people depart.

That is the first ruling of the return journey and it is a levelling one. The old custom let the tribe of the sanctuary begin its descent from a nearer, more privileged station, on the argument that the House was theirs. The verse abolishes the shortcut by naming the point of departure as the one everybody else uses.

And from Ali: The Messenger of God departed from Arafah when the sun had set.

And from him: When you depart from Arafah, proceed with calm and dignity, occupying yourselves with seeking forgiveness. God says: Then overflow from where the people have overflowed, and seek God's forgiveness (chapter 2, verse 199). Keep to a measured pace in your walking. You must observe your prayers, and do not follow the example of the wretched. The Messenger of God departed from Arafah holding al-Qaswa loosely by the reins, so that her head reached the front of his saddle, and he gestured with his right hand to the people: O people, tranquility, tranquility. Whenever he reached a hill, he would ease his mount's pace a little until it climbed, continuing in this way until he arrived at Muzdalifah. His practice is to be followed.

Ali said: When the Messenger of God departed from Arafah, he continued until he came to Muzdalifah, where he combined the sunset and evening prayers with one call to prayer and two announcements of its beginning.

Al-Sadiq said: The Messenger of God granted permission for the elderly, the infirm and the weak to depart from Muzdalifah to Mina before dawn.

And from him: When the Messenger of God prayed the dawn prayer on the day of sacrifice, he rode al-Qaswa until he came to the Sacred Monument. He faced the direction of prayer, declared God's greatness, and raised the call of talbiyah. He stood alone, remaining there until the sky grew very bright, then he departed before the sun had risen.

And from him: The Messenger of God said: All of Arafah is a standing place, all of Muzdalifah is a standing place, and all of Mina is a place of sacrifice. The Messenger of God stood at a rocky mound, which is the hill upon which one should stand.

That saying is doing quiet work. The Messenger of God stood at a particular hill; his practice is to be followed; and yet the whole plain counts. What is fixed is the rite, not the spot — so a pilgrim who cannot reach the rock has not missed the standing, and a crowd of any size can perform it at once without the ground becoming a scarce good to be fought over.

He said: When the Messenger of God came from Muzdalifah, he set his mount to a brisk pace and would say: O people, tranquility, tranquility — until he reached the valley floor, where he slowed his camel to a walk until he emerged from it, then he returned to his former brisk pace. Al-Sadiq said: Moving at a brisk pace through the valley floor is obligatory.

He said: Then the Messenger of God continued until he reached the Jamrat al-Aqabah, and he pelted it with seven pebbles.

It was narrated from him: When the Messenger of God came from Muzdalifah, he passed by the Jamrat al-Aqabah on the day of sacrifice and pelted it with seven pebbles. Then he came to Mina, and that was according to the established practice. As for the days of drying the meat, the three pillars are stoned each day after the sun has passed its zenith — indeed it is better to throw from the beginning of the day until its end. The pillars should only be stoned while in a state of ritual purity, though if someone throws while not in that state, there is no penalty upon him.

And from him: The Messenger of God used to stone the pillars on foot. Whoever rides to them, there is no penalty upon him.

The pattern of those last two rulings is the pattern of the whole section. A preferred form is stated with the reason for preferring it, and then the penalty is removed from the man who cannot manage it. Throw in a state of purity — and if you did not, nothing is owed. Walk to the pillars as he walked — and if you rode, nothing is owed. The rite keeps its shape without becoming a trap.

And from Ali ibn Abi Talib: When the Messenger of God stoned the Jamrat al-Aqabah on the day of sacrifice, he came to the slaughtering ground of Mina and said: This is the place of sacrifice, and all of Mina is a place of sacrifice. He then sacrificed his offering, and the people sacrificed their offerings in their camps throughout Mina.

It was narrated from Ali that he said: When the Messenger of God had slaughtered, he commanded that a piece from each animal be brought to him. He commanded me to eat meat from it, and he gave me a taste of the broth. He used to associate me with his sacrificial offering, and he said: Whoever has eaten of the broth has eaten of the meat.

That is the joint offering from the earlier part of this pilgrimage arriving at its conclusion, and it is settled with a rule about a bowl of soup. The two men had been consecrated into a single sacrifice because one of them had arrived from Yemen bound by a formula rather than by an animal. Now, with a hundred beasts slaughtered between them, the share of the man who brought nothing is established by eating: a piece cut from every animal, meat and then broth, and a saying that makes the broth count as the meat.

Al-Sadiq said: When you come to Mina, shave your head or shorten your hair — either will suffice. He said: Shaving is better than shortening, because the Messenger of God shaved his head at the Farewell Pilgrimage and at the lesser pilgrimage he had made under the truce years earlier.

And from Ali: The Messenger of God went to the House on the day of sacrifice and prayed the noon prayer in Mecca. And from him: The Messenger of God shortened his prayers at Mina. Ali ibn Abi Talib said: No pilgrim should spend the night outside Mina — they must spend it in Mina.

Al-Sadiq said: It is recommended for one who departs from Mina to descend at al-Abtah, which is al-Batha', and to rest there for a while before travelling to Mecca, for the Messenger of God did so.

It was narrated from Ali ibn al-Husayn: The Messenger of God prayed inside the House between the two pillars on the red marble, and at noon he faced the House and prayed two cycles. Al-Sadiq said: Do not pray an obligatory prayer inside the Ka'bah.

One event is recorded from these days that has nothing to do with the rites.

Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr was born during that pilgrimage in Mecca. He was the son of Asma' bint 'Umays. Abu Bakr had married her after Ja'far al-Tayyar was martyred, and she bore him Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr. After Abu Bakr's death, Ali married her, following the death of Fatimah al-Zahra.

When Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr was born, his sister Aisha bint Abi Bakr brought him to the Messenger of God and said: O Messenger of God, pray for him. He said: O God, grant him the guardianship of the people of my house. She said: O Messenger of God, pray for him again. He said: I have done.

So the supplication of the Messenger of God was answered, and Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr became one of the close companions of the Commander of the Faithful. He died in obedience to him and in devotion to his guardianship; Ali drew him near and held him dear, and never turned him away or excluded him.

The history places that notice here for one reason: the word in the prayer. A child born to one house is carried to the Prophet by his sister, and the blessing asked over him is attachment to another house entirely — in the same word the next section will spend its length on.

## The designation at Ghadir Khumm

God commanded Muhammad to formally designate Ali ibn Abi Talib as his guardian and to proclaim that guardianship before all those of his community who were present. This took place after his return from Mecca, when the Prophet declared the guardianship on the eighteenth day of Dhu al-Hijjah, before the Muslims had dispersed from the pilgrimage gathering. Those present, according to the reports, numbered seventy thousand.

It is narrated from Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali. A man said to him: O son of the Messenger of God, al-Hasan al-Basri told us that the Messenger of God said: God sent me with a message and I feared that people would deny me, so He warned me that if I did not deliver it, He would punish me.

Abu Ja'far said to him: Did he tell you what the message was? The man said: No. Abu Ja'far said: By God, al-Hasan knew what it was, but he deliberately suppressed it.

The man said: O son of the Messenger of God, may God make me your ransom — what was it?

He said: God commanded the believers to pray in His Book, but they did not know what prayer was or how to perform it. So God commanded His Prophet Muhammad to show them how to pray and to explain to them everything that God had ordained for them regarding prayer. Prayer is mentioned in the Quran in summary form, and so the Messenger of God interpreted it and informed his community of what God had commanded them to do in prayer.

God also commanded that alms be given, but the people did not know what the alms were, so the Messenger of God explained it and informed them of what is to be taken from gold, silver, camels, cattle and sheep. He left nothing of what God had ordained regarding the alms without explaining it fully to his community and laying it out for them.

God made fasting obligatory upon them, but they did not know what fasting was or how to fast. The Messenger of God showed them what was required of them in fasting and how to fast. God also commanded the pilgrimage, and He commanded His Prophet to explain to them how to perform it, so that he might make clear to them all of that in his practice.

Then He commanded guardianship, and said: Your guardians are only God, His Messenger, and those who believe — those who establish prayer and give the alms while bowing (chapter 5, verse 55). Thus God imposed the guardianship upon them, and they did not know what it entailed, so God commanded His Prophet to explain to them what the guardianship is, just as he had explained to them prayer, alms, fasting, pilgrimage and struggle in His way.

When this command came to him from God, the Messenger of God was troubled by it. He feared, and was burdened — fear that the people would apostatise from his religion and call him a liar, and his heart was heavy with that concern. He turned to his Lord, and God revealed to him: O Messenger, convey what has been revealed to you from your Lord; and if you do not, then you will not have conveyed His message. And God will protect you from the people (chapter 5, verse 67).

So he carried out the command of God and proclaimed the guardianship of the Commander of the Faithful on the day of Ghadir Khumm. He called out: Prayer is a gathering. He commanded that those absent be informed. The obligatory duties had been revealed one after another, each obligatory matter descending in its time; and guardianship was the last of the obligatory duties. Then God revealed: Today I have perfected your religion for you and completed My favour upon you, and I have approved Islam as your religion (chapter 5, verse 3).

Abu Ja'far said: God is saying: I will not send down upon you after this any further obligatory duty — I have completed them for you.

That is the argument in its most compressed form, and it is built as a series rather than as a claim. Every obligation in the Quran arrived in outline and was filled in by demonstration. The command to pray does not say how; the practice does. The command to give alms does not name the rates on camels or silver; the practice does. Fasting, the pilgrimage — the same. Then a verse imposes guardianship in the same summary form, and by the logic of every previous case it must also be filled in by demonstration, because a duty nobody can identify is a duty nobody can discharge. The whole force of the passage is that this last item is not being treated differently from the first four. It is being treated identically, and the demonstration of it is what happened at a watering place on the road home.

The passage also gives that demonstration a motive that is not flattering to anyone: the Prophet is described as troubled, afraid and heavy-hearted, and what he feared was that the community would call him a liar and leave the religion over this. And the verse quoted in reply does two things at once — it makes non-delivery equivalent to having delivered nothing at all, and it removes the ground of the fear by promising protection.

It is also narrated, raised in a chain to Zayd ibn Arqam, that he said:

We went out with the Messenger of God during the Farewell Pilgrimage. When we departed and reached Ghadir Khumm, he dismounted — and it was a scorching day. He commanded that a shaded shelter be prepared, its thorns gathered beneath it; he took shelter there and called out to the people: Prayer is a gathering. They all came to him, for only a few of the Muslims who had not accompanied him on that pilgrimage were absent. When they had assembled, he stood among them, and after praising and glorifying God, he said:

O people, God — ever living and eternal — has never sent a prophet except that He granted him half the lifespan of the prophet who came before him. I am on the verge of being summoned and I shall answer. I am leaving among you two things: if you hold fast to them after me, you will never go astray — the Book of God and the people of my household.

Then he took Ali's hand and raised it up, lifting it in his own hand until the whiteness of their armpits was visible.

He said: Who has greater claim over you than your own selves? They said: God and His Messenger know best. He said: Is it not so that the Prophet has a greater claim over the believers than they have over themselves (chapter 33, verse 6)? They said: Indeed, O God, yes.

He said: Whoever I am his master — Ali is his master. O God, be the guardian of whoever is his guardian, and be the adversary of whoever is his adversary.

Have you heard, and do you obey?

Zayd ibn Arqam said: I later heard Ali in al-Rahbah calling upon those who had heard the Messenger of God say, Whoever I am his master — Ali is his master, to stand and testify. He said: So there rose from those present sixteen men, and they testified to that. As for me, I was among those who suppressed it, and my sight was then gone.

The last sentence of that report is the strangest thing in it, and it is preserved rather than tidied away. The narrator is a witness who, years later and in public, stayed seated when asked to stand — and he says so himself, and connects it to his blindness.

The structure of the proclamation is worth marking too. The declaration is not asserted; it is extracted as a consequence. A question the crowd cannot answer wrongly, a verse establishing the Prophet's own prior claim over the believers, the crowd's assent, and only then the transfer — with the same word used on both sides of it. What was granted before the assent is what is passed on after it.

And from al-Sadiq, from his father, from his forefathers:

The last obligation that God sent down was the guardianship of Ali. The Messenger of God feared — when the news reached the people — that they would deny him and that most of them would turn away from Islam, on account of the envy their hearts harboured over what had been placed in their breasts. When he performed the pilgrimage with the people, he delivered the Farewell Sermon and addressed them at Arafah. The witnesses of the pilgrimage had gathered from every horizon, and he set out the key features of their religion in his sermon and counselled them. He said:

I fear I may not see you again in this place of mine. I have left among you something that, if you hold fast to it after me, you will never go astray: the Book of God and my people — the people of my household. They will not be separated from one another until they return to the Basin — a rope stretched from the sky to you, one end in the hand of God and the other in your hands.

When he completed his pilgrimage and reached Ghadir Khumm, God sent down: O Messenger, convey what has been revealed to you from your Lord; and if you do not, then you will not have conveyed His message. And God will protect you from the people (chapter 5, verse 67). So he proclaimed the guardianship of Ali as God had commanded, and God revealed: Today I have perfected your religion for you and completed My favour upon you, and I have approved Islam as your religion (chapter 5, verse 3).

It is stated in the narrations that the Prophet received the pledge of allegiance to Ali from all the Muslims who were present that day, and that 'Umar ibn al-Khattab said to him: Congratulations, O Abu al-Hasan — you have become my master and the guardian of every believer.

The verses attributed to Hassan ibn Thabit on that day reach us broken, and nothing is repaired below — only what can still be read with confidence is given. That amounts to a poet hearing the Prophet call the people at Ghadir, the question put to them and their answer, the command to rise addressed to Ali, the appointment of him as leader after the speaker, the prayer that God befriend his friends and oppose his enemies, and then the poet's own judgement of the day: a forenoon whose like he had never seen, and a warning that has been exchanged away. The closing lines turn to what happened afterwards — an allegiance pledged elsewhere, a wrong done to the first among them, and something that was not passed on. Al-Kumait is cited as having composed on the same subject, and the history then breaks off the poetry with a note: there is a great deal of verse and report on this matter, and had it all been cited, no single book would have sufficed and the chapters would have expanded without end.

The chapter closes with the author's own argument, and it is short.

The hadith regarding the Messenger of God standing at Ghadir Khumm to proclaim the guardianship of Ali is well known to all the people of Islam, widely narrated and collected. In it is the clearest evidence and the most evident path to his leadership, and to his being the successor after the Prophet within his community, and the most deserving of those the Prophet left behind to succeed him — for the Prophet made him the foremost among them in relation to themselves. And whoever stands first among them is their master, just as the Messenger of God was the most deserving in standing before the community. Whoever the Messenger of God appoints as his guardian — that guardianship is established for him, and for every one of the Imams of truth after him, even if the people of obstinacy deny his merit, just as the Messenger of God denied the merits of the people of association and unbelief.

This is the well-known textual designation that the Prophet announced, stood up for, and proclaimed before the assembly of the Muslims, so that people would have no argument against God after the messengers, for glad tidings and forewarnings had already come to them.

## What this episode lands

The pilgrimage that this volume has been building toward turns out to be, from beginning to end, a lesson in how to hand something over. The sermon at Arafah returns trusts to their owners, cancels interest while protecting principal, wipes out the vendettas of the old order, and does both of the last two by naming the speaker's own family as the first case struck. It fixes the calendar back to its created shape so that a sacred month cannot be slid around to suit a raid. It states the rights of wives and husbands in the same sentence and makes maintenance a debt rather than a kindness. It fixes inheritance shares beyond the reach of any will. And its sharpest curse falls on a man who claims a father or a guardian that is not his.

The rites between Arafah and the last night at Mina carry the same instinct. The privileged shortcut of the tribe that owned the sanctuary is abolished by a verse that sends everyone out from the same place. The whole plain of Arafah counts, the whole of Muzdalifah counts, the whole of Mina is a place of sacrifice — so the rite cannot become a scarcity to be fought over. Stone on foot, but riding costs you nothing. Stone in purity, but there is no penalty if you could not. And the joint offering that began when a commander arrived from Yemen consecrated by a borrowed formula is completed by a piece from every animal and a taste of broth.

Then, eight days after the sacrifice and before the crowds had dispersed, the thing the journey had been carrying. The argument this history makes for it is not an appeal to sentiment. It is a list: prayer, alms, fasting, pilgrimage — every one of them sent down in outline and filled in by demonstration, because a duty nobody can identify is a duty nobody can perform. Guardianship arrives in exactly that summary form, and so must be filled in the same way. The demonstration was made in the heat at a watering place, under a shelter with thorns swept beneath it, with the crowd called by the summons used for prayer and the absent sent for. A question that could not be answered wrongly; a verse establishing the claim the Prophet already held; the crowd's assent; and then that same claim transferred in one sentence, with a hand raised high enough that the whole gathering could see whose it was.

And the volume records what followed on the same page: a pledge taken from everyone present, a congratulation offered by name, a poet who called it a forenoon without equal and then, in his closing lines, said that something was not passed on. One of the witnesses transmits the whole account and admits inside it that when he was later asked in public to stand and confirm what he had heard, he stayed in his seat.
