## Where this episode picks up

Ask a man to name the greatest thing about him and you expect a list of things he did. This history records a man being asked exactly that, in public, and answering with a list of things that were done to him while he stood there and had no hand in them at all.

The previous episode closed on the return road from the last pilgrimage, with a claim transferred in front of a gathering that had been called back together for the purpose. This one begins where the narrative stops. Having carried its subject to within roughly three months of his death, the history breaks off the story and does something else instead: it assembles, one report after another, the sayings and the incidents it regards as evidence for the succession. Only when that file is closed does the narrative resume — and it resumes with a fever.

Three movements. First, what the Messenger of God said about Ali in front of other people — reports gathered from several directions, in which the same threat keeps recurring in different mouths, in different towns, and always with the same man attached to it. Second, the answer Ali himself gave when a delegation asked him to name his greatest distinctions: four episodes he insists he played no part in, and the case the history builds on each of them. And third, the turn — the envy the history says those repeated designations produced, an army ordered out of the city and turned back before it had gone a mile, a prayer led by the wrong person, and a document that was called for and never written.

Listen, in the first section, for who is holding whose hand at the end of each report.

## A man like myself

The history opens with a saying this volume has already taught: the comparison of Ali's position to Aaron's beside Moses, spoken at the departure for Tabuk when the army marched north and one man was left behind in the city. It is not argued again here. What the history adds is a correction to the analogy, made in order to keep it precise.

Aaron was Moses's brother by blood, his legatee, his successor among his people, and a prophet besides. Ali was not a prophet — prophecy was sealed with Muhammad, the divine messages were fulfilled in him, and there is no prophet after him. Nor was Ali a brother by blood. The brotherhood between them was the one established among the companions, which this volume recorded when it happened. So two of Aaron's four descriptions do not transfer. The history's point is that they were never the ones being claimed. What carries across is the office: Aaron was the successor Moses left over his people and the guardian set above them, and it is that position, and only that, which the comparison assigns.

With the frame set, the reports begin.

It is narrated from Jabir ibn Abdullah, and by another path from Abu Ishaq on the authority of Hubayrah ibn Yarim, that the Messenger of God said to Ali:

O Ali, you are my brother, my guardian, and my successor after me, and the father of my progeny. You fight in defence of my Sunnah. You will fulfil my religion and repay my debts. Whoever loves you during your lifetime, God holds him as a treasure. Whoever loves you after your death, God seals for him security and faith. Whoever dies loving you has shed his sins as he departs, and whoever dies hating you dies the death of the ignorant, and will be called to account for what he did in Islam.

Abdullah ibn Shaddad narrated that a delegation came to the Messenger of God from Yemen. He said to them: Establish the prayer and pay the zakat, or I will send to you a man like myself who will fight your warriors, enslave your children, and take your wealth. Then he took hold of Ali.

Safiyyah bint Shaybah narrated, on the authority of Ibn Anas, that the Messenger of God addressed the people of Ta'if and said: O people of Ta'if, establish the prayer and pay the zakat, or I will send to you a man like myself who will strike you with the sword. He then took Ali's hand and raised it. 'Umar said: This is enough — this is for virtue.

Muhammad ibn Humayd narrated an occasion on which the Messenger of God was saying: If the Banu Wali'ah do not desist, I will send against them a man like myself who will kill their fighters and take their women and children captive. 'Umar said to Abu Dharr: O Abu Dharr, whom does he mean? Abu Dharr answered — and the Messenger of God could hear him say it — It does not concern you, O 'Umar, nor your companion. He means the owner of the sandal.

Three separate occasions, three separate audiences, and one sentence common to all of them. The threat is not that the Messenger of God will come; it is that he will send someone who is like him. That formula is doing precise work. A messenger cannot be duplicated — the previous paragraph has just finished saying prophecy is sealed — so the likeness being asserted is not in office but in what the man will do and by what authority he will do it. And in two of the three reports the sentence is not left to interpretation. The speaker finishes it by reaching out and taking hold of a particular person in front of the audience he has just threatened.

The third report is the one where nobody reaches for anything, and it is the most pointed. A companion asks who is meant. The answer comes from Abu Dharr, given loudly enough that the Messenger of God can hear it and does not correct it, and it refuses to use a name. It also refuses to grant the questioner standing to ask: this does not concern you, nor the man you are standing with. Then, instead of a name, a description — the owner of the sandal — which everyone present could resolve and which the record preserves unresolved.

The next report changes register entirely. It comes through Malik ibn Anas, from a man who served in the household.

I was the servant of the Prophet. He called me to bring water for ablution, so I came to him. He performed the ablution, then prayed two cycles, then called me again and said: O Anas, there will enter upon you now the Commander of the Faithful, the Master of the Muslims, the Best of the Guardians, and the First of all people to believe.

Anas said: I said to myself, O God, let him be one of the Ansar. And then someone knocked at the door. I opened it, and it was Ali ibn Abi Talib.

Two details in that are worth slowing over. The first is the announcement itself: four titles, given before the door opens, to a servant who has not been told who is coming. The second is the servant's private wish, which the history preserves without comment or apology. He hears the titles, he does not know whose they are, and he silently hopes they will land on his own people. They do not. The report is being transmitted by the man who wished it, and he keeps the wish in.

What follows is stranger still.

The Prophet rose to receive him and wiped his own face with his hand, then wiped Ali's face with that same hand. Ali's eyes filled with tears and he said: O Messenger of God, has something been revealed? I have never seen you do anything like this for me before.

He said to him: What I have done to you is so that you may hear my voice, relieve me of my burden, and make clear to the people the matters over which they will differ after me. And it shall be made clear to the people what they differ about after me.

The history attaches a verse to that: We have not sent down the Book to you except that you may make clear to them that wherein they differ (chapter 16, verse 64). The verse defines the Messenger's own task as clarification of disputed matters, and the history's reading is that the gesture at the door transferred exactly that task, and nothing wider. The three things named are a set. Hear my voice. Lift what I am carrying. Settle for the people the questions they are going to fight about once I am not here to settle them.

Notice also that Ali does not understand it. He assumes something has been revealed, because nothing in the relationship up to that moment has prepared him for it, and he says so. The report keeps his bewilderment on the record alongside the answer.

The last of these reports comes from Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman.

The Messenger of God came to us one day carrying al-Hasan and al-Husayn, one on each arm, and said: These two are the best of people in father and mother. Their father is Ali ibn Abi Talib, the brother of the Messenger of God, his minister, his guardian, his cousin, and his successor after him — and the first among men in all the worlds to believe in God and His Messenger. Their mother, Fatimah, the daughter of the Messenger of God, is the best woman of the two worlds.

These two are the best of people in grandfather and grandmother. Their grandfather is the Messenger of God, and their grandmother was among the first to believe in God.

These two are the best of people in paternal uncle and paternal aunt. Their uncle is Ja'far al-Tayyar, who is in Paradise. Their aunt, Umm Hani bint Abi Talib, never associated anything with God, not even for the blink of an eye.

These two are the best of people in maternal uncle and maternal aunt. Their maternal uncle is al-Qasim, son of the Messenger of God, and their maternal aunt is Zaynab, daughter of the Messenger of God.

That is a genealogy delivered as a speech, and it is built as a grid — father and mother, grandfather and grandmother, uncle and aunt on one side, uncle and aunt on the other, eight positions filled by name. It is being recited over two small boys who are being held up while it is said. The history closes the sequence by noting that reports of this kind are numerous, and that they come both from those closest to the Prophet and from the wider community — which is the claim it will lean on hardest in the section that follows.

## The unshared virtues

The history now hands the argument over to Ali himself, and it does so through a scene rather than a treatise.

Some people came to him and said: O Commander of the Faithful, tell us the greatest of your virtues. He replied: My greatest virtues are those in which I played no part myself. They said: And what are those?

That answer sets the terms for everything that follows. He is not being modest. He is applying a criterion, and the criterion is that a distinction you produced is evidence about you, whereas a distinction that was produced around you while you did nothing is evidence about the one who produced it. Four episodes follow, and in every one of them he is the object rather than the actor.

The first is the doors, and this volume told that story in its place, among the events of the arrival in Madinah. It is not retold here. The order came through Gabriel to close every door that opened from a companion's house into the new mosque and to leave one open; Mu'adh ibn Jabal carried it round; the objection was raised by the Prophet's own uncles; and the answer given was a refusal of authorship, sworn twice, resting on a house of purification set apart under an earlier prophet whose residents God had named rather than the prophet living in it.

What is new is not the incident but where it now sits. In its first telling it was one event among many in a season of arrivals and buildings. Here it has been selected — by the man it favoured, under a rule he announced before he began — and it satisfies that rule at every point. He did not request the door. He did not defend it when the complaint came. He is not the one who answered the complaint. Every actor in that story is somebody else, which is precisely why it heads his list.

They said: Tell us more. He said:

The Messenger of God received a delegation of Christian scholars from Najran, and they disputed with him concerning Jesus. Then God sent down upon him: Truly, the likeness of Jesus in the sight of God is as the likeness of Adam, whom He created from dust (chapter 3, verse 59), to the end of the verse. The Messenger of God then went in and took by the hand himself, al-Hasan, al-Husayn and Fatimah, and went out to perform the mutual invocation. He raised his palm to the sky and interlaced his fingers with theirs, and invited the delegation to it. When the two leading men of the delegation saw him, one said to the other: By God, if he is a prophet, we are destroyed; and if he is not a prophet, his own people will suffice against us. So they held back and withdrew.

They said: Tell us more. He said:

The Messenger of God sent Abu Bakr with the declaration of acquittal to the people at the season of the pilgrimage, to recite it to them. Then Gabriel descended and said: O Muhammad, none may convey this on your behalf except Ali. So the Messenger of God called me and commanded me to ride his she-camel, to ride after Abu Bakr, to take the declaration from him, and to recite it to the people in Mecca. Abu Bakr said: Is this a reproach against me? I said: No, but it was revealed to him that only a man of his household should convey it.

When we reached Mecca — it was the day of sacrifice in the afternoon, the Day of the Greater Pilgrimage — I stood when the people had gathered and said: Am I not a messenger of the Messenger of God to you? Then I recited to them: A declaration of acquittal from God and His Messenger to those polytheists with whom you made a covenant — travel freely in the land for four months (chapter 9, verses 1 and 2) — twenty days of Dhu al-Hijjah, then Muharram, Safar, Rabi' al-Awwal, and the tenth of Rabi' al-Thani. And I said: No one shall circumambulate the House naked; no polytheist, male or female, shall do so — except those who had a covenant with the Messenger of God, whose covenant stands for those four months.

They said: Tell us more. He said:

I was in the Sacred Mosque with al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib and 'Uthman ibn Shaybah. 'Uthman boasted, saying: The Messenger of God entrusted me with the keys of the House. Al-'Abbas said: The Messenger of God entrusted me with the watering — that is, Zamzam. And they said: He gave you nothing, O Ali. Then God sent down: Do you consider the watering of the pilgrim and the upkeep of the Sacred Mosque equal to those who believe in God and the Last Day and strive in the cause of God? They are not equal in the sight of God. God does not guide the wrongdoing people. Those who believed, emigrated, and strove for the cause of God with their wealth and their persons are far greater in rank with God — and it is they who are the triumphant. Their Lord gives them glad tidings of mercy from Him, and His pleasure, and gardens in which there is for them everlasting bliss, abiding therein forever. Truly with God is a great reward (chapter 9, verses 19 to 22).

They asked once more, and the answer was the proclamation at the watering place on the road home from the last pilgrimage — which this volume gave in full in the previous episode, and which the history repeats here only as the last item on the list.

Now look at what those four have in common, because the criterion he set at the start holds all the way through. In the first, doors are closed by an order he did not give and does not defend. In the second, he is taken by the hand and led out. In the third, a mission already assigned to someone else is taken off that person and handed to him mid-journey by an instruction from outside the situation. In the fourth, two men boast about offices they hold, he says nothing at all, and a verse arrives that reorders the ranking. He is never the one who acts. That is the point he made before he began.

The history then argues each one.

On the doors: whoever had a door closed was cut off by God and His Messenger from that particular nearness, and it would be inconceivable for the Messenger of God to do such a thing without purpose or in vain. The argument runs on the impossibility of a pointless act. If the closures meant nothing, the order was arbitrary; if they meant something, then so did the exception.

On the mutual invocation: the verse of it is Say: Come, let us call upon our sons and your sons, our women and your women, and ourselves and yourselves — then let us pray earnestly and invoke the curse of God upon the liars (chapter 3, verse 61). The history observes that numerous narrations, with no dispute or ambiguity among them, place this verse at that encounter, and that the pronoun in the phrase and ourselves refers to the guardian. The conclusion drawn is a strong one: that Ali was, in a real sense, as the self of the Prophet, because that is the word under which God summoned him to stand there.

The argument turns on a count. The verse names three categories to be brought — sons, women, selves — and the report says four people went out with him. Two boys answer to the first category. One woman answers to the second. That leaves one man and one unfilled slot, and the slot is the one the verse describes with the word for oneself. The history's reading is therefore not an inference about merit; it is an assignment of a pronoun that has to land on somebody. It is also worth noticing what the whole episode was for. This was not a demonstration staged for the community. It was an offer made to visiting opponents in a doctrinal dispute — that both sides call down a curse on whichever of them is lying — and it required each party to bring what it valued most into the range of the curse. The delegation looked at what he brought and declined. Their reasoning, preserved in their own words, is entirely practical: if he is a prophet we are finished, and if he is not, his own people will deal with him without our help. They did not test the claim. They calculated the downside and withdrew.

On the declaration of acquittal: the history quotes the reason given at the time — none can deliver it on my behalf except a man of my own household — and then records the objection the Shi'a raised against those who advanced Abu Bakr. The man who was not permitted to complete the recitation of that chapter of the Quran: how is he then fit to succeed the Messenger of God? The history calls this one of the clearest arguments, and one that cannot be deflected by anyone whose faculties have not been sealed against it.

Two features of that episode are worth separating out, because the history leans on both. The first is that the recall was not a rebuke, and the record says so in the words of the man it happened to. Abu Bakr asks whether he is being reproached and is told plainly that he is not; the reason given is a restriction on who may carry this particular text, not a judgement on who was carrying it. The second is that the restriction is stated as a rule about household membership rather than about competence, rank or seniority — only a man of his own house may convey it — which is why the history treats the episode as evidence rather than as an administrative detail. And the content that was conveyed matters to the argument too. What was recited in Mecca on the day of sacrifice was the dissolution of standing treaties, a four-month period of safe passage counted out month by month, and a ban on circling the House unclothed and on any polytheist performing the rite at all. It was, in other words, the announcement that ended one settlement and imposed another — the single most consequential public statement of that year.

Two further reports close the section. Al-Baqir, Muhammad ibn Ali, was asked about the words Is one who stands upon a clear proof from his Lord, and a witness from him follows him? (chapter 11, verse 17), and said: The one who stands upon a clear proof from his Lord is the Messenger. And the witness from him who follows him is Ali — reciting as an Imam after him, and as an argument against those of his community who have deviated from him. And from the Messenger of God: Ali is from me and I am from him, and he is the guardian of every believer after me.

Then the history stops itself. Any one of these virtues alone would be sufficient, it says, since none of them is shared by anyone else. And it adds that if everything transmitted on the matter were set down, this book could not contain it.

## Envy and the unwritten document

Here the narrative resumes, and the first thing it does is name a motive.

When the Prophet repeatedly designated Ali ibn Abi Talib as his guardian and declared that guardianship obligatory, certain people among his companions became consumed with envy. They conspired to refute what God and His Messenger had established and to cut off what he had been commanded to convey, and they agreed among themselves that prophecy and guardianship should not both reside in Bani Hashim. They said: We are the first and the foremost.

The history sets a verse beside that: And there were in the city nine factions who were spreading corruption in the land, and they would not reform. They swore by God: We will attack him and his family by night, and then we will say to his avenger: We did not witness the destruction of his family, and we are truthful (chapter 27, verses 48 and 49). The verse is about an earlier prophet's town. It is being applied because of its shape — a fixed number of men, an agreement sworn to, a night attack on a household, and a prepared denial to give afterwards.

One name is attached. The Messenger of God cursed a number of them, and among those cursed was Abu Musa Abdullah ibn Qays al-Ash'ari. Years later, in Kufa, while Abu Musa was discouraging people from fighting alongside the Commander of the Faithful, 'Ammar ibn Yasir said to him: I heard the Messenger of God curse you, O Abu Musa. Abu Musa replied: That did happen, but he forgave me afterward. 'Ammar said: I witnessed the curse. I did not witness the forgiveness.

That exchange is worth reading twice. The charge is not denied. What is offered instead is a second event, unwitnessed, that is said to cancel the first — and the reply refuses it on the plainest possible ground: one of these two things I saw with my own eyes, and the other I did not.

Then Abu Dharr. He learned what had been secretly agreed — to betray the Messenger of God and his family if they found the means, and, if they could not act themselves, to bequeath that betrayal as a charge to those who came after them. He went and told the Prophet, who confronted them; they denied it in the most vehement terms and swore that they had said nothing of the sort, in the open or in private. The Prophet said to Abu Dharr: By God, were it not that a sign from heaven has come down to me confirming the truth of your words, I would not have believed you over them.

Then God revealed: They swear by God that they said nothing, yet they did say the word of disbelief; they disbelieved after their Islam, and they aspired to something they could not attain (chapter 9, verse 74). And the Prophet said: I have never seen, under green sky or upon brown earth, anyone more truthful in speech than Abu Dharr.

The sequence there is unusual and the history leaves it intact. A man brings a report; the accused deny it under oath; and the Prophet tells the informant to his face that the oaths would have carried against him. Only revelation reverses it. The praise that follows is issued afterwards, not before.

Against that background the last order of the reign is given. The Messenger of God returned to Medina and spent the rest of Dhu al-Hijjah and the month of Muharram there, and then dispatched Usama ibn Zayd to the Levant, commanding him to set his horsemen on the borders of Balqa and Darum in Palestine, so that the people would be prepared and provisioned and no one would lag behind Usama's army. Among those he sent out with him were Abu Bakr, 'Umar and 'Uthman ibn 'Affan. And he said: Dispatch the army of Usama. May God curse those who fall behind.

And then the illness begins.

According to what Ibn Hisham reported, the Messenger of God fell ill in Safar, or at the start of Rabi' al-Awwal. On the first day he felt unwell he went out at the end of the night to Baqi' al-Gharqad, and when he reached the graves he prayed forgiveness for those buried there and said: Peace be upon you, O people of the graves. May you be congratulated for the state you have entered, compared with what the people now face — trials advancing like the dark waves of the night, each one worse than the last.

He then turned to those present and said: I was given the treasuries of this world and an abiding place within it, and then Paradise — and I was given the choice between that and the meeting of my Lord. And he said: So I chose the meeting of my Lord and Paradise. Then he prayed forgiveness for the people of al-Baqi' and departed.

His pain grew from that day. The history records a second account of the cause: that his death traced back to the poison prepared for him in a sheep's foreleg in the days of Khaybar, something of which had remained in him and now made itself known. He cried out: I am poisoned. The same illness took the sister of Bishr ibn al-Bara' ibn Ma'rur, and it was said to her that the time had come for her to find the artery severed by the food she had eaten with her brother at Khaybar. On this account the Muslims hold that the Prophet died a martyr, honoured by God with the rank of prophethood alongside it.

Now the orders start failing.

When the illness grew severe, the army that had gone out with Usama — who had taken them no more than a league beyond the city — turned back. They abandoned the command of the Messenger of God and returned with their commander alone, paying no heed to what he had said about dispatching them and about those who fall behind. Among those who came back were Abu Bakr, 'Umar, Abu 'Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah and others. The history states plainly what it takes the purpose of the order to have been: that all of them should be out of the city together, so that the matter of the guardian would be settled without dispute.

Then the prayer. Abu Bakr led the people, and when the Messenger of God heard his call to begin — loudly given — he came out, supported between Ali ibn Abi Talib and his uncle al-'Abbas, whereupon Abu Bakr stepped back and the Prophet led the people himself. The history notes that the community differed greatly over this episode, and then gives the account it holds sound, as transmitted from the people of the household:

When the Messenger of God was at the height of his illness, Bilal came to give the call to prayer. Aisha said: The Messenger of God has fainted — do not disturb him. Tell Abu Bakr to lead the people in prayer. So word was sent, and he came forward and led it. The Messenger of God heard the sound of his call and said: What is this? He was told: Abu Bakr is leading the people in prayer. He said: You are the companions of Yusuf. He then took Ali's hand, leaned upon him, went out, and removed Abu Bakr from the prayer, and led the people himself.

The whole weight of a later argument rests on this scene, and the history answers that argument directly. The claim advanced in favour of Abu Bakr is that whoever the Prophet appointed to lead the prayer must be given precedence over the whole community. Three replies are given. The narrations of what happened differ, and no consensus exists on them, so the episode cannot be treated as decisive. Those who make the argument themselves permit both the righteous and the immoral to lead prayer, and allow prayer behind anyone — which drains the office of the significance the argument needs it to carry. And the Prophet had prayed alongside Abu Bakr and 'Amr ibn al-'As during the campaign of Dhat al-Salasil, and had placed Usama ibn Zayd in command over both Abu Bakr and 'Umar, with one of them praying behind him. Leading a prayer, on the record of the same people making the claim, does not establish precedence.

Two short reports from inside the sickroom follow. Al-A'mash transmitted from Abu Ayyub al-Ansari that when Fatimah came in and saw the state her father was in, she wept, and he said to her: O my daughter, do you not know that God looked upon the people of the earth with a look of knowledge, then chose from among them a companion for you, and revealed to me that I should marry you to him? And Ja'far ibn Muhammad said that when the Messenger of God was in the throes of death he fell into a faint, and Fatimah woke and wept, saying: Who will there be for us after you, O Messenger of God? And he said: After me, the weak will be honoured.

And then the last of the failed orders, and the one the history treats as the largest.

Muhammad ibn Isma'il al-Bukhari narrated from Abdullah ibn Abbas: When the Prophet was in the illness from which he died, he said: Bring me writing materials, and I will write you a document so that you will never go astray after me. 'Umar said: The Messenger of God has been overcome by pain. We have the Book of God — it is sufficient for us. Those present fell to disputing, and the noise grew. The Prophet said: Get up and leave me — disputing in my presence is not fitting. Ibn Abbas said: The calamity — all of it, every bit of it — was what came between us and the writing of the Messenger of God.

The history sets one verse against that: O you who believe, do not raise your voices above the voice of the Prophet, and do not speak loudly to him as you speak loudly to one another, lest your deeds be rendered void while you are unaware (chapter 49, verse 2). The point is not the refusal of the pen so much as the noise. A room was asked for writing materials and produced an argument instead, loud enough that the man who had asked told everyone to get out.

## What this episode lands

The first two-thirds of this episode are a file, not a story. The history stops narrating, assembles what it regards as the evidence, and lays it down in two layers. The outer layer is what other people heard: a threat issued three times in three places, each time promising to send a man like himself, and twice completed by taking hold of a particular hand in front of the audience; a servant told four titles before a door opened and quietly hoping they belonged to somebody else; a face wiped with a hand and a task assigned in three parts, the last of which is to settle after his death the questions his community was going to differ over; and two boys held up while eight relationships were named over them.

The inner layer is what Ali said when he was asked directly, and the striking thing is the criterion he chose. He would not count anything he had done. Doors closed by an order he neither gave nor justified. A hand taken and led out to a mutual invocation while a delegation watched and decided not to go through with it. A mission removed from one man on the road and given to another, with the reason stated aloud so that nobody could read it as a rebuke. A boast about the keys and a boast about the well, answered not by him but by a verse that ranked belief and struggle above both. Four occasions, and on none of them is he the one who acts.

Then the narrative resumes and the tone changes completely. The history names envy as the motive, quotes a verse about nine men who swore to attack a household by night and to have their denial ready afterwards, and records one man who admitted the curse he had received and offered an unwitnessed pardon against it. And then, inside the space of a few weeks: an army ordered to Palestine with three named men in it, which travelled about a mile and came home; a fever that began at a graveyard before dawn, with its owner congratulating the dead on what they had missed and announcing which of two offers he had accepted; an old poisoning from Khaybar surfacing to finish what it started; a prayer led by a man who was told to lead it by someone in the household while the Prophet lay unconscious, and who was removed from it as soon as the Prophet could stand; and finally a request for writing materials, made in order to leave behind something that would prevent the community going astray, which was met with the observation that the Book was enough and then buried under an argument.

One of the men in that room transmitted the account himself, and gave his own verdict on it in a sentence that does not distribute blame or name anyone: that the whole calamity was what came between them and the writing.
