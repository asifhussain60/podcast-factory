The editor introduces *Foundations of Inner Interpretation* — the foundational Ismaili treatise on inner interpretation, written by a Fatimid judge who served the dynasty in the tenth century and presented for publication by the editor in Beirut in 1960. The introduction does three things at once. It explains why the work was held so long in private custody and why its disclosure is unusual. It sets out what the Ismailis mean by inner interpretation, and how that word differs from outward commentary — the more familiar word for Qur'anic exegesis. And it sketches the intellectual world in which the author worked: a tradition that anchored the apparent law in one of his books and the inner truth in this one, that traced its science of interpretation back through Alexandria and forward through the Fatimid court at Cairo, and that insisted, with unusual force for its time, that no one truly believes who does not also understand.

The editor opens with a confession. He says he hesitated a long time before sending this book to print. The reason was that he wished to keep it longer in the cave of prudent concealment — alongside the other Ismaili manuscripts that have not yet been published. He also believed his information about the book was not yet complete, and that other Ismaili manuscripts, lesser in rank but indispensable as scientific preliminaries to it, gateways through which one must pass before approaching it, still needed to appear in print before it. So he delayed it.

The image of "the cave of concealment" is worth pausing over. In the Ismaili intellectual order, certain doctrinal materials are not held back because they are embarrassing. They are held back because the doctrine itself teaches that not every truth is fit for every ear at every moment. A truth disclosed to the wrong audience at the wrong stage of training can be heard as nonsense, or worse, as scandal. So the books wait. Some of them wait centuries. The editor is telling us that this book has been waiting, and that what he is about to do — print it in Beirut in 1960 — is a small departure from the usual discipline. He explains it by saying: now, whenever the word inner interpretation comes to mind, only one book presents itself to him, and this is the book.

He calls it the single book in the Ismaili manuscript corpus that takes up the subject of interpretation as its certain theme. It is the precious work, he says, that lays out the basic idea of this science in a measured and balanced way, casting light on its sections and materials so as to bring them near to the mind and lower them within reach of thought. Interpretation is called inner interpretation, he says, because it is a return — a movement back to the outcome and to the origin of a thing: interpreting first what is and what is not, then bringing the matter back and resolving it, so that the fate of the words is their meaning and their substance.

Already we are in the middle of the central claim. Interpretation, in this tradition, is not the addition of one more layer to a text. It is a return: a movement back through the words to the meaning the words were sent to carry. The words have a fate, the editor says, and that fate is their meaning.

This is why, in the early Ismaili mission in its flourishing, inner interpretation was the primary theme of every esoteric philosophical idea. It was the foundation upon which the pillars of this intellectual movement rested, the nourishment by which esoteric philosophy fed its judgment, its logic, and its exposition. For all of this, the book *Foundations of Inner Interpretation* is counted among the Ismailis as one of the precious books — a treasury whose doctrinal teachings demand the preservation of secrecy and whose materials must be guarded from leaking to anyone who is not Ismaili. All of it, the editor says plainly, is regarded as the secret of the creed and the key to the door of the mission.

That instinct toward layered disclosure has a long pedigree the editor does not pause to argue for. The Father of Imams is reported in his collected sayings to have said: "Indeed, the Book of God carries four registers — its expression, its allusion, its subtle indication, and its inner reality; the expression is for the common people, the allusion for the elect, the subtle indication for the friends of God, and the inner reality for the prophets." That fourfold ladder, recorded in the eleventh-century collection *Brilliant Aphorisms*, edited by the historian al-Amidi and rendered into English in modern editions, is the structural assumption the editor is operating from. There is a public meaning. There is an interior. And there are stations between them.

Added to this, the book contains an interpretation of the stories of the prophets that appear in the three heavenly books: the Torah, the Gospel, and the Quran. This too constitutes a subject whose secrecy the doctrine demands be preserved, lest it pass beyond the circle of the general community, who, in the editor's frank phrase, are held to have grasped only the husks of culture and only the outward appearances of science.

Now the editor turns to the definition that matters most for a first-time reader: the difference between two words that the wider Islamic literature often runs together. Inner interpretation in its realistic Ismaili sense, he says, differs from outward commentary in its conventional sense among the other Islamic sects. Outward commentary means clarifying the meaning of any obscure word whose sense the reader does not understand. If we are asked, for example, "What is the commentary on the word tree?" we answer: "It is a plant that is set down small, then grows, and from it limbs branch out upon which green leaves grow, and in spring it bears flowers that turn into good fruit." That is outward commentary: clarification at the level of the dictionary, the picture, the ordinary sense.

But if we are asked, "What is the inner interpretation of the word tree?" the answer follows the view of the one giving the interpretation: he may say it is a chamber, or a cow, or a rock, or anything else — provided it is in accord with truth, with reality, and with reason, that it is not foreign to belief, nor distant from sound thought. Inner interpretation, the editor concludes, is the essence of the meaning, its symbol, or its inner reality — a truth hidden behind a word that does not openly signify it.

Two things follow. The first is structural. There has to be an authority who knows what the tree actually is. The reader, holding only the word, cannot generate this answer by his own dictionary. He needs a teacher who stands in a chain that goes back to the moment when the meaning was placed inside the word. The second is doctrinal. The Ismaili intellectual order, the editor explains, assigns the authority of revelation to the Speaker-Prophet. And it assigns the authority of inner interpretation to the Imam. The first represents the sacred law — rulings, jurisprudence, the law of the outward, what they call the apparent. The second represents the truth, the interpretation, the philosophy, the inward — what they call the hidden.

This pairing — the Speaker-Prophet and the Imam, revelation and interpretation, the apparent and the hidden — is the structural spine of the entire book. The six chapters that follow this introduction are organized by Speaker-Prophet, and each Speaker is paired with the figure who carried the inner meaning of his message. The Prophet was the bearer of the revelation of the Quran in its words and outward sense for the people at large, the editor says, while its mystical secrets were entrusted to the Father of Imams. The bearer of the outward speaks to everyone. The bearer of the inward speaks to those whose insight has been opened. They are two faces of the same revelation. Neither is complete without the other.

The editor then walks the reader through the Qur'anic verses the Ismailis take as proof that interpretation is not optional but obligatory. He quotes them in turn. "Thus your Lord shall choose you and teach you the interpretation of events" — spoken to Joseph, from verse six of the chapter on Joseph. "And thus We established Joseph in the land, that We might teach him the interpretation of events" — verse twenty-one of the same chapter. "I shall inform you of the interpretation of that which you could not bear patiently" — verse seventy-eight of the chapter on the cave, where a strange teacher tells a frustrated student that what looked like cruelty was mercy hidden inside cruelty. The translations here follow the standard rendering by A. J. Arberry in *The Koran Interpreted* (Macmillan, 1955).

And then the central verse, the one the whole tradition turns on — verse seven of the chapter on the family of Imran. "He it is who has sent down to you the Book, in which are verses precisely defined — they are the foundation of the Book — and others ambiguous. As for those in whose hearts is deviation, they pursue what is ambiguous of it, seeking discord and seeking its interpretation, though none knows its interpretation save Allah and those firmly grounded in knowledge. They say: 'We believe in it; the whole of it is from our Lord.' But none take heed except those of understanding."

The verse cuts both ways. It says that some verses have an inner interpretation, and that the wrong people seek it for the wrong reasons. It also says that there are people — "those firmly grounded in knowledge" — who know that inner interpretation. The Ismaili reading takes that phrase to name a specific class of teachers, not a general intellectual virtue. The class begins with the Speaker-Prophet and continues, in each cycle, through the Imam-Foundation, who carries the inner meaning of what the Speaker delivered. The scholar Farhad Daftary, in *The Ismailis: Their History and Doctrines*, second edition (Cambridge University Press, 2007), describes this Ismaili identification of "those firmly grounded in knowledge" with the line of Imams as "the cornerstone of their entire hermeneutic" — page one hundred thirty-seven — noting that the reading turns on whether one places a grammatical pause after "save Allah" or after "those firmly grounded in knowledge," and that the Ismaili tradition adopts the second reading without hesitation.

He moves to the rational proofs. "We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and within themselves" — verse fifty-three of the chapter that opens with the letters meaning the Detailed Exposition. And: "And in the earth are signs for those certain in faith — and in your own selves: do you not see?" — verses twenty and twenty-one of the chapter on the scattering winds. The structure of both verses is the same. There are signs out there in the world; there are signs inside the human person; the two answer to each other. The apparent, the editor says, was created in order to point to the subtle. The great supporter of religion among the Ismailis, an eleventh-century thinker who served as chief preacher in the Fatimid court at Cairo, put the matter directly: "Allah has made parables and likenesses: the human body is a likeness, and the soul is a likeness; this world is a likeness, and the Hereafter is a likeness." He also said: "By the parable I mean a parable struck — like the bee's needles, and what comes from it like honey."

The image of the bee is worth slowing down on. A bee has needles, painful things, sharp things. But the bee's reason for being is not the needle. The bee's reason for being is the honey. If you are not careful, the needles are all you encounter. But the honey is what was being made all along. Outward commentary attends to the needles — to the words, the grammar, the apparent legislation. Inner interpretation extracts the honey.

Here the editor steps back and frames the Ismaili science of interpretation in world-historical terms. All of this, he says, carries us to the city of Alexandria and to its philosophical school — to what Philo and his disciples said when they tried to interpret the Torah esoterically but did not succeed. Augustine and his disciples then tried to follow that school in interpreting the Gospel esoterically, but they too could not bring the idea to completion. Then came the Ismailis, who spread their system of thought; and this was the first curriculum they put forward — a system of interpretation. They refined it with their own ideas and added to it a rational interpretation that pruned bare transmission and produced conclusive proof for the Islamic world.

This is a strong claim. The editor is positioning the Ismaili tradition as the only one in the long history of esoteric scriptural reading that actually finished the project Philo and Augustine had begun. Whether or not one accepts the claim in full, the comparative point is well attested in modern scholarship. Paul E. Walker, in *Early Philosophical Shiism: The Ismaili Neoplatonism of Abu Yaqub al-Sijistani* (Cambridge University Press, 1993), pages five through twelve, traces how the Ismaili thinkers of the tenth century drew on Alexandrian Neoplatonism — through Arabic translations of Plotinus and Proclus — to construct what he calls "a complete philosophical theology in which scripture and reason are not merely compatible but mutually constituting." The editor's claim, stripped of its triumphalism, names a real intellectual continuity.

The Ismailis, the editor says, are the most ancient in their understanding of Islamic principles. They affirmed the inward and its necessity, just as they affirmed the outward alongside it. The apparent is not accepted without the inward, and the inward is of no use without the apparent — for outward and inward are like body and soul, and benefits arise only from their meeting, through which the ends are known.

Note what is being refused here. The editor is not arguing that the inner sense replaces the outer. He is not saying that, once you know the secret, you can drop the prayer and the fast and the law. He is saying the opposite. A body without a soul is a corpse just as a soul without a body has nothing in this world to do. The Ismaili reader is supposed to do both: keep the law fully and read the law as parable fully. That insistence on both at once is, in the editor's view, what distinguishes the Ismaili school from every esoteric movement before it.

This balance has a Prophetic warrant the editor leaves implicit. The Prophet, peace be upon him, is reported in a well-known narration preserved by the eleventh-century traditionist Ibn Hibban in his collection *The Sound Compendium*, and recorded as well by other early collectors, to have said: "There is no verse in the Quran that does not have an outward and an inward, a limit, and a place of ascent." The outward, the inward, the limit, and the place of ascent — four registers, the same fourfold structure preserved in the Father of Imams' saying quoted earlier. The Ismaili order is not inventing the architecture; it is claiming custody of it.

He returns to the division of labor between the Speaker-Prophet and the Imam-Foundation. Revelation, he says, was specialized in by the Prophet during his lifetime. It is the knowledge of the apparent and of the rulings of the sacred law, to which the public is permitted access. As for the Imam, the foundational figure — and the editor adds the term "Foundation," a designation given to every Imam contemporary with the Speaker-Prophet — he is responsible for the inward, for the interpretation, for the truth and its secret symbols, which may not be disclosed except to the loyal soldiers of the mission who have risen in its ranks. These are, he says, the "specially favored" classes.

For these reasons, the editor continues, the two books *Foundations of Inner Interpretation* and *Pillars of Islam* — both authored by the same Fatimid judge — represent two related and fully interconnected directions: the inward and the outward. The first is the foundation of esoteric philosophy. The second is the foundation of jurisprudence and the sacred law, narrated by the sixth Imam from his grandfather, the Prophet — for the Prophet, as we have said, is the bearer of the sacred law. This shows that Ismaili esoteric studies proceeded by degrees, and that their ranks were divided into ranks through which the respondent must pass.

The phrase "by degrees" matters. This is not a tradition you can drop into. There are stages. You begin with the apparent because the apparent is the gate. You learn the law. You practice the law. You are admitted, by a teacher, to a first interpretation. You are admitted, later, to a second. The classes the editor calls "specially favored" are not a social caste; they are people who have walked some distance.

The Ismailis, the editor adds, were alone in interpreting the verses of the Quran that require interpretation, where other sects content themselves with mere outward commentary. This is plain in the books of the earliest Qur'anic commentators who preceded the great ninth-century exegete whose massive commentary became the reference work of the broader tradition — whose work, the editor adds, is the great scientific tribunal for weighing among Islamic sects. The editor is not dismissing the great tradition of outward commentary. He is saying that outward commentary, even at its best, settles a different question than the one this book is trying to settle. Outward commentary asks what the verse means in plain Arabic. Inner interpretation asks what truth the verse was sent to carry.

It is very difficult, he says, to understand and to reach the end of Ismaili philosophical texts, to grasp their expressions, their symbols, and their aims, unless the reader has first passed through the foundational interpretive texts. There is no doubt that this book is one of the texts that must be studied carefully. This is the editor's apology for publishing the book at all. He is publishing it because, without it, no Ismaili philosophical text can really be read. The basic grammar of inner interpretation lives in this book.

The editor notes that the author wrote many books in jurisprudence, law, rulings, history, and in the science of interpretation — which is considered an introduction to philosophy. He has not observed that the author wrote any books on philosophy itself, and does not know whether he was forbidden to do so. What is known is that another major early Ismaili thinker, contemporary with the author, was directly responsible for that field in that period, and was the only one with permission to present it. None of this, the editor says, prevents us from saying that the author is counted among the great thinking minds who laid the pillars of thought in the Fatimid state. The modern scholar Wilferd Madelung, in *Religious Trends in Early Islamic Iran* (Persian Heritage Foundation, 1988), pages ninety-three through one hundred ten, confirms this division of labor: the author held jurisprudence and outward law, his contemporary held philosophy and inner doctrine, and the Imam-Caliph of their day arbitrated which texts circulated and to whom.

The editor then permits himself a moment of polemic. The Ismailis had their share, indeed the most advanced share, in this field. They won the first prize in the arena of thought, and they enriched the Islamic library with a massive scientific production before the existence of the Fatimid state. Unfortunately, he adds, that precious heritage has been lost, and nothing remains in our hands today except the philosophical encyclopedia known as the *Epistles of the Brethren of Purity*, alone.

This is a loss the editor wants the reader to feel. There is no doubt that systems of interpretation and philosophical-ideological laws prevailed in Ismaili society before the Fatimid state; and we have the right to wonder where those intellectual fruits went, and what the names are of those who produced them. The answer is very difficult. We do not possess from those earlier eras any trace of the Ismailis except the *Epistles of the Brethren of Purity*. It is likely that those antiquities were lost during the raids that fell on the early mission center in Syria — by the Abbasid forces, by another sectarian group that broke away from the main Ismaili mission, and by other parties. For this reason, information about the early Arab poets of the period has also remained scant.

The editor then tells a story that, for him, captures how nearly the entire pre-Fatimid Ismaili archive was lost and how a small portion was rescued. He refers to the biography related by the chamberlain who served the first Fatimid Imam-Caliph, published in the modern era by the Russian orientalist Wladimir Ivanow, concerning the founder of the Fatimid state who established the dynasty in North Africa. Some thieves attacked the founder on his way from the early mission center to North Africa and stripped him of the collection of precious books he had carried with him from the Levant. Later, the second Fatimid Imam-Caliph, once the Fatimids had settled their affairs in North Africa, returned to that very place and recovered the books. The collection — which the founder had himself selected and preferred above gold and precious stones — was of great worth; and there is no doubt that it contained a system of esoteric beliefs, the rules of philosophy, of interpretation, and of other philosophical topics. The German historian Heinz Halm, in his standard history of the rise of the Fatimid state, published by Brill in 1996, pages sixty through ninety, treats this same episode and confirms that the recovered library formed the core textual inheritance of the early Fatimid court.

He sets the period in the largest possible frame. The third and fourth centuries of the Islamic calendar, he says, are among the most splendid of ages for the Arab and Islamic worlds. During these centuries the Ismaili mission flourished in a comprehensive intellectual flowering. The Ismaili preachers worked to transmit much of the sciences of the Persians, of India, and of Greece — after expounding them, adapting them, and refining them — placing the scattered theories in order and applying them in a manner suited to Islamic principles and to reason. To this must be added their firm grip on the apparent of the sacred law — its obligations, its duties, its rulings — joined to the urgent religious desire to hold also to the esoteric worship alongside it. There is no group within Islam, he says, that has so long a record in events as the Ismailis.

He acknowledges the contributions of rivals. The Buyid and Hamdanid dynasties raised the banner of culture and literature high in the Levant, and the Umayyad rulers of the Iberian peninsula made an effective contribution to building the literary renaissance in Andalusia. But all of that does not equal a small part of what the Fatimids contributed in this field, especially during the era of the fourth Fatimid Imam-Caliph, the patron under whom this book was composed. History counts his era as one of the most prosperous periods Islam ever knew. The Imam-Caliph was deeply committed to reviving the scientific renaissance throughout his realm and to strengthening the intellectual system he considered the foundation of his mission. It is not surprising, then, that we find him personally collaborating with the author on the two books that together form the basis of jurisprudence and of philosophy.

The first was meant as the foundation of the apparent law — what they call "practical worship" — namely the *Pillars of Islam*. The second was meant as the foundation of esoteric philosophy, or "scientific worship" — namely the basis of inner interpretation. As the first laid down the law, the rulings, and the body, the second laid down the truth, the philosophy, and the spirit. *Pillars of Islam* is the body the tradition gives the believer. *Foundations of Inner Interpretation* is the soul.

In the era of this Imam-Caliph, the editor adds, culture reached its peak. Cairo became a magnet toward which scholars, writers, philosophers, students, and seekers turned. A celebrated Mamluk-era historian who chronicled Egypt, and other historians, say his library overflowed with books. He delighted in convening scholarly councils, in listening to the debates of the learned, and in encouraging them; he personally supervised authors and their works, ordering changes and refinements.

The author of the book now enters the frame more fully. He was born in Morocco, into a family belonging to one of the great Arab tribes. The date of his birth is not preserved, but he died in Cairo in the year three hundred sixty-three of the Islamic calendar — that is, in the year nine hundred seventy-four of the Common Era — during the imamate of his patron. He is the most famous jurist Ismailism has produced since the dawn of Islam, and among its most productive. He entered the service of the founder of the Fatimid state in the late years of that Imam's life. Then, under the second Fatimid Imam-Caliph, he took charge of the judiciary in Tripoli. When his great patron came to Egypt he brought the author and his family with him.

The editor returns, one final time, to the central theme. This science, inner interpretation, was propagated by the Ismailis among the various classes of Muslims with great care. It is the natural outcome of their foundational learning. In their view, religion required that a person ascend through continuous discipline, rising from one degree to the next, until at last he attained knowledge of the houses of the universe — the inhabited worlds that were cut off after they were separated from the transcendent Creator: that is, from Absolute Non-Anthropomorphic Thought, the First Intellect, the Higher Light, or the Originating Power — the Pen that writes, the First that precedes existence, the one who emits lights from itself toward the Second Place, toward the Second Universal Intellect, or toward the Universal Soul.

These two principles — the First Intellect and the Universal Soul — are the two origins from which everything proceeds, after their transformation in the minds of human beings: the minds of the prophets, the Imams, and the elect. The common classes, the editor says, do not possess such distinct minds; they are destitute of them so long as they have not acquired knowledge or moved to the second station. That movement comes through enlightenment and guidance, and this kind of education has many degrees. If a person walks them, he may reach the ultimate degree of mental and literary perfection that is the highest purpose of human life.

This system of inner interpretation grew in just this way because the Ismailis gave the religious laws and the Qur'anic verses an esoteric interpretation that differed from their outward sense and yet conformed to common sense. At the same time, they did not reject the revealed laws and did not deny what the Quran and the other heavenly books brought. Rather, they affirmed them and acknowledged that their obligations could not be set aside; and they understood what God meant by them, for they were among those whose insight had been opened by knowledge.

From this, the editor concludes, the Ismailis do not operate by apparent interpretation alone. They interpret the laws, the rulings, and the Quran esoterically as well, drawing from a trusted source they consider to be the representative of the Universal Intellect. We may therefore call them rationalists who anchor their beliefs only on these foundations. Beyond that, the Ismaili movement is a universal one — or, more plainly, an intellectual system — whose mission was to overthrow the dominant political order that controlled the Islamic world and to achieve a major upheaval in ideas, systems, and beliefs.

This system required of its adherent that he affirm both the apparent and the subtle principles, for the two principles together constitute the spirit of religion. The apparent is the general framework of recommendations bound up with the rules of religious scholarship; the subtle is the spirit of religion hidden from view, reserved for the dedicated. For this reason the Ismaili preachers proclaimed their principles in two modes. The first concerned the prophecies and the necessity of accepting what they brought — laws, texts, and ordinances — in an apparent way, calling people to follow them in the manner of "obligation and imitation." The second was a call to what the heavenly books had brought through esoteric interpretation, in the manner of "choice, covenant, and pledge."

The two modes carry different relations to the will of the listener. The first asks compliance: accept the law because it is the law. The second asks consent: enter the covenant because you have chosen to enter it, having understood what you are entering. Religion, the editor says, is in a fortified citadel that cannot be breached; one cannot reach what lies behind it except through a real, abiding spiritual meaning — never through gleaming external appearances and superficial teachings that, with the passage of time, become mere habit.

The verses of the Quran, the editor says, were revealed openly, and yet they were not devoid of esoteric meaning, of soul, of essence. The texts of the Quran, or its outward commentary, often lead to differences of opinion, while inner interpretation — the disclosure of the esoteric meaning — is a fixed system that does not shift; it leads to mental reality and to the consensus of society. This is a claim that will sound strange to a reader trained on Sunni hermeneutics, where it is often the inner readings that proliferate and disagree. The Ismaili claim is the reverse. Because the inner sense is delivered through a single chain of authoritative teachers, the inner sense is stable. It is the surface that is unstable, because the surface is read by everyone with a dictionary.

Inner interpretation, the editor repeats, was the specialty of the Imam and his disciples. For this reason we see the Prophet keen to commend to his companions the search for it wherever it may be found. The knowledge of the apparent is very easy to reach; inner interpretation, by contrast, cannot be reached except after struggle, weariness, and the surmounting of obstacles — after passing through trials and entering the ranks of an intellectual system from which one does not emerge without being marked by what they have called "the covenant, the compact, and the oath."

In summary, the editor says, God revealed His religion to humanity and gave it the optional portion by way of following the clearest paths, demonstrating its justice and leaving freedom before man to act in the light of the intellect of his constitution — making that intellect the finest gift bestowed upon him. Islam, in fact, was nothing other than the forbidding of evil and the permitting of the good. Its inner reality is absolute faith — the inward spirit that runs in the blood of every human being.

Inner interpretation is a philosophical and religious theory which may be summarized thus: God Almighty has set all the meanings of religion in the creatures that surround man. One must therefore substitute what is in nature and on the face of the earth, according to one's understanding of the truth of religion, which has divided creation into two categories: an apparent part that is visible and an inward part that is hidden, with the apparent pointing to the inward — like the human body, which is apparent, and the soul, which is inward. What is disclosed from religion is practical worship, and what is set down in the apparent verses of the Quran consists of meanings articulated by the scholars of the people of the apparent and by the common public alike. Yet each of the religious duties also has an esoteric interpretation, which can only be taught by the Imams, by their senior preachers, and by the elect.

The editor closes the doctrinal section of the introduction by addressing reason directly. The creed Islam has brought is not contrary to reason, nor should it conflict with reason — nor, indeed, is it complete if it does not square with reason — particularly since the Islamic religion was born a rational religion capable of development. If we survey the Qur'anic verses and analyze what is mentioned in them in the light of reason, logic, and reality, they teach us that man must think, reflect, look, meditate, consider, and deduce.

Knowledge in Islam, the editor says with conviction, has been made a condition of faith: whoever believes without understanding and knowing has not believed well. True belief is the truth that emerges from guidance, from understanding, and from choice. Islam is a revolution against mental rigidity and a war upon reaction, fanaticism, and constriction. The Islamic creed rests upon argument and evidence, not upon compulsion. It honored the intellect and demanded that it be attentive. It was not acceptable that a person be a deaf machine accepting whatever he was told without ruling on it by his own mind. Islam, he says, awakened the mind to develop its mental and intellectual powers, that it might walk in the field of self-esteem and independence of opinion and move toward freedom of thought.

The introduction now turns to two practical matters before closing. The first is the architecture of the book. The author divided his book into six chapters, the editor says, devoting one chapter to each Speaker-Prophet, the chief of his cycle, his preachers, and his followers. Each chapter is filled with Qur'anic verses concerning their stories, their era, and the events of their time. He begins with the era of Adam and ends with the era of the Prophet. He did not write the chapter concerning the Awaited Imam — the seventh — because his time has not yet come.

The editor says that, in his judgment, it would be very difficult to explain the chapters one by one. Nevertheless, there is one chapter that deserves particular attention: the chapter dealing with Jesus, son of Mary, and the story of his birth without a father, followed by the story of Adam and the meaning of his creation from clay. All of this, he says, is worth reading and reflecting on, for it is innovative in its philosophy of doctrine, rich in ideas, and marked by sound rational maturity. The careful reader will want to slow down when those treatments arrive in the book itself.

The second practical matter is the textual record. The editor relied on two principal copies of the work. One came from a town in the Syrian mountains and served as the principal copy adopted in the present edition. The other came from copies preserved in Yemen, presented to him by a colleague who hosted him during a visit to East Africa in 1956. The editor adds that he has made grammatical and orthographic corrections, restored some sentences that had dropped out, and revised Qur'anic verses that had been corrupted in transmission — all in order to produce a sound text upon which no dust settles.

Before the editor signs off in Beirut in 1960, he offers a small but sharp clarification of terminology. There are scholars, he says, who have not yet reached the point of distinguishing between two terms — the name of the Ismaili tradition and the name of the Fatimid dynasty. To these, he says: we have found no mention of a dynastic name applied to the philosophy itself. The dynastic name should be confined to the family that ruled the Ismailis — namely the line of Imams who held the caliphate in North Africa and Egypt — and has nothing to do with the philosophy itself. The use of the word Fatimid should not extend beyond the Fatimid dynasty during its reign in Morocco and Egypt. The reason is well known: the dynasty wished to draw near to the broader Islamic world, which honors the name of the Prophet's daughter, and to distinguish themselves at the same time from the descendants of the Father of Imams by women other than the Prophet's daughter. So every name applied to literature, philosophy, doctrine, or anything else pertaining to this mission must not stray beyond the scope of the word Ismaili.

With this clarification the introduction closes, signed by the editor at Beirut, Lebanon, in 1960. What follows in the six chapters of the book is the substance the editor has been preparing us to read. Each chapter takes a single Speaker-Prophet and walks through the events of his cycle by way of the Qur'anic verses that report them — not asking what those events were, but asking what those events meant, what truths they were sent to carry, what return the words are making to their substance. The six are Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and the Prophet. The seventh, the Awaited Imam who completes the cycle, is left unwritten because his time has not yet come. The reader who walks through the six in order will, by the end, hold in his hand the basic grammar of Ismaili inner interpretation as the author received it from the imamate of his patron and transmitted it forward.
