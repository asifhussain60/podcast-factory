This chapter covers the editor's introduction to Asas al-Ta'wil (ah-SAAS al-taa-WEEL), the foundational Ismaili (is-maa-EE-lee) treatise on inner interpretation, written by the Fatimid (FA-ti-mid) judge al-Nu'man ibn Hayyun and presented for publication by Arif Tamir in Beirut in 1960. The introduction does three things at once. It explains why the work was held so long in private custody and why its disclosure is unusual. It sets out what the Ismailis mean by inner interpretation — ta'wil — and how that word differs from tafsir (taf-SEER), the more familiar word for Qur'anic exegesis. And it sketches the intellectual world in which al-Nu'man worked: a tradition that anchored the apparent law in one of his books and the inner truth in this one, that traced its science of interpretation back through Alexandria and forward through the Fatimid court at Cairo, and that insisted, with unusual force for its time, that no one truly believes who does not also understand. Listeners who stay with us through this chapter will leave with the conceptual vocabulary they need to walk into the six chapters that follow — Adam, Noah, Ibrahim, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad — without being lost in technical terms.

The editor opens with a confession. He says he hesitated a long time before sending this book to print. The reason was that he wished to keep it longer in the cave of taqiyya (ta-ki-yya) — the cave of prudent concealment — alongside the other Ismaili manuscripts that have not yet been published. He also believed his information about the book was not yet complete, and that other Ismaili manuscripts, lesser in rank but indispensable as scientific preliminaries to it, gateways through which one must pass before approaching it, still needed to appear in print before it. So he delayed it.

The image of "the cave of taqiyya" is worth pausing over. In the Ismaili intellectual order, certain doctrinal materials are not held back because they are embarrassing. They are held back because the doctrine itself teaches that not every truth is fit for every ear at every moment. A truth disclosed to the wrong audience at the wrong stage of training can be heard as nonsense, or worse, as scandal. So the books wait. Some of them wait centuries. The editor is telling us that this book has been waiting, and that what he is about to do — print it in Beirut in 1960 — is a small departure from the usual discipline. He explains it by saying: now, whenever the word inner interpretation comes to mind, only one book presents itself to him, and this is the book.

He calls it the single book in the Ismaili manuscript corpus that takes up the subject of interpretation as its certain theme. It is the precious work, he says, that lays out the basic idea of this science in a measured and balanced way, casting light on its sections and materials so as to bring them near to the mind and lower them within reach of thought. Interpretation is called inner interpretation, he says, because it is a return — an awl — to the outcome and to the origin of a thing: interpreting first what is and what is not, then bringing the matter back and resolving it, so that the fate of the words is their meaning and their substance.

Already we are in the middle of the central claim. Interpretation, in this tradition, is not the addition of one more layer to a text. It is a return: a movement back through the words to the meaning the words were sent to carry. The words have a fate, the editor says, and that fate is their meaning.

This is why, in the earliest da'wa (da-wa) — the early Ismaili mission, in its flourishing — inner interpretation was the primary theme of every esoteric philosophical idea. It was the foundation upon which the pillars of this intellectual movement rested, the nourishment by which esoteric philosophy fed its judgment, its logic, and its exposition. For all of this, Asas al-Ta'wil is counted among the Ismailis as one of the precious books — a treasury whose doctrinal teachings demand the preservation of secrecy and whose materials must be guarded from leaking to anyone who is not Ismaili. All of it, the editor says plainly, is regarded as the secret of the creed and the key to the door of the mission.

Added to this, the book contains an interpretation of the stories of the prophets that appear in the three heavenly books: the Torah, the Gospel, and the Qur'an (kur-AAN). This too constitutes a subject whose secrecy the doctrine demands be preserved, lest it pass beyond the circle of the general community, who, in the editor's frank phrase, are held to have grasped only the husks of culture and only the outward appearances of science.

Now the editor turns to the definition that matters most for a first-time reader, the difference between two words that the wider Islamic literature often runs together. Inner interpretation in its realistic Ismaili sense, he says, differs from tafsir in its conventional sense among the other Islamic sects. Tafsir means clarifying the meaning of any obscure word whose sense the reader does not understand. If we are asked, for example, "What is the tafsir of the word tree?" we answer: "It is a plant that is set down small, then grows, and from it limbs branch out upon which green leaves grow, and in spring it bears flowers that turn into good fruit." That is tafsir: clarification at the level of the dictionary, the picture, the ordinary sense.

But if we are asked, "What is the inner interpretation of the word tree?" the answer follows the view of the one giving the inner interpretation: he may say it is a chamber, or a cow, or a rock, or anything else — provided it is in accord with truth, with reality, and with reason, that it is not foreign to belief, nor distant from sound thought. Inner interpretation, the editor concludes, is the essence of the meaning, its symbol, or its inner reality — a truth hidden behind a word that does not openly signify it.

Two things follow. The first is structural. There has to be an authority who knows what the tree actually is. The reader, holding only the word, cannot generate this answer by his own dictionary. He needs a teacher who stands in a chain that goes back to the moment when the meaning was placed inside the word. The second is doctrinal. The Ismaili intellectual order, the editor explains, assigns the authority of tanzil (tan-ZEEL) — revelation — to "the Speaker," al-Natiq (al-na-tik). And it assigns the authority of inner interpretation to the Imam (i-MAAM). The first represents the sacred law — rulings, jurisprudence, the law of the outward, what they call al-zahir (al-za-hir), the apparent. The second represents the truth, the interpretation, the philosophy, the inward — what they call al-batin (al-ba-tin), the hidden.

This pairing — the Speaker and the Imam, revelation and interpretation, the apparent and the hidden — is the structural spine of the entire book. The six chapters that follow this introduction are organized by Speaker-Prophet, and each Speaker is paired with the figure who carried the inner meaning of his message. Muhammad was the bearer of the revelation of the Qur'an in its words and outward sense for the people at large, the editor says, while its mystical secrets were entrusted to the Father of Imams. The bearer of the outward speaks to everyone. The bearer of the inward speaks to those whose insight has been opened. They are two faces of the same revelation. Neither is complete without the other.

The editor then walks the reader through the Qur'anic verses the Ismailis take as proof that interpretation is not optional but obligatory. He quotes them in turn. "Thus your Lord shall choose you and teach you the interpretation of events" — spoken to Joseph (YOO-suf). "And thus We established Joseph in the land, that We might teach him the interpretation of events." "I shall inform you of the interpretation of that which you could not bear patiently" — the famous verse from the story of Khidr, where a strange teacher tells a frustrated student that what looked like cruelty was mercy hidden inside cruelty.

And then the central verse, the one the whole tradition turns on. "He it is who has sent down to you the Book, in which are verses precisely defined — they are the foundation of the Book — and others ambiguous. As for those in whose hearts is deviation, they pursue what is ambiguous of it, seeking discord and seeking its interpretation, though none knows its interpretation save Allah and those firmly grounded in knowledge. They say: 'We believe in it; the whole of it is from our Lord.' But none take heed except those of understanding."

The verse cuts both ways. It says that some verses have an inner interpretation, and that the wrong people seek it for the wrong reasons. It also says that there are people — "those firmly grounded in knowledge" — who know that inner interpretation. The Ismaili reading takes that phrase to name a specific class of teachers, not a general intellectual virtue. The class begins with the Speaker-Prophet and continues, in each cycle, through the Imam-Foundation, al-Asas (al-ah-SAAS), who carries the inner meaning of what the Speaker delivered.

He moves to the rational proofs. "We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and within themselves." And: "And in the earth are signs for those certain in faith — and in your own selves: do you not see?" The structure of both verses is the same. There are signs out there in the world; there are signs inside the human person; the two answer to each other. The apparent, the editor says, was created in order to point to the subtle. The great supporter of religion among the Ismailis, al-Mu'ayyad fi al-Din (al-moo-AY-yad fee ad-DEEN), put the matter directly: "Allah has made parables and likenesses: the human body is a likeness, and the soul is a likeness; this world is a likeness, and the Hereafter is a likeness." He also said: "By the parable I mean a parable struck — like the bee's needles, and what comes from it like honey."

The image of the bee is worth slowing down on. A bee has needles, painful things, sharp things. But the bee's reason for being is not the needle. The bee's reason for being is the honey. If you are not careful, the needles are all you encounter. But the honey is what was being made all along. Tafsir attends to the needles — to the words, the grammar, the apparent legislation. Inner interpretation extracts the honey.

Here the editor steps back and frames the Ismaili science of interpretation in world-historical terms. All of this, he says, carries us to the city of Alexandria and to its philosophical school — to what Philo and his disciples said when they tried to interpret the Torah esoterically but did not succeed. Augustine and his disciples then tried to follow that school in interpreting the Gospel esoterically, but they too could not bring the idea to completion. Then came the Ismailis, who spread their system of thought; and this was the first curriculum they put forward — a system of interpretation. They refined it with their own ideas and added to it a rational interpretation that pruned bare transmission and produced conclusive proof for the Islamic world.

This is a strong claim. The editor is positioning the Ismaili tradition as the only one in the long history of esoteric scriptural reading that actually finished the project Philo and Augustine had begun. The Ismailis, he says, are the most ancient in their understanding of Islamic principles. They affirmed the inward and its necessity, just as they affirmed the outward alongside it. The apparent is not accepted without the inward, and the inward is of no use without the apparent — for outward and inward are like body and soul, and benefits arise only from their meeting, through which the ends are known.

Note what is being refused here. The editor is not arguing that the inner sense replaces the outer. He is not saying that, once you know the secret, you can drop the prayer and the fast and the law. He is saying the opposite. A body without a soul is a corpse just as a soul without a body has nothing in this world to do. The Ismaili reader is supposed to do both: keep the law fully and read the law as parable fully. That insistence on both at once is, in the editor's view, what distinguishes the Ismaili school from every esoteric movement before it.

He returns to the division of labor between the Speaker and the Imam-Foundation. Revelation, he says, was specialized in by the Prophet during his lifetime. It is the knowledge of the apparent and of the rulings of the sacred law, to which the public is permitted access. As for the imam, the foundational figure — and the editor adds the phrase "al-asas," a designation given to every Imam contemporary with the Speaker — he is responsible for the inward, for the interpretation, for the truth and its secret symbols, which may not be disclosed except to the loyal soldiers of the da'wa who have risen in its ranks. These are, he says, the "specially favored" classes.

For these reasons, the editor continues, we see that the two books Asas al-Ta'wil and Da'a'im al-Islam (daa-im al-i-slaam) — both authored by al-Nu'man ibn Hayyun — represent two related and fully interconnected directions: the inward and the outward. The first is the foundation of esoteric philosophy. The second is the foundation of jurisprudence and the sacred law, narrated by Imam Ja'far ibn Muhammad al-Sadiq (JAA-far as-SAA-diq) from his grandfather, the Prophet Muhammad — for the Prophet, as we have said, is the bearer of the sacred law. This shows that Ismaili esoteric studies proceeded by degrees, and that their ranks were divided into ranks through which the respondent must pass.

The phrase "by degrees" matters. This is not a tradition you can drop into. There are stages. You begin with the apparent because the apparent is the gate. You learn the law. You practice the law. You are admitted, by a teacher, to a first interpretation. You are admitted, later, to a second. The classes the editor calls "specially favored" are not a social caste; they are people who have walked some distance.

The Ismailis, the editor adds, were alone in interpreting the verses of the Qur'an that require interpretation, where other sects content themselves with mere tafsir. This is plain in the books of the earliest Qur'anic commentators who preceded Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari (mu-HAM-mad ibn ja-REER at-ta-BA-ree) — whose tafsir, he adds, is the great scientific tribunal for weighing among Islamic sects. The editor is not dismissing the great tradition of tafsir. He is saying that tafsir, even at its best, settles a different question than the one al-Nu'man's book is trying to settle. Tafsir asks what the verse means in plain Arabic. Inner interpretation asks what truth the verse was sent to carry.

It is very difficult, he says, to understand and to reach the end of Ismaili philosophical texts, to grasp their expressions, their symbols, and their aims, unless the reader has first passed through the foundational interpretive texts. There is no doubt that Asas al-Ta'wil is one of the texts that must be studied carefully. This is the editor's apology for publishing the book at all. He is publishing it because, without it, no Ismaili philosophical text can really be read. The basic grammar of inner interpretation lives in this book.

The editor notes that al-Nu'man ibn Hayyun wrote many books in jurisprudence, law, rulings, history, and in the science of interpretation — which is considered an introduction to philosophy. He has not observed that al-Nu'man wrote any books on philosophy itself, and does not know whether he was forbidden to do so. What is known is that Ja'far ibn Mansur al-Yaman was directly responsible for that field in that period, and was the only one with permission to present it. None of this, the editor says, prevents us from saying that al-Nu'man is counted among the great thinking minds who laid the pillars of thought in the Fatimid state.

The editor then permits himself a moment of polemic. The Ismailis had their share, indeed the most advanced share, in this field. They won the first prize in the arena of thought, and they enriched the Islamic library with a massive scientific production before the existence of the Fatimid state. Unfortunately, he adds, that precious heritage has been lost, and nothing remains in our hands today except the Rasa'il Ikhwan al-Safa (ra-SAA-il ikh-WAAN as-sa-FAA), the Epistles of the Brethren of Purity, alone.

This is a loss the editor wants the reader to feel. There is no doubt that systems of interpretation and philosophical-ideological laws prevailed in Ismaili society before the Fatimid state; and we have the right to wonder where those intellectual fruits went, and what the names are of those who produced them. The answer is very difficult. We do not possess from those earlier eras any trace of the Ismailis except the Rasa'il Ikhwan al-Safa. It is likely that those antiquities were destroyed at the mission center at Salamiyya (saa-la-mi-yyaa), in Syria, during the raids of the Abbasids, the Qaramita (ka-ra-mi-ta), and other groups. For this reason, information about the poet Abd al-Salam ibn Raghban — known as Dik al-Jinn (DEEK al-JINN), the Rooster of the Jinn — and about al-Mutanabbi (al-mu-ta-NAB-bee), al-Ma'arri (al-maa-AR-ree), and others, has remained scant.

The editor then tells a story that, for him, captures how nearly the entire pre-Fatimid Ismaili archive was lost and how a small portion was rescued. He refers to the biography related by Ja'far al-Hajib (JAA-far al-HAA-jib), published by the orientalist Ivanow, concerning Muhammad al-Mahdi (al-mah-dee) — Ubaydallah (u-ba-yda-llah) — the founder of the Fatimid state in the Maghrib. Some thieves attacked al-Mahdi on his way from Salamiyya to the Maghrib and stripped him of the collection of precious books he had carried with him from the Levant. Later, the Fatimid Imam al-Qa'im (al-QAA-im), once the Fatimids had settled their affairs in the Maghrib, returned to that very place and recovered the books. The collection — which al-Mahdi had himself selected and preferred above gold and precious stones — was of great worth; and there is no doubt that it contained a system of esoteric beliefs, the rules of philosophy, of interpretation, and of other philosophical topics.

He sets the period in the largest possible frame. The third and fourth centuries of the Hijra, he says, are among the most splendid of ages for the Arab and Islamic worlds. During these centuries the Ismaili da'wa flourished in a comprehensive intellectual flowering. The Ismaili preachers worked to transmit much of the sciences of the Persians, of India, and of Greece — after expounding them, adapting them, and refining them — placing the scattered theories in order and applying them in a manner suited to Islamic principles and to reason. To this must be added their firm grip on the apparent of the sacred law — its obligations, its duties, its rulings — joined to the urgent religious desire to hold also to the esoteric worship alongside it. There is no group within Islam, he says, that has so long a record in events as the Ismailis.

He acknowledges the contributions of rivals. The Buyids and the Hamdanids raised the banner of culture and literature high in the Levant, and the Umayyads made an effective contribution to building the literary renaissance in Andalusia. But all of that does not equal a small part of what the Fatimids contributed in this field, especially during the era of Imam al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah (al-moo-IZ lee-DEEN al-LAH). History counts his era as one of the most prosperous periods Islam ever knew. Al-Mu'izz was deeply committed to reviving the scientific renaissance throughout his realm and to strengthening the intellectual system he considered the foundation of his da'wa. It is not surprising, then, that we find him personally collaborating with the da'i (DAA-ee) al-Nu'man on the two books that together form the basis of jurisprudence and of philosophy.

The first was meant as the foundation of the apparent law — what they call "practical worship" — namely Da'a'im al-Islam, the Pillars of Islam. The second was meant as the foundation of esoteric philosophy, or "scientific worship" — namely the basis of inner interpretation. As the first laid down the law, the rulings, and the body, the second laid down the truth, the philosophy, and the spirit. Da'a'im al-Islam is the body the tradition gives the believer. Asas al-Ta'wil is the soul.

In the era of al-Mu'izz, the editor adds, culture reached its peak. Cairo became a Ka'ba (KAA-ba) toward which scholars, writers, philosophers, students, and seekers turned. Al-Maqrizi (al-ma-KREE-zee) and other historians say his library overflowed with books. He delighted in convening scholarly councils, in listening to the debates of the learned, and in encouraging them; he personally supervised authors and their works, ordering changes and refinements.

The author of the book now enters the frame more fully. Al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad ibn Mansur ibn Ahmad ibn Hayyun, the editor says, was born in Morocco, into a family belonging to the Arab tribe of Banu Tamim (ba-nu ta-mim). The date of his birth is not preserved, but he died in Cairo in the year 363 AH, during the imamate of al-Mu'izz. Al-Nu'man is the most famous jurist Ismailism has produced since the dawn of Islam, and among its most productive. He entered the service of Imam Muhammad al-Mahdi in the late years of that Imam's life. Then, under the Fatimid al-Qa'im, he took charge of the judiciary in Tripoli. When the Imam al-Mu'izz came to Egypt he brought al-Nu'man and his family with him.

The editor returns, one final time, to the central theme. This science, inner interpretation, was propagated by the Ismailis among the various classes of Muslims with great care. It is the natural outcome of their foundational learning. In their view, religion required that a person ascend through continuous discipline, rising from one degree to the next, until at last he attained knowledge of the houses of the universe — the inhabited worlds that were cut off after they were separated from the transcendent Creator: that is, from Absolute Non-Anthropomorphic Thought, the First Intellect, the Higher Light, or the Originating Power — the Pen that writes, the First that precedes existence, the one who emits lights from itself toward the Second Place, toward the Second Universal Intellect, or toward the Universal Soul.

These two principles — the First Intellect and the Universal Soul — are the two origins from which everything proceeds, after their transformation in the minds of human beings: the minds of the prophets, the imams, and the elect. The common classes, the editor says, do not possess such distinct minds; they are destitute of them so long as they have not acquired knowledge or moved to the second station. That movement comes through enlightenment and guidance, and this kind of education has many degrees. If a person walks them, he may reach the ultimate degree of mental and literary perfection that is the highest purpose of human life.

This system of inner interpretation grew in just this way because the Ismailis gave the religious laws and the Qur'anic verses an esoteric interpretation that differed from their outward sense and yet conformed to common sense. At the same time, they did not reject the revealed laws and did not deny what the Qur'an and the other heavenly books brought. Rather, they affirmed them and acknowledged that their obligations could not be set aside; and they understood what God meant by them, for they were among those whose insight had been opened by knowledge.

From this, the editor concludes, the Ismailis do not operate by apparent interpretation alone. They interpret the laws, the rulings, and the Qur'an esoterically as well, drawing from a trusted source they consider to be the representative of the Universal Intellect. We may therefore call them rationalists who anchor their beliefs only on these foundations. Beyond that, the Ismaili movement is a universal one — or, more plainly, an intellectual system — whose mission was to overthrow the dominant political order that controlled the Islamic world and to achieve a major upheaval in ideas, systems, and beliefs.

This system required of its adherent that he affirm both the apparent and the subtle principles, for the two principles together constitute the spirit of religion. The apparent is the general framework of recommendations bound up with the rules of religious scholarship; the subtle is the spirit of religion hidden from view, reserved for the dedicated. For this reason the Ismaili preachers proclaimed their principles in two modes. The first concerned the prophecies and the necessity of accepting what they brought — laws, texts, and ordinances — in an apparent way, calling people to follow them in the manner of "obligation and imitation." The second was a call to what the heavenly books had brought through esoteric interpretation, in the manner of "choice, covenant, and pledge."

The two modes carry different relations to the will of the listener. The first asks compliance: accept the law because it is the law. The second asks consent: enter the covenant because you have chosen to enter it, having understood what you are entering. Religion, the editor says, is in a fortified citadel that cannot be breached; one cannot reach what lies behind it except through a real, abiding spiritual meaning — never through gleaming external appearances and superficial teachings that, with the passage of time, become mere habit.

The verses of the Qur'an, the editor says, were revealed openly, and yet they were not devoid of esoteric meaning, of soul, of essence. The texts of the Qur'an, or its tafsir, often lead to differences of opinion, while inner interpretation — the disclosure of the esoteric meaning — is a fixed system that does not shift; it leads to mental reality and to the consensus of society. This is a claim that will sound strange to a reader trained on Sunni hermeneutics, where it is often the inner readings that proliferate and disagree. The Ismaili claim is the reverse. Because the inner sense is delivered through a single chain of authoritative teachers, the inner sense is stable. It is the surface that is unstable, because the surface is read by everyone with a dictionary.

Inner interpretation, the editor repeats, was the specialty of the Imam and his disciples. For this reason we see the Prophet Muhammad (mu-HAM-mad) keen to commend to his companions the search for it wherever it may be found. The knowledge of the apparent is very easy to reach; inner interpretation, by contrast, cannot be reached except after struggle, weariness, and the surmounting of obstacles — after passing through trials and entering the ranks of an intellectual system from which one does not emerge without being marked by what they have called "the covenant, the compact, and the oath."

In summary, the editor says, God revealed His religion to humanity and gave it the optional portion by way of following the clearest paths, demonstrating its justice and leaving freedom before man to act in the light of the intellect of his constitution — making that intellect the finest gift bestowed upon him. Islam, in fact, was nothing other than the forbidding of evil and the permitting of the good. Its inner reality is absolute faith — the inward spirit that runs in the blood of every human being.

Inner interpretation is a philosophical and religious theory which may be summarized thus: God Almighty has set all the meanings of religion in the creatures that surround man. One must therefore substitute what is in nature and on the face of the earth, according to one's understanding of the truth of religion, which has divided creation into two categories: an apparent part that is visible and an inward part that is hidden, with the apparent pointing to the inward — like the human body, which is apparent, and the soul, which is inward. What is disclosed from religion is practical worship, and what is set down in the apparent verses of the Qur'an consists of meanings articulated by the scholars of the people of the apparent and by the common public alike. Yet each of the religious duties also has an esoteric interpretation, which can only be taught by the imams, by their senior preachers, and by the elect.

The editor closes the doctrinal section of the introduction by addressing reason directly. The creed Islam has brought is not contrary to reason, nor should it conflict with reason — nor, indeed, is it complete if it does not square with reason — particularly since the Islamic religion was born a rational religion capable of development. If we survey the Qur'anic verses and analyze what is mentioned in them in the light of reason, logic, and reality, they teach us that man must think, reflect, look, meditate, consider, and deduce.

Knowledge in Islam, the editor says with conviction, has been made a condition of faith: whoever believes without understanding and knowing has not believed well. True belief is the truth that emerges from guidance, from understanding, and from choice. Islam is a revolution against mental rigidity and a war upon reaction, fanaticism, and constriction. The Islamic creed rests upon argument and evidence, not upon compulsion. It honored the intellect and demanded that it be attentive. It was not acceptable that a person be a deaf machine accepting whatever he was told without ruling on it by his own mind. Islam, he says, awakened the mind to develop its mental and intellectual powers, that it might walk in the field of self-esteem and independence of opinion and move toward freedom of thought.

The introduction now turns to two practical matters before closing. The first is the architecture of the book. Al-Nu'man divided his book into six chapters, the editor says, devoting one chapter to each Speaker-Prophet, the chief of his cycle, his preachers, and his followers. Each chapter is filled with Qur'anic verses concerning their stories, their era, and the events of their time. He begins with the era of Adam (AA-dam) and ends with the era of Muhammad. He did not write the chapter concerning the Awaited Imam — the seventh — because his time has not yet come.

The editor says that, in his judgment, it would be very difficult to explain the chapters one by one. Nevertheless, there is one chapter that deserves particular attention: the chapter dealing with 'Isa ibn Maryam (EE-saa ibn MAR-yam) and the story of his birth without a father, followed by the story of Adam and the meaning of his creation from clay. All of this, he says, is worth reading and reflecting on, for it is innovative in its philosophy of doctrine, rich in ideas, and marked by sound rational maturity. Listeners of this series can mark that hint and listen carefully when those chapters arrive.

The second practical matter is the manuscripts themselves. The editor obtained two manuscripts of Asas al-Ta'wil. The first came from the town of Masyaf (MAS-yaaf) in Syria, and it was the principal manuscript adopted in the present edition; it is designated by the letter S, and was copied in 1183 AH. The second came from manuscripts of Yemen, presented to him by his honored brother Hakim Luqman of the town of Kampala in Uganda, East Africa, during his visit to that country in 1956. It was copied in 1033 AH and is designated by the letter e. The editor adds that he has made grammatical and orthographic corrections, restored some sentences that had dropped out, and revised Qur'anic verses that had been corrupted in transmission — all in order to produce a sound text upon which no dust settles.

Before the editor signs off in Beirut in 1960, he offers a small but sharp clarification of terminology. There are scholars, he says, who have not yet reached the point of distinguishing between two terms — Ismailiyya (is-maa-EE-li-yya) and Fatimiyya (fa-ti-mi-yyaa). To these, he says: we have found no mention of the name Fatimiyya in our studies. The name should be confined to the family that ruled the Ismailis — namely the line of Imams — and has nothing to do with the philosophy itself. The use of the word Fatimid should not extend beyond the Fatimid dynasty during its reign in Morocco and Egypt. The reason is well known: their wish to draw near to the Islamic world, which honors the name of Fatima al-Zahra (FAA-ti-ma az-ZAH-raa), daughter of Muhammad, and, on the other hand, to distinguish themselves from the descendants of the Father of Imams by women other than Fatima. So every name applied to literature, philosophy, doctrine, or anything else pertaining to this da'wa must not stray beyond the scope of the word Ismailiyya.

With this clarification the introduction closes, signed Aref Tamer, Beirut, Lebanon, 1960. What follows in the six chapters of the book is the substance the editor has been preparing us to read. Each chapter takes a single Speaker-Prophet and walks through the events of his cycle by way of the Qur'anic verses that report them — not asking what those events were, but asking what those events meant, what truths they were sent to carry, what return the words are making to their substance. The six are Adam, Noah, Ibrahim (ib-rah-HEEM), Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad. The seventh, the Awaited Imam who completes the cycle, is left unwritten because his time has not yet come. The reader who walks through the six in order will, by the end, hold in his hand the basic grammar of Ismaili inner interpretation as al-Nu'man received it from the imamate of al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah and transmitted it to us.
