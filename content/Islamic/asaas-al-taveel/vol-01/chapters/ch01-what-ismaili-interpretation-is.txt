The editor of this volume, Arif Tamir, hesitated a long time before sending this book to print. The reason was that he wished to keep it longer in the cave of prudent concealment — alongside the other Ismaili manuscripts that have not yet been published. He also believed his information about the book was not yet complete, and that other Ismaili manuscripts, lesser in rank but indispensable as scientific preliminaries to it, gateways through which one must pass before approaching it, still needed to appear in print before it. So he delayed it. But now, whenever the word inner interpretation comes to mind, only one book presents itself to him, and this is the book.

In the Ismaili intellectual order, certain doctrinal materials are not held back because they are embarrassing. They are held back because the doctrine itself teaches that not every truth is fit for every ear at every moment. A truth disclosed to the wrong audience at the wrong stage of training can be heard as nonsense, or worse, as scandal. So the books wait. Some of them wait centuries. What he is about to do — print this volume in Beirut in 1960 — is a small departure from the usual discipline.

*Foundations of Inner Interpretation* is the single book in the Ismaili manuscript corpus that takes up the subject of interpretation as its certain theme. It is the precious work that lays out the basic idea of this science in a measured and balanced way, casting light on its sections and materials so as to bring them near to the mind and lower them within reach of thought. Interpretation is called inner interpretation because it is a return — a movement back to the outcome and to the origin of a thing: interpreting first what is and what is not, then bringing the matter back and resolving it, so that the fate of the words is their meaning and their substance.

In the early Ismaili mission in its flourishing, inner interpretation was the primary theme of every esoteric philosophical idea. It was the foundation upon which the pillars of this intellectual movement rested, the nourishment by which esoteric philosophy fed its judgment, its logic, and its exposition. For all of this, the book is counted among the Ismailis as one of the precious books — a treasury whose doctrinal teachings demand the preservation of secrecy and whose materials must be guarded from leaking to anyone who is not Ismaili. All of it is regarded as the secret of the creed and the key to the door of the mission.

That instinct toward layered disclosure has a long pedigree. The Father of Imams is reported in his collected sayings to have said: "Indeed, the Book of God carries four registers — its expression, its allusion, its subtle indication, and its inner reality; the expression is for the common people, the allusion for the elect, the subtle indication for the friends of God, and the inner reality for the prophets." That fourfold ladder, recorded in the eleventh-century collection *Brilliant Aphorisms*, edited by the historian al-Amidi, is the structural assumption the entire enterprise operates from. There is a public meaning. There is an interior. And there are stations between them.

Added to this, the book contains an interpretation of the stories of the prophets that appear in the three heavenly books: the Torah, the Gospel, and the Quran. This too constitutes a subject whose secrecy the doctrine demands be preserved, lest it pass beyond the circle of the general community, who are held to have grasped only the husks of culture and only the outward appearances of science.

Two words that the wider Islamic literature often runs together carry different meanings in Ismaili tradition: inner interpretation and outward commentary. Outward commentary means clarifying the meaning of any obscure word whose sense the reader does not understand. If we are asked, "What is the commentary on the word tree?" we answer: "It is a plant that is set down small, then grows, and from it limbs branch out upon which green leaves grow, and in spring it bears flowers that turn into good fruit." That is outward commentary: clarification at the level of the dictionary, the picture, the ordinary sense.

But if we are asked, "What is the inner interpretation of the word tree?" the answer follows the view of the one giving the interpretation: he may say it is a chamber, or a cow, or a rock, or anything else — provided it is in accord with truth, with reality, and with reason, that it is not foreign to belief, nor distant from sound thought. Inner interpretation is the essence of the meaning, its symbol, or its inner reality — a truth hidden behind a word that does not openly signify it.

Two things follow from this definition. The first is structural. There has to be an authority who knows what the tree actually is. The reader, holding only the word, cannot generate this answer by his own dictionary. He needs a teacher who stands in a chain that goes back to the moment when the meaning was placed inside the word. The second is doctrinal. The Ismaili intellectual order assigns the authority of revelation to the Speaker-Prophet. And it assigns the authority of inner interpretation to the Imam. The first represents the sacred law — rulings, jurisprudence, the law of the outward, what they call the apparent. The second represents the truth, the interpretation, the philosophy, the inward — what they call the hidden.

This pairing — the Speaker-Prophet and the Imam, revelation and interpretation, the apparent and the hidden — is the structural spine of the entire book. The six chapters that follow are organized by Speaker-Prophet, and each Speaker is paired with the figure who carried the inner meaning of his message. The Prophet was the bearer of the revelation of the Quran in its words and outward sense for the people at large, while its mystical secrets were entrusted to the Father of Imams. The bearer of the outward speaks to everyone. The bearer of the inward speaks to those whose insight has been opened. They are two faces of the same revelation. Neither is complete without the other.

The Qur'anic verses the Ismailis take as proof that interpretation is not optional but obligatory are cited in turn. "Thus your Lord shall choose you and teach you the interpretation of events" — spoken to Joseph, from verse six of the chapter on Joseph. "And thus We established Joseph in the land, that We might teach him the interpretation of events" — verse twenty-one of the same chapter. "I shall inform you of the interpretation of that which you could not bear patiently" — verse seventy-eight of the chapter on the cave, where a strange teacher tells a frustrated student that what looked like cruelty was mercy hidden inside cruelty. Translations here are rendered in the idiom of the standard English editions.

And then the central verse — verse seven of the chapter on the family of Imran: "He it is who has sent down to you the Book, in which are verses precisely defined — they are the foundation of the Book — and others ambiguous. As for those in whose hearts is deviation, they pursue what is ambiguous of it, seeking discord and seeking its interpretation, though none knows its interpretation save Allah and those firmly grounded in knowledge. They say: 'We believe in it; the whole of it is from our Lord.' But none take heed except those of understanding."

The verse cuts both ways. It says that some verses have an inner interpretation, and that the wrong people seek it for the wrong reasons. It also says that there are people — "those firmly grounded in knowledge" — who know that inner interpretation. The Ismaili reading takes that phrase to name a specific class of teachers, not a general intellectual virtue. The class begins with the Speaker-Prophet and continues, in each cycle, through the Imam-Foundation, who carries the inner meaning of what the Speaker delivered. The broader Islamic grammar community placed a pause after "save Allah," limiting knowledge to God alone. The Ismaili reading places the pause after "those firmly rooted in knowledge," making the Imams full partners in that knowledge — the structural move on which all interpretation depends.

The rational proofs follow. "We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and within themselves" — verse fifty-three of the chapter on the detailed exposition. And: "And in the earth are signs for those certain in faith — and in your own selves: do you not see?" — verses twenty and twenty-one of the chapter on the scatterers. There are signs out there in the world; there are signs inside the human person; the two answer to each other. The apparent was created in order to point to the subtle. The great supporter of religion among the Ismailis, an eleventh-century thinker who served as chief preacher in the Fatimid court, put the matter directly: "Allah has made parables and likenesses: the human body is a likeness, and the soul is a likeness; this world is a likeness, and the Hereafter is a likeness." And also: "By the parable I mean a parable struck — like the bee's needles, and what comes from it like honey."

All of this carries us, the editor says, to the city of Alexandria and to its philosophical school — to what Philo and his disciples said when they tried to interpret the Torah esoterically but did not succeed. Augustine and his disciples then tried to follow that school in interpreting the Gospel esoterically, but they too could not bring the idea to completion. Then came the Ismailis, who spread their system of thought; and this was the first curriculum they put forward — a system of interpretation. They refined it with their own ideas and added to it a rational interpretation that pruned bare transmission and produced conclusive proof for the Islamic world. The Ismailis are the most ancient in their understanding of Islamic principles. They affirmed the inward and its necessity, just as they affirmed the outward alongside it. The apparent is not accepted without the inward, and the inward is of no use without the apparent — for outward and inward are like body and soul, and benefits arise only from their meeting, through which the ends are known.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, is reported in a well-known narration preserved by the eleventh-century traditionist Ibn Hibban in his collection *The Sound Compendium* to have said: "There is no verse in the Quran that does not have an outward and an inward, a limit, and a place of ascent." The outward, the inward, the limit, and the place of ascent — four registers, the same fourfold structure preserved in the Father of Imams' saying quoted earlier. The Ismaili order is not inventing the architecture; it is claiming custody of it.

Revelation was specialized in by the Prophet during his lifetime. It is the knowledge of the apparent and of the rulings of the sacred law, to which the public is permitted access. The Imam, the foundational figure — designated "Foundation," a title given to every Imam contemporary with the Speaker-Prophet — is responsible for the inward, for the interpretation, for the truth and its secret symbols, which may not be disclosed except to the loyal soldiers of the mission who have risen in its ranks. These are the "specially favored" classes.

The two books *Foundations of Inner Interpretation* and *Pillars of Islam* — both authored by the same Fatimid judge, al-Nu'man — represent two related and fully interconnected directions: the inward and the outward. The first is the foundation of esoteric philosophy. The second is the foundation of jurisprudence and the sacred law, narrated by the fifth Imam from his grandfather, the Prophet — for the Prophet, as has been said, is the bearer of the sacred law. This shows that Ismaili esoteric studies proceeded by degrees, and that their ranks were divided into stages through which the respondent must pass.

The Ismailis were alone in interpreting the verses of the Quran that require interpretation, where other sects content themselves with mere outward commentary. This is plain in the books of the earliest Qur'anic commentators. Outward commentary asks what the verse means in plain Arabic. Inner interpretation asks what truth the verse was sent to carry.

It is very difficult to understand and to reach the end of Ismaili philosophical texts, to grasp their expressions, their symbols, and their aims, unless the reader has first passed through the foundational interpretive texts. There is no doubt that this book is one of the texts that must be studied carefully. The basic grammar of inner interpretation lives in this book. Without it, no Ismaili philosophical text can be fully read.

The author wrote many books in jurisprudence, law, rulings, history, and in the science of interpretation — which is considered an introduction to philosophy. Another major early Ismaili thinker, contemporary with the author, was directly responsible for the philosophy field in that period, and was the only one with permission to present it. None of this prevents the author from being counted among the great thinking minds who laid the pillars of thought in the Fatimid state.

The Ismailis had their share — indeed the most advanced share — in the arena of thought, and they enriched the Islamic library with a massive scientific production before the existence of the Fatimid state. The third and fourth centuries of the Islamic calendar are among the most splendid of ages for the Arab and Islamic worlds. During these centuries the Ismaili mission flourished in a comprehensive intellectual flowering. The Ismaili preachers worked to transmit much of the sciences of the Persians, of India, and of Greece — after expounding them, adapting them, and refining them — placing the scattered theories in order and applying them in a manner suited to Islamic principles and to reason.

The fourth Fatimid Imam-Caliph, the patron under whom this book was composed, is counted among the most prosperous rulers Islam ever knew. He was deeply committed to reviving the scientific renaissance throughout his realm and to strengthening the intellectual system he considered the foundation of his mission. He personally collaborated with the author on the two books that together form the basis of jurisprudence and of philosophy. The first was meant as the foundation of the apparent law — what they call "practical worship" — namely the *Pillars of Islam*. The second was meant as the foundation of esoteric philosophy, or "scientific worship" — namely the present work. *Pillars of Islam* is the body the tradition gives the believer. *Foundations of Inner Interpretation* is the soul.

The author was born in Morocco, into a family belonging to one of the great Arab tribes. He died in Cairo in the year three hundred sixty-three of the Islamic calendar — nine hundred seventy-four of the Common Era — during the imamate of his patron. He is the most famous jurist Ismailism has produced since the dawn of Islam, and among its most productive. He entered the service of the founder of the Fatimid state in the late years of that Imam's life, then took charge of the judiciary in Tripoli under the second Fatimid Imam-Caliph, and finally came to Egypt when his great patron brought him and his family.

This science, inner interpretation, was propagated by the Ismailis among the various classes of Muslims with great care. In their view, religion required that a person ascend through continuous discipline, rising from one degree to the next, until at last he attained knowledge of the houses of the universe — the inhabited worlds separated from the transcendent Creator: that is, from Absolute Non-Anthropomorphic Thought, the First Intellect, the Higher Light, or the Originating Power — the Pen that writes, the First that precedes existence, the one who emits lights from itself toward the Second Place, toward the Second Universal Intellect, or toward the Universal Soul. These two principles — the First Intellect and the Universal Soul — are the two origins from which everything proceeds, after their transformation in the minds of the prophets, the Imams, and the elect. The common classes do not possess such distinct minds; they are destitute of them so long as they have not acquired knowledge or moved to the second station.

This system of inner interpretation grew because the Ismailis gave the religious laws and the Qur'anic verses an esoteric interpretation that differed from their outward sense and yet conformed to common sense. At the same time, they did not reject the revealed laws and did not deny what the Quran and the other heavenly books brought. Rather, they affirmed them and acknowledged that their obligations could not be set aside.

The Ismaili preachers proclaimed their principles in two modes. The first concerned the prophecies and the necessity of accepting what they brought — laws, texts, and ordinances — in an apparent way, calling people to follow them in the manner of "obligation and imitation." The second was a call to what the heavenly books had brought through esoteric interpretation, in the manner of "choice, covenant, and pledge." Religion is in a fortified citadel that cannot be breached; one cannot reach what lies behind it except through a real, abiding spiritual meaning — never through gleaming external appearances and superficial teachings that, with the passage of time, become mere habit.

Inner interpretation was the specialty of the Imam and his disciples. The verses of the Quran were revealed openly, and yet they were not devoid of esoteric meaning, of soul, of essence. The outward commentary on the Quran often leads to differences of opinion, while inner interpretation — the disclosure of the esoteric meaning — is a fixed system that does not shift; it leads to mental reality and to the consensus of society. The inner sense is delivered through a single chain of authoritative teachers. The knowledge of the apparent is very easy to reach; inner interpretation, by contrast, cannot be reached except after struggle, weariness, and the surmounting of obstacles — after passing through trials and entering the ranks of an intellectual system from which one does not emerge without being marked by what they have called "the covenant, the compact, and the oath."

God revealed His religion to humanity and gave it the optional portion by way of following the clearest paths, demonstrating its justice and leaving freedom before man to act in the light of the intellect of his constitution — making that intellect the finest gift bestowed upon him. Inner interpretation is a philosophical and religious theory which may be summarized thus: God Almighty has set all the meanings of religion in the creatures that surround man. One must therefore substitute what is in nature and on the face of the earth, according to one's understanding of the truth of religion, which has divided creation into two categories: an apparent part that is visible and an inward part that is hidden, with the apparent pointing to the inward — like the human body, which is apparent, and the soul, which is inward. What is disclosed from religion is practical worship, and what is set down in the apparent verses of the Quran consists of meanings articulated by the scholars and by the common public alike. Yet each of the religious duties also has an esoteric interpretation, which can only be taught by the Imams, by their senior preachers, and by the elect.

The creed Islam has brought is not contrary to reason, nor should it conflict with reason — particularly since the Islamic religion was born a rational religion capable of development. The Qur'anic verses teach that man must think, reflect, look, meditate, consider, and deduce. Knowledge in Islam has been made a condition of faith: whoever believes without understanding and knowing has not believed well. True belief is the truth that emerges from guidance, from understanding, and from choice. It honored the intellect and demanded that it be attentive.

The author divided his book into six chapters, devoting one to each Speaker-Prophet, the chief of his cycle, his preachers, and his followers. Each chapter is filled with Qur'anic verses concerning their stories, their era, and the events of their time. He begins with the era of Adam and ends with the era of the Prophet. He did not write the chapter concerning the Awaited Imam — the seventh — because his time has not yet come. There is one chapter that deserves particular attention: the chapter dealing with Jesus, son of Mary, and the story of his birth without a father, followed by the story of Adam and the meaning of his creation from clay. All of this is innovative in its philosophy of doctrine, rich in ideas, and marked by sound rational maturity.

The edition relied on two principal copies of the work. One came from a town in the Syrian mountains and served as the principal copy. The other came from copies preserved in Yemen, presented by a colleague who hosted the editor during a visit to East Africa in 1956. Grammatical and orthographic corrections were made, some sentences that had dropped out were restored, and Qur'anic verses that had been corrupted in transmission were revised — all to produce a sound text upon which no dust settles.

Before closing at Beirut in 1960, the editor offers a sharp clarification of terminology. There are scholars who have not yet reached the point of distinguishing between two terms — the name of the Ismaili tradition and the name of the Fatimid dynasty. The dynastic name should be confined to the family that ruled the Ismailis — namely the line of Imams who held the caliphate in North Africa and Egypt — and has nothing to do with the philosophy itself. The dynasty wished to draw near to the broader Islamic world, which honors the name of the Prophet's daughter, and to distinguish themselves from the descendants of the Father of Imams by women other than the Prophet's daughter. Every name applied to literature, philosophy, doctrine, or anything else pertaining to this mission must not stray beyond the scope of the word Ismaili.

With this clarification the introduction closes, signed by the editor at Beirut, Lebanon, in 1960.
