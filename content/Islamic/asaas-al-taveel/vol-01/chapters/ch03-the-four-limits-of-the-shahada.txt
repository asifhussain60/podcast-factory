The author opens the testimony — "There is no god but Allah" — and shows what is inside it. He had argued in the prior treatment that the testimony contained the whole sacred law from the very first day the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, spoke it. Now he opens it. The form of the argument is simple and severe. The testimony has four words. Those four words stand for four limits that organize the whole created order. Two of the limits are above, in the spiritual world. Two of the limits are below, in the world of bodies. The two above are the Pen and the Preserved Tablet, named in the prophetic report that says the first thing God created was the Pen. The two below are the speaker-prophet, who articulates the sacred law in his time, and the Silent One, who succeeds him as the master of its interpretation. To recognize all four, and to accept them, is to complete the testimony. To acknowledge only some — as the philosophers do when they affirm intellect and soul but deny the messengers, or as those of the outward Islam do when they affirm the prophets but do not know their inner office — is to leave the testimony broken. From the four, the author turns to the seven and the twelve. There are seven speaker-prophets across history — Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, the Prophet, and the Awaited Riser — and each speaker-prophet has seven Imams in his cycle and twelve successors arranged in his time as proofs. The numbers are not ornamental. They are the architecture by which the author reads the heavens, the earth, the human body, the months of the year, and the letters of the testimony itself as one continuous sign of the same structure.

He opens with the grammar of the testimony. The testimony is divided into two clauses, he says — one negation, one affirmation. "There is no god" is the negation. "But Allah" is the affirmation. Each clause is made of two words. The first word in each pair is joined to the second; the second is elevated above the first. The separation between the two clauses marks the affirmation as higher than the negation that precedes it. The negation is like the lower limit, he says, and the affirmation is like the upper limit. Within the upper, the first word of the affirmation stands as the highest boundary, and the word preceding it stands as the next. Within the lower, the second word of the negation stands as the lower of the lower boundaries, and the word preceding it, joined to it and yet beneath it, stands as the boundary beneath. Four words, four positions, four limits. The whole architecture is already in the syntax.

He pauses, characteristically, on what these joined pairs do and do not signify. The two words that meet in the limit of affirmation and in the limit of negation function as two words and no more. They do not collapse into one. If they did, he says, the joined pair would be loftier and more freighted with meaning than they ought to be, and the result would be God Himself. He is being precise. The words point to the limits; they are not the limits. The limits point to the One; they are not the One. The architecture of pointing is preserved through the architecture of separation. The Fatimid philosopher who came a generation later, the author of the book *The Wellsprings*, made the same point in a sharper key: the unity of God is so radically transcendent that even the negation "there is no god" must itself be negated, lest the negation become a description (see Paul Walker, *Early Philosophical Shiism*, Cambridge University Press, 1993, pages 76 to 80). The author's caution about the words not collapsing into one is the same caution put earlier and in a sober legal voice.

By Allah, he says, this points to the noblest of the upper and lower boundaries. Each of the four words is joined to the others and dependent upon them. Each has fewer letters than the cluster as a whole. And the final word — the name Allah itself — carries three letters that together gather every letter that appears anywhere in the testimony. This is the sentence of the saying within the limits of the testimony, the author says. He will now explain it.

He begins from the prophetic report. What has been transmitted from the Messenger of Allah, both by the elect and by the general public, is that he said: "The first thing Allah Almighty created was the Pen. Then He set running a river sweeter than honey, whiter than milk, and softer than butter. Then He created the Preserved Tablet, which Allah Almighty mentioned in His mighty Book." This is the report. The general public, the author says, carried this report toward what they already knew — that pens are made of reed and tablets of wood, as we observe — and did not turn to the fact that these are names, titles, and proverbs that need to be uncovered, unveiled, and explained. This, he says, is from the interpretation of the report mentioned by Allah, the Almighty. The same report circulates through the canonical prophetic-tradition collections in slightly different wordings — the one in the canonical ninth-century compendium known in English as *The Refined Collection* reads, "The first thing Allah created was the Pen; He said to it, write; it said, what shall I write; He said, write the decree of all that will be until the Day of Judgment" (see *The Refined Collection*, "Book of the Decree," report number 2155, in the standard Cairo edition). The author's reading does not deny the literal chain of transmission. It says that the chain delivers an outer wording whose figures — Pen, river, Tablet — must be opened.

Then the inner reading. The Pen, the author says, is the highest limit that Allah has set for His servants. They may not transgress it. As He said: "Whoever transgresses the limits of Allah has wronged his own soul" (verse one of the chapter on divorce, in the Quran, in the translation of M. A. S. Abdel Haleem, Oxford University Press, 2004). The Almighty stands above the boundaries and has erected them for His creation beneath Himself, so that they may reach knowledge of Him through them, and that the creation might be led to command and prohibition. The Pen, he says, is the bearer of knowledge — derived from Allah and fixed in the Tablet. The Preserved Tablet then limited the limits of God beneath itself, meaning that, beneath the Pen, the Tablet took its knowledge from the Pen, and the knowledge became, for the Tablet, from the Pen. These, he says, are the two upper boundaries we have mentioned. The Pen above, the Tablet beneath the Pen, and both of them above the world of bodies — the two upper limits of the four.

He turns to the river. As for the river that Allah Almighty made to flow for the Pen — the river sweeter than honey, whiter than milk, softer than butter — the Almighty has spoken of it: "And if they had remained on the right way, We would have given them water in abundance" (verse sixteen of the chapter on the jinn). The way, the author says, is the prophet who speaks in his time, and the Imam in his time after him. The water is abundant knowledge. And again: "And We have created above you seven ways, and We are not heedless of creation" (verse seventeen of the chapter on the believers). This too he will mention in its entirety in its proper place.

He pauses on the polemic. The general public, he says, have confirmed this much in their interpretation of the esoteric, because they have found no evidence against it. By saying that the way provides water with knowledge, they understand it as the apparent water that Allah causes to descend upon the righteous land and upon the immoral alike. And Allah has said that if creatures were to stand upright before their guardian, they would have learned the most abundant knowledge — "that We may try them therein," meaning, He says, We test them, see what they say, and teach them about it. Then He said: "And whoever turns away from the remembrance of his Lord, He will lead him into an ever-increasing torment." So He mentioned here, the author says, that whoever turned away from his guardian, the punishment of God became his — and He called the guardian a "remembrance," that is, a person of remembrance, so the thing was named for him. This is well known in the language of the Arabs. They call a thing by what is suited to it and to its owner. They call the water-skin a "narrator," although in their language the narrator is the camel that carries the water — so they named the water-skin after that camel because it accompanies him. Such things are many in their language. The Almighty Himself, he reminds us, said: "Indeed, Allah has sent down to you a Reminder — a Messenger" — He called the Messenger a "Reminder" because he is the bearer of the remembrance. The naming carries through. The way is the prophet. The water is the knowledge. The remembrance is the messenger. The river above the Pen is the abundant knowledge that Allah caused to flow for the Pen to draw from.

Now the two upper limits have been placed: Pen above, Tablet below the Pen, and the river of knowledge flowing for the Pen. The author turns to the two lower limits.

Then Allah Almighty, he says, established, in correspondence with these two upper spiritual limits, two lower physical limits — namely the speaker-prophet and the Silent One. The speaker-prophet is the Ismaili term given to every messenger who came with a message and articulated a law. The foundational speaker-prophets, the foundational seven, are Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, the Prophet, and the Awaited Riser. The Silent One is the Ismaili term given on the basis of the speaker-prophet. He is the speaker-prophet's successor after him, the one from whom the Imams come in succession, the one entrusted with the affairs of the esoteric mission during the speaker-prophet's own lifetime. The foundational Silent Ones among the Ismailis are likewise seven, paired with the seven speaker-prophets: Seth, Shem, Isaac, Aaron, Simeon, the Father of Imams, and the awaited successor of the seventh cycle. Each speaker-prophet has his Silent One. Each Silent One stands as the foundation of his speaker-prophet's sacred law. The leading modern historian of the tradition lays out the same paired structure in the standard reference work *The Ismailis: Their History and Doctrines*, second edition, Cambridge University Press, 2007, pages 137 to 140, observing that this pairing is the spine on which classical Ismaili cyclical history is built and that no other Shia school maps the seven-and-seven so symmetrically.

The author returns to the doctrine in his own voice. The speaker-prophet is the master of the sacred law. The Silent One is the foundation of the sacred law in the era of the speaker-prophet, and the master of its interpretation. The Messenger utters the manifest. The Foundation is silent about that manifest — that is, from the manifest, he conducts toward the subtle, toward the inner meaning that the Prophet himself has affirmed. The standing of the two below echoes the standing of the two above. The Pen has knowledge. The Tablet has the knowledge of the Pen. The speaker-prophet has the sacred law. The Silent One has the inner sense of that law, which the speaker-prophet authorized. The structure of derivation is the same upstairs and downstairs. The Father of Imams gave the relation its starkest sentence in a sermon collected long after his death in the book *The Peak of Eloquence*: "I am the speaking Quran, and this is the silent Quran" (the book *The Peak of Eloquence*, sermon one hundred and fifty-eight, in the standard Beirut edition of 1980, page 222). The geometry the author is drawing is older than his book; he is naming the architecture his community already saw in that sermon.

Then a striking, very Ismaili turn. The world cannot be left without a witness. When the house that God Almighty has established holds the two lower boundaries as a place of transformation, and the bodies in it are mortal, then by the wisdom of God the house must not be left empty of an argument. So Allah Almighty established a foundation after the speaker-prophet to take his place in the apparent matter and in the other duties the speaker-prophet had been performing, and He instructed him to appoint an argument for himself, to entrust him with the knowledge of interpretation, and to install it with him just as it had been with the speaker-prophet. Then He established an argument after him before him, and ordered him to take an argument for himself in turn — according to the pattern that the way of Allah Almighty has established, as He said in His Mighty Book: "And you will not find any change in the way of Allah" (verse forty-three of the chapter on the creator of the heavens). This continues, the author says, until the role of that speaker-prophet turns over seven Imams. Then the cycle completes. The succession of the Imams of a speaker-prophet is seven; the succession of the speaker-prophets across the whole of history is also seven.

He restates the count of the speaker-prophets because he wants the listener to hold it. The speaker-prophets, he says, are seven: Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, the Prophet, and the Awaited Riser. Six of them, he notes, are designated by a covenant — six, not seven — and the exception is Adam. He cites the Quran: "And We had already taken a promise from Adam before, but he forgot, and We found in him no firm determination" (verse one hundred and fifteen of the chapter Ta-Ha). This indicates, the author says, that Adam has no firm determination. Every speaker-prophet — whether messenger or Imam — has twelve successors in his time, and the twelve and the seven, he says, will both be mentioned in due course, God willing.

He pulls the diagram together. The four words of the testimony, he says, stand as a likeness for these four limits. Whoever recognizes them, accepts them, and bears witness to them completes the limits of the testimony. The testimony gathers them, and gathers its boundaries within them. This is the structural statement. The two words of the affirmation correspond to the two upper limits — the Pen and the Tablet. The two words of the negation correspond to the two lower limits — the speaker-prophet and the Silent One. Four words, four limits, one testimony.

Then the diagnostic. There are those, he says, who affirmed the Creator Almighty by their own claim — from among the philosophers, the logicians, and others — and yet denied the messengers. These people, he notes, affirmed the two upper boundaries and named them by other than these two names. Instead of "Pen" and "Tablet," they called them "the intellect" and "the soul." But they did not know the two lower boundaries, so the testimony was not established for them, because they acknowledged some and denied others. This is the precise charge: the philosophers came close. They saw the upper architecture. They renamed it in their idiom — intellect and soul — and read it well enough on its own terms. But they could not see, or would not see, that the upper architecture has a worldly correspondent below it, in the person of the speaker-prophet and his Silent successor. Without that, the testimony is not whole. The Fatimid philosopher of the following century gave the polemic a sharper edge: the intellect-and-soul of the Greek philosophers, he wrote, is a true reading of the upper world, but a reading that has stopped halfway — it knows the writing in the Tablet and refuses to follow the writing down to the human teacher who holds the key (see the book *The Face of Religion*, by the Fatimid philosopher writing in Persian in the eleventh century, chapter twenty-two, in the English translation and study published by the Institute of Ismaili Studies, London, 2012, pages 195 to 198).

Then the second diagnosis. As for those from among the "people of refuge" who hold fast to the testimony and the outward marks of Islam, who acknowledge the Tablet, the Pen, and the speaker-prophet among the prophets — these people did not have proper knowledge of these matters, and they professed something other and so did not complete the testimony. From them, this incomplete testimony is accepted only insofar as their blood is spared and their wealth made inviolable. They are also prevented from being among the protection-tax payers, as the Prophet established for them by the command of Allah Almighty when they became Muslim. The form of the testimony was on their tongue. The substance of it — its four limits — was not in their knowledge. They were placed under the rule of Islam. They were not placed in the rank of completed faith.

The author pauses on the words themselves. Islam, he says, is a word taken from a root meaning reconciliation and surrender — the casting of one's hands into obedience. Faith is a limit that follows Islam. It is the knowledge of the limits of the testimony, the belief in them, and the rendering of their due. And this, he says, is sincerity, as the Messenger of Allah said in the saying the author cites here. The Messenger promised the one who is sincere to the testimony Paradise, and told us that the testimony is returned to whoever is not sincere to it. One's religion, the Prophet said, is diminished by however much one's sincerity falls short. So the testimony, the author insists, is the root of the religion. Indeed the whole religion is in it. And so the call to it is as he has said: a call to the root that already contained the trunk and the branches.

Then he counts. If we look at the count of the letters of the testimony and what they signify, he says, "There is no god but Allah" comprises seven clauses. This is a proof and a likeness, he says, for the seven speaker-prophets and for the seven Imams in the role of every speaker-prophet. These he has already mentioned. They are the signs of Allah that He mentioned in His Book and in His speech. He cites the Quran: "And We made the son of Mary and his mother a sign" (verse fifty of the chapter on the believers). And: "Indeed, the Messiah, son of Mary, is the Messenger of Allah and His Word, which He bestowed upon Mary" (verse one hundred and seventy-one of the chapter on women). And: "When the angels said, O Mary, indeed Allah gives you good tidings of a Word from Him whose name will be the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary" (verse forty-five of the chapter on the family of Imran). And: "We will show them Our signs in the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth" (verse fifty-three of the chapter on the explained). And: "And in the earth are signs for the certain in faith, and in your own selves — do you not then see?" (verses twenty and twenty-one of the chapter on the scatterers).

These signs, the author says, are like those of the testimony, but their likenesses come from heaven and earth. From the side of religion, the heaven is the likeness of the master of the age — whether he is a speaker-prophet or an Imam — and the earth is the likeness of his argument. Water descends from heaven onto the earth, he says, and from it trees and plants sprout, grow, and flourish. So too does knowledge descend from the speaker-prophet to the Silent One, and from it the believers sprout and live. The believers, in the simile of trees and plants, live by the knowledge that comes to them from the argument.

And like them too is the sun. In the two heavens — the spiritual and the physical — the sun is like the speaker-prophet who illuminates the world. The speaker-prophet likewise illuminates the world with his knowledge. Hence, the author cites, the report transmitted from the Messenger of Allah that he said: "It will rise from its west at the head of the three hundred" — and that, the tradition holds, was the appearance of the Awaited Riser. The moon, he says, is like the argument, because it draws its light from the sun. Likewise the knowledge of the argument is derived from the knowledge of the Imam. The speaker-prophet in the body, he says, is like the heart, which is the prince of the bodily limbs. The argument is like the brain, which is the seat of the mind. Each of them is one within the body — and just so, the speaker-prophet is one in the world. The speaker-prophet is also like the tongue, for through it speech is made. The argument is like the two eyes through which sight is made, like the ears through which hearing is made, like the two hands through which both restraint and action are accomplished. The encyclopedists of the same Fatimid century — the philosophical collective known in the modern register as the Brethren of Purity — extended this body-and-cosmos correspondence into a full thesaurus of micro-and-macrocosm parallels; the author is using a small portion of a much larger map the community already shared (see *The Epistles of the Brethren of Purity*, the modern Oxford University Press edition in association with the Institute of Ismaili Studies, 2008, pages 7 to 10 of the editor's introduction).

From the earth, he says, the likeness is the west and the east. The east is like the speaker-prophet, for from it the light comes. The west is like the argument that receives that light and is where it comes to rest. The seven heavens are like the seven speaker-prophets. The seven earths are like the seven arguments. Likewise the seven planetary stars — the arrangers — that Allah Almighty mentioned in His Book when He said: "And those who arrange the affair" (verse five of the chapter on the snatchers). These too are likenesses of the same. So too are the seven climes of the earth, and so too in the human being. The human being was created from seven things, the author says, as the Almighty said: "And certainly We created the human being from an extract of clay; then We placed him as a sperm-drop in a firm lodging; then We made the sperm-drop into a clinging clot; then We made the clot into a lump of flesh; then We made out of the lump bones; then We clothed the bones with flesh; then We brought him forth as another creation. So blessed is Allah, the best of creators" (verses twelve through fourteen of the chapter on the believers).

He continues the body's geometry. The parts of the human being, he says, are seven: hair, skin, flesh, blood, bones, veins, and brain. The height of the human being is seven cubits, he says, and his breadth as well — that is, the span between the tips of his two hands when he extends them. He prostrates on seven parts: the face, the hands, the knees, and the feet. In his forehead are seven openings: the eyes, the ears, the nostrils, and the mouth. These, he says, are some of the witnesses to the signs of Allah, His limits, and their likenesses, and their inward meanings in the greater and the smaller worlds. Many further proofs are given at length, and he has mentioned them in the book *Mention of the Preparations*. There is more than this besides, he says, although he has abridged that work as well.

These, the author says, are the limits that Allah Almighty has commanded be safeguarded, and by which the believers are marked with faith — for they enacted them and preserved them, and by that He made Paradise obligatory for them. He cites at length: "Indeed Allah has purchased from the believers their souls and their wealth, in exchange for Paradise being theirs. They fight in the way of Allah, so they slay and are slain — a true promise upon Him in the Torah, the Gospel, and the Quran. And who is more faithful to his covenant than Allah? So rejoice in your transaction which you have contracted, and that is indeed the great success" (verse one hundred and eleven of the chapter on repentance). Then He described the believers to whom this was enjoined, the author says, by their attributes: "Those who repent, who worship, who praise, who fast, who bow down, who prostrate themselves, who enjoin the good and forbid the wrong, and who keep the limits of Allah — and give glad tidings to the believers" (verse one hundred and twelve of the same chapter).

Now he turns from the seven to the twelve. As for the twelve successors, he says, they are instruments — causes — for every speaker-prophet in every age. Four of them are above and closest to the speaker-prophet, and each of these four has two — one to the right and one to the left — making twelve in all. The four, he says, are like the four birds of Abraham in his story, as the Almighty said: "He said, Then take four birds and incline them toward yourself" (verse two hundred and sixty of the chapter on the cow). They are among the twelve limits, and they are like the four sacred months, according to His saying: "The number of months with Allah is twelve months in the Book of Allah from the day He created the heavens and the earth, of which four are sacred. So do not wrong yourselves with them" (verse thirty-six of the chapter on repentance). Their likeness, he says, is also found in the testimony, whose letters number twelve. The twelve letters that together form the negation-and-affirmation correspond to the names of the twelve islands of the earth, or, in the editor's gloss, to the twelve missionaries who are charged with the propagation and organization of the mission. Their likeness, he continues, is in the twelve constellations of the heavens, and in the twelve islands of the earth — each of them an island — and in the twelve spiritual ones, the angels who are the messengers, who are the first wings, for it is through them that the command in the message of the Companion is directed. Their wings are the preachers, for each of them is sent to one of the islands, which has a limit corresponding to it. He cites: "Praise be to Allah, who made the angels messengers having wings — two, three, and four. He increases in creation what He wills. Indeed, Allah is able to do all things. Whatever mercy Allah opens to mankind, none can withhold; and whatever He withholds, none can release after Him. He is the Mighty, the Wise" (verses one and two of the chapter on the originator).

The inner-circle vocabulary deserves a word. The inner council of each Imam, in Ismaili belief, numbers four; one of the four who holds the presidency is called the Door. They are also called the Substitutes — those who have changed — for they were re-created after their first creation, refined after refinement, as is said in the words of the Brethren of Purity.

The author pulls the numbers together. Understand, then, he says, that the twelve limits in the lower world correspond to the angels in the upper world. The Almighty has said: "And of all things We created pairs" (verse forty-nine of the chapter on the scatterers). The seven and the twelve together total nineteen — and these are the ones whom Allah has entrusted. The Almighty said: "Over it are nineteen" (verse thirty of the chapter on the cloaked one). The testimony has gathered them all together. Whoever knows them, believes in their allegiance, and acts upon what he has taken from them, is a believer among the people of Paradise — accepted in his testimony. Whoever is unable to do any of that, or falls short of it, but bears witness with his tongue alone, is a Muslim whose testimony is not accepted in the rank of faith, and his judgment rests with Allah. For this reason, he says, it is mentioned in the book *Pillars of Islam*, from the saying of the sixth Imam: "Faith encompasses Islam, but Islam does not encompass faith. A man may be a Muslim without being a believer, but he is not a believer until he is also a Muslim." (The book *Pillars of Islam* is the author's own legal compendium; the saying from the sixth Imam is reproduced and translated in Ismail Poonawala's English edition: *The Pillars of Islam, Volume One: Acts of Devotion and Religious Observances*, Oxford University Press, 2002, page 18.) The two clauses, faith and Islam, fit inside each other in only one direction. The outward Islam is the gate. The inward faith is the room. To enter the room, one must pass through the gate. But to stand in the gate is not yet to have entered the room.

He has now placed the four limits, the seven speaker-prophets and seven Imams, and the twelve successors. He turns to the second testimony. This is the testimony, he says, whose limits we have laid out with respect to the foundations. Connected to it is the second testimony, which pertains to the branches: the testimony that the Prophet is the Messenger of Allah. This second testimony is three words, and these three words represent three subordinate spiritual limits — the upper spiritual ones — namely Raphael, Michael, and Gabriel. They correspond to three earthly corporeal limits, in extension of the speaker-prophet and the Foundation — namely the Imam, the Proof, and the Successor. Together they also form six divisions corresponding to the six pronunciations of the first determination, which he has already mentioned. Together they make twelve letters, like the twelve proofs already mentioned. Thus the testimony to Allah Almighty is the highest degree — the foundations. The testimony to the Messenger is the lowest degree — the branches. The two testimonies share the architecture, and they share it by twelve letters apiece.

The upper and lower limits, the author says, are contained within the letters of the testimony. The outward of the testimony is a statement with the tongue. Its inner meaning is statement and knowledge together. Its substance is action. Its outward form is Islam. And at its core is faith. On this, the sixth Imam said — as the author has established in the book *Pillars of Islam*, when he was asked whether faith is speech or action — "Faith is all action, and speech is only a part of it." The verdict is clear. The testimony lives on the tongue, true. But the tongue is the smallest part of it. Knowledge is what gives the tongue what to say. Action is what makes the testimony real in the world.

He turns to direct address. So preserve, O believers, the testimony to which you have been called. Be sincere in it. He has shown them, he says, the sincerity due to it — that for which Allah Almighty has appointed reward by His saying: "And the witnesses do not refuse when they are called" (verse two hundred and eighty-two of the chapter on the cow). Whatever you have been called to, be faithful to it in word and deed. Fulfill His promise to you, in the words of the Almighty: "And fulfill My covenant and I will fulfill your covenant" (verse forty of the chapter on the children of Israel, more commonly cited as verse forty of the chapter on the cow). Allah has promised you, through the tongue of His Messenger, Paradise if you are sincere in your testimony. Know this, then believe and do not doubt. Act and do not stray. Do not be complacent, do not be discouraged — and you will receive what Allah has promised you of Paradise, outward and inward, as He said: "And for him who fears the standing before his Lord, there are two gardens" (verse forty-six of the chapter on the Most Merciful).

The doubling of the garden is not incidental. The testimony is two clauses, outward and inward. The testimony to Allah is the foundation; the testimony to the Messenger is the branch. The believer who keeps both receives both. It is wise, he says, to know that you will not leave the gardens, and you will receive their fruits and their spouses. You will enter the Garden of Eternity in the abode of permanence in the hereafter, and you will gain therein the reward of Allah, which He has promised you. And you will be safe from His punishment, with which He warned you should you disobey what He has commanded and commit what He has forbidden — should you neglect what He has enjoined upon you and to which He has called you to be sincere, by the testimony in which He gathered together all that by which you worship Him.

He brings the chapter to its close. What he has mentioned in this chapter — the chapter on faith — should suffice, he says, as an explanation of the testimony and what it indicates of the limits. He will mention in the chapters of this book all of them in like manner. When mentioning the obligatory prayers, he says, you will know that this testimony has descended upon her and has encompassed the totality of that by which you worship Allah. That is why the call of the Prophet was directed to it, and why he took upon you the covenant that you would testify with sincerity after him. You have borne witness to it with your tongue. You bear witness to it as a testimony of belief in your hearts. And you act upon it as a ruling next to a deed. The threefold pattern — tongue, heart, limbs — is the definition of faith from the sixth Imam carried over from the previous chapter, returned now to close this one. The testimony is what the tongue says, what the heart believes, what the limbs do.

He ends in benediction. Take notice, he says, that you have been appointed to be encompassed by the limits that have been set for you, and the proofs that have been shown to you, upon the right path by which you have been saved. May Allah aid you in that, and guide you to it. May Allah's blessings be upon the Prophet and upon the righteous of his descendants who succeed one another until the Day of Judgment. And praise be to Allah, Lord of the Worlds.

The diagram is now complete, and the listener should hold it as a single picture. The testimony has four words, four limits, seven clauses, twelve letters, nineteen entrusted ones. The Pen and the Tablet stand above. The speaker-prophet and the Silent One stand below. The seven speaker-prophets of history each carry a sevenfold succession of Imams. The twelve successors stand as the architecture of every age. Every later chapter of the book *Foundations of Inner Interpretation* — on prayer, on fasting, on the pilgrimage, on the prophetic cycles to come — will read its subject through this same architecture. The testimony was never a starter creed. From the first day the Prophet spoke it, the four words contained the four limits, and the four limits contained the whole of the world that the believer is to confess and obey.
