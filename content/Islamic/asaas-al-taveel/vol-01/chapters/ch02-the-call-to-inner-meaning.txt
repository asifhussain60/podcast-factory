In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful. Praise be to Allah, the Bestower of grace. Blessings upon the Prophet of mercy, the executor appointed for the community, and the pure Imams from his goodly progeny. Prophet, executor, Imams, in one breath. The whole structural claim of the book is folded into the salutation.

He has already published, with the support of Allah, a book for those who respond to the call of truth, those who cling to the rope of the Creator, who hold fast to the trustworthy handhold, who adhere to the straightest path, a book treating the outward knowledge of the sacred law. He called it The Pillars of Islam. In that book, he set forth the limits of faith and the distinction between faith and submission; the obligation of the bond of allegiance; the proofs of the Imams; the requirements of duty; the chapters of the lawful and the forbidden; the questions and the rulings, so that those who respond to the call might know the matter that is outward in their religion, believe in it, act by it, and neither contradict it nor abandon it. That is the first frontier of education. The lowest degree of knowledge and understanding.

Not the contemptible degree, not the dispensable degree — the lowest in the sense of the first, the foundational, the one without which no higher rung can be reached. The one in whom the spirit of faith and wisdom is to be inscribed must begin from this point, and must pass from the realm of darkness into the realm of light. This is the first stage for the one newly born in faith, the stage of nurture at birth, when the spirit of life is breathed into him.

Just as the newborn emerges from the womb into the light of the world, his outward form is repaired by the cutting of the umbilical cord, which had been the link between what was and what was not yet of him, and his limbs are adjusted, his skin anointed, his swaddling tightened, lest his body slacken and his limbs become deformed, his joints weakened, his constitution distorted, or some other ruin overtake him. So too is the case of the one who does not repair the outward of his religion and establish it: he is on the verge of breaking his religion and ruining it.

That is why he furnished those who responded to the call with what they need in order to repair and rectify themselves. He read it out to them; he put it in the hands of those who wished to copy it; he did not deny it to those who wished to recite it in full. Each took from it according to his measure. Some, though they were few, benefited from it. And he cites the Quran in a stream: "Except for those few who believed and did righteous deeds, and how few they are" (verse twenty-four of the chapter Sad). "But most of them are insolently disobedient and do not hear" (verse fifty-nine of the chapter on the table). "But most people do not know" (verse one hundred and eighty-seven of the chapter on the heights). "How many people, even if you were eager, will not believe" (verse one hundred and three of the chapter on Joseph). "Among them are the believers, but most of them are transgressors" (verse one hundred and ten of the chapter on the family of Imran). "Among them are those who lie in wait, ignorant of the light" (verse one hundred and seven of the chapter on repentance). The renderings track closely with what the modern translator Muhammad Asad, in his work The Message of the Quran, calls the steady refrain of the revelation: that the message is offered freely, and that most who hear it turn aside.

He is conceding, in advance, that the book he is about to write will not be received by most people. This is not a defect in the book; it is the nature of the audience. The Quran itself reports it. Some of those who hear the outward say: this is what we know, what we see, what is useful, and turn away in disgust from anything deeper. Some say of the Messenger himself that he has been helped unjustly, and that what he brings is nothing but "fables of the ancients which he has written down; they are dictated to him morning and evening" (verse five of the chapter on the criterion). This is one of the ways of denying the limits of religion. They departed from what their friends had thought of them, disobeyed their command, altered the order arranged for them, transgressed their limits, and prevented others from fulfilling their duty. "They went astray before, and they led many astray, and they strayed from the right way" (verse seventy-seven of the chapter on the table).

The measure of the outward, and the share that each receives of it, though they be few of many, has not moved him to neglect them. He has resolved to raise them to the next stage. He calls that stage the limit of the responding initiate: the stage of symbol and sign, which is the gentlest and most accessible of the inner degrees.

After repairing their outward form at birth, they are begun on milk, because of the delicacy of their constitution before the possibility of solid food, and their inability to digest it until their bodies grow strong enough to bear it. They take it gradually. The symbols and signs presented at this stage do something more precise than simply introduce ideas: they stimulate the imagination and the senses of the listener, preparing the ground within him for the wisdom these signs point toward. This preparation creates a genuine yearning to hear more and a readiness to accept it that could not have been manufactured by force. So too with symbols and signs. These impregnate the imaginations of the listeners, who are stirred by what is symbolized to them, who are pointed to by wisdom, who are eager to hear it and ready to accept it, whereas if the same content were thrust upon them all at once, without preamble, and arrived upon them suddenly when their forces of comprehension were not yet equal to it, it would harm them, just as feeding solid food to children prematurely harms them. He cites the Quran directly, and the verse he chooses is not decorative: it is the full measure of an obligation. "Mothers shall suckle their children for two full years — for those who wish to complete the nursing period. The father of the child bears responsibility for the mothers' provision and clothing in a reasonable manner. No soul is burdened beyond its capacity. A mother shall not be harmed on account of her child, nor shall a father be harmed on account of his child. The same duty falls on the father's heir. If both parents desire weaning by mutual consent and consultation, there is no fault in them. And if you wish to have your children nursed by a foster mother, there is no fault in you, provided you hand over what you have given in a reasonable manner. And be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah sees all that you do" (verse two hundred and thirty-three of the chapter on the cow). The length of the verse is part of its meaning here: nursing is not a casual act but a sustained commitment governed by rights, consultation, and mutual responsibility — and the pedagogy of the inner degrees carries that same character. Two full years, not rushed, not cut short; the measure is set by God, not by the student's impatience or the teacher's convenience.

There is an earlier work of his, the book The Limits of Knowledge, in which he already established the limits explaining the proof of inner interpretation, known as ta'wil, against the one who denied it, and the proof of the inward sense against the one who rejected it, and the manner of ascending its degrees. That earlier work set forth the symbols and signs of the hidden and of inner interpretation that benefit the people of intellect, those whose way is the way of acceptance, of desire, of withdrawal from worldly attachments, and of asceticism.

When that earlier work reached a certain measure, those who desired to attain what they deserved obliged him to compose a further book unfolding a degree of ta'wil and subtlety beyond the degree of symbol. That is the present work, Foundations of Inner Interpretation. Whoever hears anything of it that seems to contradict its proper limits as he has set them forth, and does not act accordingly, referring it through himself to other than what he has heard, or interpreting it without the prior explanation that has been given, his way must be the way of those just mentioned: those who submit themselves to those who came before them, follow in their path, stand in their footsteps, and let themselves be clothed in the religion of their judgment. "That Allah may test those who believe and destroy the disbelievers" (verse one hundred and forty-one of the chapter on the family of Imran).

The speech of the truthful Imam — meaning the fifth Imam in the line, Ja'far al-Sadiq (peace be upon him) — reached some of the missionaries when he initiated them. A man said to him: "Son of the Messenger of Allah, we have heard from you before this time something other than this, a different aspect?" And the Imam said: "I speak one word with seven faces." The man, astonished, said: "Seven, son of the Messenger of Allah?" The Imam said: "Yes, and seventy. And were we to add more, we would add." The saying is preserved in al-Nu'man's own The Pillars of Islam, Book of Faith, in the chapter on the limits of interpretation, and is echoed in early collections of the sayings of Ja'far al-Sadiq. What the Imam is describing is not inconsistency or contradiction; he is describing the structure of living knowledge. Each face of the word corresponds to a level of comprehension — a rung on the ladder of understanding that the seeker has reached. The beginner hears the first face and is nourished by it. The one further along hears a second face in the same utterance, one that the beginner is not yet able to receive. At seventy faces, the same word is still speaking, still disclosing, still pointing. And the Imam's "were we to add more, we would add" is the most precise statement of this: the word of revelation is not finite in what it contains. Surface commentary reads the first face. Inner interpretation reads the faces beneath it. And the one entrusted with knowing which face belongs to which listener is the Imam.

This knowledge has been displayed in proportion to its limits, so that the one who has heard it and benefited from it may know it page by page in its degrees. The present book, which he has set forth in its foundational form, being the first of its limits, he has called Foundations of Inner Interpretation. By it, he means to explain what was established in The Pillars of Islam, so that this work may serve as the foundation for the hidden, just as that work was the foundation for the apparent. The body and the soul. With Allah, he closes the introduction, we seek help, there is no power and no strength except in Allah the Almighty.

He then turns to chapters of discourse on the interpretation of interpretation, and on the inner meaning of what is mentioned in the prophetic reports and in revelation. For every intelligible thing, there must be an outward and an inward. Its outward is what the senses perceive. Its inward is what it contains, the knowledge that resides within it, and the meaning that its outward form embraces. The two are each other's spouse and partner.

The outward and the inward are not in competition; they are married. Each needs the other to be itself. The body without the soul is a corpse. The soul without the body has nothing in the world to do. But this pairing goes deeper than metaphor: the body is composed of coldness and dryness, and the spirit is composed of heat and moisture. When the spirit departs, the body turns cold and dry — the very qualities that define a corpse are simply the body's qualities when its pairing is broken. The outward/inward relationship works the same way. Strip the outward form of its inward meaning and you do not have a half-truth; you have the spiritual equivalent of a corpse: something that once lived and no longer does. The two are not merely associated; they require each other in order to be what they are. He quotes the Quran: "And He has lavished upon you His blessings, outward and inward" (verse twenty of the chapter Luqman). God Himself has named the pairing, and informed His servants that He will question them concerning His blessings: "Then on that Day you will be asked about the bliss" (verse eight of the chapter on rivalry). Whoever does not know what the inner bliss is, though he knows that he is held responsible for it, how will he then answer when he is questioned about it?

Likewise with sin. "And forsake the outward and inward of sin" (verse one hundred and twenty of the chapter on the cattle). Whoever does not know the secret of sin, and is therefore warned of it, is he not in danger of falling into it precisely because he does not know it? Then the verse the whole tradition turns on: "It is He who has sent down to you the Book; in it are verses precise in meaning, they are the foundation of the Book, and others ambiguous" (verse seven of the chapter on the family of Imran). And: "And none knows its interpretation except Allah and those firmly rooted in knowledge" (verse seven of the chapter on the family of Imran). This is the same verse on which the Commander of the Faithful, in the collection of his sayings known as The Peak of Eloquence (compiled by al-Sharif al-Radi, in the rendering of Sayed Ali Reza), is reported to have said that he himself was among those firmly rooted in knowledge, and that no verse came down to the Prophet without his knowing where it came down, in whom it came down, and what it pointed toward. In his earlier work he set out the position of those who acknowledge inner interpretation but are ignorant of its proper face; the position of those who reject it and deny it altogether; and the proof against both groups. "Do they await anything but its interpretation? On the Day its interpretation comes, those who had forgotten it before will say: indeed the messengers of our Lord brought the truth" (verse fifty-three of the chapter on the heights).

The Messenger of Allah said: "Not a single verse of the Quran has been revealed to me without having an outer and an inner." Al-Nu'man cites this report in The Pillars of Islam, Book of Faith, and notes that the community itself reached agreement on its soundness, and on the corruption of any interpretation that twists it incorrectly.

Even those who reject inner interpretation have been compelled, unconsciously, to acknowledge it, because their own teachings hold that certain Quranic words cannot be taken in their outward sense. Take the word water. Their own teachings hold that Allah intended by it knowledge in one place, and death and disbelief in another, and life elsewhere meaning faith, and light meaning guidance, and darkness meaning error; and that the blind, the deaf, and the dumb mean something other than their outward sense. Yet, when they hear of such things which are obligatory in order to affirm the absolute oneness of Allah Almighty and to purify Him from any resemblance to His creation, they deny it and liken Him instead. They already practice inner interpretation. They only reject it when it touches the verses they would rather read on the surface. And the verses they would rather read on the surface are the ones about God Himself — exactly the verses where reading on the surface produces a God who looks like His creatures. He cites the parable-verses to confirm it: "Such are the parables We set forth for mankind, but none understand them except those endowed with knowledge" (verse forty-three of the chapter on the spider). "Indeed, Allah is not ashamed to set forth the parable of a mosquito or anything above it. Those who believe know that it is the truth from their Lord. But those who disbelieve say: what does Allah intend by this as an example? He leads many astray by it and guides many by it, and He does not lead astray by it except the defiantly disobedient" (verse twenty-six of the chapter on the cow). "And for each We set forth parables, and each We utterly destroyed" (verse thirty-nine of the chapter on the criterion). "And We have certainly set forth for the people in this Quran every kind of parable" (verse twenty-seven of the chapter on the throngs).

The Almighty informed us that the prophetic narratives themselves have an interpretation. He cites the story of Joseph: "And thus your Lord will choose you and teach you the interpretation of narratives" (verse six of the chapter on Joseph). And: "And thus We established Joseph in the land, that We might teach him the interpretation of narratives" (verse twenty-one of the chapter on Joseph). In many verses of His Book, God mentions parables, subtleties, and interpretation. It is among the miracles and wonders of the Quran's composition that He brings forth a single utterance which has one meaning in its outward form and another meaning in its inward form. The Almighty made its outward form the miracle of His Messenger, and its inner form the miracle of the Imams of his Household. The inner exists only with them: no one can match the outward of the Book except the Prophet, the Messenger of Allah, who is their grandfather; and no one can match the inner except the Imams from his descendants.

It is a knowledge held among them. They speak to each one to the measure he can comprehend; they give every member of his family what he deserves; they withhold from one who must be withheld from; they entrust it to those who deserve to receive it, as the Bestower said: "This is Our gift, so bestow or withhold without reckoning" (verse thirty-nine of the chapter Sad). Now, with Allah's help and support, we begin to set down the interpretation of what was laid out concisely in The Pillars of Islam. This is the boundary at which we begin, in the language of the one whose hearing and measure of understanding you know. "And We did not send any Messenger except in the tongue of his people, so that he might make it clear to them" (verse four of the chapter on Abraham). For if the general people were addressed in specialized language, they would understand less of the speech than they do. The doctrine emerges from inside the revealed text rather than from outside it. "Upon Allah we rely; He is our Lord, and He is the One in whose care we are entrusted" (verse fifty-one of the chapter on repentance, paraphrased). With that, the introduction proper ends. What follows is the first chapter.

The treatment is titled, simply, Faith. In The Pillars of Islam he gave separate treatments to faith and to the distinction between faith and submission. Here, he has gathered their interpretations into a single treatment, since at root they share one origin, and this is where the inner work must begin.

Each of the foremost Messengers — and the "foremost in resolve" in his tradition are Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and the Prophet, while Adam is not counted among them because the Quranic verse was revealed concerning him: "And We had already taken a covenant from Adam before, but he forgot, and We found in him no firm determination" (verse one hundred and fifteen of the chapter Ta-Ha) — each of the foremost Messengers instituted two foundations of his law. An outward foundation, and an inward continuation of it. Together, the two comprise the limits of the sacred law.

Noah instituted the ark, and called those who believed in him to embark with him; whoever did not embark drowned and perished. Abraham raised up the Sacred House, and proclaimed the pilgrimage to the people, calling them to circumambulate it and to perform its rites. Moses took up the staff, and cast it at the one who gave him the lie, and it overpowered him from his enchanter. Jesus instituted the cross, and said to those who believed in him and trusted him: "Whoever wishes to follow me, let him take up a cross with him." And the foundation of the Prophet is the testimony that there is no god but Allah; he summoned the people to it, and they believed in it. He said: "I have been commanded to fight the people until they say with me, 'There is no god but Allah.' If they say it, they have protected from me their blood and their wealth, except for what it rightly requires, and their reckoning is with Allah." Al-Nu'man cites this saying in The Pillars of Islam, Book of Jihad, on the conditions of the testimony.

The outward of these foundations differs. The ark is not the Sacred House. The Sacred House is not the staff. The staff is not the cross. The cross is not the testimony. Each is something other than the rest. But their inward, and this is the load-bearing claim, is the same. Each foundation contains all the limits of the sacred law of its bearer, all that Allah Almighty sent him with to be worshipped by. Otherwise, the messenger would not have summoned the people to that foundation, since it would not have been a comprehensive container of all by which Allah is to be worshipped. The ark contained Noah's whole law. The Sacred House contained Abraham's whole law. The testimony contained the whole law of the Prophet. Not in the sense that the outer object literally held them, but in the sense that the act of believing in the foundation was an act of accepting the entire law that the bearer was sent with.

The general public of this community claimed that the Messenger of Allah called people at the outset of the religion only to bear witness that there is no god but Allah, because that was easier for the people. Then, when they answered him, he obliged them to the easier of the obligatory duties, and progressed onward up to the struggle on the path of God, in which souls and wealth are spent. This is a statement contradicted by the wise, because it amounts to a form of deception. Where enticement is concerned, one does not invite to a pledge of allegiance, nor establish one's claim to anything, except by laying out the totality of what is required. The one called to pledge does not pledge allegiance unless the sum of what he is obliged to is held at the call.

The Messenger of Allah, when he called for the sincere saying of "There is no god but Allah," made Paradise obligatory for whoever made it known sincerely. He was asked about sincerity in this, and he said: "Belief in it, and knowledge of its limits." He said: "The saying 'There is no god but Allah' continues to ward off the wrath of the Lord from this nation, so long as they do not detract from their religion. But when one of them does so, his words will be turned back upon him." Both sayings are preserved in The Pillars of Islam, Book of Faith, in the chapter on the conditions of the testimony.

He invokes the truthful Imam directly. The Imam said, among the sayings narrated from him in The Pillars of Islam: "Faith is a saying with the tongue, a conviction in the heart, and an action with the limbs." And his saying: "Faith is wholly action, and saying is part of that action." All of this makes it necessary that the testimony comprehend the whole of what the sacred law requires. The one who stands in the secret of the wisdom that Allah has entrusted to His friends sees that the testimony "There is no god but Allah" contained the affirmation of Allah's oneness, the acknowledgement of His Messenger, the Imam of His religion and his limits, the obligation of obedience to them, the bond of allegiance toward them, and acceptance of their command. It contained all by which Allah is worshipped. Therefore it became the foundation of the sacred law. The Prophet called upon it and made Paradise obligatory for whoever testifies to it sincerely. He showed that its sincerity is its belief, the knowledge of its limits, and the performance of its rights.

He grounds it again in the Quran. This is what Allah said in the story of Abraham: when his Lord said to him, "Submit," he said, "I have submitted to the Lord of the Worlds" (verse one hundred and thirty-one of the chapter on the cow). And Abraham charged this upon his sons, and so did Jacob: "My sons, Allah has chosen for you the religion, so do not die except in submission, and I am submitted" (verse one hundred and thirty-two of the chapter on the cow). Submission is the testimony to Allah by oneness, and to the Messenger by the message. Whoever bears witness to it is obliged to bear witness in truth and sincerity.

It is obligatory in ruling that whoever bears witness to a testimony of which he is not adequate, and does not stand upon its truths, and does not know him for whom or against whom he is testifying, his testimony is not accepted from him, and is rebutted in due course. How then could a testimony be accepted in the greatest of all matters, when it is its postponement? With this great testimony, whose magnitude its bearer testifies to, and whose danger is great, knowing its reality and bearing witness to it outwardly and inwardly, believing in it and fulfilling its obligations and conditions, is obligatory upon the one who testifies to it — only then is it accepted from him, and his reward is not turned away. When he is ignorant of any of it, or denies it, his testimony is not complete, and is not accepted.

The inner reading of the testimony is this: the one who stands in the secret of the wisdom that Allah has entrusted to His friends sees that "There is no god but Allah" contained the affirmation of Allah's oneness, the acknowledgement of His Messenger, the Imam of His religion and his limits, and the obligation of obedience to them. It contained all by which Allah is worshipped. The letters of the testimony are what express it. What is signified by it is what is within it, since it is an expression of what is uttered. The truthful Imam said, in a saying al-Nu'man cites in The Pillars of Islam, Book of Faith, in the chapter on the inner meaning of the testimony: "When Allah first created the letters, He made known by the letters the names, and by the names He made known the people." So the Imam continued: empty your hearts of created beings to hear wisdom; accept it, and govern it as it has the right to be governed, and its lights will shine for you, and it will unveil to you its realities and its secrets. For this is the testimony that there is no god but Allah, which is obligatory.

Outwardly, this means that Allah Almighty is the One and only God, with no partner: He is alone in divinity, and He is one as none of His creatures is one. The name "one" may fall upon a creature — and this is so in its outer aspect — but if one returns from it to the inner secondary sense, that creature is paired, and is in need of what it is joined to and compared with. Likewise, if it is considered as a body and a soul, the body is the outer and the soul is the inner. Nothing in the world is without two essences, nor without two qualities, either hot and humid, or hot and dry, or cold and humid, or cold and dry; nor without two conditions, either witnessed or absent; nor without two forms, either small or large. The Almighty mentioned all of these clearly from among the conditions of His creation, yet He cannot be described by any of them. He is not non-existent; rather, He exists. He is established as the Sign, and the signs are what He created. He is clear of all of them, and nothing resembles Him, and nothing is like Him. Everything in the created order is paired. The pairing is the structure of creation. Allah, the Creator, is described by none of the pairs. And the testimony "There is no god but Allah," read inwardly, is the affirmation of exactly that: that the One who established the paired creation is Himself outside the paired creation, and that to confess Him is to refuse to assign Him any of the descriptions that hold for the things He made.

The Almighty said: "But of it there is one whose interpretation will yet come" (verse thirty-nine of the chapter on Jonah) — a verse whose meaning will only become clear in its own time. The general public take this as a metaphor by which God addressed the Arabs in their own tongue, since someone who says "he is not like me" or "he is not like so-and-so" is invoking only the name and nothing further. And Allah has said, in a divine-saying preserved in al-Nu'man's The Pillars of Islam, Book of Faith, in the chapter on the inner meaning of obedience (rendered here directly from the Arabic): "Son of Adam, obey Me, and I will make you like Myself — living without dying, mighty without being humbled, rich without being impoverished." All of this has an explanation whose remembrance will come, God willing, in its proper place, and were we to elaborate fully here, the exposition of the testimony would be cut short.
