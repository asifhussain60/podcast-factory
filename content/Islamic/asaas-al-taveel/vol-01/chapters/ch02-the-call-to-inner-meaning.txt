This chapter is al-Nu'man's own opening of his book — first his Author's Introduction, in which he explains why a second book was needed after the apparent-law volume Da'a'im al-Islam (daa-im al-i-slaam), and then his first doctrinal chapter, the Chapter on Faith. The two run as one arc. The introduction argues that inner interpretation, ta'wil (taa-WEEL), must be taught by degrees — that a responder to the call cannot be fed the inner meaning all at once any more than a newborn can be fed solid food — and that the entire project of Asas al-Ta'wil (ah-SAAS al-taa-WEEL) is the gentle next step after the law. The faith chapter then opens the actual doctrinal work by asking what the testimony "There is no god but Allah" really contained, from the very first day the Prophet Muhammad (mu-HAM-mad) spoke it. Al-Nu'man's answer is that the shahada (sha-HAA-dah) contained everything from the start — every obligation, every limit of the sacred law, the apparent and the inward together — and that the common opinion which says the Prophet began with an easy creed and only later added the harder duties is, in his view, a kind of misreading that turns the message into bait. Listeners who walk through this chapter will leave with two things they will need across the rest of the book: a clear sense of why inner interpretation has to be staged, and a clear sense of why the whole of the religion is folded into the shahada as a seed is folded into a husk.

Al-Nu'man opens in the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful, and praises Allah (al-LAH), the Bestower of grace, the Mufid. The early footnote tells us that one manuscript reads Mu'ti, Giver, and another reads Mufid, Bestower or Conferrer, and that the editor regards the second as the better reading. The author then blesses Muhammad, the Prophet of mercy, the wasi (wa-see) — the executor appointed for the umma (UM-ma) — and the pure Imams from his goodly progeny. It is a deliberate opening: Prophet, executor, Imams, in one breath. The whole structural claim of the book is folded into the salutation.

He then states, formally, what he has already done and what he is now doing. He has already published, with the support of Allah, a book for those who respond to the call of truth — those who cling to the rope of the Creator, who hold fast to the trustworthy handhold, who adhere to the straightest path — a book treating the outward knowledge of the shari'a (sha-REE-ah). He called it Da'a'im al-Islam, the Pillars of Islam. In that book, he says, he set forth the limits of faith and the distinction between iman (ee-MAAN) and islam (IS-laam); the obligation of walaya (wa-LAA-ya); the proofs of the Imams; the requirements of duty; the chapters of the lawful and the forbidden; the questions and the rulings — so that those who respond to the call might know the matter that is outward in their religion, believe in it, act by it, and neither contradict it nor abandon it. That, he says, is the first frontier of education. The lowest degree of knowledge and understanding.

The phrase is important. The lowest degree. Not the contemptible degree, not the dispensable degree — the lowest in the sense of the first, the foundational, the one without which no higher rung can be reached. The one in whom the spirit of faith and wisdom is to be inscribed must begin from this point, he says, and must pass from the realm of darkness into the realm of light. This is the first stage for the one newly born in faith — the stage of nurture at birth, when the spirit of life is breathed into him.

Then comes the image al-Nu'man stretches across the whole book. Just as the newborn emerges from the womb into the light of the world, he says, his outward form is repaired by the cutting of the umbilical cord — which had been the link between what was and what was not yet of him — and his limbs are adjusted, his skin anointed, his swaddling tightened, lest his body slacken and his limbs become deformed, his joints weakened, his constitution distorted, or some other ruin overtake him. So too is the case of the one who does not repair the outward of his religion and establish it: he is on the verge of breaking his religion and ruining it.

That is why, the author says, he furnished those who responded to the call with what they need in order to repair and rectify themselves. He read it out to them; he put it in the hands of those who wished to copy it; he did not deny it to those who wished to recite it in full. Each took from it according to his measure. Some — though they were few — benefited from it. And here he begins to cite Qur'an (kur-AAN) in a stream. "Except for those few who believed and did righteous deeds — and how few they are." "But most of them are insolently disobedient and do not hear." "But most people do not know." "How many people, even if you were eager, will not believe." "Among them are the believers, but most of them are transgressors." "Among them are those who lie in wait, ignorant of the light."

The verses pile up because al-Nu'man is doing something specific. He is conceding, in advance, that the book he is about to write will not be received by most people. He is saying that this is not a defect in the book; it is the nature of the audience. The Qur'an itself reports it. Some of those who hear the outward say: this is what we know, what we see, what is useful — and turn away in disgust from anything deeper. Some say of the Messenger himself that he has been helped unjustly, and that what he brings is nothing but "fables of the ancients which he has written down; they are dictated to him morning and evening." This, the author says, is one of the ways of denying the limits of religion. They departed from what their friends had thought of them, disobeyed their command, altered the order arranged for them, transgressed their limits, and prevented others from fulfilling their duty. "They went astray before, and they led many astray, and they strayed from the right way."

He shifts tone. The measure of the outward, and the share that each receives of it — though they be few of many — has not moved him to neglect them, he says. He has resolved to raise them to the next stage. He calls that stage the limit of the responding initiate, the mustajib (mu-sta-jib): the stage of symbol, ramz (ramz), and sign, ishara (i-SHAA-ra), which is the gentlest and most accessible of the inner degrees.

Then the second image, the one that orders the whole pedagogical claim of the book. This is parallel, he says, to the raising of children and the nourishment of their bodies. After repairing their outward form at birth, they are begun on milk — because of the delicacy of their constitution before the possibility of solid food, and their inability to digest it until their bodies grow strong enough to bear it. They take it gradually. So too with symbols and signs. These impregnate the imaginations of the listeners, who are stirred by what is symbolized to them, who are pointed to by wisdom, who are eager to hear it and ready to accept it — whereas if the same content were thrust upon them all at once, without preamble, and arrived upon them suddenly when their forces of comprehension were not yet equal to it, it would harm them, just as feeding solid food to children prematurely harms them. He cites the Qur'an directly. "And mothers shall suckle their children two full years, for those who wish to complete the suckling."

This is the heart of the introduction. Inner interpretation is not held back because it is dangerous in itself. It is staged because human beings come to it with constitutions that are still forming. The teacher who would not hesitate to put solid food in front of an adult will starve a newborn by doing the same thing. The author is telling his reader, in advance, that the book in his hand is the second course, not the first; that there is a Hudud al-Ma'arifa (hu-DOOD al-ma-AA-ri-fa), The Limits of Knowledge, which the editor's footnote tells us was found in numerous copies at the town of Masyaf (MAS-yaaf) in Syria, and in which al-Nu'man already established the limits explaining the proof of inner interpretation against the one who denied it, and the proof of the inner sense — al-batin (al-ba-tin), the hidden — against the one who rejected it, and the manner of ascending its degrees. That earlier work, he says, set forth the symbols and signs of the hidden and of inner interpretation that benefit the people of intellect — those whose way is the way of acceptance, of desire, of withdrawal from worldly attachments, and of asceticism.

When that earlier work reached a certain measure, he says, those who desired to attain what they deserved obliged him to compose a further book unfolding a degree of inner interpretation and subtlety beyond the degree of symbol. That is the book in our hands. Whoever hears anything of it that seems to contradict its proper limits as he has set them forth, and does not act accordingly — referring it through himself to other than what he has heard, or interpreting it without the prior explanation that has been given — his way must be the way of those just mentioned: those who submit themselves to those who came before them, follow in their path, stand in their footsteps, and let themselves be clothed in the religion of their judgment. "That Allah may test those who believe and destroy the disbelievers."

Then a small, very characteristic Ismaili (is-maa-EE-lee) moment. If they had only understood, al-Nu'man says, the speech of the truthful one, the trustee — meaning Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq (JAA-far as-SAA-diq), as a footnote spells out — for it reached them when he initiated some of his preachers, his du'at (doo-AAT), into it. A man said to him: "O son of the Messenger of Allah, we have heard from you before this time something other than this — a different aspect?" And the Imam said: "I speak one word with seven faces." The man, astonished, said: "Seven, O son of the Messenger of Allah?" The Imam said: "Yes — and seventy. And were we to add more, we would add."

This is the saying that authorizes the whole project. One word can have seven faces. The same passage of revelation can carry seven readings, seventy readings, more — and the variations are not a defect of the word; they are the word's depth. Tafsir (taf-SEER) reads the face of the word. Ta'wil reads the seventy faces beneath it. And the one entrusted with the reading of those faces is the Imam.

Al-Nu'man steps back to introduce his own title. This knowledge has been displayed in proportion to its limits, he says, so that the one who has heard it and benefited from it may know it page by page in its degrees. The present book, which he has set forth in its foundational form — being the first of its limits — he has called Asas al-Ta'wil, The Basis of Interpretation. By it, he says, he means to explain what was established in Da'a'im al-Islam, so that this work may serve as the foundation for the hidden, just as that work was the foundation for the apparent. The body and the soul. With Allah, he closes the introduction, we seek help — there is no power and no strength except in Allah the Almighty.

The next section is a transitional one that the editor labels Chapters of Discourse on the Interpretation of Interpretation, and on the Inner Meaning of What Is Mentioned in Hadith (ha-DEETH) and Revelation. Here al-Nu'man begins to make the philosophical case that grounds everything that follows. For every intelligible thing, he says, there must be an outward and an inward. Its outward is what the senses perceive. Its inward is what it contains — the knowledge that resides within it, and the meaning that its outward form embraces. The two are each other's spouse and partner.

The metaphor is striking. The outward and the inward are not in competition; they are married. Each needs the other to be itself. The body without the soul is a corpse. The soul without the body has nothing in the world to do. He quotes the Qur'an: "And He has lavished upon you His blessings, outward and inward." God Himself, he says, has named the pairing — and informed His servants that He will question them concerning His blessings: "Then on that Day you will be asked about the bliss." Whoever does not know what the inner bliss is, though he knows that he is held responsible for it, how will he then answer when he is questioned about it?

Likewise with sin. "And forsake the outward and inward of sin." Whoever does not know the secret of sin, and is therefore warned of it — is he not in danger of falling into it precisely because he does not know it? Then the verse the whole tradition turns on: "It is He who has sent down to you the Book; in it are verses precise in meaning — they are the foundation of the Book — and others ambiguous." And: "And none knows its interpretation except Allah and those firmly rooted in knowledge." In Hudud al-Ma'arifa, he reminds us, he set out the position of those who acknowledge inner interpretation but are ignorant of its proper face; the position of those who reject it and deny it altogether; and the proof against both groups. "Do they await anything but its interpretation? On the Day its interpretation comes, those who had forgotten it before will say: indeed the messengers of our Lord brought the truth."

Then the Prophetic hadith. The Messenger of Allah, he says, said: "Not a single verse of the Qur'an has been revealed to me without having an outer and an inner." In The Limits of Knowledge, al-Nu'man notes, he already mentioned the consensus of the Umma (UM-ma) on the soundness of this report, and the corruption of any interpretation that twists it incorrectly.

He turns then to a point of polemic that does the philosophical work elegantly. He says that even those who reject inner interpretation have been compelled, unconsciously, to acknowledge it — because their own teachings hold that certain Qur'anic words cannot be taken in their outward sense. Take, he says, the word water. Their own teachings hold that Allah intended by it knowledge in one place, and death and disbelief in another, and life elsewhere meaning faith, and light meaning guidance, and darkness meaning error; and that the blind, the deaf, and the dumb mean something other than their outward sense; and that the "sheep of women," and many others of this sort, are explained at length in this way. Yet, when they hear of such things which are obligatory in order to affirm the absolute oneness of Allah Almighty and to purify Him from any resemblance to His creation, they deny it and liken Him instead. They set up their idol and described Him by what Allah transcends.

It is a sharp move. He is saying: you already practice ta'wil. You already read water as knowledge. You already read light as guidance. You only reject inner interpretation when it touches the verses you would rather read on the surface. And the verses you would rather read on the surface are the ones about God Himself — exactly the verses where reading on the surface produces a God who looks like His creatures. The denial of ta'wil, in other words, is the engine of anthropomorphism. He cites the parable-verses to confirm it: "Such are the parables We set forth for mankind, but none understand them except those endowed with knowledge." "Indeed, Allah is not ashamed to set forth the parable of a mosquito or anything above it." "And for each We set forth parables, and each We utterly destroyed." "And We have certainly set forth for the people in this Qur'an every kind of parable."

He turns to the prophetic narratives. The Almighty informed us, he says, that the prophetic narratives themselves have an interpretation. He cites the story of Yusuf (YOO-suf): "And thus your Lord will choose you and teach you the interpretation of narratives." And: "And thus We established Joseph in the land, that We might teach him the interpretation of narratives." In many verses of His Book, he says, God mentions parables, subtleties, and interpretation. This is well known in the tongue of the Arabs, in which the Qur'an was revealed and in which God addressed them. It is among the miracles and wonders of the Qur'an's composition that He brings forth a single utterance which has one meaning in its outward form and another meaning in its inward form. The Almighty made its outward form the miracle of His Messenger, and its inner form the miracle of the Imams of his Household. The inner exists only with them: no one can match the outward of the Book except Muhammad, the Messenger of Allah, who is their grandfather; and no one can match the inner except the Imams from his descendants.

It is a knowledge held among them, he says. They speak to each one to the measure he can comprehend; they give every member of his family what he deserves; they withhold from one who must be withheld from; they entrust it to those who deserve to receive it — as al-Wahhab (al-wah-HAAB) said: "This is Our gift, so bestow or withhold without reckoning." Now, he says, with Allah's help and support, we begin to set down what we promised — the interpretation of what was laid out concisely in The Pillars of Islam. And he adds a small but very important methodological note. This is the boundary at which we begin, he says, in the language of the one whose hearing and measure of understanding you know. "And We did not send any Messenger except in the tongue of his people, so that he might make it clear to them." For if the general people were addressed in specialized language, they would understand less of the speech than they do. By addressing the audience, he means to address them at the level of their understanding. He has not taken them out, he says, through the exits used by the people of logic and philosophy, as some preachers have done.

This is a humble but crucial note. Al-Nu'man is writing a philosophy book, but he is not writing it in the language of the philosophers' schools. He is writing it in the tongue of his audience. He is keeping the Qur'an and the hadith at the center and letting the doctrine emerge from inside them rather than from outside them. "Upon Allah we rely; He is our Lord, and He is the One in whose care we are entrusted." With that, the introduction proper ends. What follows is the first chapter.

The chapter is titled, simply, Faith. Al-Nu'man notes that in The Pillars of Islam he gave separate chapters to faith and to the distinction between faith and Islam. Here, he says, he has gathered their interpretations into a single chapter, since at root they share one origin, and this is where the inner work must begin.

He begins from a structural claim that is going to organize the rest of the book. Each of the foremost Messengers, he says — and a footnote pauses here to remind the reader that the "foremost in resolve" in Ismaili belief are Noah (NOO-ah), Ibrahim (ib-rah-HEEM), Moses (MOO-saa), Jesus (EE-saa), and Muhammad, and that Adam (AA-dam) is not counted among them because the Qur'anic verse was revealed concerning him: "And We had already taken a covenant from Adam before, but he forgot, and We found in him no firm resolve" — each of the foremost Messengers instituted two foundations of his law. An outward foundation, and an inward continuation of it. Together, the two comprise the limits of the sacred law.

He walks through them. Noah instituted the ark, and called those who believed in him to embark with him; whoever did not embark drowned and perished. Ibrahim raised up the Ka'bah (KAA-ba), and proclaimed the Hajj (HAJJ) to the people, calling them to circumambulate it and to perform its rites. Moses took up the staff, and cast it at the one who gave him the lie, and it overpowered him from his enchanter. Jesus instituted the cross, and said to those who believed in him and trusted him: "Whoever wishes to follow me, let him take up a cross with him." And the foundation of Muhammad is the testimony that there is no god but Allah; he summoned the people to it, and they believed in it. He said: "I have been commanded to fight the people until they say with me, 'There is no god but Allah.' If they say it, they have protected from me their blood and their wealth, except for what it rightly requires, and their reckoning is with Allah."

The outward of these foundations differs, he notes. The ark is not the Ka'bah. The Ka'bah is not the staff. The staff is not the cross. The cross is not the testimony. Each is something other than the rest. But their inward — and this is the load-bearing claim — is the same. Each foundation contains all the limits of the sacred law of its bearer, all that Allah Almighty sent him with to be worshipped by. Otherwise, he says, the messenger would not have summoned the people to that foundation, since it would not have been a comprehensive container of all by which Allah is to be worshipped. The ark contained Noah's whole law. The Ka'bah contained Ibrahim's whole law. The shahada contained Muhammad's whole law. Not in the sense that the outer object literally held them — but in the sense that the act of believing in the foundation was an act of accepting the entire law that the bearer was sent with.

This is the place where he opens his disagreement with the common opinion of the community. The general public of this community, he says, claimed that the Messenger of Allah called people at the outset of Islam only to bear witness that there is no god but Allah, because that was easier for the people. Then, when they answered him, he obliged them to the easier of the obligatory duties, and progressed onward up to jihad (ji-HAAD) — in which souls and wealth are spent — and according to some, the last thing imposed was the wilaya. This is a statement, al-Nu'man says, contradicted by the wise, because it amounts to a form of deception.

The polemic is precise. Where enticement is concerned, he says, one does not invite to a pledge of allegiance, nor establish one's claim to anything, except by laying out the totality of what is required. The one called to pledge does not pledge allegiance unless the sum of what he is obliged to is held at the call. The caller then clarifies it, expounds it, and explains its meanings — as when one calls another to the religion of Islam and that person pledges allegiance to it, since religion is a general statement within which obligation is established. Then it becomes clear to the one who answered, and is explained to him.

The Messenger of Allah, when he called for the sincere saying of "There is no god but Allah," made Paradise obligatory for whoever made it known sincerely. He was asked about sincerity in this — what it is — and he said: "Belief in it, and knowledge of its limits." It has its conditions. He said: "The saying 'There is no god but Allah' continues to ward off the wrath of the Lord from this nation, so long as they do not detract from their religion. But when one of them does so, his words will be turned back upon him, and it will be said to him: 'You have lied; you are not among the truthful.'"

He invokes al-Sadiq directly. Al-Sadiq said, among the sayings narrated from him in The Pillars of Islam: "Faith is a saying with the tongue, a conviction in the heart, and an action with the limbs." And his saying: "Faith is wholly action, and saying is part of that action." And what he described of the division of faith among the parts of a person — that each part is entrusted with an action that constitutes faith, distinct from what is entrusted to the others. All of this, al-Nu'man says, makes it necessary that the testimony comprehend the whole of what the sacred law requires. The Prophet's practice itself confirms it: when he stood against the polytheists he invited them to it; if they answered him and bore witness, he brought them under the rule of Islam and ceased fighting them, and their wealth was made safe except for what Islam obliges upon it. If they refused, he called them to pay the jizya (JIZ-yah) from their own hand while in submission. If they refused that, he fought them. He held that the outward sacred law was inseparable from the rule of Islam. The jizya substitutes for the testimony and for the acknowledgement of the Prophet. Likewise, it is necessary for the believer received under a covenant to acknowledge it.

He grounds it again in Qur'an. This is what Allah said in the story of Ibrahim: when his Lord said to him, "Submit," he said, "I have submitted to the Lord of the Worlds" — meaning, al-Nu'man notes, the One who raised him with wisdom. And Ibrahim charged this upon his sons, and so did Yaqub (ya-QOOB): "O my sons, Allah has chosen for you the religion, so do not die except as Muslims, and I am a Muslim." Islam, he then states with great compression, is the testimony to Allah by oneness, and to the Messenger by the message. Whoever bears witness to it is obliged to bear witness in truth and sincerity; if he does not, his account is with Allah, as the Messenger said: "His testimony has not lost him anything with Allah — if Allah does not save him by it, He still rewards him for it."

But this concession does not weaken the structural point. It is obligatory in ruling, he says, that whoever bears witness to a testimony of which he is not adequate, and does not stand upon its truths, and does not know him for whom or against whom he is testifying — his testimony is not accepted from him, and is rebutted in due course. How then could a testimony be accepted in the greatest of all matters, when it is its postponement? With this great testimony, whose magnitude its bearer testifies to, and whose danger is great, knowing its reality and bearing witness to it outwardly and inwardly, believing in it and fulfilling its obligations and conditions, is obligatory upon the one who testifies to it — only then is it accepted from him, and his reward is not turned away. When he is ignorant of any of it, or denies it, his testimony is not complete, and is not accepted.

He gives the inner reading. The one who stands in the secret of the wisdom that Allah has entrusted to His friends — having made it a miracle for them — sees that the testimony "There is no god but Allah" contained the affirmation of Allah's oneness, the acknowledgement of His Messenger, the Imam of His religion and his limits, the obligation of obedience to them, walaya toward them, and acceptance of their command. It contained all by which Allah is worshipped. Therefore it became the foundation of the sacred law. The Prophet called upon it and made Paradise obligatory for whoever testifies to it sincerely. He showed that its sincerity is its belief, the knowledge of its limits, and the performance of its rights. The letters of the testimony are what express it. What is signified by it is what is within it, since it is an expression of what is uttered.

That is why al-Sadiq said: "When Allah first created the letters, He made known by the letters the names, and by the names He made known the people." So, the Imam continued: empty your hearts of created beings to hear wisdom; accept it, and govern it as it has the right to be governed, and its lights will shine for you, and it will unveil to you its realities and its secrets. For this is the testimony that there is no god but Allah, which is obligatory.

Al-Nu'man then gives the testimony its outward reading, and immediately turns it inward. Outwardly, he says, this means that Allah Almighty is the One and only God, with no partner: He is alone in divinity, and He is one as none of His creatures is one. The name "one" may fall upon a creature — and this is so in its outer aspect — but if one returns from it to the inner secondary sense, that creature is paired, and is in need of what it is joined to and compared with. Like the human being and the other animals, each of whom is one in number, yet stands as a composite. Likewise, he says, if it is considered as a body and a soul, the body is the outer and the soul is the inner, as we have mentioned above.

He widens the point. Nothing in the world is without two essences, nor without two qualities — either hot and humid, or hot and dry, or cold and humid, or cold and dry; nor without two conditions — either witnessed or absent; nor without two forms — either small or large; nor without two kinds — either animal or human, fine or dense, heavy or light. The contrasts and oppositions are too many to list. The Almighty mentioned all of these clearly from among the conditions of His creation, yet He cannot be described by any of them. He is not non-existent; rather, He exists. He is established as the Sign, and the signs are what He created. He is clear of all of them, and nothing resembles Him, and nothing is like Him.

That is the move that will set up the next chapter. Everything in the created order is paired — outward and inward, body and soul, hot and cold, witnessed and absent. The pairing is the structure of creation. Allah, the Creator, is described by none of the pairs. He is the Sign without which the signs would not be. And the testimony "There is no god but Allah," read inwardly, is the affirmation of exactly that: that the One who established the paired creation is Himself outside the paired creation, and that to confess Him is to refuse to assign Him any of the descriptions that hold for the things He made.

Al-Nu'man closes the chapter with one final restraint. The Almighty said, he reminds us: "But of it there is one whose interpretation will yet come" — a verse, he says, whose meaning will only become clear in its own time. The general public take this as a metaphor by which God addressed the Arabs in their own tongue, since someone who says "he is not like me" or "he is not like so-and-so" is invoking only the name and nothing further. This is pure analogy, and the Arabs have many proverbs of this kind. And Allah has said: "O son of Adam, obey Me, and I will make you like Myself — living without dying, mighty without being humbled, rich without being impoverished." All of this, the author says, has an explanation whose remembrance will come, God willing, in its proper place — and were we to elaborate fully here, the exposition of the shahada would be cut short.

So he stops. He has done what he set out to do. He has explained why a second book had to be written: because the responder to the call grows by stages, and the inner cannot be served all at once. He has explained why inner interpretation is not optional but necessary: because the outward and the inward are spouse and partner, and because even those who reject ta'wil already practice it whenever they read water as knowledge or light as guidance. He has explained why the testimony — the shahada that the Prophet placed at the beginning of his call — already contained the whole religion, against the common opinion that says it was only a starter creed. And he has set the testimony's inner sense for the next chapter to unfold: that every created thing is paired, and that to confess "There is no god but Allah" is to confess the One who is paired with nothing. The next chapter will take up what those paired creatures actually are, and how the testimony names not only the Creator but the chain of teachers — Speaker, Imam-Foundation, and their limits — through whom His oneness is preserved and transmitted. That is where the next episode begins.
