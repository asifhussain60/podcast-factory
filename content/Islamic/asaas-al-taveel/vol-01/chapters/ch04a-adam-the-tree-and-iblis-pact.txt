This chapter opens al-Nu'man's interpretation of the prophetic cycles themselves. He has already laid out the structural diagram of the book in the previous chapter — the shahada (sha-HAA-dah) has four words and four limits, two above and two below, and the architecture extends outward into the seven Speaker-Prophets, the seven Imams per cycle, the twelve successors, and the nineteen entrusted ones. Now he begins to walk that architecture through history, starting where the lower hadd (hadd) of Imamate itself begins — with Adam (AA-dam), peace be upon him. The chapter is a continuous unfolding of one cosmology. Al-Nu'man takes the listener from the Qur'anic scene of the angels' objection, through the formation of Adam from clay and the long pause before the spirit is breathed into him, through Iblis's (IB-lees) refusal to prostrate and his transformation into Satan (al-shay-TAAN), through the creation of Eve (Hawwa, haw-wa) as Adam's hujja (HUJ-jah) in place of Iblis, through the inner meaning of the Garden as the ranks of supportive knowledge — al-ta'yid (al-taa-YEED) — and finally through the forbidden tree, which is not a tree of wheat or fig but a likeness for the Master of the Resurrection (Sahib al-Qiyama, sa-hib al-ki-YAA-ma) and the inner disclosure that has been entrusted to him alone. The chapter closes with the famous pact that Iblis extracts after his expulsion — the request for respite, the boast that he will share with Adam's children in their wealth and offspring — and with Adam's repentance, which restores him to the rank of caliph and Imam even after the fall. Throughout, al-Nu'man is doing one thing: he is showing that the Qur'anic story of Adam, read on its inner side, is the story of how the imamate enters the world, of how a foundation is supplied for it when its first proof becomes its first enemy, and of how the testimony to that hidden order survives even the breach that opens within it. The listener who walks through this episode will see why, for al-Nu'man, the first lesson of prophecy is not creation in the abstract but the way support is taught, withdrawn, and restored.

He begins by gathering the seven. We have already mentioned, he says, the interpretation of faith and Islam, which is the opening of the chapters of The Pillars of Islam — the book in which we have undertaken to simplify the interpretation of what it contains. The door of walaya (wa-LAA-ya), the guardianship, is recited for the Messengers and for the first of those in authority whose obedience Allah has joined to obedience to Himself. He cites the Qur'an (kur-AAN): "Obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you." We have gathered, he says, the interpretation of what is said about them, for Allah has gathered them together in the duty of obedience, even if their degrees and ranks differ. The pronouncement is a single matter of knowledge. They are: Adam, Noah (NOO-ah), Abraham (ib-rah-HEEM), Moses (MOO-saa), Jesus (EE-saa), Muhammad (mu-HAM-mad), and the seventh of them — the Qa'im of the Age (al-QAA-im). Then there follow, in their virtue, the Asas-foundations (al-ah-SAAS) that He established from among them in their lifetime, to carry out the inner meaning. The Asas rises after the Natiq (NAA-tiq) and succeeds him, becoming an Imam (i-MAAM), and establishing for himself a Hujjah to take his place — likewise to circulate his cycle until that Natiq passes away. Then another Natiq comes after him with another shari'a (sha-REE-ah), and so it proceeds. The earth, he insists, is never devoid of a Wali of Allah (wa-LEE) with His proof, even for the twinkling of an eye. He quotes al-Sadiq (al-SAA-diq), peace be upon him, who was asked when the second comes in the last report and answered: in another minute that remains from the same past — so that full credit may be given to the two of them. And it is only ever one after one, a proof of the unity of the Almighty. He cites: "If there were gods in them other than Allah, they would both be corrupted." The companion of the age — whether prophet, messenger, or imam — is called the Natiq because he pronounces what is apparent and carries it out. His Hujjah is called the samit (saa-mit), the silent one, because he is silent from the outward. This, he reminds us, is the reason for the twelve hujjas (HUJ-jas) that Allah mentions in His Book when He says: "And We sent forth from among them twelve pure ones." The Imam and the Hujjah, he adds, are successive like day and night. The day is like the Imam; the night is like the Hujjah and the concealment of the inner. The Qur'an confirms: "And We have made the night and the day two signs." The verses of Allah, in the inner meaning, are His proofs against His creation. They are the inner ones whose obedience is bound to obedience to Him.

He pauses on the way the name of the Imam and the name of the Natiq pass into each other. The Imam is called Natiq because he speaks what the prophet used to speak — in his turn — from the apparent shari'a with which he was sent. He is called Imam because the servants follow his example. He is called the one prayed behind in congregation, because he is in front of them and they pray behind him, so he is called the leader. The Qur'an speaks also of leaders of misguidance, an imam who is not to be followed: "And We made them leaders inviting to the Fire." The name of prophecy was given only to those who were connected to the upper substance without an intermediary — no lower hadd between him and it. So the imamate is one hadd below the hadd of prophecy, even though their obedience is equal in obligation, and is likewise coupled with obedience to Allah. We must first, he says, mention the origin of the Imamate in the lower hadd, which is Adam. Then we will coordinate what follows and recite it little by little, as much as one can bear of this limit of speech that we say — and by Allah we seek help.

He begins from the Qur'anic scene. "And when your Lord said to the angels: 'I am placing upon the earth a successor,' they said: 'Will You place therein one who will corrupt it and shed blood, while we glorify You with praise and sanctify You?' He said: 'Indeed, I know what you do not know.'" And: "And when your Lord said to the angels: 'I am creating a human being from clay. So when I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My spirit, fall down to him in prostration.'" The first thing that must be mentioned in this statement, al-Nu'man says, is the origin of the knowledge of the saints of Allah. The words and speech attributed to Allah refer to what happens to those who hear, not as you see and hear from the speech of those who speak — sounds that arise about the bodies and cavities, broken by the playthings of tongues and lips, nor like the voices that come from the friction between created bodies. Allah is exalted above being likened to anything He has created, infinitely high. It may be permissible in the language to call something "speech" or "saying" without it being speech with audible sound or known pronunciation. The Arabs say "the money is gone," "the wall has fallen," "the world has preached to its people and addressed them with her sermons," "time has reproached so-and-so." So He addressed them, and Allah knows what they know of it. A man may see in his dream what he did not see with his eyes, and he hears speech without a speaker, and the voice is heard without a voicer. It is within the power of Allah to do what He says without it being done in this way. He has said: "Indeed, We are mighty; if We wish, We say to it: 'Be,' and it is."

The angelic scene, al-Nu'man explains, is one of these parables of God's relation with His angelic guardians — not all the angels, but those whom Allah mentioned when He spoke to them concerning the command of Adam. When Allah wanted to create Adam — the second creation, which is the creation of knowledge, as the Almighty said, "Then We brought him forth as another creation," and "And He will form you in the wombs of your mothers, creation after creation" — and to appoint him as an Imam, He delegated from among the angels twelve kings to establish him for them as leader. They take from him and report him to the people of the earth. When He knew that He would create him from clay — and clay, in the inner sense, is the outward, the dense bodily knowledge — He created the angels in the creation of the gentle and subtle spiritual knowledge. They had already been taught that gentle knowledge is not connected to matter, and that dense knowledge comes only by teaching. They were not tested by deeds. They already had the knowledge that the one who is charged must be humbled and held short. They said: "Will You place therein one who will corrupt it and shed blood, while we glorify You with praise and sanctify You?" And He said: "I know what you do not know."

What the Almighty meant, al-Nu'man says, is that Adam was permitted whatever the angels had been permitted, and was warned against the same things — lest he fall short, err, or be ruined. Iblis was among them. He saw himself as better than Adam. He claimed for himself Adam's rank, refused to be his follower, and grew arrogant before the command of his Lord. He wanted God to see the angels in their incapacity, so He created Adam, as He said, from clay. That is, He began with apparent knowledge — al-zahir (al-za-hir) — as is the established custom He set down for His servants. Adam remained in that state for a long time, knowing nothing other than what he had learned. This, al-Nu'man reminds the listener, is what the hadith (ha-DEETH) mentions: that when God formed his image from clay, it remained a lifeless body for a long stretch before the spirit was breathed into it — meaning the inner, spiritual knowledge, al-batin (al-ba-tin). Iblis, in that interval, would come to him and speak to him of knowledge that Adam did not yet possess. He found in him only the apparent knowledge, in which Adam took delight, and Iblis reported back that Adam was hollow — that is, empty of any inner or esoteric content. This was before the angels, and Iblis among them, were commanded to obey him. The Almighty had said to them: "When I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My spirit, then fall down to him in prostration." That is, submit to him in obedience once I have endowed him with spiritual knowledge. At that, the devil among them turned against him — out of the enmity he harbored, the envy that had taken hold of him, and the arrogance he felt toward him. He saw himself as better than Adam and had taken pleasure in what he had observed of Adam's lagging behind in inner knowledge — until now he was filled with regret and pride.

Then comes the reversal. When God willed to bring Iblis low, He extended Adam's reach and taught him with that inner knowledge. As the Almighty said: "And He taught Adam the names — all of them." That is, the names of every thing that human beings need to know, since each thing has a name. He taught him this and then summoned the angels, whom He had permitted to call upon him, and began questioning them about what He had taught Adam. They had nothing to answer, and said exactly what God reported of them: "Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. Indeed, it is You who is the All-Knowing, the All-Wise." He said: "O Adam, inform them of their names." And when he had informed them, He said: "Did I not tell you that I know the unseen of the heavens and the earth, and I know what you reveal and what you have been concealing?" Al-Nu'man pauses on the closing phrase and cites al-Sadiq, peace be upon him, on what exactly the angels were concealing. The hadith is from the Father of Imams, who set out one night to walk around the House, and a man came and sat beside him in prayer and asked him about the beginning of the creation of this House. The Father of Imams recounted Adam's story, the command given to the angels, and what they said to him, and concluded: as for what they revealed, it was their saying, "Will You place upon it one who will spread corruption in it and shed blood, while we declare Your praise and sanctify You?" As for what they concealed, they said within themselves: we did not think that God would create any creation more honorable in His sight than we are. When God commanded them to prostrate to him and they had said that within themselves, they recognized that they had fallen into disobedience. They took refuge at the Throne and circled it for a week to seek their Lord's pleasure. He said to them: "Descend to the earth and build a house at which to seek refuge — to which the sinner from the children of Adam may come, and they will circumambulate it as you have circled the Throne, and I will be pleased with them as I was pleased with you." So they built this House. Then God raised it up at the time of the flood, so that even now, from the fourth heaven, the angels make pilgrimage to it until the Day of Resurrection. Ibrahim al-Khalil rebuilt this house upon its foundations. The story is long, al-Nu'man notes, and he has taken only what is needed.

Then God commanded the angels to prostrate to Adam — that is, He commanded them to obey him, to call upon him, and to accept him. "Do you not see that to God prostrates whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on the earth, and the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, the animals, and many of mankind — and many are deserving of punishment?" And they prostrated, as the Almighty said: "Except Iblis: he was arrogant and became one of the disbelievers." Al-Nu'man pauses to explain why Iblis is called Satan only after this point — that is, once Iblis had said what he said. The people of the language hold that shaytan follows the pattern of words meaning ruined or perished: it is said shata fulanun when a man perishes, and likewise Iblis was called Satan once he was destroyed by the limit at which he had become Satan. It is also said that the shaytan is the man who is cut off and has no proof. All these conditions were gathered in Iblis. God spoke, and He is the most truthful of those who speak: "And the Hour is their appointed time, and the Hour shall be more calamitous and more bitter."

The Almighty said: "What prevented you from prostrating when I commanded you?" He said: "I am better than him. You created me from fire, and You created him from clay." That is — and here al-Nu'man translates Iblis's claim into the doctrine of the chapter — You raised me up in knowledge through spiritual cultivation, and granted me brilliant supportive knowledge, al-ta'yid, that carries no dense physical body with it, and You created Adam with a thick, heavy, corporeal knowledge fitted to a physical body — so how should I prostrate to one whom I am better than? In this way he exposed what he had been concealing and openly declared his disobedience. God was angered with him and cursed him — that is, expelled him — saying: "Get out of it: you are accursed, and indeed upon you is My curse until the Day of Recompense." So He brought him out of the bounds of supportive knowledge, cut him off from it, expelled him from it, and cursed him. Curse, in the language, is expulsion. The hadd that Iblis had occupied — the hadd of being the proof of Adam — is now empty.

What al-Nu'man does next is the structural turn that gives the chapter its inner shape. God created Eve from Adam, because He said: "And from it He created its mate." But the creation here, al-Nu'man insists, is the creation of support — the creation of al-ta'yid — not a physical creation. That is, God took her up and supported her, taught her, illuminated her, and joined her to Adam in marriage, made her his wife, and made her his proof. God compensated Adam with her in place of Iblis. It is not — as the common folk claim — that God cast Adam into sleep, and that while he slept God took one of his ribs and created Eve from it. They heard a fragment of the interpretation which they did not grasp and did not truly know: they heard that God exchanged for Adam one of his ribs and made that rib Eve. This is not so. Rather, God compensated Adam with Eve in place of Iblis — who had been qualified to be a proof for Adam, as we have said — and made her one of the twelve chiefs, the nuqaba (al-nu-qa-BAA). For their likeness, in the apparent creation of the human being, is the ribs of the body: a human being has twelve ribs on each side, and just as the right side resembles the inner knowledge, and the left side the apparent knowledge, so the chiefs of inner subtle knowledge are twelve, and likewise their counterparts for the knowledge of the apparent.

This is one of the chapter's central doctrinal moves, and al-Nu'man states it plainly. Eve is not a wife in only the household sense. She is the replacement Hujjah. She is, on the inner reading, the first of the twelve chiefs of the inner knowledge, supplied to Adam to fill the hadd that Iblis vacated when he was expelled from the rank of support. The garden into which God settles them is, accordingly, the garden of that support itself. He says it directly. Then God settled Adam and his wife in the Garden — which inwardly is the ranks of the messengers of support, and beneath them the chiefs. Our saying this, and the like of it concerning what is subordinate, al-Nu'man hastens to clarify, is not a denial of the outer reality — namely that the Garden is itself a created entity — because the Almighty has said: "And of every thing We created pairs, that perhaps you may remember." Paradise is among the greatest of the things God has created. He has prepared it, and the Almighty has said: "On the Day God will not disgrace the Prophet and those who believed with him: their light will proceed before them and on their right hands." And: "Give them glad tidings of gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein forever. That is the great triumph." And: "And for him who fears the standing before his Lord are two gardens." And: "Give glad tidings to those who believe and do righteous deeds that for them are gardens beneath which rivers flow. Whenever they are provided with fruit from them, they will say: 'This is what we were provided with before,' and they will be given things in likeness."

So He made Paradise a glad tiding for the believers, al-Nu'man says, and He said in another place: "Their reward is with their Lord — gardens of eternity beneath which rivers flow." He told them that for them is Paradise in this world and in the Hereafter. Let the listener not be misled, he warns, into thinking that when we mention the inner meaning of Paradise we deny that there is an eternal Paradise and an abode of bliss, or that when we mention the inner meaning of the Fire we deny that there is a fire and a torment that has come to pass, or that when we mention a subtle inner interpretation of something we annul its outer reality — we seek refuge in God from that. For He does nothing except that it has an inner aspect, and we do not see in this world a soul rising in a human being except within a body, nor an inner reality except within an outer one — and if it were not for the outer, the name "inner" would not even apply. So understand this, O company of believers, with full understanding, and let your hearts feel that many among the people of this knowledge have perished by this very point. It was for this same reason, he says, that Adam fell into sin, as we shall mention at the proper place. So believe in what God has revealed outwardly and inwardly, believe in it, and act upon it. The Almighty said: "And forsake the outwardness of sin and its inwardness," and: "He has lavished upon you His bounties, outward and inward." The outward lawful is lawful, and the inward is lawful; the outward forbidden is forbidden, and the inward is forbidden. Everything, as he has said, has an outer and an inner.

He pauses here on the way Qur'anic parables work, because the same logic governs the tree we are about to meet. As for the parables that God has struck for His servants — when He says: "And these parables We strike for the people, but none understand them except those who know" — and when He says: "Indeed, God is not ashamed to strike a parable, even of a mosquito or what is above it. As for those who believe, they know it is the truth from their Lord; and as for those who disbelieve, they say: What did God intend by this parable?" The way of these parables, he says, is not that of the outer-and-inner symbolism in which what is symbolized and what symbolizes are both real, established things. Rather, the established and obligatory thing represented by the parable is like the parable of His saying: "Guide us to the straight path — the path of those upon whom You have bestowed favor, not of those who incurred wrath, nor of those who are astray." The path, in the language, is the road. The Imam, al-Nu'man says, stands in the place of the road, for whoever is bound to follow the Imam will not go astray, and whoever needs guidance will not be misled. What is meant by "road" here is the Imam, not a road taken on the ground.

Let us return, he says, to the mention of Adam and to the inner meaning of the Garden, which is supportive knowledge with its true, pure, spiritual content — for the bliss in it surpasses every other bliss, and the pleasure in it exceeds every other pleasure. For this reason, whoever attains it renounces all worldly attachments; they shrivel in his eye, every thing in this world is diminished in his sight, and he comes to make light of his attachment to anything but that knowledge. His union with it he finds to be the perfect bliss and the glory that knows no humiliation thereafter — as in the hadith of the Father of Imams to Kumayl ibn Ziyad: "Knowledge is the ruler, and wealth is the ruled. Wealth runs out as it is spent, while knowledge purifies as it is spent. Wealth's storage decays, but the scholars endure as long as time itself endures. Their persons are absent, yet their traces remain in the world." It is a long hadith, of which al-Nu'man has mentioned only what is needed.

Now the tree. The Almighty said: "O Adam, dwell — you and your wife — in the Garden, and eat freely from it wherever you wish, but do not approach this tree, or you will be among the wrongdoers." He warned him against Iblis: "Indeed, this is an enemy to you and to your wife, so let him not drive you both out of the Garden so that you become miserable." And He entrusted this to him: "And We had already taken a covenant with Adam before, but he forgot, and We found no firm resolve in him." So He permitted to them, al-Nu'man says, the ranks of the messengers in accordance with their conditions and the duties He had laid upon them — which include declaring lawful what is lawful, declaring forbidden what is forbidden, warding against what is forbidden, and warning of it. And the tree which He forbade Adam to approach, prevented him from, and made unlawful for him, He made a likeness for the Master of the Time — the Possessor of the Resurrection, Sahib al-Qiyama — within whose limits the obligations of charge lie, and before whose appearance they do not yet apply. The Almighty said: "No soul will be benefited by its faith if it had not believed before, or earned good through its faith. Say: Wait — indeed we too are waiting." From such a soul no recompense will be accepted, no punishment exacted, and no intercession received.

This is the doctrinal core of the chapter, and al-Nu'man wants the listener to feel its weight. The forbidden tree is the disclosure that has been entrusted to the Master of the Resurrection alone. The inner — al-batin — will be stripped away at his coming, the outer will fall, and the inner will become apparent. The Master of the Resurrection stands beyond the bounds of the messengers who preceded him, because the inner knowledge entrusted within their ranks is paid out to their hujjas, concealed from them, carried from one to the next — a deposit and a knowledge by which he is illuminated until it reaches its rightful holder, namely the Master of the Day of Resurrection, who will then make it openly manifest and strip away its outer covering. That manifest disclosure was forbidden and unlawful before him. So God forbade Adam at the outset of the matter — so that the unalterable custom of God might be carried out upon him. And Iblis asked for respite until the appointed Day, meaning the Day of Resurrection. He said: "Grant me respite until the Day they are resurrected" — so that I may have a little reach into his offspring.

Then comes the pact itself, the one piece of the chapter that the listener will likely know in some form already, and al-Nu'man reads it through the doctrine of the two parties. He said: "Go, and whomever of them you can rouse with your voice — meaning by stirring them to remember you — and assault them with your cavalry and your foot — meaning whoever of them follows you in your falsehood, so press them into your command — and become a partner with them in wealth and offspring." That is, al-Nu'man says, a partner with them in the knowledge of wealth, if your doubt and falsehood enter them. He means the children born through faith, because they are like the people of the call in the inner sense. Concerning His children, the Almighty said: "It is the religion of your father Ibrahim. It is He who named you Muslims." The one who calls the believers is like a father, and the hujja is like a mother, because she carries them, nurses them, and raises them with knowledge. Therefore the Messenger of Allah said: "I and you, O Ali, are the two fathers of this nation." So Iblis wished to say: share with them in their sons who believe in your falsehood — those who became, by right, their sons — and whoever among them you have overcome with your falsehood, you have transferred from their birth (which is the birth of faith) into your birth (which is the birth of disbelief); share with them in this, having promised them your arrogance and your falsehood. And Allah made it a trial for them.

Then He told them about His servants who are loyal to Him — that Iblis does not prevail over them, that He returns them to Himself in full, and that they are of His party and His sons. The Almighty said: "Indeed, over My servants you will have no authority." He made for Himself a party from among them. The doctrine of the two parties — the Party of Allah, Hizb Allah, against the party of Satan — which will dominate the next half of al-Nu'man's exposition, is established here on the floor of the chapter on Adam. It is established, as al-Nu'man wants the listener to see, by the pact that Iblis extracted after his expulsion: the pact to share with Adam's descendants in their wealth, which is knowledge, and in their offspring, which is the line of those who answer the call. The next episode will follow al-Nu'man as he develops that doctrine — what defines the party of Adam, what defines the party of Iblis, what Adam's repentance accomplishes within the architecture of the cycle, and how the Imamate is preserved through Hibat Allah and the named successors all the way down to Noah, where the cycle of the first Speaker closes and the cycle of the second opens.

For now, the lesson of the tree is what the listener should carry. The tree is not wheat. The tree is not fig. The tree is not grape. The tree is the disclosure that belongs to the Master of the Resurrection — the manifest unveiling of the inner that has not yet been authorized — and Adam transgressed it not because he was greedy for a fruit but because, prompted by Iblis, he reached for a rank that was not his to occupy. Iblis swore to them — al-Nu'man cites the Qur'an — that the limit forbidden them was forbidden only so they should not become kings or among the immortals, and he swore: "Indeed I am to you among the sincere advisors." He made it clear, in his lying advice, that if they were to leave the obligation of restraint they would end up among the gentle spiritual angels and be stripped of the dense material world — that they would be two kings eternal in what they desired. As the Almighty said: "But if they had not been beguiled, they would not have tasted of the tree, and it appeared evil to them." That is, they transgressed the limits of speech that had been permitted to them, into the limit belonging to the Lord of the Resurrection, and they extended their hands to what was not theirs. So they ate from it; they ate from the tree, and they fell into sin and disobedience, and that which supported them was cut off. That support is the garment of piety which Allah mentioned in His Book, saying: "And that is from the signs of Allah."

Adam was stripped bare of it, al-Nu'man says, and there appeared to them what was reprehensible — the intensity of the natural image — because of the nakedness of the garment of piety, which is the word, by which they saw, by its light, all the darkness in which they were now compounded. For support does not accompany sins, and extending one's hand to what does not belong to one is, by ruling, obligatory to cut off. When the support for it ceased — as the Almighty said: "They began to stitch upon themselves leaves from Paradise" — that is, they joined together what remained to them of the light of wisdom in order to cover some of the shame that had appeared in them. What was revealed about their condition was the disappearance of support, and through ignorance many of those attached to this matter perished, and it became permitted.

Then comes the cry, and the words. "And they cried out to their Lord" — as the Almighty said: "Did I not forbid you that tree and tell you that Satan is to you a clear enemy? They said: 'Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves; if You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will surely be among the losers.'" Adam did not turn aside as Iblis turned aside, al-Nu'man emphasizes; rather he repented to Allah and prayed to Him, asking for His mercy and forgiveness. "Then Adam received from his Lord words, so He turned to him in forgiveness; indeed He is the Accepter of Repentance, the Merciful." This means, al-Nu'man explains, that he drew close to Him and found Him repenting from the side of the higher limits, so He turned to him in mercy. Satan had no authority over him to drag him to himself and take possession of him, so that he might become of his party — rather, he was of the Party of Allah. The Almighty turned to him in mercy, forgave him, and received his words. Likewise those of his descendants who did evil and then repented from it — He turned to them in forgiveness, and they were of His party. But whoever persisted in his sin and did not repent of it, and to whom no words came from his Lord, was of the party of Iblis and his soldiers. The Almighty said: "If, when they wronged themselves, they had come to you and asked forgiveness of Allah, and the Messenger had asked forgiveness for them, they would have found Allah Accepting of Repentance and Merciful." The repentance of the repentant, al-Nu'man comments, is not accepted except by His receiving His words for them, and they are not forgiven until after the seeking of forgiveness for them and the seeking by the words on their behalf.

This is the closing note that the listener should hold as the episode ends. The tree was the limit of the Master of the Resurrection. The transgression was to seize a disclosure that was not yet authorized. The fall stripped the garment of piety — that is, the word, the support of al-ta'yid. And the words that returned were the words of repentance, by which Adam re-entered the line of those whom the Imamate could pass through. He had stumbled, but he had not turned aside. He had stretched out his hand to the limit of the Sahib al-Qiyama, but he had then asked forgiveness through the wali of his own time — which, after the expulsion of Iblis, was the rank that had been filled by Eve. Adam was forgiven, and the line of the Imamate that begins with him was preserved. The next episode opens at this point. Al-Nu'man will now develop the doctrine of the two parties at length, refuse the popular literalist readings of the fall, walk the chain of supportive knowledge from the upper to the lower limits, and trace the lineage from Adam through Seth and Idris to the close of the first cycle and the opening of the second — the cycle of Noah, the second Speaker, whose own chapter has just begun in al-Nu'man's text.
