## Where the argument picks up

If a world with none but sinless angels in it was given a vicegerent to stand over it, what excuse could a world crowded with flawed, forgetful, quarrelling human beings ever have for going without one? Hold that question in your mind, because it is the hinge on which this whole chapter turns. It sounds almost backwards at first. We expect the argument to run the other way — that pure and impeccable creatures, of all beings, would have the least need of a guide, and that guidance is a concession made to the weak. Al-Naysaburi takes the intuition and flips it. If even the angels, who never sinned and never rebelled, were placed under a designated authority, then the need for that authority cannot be a mere remedy for human weakness. It is written into the order of creation itself, from the very first day there was anyone on earth to be led.

The preceding stretch of the treatise showed the imamate written into the architecture of worship — into the daily prayer, the fast, the pilgrimage, the alms, the whole visible frame of the law — so that a Muslim who performs the rites is, whether he knows it or not, enacting a testimony to an ever-present guide. Now the argument widens the lens from the rites to the record. It shows the same testimony written not into the pillars of religion but into the long story of religion — into the span of sacred history from the first man forward. Al-Naysaburi is about to walk down the whole line of the prophets — Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and finally Muhammad, may God bless him — and at each stop he asks one question and gives one answer. The question is: was the earth ever, even for a moment, left without a designated guide at its head? The answer, at every stop, is no.

And there is a second thread braided through the historical one, sharper and more polemical. Once al-Naysaburi has established from the prophets that the imamate always passes by God's designation and never by human choice, he turns that principle against a specific target — the first three caliphs of Sunni Islam, and the claim that the community rightly chose them. He does not attack them with insult. He does something more disciplined and, in its way, more devastating: he refutes them from their own recorded words, measuring them against three virtues he says God Himself named as the marks of excellence. So the argument has two movements. First, the sweep of history, from Adam's vicegerency to the return of the pilgrimage to the house of Abraham, all of it teaching that the guide is never absent and never elected. Then the turn to the succession after the Prophet, where that same teaching becomes an argument about who did, and who did not, belong at the summit.

## The lesson of Adam and Noah

Begin where al-Naysaburi begins, at the very first scene of the human story, before there was a single human being in it. Suppose, he says, that it were ever acceptable for the earth to hold creatures with no imam and no caliph over them. If that were acceptable anywhere, it would have been acceptable at the dawn of things, when the only inhabitants of the higher world were the angels — beings who never disobeyed, never lusted after power, never shed blood. And yet at exactly that moment, when the case for going without a guide was at its very strongest, God announced the opposite. "Indeed, I am placing a vicegerent on earth," He said. The word is khalifa — a deputy, one who stands in another's place. God did not wait for corruption to appear and then supply a remedy. He installed the office at the outset, into a world that had not yet done anything wrong.

Notice what the angels do next, because al-Naysaburi builds a whole argument on their objection. They question the decision: "Are You going to place upon it one who will corrupt it and shed blood, while we glorify Your praise and declare Your holiness?" It is a reasonable-sounding worry. Why hand the earth to a creature who will ruin it, when there are already beings here who do nothing but worship? And God's answer is not an explanation but a rebuke: "Surely, I know what you do not know" (chapter 2, verse 30). Sit with the shape of that exchange. The angels proposed, in effect, a merit-based objection — they nominated themselves, the impeccable worshippers, as the fitter custodians. And God overruled them. He did not accept their reasoning; He corrected it and preserved the correction.

From that single scene al-Naysaburi draws two conclusions, and they are the load-bearing beams of the argument. The first: the earth is never without an imam. If a world of pure angels needed a designated vicegerent, then a world of flawed and forgetful people, most of whom are heedless, needs one all the more. The second conclusion is subtler and cuts deeper. If the imamate could ever have been settled by choice — by weighing candidates and picking the worthiest — then the angels were the ones who should have been given that choice. They were immune to jealousy, immune to the craving for domination, immune to every motive that corrupts an election. There has never been, and never will be, a more qualified electorate than the sinless angels. And God did not let them choose. He overruled their objection and appointed His own vicegerent by His own decree. So if choice was not permitted even to the angels, it is certainly not permitted to anyone beneath them. The imamate is a designation, never an election — and it is not for any human assembly, however pious, to declare who is fit for it and who is not, because the one time creatures tried to make that judgment, God corrected them and recorded the correction as a lesson for everyone who came after.

It is worth staying a moment longer with that final phrase — "I know what you do not know" — because it is quietly doing more work than it appears to. The angels' objection was, at bottom, an argument from visible merit: we worship without fault, this new creature will sin, therefore we are the fitter stewards. And God's reply does not dispute the facts they cite. He does not say the human being will not corrupt or shed blood. He says, rather, that the decision rests on a knowledge the angels do not possess — that the grounds of the appointment lie beyond what even sinless observers can see. That is precisely why al-Naysaburi insists the imamate cannot be settled by human choice. Choice is only as good as the knowledge behind it, and the qualification for this particular office is a hidden fitness that only God can assess. If the angels, with all their purity, lacked the knowledge to make the call, then a human assembly, weighing visible reputations and public deeds, lacks it far more. Designation is not an arbitrary preference for God's favorites over the community's; it is the recognition that the one relevant qualification is invisible to everyone but God, so the appointment must come from Him.

Al-Naysaburi is careful to note that this is not a one-off scene but a pattern God deliberately laid down to repeat. God recounted the stories of the past communities and prophets, he says, precisely so that people would recognize that the same shape would unfold again in the age of Muhammad. And the Prophet confirmed it: "My community will experience exactly what happened among the Children of Israel," and "you will follow the footsteps of those before you, step by step, in an identical manner, to the point that if they entered the hole of a lizard, you would enter it too." The history is not a museum of finished events. It is a template. What was true of the guide in Adam's day is true of the guide in every day, because God set it up that way and told us he had.

Then comes Noah, and the pattern advances one full turn. When the flood was coming and Noah called the people to the ark, he knew — al-Naysaburi is explicit about this — that those who refused to board would drown, and that they would drown by their own choice, convinced their safety lay somewhere else. That detail matters, because it recurs across the whole of sacred history: every people summoned by their prophet to a matter, who then refused it, were destroyed by their refusal. But the striking move is what al-Naysaburi says the ark actually was. The ark, he tells us, was none other than the imam himself, and Noah's call to the ark was a call to the imam. He grounds the reading in a saying of the Prophet that his opponents also transmit: "The likeness of my family among you is like Noah's ark: whoever boards it is saved, and whoever stays behind is drowned." The ark is a vessel of rescue that you must physically get inside to be saved by; the family of the Prophet is a guidance you must attach yourself to on the same terms. To stay outside either one, however confident you feel on the shore, is to drown.

There is a lesson folded into that image that al-Naysaburi wants his listener to feel, not just follow. The people who drowned did not drown because the ark was hidden from them or because no one warned them. They drowned in full view of the vessel, having heard the call, because they were persuaded their safety lay elsewhere — in their own judgment, their own numbers, the solid-looking ground they were standing on. That is the recurring shape of every rejection in sacred history: a guide is plainly offered, and a people, confident in an alternative of their own devising, declines. The point is not that the lost were ignorant. It is that they preferred their own choosing to the guide God had provided — which is exactly the error al-Naysaburi will accuse the later community of committing when it prefers an elected caliph to a designated imam. To insist on choosing your own vessel of rescue, when the vessel has already been named for you, is not independence. In the story it is drowning.

That the family is a thing you take hold of, and not merely admire from the bank, is exactly how the Prophet framed his own household when he set the ark image aside and spoke without metaphor. At the end of his life, binding his family to the Book itself, he charged the community:

> I am leaving among you two weighty things: the Book of God, and my family, the people of my house. So long as you hold fast to them, you will never go astray after me, and the two will not part until they return to me. — the Prophet.

The words match the ark exactly. There is a designated thing to hold; safety lies in holding it; and to let go of it, however solid the ground you let go for, is to be lost.

And Noah, having led his people through the flood, did what every guide in this history does — he did not leave the office empty. He appointed his son Shem to the legateeship and the imamate by designation, by nass, an explicit naming and handing-on, and by tawqif, a formal appointment. This is the technical heart of al-Naysaburi's claim, so it is worth pausing on the word. Nass means a clear, verbal designation — the outgoing guide names the incoming one, plainly, so there is no room for a community to convene and vote. From Shem the line continued, one imam after another, unbroken, all the way down to the time of Abraham. Not a single gap, not a single interval in which the earth was left leaderless, and not a single transfer that happened by election.

Which sets up the accusation al-Naysaburi has been building toward. His opponents, he observes, are perfectly happy to accept all of this for the ancient cycles. They grant that Adam was God's appointed vicegerent, that Noah's line passed by designation, that the imams of those early ages were real and necessary. What they will not do is grant the same thing in the family of Muhammad. And here al-Naysaburi reaches for a Qur'anic principle that his opponents themselves revere: "Such was Our way with the messengers We sent before you, and you will find no change in Our way" (chapter 17, verse 77); "you will find no change whatsoever in Our way" (chapter 33, verse 62). The word is sunna — God's established way of doing things. If God's way does not change, and His way in every prior cycle was to appoint the guide by designation, then His way in this cycle is the same. Al-Naysaburi anticipates the obvious reply — but the Prophet did change the rites; he altered the direction of prayer, the form of fasting, the shape of worship that earlier prophets had practiced. Yes, al-Naysaburi answers, and that is exactly the distinction. The sunna that God says never changes is not the sunna of ritual practice — prayer, fasting, and the like plainly did change from prophet to prophet. The unchanging sunna is the sunna of the imamate: the constant, cycle-after-cycle fact that the earth has a designated guide at its head. The rites are the variable; the guide is the constant. To accept the constant everywhere in history and deny it in the one place it now matters is not consistency. It is the self-contradiction the rest of the chapter will expose.

## The qibla and the imam

Now al-Naysaburi does something more inventive, and it repays close attention, because it turns the most public act of every religion — the direction you turn your body to pray — into a coded message about where the living guide stands. His premise is the one established in an earlier part of the book: the physical world is a system of symbols, deliberately arranged so that visible things point to invisible realities. The direction of prayer, the qibla, is one of those signs. Read rightly, each prophet's chosen direction is not merely a geographical fact but an instruction: it tells the community where to look for the imam of their age.

Take Abraham first. When he arose, al-Naysaburi says, he commanded that fire be glorified — and if the reports about this are sound, it was a symbolic indication of the glorification of the light of the imamate, the nur al-imama, the radiance that the imam carries and transmits. Fire that is honored rather than feared stands in for that light. And then Abraham and Ishmael together built the House — the Ka'ba — and commanded the people to venerate it. That act, too, was a sign, and a pointed one: it indicated that the imamate was to reside within Abraham's own household and lineage. In building the House and calling people to honor it, Abraham was in effect telling the future where to find its guide. He was urging the people to venerate and obey the imams of his family, the line that would descend from him.

That reading of the fire is not a private flourish of al-Naysaburi's. It reaches toward the very image the Qur'an makes the master-metaphor of guidance itself:

> God is the light of the heavens and the earth. The likeness of His light is as a niche wherein is a lamp, the lamp in a glass, the glass as it were a glittering star — light upon light; God guides to His light whom He wills (chapter 24, verse 35).

If guidance is a light God kindles and steers people toward, then a fire held in honor is a fitting sign for the nur al-imama, the radiance the imam carries. To venerate the flame is to be pointed, underneath the gesture, at the lamp it stands for.

It is worth being clear about what kind of reading this is, because a modern listener may hear it as a stretch — reading fire and Ka'ba and compass points as messages about an office. But al-Naysaburi is not improvising. He is applying a principle established earlier in the book: that creation is a fabric of signs, that God arranges visible things to disclose invisible ones, and that a prophet's public acts are among the most deliberate of those signs. On that view, a direction of prayer is never merely a direction. It is a fixed, repeated, communal gesture that a whole people performs five times a day for generations — which makes it one of the most powerful teaching instruments a prophet has. To set the qibla is to point an entire community's attention, bodily and habitually, at something. And what al-Naysaburi says every prophet was pointing it at, underneath the geography, was the source of guidance for that age. The fire Abraham honored, the House he built, the city Moses faced, the east Jesus turned to — read as signs, they all say the same thing in different grammar: here is where the light of the imamate is; orient your life toward it.

Then the pattern bends to accommodate a hard case, and this is where al-Naysaburi's reading shows its ingenuity. Moses arose — but Moses had no offspring, no family line through which the imamate could physically continue in his own blood. So what direction did he give? He directed the Children of Israel to face Jerusalem in prayer. And al-Naysaburi reads the choice as saying, in effect: since the imamate cannot run through my own children, look instead to whoever among you carries the sanctified spirit — that person is your imam and your qibla. The direction of prayer is decoupled from Moses's lineage and reattached to a quality, a spiritual endowment, wherever it appears. Jesus presents the same structural problem and gets a parallel answer. He, too, had neither family nor children to carry the office. And it is reported that he directed the people to face the east — toward the place where the light rises. The meaning, al-Naysaburi says, is the same as with Moses: turn toward the place from which the light of the imamate dawns, which is to say, whoever radiates that light is your imam and your direction. In both cases, the qibla points not to a spot on a map but to the location of the living guide.

And now the whole sequence resolves, because the final prophet does something that gathers up everything before him. When Muhammad, the seal of the prophets, arose, he turned the people away from Jerusalem and back toward the Ka'ba — the very House that Abraham and Ishmael had built. Read as a symbol, this is a homecoming. The direction of prayer had returned to the house of Abraham, which is to say to the family of Ishmael, which is to say — and here is the point of the whole construction — to the children of Muhammad himself, who descend from Ishmael's line. The qibla came back to the house where it began, and the imamate came back with it. And the Prophet did one thing more that al-Naysaburi treats as decisive: he commanded the people to make the pilgrimage to that House, an obligation no prophet before him had ever imposed. Why single out this House for a duty none of the earlier cycles carried? To impress upon the people, al-Naysaburi says, the supreme excellence of the imams of Muhammad's family above all the imams of every past cycle. The pilgrimage is a standing announcement that the line which now holds the House holds the highest imamate there has ever been.

Which brings al-Naysaburi to the sharpest rhetorical question in this stretch of the argument. Is there, he asks, anyone in the world more perverse than a community that accepts the imams of every prophetic period, that affirms the world can never be without an imam, and that affirms no imam in any previous cycle ever came to power by choice — and then, having conceded all of that, turns around and denies the imamate to the family of Muhammad, rejects designation, and demands election instead? The inconsistency is total. They apply one rule to the entire span of history and the opposite rule to the single case in front of them. And al-Naysaburi seals the point with scripture: "Your Lord creates what He wills and chooses; the choice is not theirs" (chapter 28, verse 68). Choosing the guide is God's prerogative, not the community's. If the imamate were open to human choice, al-Naysaburi observes, then every people would simply pick whomever they pleased, and the office would fracture into as many claimants as there were factions to nominate them. The whole point of designation is that it takes the decision out of exactly the hands most likely to corrupt it. So he draws the historical movement to its close and states where he is going next: he has shown that the earth is never without a guide and that the guide always comes by designation; now he will show that this guide must be the most excellent, the most knowledgeable, and the noblest of mankind — and he will measure the claimants against that standard.

## The virtues and the caliphs

Here the argument turns from the sweep of history to a courtroom, and al-Naysaburi opens by naming the standard he will judge by. The virtues by which God has distinguished some people above others, he says, are three: knowledge, jihad, and piety. He does not assert this on his own authority; he grounds each of the three in scripture. For striving in God's cause: "God has favored those who strive over those who sit back by a great reward in degrees of rank" (chapter 4, verses 95 to 96). For knowledge: "Are those who know and those who do not know equal?" (chapter 39, verse 9), and "those who have been given knowledge, God raises by degrees of rank" (chapter 58, verse 11). And for piety: "the noblest of you before God is the most God-fearing among you" (chapter 49, verse 13). Three virtues, each certified by revelation as a genuine mark of rank. These are the measuring rods. The question is simply who measures up.

Take jihad first, striving in the cause of God, because here al-Naysaburi says the record speaks plainly. It is well known, he argues — known to the learned elite and the ordinary believer alike — that the men he is examining never personally fought in a single battle. Against that, the military exploits of the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, peace be upon him, are famous and celebrated across the community, friend and opponent alike. And al-Naysaburi presses on one battle in particular to make the contrast concrete: Hunayn. There, he recounts, Umar disobeyed the Prophet's explicit order to hold the mountain pass and abandoned the position, drawing the censure of the Muslims for the flight — while the Prophet and Ali b. Abi Talib fought on, cutting down the enemy until they were routed. One party left the post it was ordered to hold; the other held the field. If striving is a genuine mark of excellence, and one candidate has a celebrated record of it while the other has a recorded lapse, the verdict on this virtue writes itself.

There is a further point worth drawing out of the Hunayn example, because al-Naysaburi is not merely tallying a hero's deeds against a rival's lapse. What the battle stages, in miniature, is the whole difference between a designated guide and a chosen leader. The Prophet gave an explicit order — hold the pass. Obedience to that order was the test. And in al-Naysaburi's telling, the man later elevated to the caliphate is precisely the one who, at the decisive moment, substituted his own judgment for the command and left the position, while the Prophet and the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, held to the order and held the field. The scene is a small enactment of the larger claim: authority is proven not by standing near the leader in calm times but by fidelity to his command when the ground gives way. A striving measured by that standard is not a line on a résumé; it is evidence of the exact quality the imamate requires — the constancy to hold the post you were assigned when everyone around you is fleeing it.

Then piety, and here al-Naysaburi makes a move that quietly folds all three virtues into one. Where there is no knowledge, he argues, there can be no real piety — because piety consists in knowing what belongs to you and what belongs to others, in knowing what is lawful and unlawful, and acting accordingly. You cannot be scrupulous about what you consume if you do not know which earnings are lawful and which are not. The Father of Imams had already fixed that very dependence in a comparison meant to outlast argument, ranking knowledge above the very wealth that men spend their lives chasing:

> Knowledge is better than wealth: knowledge guards you, while you must guard your wealth; knowledge rules, and wealth is ruled over; wealth diminishes as it is spent, and knowledge multiplies as it is given away. — the Father of Imams.

He drives the same point home with a memorable anecdote about a jurist who was asked to write a book on asceticism and instead wrote a book on trade. Asked why, the jurist replied: whoever has not mastered the law of buying and selling cannot know that he is consuming only what is lawful — and whoever does not consume only what is lawful has no asceticism at all. Piety, in other words, is downstream of knowledge; it is knowledge applied, and it can no more precede knowledge than a harvest can precede its seed. And so, al-Naysaburi concludes, all three of the Qur'anic virtues converge on the same person — the one with precedence in every one of them, the most knowledgeable, the most God-fearing, and the one who fought most in the cause of Islam: the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib. The virtues do not point in three different directions. They point, all of them, to one man.

And the community was not left to reason its way to that one man unaided; in this final cycle the designation was spoken aloud and in the open. Returning from his farewell pilgrimage, at a pool called Ghadir Khumm, Muhammad halted the caravan, took Ali by the hand, and raised it before the assembled people:

> Whoever's master I am, this Ali is his master. O God, be the friend of whoever befriends him, and the enemy of whoever opposes him. — the Prophet.

It is the very act al-Naysaburi says every prophet performed for his age — the outgoing guide naming his successor plainly, so that no assembly need convene to choose — performed here by the last of the prophets, in words, before his people, before he departed.

Now comes the part of the argument al-Naysaburi is most careful with, because it is the most easily mistaken for mere insult, and it is not: the refutation of the caliphs from their own recorded words. He begins with a general observation. Those who ruled, he notes, did not actually claim knowledge, excellence, or piety for themselves — it was their partisans who made those claims on their behalf. And then he turns to the record. Abu Bakr, he recounts, was the first to ascend the pulpit and say of himself: "I have been put in charge of you, though I am not the best among you." Al-Naysaburi treats this as a self-inflicted dilemma, and lays out both horns coolly. Either Abu Bakr was telling the truth, in which case those who now claim he was the most excellent of the community are contradicting the man himself — they are calling him better than he called himself, which makes them the liars. Or he was not telling the truth, in which case he was a liar — and a liar, al-Naysaburi says flatly, cannot be an imam. There is no third position that leaves the superiority claim standing.

He then closes off the obvious escape route. Someone will say: surely this was humility, the modest self-deprecation of a good man, not a literal confession of inferiority. Al-Naysaburi grants that humility has its proper time and place — but he denies that this is one of them, and he proves it by contrast. The Prophet himself would openly name the rank and status God had given him, and no one calls that arrogance: "I am the master of the children of Adam, without boasting." A rightful leader can state his rank plainly, without pride, because the rank is simply true. So could the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, who said "Ask me before you lose me" — the confidence of a man who knew the depth of his own knowledge — and who said that were a cushion folded for him to sit and judge upon, he would rule the people of the Torah by their Torah and the people of the Gospel by their Gospel, so complete was his command of every revealed law. That is not arrogance; it is an accurate report of a real capacity. The point of the contrast is sharp: the true guides described their standing openly and truthfully, so a man who publicly declares he is not the best of his people is not being modest about a hidden excellence — he is telling the truth about its absence. And al-Naysaburi adds the structural reading: Abu Bakr's words show that neither God nor His Messenger had granted him any preference or designated him for the office, whether by ordering him to lead the prayer or in any other way — for a man who had been designated would have known it, and would not have disowned the very superiority the designation would confer.

It is worth registering the discipline of al-Naysaburi's method here, because it is easy to mistake for something cruder than it is. He is not trading in mockery, and he is not manufacturing scandal. His entire case against the first caliphs is built from materials his opponents themselves preserve and revere — the pulpit sermons, the transmitted sayings, the accounts of the succession that the community treasures as history. He simply reads them closely and asks them to be consistent. Abu Bakr's own confession, the Prophet's own manner of naming his rank, the Commander of the Faithful's own words about his knowledge — these are the exhibits, and al-Naysaburi's argument is only that they cannot all be held together with the claim of superiority the partisans want to add on top. That restraint is deliberate. A polemic that relied on insult could be dismissed as partisanship; a polemic that relies entirely on the opponent's own record is far harder to wave away, because to reject it the opponent has to reject his own sources. Al-Naysaburi has chosen the second kind, and it is the more formidable for being the quieter.

Behind that close reading of the pulpit words stands the testimony of the one they passed over, who described in his own voice what it was to watch the office go elsewhere:

> By God, so-and-so wrapped himself in the caliphate, and he knew full well that my place in relation to it was that of the axle to the millstone. The torrent of knowledge pours down from me, and no bird can climb to my heights. — the Father of Imams.

From that vantage — the first claimant standing aside while the office passed from hand to hand — the caliphs' own admissions read not as isolated slips of modesty but as confirmations. And Umar, in al-Naysaburi's telling, then seals the case with a single sentence that demolishes the succession claim from the inside. Ascending the pulpit after Abu Bakr, Umar declared that the oath of allegiance given to Abu Bakr had been a falta — a hasty, unconsidered slip, a decision made in a rush — but that God had averted its evil, and that anyone who attempted the like of it again should be killed. Al-Naysaburi seizes on the admission. By his own account, Umar is saying the foundational act of the whole succession, the pledge to Abu Bakr, was not a considered execution of a divine command but a scramble whose danger God happened to spare them. And that concession, al-Naysaburi argues, single-handedly demolishes the central Sunni proof — the claim that the Prophet's asking Abu Bakr to lead the prayer in his final illness amounted to a designation of him for the caliphate. If leading the prayer had been a designation, the succession would have rested on the Prophet's clear instruction, not on a hasty oath whose evil had to be averted. Umar's own word for it — a slip — concedes there was no designation to execute. The refutation, al-Naysaburi is at pains to show, is not his invention. He has merely read back to the caliphs and their partisans what the caliphs said about themselves.

## What this chapter establishes

Step back and see the whole shape of what al-Naysaburi has built here, because it is a single argument in two movements, and each movement reinforces the other. The first movement was historical. He walked the entire line of the prophets — Adam given a vicegerent in a world of pure angels, Noah appointing Shem by explicit designation, Abraham lodging the imamate in his own lineage and building the House to mark it, Moses and Jesus pointing the direction of prayer toward the bearer of the sanctified spirit, and Muhammad turning the qibla home to the house of Abraham and commanding the pilgrimage to seal it. And at every stop the lesson was the same: the earth is never left without a designated guide, and that guide never once came by human choice. The whole span of sacred history testifies, with one voice, to an ever-present, divinely appointed imam.

The second movement turned that principle into a verdict. If God names three virtues as the marks of true excellence — knowledge, jihad, and piety — and if the imamate belongs to whoever holds all three at their summit, then the succession after the Prophet can be examined like any other claim, against the evidence. And when al-Naysaburi holds the first caliphs up to that standard, they fall short on every count — no record of striving where the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, has a celebrated one; no independent claim to knowledge or piety, only claims made for them by their partisans; and, most tellingly, their own recorded words turning against them, from Abu Bakr's confession that he was not the best of his people to Umar's admission that the whole succession was a hasty slip. The tests God set, on al-Naysaburi's reading, disqualify the men the community chose and point instead to the one it passed over.

And this is the threshold the treatise now stands on. Al-Naysaburi has spent this stretch clearing the ground — showing that the guide is always present, always designated, and always the most excellent of his age, and showing that the first caliphs cannot be that figure. What remains is to name and portray the one in whom all three virtues reach their fullness, the figure the entire argument has been circling: the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, the Father of Imams, and the imam who mirrors God in his dealings with creation. Having established that there must be a summit of the human world, and that it cannot be the men history handed the office to, al-Naysaburi turns at last to the one he says truly stands there — and to what it means for a human being to reflect, in his conduct toward people, the way God Himself acts toward them.
