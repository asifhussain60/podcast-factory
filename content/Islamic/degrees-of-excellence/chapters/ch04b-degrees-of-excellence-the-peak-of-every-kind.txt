## The witness of nature

What could a flame that must never be let die, a red stone that comes out of the fire unchanged, and a single grain of wheat possibly have to prove about who should lead a religious community? On the face of it, nothing. They belong to different worlds — chemistry, mineralogy, the farmer's field — and none of them has anything to say about human authority. And yet the scholar we are following is about to march us through all three, and through a dozen more like them, as if each were a witness giving sworn testimony in a trial about the imamate. That is the strange and rather thrilling thing about this stretch of the book: it reads at first like a naturalist's catalogue, a tour of the finest specimens creation has to offer, and it turns out to be an argument, tightly built, about the one figure the whole cosmos is arranged to point toward.

The introduction previewed this ladder as an abstract engine — the plain fact that things are not equal, refined into a hierarchy of better and best, with a note that it climbs from the elements to the stars to gold to a ruby and finally to the human being. That was the blueprint. Now we hear al-Naysaburi build it himself, in his own order, with the concrete detail the preview had to skip. And the detail is where the interest lives: the fire that Abraham told his people to honor, the sun that the Prophet said would one day rise in the west, the full catalogue of jewels laid out color by color, the seven grains of the field, the date palm that is oddly like a human being, the antidotes and the poisons, the fragrant plants and the foul ones, the noblest of the animals, and the honeybee with its chief. None of that was in the preview. All of it is here.

The treatise has just finished laying the groundwork for exactly what this stretch delivers. It established the imamate as the pole and foundation of religion, the office without which the whole structure has no center — and it closed on the idea that this office is testified to by two witnesses, the revealed law on one side and the natural world on the other. It named the two witnesses and let them stand. Now al-Naysaburi calls the second of them to the stand. If the natural world really testifies to the necessity of a supreme guide, then al-Naysaburi owes us the testimony in detail — domain by domain, specimen by specimen, until the pattern is impossible to miss. So he opens the treasuries of creation and begins pulling out one perfect thing after another, and behind every one of them the same question waits: if even the metals and the grains and the beasts each have a summit, how could the human world be the one ordered thing in creation with no summit at all?

## The peak of every kind

He begins with fire, and the choice is deliberate. Of the four elements the ancients recognized as the raw stuff of the physical world — the arkan, the pillars from which every composite body is assembled — al-Naysaburi puts fire at the top. It stands at a height, he says, that no other element and no other nature can reach. Fire is the one that gives off light, that moves and lifts and rises, and that keeps the other three quickened; the rest are sustained through it. And then the analogy that the whole catalogue will keep repeating in new keys: just as fire is the element that illumines and elevates, the imam is the highest religious pillar of his age, the one through whom souls are given light. The summit of the elements and the summit of the human world are doing the same work, one in matter and one in spirit.

It repays a moment to see why fire earns the top rung rather than one of the other three. Cold, dryness, and humidity are, in a sense, receptive; they take on qualities and are worked upon. Fire is the one that acts — it is the source of luminosity and heat, the cause of upward motion, the thing that stirs the others into life and keeps them from lapsing into inertness. A domain, on al-Naysaburi's reading, is crowned not by the member that is most acted upon but by the one that most gives — that originates, that quickens, that lifts. That is already a portrait forming in the background before a single human being has been mentioned: the summit is the giver, the source, the one on whom the rest depend for the very activity that makes them what they are. Hold that criterion in mind, because he is going to apply it, without ever restating it, to every specimen that follows.

Then he does something unexpected with the flame — he reaches for Abraham. It was on account of this hidden meaning, al-Naysaburi suggests, that Abraham once commanded his people to glorify the fire and forbade them ever to let it be extinguished, if the report about him is sound. But the real intent was never the physical fire at all. Abraham was pointing past the flame to the imamate, the light that no one is permitted to put out through the injustice of those who falsely claim its place. The fire-worshippers, who took the command literally and built a whole cult around the burning of an actual fire, missed the entire point. They grasped the symbol and never reached the reality it was standing in for — a mistake al-Naysaburi will watch people make again and again, with the sun, with the stars, with the jewels. And he seals the fire passage with scripture, turning the image of a light that cannot be extinguished back onto the enemies of the imamate: they would like to extinguish God's light with their mouths, he quotes, but God will not allow it until He perfects His light, even if the unbelievers detest it (chapter 9, verse 32). The would-be extinguishers, the fire that will not go out — the verse and the image lock together.

From the elements he lifts his eyes to the heavens, and among all the wandering lights he singles out the sun. There are gradations among the planets, he observes — differences in nobility and rank — and their peak is the sun, for a reason no one can argue with: without its light the physical eye cannot see, and without its warmth nothing grows. Not a plant, not an animal, not a newborn child comes to its full growth except under the sun's influence. So too, he says, the imam is the sun of religion, the one through whom insight is enlightened and souls are made to shine with the light of guidance. And here again come the people who worshipped the symbol — the sun-worshippers, who bowed to the disc in the sky and never saw past it to the reality it signified, falling into exactly the confusion the fire-cult fell into. But al-Naysaburi has a subtler card to play with the sun. The Prophet, may God bless him, once spoke of the sun rising from the west as one of the signs of the end. Read literally, that is astronomy, and impossible astronomy at that — for if the physical sun ever swerved from its fixed orbit even for the blink of an eye, al-Naysaburi says, the whole world would be destroyed, which reason and nature alike rule out. So the Prophet's words can only be true on the level of esoteric interpretation. The sun that rises in the west is the imam who rises in the Maghrib — and that interpretation, al-Naysaburi adds, has been borne out, witnessed with his own eyes by the grace of God. The astronomy that cannot happen becomes the history that did.

He keeps climbing through the sky. Among the twelve signs of the zodiac, he notes, four are the most glorious, and among those four one stands supreme — and just so, among the twelve proofs of the mission, the one bound to the imamate is the highest and noblest of all. Then he brings the heavens down to earth and reads them onto the map: the fourth clime, the band of the world that corresponds to the fourth heaven and aligns with the sun, is the most temperate and balanced and therefore the most excellent of the earth's regions — as the imam is the most excellent and glorious within the hierarchy, its peak and apex. Every ring he draws, whether in the stars or on the ground, has a center, and the center always names the same thing.

Now he turns to what men dig out of the ground, and the analogies sharpen. Among all the metals that melt and can be worked, gold is the finest. His reason is economic and physical at once: gold is the medium through which people carry out their transactions, the substance that fixes the value of everything else, and it is not harmed by fire or water and cannot be destroyed. In exactly that way, al-Naysaburi says, the imam is the most excellent religious and intellectual substance of his age — the one through whom the dealings of creation are settled and the well-being of people, in this world and the next, is brought to completion. Gold measures the worth of goods; the imam is the measure of religious worth. Each melting substance, he adds, has its own peak of nobility, and gold is the peak of them all.

Then he moves from the base metals to the jewels, and lingers, because this is where the argument becomes vivid. Among precious stones the sapphire is the finest, and sapphires come in several colors — white, green, yellow, red. But among all of them one excels: the red ruby. What sets the ruby apart is a kind of incorruptibility. It keeps its essence unchanged whether it is thrown into fire or plunged into water, holding onto its purity, its radiance, its weight, at all times. It draws qualities from its very source that nothing else can hold. And al-Naysaburi is emphatic about the company it beats: not the other sapphires, not the emerald, not the Badakhshi stone, not carnelian or agate, not the diamond, not turquoise, not jade — none of them endures the way the ruby endures. The red ruby is the crown of all jewels. And the imam, in the same way, is the pinnacle of all creation in his time, unique among every rank of the mission — the proofs, the adjuncts, the summoner, the wing, the licensee, the motivator. Above every one of those grades stands the imam, at a height none of them in his age can reach, exactly as the ruby stands above every other stone. The jewel that fire cannot change is a small hard image of an authority that cannot be corrupted.

And here al-Naysaburi pauses to defend his whole method, because the jewels tempt an easy objection. Someone might say the seven jewels simply stand for the seven proclaimers and the seven imams, a neat numerical match. He allows that reading, but insists the argument he is actually leaning on — degrees of excellence — carries more force. And his reason is the hinge the introduction flagged: the natural symbol is present in every age, so the reality it symbolizes must be present in every age too. The stars, the elements, the realms of nature, the substances — none of these ever goes absent from the world. Therefore the ranks of the hierarchy, and above all their summit, must likewise be present in every single age, forever exercising their influence on souls. The permanence of the symbol proves the permanence of the imam. That is why the natural catalogue is not decoration; it is load-bearing.

It is worth dwelling on the ruby a moment longer, because al-Naysaburi has chosen his criterion with unusual care and it is not the one we might expect. He does not crown the ruby for being the rarest stone, or the most valuable, or the most dazzling to look at. He crowns it for endurance under assault — for the way it holds its purity, its radiance, and its weight unchanged whether it is thrown into the fire or the flood. That is a different kind of excellence from the sun's giving of light or the gold's fixing of value. It is the excellence of a thing that stays exactly itself when everything around it is trying to alter it. And the list of stones it defeats is not idle name-dropping: the emerald, the diamond, the turquoise, each prized in its own right, each in some way vulnerable — and only the ruby comes through both trials whole. When al-Naysaburi lays that beside the imam, he is adding a new stroke to the portrait the fire began. The summit is not only the giver and the source; it is also the incorruptible one, the member of its kind that cannot be spoiled by the forces that spoil everything lesser. In a book written to defend a living, embattled ruler, that stroke is not decorative. It is saying, in the only language the natural world speaks, that pressure does not change what the imam is.

He steps down from the jewels into the field. There are seven grains, he says, that feed and nourish human beings, and their peak is wheat — the most balanced in nature and the one that best builds the body. So the imam is the peak from whom souls draw their nourishment: from him comes their livelihood, their life, and their knowledge. Then he turns to the orchard. Among fruit-bearing trees the summit is the date palm, and his reasons are wonderfully specific. The palm is like a human being in a way no other tree is: it comes in male and female, and it must be fertilized just as animals must mate. Nothing of it is wasted — every part is useful, and its fruit is eaten in every condition, raw or cooked. And there is even a reading for the dreaming mind: when a person dreams of plucking dates from a palm, the meaning is that he will receive some benefit from a certain believer — who, in truth, is the imam, the head of all believers. The tree that is shaped like us, that wastes nothing, that feeds us in every form, and that turns up in our very dreams as a figure of benefit received: of all the growing things, it is the one that most resembles the human summit toward which the whole catalogue is climbing.

Al-Naysaburi is not the first to read the palm as the tree that mirrors the human being; the Prophet had once set the same likeness before his companions as a riddle.

> Among the trees there is one whose leaves never fall, and it is like the Muslim. Tell me which tree it is. — the Prophet, in a hadith recorded by al-Bukhari.

The companions cast about among the trees of the wilderness, and the answer none of them ventured — the date palm — was the one the Prophet finally named. The likeness al-Naysaburi rests this rung on was already turning in the tradition: of all the growing things, the palm is the tree the Prophet himself had chosen as the figure of the faithful human being.

## Antidotes and poisons

There is one more thing that grows in the ground, and with it the argument takes on an edge it did not have among the jewels and the grains. Among plants, al-Naysaburi says, certain mild medicines have been refined and perfected to such a degree that they become antidotes — tiryaq, the healing compounds able to pull a person back from the very brink of death. Some cure chronic disease, others work at a lower pitch, but the finest of them reach the dying and rescue them. The peak of the medicines points, once again, to the imam: the imam and his word become the antidote for souls, the healing and relief from death, from illness, from doubt, from discord. Where the ruby image spoke of incorruptibility and the sun of light, the antidote image speaks of rescue — of a cure that reaches a soul already given up for lost.

The image of a healing word was not al-Naysaburi's own coinage; the Qur'an had already named revelation itself a cure.

> And We send down of the Qur'an that which is a healing and a mercy for the believers (chapter 17, verse 82).

The antidote that reaches the dying, on this reading, is nothing other than the revealed word — carried, interpreted, and dispensed to souls by the guide who stands at the summit.

And notice how the antidote deepens the portrait one more time. The fire made the summit a giver; the ruby made it incorruptible; now the antidote makes it a restorer. It is one thing to nourish the healthy, as the wheat does, and quite another to reach a soul that has already passed the point of ordinary help and to pull it back. The finest medicines are precisely the ones that work when it is nearly too late — the ones the physician reaches for when milder remedies have failed. By setting the imam's word beside the tiryaq, al-Naysaburi is claiming that the guide's work is not merely maintenance but rescue: healing from death, from illness, from doubt, from the discord that fractures a community. The soul dying of doubt is not beyond the imam's reach any more than the body on the brink is beyond the antidote's. That is a strong claim, and the natural image is doing the arguing for it.

But an antidote implies a poison, and this is where al-Naysaburi turns the picture dark. If the true imams are the healing medicines, then the usurpers — those who seize the imams' place by force — are the deadly poisons and harmful substances that also grow among the plants. They are fatal to whoever listens to them, keeps their company, or leans toward them; they destroy the souls of the people who trust them, unless the imam reaches those people first with the great antidote that saves them. And al-Naysaburi presses the comparison further still: the usurpers are like the useless, worthless stones as well, the rubble that serves no purpose. He anchors that in scripture with two verses that fuse the imagery of worthless stone and destroying fire. God spoke, he reminds us, of a pebble-covered hell to which they will return (chapter 21, verse 98), and of the Fire whose fuel is people and stones, prepared for those who reject the truth (chapter 2, verse 24). The rubble that is good for nothing, the fire that consumes it — the enemies of the imamate are gathered into both. The single image of medicine has split cleanly into its two faces: the cure that saves and the poison that kills, the imam and the one who counterfeits him.

Then he runs the same doubling through the taste and smell of the plant world, so that nature itself sorts the true guides from the false. Some plants are sweet and delicious and nourishing, a pleasure to eat; others are bitter, giving neither food nor delight; and still others bear no fruit at all and are covered in thorns that wound whoever comes near. The sweet and pleasant plants are like the imams — figures for their speech and their knowledge, which captivate the soul and delight the eye. The bitter, foul, thorned ones stand for the leaders of the literalists, the ahl al-zahir, the people of the surface who hold to the outer husk of religion and never reach its inner sense. And al-Naysaburi lets scripture draw the two trees for him: a good word, God says, is like a good tree whose roots are firmly set and whose branches reach the heavens, giving its fruit in every season by its Lord's leave; and a bad word is like a bad tree torn up from the surface of the earth, with no stability at all (chapter 14, verses 24 to 26). The good tree with deep roots and endless fruit against the uprooted tree with no footing — the verse hands him precisely the contrast he is drawing between the imams and the leaders of the literalists.

He does it once more with fragrance, and the point is the same but the sense is different. Some plants are sweet-smelling, and their scent refreshes people and heals them of their diseases; the summit of that fragrance is a symbol of the imam and his words, which bring ease and refreshment to whoever receives them. And the putrid, repellent plants, whose stench sickens rather than heals, stand for the imams of error, whose speech harms souls and can even destroy them. Taste and smell, the two most immediate of the senses, both testify: what the true imam offers is sweet and fragrant and healing, and what the counterfeit offers is bitter and foul and fatal. The natural world is not neutral in this contest; it is coded, top to bottom, to tell the two apart.

From the plants he moves to the animals, and the pattern reasserts itself in its plainest form. Among the animals, he says, the horse is the finest; among the birds, the eagle; among the beasts of prey, the lion. Each is the peak of its own kind — in strength, in usefulness, in the capacity to be trained, in whatever excellence its species can attain. And the differences among all these creatures, the way each genus rises to its own summit, point to a single truth: that within the whole genus of animals there is a peak, exactly as within the human species that peak is the imam. The horse and the eagle and the lion are not being praised for their own sake; they are three more witnesses that a domain naturally arranges itself around its highest member.

There is a quiet escalation across these animal images that is easy to miss. The horse, the eagle, and the lion are each the summit of their kind by a single outstanding trait — swiftness and service, mastery of the air, mastery of the hunt. Each shows that a genus rises to a peak, and that is all al-Naysaburi needs from them to keep the pattern intact. But he does not stop at creatures that are merely superior; he moves to creatures that are generous, that yield up something the world could not otherwise have. That step matters, because it carries the argument back toward the criterion the fire established at the very start. The finest members of a kind, on al-Naysaburi's reckoning, are not the ones that merely dominate but the ones that give — and among the animals, the gazelle and the bee give the most, one surrendering its musk and the other its honey. So the animal survey does not simply repeat that summits exist; it bends, at its end, toward the particular kind of summit the whole book is arguing for: the one whose excellence overflows into benefit for others.

And then al-Naysaburi reaches for two animals that do more than sit at the top of their kind — two that actually produce something precious, and so make the finest emblems of all. The musk-giving gazelle and the honey-producing bee, he says — and especially the bee's own chief, its ya'sub, the lead bee of the hive — are an indication of the imam. The word matters, because it carries a saying with it. It was for this reason, al-Naysaburi tells us, that the Prophet said to the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib: you are the ya'sub, the chief, of the believers. The lead bee around whom the whole hive orders itself is the natural image of the Father of Imams, the chief around whom the whole community orders itself. And al-Naysaburi finishes with the Qur'an's own words about the bee, which turn the image toward healing one last time: your Lord inspired the bee, God says, and from within its bellies comes a drink of varying colors in which there is healing for mankind (chapter 16, verses 68 to 69). The creature that follows its chief and produces, out of its own body, a cure for human beings — the whole of this section, the antidotes and the sweet plants and the fragrant ones and the bee, has been circling the same figure: a guide whose word is medicine, set against a counterfeit whose word is poison.

## Humankind and its summit

Now the ladder resolves, and al-Naysaburi states the turn plainly: humankind is the ultimate of the animals and of all the realms of nature. Everything that was created before the human being — the elements, their derivatives, every plant and animal and mineral — has its advantage, in the end, returned to us. The merits of the whole world are gathered up and made to appear in the human being. And al-Naysaburi does not leave this as a pious generality; he grounds it in what the human being actually does. It is man, he says, whose reason lets him harness water and draw it out of valleys and rivers and wells to irrigate the land; man who tills the earth and cultivates it and raises buildings on it; man who mastered wind and air, who discovered fire by striking flint, who crossbred animals that had never been bred before and grafted trees to yield fruits the world had never seen. It is the human being who extracts the jewels from the mineral seams, who brings the beneficial trees and seeds and fruits under his governance, who guards the welfare of the animals and the plants and brings the beasts under his command. Left to themselves, none of these things would show their worth; without the human being to draw it out, al-Naysaburi says, the merit of all things would never become apparent, and the order of the world itself would not hold. Creation needs a creature who can read it and use it, or its excellence stays buried. Man is that creature — the pinnacle of the animals, the plants, the minerals, and every realm of nature, the point at which everything below him is perfected and set in order.

The intuition that the whole cosmos is folded into the single human frame is older than this treatise; it is carried in a couplet long ascribed to the Father of Imams.

> Do you reckon yourself a small thing, when within you the greater world lies enfolded? — the Father of Imams.

What the couplet asserts, al-Naysaburi is here proving — showing, domain by domain, precisely how the greater world comes to be gathered and enfolded in the one creature able to read it and command it.

There is a subtle point buried in that list of human works that is worth drawing out, because it is what makes the human being genuinely the summit of nature rather than just one more animal near the top. The horse is faster than a man and the eagle sees farther, but neither can take the raw materials of the world and transform them. The human being can. He does not merely live within nature; he redirects it — turning the course of rivers, forcing barren ground to bear, coaxing from grafted trees a fruit that never existed, drawing jewels out of stone that would otherwise lie hidden forever. On al-Naysaburi's reading, this is exactly why the merit of the lower creation is said to return to the human being: without the creature who can extract and combine and cultivate, the sun's light and the soil's richness and the seam's buried gem would all stay latent, their excellence real but invisible. Reason is the faculty that makes the hidden worth of creation appear. And that is precisely the hinge that lets him claim, a breath later, that the human being in turn needs a summit above himself — because reason is also the faculty that, ungoverned, can turn the whole of that mastery toward ruin.

And so, by the very logic that gave a summit to the metals and the stars and the grains, the human world must have its summit too — and that summit is the imam. Just as the benefit of all the lower creation flows up and gathers in the human being, al-Naysaburi says, the benefit of all humanity flows up and gathers in the imams. They are the quintessence and perfection of the human race, the ones through whom human affairs are set in order. From them people learn virtues and knowledge; through them minds are awakened and understanding is acquired; through them human welfare is safeguarded in this world and the next. It is the imams who guide people toward the recognition of the Creator — teaching them that gratitude is owed to the one who has given them everything, and teaching them the proper way to render it. It is through the imams, too, that people come to recognize the messengers and to learn how they ought to be obeyed. The human being is the reader of the natural world; the imam is the one who teaches the human being how to read.

Then al-Naysaburi delivers the warning that gives the whole argument its edge, and it is a sobering one. Were it not for the imams — for their direction and guidance, for their protection of human welfare in this life and the next, for the way they press human beings toward the virtues and away from vice and animal habits — there would be no difference at all between beasts and human beings. In fact, he says, the beasts would be the superior of the two. That is a startling thing to assert, and he means it precisely. An animal, left to its nature, is not as given to evil as a human being is; an animal has its uses and its benefits and does not scheme its own corruption. But a human being who refuses the training that would build virtue into his soul, who never lets the imams stir his intellect to life, sinks below the animal, because without intellect and religion the human being is wholly given over to evil and corruption. The very faculty that makes us the summit of nature — reason — becomes, unguided, the instrument of a ruin no animal is capable of. And al-Naysaburi seals the point with scripture that says exactly this: the worst of creatures in God's sight, God declares, are the deaf and dumb who do not reason (chapter 8, verse 22); and of the heedless He says they are like cattle — no, more misguided still (chapter 7, verse 179). The human being without guidance is not merely a lesser man; he is, in the Qur'an's own measure, worse than the beast he was meant to command.

Which is why the whole cascade lands not on a fact but on a duty. Stack up everything al-Naysaburi has shown: that without human beings there would be no wisdom in creating the animals and plants, because no one would be there to benefit from them; and likewise, he says, that without the imams there would be no wisdom in creating humanity at all, because human excellence would never be made to appear. Human beings were created for religion — for the acquiring of virtues and knowledge — and that religion is precisely what the imams exist to give. So the closing imperative is not a suggestion but the necessary consequence of the entire natural survey: learn knowledge and religion from the imams, for it is through them that religion is established and perfected. If the excellence of the human being were never made manifest through religion, al-Naysaburi reasons, then the worth of the plants and animals would never be known either, and there would be no wisdom in the creation of any of it — no wisdom, in the end, in the making of all the worlds. The ladder that began with a flame and a stone has climbed, rung by rung, to a claim about the purpose of creation itself: the natural order terminates not in an abstraction, not even in the human being, but in an obligation to receive religion from the guides for whose sake humanity was made.

That knowledge is the faculty by which the human being rises above the whole of creation found its most memorable voice in the Fatimid poet Nasir-i Khusraw, who wrote in the same Ismaili world that produced this treatise.

> Should your tree but bear the fruit of knowledge, you will bring the turning heavens beneath your hand. — Nasir-i Khusraw.

Yet the fruit, for al-Naysaburi, ripens from a single root: the knowledge that lifts a person above the beasts, and above the very stars, is received from the imams, or it is never received at all.

## What the natural witness proves

This is the natural witness giving its full testimony, and it is worth naming what kind of testimony it turned out to be. Al-Naysaburi did not, in these sections, simply pile up pretty comparisons. He walked a single argument through the whole of creation — fire among the elements, the sun among the planets, the fourth clime among the regions of the earth, gold among the metals, the red ruby among the jewels, wheat among the grains, the date palm among the trees, the antidote among the medicines, the sweet and fragrant plants against the bitter and the foul, the horse and eagle and lion among the animals, the bee and its chief — and every one of them taught the same lesson: that an ordered domain arranges itself around its highest member. Then he showed that humanity is the summit of the whole natural order, the creature in whom the worth of everything below is gathered and made to appear. And from there the conclusion was unavoidable: the human world, like every other ordered domain, must have its summit, and that summit is the imam — with the sting in the tail that a humanity cut off from its imams would sink beneath the very beasts it was made to command.

Notice what the argument has and has not done. It has not proven who holds that summit in any given age, or by what right, or through what line of succession. It has proven only that the summit must exist — that reality requires it, from the stars down to the grains of the field. The catalogue was never really about gold or rubies or bees. It was about training the eye to see that a peak is not a luxury a domain might happen to enjoy but a necessity woven into the structure of every ordered thing, so that when al-Naysaburi says the human race must have its imam, we have already agreed, a dozen times over, without noticing.

One question the argument has carefully left open remains. If the imam is the summit of all humanity, then the natural next move is to ask what that summit actually commands — how far the imam's authority reaches, and what it means to stand inside it or outside it. From here al-Naysaburi follows the logic of the pinnacle into the question of dominion: that just as the benefit of every lower creature returns to the human being who governs it, and the benefit of all humanity returns to the imams, so everything created is finally referred back to the imam and falls under his rule — and to reject him is not to win a small argument but to place oneself outside the whole order that his summit governs. The catalogue has proved that a summit must exist. What it opens onto is the meaning of living under that summit, or refusing it.
