## Where the treatise begins

Ask a hundred believers what stands at the very center of their religion, and almost all of them will point to the same two things: the Prophet who was sent, and the revelation he carried. That answer feels not just correct but obvious — the messenger and his book are where a religion begins. And yet the scholar we are about to follow opens his treatise by turning that instinct on its head. The true pole of the faith, he insists, is not the messenger but the imam. Stranger still, he argues that you cannot even affirm the messenger properly until you have first affirmed the imam — that acknowledging the imam already carries the messenger inside it, while acknowledging the messenger does not, by itself, deliver you the imam. That is a bold way to begin a book, and it is exactly where al-Naysaburi chooses to plant his flag.

The treatise's introduction had set the stage — the frame around the argument rather than the argument itself. It laid out the world the book was written into, an empire that had staked its legitimacy on the imamate, and it exposed the machine that drives everything to come: the theory of degrees of excellence, the claim that every ordered domain of creation has a single summit, and that the imam is the summit of the human world. All of that was preparation, a map handed to you before the journey. Now the map is folded away and the journey starts. Here we step inside al-Naysaburi's own text, hearing him in his own voice, watching him make his opening moves.

This opening does three things, following the treatise closely. First, it states the central claim in its strongest form — that the imamate is the pole and the foundation of religion, the axis around which everything else turns, and it lays out al-Naysaburi's reasons for refusing to rank the messenger above the imam. Second, it grounds the necessity of the imam not in scripture alone but in the deepest layers of human nature and reason, and it explains why people who agree that there must be an imam still fall to quarreling over who he is. Third, it unfolds one of the freshest ideas in the whole book: that God wrote two parallel creations, one of bodies and one of souls, and appointed two witnesses, one of sacred law and one of unbending nature, so that the truth of the imam is testified to from every side at once. By the end you will see the foundation stone laid, and the pattern set for everything the treatise builds on top of it.

## The pole of religion

Al-Naysaburi opens with a sentence that functions almost like a thesis carved over a doorway: the imamate is the pole and foundation of religion. He reaches for two words in particular, and both are worth pausing on because they carry the weight of the claim. The first is qutb, the pole — the fixed pivot around which a wheel or a millstone turns, the still point that lets everything else revolve without flying apart. The second is asas al-din, the foundation of religion — the buried course of stones on which a whole building rests, the part you never see but without which nothing above it can stand. Put them together and you have his picture of the imam's place in the faith: he is at once the axis that everything moves around and the base that everything is built upon. Around him, al-Naysaburi says, all religious and worldly affairs revolve. Through him the affairs of God's servants are governed, the land is set right, and reward is made acceptable in the life to come. And it is through the imamate, he adds, that three of the greatest things a person can know are actually attained — knowledge of God's absolute unity, which the tradition calls tawhid; knowledge of the revealed Message; and knowledge of the sacred law together with its inner interpretation, its ta'wil, the hidden meaning beneath the plain command. These are not delivered raw into the believer's hands. They arrive by way of the imam. It is worth dwelling on that trio for a moment, because the range of it is the argument in miniature. Knowledge of God's unity is the highest and most abstract thing a mind can reach for, the very summit of what religion is about; knowledge of the Message is the historical fact that God has spoken and sent; and knowledge of the law with its interpretation is the daily, practical business of how a person is meant to live and what the commands secretly mean. The imam, on al-Naysaburi's account, is the single channel through which all three flow — the loftiest truth, the founding event, and the ordinary rule of life alike. Remove him and it is not one corner of the religion that goes dark but the whole of it, from its peak to its floor. That is what it means to call something a pole and a foundation rather than merely an important part: a pole is not one spoke among the others, and a foundation is not one wall among the walls. It is the thing the rest depends on to be anything at all.

This is not a claim al-Naysaburi invents against the grain of the wider tradition. The Prophet, may God bless him, is remembered as having tied the revealed Book to a living family in one breath, warning that the two could never be prised apart:

> I leave among you two weighty things: the Book of God, and my kindred, the people of my house. The two shall never part until they return to me at the Pool. — the Prophet, in the hadith known as the two weighty things, the thaqalayn.

A book, on that reading, was never meant to travel alone. It arrives lashed to a line of guardians who carry its meaning forward — which is precisely the dependence al-Naysaburi is about to press into a full doctrine.

Now comes the move that must have startled some of his first readers, and that al-Naysaburi clearly expects to be challenged on. He has just called the imamate the pole and foundation of religion, and he has pointedly not given precedence to the messengership over it. A pious objector would bristle: surely the one who brings the revelation outranks the one who merely guards and interprets it? Al-Naysaburi meets the objection head-on with three linked reasons, and they are worth taking one at a time because together they form the spine of his whole opening.

The first reason is a matter of what is contained in what. To affirm the imamate, he argues, is already to affirm the messengership — the one folds the other inside it. Whoever acknowledges the imam has, in the very act, acknowledged the messenger whose law the imam upholds; you cannot recognize the guardian of the revelation without recognizing that there is a revelation and a revealer to be guarded. But it does not run the other way. Not everyone who acknowledges the messenger goes on to acknowledge the reality of the imam; a person may accept that a prophet was sent and stop right there, never grasping that a living guide must carry the meaning forward. So the imamate is the larger, more comprehensive affirmation. Prove it, and you have proven the messengership along the way. Prove only the messengership, and you may still be left short of the imam.

The second reason is about audience — about which struggle actually needs fighting, and among whom. Proving that a messenger was sent, al-Naysaburi observes, is the argument you make to people who stand outside Islam altogether, the polytheists to whom the summons has not yet reached or landed. But that summons, in his reckoning, has already gone out; the proof of the messengership has already been established against those who reject it, so the fitting response to their continued denial is no longer patient debate. Affirming the imamate, by contrast, is the live task among Muslims themselves — among people who are already close to true belief and who nonetheless disagree, sometimes bitterly, about the guide. The real intellectual labor, the argument that still needs winning through reasoning and evidence rather than through force, is the one about the imam. That is where a book like this can do its work.

The third reason is the deepest, and al-Naysaburi plainly regards it as the decisive one. The world, he says, can never be without an imam for a single moment. The messenger, by contrast, is present at some times and absent at others; prophets arise at particular junctures in history and then are gone. But the imam is a permanent fixture of reality, as necessary to the world as breath is to a body. A guidance that switches on and off cannot be the true foundation of anything; the foundation has to be always there. So the office that is ever-present must be more fundamental than the office that comes and goes. And this is the same logic by which every speaker-prophet stands as the visible peak of his cycle while the silent one beside him carries the inner meaning through — the pattern of a voice that speaks and a keeper who holds, running unbroken down the generations. The imam is the office that never lapses.

That conviction — that the earth is never, at any moment, left without such a guide — did not begin with al-Naysaburi. It is one of the oldest teachings carried down from the Father of Imams (may Allah be pleased with him), remembered telling his closest confidant that the ground beneath us is never vacant of God's proof, His hujja:

> The earth is never left without one who stands for God with a proof — whether manifest and well known, or fearful and hidden — so that God's proofs and His clear signs are never lost. — the Father of Imams.

The hidden case in that saying matters as much as the manifest one: a proof unseen is still a proof present. That is exactly the possibility al-Naysaburi keeps open when he speaks of a master of the age whom he will never name.

To seal the point, al-Naysaburi does something clever with scripture: he shows the two titles trading places, the imam called a messenger and the messenger called an imam, so that neither can be neatly ranked above the other. God, he notes, called the imam a messenger, citing the verse that remembers Ismail as one who was faithful to his promise, a messenger and a prophet (chapter 19, verse 54). And God called the messenger an imam, in the great address to Abraham — "Indeed, I am appointing you an imam for all humanity" (chapter 2, verse 124) — a word spoken, al-Naysaburi is careful to say, only after Abraham had already completed his work as a messenger. The imamate comes to Abraham as the crown of everything else, the last and highest station, not the first. He reinforces this with a tradition handed down from Ja'far al-Sadiq, peace be upon him, describing the ascent by stages: God chose Abraham first as a prophet, then as a friend, then as a messenger, and only at the end declared, "I am appointing you an imam for all humanity." The rank of imam sits at the summit of that ladder. It is what a prophet grows into, not what he starts from.

Out of these pieces al-Naysaburi assembles a doctrine that reorders the whole relationship between prophecy and the imamate — and it repays slowing down over, because it is easy to hear as paradox and it is not. A messenger, on this account, is first of all an imam. Before he ever rises to deliver a law, he has already attained the rank of the imamate, having received command from the imam before him. Then, when the times call for it — when a whole new sacred law is needed — that imam ascends to become the speaker of his cycle, the seventh imam of his era, and takes up the promulgation of the law. And when his work as lawgiver is done, he hands command onward to the next imam in the line. So messengership is not a separate and higher species of office; it is something the imam does at a particular moment, when history demands a new law, before returning the succession to its ordinary course. The imamate is the deep, continuous current; prophecy is the wave that rises out of it when the moment requires and then subsides back into it. Which is exactly why, on al-Naysaburi's picture, the imam is always present and the world is never for an instant deprived of him. The messengers come and go; the imam abides, upholding the law together with its inner reality, and it is through him alone that anyone reaches the true meaning of the revelation, its interpretation, and its purpose.

Al-Naysaburi closes his opening by telling you, with disarming frankness, what kind of book he is not going to write — and the disclaimer is really a boast about method. The elders of the da'wa, the mission's earlier scholars, had already written a great many works affirming the imamate, drawing their proofs from the well-known Qur'anic verses revealed about the imams and from traditions reported of the Prophet. Those who came before, he says with real respect, left nothing in that vein for those who came after; there is little use in restating the same familiar arguments in slightly different words. So he will do something the elders had not attempted. He will affirm the imamate by evidence and deduction drawn from the outer horizons and the inner selves, from politics and governance — the domain he calls siyasa — and from established norms; by way of the intellect and of what is self-evident; from innate constitution, fitra, and natural disposition; from the agreement of every religious community; and, most strikingly of all, from the philosophers and the mathematicians. He will meet unbelief on the ground it thinks it owns. And with a courtier's grace he adds that if the result is sound it is owing to the support of the master of the age — the imam of his own day, whom he will never name — and if there is any error, it belongs to his own weakness alone. That is the promise the rest of the treatise sets out to keep.

## Necessity by nature and intellect

Having planted the flag, al-Naysaburi now asks a more basic question: on what grounds is the imamate necessary at all? His answer is a deliberately long list, and the length is the point. The necessity of the imam, he says, is grounded in natural disposition and innate constitution — in jibla and fitra, the twin words for the shape a thing is born with — and in nature itself, and in the intellect, and in governance, and in prescription, and in established norms. It is obligatory, he continues, in every revealed law, in every religion, and in every religious community. He wants you to feel the imamate pressing in from all directions at once: not a doctrine you might arrive at by one narrow path, but a conclusion that reason, instinct, nature, and social order all converge upon independently. And he offers a parallel to make the scale of the claim vivid. The imam is necessary, he says, in exactly the way the existence of the Creator is necessary — known by innate constitution and by intellect in every community and every faith. The need for a guide at the summit of humanity is written as deep in us as the need to acknowledge that the world has a Maker.

Scripture puts the same necessity in the plainest terms. Addressing the Prophet, the Qur'an pairs the one who warns with the one who guides, as though a people is never given the first without the second: "You are only a warner, and for every people there is a guide" (chapter 13, verse 7). A warner to sound the alarm, and, for every community without exception, a guide — a hadi — to walk them through: the office of guidance is not the privilege of a few favored ages but a standing provision, extended to all peoples at all times. In the reading al-Naysaburi belongs to, that guide is precisely the imam, and the verse quietly forecloses the idea of a community left to find its own way.

That parallel between the necessity of the imam and the necessity of the Creator is not a passing flourish; it becomes the engine of the whole section, because al-Naysaburi immediately notices that the two necessities share a second feature, a darker one. People agree that there must be a Creator — and then they fall to pieces over what He is. Having conceded His necessity and accepted His existence, al-Naysaburi says, they began reasoning by their own whims and opinions, and imitating their forebears and teachers, until they had talked themselves into strange conclusions. Some reduced the Creator whose existence their own nature demanded to nothing more than substance, or the four elements, or the stars, or the sun — collapsing the Maker into a piece of what He made. Others went the opposite way and described Him with attributes that amounted to likening Him to His creation, the error the tradition calls tashbih, anthropomorphism, dressing God in human features until He borders on being just another creature; or else they stripped Him of every attribute at all, emptying Him out until nothing was left to worship. The point is not the catalogue of errors for its own sake. The point is the shape of the failure: universal agreement on the necessity, followed by ruinous disagreement about the reality.

And now the parallel snaps shut, because the very same thing, al-Naysaburi argues, happened with the imam. People committed themselves to the necessity of an imam — and then split over who or what he is. Some decided that the imam whom intellect and self-evidence require is simply the wise man of every age, the cleverest head in each generation. This, he notes, is close to the way of the philosophers, who hand the title around among their own: they call their teachers imams, and they call them sages, and the head of their scholars, and the master of the world, and the teacher. Strip the imam of anything more than earthly wisdom and you get the philosopher's version — a smart man, admired, consulted, but nothing that the fabric of reality actually hangs upon. That reduction, for al-Naysaburi, is the exact counterpart in the human realm of reducing the Creator to a star or the sun: taking something that had to be affirmed and shrinking it down into a thing far too small to do the work the affirmation required.

Then he presses a point that is easy to miss and genuinely sharp: even the wrong answers confirm the necessity. Followers of the various religions, he observes, have called their past imams prophets and sages in every cycle; and within Islam certain people have gone so far as to apply the noble title of imam to usurping, oppressive pretenders, false claimants with no right to it, while others hand the same word to their scholars and their jurists. These are, in al-Naysaburi's eyes, misapplications — for in his view only the true prophets and imams are entitled to the name at all. But watch what he does with them. If you examine every one of these cases carefully, he says, each of them confirms the imam as a necessity — a darura, something reason simply cannot do without — and none of them is able to deny it. The usurper who steals the title, the jurist who is flattered with it, the philosopher who bestows it on his teacher: every one of them, in reaching for the word, is testifying that the office must exist. You do not fight over a crown unless there is a throne. The very abuse of the title is evidence for the reality it points to. People never invent a counterfeit of something that was not worth having in the first place. And there is a second twist worth drawing out of this, because it tells you how confident al-Naysaburi is. He does not treat the sheer profusion of claimants as an embarrassment to his case, the way an opponent might — look how many "imams" there are, how can you possibly say which is real? He treats it as a proof. If the title were empty, if the office answered to no genuine need in the structure of things, the claims would simply have died out; nobody bothers to impersonate a role that reality has no use for. Instead the claims multiply in every religion and every age, which is exactly what you would expect if the office were a permanent fixture of the human world and only its rightful occupant were in dispute. The crowd of pretenders is not noise drowning out his argument. It is the argument, shouting from every direction at once that there must be a true imam behind all these false ones.

From this al-Naysaburi draws a practical charge for anyone who actually wants to be saved, and here the argument turns from diagnosis to demand. Whoever seeks salvation and guidance, he says, must begin by setting aside his own inclinations and desires — because it is desire, the wish for an easy or a flattering answer, that keeps a person clinging to a false claimant in the first place. Then he must learn to see the pretender clearly: to recognize the deficiency in every false claimant to the office, his want of real knowledge and gnosis and cognition, his lack of genuine piety, and his failure to meet the other conditions that the imamate demands. Once a seeker perceives that the pretender is ignorant where the imam must be knowing, and shallow where the imam must be deep, and unqualified where the imam must be complete, he will reject the false claim — and keep rejecting it until he arrives at the thing al-Naysaburi calls ma'rifat imam al-haqq, the recognition of the true imam. Notice that this is a process of elimination as much as of discovery. You clear away the counterfeits one by one, and the true one is what remains standing.

Poets in other quarters of the tradition felt the same necessity from the inside, as a matter of the seeking heart rather than the arguing intellect. Rumi put it as bluntly as a traveler's warning:

> Whoever sets out on the road without a guide, a two-day journey stretches into a hundred years. — Rumi.

The point is not decoration. It is that the guide is not a luxury added to an otherwise complete religion; without one, al-Naysaburi would say, the seeker does not merely slow down — he loses the road entirely, mistaking a counterfeit for the summit and building his whole life on it.

Which is precisely the method al-Naysaburi now announces he will use, and it is worth naming because it shapes long stretches of the book to come. He will examine the claims made on behalf of each pretender, he says, along with his weaknesses and his limitations — and then he will turn the pretender's own principles against him. This is a fighter's technique, not a preacher's. Rather than simply asserting the true imam and asking you to believe it, he will take each rival account of who deserves the title, follow that account's own logic to its end, and show that by its own standards its candidate falls short — stripping the imamate away from those who are unfit for it using nothing but the tools they themselves supplied. Only after the ground has been cleared this way will he establish the imamate of the true imams, peace be upon them, with proofs and demonstrations, and with it the obligation to obey them, to entrust one's affairs to their command, and to receive revelation, interpretation, and the confession of God's unity from them. The whole labor, he says, keeps the divine unity pure — free at once from likening God to His creation and from stripping Him of all attributes. So the necessity that opened the section and the purity that closes it are two ends of one thread: get the guide right, and you can finally get God right; keep chasing counterfeit guides, and you will keep falling into counterfeit pictures of God. The imam, on this reading, is not merely necessary in the abstract. He is the safeguard of the very knowledge the whole religion exists to protect.

## Two creations, two witnesses

Here al-Naysaburi reaches for the idea that gives this opening its real originality, and it is worth taking slowly because everything later in the treatise leans on it. God, he says, created for the human body a set of principles, causes, and means — the raw materials and hidden supports out of which a living, sensing body is assembled and kept alive, its nourishment and survival built right into its makeup. And then, in exactly the same fashion, God established principles, causes, and means for souls, spirits, and the higher forms of religious understanding. There are, in other words, two creations running in parallel: one of bodies and one of souls, each with its own supports, its own sustenance, its own life and death. The religious creation is built to mirror the physical creation, corresponding to it point for point. This is the master key. Once you accept that God deliberately made the visible world of bodies as a working model of the invisible world of souls, then every fact about how bodies live becomes a coded lesson about how souls live — and al-Naysaburi is about to start reading the code.

His first reading is the one that carries the whole section, and it hangs on a single careful observation about the heavens. A physical body, he points out, is perfected and sustained only through the celestial spheres together with the stars — and he leans hard on that word together. The spheres alone are not enough; it is the stars set within them that actually shape the course of events below. Strip the stars out and leave only the bare turning spheres, and the machinery would run empty, perfecting nothing. Now transpose that structure into the world of souls. Just as the body needs the stars and not merely the spheres, the soul and the higher religious forms are perfected only through the influence of the imams — for the imams, al-Naysaburi says plainly, are the religious stars. Their knowledge is the light that souls feed on, exactly as the stars' light governs the growth of bodies. He anchors the image in a saying of the Prophet: "My companions are like the stars; whomever you follow, you will be guided." The imams are those guiding stars. And to keep the whole picture in proportion he adds the other half of the correspondence: the imams act in the place of the prophets, and the prophets are like the celestial spheres — the vast turning frames within which the stars are set. So the architecture of the sky becomes a diagram of the architecture of guidance: prophets as the great spheres, imams as the stars that make those spheres actually bear fruit, and the whole course of affairs for every created being bending, in the end, back toward the imams. He notes, almost with relief, that he will not chase this parallel out to its full length, or the epistle would run past its bounds — a rare glimpse of an author choosing compression, and a signal that this correspondence between the two heavens could fill a book on its own.

From the parallel of the two creations al-Naysaburi draws a hierarchy of worth, and it turns on a distinction between a symbol and the thing it stands for. The elements and realms of nature that keep our bodies alive are, in his language, the ephemeral, obligatory symbols — al-mithalat al-jabriyyat, the passing signs that nature compels us to depend on. We cannot, while we live in the body, go a single moment without breathing and without drawing benefit from the elements and nourishment of the physical world; that dependence is not chosen, it is forced on us. But these compulsory natural symbols exist, he argues, in order to point beyond themselves — to the symbolized realities that are religious, eternal, and chosen. And the symbolized realities matter more than the symbols that indicate them, by a wide margin. The benefit of the natural symbol returns only to the body; the benefit of the symbolized religious reality returns to the spirit and the soul. Just as the spirit outranks the body, the intellectual reality outranks the natural sign; just as the everlasting outranks the perishable, so the thing symbolized outranks the symbol. He draws the stakes with real severity. Deprive a person of the physical things his body needs, and at worst you destroy his body — which was going to die in any case, so the loss, however painful, is bounded. But deprive a soul of the religious reality it needs, and the soul itself faces destruction and lasting ruin. And then the line that turns the whole doctrine into a reproach: if only people would strive after what the soul needs with the same energy and cunning they pour into feeding the body, no two of them would fall into doubt, or wander off, or slide into a crude and human picture of God. The evidence is everywhere; the negligence is entirely ours.

Al-Naysaburi now gives this parallel a legal and even a courtroom shape, and it is one of the most memorable turns in the opening. God, he reminds us, ordained that judgement be rendered on the testimony of two witnesses, quoting the command that if one of them should err, the other will remind him, and that witnesses must not refuse when they are summoned (chapter 2, verse 282). True judgement in religion, and above all in affirming the imamate, likewise rests on two witnesses: one established by sacred law, and one compelled by nature. The legal witness is the revelation itself, the scripture and the reported testimony the Prophet called people to bear. The natural witness is the created order — the outer horizons and our own inner selves, the world around us and the world within. And the beauty of the second witness, for al-Naysaburi, is that no one can tamper with it. People have distorted the legal testimony; they have changed and suppressed the revelation, altering words from their places, as scripture itself records. But the horizons and the souls cannot be edited. When people deny or corrupt the legally established testimony, the witness bound by nature steps in — to remind, to enforce, and to verify what the tampered-with law can no longer securely say on its own. He seals it with the verse that has hovered over this whole section: "We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth" (chapter 41, verse 53). And he adds a small, arresting tradition to bring it down to earth: when the Prophet was asked who could witness to his prophethood, he answered, "My witness is every stone and every piece of clay" — pointing, al-Naysaburi says, to the testimony of the horizons and the souls, the mute creation itself rising to vouch for the truth. When the witness of law and the witness of nature both testify, and the intellect is allowed to weigh them fairly, without leaning toward desire or prejudice, then truth, he says, becomes manifest with startling speed.

And that promise of a swiftly manifest truth marks exactly what al-Naysaburi has and has not yet done. He has told you that nature is a second, unforgeable witness. What he has not yet done is call that witness to the stand. His master proof — the claim that every ordered domain of creation has a single summit, and that every one of those summits points, by the same logic, to the imam — has so far been shown only as an abstract engine: the ten categories resolving into substance, the four constituents resolving into heat, the many spheres resolving into the one outermost sphere. Those were the sample gears, shown turning in isolation. What follows in the treatise feeds the whole storehouse of creation through that engine, specimen by specimen — the sun among the planets, gold among the metals, the ruby that will not burn among the stones, the wheat and the date palm among the growing things — until the pattern has been demonstrated so many times over that its final application to the human race feels less like a leap than like a debt already owed. Here the witness of nature is sworn in. What remains is to let it speak.

## The foundation laid

Step back from al-Naysaburi's opening and you can see how much he has fixed in place before offering a single one of his famous natural proofs. He has established, first, the central claim of the whole treatise: that the imamate is the pole and the foundation of the religion — the axis everything turns on and the base everything rests upon — and that it is through the imam that a believer actually comes to know God's unity, the revealed Message, and the sacred law with its inner meaning. He has defended that claim against the obvious objection by showing that the imamate is the more comprehensive affirmation, the one that carries the messengership inside it, the office that never lapses while prophecy comes and goes; and he has read the traditions of Abraham and Ismail to show the two titles trading places, so that neither can be simply ranked above the other.

He has established, second, that the necessity of the imam runs as deep as the necessity of the Creator — written into innate nature, natural disposition, intellect, governance, and the settled norms of every community — and he has explained the strange, recurring pattern by which people concede that necessity and then quarrel endlessly over who fills it. He turned even that quarrel into evidence: the usurper, the flattered jurist, the philosopher crowning his teacher all confirm, in the very act of misusing the title, that the office must exist. And he has set out his working method, the fighter's method of turning each pretender's own principles against him and clearing the counterfeits away until only the true imam is left standing.

He has established, third and most originally, the doctrine of the two creations and the two witnesses — a physical world built as a working model of a religious one, and a truth vouched for at once by the sacred law and by the unforgeable testimony of the horizons and the souls, so that when law and nature agree and the intellect weighs them without bias, the truth comes quickly clear. That is the foundation stone, fully laid.

What remains is to build on it, and the building is where the treatise earns its name. From here al-Naysaburi calls the witness of nature to the stand and lets it testify in full — climbing the concrete ladder of excellence rung by rung through the metals and the stars and the growing things, showing in domain after domain that creation is arranged around its highest points, and carrying that pattern all the way up to the one summit toward which, on his reading, the entire ordered world has been quietly pointing from the start.
