## Where the argument picks up

What would it mean for a human being to treat people exactly the way God treats them — to give to the faithful and the faithless alike, to withhold from each in turn, to conceal His reasons, to draw near and to hold back — and why would that be the highest praise a person could receive, rather than a scandal? Set beside the way we usually judge a ruler, the idea sounds almost offensive. We expect a good king to reward his friends and punish his enemies, to make his favor legible so that loyalty is repaid and treachery is checked. A ruler who gave to believer and unbeliever without distinction, who kept the grounds of his generosity hidden, who came close and then withdrew for no announced reason, would strike most people as capricious, even unjust. And yet the scholar we have been following ends his treatise by holding up exactly that portrait as the summit of human excellence — the one thing that proves a man is not a mere monarch but the vicegerent of God on earth. This closing chapter is about why he thought so.

The preceding stretch of the treatise traced the thread that has run through the entire argument: that the world is never, for a single moment, without a designated guide standing at its peak, from the first man down to the present day. It walked back through sacred history and measured the men the community had actually followed against the standard the imamate demands — knowledge that reaches the inner meaning of revelation, striving in God's cause, and a piety without compromise — and found the first successors wanting on every count. That movement cleared the ground. This one raises the building. For if there must always be a summit, and if the ordinary claimants fall short of it, then the question becomes unavoidable: who is the summit? Who is the figure in whom knowledge, striving, and nearness to God actually culminate, so completely that nothing higher can be imagined?

This is the last movement of the treatise, and it is fitting that it should be the boldest. Everything the book has built — the pole of religion, the ladder of degrees that climbs from the elements to the stars to humankind, the pillars of worship, the reading of history — was always pointing here, at a single person and the office he fills. The scholar names that summit plainly: the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, peace be upon him, the Father of Imams, the furthest limit beyond which there is no further limit. And then, because his own theology treats that summit as continuous rather than sealed off in the past, he moves without a break from Ali b. Abi Talib to the imam reigning in his own age — the qa'im and perfector of the whole line — and shows that man dealing with creation precisely as God deals with it. Two figures, one office, one ascent. By the end, the whole edifice of the book will rest on this claim, and the treatise will close, as it should, on its author's own prayer.

## The summit of summits

Begin where the scholar begins, with a rule he takes to be written into the structure of everything that exists: in every species, every genus, every root and branch and law, there is a peak — a summit of nobility and rank and station that nothing else of its kind can reach. You have watched this rule at work across the whole treatise. Among the elements, one stands supreme; among the metals, gold; among the stones, the ruby that will not burn; among the grains, wheat; among the trees, the date palm; among the heavenly bodies, the outermost sphere and the sun. Nature does not spread its excellence evenly. It gathers excellence to a point. And if that is true of metals and stars and grains, the scholar reasons, it must be true of the imams as well. They too must have their peak, their utmost degree, a summit no one before them ever attained. There is no exemption from the rule for the highest realm of all.

And who is that summit? He says it without hedging: the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib. He is the summit of all summits, the furthest limit beyond which there is no other limit. Notice the doubling in the phrase — not merely a summit, but the summit of summits; not a limit, but the limit past which no limit lies. The scholar is reaching for language that closes the door on any higher term. Wherever else in creation you find a peak, that peak is still a peak of its own kind, one summit among many. But the Father of Imams is presented as the point at which the whole hierarchy of peaks itself comes to rest — the apex not of one domain but of the ascent that runs through all of them.

To feel the force of the claim you have to remember the chain of reasoning the book has already laid down, because the scholar now runs that chain one link further than before. Recall how it went. The wisdom of the whole world, and of all the animals and plants within it, came to its full expression only in the emergence of the human being; the well-being of all creation was completed through humanity, and the benefit of every created thing flows back, in the end, to mankind. Humanity is the summit of nature. Then a second step: just as nature culminates in humanity, humanity itself culminates in the imams. It is through the imams that the merits of the human race are perfected and that wisdom becomes visible in creation, so that the benefit of all human beings flows back to the imams as the benefit of all nature flows back to man. The imams are the summit of humanity. And now the third step, the one this final movement adds: just as nature rises to humanity and humanity rises to the imams, the whole line of imams itself rises to a single point. Through the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, all the affairs of the prophets, the legatees, and the imams reached their completion. Their order was set in order through him; the full wisdom of their laws and their inner interpretations became manifest in his person. He is the summit toward which even the summits incline.

The scholar reaches for a bodily image to make the relation vivid, and it is worth hearing in full because he lets it unfold in stages. The Father of Imams, he says, is like the head, and all the others — the prophets and legatees and imams who came before — are like the body; and no one disputes that the head governs the body. Then he deepens the picture: they are the body, and he is the spirit, and no body is complete or alive without its spirit. Then deeper still: he is the heart, and all who have passed away are like the limbs, the hands and the hair and the nails, each of which receives its life and nourishment only through the beating of the heart. Every limb, down to the smallest, draws its vitality from that one central organ. So it is, he says, that through the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, the whole line receives its life; he is the one who awakens in every soul the longing to draw near, and the one through whom God has promised His creation the fulfillment of its reward. The head, the spirit, the heart: three images for a single claim, that the entire body of guidance lives only because this one figure stands at its center.

And he presses the point about gathering, because it is the mechanism behind the claim. Just as the human being, arriving last in the order of nature, absorbed into himself everything that existed in the realms below and then surpassed it, and just as the imams gathered within themselves all the merits scattered through humanity and then exceeded them, so the Father of Imams gathers within himself all the merits of everyone who came before — every prophet, every imam, every sage of every earlier cycle — and their knowledge and their benefit all converge in him, and he surpasses them many times over. The scholar draws the parallel that gives the claim its warrant: just as the Prophet, may God bless him, drew his power and his merit from gathering into himself the merits of all who lived in the cycles before him, and their knowledge and more besides, so the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, gathers into himself the merits of all the prophets and imams and sages who preceded him, and exceeds them in matters beyond their reach. Had he not arisen to fill this station, the scholar says, the perfection of wisdom in all their arisings would never have been achieved. This is the word he settles on: the Father of Imams is the perfector — in the Arabic, the mutimm, the one who completes and brings to fullness — of every one of them who has passed away. It is for the sake of such a figure, he adds, that these worlds were made at all.

This intuition — that the whole hierarchy of being ascends toward the imam and comes to rest in him — was not al-Naysaburi's alone. The Ismaili philosophers of his own tradition had built entire cosmologies on it. Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani, in his Rahat al-Aql, mapped the ranks of religion onto the very structure of the cosmos, so that the imam stands at the human summit as the outermost sphere stands at the summit of the heavens — the point where the whole ladder of being is completed. What al-Naysaburi calls the summit of summits, that tradition had already learned to see as the place where the cosmic order and the order of guidance meet in a single person.

Here the scholar anticipates an objection, and the way he answers it is the hinge on which this whole chapter turns. Someone might say: surely this towering description — the perfector of all, the one for whose sake the worlds exist — belongs only to the final qa'im, the awaited one who arises at the very end to seal all the cycles. And the Father of Imams, whatever his rank, is not that final figure. The scholar's reply is quietly radical. The imam reigning in his own age, he says, is the qa'im of his own time and age, and the qa'im of all those who preceded him, and the perfector of them all. The office of the perfector is not reserved for one distant, terminal figure at the end of history. Every reigning imam stands, for his own age, in exactly that place — completing and bringing to fullness the whole line that came before him.

The scholar then lays out this completion in the numerical language of his tradition, and here we should follow his sense while presenting his numbers abstractly, without naming any past or present ruler. He speaks of the reigning imam as the one who completes a cycle that others opened — the sixth through whom the command of a seventh is brought to its perfection, the one who finishes what that seventh required, much as the Prophet established everything a later completer would need. In another reckoning he calls him the ninth, the figure through whom the command of an earlier one, now passed away, reaches its fullness — and he grounds this in the way the parts of the human body attain their order and function only through a set of nine, and the way the human creature itself is perfected over nine months in the womb. In yet another calculation he calls him the second, since the order set down at the beginning of every cycle is brought to completion by the one who follows. The particular numbers matter less than the single idea they all encode: the reigning imam is not a lesser stand-in awaiting some greater figure. He is himself the one who completes the cycle begun in those before him — the living perfector of a line that stretches back through the whole of sacred history.

So keep the two referents clear, because the scholar deliberately lets them flow together. Ali b. Abi Talib is the historical summit — the Father of Imams, the head and heart and spirit of the whole body of guidance, the perfector of every prophet and imam who preceded him. And the imam reigning in the scholar's own age is the one who continues and completes that summit in the present, the qa'im of his own time, gathering the entire inheritance into himself. In this theology the two are not rivals and not really separate; the reigning imam is the summit begun in the Father of Imams, carried forward and brought to completion for a new age. The summit of summits is not a point fixed in the past. It is a living station, occupied now.

## The imam who mirrors God

The philosophers, the scholar reminds us, had their own most exalted definition of what they were doing. Philosophy, they said, is the pursuit of resemblance to the Creator, so far as a human being is able. It is a noble formula, ascribed across the traditions ultimately to the Greeks, and the scholar does not sneer at it. He does something more interesting: he takes it perfectly seriously and then claims that not one of the philosophers ever actually achieved the thing they defined. To seek resemblance to the Creator, he explains, means to deal with mankind the way God deals with them — to interact with people, and with oneself, in the manner and along the grain of God's own conduct toward human beings. The philosophers, he says, borrowed this idea from the people of the truth, his own community, and yet none of them ever realized its true meaning. Only one figure ever has: the imam, whose every dealing with humankind mirrors God's own dealing with them, in the ordering of the world, in the giving of sustenance, in all related matters. The philosophers drew the target. The imam alone hit it.

Everything now depends on making that mirroring concrete, and this is the heart of the chapter — the great analogy the scholar develops at length, because he knows the claim is startling and needs to be shown, not merely asserted. Start with the plainest fact about how God distributes worldly fortune. God, in His bounty, bestows blessing on people whether they are pious or impious, believers or unbelievers, and He withholds from each of them too, giving to some and denying others, granting at one time and refusing at another. Scripture states the pattern as a bare fact of the divine economy: God extends provision to whom He wills, and restricts it (chapter 13, verse 26). Why not simply reward the faithful? Because — and here is the wisdom the scholar wants us to see — had God given worldly benefit to believers alone and denied it to unbelievers, then faith would become a business proposition. People would believe for the sake of the profit, and their religion would be a calculation about this world rather than a turning toward God. And had God done the reverse, withholding all worldly good from believers and lavishing it only on unbelievers, then most people's faith would collapse into despair and revert to unbelief; who could hold to a religion that guaranteed him ruin? So God threads between the two. He gives to some believers and withholds from others; He gives to some unbelievers and withholds from others; He grants and denies at different times to each. And in exactly this way, the scholar says, the reigning imam bestows worldly benefit on Muslim and unbeliever alike and withholds it from both, gives to believer and hypocrite alike and withholds from both, following God's own conduct — so that obedience to him is never bought by worldly advantage, and never abandoned for the lack of it. He gives to the believers, for a season, as no one else ever gave; and he withholds from them too, for a season, for their own good, lest they grow complacent and become like the one of whom God said, "man is rebellious, for he thinks himself self-sufficient."

The scholar keeps widening the circle, pairing off every human type to show there is no category the pattern misses. God gives worldly goods to the pious and the impious alike, so that the pious will not do good merely for a worldly return, nor abstain from wrong merely to protect a worldly interest, which would rob their conduct of its heavenly reward; their obedience should be for God's sake and nothing less. And so the imam gives to the righteous and the immoral alike. God gives to the worshipper and the non-worshipper, lest worship itself become a means of gain; and so the imam looks on his people not fixing on their faults but regarding them with a merciful eye. The scholar's whole account of this open-handedness rests on a mercy that runs ahead of judgment — the mercy God announced of Himself when He said, in the words the Prophet transmitted from Him:

> My mercy has overtaken My wrath. — the Prophet.

It is that priority of mercy over reckoning that the imam is imitating when he gives to the undeserving. God gives worldly benefit to the simpleton and the ignorant as freely as to the learned and the intelligent — and withholds from both — for had He given only to the learned, people would pursue knowledge purely as a road to wealth, and the ignorant would be left in despair; and so the imam gives to the ignorant and the learned alike. God gives to the wrongdoer and the just, to the generous and the miser, to the noble and the depraved, to those full of virtue and those empty of it, distributing to all out of His wisdom and mercy so that each receives a fair share of His bounty; and the imam, following God's own actions and His mercy toward His creatures, treats every one of them the same. Type after type, the mirror holds.

Then the scholar turns from what God gives to how God conceals, and the concealment is just as deliberate as the giving. God has hidden the workings of the world and the means by which His creatures come to benefit from it. He has kept the methods of worldly gain obscure, so that one person grows wealthy while another has nothing to eat, one prospers in trade while another goes bankrupt, one flourishes in farming while another loses even the seed he planted, one man is made rich by hunting while another is left with nothing. Why the hiddenness? Because if a single sure path to fortune were universally known, everyone would pour down that one road, every other path would fall idle, and the whole ordered variety of the world would be thrown into confusion and corrupted. The concealment protects the world from becoming a formula. And the imam, the scholar says, conceals in exactly the same way. He hides the means of gaining benefit through him, so that one man seeks his favor by a particular route and receives it, while another who seeks it by that very same route is denied. For if people ever discovered a definite method for obtaining the imam's bounty, all other means would become futile, and everyone who lacked that one key would despair of his mercy and be left deprived — and there would be no merit left in any of it. The hiddenness keeps hope open for everyone and certainty available to no one.

The same principle governs the imam's nearness and his distance, and here the scholar states the purpose most beautifully. Were the imam to push the hypocrites away and draw only the sincere believers close, then people would perform belief in order to win his nearness, and their faith would be a courtship rather than a conviction. And were he to hold himself at a distance from all believers at all times, they would withdraw from him, anxiety creeping into their hearts, imagining they had displeased him or committed some slip that earned his coldness. So he deals with people as the times and conditions require — arousing longing and alarm, drawing near and pushing away, granting and withholding, punishing and forgiving — all of it in emulation of God's own manner with His creation, so that people remain perpetually suspended between fear and hope: never so secure that they grow bold, never so despairing that they collapse. That suspension is not neglect. It is the precise spiritual climate in which a soul stays awake.

And there is one more feature of the imam's giving that sets it wholly apart, the scholar insists, from the giving of every other monarch. The gifts of ordinary kings, people rightly perceive, are always payment for something — a service rendered, a relationship maintained, an obligation discharged, a debt repaid, a fear of some calamity, a hope of some advantage, a wish to be praised or thanked. Every such gift is a transaction, calculated and assessed; and a gift given for any of those reasons, the scholar argues, cannot honestly be called generosity at all. To delay what you owe and then pay it late is miserliness, not virtue; to advance money in expectation of a return is investment, not bounty. The gifts of God transcend all of that, for God is under no obligation to anyone and seeks no reward from any creature. And in just this way the imam gives to no one for any of those reasons, and seeks no recompense for what he gives — as God said of the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, "we desire neither reward nor thanks from you." He bestows freely, spontaneously, out of pure generosity, following God's own conduct with creation. His giving is the one giving in the human world that asks for nothing back.

That difference — between worship or service offered for a wage and service offered for nothing — is exactly the difference the Father of Imams himself once drew when he sorted all devotion into three kinds:

> A people worship God out of desire for reward — that is the worship of merchants; and a people worship Him out of fear — that is the worship of slaves; and a people worship Him out of gratitude — that is the worship of the free. — the Father of Imams.

The imam's giving is the giving of the free: not the merchant's, angling for a return, nor the slave's, extracted by fear, but the open hand of one who owes nothing and asks nothing, moving only out of a fullness that answers to God alone.

The scholar knows that such a figure is easy to misread, and he marks out the two errors that surround him — one on each side, with the truth passing between. On one side stand those who look at the imam with short sight, judging his actions by the standard of ordinary human beings and ordinary rulers. They compare what he does against what they have seen and heard and grown accustomed to; and finding his conduct at odds with their expectations — giving where a king would withhold, withholding where a king would give — they fall into perplexity and confusion, and begin to speculate. If they would only measure his actions against God's dealings with creation rather than against other men's, the scholar says, everything would grow clear; the trouble is that from the time of Adam to the present day, no one has acted in this manner, and so people have no precedent to reach for. On the other side stand those who look at the imam with exaggeration, and, dazzled, claim divinity for him — the ghulat, the extremists who inflate the office past its truth. The imam's actions surpass both errors. He is not a common ruler to be measured by common rulers, and he is not God. He is the Universal Vicegerent of God over His servants — a shadow, as the Prophet said, that perfectly imitates in every detail the thing that casts it. That single image holds the whole doctrine: near enough to God to reproduce His conduct exactly, and yet, being a shadow, never for a moment God Himself.

## The vicegerent and the closing prayer

The scholar now draws the two halves of his argument together — the concealing and the disclosing — into one final statement of how the imam governs, and it is here that the mirror is completed. Just as God, he says, has concealed from mankind the reasons for creating the world while openly explaining the means of attaining religion and His good pleasure; just as God gave knowledge and wisdom to those who deserved them and denied them to those who did not, setting a barrier before and behind, planting the signposts of guidance and raising up the summons of the proof, opening the gates for whoever is worthy and letting the unworthy miss them, all in perfect wisdom — so the reigning imam does the same across both worlds at once. In worldly matters he conceals: he hides the means of gaining fortune through him and gives it to deserving and undeserving alike, so that no one can reduce his bounty to a formula. In religious matters he discloses: he openly teaches the means of attaining religion, knowledge, and wisdom, and bestows them generously on those who deserve them, neither withholding nor hoarding, giving more than anyone in any age could give. And he withholds them from the undeserving, drawing the line that must be drawn — distinguishing the people of truth, the ahl al-haqq, from the people of falsehood, the ahl al-batil. In all of it he is following the ways of God, the scholar says, in governing the world and religion together, and in building up both this present life and the life to come. The vicegerent does over those entrusted to him exactly what the One who appointed him would do — which is what the word vicegerent, khalifa, has meant from the beginning.

The scholar pauses on that word, because it carries the whole weight of the claim. Adam was called the vicegerent, he reminds us, because he was the first to take up the vicegerency; and those who came after him were called by the same name because each discharged some part of its duty, and whoever performs part of a charge is known by its name. Scripture uses the word in exactly this governing sense when it charges one of the prophet-kings: "O David, We have made you a vicegerent on the earth, so judge between the people in truth" (chapter 38, verse 26). To be a khalifa, in that verse, is precisely to govern God's servants as God would have them governed. But the Father of Imams, and the reigning imam who continues him, perform the vicegerency whole — both its worldly and its religious dimensions, in full reality, not in part. When the Prophet said, "the ruler is the shadow of God on earth," he meant this vicegerent and no other; for no one deals with creation as God does except the one who stands in God's place over God's servants. All the earlier vicegerents fulfilled a small portion of God's manner of dealing; this one fulfills the whole of it. That is the difference between discharging part of a duty and completing it, between wearing the name and being the reality the name was made for.

And now the scholar lets the treatise come to rest on the thing a book of this kind should end on: not a fresh argument but a prayer. Having spent the entire work proving by reason and revelation that the world must have its summit, that the summit is the imam, and that the imam's dealings mirror God's own, he turns from proving to asking. This is his own valedictory supplication, and it deserves to be heard in full rather than summarized away, because it tells you what the whole austere machinery of the argument was finally in service of. He asks God to bestow upon him and his community gratitude for the blessings God has given — gratitude expressed not in words only but in words and deeds and intention together, the whole person turned toward thankfulness. He asks for success in obedience to the imam of the age, and for the means to strive on that imam's behalf with hand and tongue and heart — the three instruments of a life, the body's action and the mouth's speech and the heart's devotion, all of them offered up in service. He prays that God open the whole world to that imam and, through him, purify the earth of every unbelief and every hypocrisy, by his bounty and his grace. It is a prayer that gathers the book's argument into a posture of the soul: having shown that the imam stands at the summit, the scholar asks only to be allowed to serve that summit truly, and to be given the gratitude such a station deserves.

And then the treatise moves to its final words, and they are words of praise, the natural close of a work of devotion: "Praise be to God, the praise of the thankful, the mindful, and the pious, and blessings upon His Messenger, our master Muhammad, and all his progeny." That is where the book ends. A treatise that opened by reasoning about elements and stars closes by blessing the Prophet's family — the argument dissolving, in its last breath, into worship.

It is worth sitting for a moment with the shape of that ending, because it tells you something about the whole enterprise. The scholar could have closed with a flourish of proof, a final crushing syllogism. He does not. He closes with thanks and a blessing, because for him the argument was never the point in itself; it was the road to a relationship — to obedience, to service, to gratitude, to love of the summit God set at the peak of the human world. The reasoning was the ladder. The prayer is what you say when you have climbed it and arrived. And so the last note of the treatise is not the sound of a case being won but the sound of a servant giving thanks for having been shown where to stand.

## What this chapter lands

The treatise ends exactly where its ladder always pointed. From the opening pages, everything in the book leaned toward a single figure — the summit of creation, the peak toward which nature and humanity and the whole line of guides all incline — and in this final movement that figure steps fully into view. He is the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, the Father of Imams, the head and heart and spirit of the entire body of guidance, the perfector of every prophet and imam before him; and he is the reigning imam of the age who continues that summit into the present, the qa'im of his own time, the vicegerent who deals with creation as God deals with it. The book set out to prove that the world must have a peak, and it closes by naming who stands there.

Look back now across the whole treatise and you can see how tightly the pieces fit. The early chapters established the pole of religion — the conviction that guidance is a necessity woven into reality, not a convenience the mind can do without. The theory of degrees of excellence climbed rung by rung from the four natures through the stars to humankind, teaching the eye to see that every ordered domain gathers its excellence to a summit. The middle stretch turned to worship and to the pillars that hold a religious life upright. The later chapters read history itself as one unbroken succession of guides, and measured the ordinary claimants against the standard the office demands. Every one of those movements was preparing the ground for this: the recognition that the summit of the human world is the imam, and that the imam's whole conduct — his giving and his withholding, his nearness and his distance, his concealing of worldly fortune and his open teaching of religion — is a shadow cast by God's own dealing with His creation.

That last idea is the one to carry away, because it is the boldest thing the scholar says and the truest to his purpose. The highest praise he can offer a human being is not that he is wise, or just, or generous in the ordinary way, but that he treats people the way God treats them — and that this, far from being a scandal, is the very mark of the vicegerent. The imam gives without being paid, conceals without being cruel, draws near and withdraws to keep the soul awake between fear and hope, and asks for nothing in return, because he is imitating the One whose conduct owes nothing to anyone. To judge him by the measure of ordinary rulers is to miss him entirely; to inflate him into a god is to miss him just as badly. He is the shadow that perfectly reproduces what casts it — and to recognize him, the scholar has spent a whole book teaching, is to recognize the point at which the visible world touches the invisible one.

And so the treatise closes not with a further argument but with the argument's completion. The engine that reached for a lump of gold and a red ruby has run its full course and arrived at its destination: the human being at the summit of nature, the imam at the summit of humanity, and God's own conduct reflected in the imam's dealings with the world. There is no higher rung to climb. The ladder has reached its top, and the scholar, having climbed it, lays down his proof and lifts up a prayer — which is, in the end, exactly where a book like this was always meant to arrive.
