## Where the argument picks up

A man may look at an animal and decide, on the spot, what its life is for. He may ride it where he needs to go, milk it, shear it, put it to work in the field, slaughter it for a feast, or simply let it live and graze — and he does all of this by his own judgment, against the animal's own inclination, and no one thinks him a tyrant for it. Now hold that ordinary picture in your mind and hear the sentence al-Naysaburi (النيسابوري) builds on it: humanity stands to the imam exactly as the animals stand to humanity. That is not a throwaway comparison. It is a claim about who owns the world, who may take from it, and by whose leave anyone holds anything at all. And it is where this chapter lives.

The previous stretch of the treatise climbed a ladder. It began at the four bare elements and rose, rung by rung, through the metals and the stars, through gold and the unburnable ruby and the balanced grain, until it reached the human being — and then it took one step further and reached the imam. Two conclusions sat at the top of that climb, locked together: humanity is the summit of the whole lower creation, and the imam is the summit of humanity. But a summit is a strange thing to arrive at and then say nothing more about. To be at the top of an ordered world is to be owed something by everything beneath you — or to owe something to it. So the question this chapter asks is the one the ladder left hanging. Granted that the imam is the summit: what does that summit actually authorize? What may he do, what may he demand, and what does his being at the top mean for everyone standing below?

Al-Naysaburi answers in three moves, and this chapter follows all three. First, the doctrine of dominion: the whole world was made for the imams, its benefit belongs to them, and to take from it without their leave is theft. Second, the doctrine of wisdom: because the imam is wise without qualification, the believer owes him a submission that does not stop to argue. Third, the doctrine of necessity: reason itself, with no scripture required, shows that the world must have a single supreme leader, and that this leader must be the best of all who live. Dominion, wisdom, necessity — the ownership, the trust it demands, and the proof that the office cannot be empty or filled by a lesser man. That is the whole of what follows.

## Everything returns to the imam

Start with a question that sounds almost accusatory: why was any of it made? The stars, the seas, the metals in the rock, the grain in the field, the animals in their herds — al-Naysaburi has already argued that each of these exists for the sake of what stands above it, that the lower rungs are there to serve the higher. Now he closes the chain. The whole of natural creation, he says, was made for the sake of humanity, because only a being who can know and benefit gives the rest of it any point at all. Were there no human being to eat the fruit, ride the beast, mine the ore, and read the sky, there would be no wisdom in a single plant or animal ever having been made. But humanity, in turn, was not made for its own sake either. Human beings were created for the imams — created to receive religion from them, to be perfected through them, and to make manifest their order, their organization, and their foundation. Take that as a single descending sentence: the worlds exist for humanity, and humanity exists for the imams. The point of everything is finally the imam, because it is only in him that the whole ascent completes itself.

From that one premise a startling consequence falls out, and al-Naysaburi does not soften it. If all the worlds were made for the imams, then all the benefit of the worlds belongs to the imams. The rest of creation does not stand alongside them as an equal or a partner; it stands to them as children stand to a parent — dependent, provided for, owing everything and owed nothing. Whatever the imams give to creation is given by their favour, their generosity, their mercy; it is a gift, not a debt being repaid. And if they withhold anything, they are under no obligation to give it, and owe no one an account for the withholding. This is the frame inside which al-Naysaburi says the hardest thing in the section: whoever takes anything of this world — anything it contains — without the permission and approval of the imam has taken what is haram, what is forbidden, and has committed an act of usurpation. Not a discourtesy. A seizure of what is not his to seize, from an owner who never granted it.

He grounds the claim in scripture, and the verse he chooses is exact. God says of the spoils that come to the community, "That which God bestows on His Messenger from the people of the villages, it is for God and His Messenger" (chapter 59, verse 7). What matters to al-Naysaburi is the word the verse uses for that bestowal — fay'. Its root meaning, he points out, is not "gift" but "return." It is the same word Arabic uses for a shadow: a shadow is called fay' because in the evening it comes back to the place from which it first stretched out. And that, he says, is the truth about the whole world. Everything in it comes from the fortune and property of the imams in the first place; so whatever passes into their hands is not being handed over to a stranger. It is a return — a shadow coming home at evening to the object that cast it. The world does not become the imams' property when they receive it. It was always theirs. Receiving it only makes the ownership visible. And that single etymological point, small as it looks, is doing heavy work in the argument. If the world were originally no one's, then the imam's claim on it would be one claim among many, to be weighed against everyone else's. But if the world was the imams' from the start — if every good in it is on loan from a source it is always tending back toward — then no rival claim can even get started, because there is no ownerless ground on which a rival could plant his flag. The doctrine of usurpation follows straight from the doctrine of return: you cannot usurp what is unowned, and once al-Naysaburi has shown that nothing here is unowned, taking without leave has only one name left.

Al-Naysaburi is not building this authority out of nothing; the obligation to defer to it is written into the revealed command itself. The same scripture that tells the believer to obey God tells him, in the very same breath, to obey a human authority set over him.

> O you who believe, obey God, and obey the Messenger, and those in authority among you (chapter 4, verse 59).

The verse names three objects of obedience and binds them into a single imperative — God, the Messenger, and uli'l-amr, those charged with the command. For al-Naysaburi the third is not a loose reference to whoever happens to hold power; it is the imam, the summit at the top of the ladder. The world's benefit returning to its rightful owner and the believer's obedience flowing toward that same owner are two faces of one order. The doctrine of return fixes the first; the verse fixes the second.

Now, a claim that large needs a foundation, and al-Naysaburi builds one out of the same ladder of nature he used before — but he reads it here for what it says about power. Consider, he says, how one rung actually rules the rung below it. A plant surpasses the bare earth by exactly one added thing: a single spirit, the spirit of growth, the ruh al-nama'. And that one addition is enough to make the earth subservient to the plant — the soil now feeds the root, the ground becomes something the plant draws its strength from. Go up a rung. An animal surpasses the plant by one further spirit added to growth: the spirit of sensation, the ruh al-hiss, the power to feel and to move. And that one addition lets the animal subdue the plant — it walks over the ground the plant is rooted in and eats the plant at will. Go up once more. A human being surpasses the animal by one further spirit still: the spirit of articulate reason, the ruh al-nutq, the power of speech and thought. And that single addition puts the whole lower world under human hands. Man builds on the earth, sows and plants it, bends it to his wisdom; he eats the fruit and burns the wood; he rides the animals he needs, slaughters some, milks and shears others, and uses every one of them as he sees fit, even against its own will. Notice the pattern precisely, because al-Naysaburi means it as a law and not a series of examples: at every level, one added spirit is the whole difference between ruling and being ruled.

Then he applies the law to the imam, and the law does the rest of the work for him. When the imam takes up his charge, al-Naysaburi says, he is not left at the human rung. He is endowed with further spirits, further powers, and a purity that no ordinary human being possesses. In him the very pinnacle of human purity is joined to the subtlety and refinement of nature itself — the choicest of the animal powers, the balance of the elements, the finest influence of the spheres and stars, all gathered up so that he becomes, in al-Naysaburi's phrase, the quintessence of the whole world. And beyond even that, the higher spirits unite in him: the full sanctified spirit, the ruh al-quds, of which an ordinary person holds only a portion — and no more of that portion than the imam chooses to bestow on him by grace. Bound to it is the creative spirit, the ruh al-ibda', which al-Naysaburi calls the perfect seventh spirit, the one God has prepared for humanity in the world of reward — the spirit of Oneness, the ruh al-wahda. Count the movement of the argument: the human being climbed above the animal by one added spirit and thereby won dominion over the animal; the imam is lifted above the ordinary human by a whole array of added spirits, up to the seventh and highest. If one spirit was enough to make man the master of the beast, what does a ladder of them make the imam?

It is worth pausing to see that this dominion is not a one-way seizure, because al-Naysaburi is careful to bind the imam to a duty in the same breath that he grants him the power. Look again at how a man rules his animals, he says. He does not merely exploit them; by the very norms of that rule he protects them — guarding their watering places and grazing grounds, shielding them from what would harm them, tending their well-being even against their own foolish appetites. The mastery and the care are the same relationship read from two sides. And so it is obligatory upon the imam, by the norm of kingship, the demands of nature, sound policy, and law, to protect the welfare of mankind exactly as man protects the welfare of the animals he governs. The dominion, in other words, comes with a charge attached; the one who owns the flock is precisely the one obliged to keep it alive. Which is why al-Naysaburi can then sort people by how they answer that charge. Whoever submits to the imam's rule over both spirit and body is saturated with knowledge and wisdom, lifted above his fellows, and departs this world in an upright form, ready to receive his reward. But whoever submits only outwardly, keeping the inward assent back, falls into the torment of doubt and discord and arrives crooked, unprepared, at the place of recompense. The difference is not in the outward act, which may look identical in both men. It is in whether the submission reached the inside.

This duty of care is not al-Naysaburi's invention either; it is the very heart of how the tradition has always described just rule. The Father of Imams, instructing a governor he was sending to Egypt, made mercy toward the governed the first charge of the office:

> Let your heart be filled with mercy for the subjects, and love for them, and kindness toward them; do not be a ravening beast over them, seizing what they have. — the Father of Imams (peace be upon him).

The one set above the flock is precisely the one bound to tend it, and to turn predator over it is to betray the office rather than to exercise it. Al-Naysaburi's imam, master of humanity by the sheer weight of his spirits, stands under exactly that older charge: the higher the dominion, the heavier the duty of care that comes bound to it.

Al-Naysaburi draws the conclusion in a single stroke, and it is the sentence the whole section has been building toward. Because God gathered in the imams all these faculties, merits, and spirits — over and above what humanity already has — He subjected all of humanity to them, just as He had already subjected the animals to man by one added spirit, the plants to the animals by one, and the earth to the plants by one. The dominion is not a title conferred by decree from outside; it is the natural weight of a being who carries that many more spirits than those beneath him. And here is the measure al-Naysaburi lays down explicitly: the number of spirits residing in the imams determines the extent of their control and dominion over humanity. Rank is not a symbol of authority in this scheme. Rank is authority; the two are the same quantity read twice. So the final line of the section closes the circle back to where the argument opened. It is no more for a human being to remove himself from the imam's domain and rule than it is for an animal to remove itself from the rule of man. The animal that will not submit, that offers no benefit and only preys, is judged by both reason and law to be dealt with as a danger. And the person who sets himself outside the imam's authority has, on al-Naysaburi's reading, stepped into exactly that category — not because a ruler is angry with him, but because he has torn himself out of the order that the added spirits established, and there is no rung below the human rung for him to stand on except the beast's.

## The absolute wisdom of the imam

If the first doctrine tells you that everything belongs to the imam, the second tells you how the believer is to hold himself before that authority — and the answer is a word that will make a modern reader flinch until the argument behind it is heard: taslim, an unquestioning submission. Al-Naysaburi knows it needs earning, so he does not begin with a command. He begins with a farmer.

Picture, he says, a man of real insight walking over the land. He comes on a stretch of good, fertile soil, and he knows at once what it is for — he can see that it will take seed and give back growth. He finds water where others would never look for it, down in distant valleys or hidden underground, and rather than shrug and walk on he labours: he digs canals, sinks wells, and directs that buried water to the waiting ground. Why does he go to the trouble? Because a man of insight sees no wisdom in leaving good land idle and good water sealed away — it would be a waste that his very care for the world will not tolerate. Now transpose that, al-Naysaburi says, to the imams. When they look at people, they see fertile ground: souls ready, or nearly ready, to receive the seed of wisdom and knowledge, even when those people are still very far from being able to take it in. And so the imams dig their canals. They labour through preparatory religious exercises, disciplines whose purpose the people themselves often cannot yet perceive, patiently channelling toward the soul the water it will one day drink. And where people simply cannot receive the wisdom plainly, in open speech, the imams do the other thing a wise cultivator does with difficult ground: they embed the wisdom in dense symbols — in words and in actions both — so that people may take the symbol now and be raised, by slow degrees, to the meaning inside it later. The obscurity, in other words, is not withholding. It is irrigation. It is wisdom being routed through a channel the ground can actually absorb.

Hold that image, because it is the whole ground of what comes next. It is therefore necessary, al-Naysaburi says, to believe in the supreme leadership of the imam of the time — to know that he is the head of the world and the vicegerent of God over His creation, the khalifa through whom God's governance reaches what He made. And it is necessary never to object to any of the imam's commands, his prohibitions, his actions, or his teachings — to hold, in general and in advance, that all of it embodies wisdom and is for the welfare of creation, even when the particular point of a particular thing is hidden and stays hidden for a long while. He grants the difficulty openly: the true meaning of some command may be genuinely hard for a believer to grasp, and he may feel real resistance to it. But the resistance, al-Naysaburi says, is a fact about the believer's sight, not about the command's wisdom — and he offers the sharpest possible parallel from inside the religion the believer already accepts. Think how much of the sacred law, the shari'a, works exactly this way. The faithful frequently do not understand, at first, what the Lawgiver was after in some ruling; they may bristle at it; and then those to whom God grants recognition come, later, to see the wisdom that was folded inside it the whole time. If that is how a believer already relates to the law he obeys, al-Naysaburi is asking only that he extend the same patience to the living guide of the law.

He widens the point past the law to the world at large, because the same pattern shows up everywhere a person looks, if he looks long enough. There are countless things in creation, al-Naysaburi says, whose making people take to be simply harmful — dangers, pests, disasters — in which they can see no wisdom and no benefit at all, and about which they are quick to conclude that no good was ever in them. And then, later, when the wisdom of the thing finally becomes visible to them, they grow calm, and the objection they had been carrying quietly falls away — and once they have seen it they cannot honestly deny it. This is the human situation as al-Naysaburi reads it: not that the wisdom is absent, but that it is often on a delay, arriving after the complaint. So the believer who suspends his objection to the imam is not being asked to believe something he has no experience of. He is being asked to do, in the one case that matters most, what life has already taught him to do a hundred times in smaller matters — to grant that his present inability to see the point is a fact about the length of his sight, not about the emptiness of the thing.

Scripture gives this exact situation a story, and it is the story of a prophet failing the very test al-Naysaburi is describing. When Moses set out to learn from the servant of God whom the tradition calls al-Khidr, the guide warned him at the outset that he would not be able to bear what he was about to see:

> And how can you have patience for that which you do not encompass in knowledge? (chapter 18, verse 68).

And Moses could not. The guide scuttled a sound boat, killed a young man, and rebuilt a wall in a town that had just refused them hospitality — and at each act Moses objected, unable to hold his peace, until the guide parted from him and only then disclosed the wisdom hidden inside all three: the boat scuttled to save it from a seizing tyrant, the young man taken before he could ruin his believing parents, the wall raised to guard an orphan's buried treasure until he came of age. The wisdom had been there the whole time; what Moses lacked was not the wisdom but the patience to wait for it. That is taslim shown from the inside — the recognition that an act which looks like sheer damage from below may be rescue seen from above, and that the whole distance between the two is only the length of one's own sight.

Then he tightens the argument from a plea for patience into something closer to a proof, and it turns on the name of God he has been circling: al-Hakim, the Wise One. Whoever truly recognizes the Wise One, he says — whoever grants that God is wise — has already, whether he has noticed it or not, granted a great deal more. For to call someone wise without qualification is to say that everything he does is full of wisdom, that he would never do a single thing empty of it, and that he would neither command nor forbid nor say anything except out of wisdom. A futile act is simply not available to such a being. And al-Naysaburi is precise about what "futile" means: a futile act is one whose doing and not-doing come to exactly the same thing — an act you could just as well have skipped, with nothing lost either way. That kind of act, he says, is impossible for the Wise One, because it would be wisdom sitting idle, and a wisdom that idles is not the wisdom the name asserts. So far this is about God. But now watch the hinge. If it is not permissible for the Wise One to perform even one act whose presence and absence are equivalent, then how — al-Naysaburi asks — could the imam perform an act when the wisdom lay somewhere else, or in the very opposite of what he did, and when the welfare of creation was to be found not there but elsewhere? He cannot. The imam is absolutely wise; his wisdom is not his own achievement but a bestowal, and whatever he bestows of it on a servant makes that servant wise in turn. To do a thing at random, without wisdom or goodness, is to act corruptly and in vain — and that is precisely the thing the office forbids.

Which is why the last step of this doctrine is so severe, and why al-Naysaburi frames it as a danger to be prayed against rather than a position to be debated. To believe of the imam of the time that he has acted without wisdom is not a small error of judgment about one decision. It is to say that this individual has departed from wisdom. And whoever has departed from wisdom, al-Naysaburi reasons, has by that very fact departed from the domain of his imamate (الإمامة) — because the imamate simply is the seat of wisdom in the world, and a wisdomless imam is a contradiction in terms. To depart from the domain of the imamate is, in this system, to depart from the faith itself, since the faith is built on that seat. So the objection that looked like ordinary criticism — "this command of his makes no sense" — turns out, followed to its root, to be a denial of the whole structure the believer claims to stand inside. Al-Naysaburi presses the same charge through two images: the teacher who drives students through lessons they resent, knowing they will thank him once they mature and see the benefit; and the physician who forces bitter medicine, or even lancing and cauterization, on a patient who cannot yet see that the pain is the cure. Both are guides whose severity is a difficult mercy, cutting only to heal. Here he supplies the logic beneath those pictures: it is not merely that the imam means well when he is harsh; it is that a wise agent cannot act pointlessly, so there is no such thing as an imam's act that is really empty, however empty it looks from below. The submission he calls taslim, then, is not a surrender of the mind. It is what the mind concludes once it has actually granted that the Wise One is wise.

## The necessity of the best

The first two doctrines lean, in part, on who the imam is. The third leans on nothing but reason, and al-Naysaburi means it to persuade even someone who accepts no revelation at all. Its claim comes in two joined halves: that the world must have a single supreme leader, and that this leader must be the most excellent of everyone alive. He proves the first with three pictures and the second with a point about the meaning of words.

Begin with the shepherd, because it is the plainest. Take a flock of livestock grazing free on open ground. The animals have real worth — there is nothing deficient about them as animals — and yet, left to themselves, they perish. They cannot choose their own pasture wisely or find their own water; they cannot fend off the thief or the beast of prey; they cannot, in a word, manage their own survival. What they lack is not strength or value but a shepherd: someone to select the grazing, guard the watering places, and stand between them and what would kill them. Now people, al-Naysaburi says, are exactly like this. For all their gifts, without a shepherd to protect their worldly and their religious life they would come apart — devoured, scattered, unable to secure their own good. The imam is that shepherd. And here al-Naysaburi turns the picture into an edge: if the imam is the true shepherd, then everyone else who claims that role over the flock is not a rival shepherd but a wolf — a predator dressed as a protector, thinning the flock it pretends to guard. He even reaches for the ordinary word to seal it. People everywhere are called subjects, ra'iya — and ra'iya, he notes, means precisely "a flock under a shepherd's care." The very language a society uses for itself confesses that it expects to be shepherded. The only question the word leaves open is who the shepherd is, and who the wolves are.

The word al-Naysaburi leans on here carries a saying with it, one preserved in both of the great Sahih collections. Speaking of responsibility as a thing no one escapes, the Prophet ﷺ made every person a shepherd of some flock:

> Each of you is a shepherd, and each of you is responsible for his flock. — the Prophet.

The saying runs the image all the way down — the ruler over his people, the man over his household, the woman over her home — so that shepherding is not one office among others but the shape of every responsibility there is. And if even the head of a single household stands as a shepherd answerable for those in his care, then the head of the whole community stands as the shepherd of shepherds, the one on whom every lesser flock finally depends. The truth al-Naysaburi caught inside the ordinary word ra'iya, the Prophet had already made into a law of responsibility.

The second picture is the body, and it raises the stakes from perishing slowly to collapsing at once. A body without a head, al-Naysaburi says, is not an injured body — it is a decaying corpse, of no use to anyone. Apply that to the world. A world with no head, no leader, no master, no sa'is to steer its worldly and religious affairs, would not limp along in disorder; it would rot. People would turn into beasts devouring one another, and — this is the phrase he insists on — the world would not endure even a single day. Not a decline over generations. A day. The claim is deliberately extreme because it is meant to rule out a tempting middle position: the idea that leadership is good to have but that a society could, at a pinch, stumble on without it. No, al-Naysaburi says: headship is to the world what the head is to the body, and a body can survive the loss of many things but not that one. Remove it and what remains is not a wounded order but a carcass.

The third picture is the widest, and it is meant to close off the last escape — the suspicion that leadership is a merely human arrangement, a convention we invented and could have done without. Look at nature, al-Naysaburi says, and you find headship built into it everywhere, long before any human agreed to anything. The stallion guards and disciplines the mares; the rooster watches over the hens; every species defends its females and its young as far as its strength allows and guides the weak among its own kind. The crane, he notes, obeys its leader in flight; the bee obeys its queen. God arranged the whole of creation on this pattern of gradation and rank, so that it would be unmistakable that within every single species and genus there is a peak, a summit, a chief in nobility and worth. And then comes the inference the three pictures were laid down to earn: by that very same reasoning, all of mankind must likewise have a peak and a summit in nobility and worth, to serve as leader and guide. Headship is not a custom humans dreamt up. It is a feature of nature and of creation, of instinct and intuition — so deep that, al-Naysaburi says, even those who deny revelation and religion outright cannot deny, either by reason or by plain observation, that the world requires leadership. He has now argued the first half of the claim without once leaning on a prophet: the world must have a single head.

It is worth dwelling on why al-Naysaburi stacks three pictures rather than resting on one, because each is aimed at a different way of shrugging the claim off. The shepherd answers the person who thinks a capable society could look after itself: no, he says, competence is not the issue — the flock is not defective, it is simply unshepherded, and unshepherded it dies. The body answers the person who imagines leadership is a slow-acting good the world could survive without for a while: no, remove the head and what you have is not a struggling body but a corpse within a day. And the ranks of nature answer the person who suspects the whole thing is a human invention we could uninvent: no, the stallion and the rooster and the crane and the bee never signed a contract, and headship runs through them all. Taken singly, each picture could be dodged. Taken together they close every exit at once — which is exactly why al-Naysaburi lays all three down before he moves on. By the end of them the claim he wants is no longer a doctrine one might accept or decline; it has the feel of a thing one cannot honestly go on denying, whatever one's religion or lack of it.

The tradition had already met this question in its sharpest political form. When a faction raised the slogan that judgment belongs to God alone, and pressed it into the claim that no human government was needed at all, the Father of Imams answered that the words were true but the conclusion false:

> These people say there is no rule but God's — and yes, there is no judgment but God's; but they mean by it that there is no government, and the people cannot do without a leader, righteous or wicked. — the Father of Imams.

The point is exactly al-Naysaburi's: leadership is not an optional good a community might forgo in favour of pious anarchy. Even the imperfect form of it is preferable to its absence, because its absence is not disorder but dissolution. Where al-Naysaburi presses further is only in the next step — that the leader a people cannot do without ought not to be merely present but the best of them; and to that step the argument now turns.

The second half — that the head must be the best — he settles almost as a matter of definition, and this is the tightest turn in the section. Grant, he says, that the world must have a supreme leader. Then it is mandatory that this leader be the best of all people: the most noble, the most perfect, the most knowledgeable, the most pure. And the reason is nearly a point of grammar. The title "supreme leader," al-imam, simply cannot be laid on the one who is himself led, the ma'mum — the words mean opposite things. The title "the excellent," al-fadil, cannot be laid on the one who is surpassed by another, the mafdul. The title "the one who precedes," al-sabiq, cannot be laid on the one who is preceded, the masbuq. To call the led man the leader, or the surpassed man the excellent, is not a moral mistake; it is a misuse of language, a word applied to its own contrary. So the supremacy is not an extra honour we choose to attach to the leader after the fact. It is contained in the very idea of "the leader," the way being surpassed is contained in the idea of "the surpassed." Al-Naysaburi even claims the widest possible agreement for it: people of every community and every religion — philosophers, deniers of God, those who strip Him of every attribute — all concede that the leader of the world ought to be the best, most perfect, and most knowledgeable of all. On the principle, everyone agrees. And that is the ground on which he lands the whole argument: in all the world, he says, no one fits this description — most knowing, most pure, most complete — except the imams.

Scripture had already turned that same point into a question that answers itself:

> Is He who guides to the truth more worthy to be followed, or he who cannot guide unless he himself is guided? So what is wrong with you; how do you judge? (chapter 10, verse 35).

The verse does not so much argue the case as expose it as already settled in the meaning of the words: the one who must be led cannot coherently be called the leader. Al-Naysaburi's grammatical point — that al-imam cannot name the ma'mum, that al-fadil cannot name the one surpassed — is the same recognition the verse presses. To follow the guided in place of the guide is not a debatable preference. It is, the verse says, a failure of judgment about the plain sense of what a guide is.

And this is exactly where the pretenders fall out of the running, by their own qualifications. Al-Naysaburi grounds the point in the philosophers' three governances — of oneself, of one's household, of the public — and the strict chain that binds them: no one who cannot rule his own soul has any business ruling a family, and no one who cannot rule a family has any claim on ruling a people. He needs only the scheme's lowest rung here. The men who have seized the imams' place and titles, he says, cannot govern themselves, their families, or the people — they are the ones plainly given over to amusement, depravity, and corruption. The disqualification does not even reach the hard question of who is best. It stops at the first gate: a man who cannot command his own appetites has failed the entrance requirement for every rule above him. So the field is not crowded with strong candidates from whom the imam must be singled out. By the standard everyone already accepts, it is empty of everyone but him.

## What this chapter establishes

Three doctrines, and they interlock. The doctrine of dominion said that the whole world was made for the imams, that its benefit returns to them like a shadow to the thing that cast it, and that to take from it without their leave is not liberty but usurpation — because the added spirits that lift the imam above the human rung give him the same natural mastery over people that the single added spirit of reason gives a human over the beasts. The doctrine of wisdom said that this mastery is not to be feared but trusted, because the imam is absolutely wise, and a wise agent cannot act in vain; so the believer's submission, the taslim, is not the surrender of his judgment but its conclusion, once he has granted that the Wise One is wise. And the doctrine of necessity said that none of this rests on taking anyone's word for it: reason alone, reading the shepherdless flock, the headless body, and the ordered ranks of nature, forces the admission that the world must have one supreme head — and that the head, by the plain meaning of the word, must be the best of all. Ownership, trust, and inevitability: the imam's authority established from three directions that do not need each other to stand.

But notice what has, and has not, been shown. Everything in this chapter has been about the authority in the abstract — its ground, its extent, its logic. Al-Naysaburi has proven that the office must exist, that it must be filled by the best, and that everything belongs to the one who fills it. What he has not yet done is show that authority actually doing anything. And that is the objection a careful listener should now be feeling: this is all very grand, but is it not floating free of ordinary religious life? Does the imam's supremacy touch the things a believer actually does — the prayer, the fast, the alms, the pilgrimage, the whole daily and yearly fabric of worship and law?

But that objection has an answer, and it is the one al-Naysaburi presses hardest: the authority mapped here is anything but abstract. It is enacted, concretely, in every pillar of the religion — not one of them stands, or even means what it means, without the imam at its centre. The sacred law is not a self-running machine the imam merely presides over from a height; it is a body whose every limb draws its life from that head. The dominion, the wisdom, and the necessity established here are not, in the end, doctrines to be granted and then set aside. They are the living hinge on which each act of worship actually turns.
