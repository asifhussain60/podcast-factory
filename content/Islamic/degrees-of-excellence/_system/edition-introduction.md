This is a work of argument, not of story. Its original title, *Kitab ithbat al-imama*, names its purpose exactly: *ithbat* means demonstration, the establishing of a claim by proof, and the claim being established is the imamate — the necessity of a divinely appointed guide standing at the center of religion in every age. What you are about to read is a treatise that reasons its case out step by step, moving from first principles toward a single conclusion, rather than a narrative or a dialogue between named speakers.

The book is attributed to Ahmad b. Ibrahim al-Naysaburi, a scholar of the Ismaili tradition as it flourished under the Fatimids in the late tenth and early eleventh centuries — the intellectual world that also produced al-Kirmani and his *Rahat al-Aql*, names you will meet among its technical vocabulary. When the text says "I," that first person is the author himself, building his argument in his own voice; there is no character speaking through him. Read the "I" as the man making the case.

The argument climbs deliberately. It begins by calling the imam the pole and foundation of religion, grounds the need for him in both nature and reason, and then arranges creation into ranks and degrees before naming the imam as the physician of souls and the governing principle beneath the visible order. From there it turns to the institutions of faith — the direction of prayer, the sermon, the alms and the fifth — showing the imam present within each, and finally traces a single line of guides from Adam to Ali and calls the imam God's vicegerent for his own time.

The recurring terms cluster tightly around this one theme. *Qutb* (pole), *hudud* (the graded ranks of the hierarchy), and the institutional words — *qibla*, *khutba*, *zakat*, *khums* — are the load-bearing vocabulary; keeping their sense in view is most of what it takes to follow the book.
