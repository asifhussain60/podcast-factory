In the Name of God, this book undertakes a single task, and its original title names that task exactly. *Kitab ithbat al-imama* means the demonstration of the imamate — *ithbat* being the establishing of a claim by proof, and the imamate the necessity of a divinely appointed guide standing at the center of religion in every age. What follows is a treatise, not a story: it reasons its case out step by step, moving from first principles toward one conclusion, without characters or dialogue.

The work is ascribed to Ahmad b. Ibrahim al-Naysaburi, a scholar of the Ismaili tradition as it flourished under the Fatimids in the late tenth and early eleventh centuries — the same intellectual world that produced al-Kirmani and his *Rahat al-Aql*, a name the reader will meet among these pages. When the text says "I," that voice is the author himself, building his argument; read it as the man making the case.

The argument climbs deliberately. It calls the imam the pole and foundation of religion, grounds the need for him in both nature and reason, and arranges creation into ranks and degrees before naming the imam the physician of souls and the governing principle beneath the visible order. It then turns to the institutions of faith — the direction of prayer, the sermon, the alms, the fifth — showing the imam present within each, and finally traces a single line of guides from Adam to Ali, naming the imam God's vicegerent for his own time.
