# The Virtues of Ali, the Imam Who Mirrors God

## Where the argument picks up

Contrast pair. The scandal at the center, how we judge a ruler against what the scholar calls the summit.

Column A: How we ordinarily judge a good ruler
- Reward: he favors his friends and repays their loyalty.
- Punishment: he checks his enemies and answers treachery.
- Transparency: he makes the grounds of his favor legible, so loyalty is repaid and betrayal deterred.
- Verdict on the opposite conduct: a king who gave to friend and enemy alike, concealed his reasons, and drew near then withdrew for no announced cause would strike us as capricious, even unjust.

Column B: The portrait the scholar calls the summit of human excellence
- Reward: he gives to the faithful and the faithless alike.
- Withholding: he withholds from each in turn, believer and unbeliever both.
- Concealment: he hides the grounds of his generosity.
- Verdict the scholar reaches: this exact conduct proves the man is not a mere monarch but the vicegerent of God on earth.

Shared row: what would be a scandal in a king is, in this one figure, the very mark of the summit. This closing chapter is about why the scholar thought so.

Process flow. Where the preceding stretch left off, and what this final movement adds.

Start: the earlier chapters traced one thread, the world is never for a moment without a designated guide at its peak, from the first man to the present.
  Step: they measured the men the community actually followed against the standard the imamate (الإمامة) demands, knowledge reaching the inner meaning of revelation, striving in God's cause, piety without compromise.
  Step: they found the first successors wanting on every count. That movement cleared the ground.
  Turn: this movement raises the building. If there must always be a summit, and the ordinary claimants fall short, the question becomes who is the summit.
End: the last movement names the figure in whom knowledge, striving, and nearness to God actually culminate, so completely that nothing higher can be imagined.

Annotated structure. Two figures, one office, one ascent.

Whole: the summit the whole book has been pointing toward, named as a continuous station rather than a sealed-off past.
Parts:
- The historical summit: the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, peace be upon him, the Father of Imams, the furthest limit beyond which there is no further limit.
- The living continuation: the imam reigning in the scholar's own age, the qa'im and perfector of the whole line, shown dealing with creation exactly as God deals with it.
Annotation: the theology treats the summit as continuous, so the scholar moves without a break from Ali b. Abi Talib to the reigning imam. By the end the whole edifice rests on this claim, and the treatise closes on its author's own prayer.

## The summit of summits

Annotated structure. The rule written into the structure of everything that exists.

Whole: in every species, genus, root, branch, and law, there is a peak, a summit of nobility no other of its kind can reach. Nature gathers excellence to a point rather than spreading it evenly.
Peaks the treatise has already named:
- Among the elements: one stands supreme.
- Among the metals: gold.
- Among the stones: the ruby that will not burn.
- Among the grains: wheat.
- Among the trees: the date palm.
- Among the heavenly bodies: the outermost sphere and the sun.
Annotation: if the rule holds for metals and stars and grains, it must hold for the imams too. They must have their peak, an utmost degree no one before them attained. There is no exemption for the highest realm of all.

Annotated structure. The doubling in the phrase that closes the door on any higher term.

Whole: the answer given without hedging, the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, named as the summit.
Parts:
- Not merely a summit, but the summit of summits.
- Not a limit, but the limit past which no limit lies.
Annotation: every other peak in creation is still a peak of its own kind, one summit among many. The Father of Imams is the point at which the whole hierarchy of peaks itself comes to rest, the apex not of one domain but of the ascent running through all of them.

Hierarchy. The ascent in three steps, each step this movement runs one link further.

- Step 1: Nature culminates in the human being. The wisdom of the whole world, and of all animals and plants, came to full expression only in the emergence of humanity, and the benefit of every created thing flows back to mankind.
  - Step 2: Humanity culminates in the imams. Through the imams the merits of the human race are perfected and wisdom becomes visible, so the benefit of all human beings flows back to the imams.
    - Step 3: The whole line of imams culminates in a single point. Through the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, all the affairs of the prophets, legatees, and imams reached completion, their order set in order through him, the full wisdom of their laws and inner interpretations made manifest in his person.

Annotated structure. Three bodily images for one claim, unfolding in stages.

Whole: the relation of the Father of Imams to everyone who came before, made vivid.
Parts:
- The head and the body: the Father of Imams is the head, the prophets and legatees and imams before him are the body, and no one disputes that the head governs the body.
- The spirit and the body: he is the spirit, they are the body, and no body is complete or alive without its spirit.
- The heart and the limbs: he is the heart, all who passed away are the limbs, the hands and hair and nails, each drawing life and nourishment only through the beating of the heart.
Annotation: through the Commander of the Faithful the whole line receives its life. He awakens in every soul the longing to draw near, and through him God has promised His creation the fulfillment of its reward.

Process flow. The gathering that is the mechanism behind the claim.

Start: the human being, arriving last in the order of nature, absorbed everything in the realms below and then surpassed it.
  Step: the imams gathered within themselves all the merits scattered through humanity, and then exceeded them.
  Step: the Prophet, may God bless him, drew his power from gathering into himself the merits of all who lived in the cycles before him, their knowledge and more besides.
  Step: so the Father of Imams gathers into himself the merits of every prophet, imam, and sage who preceded him, their knowledge and benefit converging in him, and surpasses them many times over.
End: had he not arisen to fill this station, the perfection of wisdom in all their arisings would never have been achieved. He is the mutimm, the perfector who completes and brings to fullness every one who passed away. For the sake of such a figure the worlds were made at all.

Genealogy chain. The intuition was not al-Naysaburi's alone.

The Ismaili philosophers → Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani (الكرماني) in his Rahat al-Aql → mapped the ranks of religion onto the structure of the cosmos → the imam stands at the human summit as the outermost sphere stands at the summit of the heavens.

Reading: what al-Naysaburi (النيسابوري) calls the summit of summits, that tradition had already learned to see as the place where the cosmic order and the order of guidance meet in a single person.

Contrast pair. The objection about the qa'im, and the reply that is the hinge of the chapter.

Column A: The objection
- The claim: this towering description, perfector of all, the one for whose sake the worlds exist, belongs only to the final qa'im, the awaited one who arises at the very end to seal the cycles.
- The consequence: the Father of Imams, whatever his rank, is not that final figure, so the description overreaches.

Column B: The scholar's reply, quietly radical
- The move: the office of perfector is not reserved for one distant, terminal figure at the end of history.
- The claim: the imam reigning in his own age is the qa'im of his own time and age, the qa'im of all who preceded him, and the perfector of them all.
- The result: every reigning imam stands, for his own age, in exactly that place, completing the whole line before him.

Shared row: the summit of summits is not a point fixed in the past. It is a living station, occupied now.

Comparison matrix. The reigning imam's completion, told in three numerical reckonings, numbers presented abstractly without naming any ruler.

Rows: The sixth | The ninth | The second
Columns: What the number encodes | The grounding the scholar gives

- The sixth: he completes a cycle others opened, the sixth through whom the command of a seventh is brought to perfection | much as the Prophet established everything a later completer would need.
- The ninth: he brings the command of an earlier one, now passed away, to its fullness | grounded in the way the body attains its order through a set of nine, and the human creature is perfected over nine months in the womb.
- The second: the order set down at the beginning of every cycle is completed by the one who follows | the one who follows brings the opening order to completion.

Annotation: the particular numbers matter less than the single idea they all encode, the reigning imam is not a lesser stand-in awaiting a greater figure, he is himself the living perfector of a line stretching back through sacred history.

Contrast pair. The two referents the scholar deliberately lets flow together.

Column A: Ali b. Abi Talib, the historical summit
- Office: the Father of Imams, the head and heart and spirit of the whole body of guidance.
- Reach: the perfector of every prophet and imam who preceded him.
- Position in time: the summit as it stood at the origin.

Column B: The reigning imam of the age
- Office: the one who continues and completes that summit in the present, the qa'im of his own time.
- Reach: gathering the entire inheritance into himself for a new age.
- Position in time: the summit carried forward and brought to completion now.

Shared row: in this theology the two are not rivals and not really separate. The reigning imam is the summit begun in the Father of Imams, carried forward. The summit of summits is a living station, occupied now.

## The imam who mirrors God

Contrast pair. Philosophy's noblest definition, and the one figure who ever met it.

Column A: The philosophers
- Their definition: philosophy is the pursuit of resemblance to the Creator, so far as a human being is able.
- Its meaning: to deal with mankind, and with oneself, in the manner and along the grain of God's own conduct toward human beings.
- Its origin: borrowed, the scholar says, from the people of the truth, his own community.
- The outcome: not one of them ever realized the thing they defined. They drew the target.

Column B: The imam
- The realization: his every dealing with humankind mirrors God's own dealing with them, in the ordering of the world, in the giving of sustenance, in all related matters.
- The outcome: he alone hit the target the philosophers only drew.

Shared row: the scholar takes the noble formula perfectly seriously, then claims the philosophers never achieved it and the imam alone did.

Process flow. The distribution logic, why God gives to believer and unbeliever alike.

Start: God, in His bounty, bestows blessing on people whether pious or impious, believer or unbeliever, and withholds from each in turn, giving to some and denying others, at one time and not another.
  Branch A, rejected: had God given worldly benefit to believers alone, faith would become a business proposition, people believing for the profit, religion a calculation about this world.
  Branch B, rejected: had God withheld all worldly good from believers and lavished it on unbelievers, most people's faith would collapse into despair and revert to unbelief.
  Resolution: so God threads between the two, giving to some believers and withholding from others, giving to some unbelievers and withholding from others, granting and denying at different times.
End: in exactly this way the reigning imam gives to Muslim and unbeliever alike and withholds from both, gives to believer and hypocrite alike and withholds from both, so obedience to him is never bought by advantage nor abandoned for the lack of it.

Citation. Scripture stating the divine pattern as a bare fact.

> God extends provision to whom He wills, and restricts it. (chapter 13, verse 26)

Citation. Why the imam withholds from believers for a season, lest they grow complacent.

> man is rebellious, for he thinks himself self-sufficient.

Comparison matrix. The pairing of every human type, so no category the pattern misses.

Rows: Pious and impious | Worshipper and non-worshipper | Simpleton and learned | Wrongdoer and just, generous and miser, noble and depraved
Columns: What God does | Why God does it | What the imam does in turn

- Pious and impious: gives to both | so the pious will not do good merely for a worldly return, nor abstain from wrong merely to protect a worldly interest | the imam gives to the righteous and the immoral alike.
- Worshipper and non-worshipper: gives to both | lest worship itself become a means of gain | the imam looks on his people not fixing on their faults but with a merciful eye.
- Simpleton and learned: gives to both and withholds from both | had He given only to the learned, people would pursue knowledge purely as a road to wealth, and the ignorant would despair | the imam gives to the ignorant and the learned alike.
- Wrongdoer and just, generous and miser, noble and depraved: distributes to all | out of His wisdom and mercy, so each receives a fair share | the imam, following God's own actions and mercy, treats every one the same.

Annotation: the whole account rests on a mercy that runs ahead of judgment, the mercy God announced of Himself.

Citation. The priority of mercy over reckoning that the imam imitates.

> My mercy has overtaken My wrath. (the Prophet)

Contrast pair. The concealment, as deliberate as the giving.

Column A: How God conceals
- What is hidden: the workings of the world and the means by which creatures come to benefit from it.
- The visible effect: one grows wealthy while another has nothing to eat, one prospers in trade while another goes bankrupt, one flourishes in farming while another loses even the seed, one is made rich by hunting while another gets nothing.
- The reason: if a single sure path to fortune were universally known, everyone would pour down that road, every other path would fall idle, and the ordered variety of the world would be thrown into confusion.

Column B: How the imam conceals
- What is hidden: the means of gaining benefit through him.
- The visible effect: one man seeks his favor by a particular route and receives it, while another who seeks it by that very same route is denied.
- The reason: if people discovered a definite method for obtaining his bounty, all other means would become futile, everyone lacking that one key would despair, and no merit would be left in any of it.

Shared row: the concealment protects the world from becoming a formula. The hiddenness keeps hope open for everyone and certainty available to no one.

Process flow. Nearness and distance, the soul suspended between fear and hope.

Start: the imam governs his nearness and his distance by the same principle that governs his giving.
  Branch A, rejected: were he to push the hypocrites away and draw only the sincere close, people would perform belief to win his nearness, faith becoming a courtship rather than a conviction.
  Branch B, rejected: were he to hold himself at a distance from all believers at all times, they would withdraw, anxiety creeping in, imagining they had displeased him.
  Resolution: so he deals with people as the times and conditions require, arousing longing and alarm, drawing near and pushing away, granting and withholding, punishing and forgiving, all in emulation of God's own manner.
End: people remain perpetually suspended between fear and hope, never so secure that they grow bold, never so despairing that they collapse. That suspension is the precise spiritual climate in which a soul stays awake.

Contrast pair. The one feature that sets the imam's giving apart from every other monarch's.

Column A: The gifts of ordinary kings
- What they are: always payment, for a service rendered, a relationship maintained, an obligation discharged, a debt repaid, a fear of calamity, a hope of advantage, a wish to be praised.
- Their nature: every such gift is a transaction, calculated and assessed.
- The scholar's verdict: a gift given for any of those reasons cannot honestly be called generosity. To pay a debt late is miserliness, to advance money for a return is investment, not bounty.

Column B: The gifts of God and the imam
- What they are: God is under no obligation to anyone and seeks no reward from any creature, and the imam gives to no one for any of those reasons and seeks no recompense.
- Their nature: bestowed freely, spontaneously, out of pure generosity, following God's own conduct.
- The result: the one giving in the human world that asks for nothing back.

Shared row: as God said of the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, "we desire neither reward nor thanks from you."

Citation. The three kinds of devotion, the Father of Imams sorting all worship.

> A people worship God out of desire for reward, that is the worship of merchants; and a people worship Him out of fear, that is the worship of slaves; and a people worship Him out of gratitude, that is the worship of the free. (the Father of Imams)

Annotation. The imam's giving is the giving of the free, not the merchant's angling for a return, nor the slave's extracted by fear, but the open hand of one who owes nothing and asks nothing.

## The two errors and the shadow of God

Annotated structure. The two errors that surround the figure, with the truth passing between.

Whole: a figure easy to misread, flanked by one error on each side.
Parts:
- The error of short sight: those who judge his actions by the standard of ordinary rulers, comparing what he does against what they have seen, and finding his conduct at odds with their expectations, fall into perplexity and speculation. If they measured his actions against God's dealings rather than other men's, everything would grow clear, but from Adam to the present no one has acted this way, so they have no precedent to reach for.
- The error of exaggeration: the ghulat, the extremists, dazzled, claim divinity for him and inflate the office past its truth.
- The truth between: he is the Universal Vicegerent of God over His servants, a shadow that perfectly imitates the thing that casts it, near enough to reproduce God's conduct exactly, yet, being a shadow, never for a moment God Himself.
Annotation: he is not a common ruler to be measured by common rulers, and he is not God. That single image of the shadow holds the whole doctrine.

## The vicegerent and the closing prayer

Contrast pair. The two halves of the mirror completed, concealing in one world, disclosing in the other.

Column A: In worldly matters, the imam conceals
- The model: God concealed the reasons for creating the world, and hid the means of attaining fortune.
- The action: the imam hides the means of gaining fortune through him and gives it to deserving and undeserving alike.
- The purpose: so no one can reduce his bounty to a formula.

Column B: In religious matters, the imam discloses
- The model: God openly explained the means of attaining religion and His good pleasure, giving knowledge and wisdom to those who deserved them, setting signposts of guidance and raising the summons of the proof.
- The action: the imam openly teaches the means of attaining religion, knowledge, and wisdom, bestowing them generously on the deserving, neither withholding nor hoarding, and withholding from the undeserving.
- The purpose: to draw the line that must be drawn, distinguishing the people of truth, the ahl al-haqq, from the people of falsehood, the ahl al-batil.

Shared row: in all of it he follows the ways of God, governing world and religion together, building up this present life and the life to come. The vicegerent does over those entrusted to him exactly what the One who appointed him would do.

Genealogy chain. The word khalifa, from the first who bore it to the one who fulfills it whole.

Adam (the first to take up the vicegerency, called khalifa for it) → those after him (called by the same name, each discharging some part of its duty) → David (charged to govern God's servants as God would) → the Father of Imams and the reigning imam who continues him (performing the vicegerency whole, both worldly and religious, in full reality, not in part).

Citation. Scripture using the word in its governing sense.

> O David, We have made you a vicegerent on the earth, so judge between the people in truth. (chapter 38, verse 26)

Citation. The Prophet naming this vicegerent and no other.

> the ruler is the shadow of God on earth. (the Prophet)

Annotation. All the earlier vicegerents fulfilled a small portion of God's manner of dealing; this one fulfills the whole of it. That is the difference between discharging part of a duty and completing it, between wearing the name and being the reality the name was made for.

Annotated structure. The treatise turning from proving to asking, the author's valedictory supplication.

Whole: having proved by reason and revelation that the world must have its summit, that the summit is the imam, and that the imam's dealings mirror God's, the scholar turns from proving to praying.
The petitions, in order:
- For gratitude for the blessings God has given, expressed not in words only but in words and deeds and intention together, the whole person turned toward thankfulness.
- For success in obedience to the imam of the age, and the means to strive on his behalf with hand and tongue and heart, the body's action, the mouth's speech, the heart's devotion, all offered up.
- That God open the whole world to that imam and, through him, purify the earth of every unbelief and every hypocrisy, by his bounty and grace.
Annotation: the prayer gathers the book's argument into a posture of the soul. Having shown the imam stands at the summit, the scholar asks only to be allowed to serve that summit truly.

Citation. The final words of the treatise, the argument dissolving into worship.

> Praise be to God, the praise of the thankful, the mindful, and the pious, and blessings upon His Messenger, our master Muhammad, and all his progeny.

Annotation. A treatise that opened by reasoning about elements and stars closes by blessing the Prophet's family. The scholar could have closed with a final crushing syllogism. He closed with thanks, because for him the argument was never the point in itself, it was the road to a relationship. The reasoning was the ladder. The prayer is what you say when you have climbed it and arrived.

## What this chapter lands

Genealogy chain. The whole ladder, ending where it always pointed.

Nature (gathers its excellence to a summit) → the human being (the summit of nature, all lower realms absorbed and surpassed) → the imams (the summit of humanity, its merits perfected) → the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib (the summit of summits, the head and heart and spirit of the whole body of guidance, perfector of every prophet and imam before him) → the reigning imam of the age (the qa'im of his own time, the vicegerent who deals with creation as God deals with it).

Reading: the book set out to prove the world must have a peak, and it closes by naming who stands there. There is no higher rung to climb.

Annotated structure. How tightly the whole treatise fits, movement by movement.

Whole: every earlier movement was preparing the ground for this recognition.
Parts:
- The early chapters: established the pole of religion, guidance as a necessity woven into reality, not a convenience the mind can do without.
- The theory of degrees: climbed rung by rung from the four natures through the stars to humankind, teaching the eye to see that every ordered domain gathers its excellence to a summit.
- The middle stretch: turned to worship and the pillars that hold a religious life upright.
- The later chapters: read history as one unbroken succession of guides, and measured the ordinary claimants against the standard the office demands.
Annotation: all of it prepared the recognition that the summit of the human world is the imam, whose whole conduct is a shadow cast by God's own dealing with creation.

Annotated structure. The boldest thing the scholar says, the idea to carry away.

Whole: the highest praise the scholar can offer a human being.
Parts:
- Not that he is wise, or just, or generous in the ordinary way.
- But that he treats people the way God treats them, and that this, far from being a scandal, is the very mark of the vicegerent.
- He gives without being paid, conceals without being cruel, draws near and withdraws to keep the soul awake between fear and hope, and asks for nothing in return, because he imitates the One whose conduct owes nothing to anyone.
Annotation: to judge him by the measure of ordinary rulers is to miss him entirely, to inflate him into a god is to miss him just as badly. He is the shadow that perfectly reproduces what casts it. The engine that reached for a lump of gold and a red ruby has run its full course, and the scholar, having climbed the ladder, lays down his proof and lifts up a prayer, which is exactly where a book like this was always meant to arrive.
