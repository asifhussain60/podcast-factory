# The Imam and the Authority over Sacred Law

## Where the argument picks up

Annotated structure. The opening image the whole chapter builds on, dissected into its claim.

Whole: a man standing over an animal, disposing of its life by his own judgment.
Parts:
- The acts: he rides it, milks it, shears it, works it in the field, slaughters it for a feast, or lets it graze.
- The manner: by his own judgment, against the animal's own inclination.
- The verdict: no one calls him a tyrant for it.
Annotation: al-Naysaburi lifts this ordinary picture into a single sentence, humanity stands to the imam exactly as the animals stand to humanity. It is a claim about who owns the world, who may take from it, and by whose leave anyone holds anything at all.

Process flow. The ladder the previous section climbed, and the question it left hanging.

Start: the four bare elements.
  Step: up through the metals and the stars.
  Step: up through gold, the unburnable ruby, the balanced grain.
  Step: up to the human being.
  Step: one step further, up to the imam.
End: two conclusions locked together, humanity is the summit of the lower creation, and the imam is the summit of humanity.
Open question left hanging: granted the imam is the summit, what does that summit actually authorize? What may he do, what may he demand, and what does his standing at the top mean for everyone below?

Annotated structure. The three moves al-Naysaburi answers with, previewed as the chapter's spine.

Whole: the whole of what the imam's summit authorizes.
Parts:
- The doctrine of dominion: the world was made for the imams, its benefit belongs to them, and to take from it without their leave is theft.
- The doctrine of wisdom: because the imam is wise without qualification, the believer owes him a submission that does not stop to argue.
- The doctrine of necessity: reason alone, with no scripture required, shows the world must have one supreme leader, and that leader must be the best of all who live.
Annotation: dominion, wisdom, necessity, the ownership, the trust it demands, and the proof that the office cannot be empty or filled by a lesser man.

## Everything returns to the imam

Process flow. The single descending sentence, why anything was made at all.

Start: a question that sounds almost accusatory, why was any of it made?
  Step: the stars, seas, metals, grain, and herds each exist for the sake of what stands above them.
  Step: the whole of natural creation was made for the sake of humanity, because only a being who can know and benefit gives the rest any point.
  Step: humanity was not made for its own sake either, human beings were created for the imams, to receive religion from them, to be perfected through them, to make manifest their order and foundation.
End: the worlds exist for humanity, and humanity exists for the imams. The point of everything is finally the imam, because only in him does the whole ascent complete itself.

Process flow. The startling consequence al-Naysaburi refuses to soften.

Start: all the worlds were made for the imams.
  Step: therefore all the benefit of the worlds belongs to the imams.
  Step: the rest of creation stands to them as children stand to a parent, dependent, provided for, owing everything and owed nothing.
  Step: whatever the imams give is favour, generosity, mercy, a gift and not a debt repaid; whatever they withhold, they owe no account for.
End, the hardest sentence in the section: whoever takes anything of this world without the permission and approval of the imam has taken what is haram, forbidden, and has committed usurpation. Not a discourtesy, a seizure from an owner who never granted it.

Annotated structure. The verse of the spoils, and the one word doing the heavy work.

Whole: the scriptural grounding of the dominion.
Citation:

> That which God bestows on His Messenger from the people of the villages, it is for God and His Messenger. (chapter 59, verse 7)

Part, the crucial word: fay', the term the verse uses for that bestowal.
Annotation: its root meaning is not gift but return. It is the same word Arabic uses for a shadow, called fay' because in the evening it comes back to the place from which it first stretched out.

Contrast pair. Why the etymology of return closes off every rival claim.

Column A: If the world were originally no one's
- Starting point: ownerless ground open to any claimant.
- The imam's claim: one claim among many, to be weighed against everyone else's.
- Result: rival flags can be planted, the matter stays contestable.

Column B: If the world was the imams' from the start
- Starting point: every good is on loan from a source it is always tending back toward.
- The imam's claim: not a handover to a stranger but a shadow coming home at evening to the object that cast it.
- Result: no rival claim can even get started, because there is no ownerless ground to plant a flag on.

Shared row: the world does not become the imams' property when they receive it, it was always theirs, and receiving it only makes the ownership visible. The doctrine of usurpation follows straight from the doctrine of return, you cannot usurp what is unowned, and nothing here is unowned.

Annotated structure. The command to obey, binding three objects into one imperative.

Whole: the obligation to defer, written into the revealed command itself.
Citation:

> O you who believe, obey God, and obey the Messenger, and those in authority among you. (chapter 4, verse 59)

Parts, the three objects of obedience:
- God.
- The Messenger.
- Uli'l-amr, those charged with the command.
Annotation: for al-Naysaburi the third is not whoever happens to hold power, it is the imam, the summit at the top of the ladder. The world's benefit returning to its rightful owner and the believer's obedience flowing toward that same owner are two faces of one order.

Comparison matrix. The law of nature, one added spirit as the whole difference between ruling and being ruled.

Rows: Earth to plant | Plant to animal | Animal to human
Columns: The one thing added | The name of that spirit | What the addition makes possible

- Earth to plant: a single spirit of growth | ruh al-nama' | the soil now feeds the root, the earth becomes subservient to the plant
- Plant to animal: growth plus the power to feel and move | ruh al-hiss | the animal walks over the ground the plant is rooted in and eats the plant at will
- Animal to human: sensation plus the power of speech and thought | ruh al-nutq | man builds, sows, plants, burns wood, rides, slaughters, milks and shears, using the whole lower world as he sees fit

Annotation: al-Naysaburi means this as a law, not a series of examples. At every level, one added spirit is the whole difference between ruling and being ruled.

Hierarchy tree. The spirits gathered in the imam, lifting him above the human rung.

Level 1: The ordinary human being, endowed with articulate reason.
  Level 2: The imam, endowed at his charge with further spirits, powers, and a purity no ordinary human possesses.
    Level 3: The quintessence of the whole world, the pinnacle of human purity joined to the choicest animal powers, the balance of the elements, the finest influence of the spheres and stars.
    Level 3: The full sanctified spirit, ruh al-quds, of which an ordinary person holds only a portion, and no more of that portion than the imam bestows by grace.
    Level 3: The creative spirit, ruh al-ibda', the perfect seventh spirit, the spirit of Oneness, ruh al-wahda, prepared for humanity in the world of reward.
Annotation: the human climbed above the animal by one added spirit and won dominion over it. If one spirit was enough to make man the master of the beast, what does a ladder of spirits, up to the seventh and highest, make the imam?

Contrast pair. The dominion read from its two sides, mastery and care.

Column A: The mastery
- Posture: the man disposes of his animals as he sees fit, even against their will.
- Ground: the added spirit that lifts him above them.

Column B: The care
- Posture: by the very norms of that rule he guards their watering places and grazing grounds, shields them from harm, tends their well-being even against their own foolish appetites.
- Ground: the same rule, obliging the one who owns the flock to keep it alive.

Shared row: mastery and care are the same relationship read from two sides. So it is obligatory on the imam, by the norm of kingship, the demands of nature, sound policy, and law, to protect the welfare of mankind exactly as man protects the welfare of the animals he governs. The dominion comes with a charge attached.

Contrast pair. How people sort themselves by how they answer the charge.

Column A: Whoever submits inwardly and outwardly
- Submission: to the imam's rule over both spirit and body.
- Result: saturated with knowledge and wisdom, lifted above his fellows.
- Departure: leaves this world in an upright form, ready to receive his reward.

Column B: Whoever submits only outwardly
- Submission: the outward act alone, the inward assent held back.
- Result: falls into the torment of doubt and discord.
- Departure: arrives crooked, unprepared, at the place of recompense.

Shared row: the difference is not in the outward act, which may look identical in both men. It is in whether the submission reached the inside.

Annotated structure. The older charge al-Naysaburi's imam stands under, mercy as the first duty of office.

Whole: the duty of care as the tradition has always described just rule.
Citation:

> Let your heart be filled with mercy for the subjects, and love for them, and kindness toward them; do not be a ravening beast over them, seizing what they have. (the Father of Imams, peace be upon him)

Annotation: the Father of Imams, instructing a governor sent to Egypt, made mercy toward the governed the first charge of the office. The one set above the flock is precisely the one bound to tend it, and to turn predator over it is to betray the office rather than exercise it. The higher the dominion, the heavier the duty of care bound to it.

Process flow. The conclusion drawn in a single stroke, closing the circle to where the argument opened.

Start: God gathered in the imams all these faculties, merits, and spirits, over and above what humanity already has.
  Step: therefore He subjected all of humanity to them, just as He subjected animals to man by one added spirit, plants to animals by one, earth to plants by one.
  Step: the dominion is not a title conferred from outside, it is the natural weight of a being who carries that many more spirits than those beneath him.
  Step, the explicit measure: the number of spirits residing in the imams determines the extent of their control and dominion over humanity. Rank is not a symbol of authority, rank is authority, the two are the same quantity read twice.
End: it is no more for a human to remove himself from the imam's domain than for an animal to remove itself from the rule of man. The person who sets himself outside the imam's authority has torn himself out of the order the added spirits established, and there is no rung below the human rung for him to stand on except the beast's.

## The absolute wisdom of the imam

Contrast pair. The word the second doctrine turns on, and what earns it.

Column A: Taslim as it first sounds
- Word: unquestioning submission.
- First impression: a demand that makes a modern reader flinch.
- Danger: heard as the surrender of the mind.

Column B: Taslim as al-Naysaburi builds it
- Word: the same, submission that does not stop to argue.
- Earned by: an argument begun not with a command but with a farmer.
- End: what the mind concludes once it has granted that the Wise One is wise.

Shared row: al-Naysaburi knows the word needs earning, so he does not begin by commanding it. He begins with an image.

Process flow. The farmer of insight, and the imams read onto him.

Start: a man of real insight walks over the land.
  Step: he finds good, fertile soil and knows at once it will take seed and give growth.
  Step: he finds water where others never look, in distant valleys or hidden underground, and rather than shrug he digs canals, sinks wells, and directs the buried water to the waiting ground.
  Step, the reason: a man of insight sees no wisdom in leaving good land idle and good water sealed away.
  Transpose to the imams:
    They look at people and see fertile ground, souls ready or nearly ready to receive the seed of wisdom, even when still far from taking it in.
    They dig their canals, labouring through preparatory religious exercises whose purpose the people cannot yet perceive.
    Where people cannot receive wisdom plainly, they embed it in dense symbols, in words and actions, so people take the symbol now and are raised to the meaning later.
End: the obscurity is not withholding, it is irrigation, wisdom routed through a channel the ground can actually absorb.

Annotated structure. What the image obliges the believer to hold.

Whole: the belief the farmer-image grounds.
Parts:
- To believe in the supreme leadership of the imam of the time, the head of the world, the vicegerent of God over His creation, the khalifa through whom God's governance reaches what He made.
- Never to object to any of his commands, prohibitions, actions, or teachings.
- To hold, in general and in advance, that all of it embodies wisdom and is for the welfare of creation, even when the particular point stays hidden a long while.
Annotation: al-Naysaburi grants the difficulty openly, the true meaning of some command may be genuinely hard to grasp, and the believer may feel real resistance. But the resistance is a fact about the believer's sight, not about the command's wisdom.

Contrast pair. The parallel from inside the religion the believer already accepts.

Column A: The sacred law the believer already obeys
- Situation: the faithful frequently do not understand, at first, what the Lawgiver was after in a ruling.
- Response: they may bristle at it.
- Later: those to whom God grants recognition come to see the wisdom folded inside it the whole time.

Column B: The living guide of the law
- Situation: the imam's command whose point is hidden.
- Response: the believer feels the same resistance.
- Asked of him: only that he extend to the guide the same patience he already extends to the law.

Shared row: if that is how a believer already relates to the law he obeys, al-Naysaburi asks only that the same patience reach the living guide of the law.

Process flow. The pattern widened past the law to the world at large.

Start: countless things in creation people take to be simply harmful, dangers, pests, disasters.
  Step: they see no wisdom and no benefit in them, and conclude no good was ever there.
  Step: later the wisdom of the thing becomes visible.
  Step: they grow calm, the objection quietly falls away, and once seen it cannot honestly be denied.
End: the wisdom is not absent, it is often on a delay, arriving after the complaint. The believer who suspends his objection is asked only to do, in the one case that matters most, what life has already taught him a hundred times in smaller matters.

Process flow. The story of a prophet failing the very test, Moses and al-Khidr.

Start: Moses sets out to learn from the servant of God the tradition calls al-Khidr.
  Warning at the outset:

> And how can you have patience for that which you do not encompass in knowledge? (chapter 18, verse 68)

  The three acts and Moses objecting at each:
    The guide scuttles a sound boat, to save it from a seizing tyrant.
    The guide kills a young man, taken before he could ruin his believing parents.
    The guide rebuilds a wall in a town that refused them hospitality, to guard an orphan's buried treasure until he came of age.
End: the wisdom had been there the whole time; what Moses lacked was not the wisdom but the patience to wait for it. Taslim shown from the inside, an act that looks like sheer damage from below may be rescue seen from above, and the whole distance is only the length of one's own sight.

Process flow. From a plea for patience into something closer to a proof, turning on the name al-Hakim, the Wise One.

Start: whoever truly recognizes the Wise One, whoever grants that God is wise, has already granted a great deal more.
  Step: to call someone wise without qualification is to say everything he does is full of wisdom, that he would never do a thing empty of it, that he neither commands nor forbids nor says anything except out of wisdom.
  Step: a futile act is one whose doing and not-doing come to exactly the same thing, an act you could just as well have skipped with nothing lost.
  Step: such an act is impossible for the Wise One, because it would be wisdom sitting idle, and a wisdom that idles is not the wisdom the name asserts.
  The hinge, from God to the imam: if it is not permissible for the Wise One to perform even one act whose presence and absence are equivalent, how could the imam perform an act when the wisdom lay elsewhere, or in the very opposite of what he did?
End: he cannot. The imam is absolutely wise; his wisdom is not his own achievement but a bestowal, and whatever he bestows on a servant makes that servant wise in turn. To act at random, without wisdom or goodness, is to act corruptly and in vain, the very thing the office forbids.

Process flow. Why the last step of the doctrine is so severe.

Start: to believe of the imam of the time that he has acted without wisdom.
  Step: this is not a small error about one decision, it is to say the individual has departed from wisdom.
  Step: whoever has departed from wisdom has by that fact departed from the domain of his imamate, because the imamate simply is the seat of wisdom in the world, and a wisdomless imam is a contradiction in terms.
  Step: to depart from the domain of the imamate is to depart from the faith itself, since the faith is built on that seat.
End: the objection that looked like ordinary criticism, this command makes no sense, turns out, followed to its root, to be a denial of the whole structure the believer claims to stand inside.

Annotated structure. The logic beneath the earlier pictures of the hard teacher and the physician of souls.

Whole: the severity of the imam re-read as difficult mercy.
Parts:
- The earlier images: the hard teacher, the physician of souls, the guide whose severity cuts only to cure.
- The logic supplied here: a wise agent cannot act pointlessly, so there is no such thing as an imam's act that is really empty, however empty it looks from below.
Annotation: it is not merely that the imam means well when he is harsh; it is that a wise agent cannot act in vain. The submission called taslim is not a surrender of the mind, it is what the mind concludes once it has actually granted that the Wise One is wise.

## The necessity of the best

Annotated structure. The third doctrine's claim, leaning on nothing but reason.

Whole: an argument meant to persuade even someone who accepts no revelation at all.
Two joined halves:
- That the world must have a single supreme leader.
- That this leader must be the most excellent of everyone alive.
Method: the first half proved with three pictures, the second with a point about the meaning of words.

Process flow. The first picture, the shepherd, aimed at the person who thinks a capable society could look after itself.

Start: a flock of livestock grazing free on open ground.
  Step: the animals have real worth, nothing deficient about them as animals.
  Step: left to themselves they perish, they cannot choose pasture wisely, find their own water, or fend off the thief and the beast of prey.
  Step: what they lack is not strength or value but a shepherd, someone to select grazing, guard the watering places, and stand between them and what would kill them.
  Step: people are exactly like this, without a shepherd for their worldly and religious life they come apart, devoured, scattered.
  The edge: everyone else who claims the shepherd's role over the flock is not a rival shepherd but a wolf, a predator dressed as a protector, thinning the flock it pretends to guard.
End: people everywhere are called subjects, ra'iya, which means precisely a flock under a shepherd's care. The very language a society uses for itself confesses it expects to be shepherded. The only question is who the shepherd is, and who the wolves are.

Annotated structure. The saying the word ra'iya carries with it, from both Sahih collections.

Whole: shepherding as the shape of every responsibility there is.
Citation:

> Each of you is a shepherd, and each of you is responsible for his flock. (the Prophet)

Annotation: the saying runs the image all the way down, the ruler over his people, the man over his household, the woman over her home. If even the head of a single household stands as a shepherd answerable for those in his care, then the head of the whole community stands as the shepherd of shepherds, the one on whom every lesser flock finally depends.

Process flow. The second picture, the body, aimed at the person who imagines leadership is a slow-acting good the world could survive without for a while.

Start: a body without a head is not an injured body, it is a decaying corpse, of no use to anyone.
  Step: apply that to the world, a world with no head, no leader, no master, no sa'is to steer its affairs.
  Step: it would not limp along in disorder, it would rot, people turning into beasts devouring one another.
End: the world would not endure even a single day. Not a decline over generations, a day. The claim is deliberately extreme to rule out the middle position, that a society could stumble on without leadership at a pinch. Remove the head and what remains is not a wounded order but a carcass.

Process flow. The third picture, the ranks of nature, aimed at the person who suspects leadership is a merely human convention.

Start: look at nature and you find headship built into it everywhere, long before any human agreed to anything.
  Step: the stallion guards and disciplines the mares.
  Step: the rooster watches over the hens.
  Step: every species defends its females and young as far as its strength allows and guides the weak among its own kind.
  Step: the crane obeys its leader in flight, the bee obeys its queen.
  Step: God arranged the whole of creation on this pattern of gradation and rank, so that within every species and genus there is a peak, a summit, a chief in nobility and worth.
End, the inference: by that same reasoning, all of mankind must likewise have a peak and summit in nobility and worth, to serve as leader and guide. Headship is a feature of nature and creation, of instinct and intuition, so deep that even those who deny revelation cannot deny, by reason or plain observation, that the world requires leadership.

Comparison matrix. Why al-Naysaburi stacks three pictures, each aimed at a different way of shrugging the claim off.

Rows: The shepherd | The body | The ranks of nature
Columns: The doubter it answers | The reply it gives

- The shepherd: the person who thinks a capable society could look after itself | competence is not the issue, the flock is not defective, it is simply unshepherded, and unshepherded it dies
- The body: the person who imagines leadership is a slow-acting good the world could survive without for a while | remove the head and what you have is not a struggling body but a corpse within a day
- The ranks of nature: the person who suspects the whole thing is a human invention we could uninvent | the stallion, the rooster, the crane, and the bee never signed a contract, and headship runs through them all

Annotation: taken singly, each picture could be dodged. Taken together they close every exit at once. By the end the claim is no longer a doctrine one might accept or decline, it has the feel of a thing one cannot honestly go on denying, whatever one's religion or lack of it.

Contrast pair. The tradition's answer to pious anarchy, the slogan and its false conclusion.

Column A: The slogan and its true half
- Claim raised by a faction: judgment belongs to God alone.
- Truth in it: yes, there is no judgment but God's.

Column B: The false conclusion drawn from it
- Claim: therefore no human government is needed at all.
- Answer of the Father of Imams: the words are true but the conclusion false.

Shared row: the citation that settles it:

> These people say there is no rule but God's, and yes, there is no judgment but God's; but they mean by it that there is no government, and the people cannot do without a leader, righteous or wicked. (the Father of Imams)

Reading: leadership is not an optional good a community might forgo in favour of pious anarchy. Even the imperfect form of it is preferable to its absence, because its absence is not disorder but dissolution. Al-Naysaburi presses one step further, the leader a people cannot do without ought not merely to be present but the best of them.

Contrast pair. The second half settled almost as a matter of definition, the title against its contrary.

Column A: The title
- Al-imam, the supreme leader.
- Al-fadil, the excellent.
- Al-sabiq, the one who precedes.

Column B: The contrary the title cannot name
- Al-ma'mum, the one who is led.
- Al-mafdul, the one surpassed by another.
- Al-masbuq, the one who is preceded.

Shared row: to call the led man the leader, or the surpassed man the excellent, is not a moral mistake, it is a misuse of language, a word applied to its own contrary.
Reading: the supremacy is not an extra honour attached after the fact, it is contained in the very idea of the leader, the way being surpassed is contained in the idea of the surpassed.
Widest agreement: people of every community and religion, philosophers, deniers of God, those who strip Him of every attribute, all concede the leader of the world ought to be the best, most perfect, most knowledgeable. On the principle, everyone agrees, and no one fits the description except the imams.

Annotated structure. Scripture turning the point into a question that answers itself.

Whole: the grammatical recognition pressed as a rebuke.
Citation:

> Is He who guides to the truth more worthy to be followed, or he who cannot guide unless he himself is guided? So what is wrong with you; how do you judge? (chapter 10, verse 35)

Annotation: the verse does not argue the case so much as expose it as already settled in the meaning of the words. The one who must be led cannot coherently be called the leader. To follow the guided in place of the guide is not a debatable preference, it is a failure of judgment about the plain sense of what a guide is.

Process flow. Where the pretenders fall out of the running, by their own qualifications.

Start: the philosophers' three governances, and the strict chain that binds them.
  Step: no one who cannot rule his own soul has any business ruling a family.
  Step: no one who cannot rule a family has any claim on ruling a people.
  Step: the men who have seized the imams' place and titles cannot govern themselves, their families, or the people, plainly given over to amusement, depravity, and corruption.
End: the disqualification does not even reach the hard question of who is best, it stops at the first gate, a man who cannot command his own appetites has failed the entrance requirement for every rule above him. The field is not crowded with strong candidates; by the standard everyone already accepts, it is empty of everyone but the imam.

## What this chapter establishes

Comparison matrix. The three doctrines, how each grounds the imam's authority and from what direction.

Rows: Dominion | Wisdom | Necessity
Columns: What it establishes | Its ground | The direction it argues from

- Dominion: the world was made for the imams, its benefit returns to them like a shadow to the thing that cast it, to take without leave is usurpation | the added spirits that lift the imam above the human rung give him the same natural mastery over people that the single added spirit of reason gives a human over the beasts | ownership
- Wisdom: this mastery is not to be feared but trusted, because the imam is absolutely wise and a wise agent cannot act in vain, so the believer's submission is not the surrender of judgment but its conclusion | that the Wise One is wise, once granted, carries the rest | trust
- Necessity: none of this rests on taking anyone's word for it, reason alone forces the admission that the world must have one supreme head, and that head must be the best of all | the shepherdless flock, the headless body, the ordered ranks of nature, and the plain meaning of the word leader | inevitability

Annotation: ownership, trust, and inevitability, the imam's authority established from three directions that do not need each other to stand.

Contrast pair. What has been shown against what has not.

Column A: What the chapter proved
- That the office must exist.
- That it must be filled by the best.
- That everything belongs to the one who fills it.
- The authority in the abstract, its ground, its extent, its logic.

Column B: What the chapter has not yet done
- Show that authority actually doing anything.
- Touch the things a believer actually does, the prayer, the fast, the alms, the pilgrimage.
- Reach the daily and yearly fabric of worship and law.
- Answer whether the imam's supremacy is not floating free of ordinary religious life.

Shared row: everything here has been about authority in the abstract. The objection a careful listener should now be feeling is exactly this, that it is all very grand but seems to hover above the things worship is actually made of.

Process flow. The answer that turns the objection back, and the door the chapter opens onto.

Start: the objection, is this authority not floating free of ordinary religious life?
  Step: the answer al-Naysaburi presses hardest, the authority mapped here is anything but abstract.
  Step: it is enacted, concretely, in every pillar of the religion, not one of them stands or even means what it means without the imam at its centre.
  Step: the sacred law is not a self-running machine the imam merely presides over from a height, it is a body whose every limb draws its life from that head.
End: the dominion, the wisdom, and the necessity established here are not doctrines to be granted and set aside, they are the living hinge on which each act of worship actually turns.
