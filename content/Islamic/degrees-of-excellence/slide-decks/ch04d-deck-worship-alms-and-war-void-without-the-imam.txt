# Worship, Alms, and War: Void Without the Imam

## Where the argument picks up

Annotated structure. The startling claim the whole chapter turns on.

Whole: the ordinary acts of a believer's religion, re-read as proofs.
Parts:
- Every prayer performed.
- Every coin given in charity.
- Every fast kept.
- The direction you face, the hands that may take your alms, the banner under which a war may be fought.
Annotation: al-Naysaburi's claim is that each of these is not a symbol of the imamate, not a reminder of it, but a proof of it, folded into the very shape of the act. Take the central figure away and the obligations do not merely lose a leader, they fall apart and become void.

Contrast pair. What the preceding stretch of the treatise reached, against what this chapter does.

Column A: The argument at its altitude
- Claim: the imam is not one leader among others but the single best of all.
- Ground: the same logic that gives every ordered domain its summit, from a lump of gold to an unburnable ruby up to a supreme guide.
- Reach: a total authority, the right to lead in the whole of the law and its inner meaning.
- Risk: an argument that lives only at that height can feel remote, beautiful reasoning that never touches the ground.

Column B: The argument brought down to earth
- Claim: that same authority is already built, brick by brick, into the ordinary machinery of Islam.
- Ground: the five pillars themselves, the courtroom, and the battlefield.
- Reach: the imam standing silently inside each duty the religion asks.
- Result: the imam is not an idea hovering above the religion, he is the hinge on which its every duty turns.

Process flow. The three moves the chapter makes in sequence.

Start: first principles, why human beings need teachers at all.
  Step: the plain need drives a chain of reasoning that ends at the prophet and the imam.
  Step: the chain blossoms into a geometry in which the circle, the number one, and the dot all symbolize the same figure.
  Turn to worship: the direction faced in prayer, the congregation, the sermon, the pilgrimage, the imam standing silently inside each.
  Turn to the hard institutions: alms, war, and punishment.
End: at the institutions al-Naysaburi springs his sharpest trap, taking the principles his opponents themselves hold and showing that by those very principles their own leaders are disqualified, and the imamate must belong to someone else.

## The chain and the circle

Contrast pair. Why knowledge cannot come from nature, the animal against the human.

Column A: What comes from nature
- Evidence: the braying of a donkey is identical in India, in Sind, among the Byzantines, among the Arabs.
- Evidence: the bark of a dog is the same everywhere on earth.
- Conclusion: what is nature's is uniform, taught to no one, the same in every land.

Column B: What comes from instruction
- Evidence: human speech splinters into a thousand tongues, a child speaks the language of the people who raised it.
- Evidence: knowledge differs from person to person, no one knows the same things equally.
- Conclusion: speech and knowledge are put there by teaching, not welled up from nature.

Shared row: human beings are born knowing nothing, and neither knowledge nor speech arises in them from nature. The plain fact that each of us must be taught is not a trivial observation, it is the first stone in a proof.

Annotated structure. Scripture stating the starting point without softening.

Whole: the premise that a person arrives with nothing and is handed the instruments of learning afterward.
Citation:

> And God brought you forth from the wombs of your mothers knowing nothing, and He appointed for you hearing and sight and hearts, that you might give thanks. (chapter 16, verse 78)

Annotation: the hearing, the sight, the heart, the very faculties by which anything is learned, are named as gifts issued to a creature who began with none of them.

Genealogy chain. The regress of teachers, run backward until it must stop.

Learner → teacher → that teacher's teacher → link after link backward

Rule of the chain: a chain of teachers cannot regress forever, and it cannot end in another human learner, because then you would only ask who taught him.

Terminus: it must end in one taught by no human being at all → one who received knowledge through divine inspiration → the prophet, and the imam.

Reading: the deficiency built into every human being, the need to be taught, necessitates a teacher, and the chain of teachers necessitates a final teacher who stands outside the chain, fed directly from God. This is among the most compelling proofs of both prophethood and the imamate.

Process flow. Why God made human beings deficient in the first place.

Start: suppose God had created them already complete, already knowing.
  Step: there would be nothing to teach them.
  Step: no commands to give, no obligations to fulfill.
  Step: no reward to earn and no punishment to fear.
End: therefore no wisdom in creating them at all. The ignorance is deliberate, it is what makes us receptive, what opens the space a guide is sent to fill.

Contrast pair. The relationship between God and humanity, with a guide and without one.

Column A: With an imam present
- Function: the imams are God's proofs, His hujaj, the standing arguments by which He holds His creatures accountable at all times.
- Disputes: a living authority resolves the conflict when one faction quotes a verse, another a saying of the Prophet, a third an analogy.
- Verdict: God's case is established, no one can plead that guidance was unavailable.

Column B: With no imam present
- Function: humanity would bear no responsibility, no one to establish God's case, no one to bring disputes to.
- Disputes: no group can establish its claim against the others, the community is left in confusion.
- Verdict: the relationship flips, God would have no proof against His creatures, and the creatures would have a proof against God, saying with justice, we found no one to guide us from falsehood to the truth.

Shared row: it is precisely to make that plea impossible that the imam exists in every age.

Annotated structure. The Father of Imams stating as fact what logic demands.

Whole: the standing presence of the proof in every generation.
Citation:

> The earth is never empty of one who stands for God with a proof, whether he is visible and well known, or fearful and hidden, so that God's proofs and His clear signs should never come to nothing. (the Father of Imams, peace be upon him)

Annotation: what al-Naysaburi presents as a demand of logic, the Father of Imams states as a standing fact of the world. There has not been, and cannot be, a single moment when the proof is withdrawn. As scripture says, to God belongs the conclusive proof (chapter 6, verse 149), and it is through the imam that this proof is made to stand.

## The circle, the one, and the dot

Annotated structure. The bold claim about where the sciences truly come from.

Whole: the origin of every branch of knowledge.
Parts:
- The rules and principles credited to the ancient sages are in reality the teachings of the imams.
- It is from the imams that people first derived the foundations of every discipline.
- Every discipline has a summit that points back to that source.
Annotation: al-Naysaburi is about to prove this by walking the eye up three ordered domains, shapes, numbers, and time, each with a peak that stands for the imam.

Annotated structure. The circle, first face of the lesson.

Whole: the highest and most perfect figure of geometry.
Parts, the one-way dependence:
- Divide a circle and a square appears inside it with arcs left over at the edges.
- Extend lines across it and four triangles emerge.
- Every other shape is contained within the circle and drawn out of it.
- The circle itself needs none of them, the square cannot generate the circle.
Annotation: that asymmetry is the whole point. Every other figure needs the circle, the circle needs no other figure, exactly as the imam needs no other rank in the mission of his age while every rank needs him and falls within his command. The imam is the circle of religion, da'irat al-din, the bounding figure within whose command all the ranks fall and from whom they draw their benefit.

Comparison matrix. The three symbols of the single lesson, an origin that gives rise to all the rest while depending on none.

Rows: The circle | The one | The dot
Columns: What it generates | What it depends on | What it symbolizes

- The circle: the square, the triangle, the arc, every figure drawn out of it | no other shape | the imam, self-sufficient at the center while every rank draws its reality from him
- The one: two is one plus one, three is one plus one plus one, the whole endless ladder of number | nothing above it | the imam, uniquely one in his age, every rank needs him and he needs none
- The dot: the line, the surface, the solid body, everything extended generated from what has no extension | nothing, it has no parts | the imam, whose rank no one can attain and whose reality cannot be truly known

Annotation: three faces of a single lesson, that in every domain there is an origin that gives rise to all the rest while depending on none of them.

Annotated structure. Heading off the natural error about the number one.

Whole: what the one does, and does not, point to.
Parts:
- The mistake: some suppose the number one points to the Creator.
- The correction: the one is the cause of mathematics and the foundation of counting, and the Creator is not the foundation of a series built up out of Him by addition.
- The truth: the one signifies the imam in his rank and station, unique and unequalled, sharing his position with no other creature in his age.
Annotation: the Creator stands above the series entirely, the imam stands at its head, and only the second is what the unit models.

Hierarchy tree. The pattern stamped into time as firmly as into geometry.

Level 1: The days of the week
  Level 2: Saturday, holding the greatest distinction among them
Level 1: The months of the year
  Level 2: Ramadan, the finest among them
Level 1: The hours of every single day
  Level 2: the one hour in which a supplication is answered

Annotation: every genus in the world, created or merely conceived, has a summit in nobility that nothing else of its kind can reach, and every one of those summits points to the imam. Al-Naysaburi does not pretend to exhaust the examples, he says plainly that to mention everything of the sort would carry the treatise past its proper length. The point was never the list, it was to train the eye to expect a peak in every ordered thing, so that when the argument names the peak of the human world, the mind has already agreed to the shape of the claim.

## Prayer, pilgrimage, and the imam

Process flow. The argument from language, following the qibla to the imam.

Start: the Prophet first commanded the believers to face the qibla.
  Step: the word qibla comes from a root meaning face to face, to turn toward it is to set your face directly toward something.
  Step: to be before a thing, amama it, is to stand in front of it and so to precede it, the opposite of being behind.
  Step: the word imam shares exactly this root, the prayer-leader is called imam because he stands before the rows and precedes them.
End: the command to face the qibla is, at its root, a command to orient yourself toward the one who goes before, toward the imam. Turning your face forward in prayer performs in miniature what your whole life of worship is meant to be, a turning of the self toward the one appointed to lead it.

Contrast pair. The exchange between follower and leader in the congregation.

Column A: What the follower owes
- Requirement: intention as well as limbs, the inner intention joined to the leader's, not only the copied movements.
- Failure: a worshipper who bows and prostrates exactly but whose intention is not joined to the leader's has no accepted prayer at all.
- Rule: the outward match is worthless without the inward one.

Column B: What the leader supplies
- Provision: if one of those he leads is negligent, the imam compensates for that negligence, covering the follower's lapse.
- Reach: the leader carries what the follower drops.
- Reward: a prayer with the congregation is worth twenty-seven of a prayer performed alone, and the multiplier comes from the leader, not the crowd.

Shared row: the follower gives his intention as well as his limbs, and in return the leader carries what the follower drops. The merit stems precisely from the prayer being done with the prayer-leader, facing him, following him in intention as well as action.

Annotated structure. The instruction to keep the rows straight, and what al-Naysaburi reads in it.

Whole: the terse, endlessly repeated command about the rows.
Citation:

> Straighten your rows, for the straightening of the rows is part of the completeness of the prayer. (the Prophet)

Annotation: the even rows, no disparity among the worshippers as they stand, are a sign that the hearts and acts of those the imam leads must not differ from one another. And do not oppose the leader in any gesture, when he bows, bow, when he prostrates, prostrate, when he recites, listen, when he gives the closing salutation, give it too.

Contrast pair. The partial imam against the supreme imam, the lesser case proving the greater.

Column A: The prayer-leader, a partial imam
- Scope: leads in one small act of worship.
- Weight: opposing him in a single bow annuls your prayer and forces you to start over.
- Standing: himself a man who needs someone to vouch for him.

Column B: The supreme imam, al-imam al-a'zam
- Scope: leads in the whole of the revealed law and its inner interpretation.
- Weight: governs not one prayer but the believer's entire life.
- Standing: guarantees the purification of those he leads, freeing them from doubt, confusion, and forgetfulness, and securing their salvation as long as they follow him.

Shared row: if even the partial imam must not be opposed in the smallest gesture, it cannot possibly be permissible to oppose the supreme imam in the commands that govern the whole of a believer's life. The lesser case proves the greater. The imam is the guarantor for those who follow him, and that guarantor is the supreme imam, not the leader of the daily prayer.

Annotated structure. The scriptural warrant for the imam's standing intercession.

Whole: the guarantee that the office of forgiveness endures beyond the Prophet's lifetime.
Citation:

> Had they, when they wronged themselves, come to you and sought God's forgiveness, and the Messenger had asked forgiveness for them, they would have found God relenting and merciful. (chapter 4, verse 64)

Annotation: the imam covers his followers' lapses and seeks forgiveness on their behalf. Were the imam to withhold that intercession from later generations, who need forgiveness more, not less, than those who lived alongside the Prophet, they would surely perish. So the office endures, whoever comes to the imam, the imam seeks forgiveness for him, and God forgives.

Contrast pair. The worshipper inside the allegiance and the worshipper who breaks it.

Column A: Within the allegiance
- Bond: the believer stays inside the walaya, the loyalty that ties the follower to the one appointed to lead him.
- State: he remains under the care of the mission throughout his prayer.
- Result: the guarantee holds, purification and salvation as long as he follows.

Column B: Breaking the bond
- Bond: he opposes the imam in action, intention, or decision.
- State: the situation is precisely parallel to the worshipper who sets himself against the prayer-leader.
- Result: his bowing and prostrating and standing and sitting are rendered worthless, his effort futile, he must begin the prayer again from the start.

Shared row: the lesser case is a working model of the greater. If this much hangs on obedience to a partial imam in a single act, obedience to the supreme imam, which God has made absolutely obligatory, cannot hang on less. Even were there no other proof anywhere in the sacred law, the structure of the prayer by itself would be sufficient to establish the imam and the duty to obey him.

Process flow. From the daily prayer outward to the larger rites, the same figure at each center.

Start: the Friday prayer.
  Node: not valid without the sermon, the khutba, delivered from the pulpit.
  Node: the sermon invalid without the imam or someone he has appointed to deliver it.
  Consequence: strip away the imam and the community's central weekly gathering cannot be validly held.
  Node: the pilgrimage, called in its entirety a proof of the imamate.
  Node: it begins with an intention, and an intention must be directed toward something known.
End: that known object toward which the pilgrim's intention is aimed is, at the inner level, the imam.

Annotated structure. The Ka'ba's barren setting, and the lesson in it.

Whole: God placing the House in a place without crops, trees, or worldly benefit, reachable only through hardship.
Citation:

> Our Lord, I have settled some of my offspring in a valley without cultivation, by Your sacred House. (chapter 14, verse 37)

Parts, what the hardship teaches:
- What the Ka'ba symbolizes, the imam, can likewise be reached only through effort, endurance, and the willingness to suffer for it.
- The turning toward the imam must arise from pure faith alone, never from greed, the hope of pleasure, or worldly prestige.
- If the pilgrim meets hardship, weariness, and attack along the road, his heart must not falter, he must press on with patience.
Annotation: every act of the pilgrimage carries the same freight, the circumambulation, the touching of the stone, the running between the hills, the casting of pebbles, the plain white garments, the giving up of perfume and sexual relations, all of it points to the affirmation of the imamate and obedience to the imam under every condition, the seeking of guidance, and the casting off of corrupt beliefs.

## Alms, war, and the law

Annotated structure. The overlooked fact about how the Qur'an gives the command to give alms.

Whole: the zakat, called among the most emphatic proofs of the imamate.
Citation:

> Take alms from their wealth, to purify them and cleanse them, and pray for them, your prayer is a comfort to them. (chapter 9, verse 103)

Parts, read closely:
- God did not tell the people to collect and distribute the alms among themselves, He told the Prophet to take it.
- The Prophet's purifying prayer over the giver is described as the recompense for the alms.
- The giver hands over a portion of his wealth, and what he receives in return is the intercession of the one appointed to take it.
Annotation: if that recompense was granted to the believers of the Prophet's own time and withheld from every generation after, generations that need it more, God would have committed an injustice, assigning a reward to an act and then making it unavailable to most who perform it. God is far above such injustice, so the office must continue, whoever comes to the imam and gives his alms, the imam purifies him and prays for him, in every age.

Annotated structure. The second clue, hidden in what was forbidden.

Whole: alms forbidden to the Prophet and to his family.
Parts:
- The prohibition: the Prophet could not himself benefit from the charity he collected.
- The reason: so no one could accuse him of collecting for personal gain, of enriching himself under cover of a religious duty.
- The revelation: the prohibition quietly shows whose the alms really are, they belong to the office the Prophet held, the office of the one who stands in his place in collecting them, the imam.
- The compensation: to make up for what was forbidden them, God assigned the family instead the fifth, the khums, a designated share of the spoils.
Annotation: the very forbidding of the charity to its collector marks the charity as the office's, not the man's.

Process flow. The fork that collapses the later collectors' position.

Start: those who took over collecting alms after the Prophet, sat in the place of the imams, claimed the collection as their own right, cited this verse, and made war on those who withheld it.
  Setup: the same people are trusted with judgments of bloodshed, rulings on sexual offences, the disposition of all property, and are prayed behind as leaders, yet are said not to be trusted with the alms.
  Fork on the accusation, either it is true or it is false:
    Branch true: if the leaders really are embezzlers who cannot be trusted with charity, they are not imams at all but thieves, and it becomes unlawful to pray behind them, to fight under them, to make the pilgrimage under their authority, to accept their rulings on blood and property.
    Branch false: if the leaders are honest and the accusation a lie, then the jurists who bear public witness to those leaders' authority are liars, and the testimony of a liar is void.
End: either way the structure falls. The inconsistency is fatal, they trust their leaders with blood and property and prayer, then withhold from them the alms.

Comparison matrix. Why war may be waged only under the imam's banner, the powers only he holds.

Rows: The treasury | Captives and borders | Command and revenue | Judgment of the war | The rebel
Columns: What it requires | Why only the imam holds it

- The treasury: the bayt al-mal guarded and kept under guarantee | he alone guards the treasury of the Muslims, under his guarantee
- Captives and borders: ransoming prisoners, protecting the frontier towns | he alone ransoms captives from enemy hands and protects the border towns
- Command and revenue: appointing commanders, gathering what sustains the fighters | he alone appoints the commanders under whom armies march and gathers the revenues that sustain them
- Judgment of the war: knowing when to fight and when to make peace, whom to pardon and whom to ransom | he alone has the knowledge to judge when to make war and when to make peace
- The rebel: identifying who has departed from lawful obedience | he alone can identify the rebel, so the party fighting on the other side can be known as the party of truth

Annotation: jihad is one of the pillars of religion and may lawfully be waged only under the banner of the imam, no command to make war can validly issue from anyone else. The reason is intensely practical, only the imam holds the things that make a just war possible.

Annotated structure. The confession from an unexpected witness.

Whole: the knowledge of just war traced to its source by an opponent's own master.
Citation:

> Had it not been for the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib, we would not have known how to fight the rebels, nor how to distinguish the rebel from the rightful believer. (al-Shafi'i)

Annotation: one of the great founders of Sunni law himself seals the point. The knowledge of how to conduct a just war traces back, on the testimony of his opponents' own master, to the Father of Imams.

Contrast pair. The bargain between rulers and jurists, each disgracing the other.

Column A: What the rulers gave
- Grant: authority over religious affairs, handed to a class of so-called scholars said to have learning but lacking its substance.
- Reward: the petty goods of this world, appointment over provinces, seats at the head of public sessions.
- Purpose: so the jurists would rule by their own desires.

Column B: What the jurists gave back
- Return: they left worldly power to the rulers.
- Return: they bore false witness to the rulers' legitimacy.
- Product: not an answer when a real case comes, but a chorus of contradictions.

Shared row: the arrangement is a squalid bargain, and each disgraced the other. In the end the leader is the mirror image of his followers, and the followers the mirror image of their leader.

Comparison matrix. Four masters, four verdicts on a single question.

Rows: On a question of blood | On a question of marriage
Columns: One jurist rules | The opposing jurist rules

- On a question of blood: the man should be executed, on the authority of Abu Hanifa | the man should not be executed, on the authority of al-Shafi'i
- On a question of marriage: the woman is divorced, on the authority of Malik | the woman is not forbidden to her husband, on the authority of Ahmad b. Hanbal

Annotation: four masters, four verdicts, on a single question of blood or marriage, and this is what passes for the settled knowledge on which lives and households turn.

Annotated structure. The Father of Imams' portrait of the doubt-ridden judge.

Whole: the man who manufactures a ruling he is himself unsure of.
Citation:

> An obscure case is brought to him, so he arranges for it a padding of his own opinion, and then is certain of it, while he is adrift in doubt. The blood he has shed wrongly cries out against him, the inheritance he has divided wrongly cries out against him. (the Father of Imams)

Annotation: the wronged blood and the misparted estates cry out behind the judge who takes his seat to decide what defeats everyone else. The Father of Imams had drawn that very portrait long before al-Naysaburi turned the jurists' contradictions against them.

Annotated structure. The penalties, the hudud, and who alone may impose them.

Whole: the amputations, floggings, and stonings the sacred law lays down for grave crimes.
Parts, points even the opponents concede:
- These may be carried out only by the imam or someone he has appointed.
- A man without lawful authority, without walaya, cannot impose them, universally accepted.
- If you witness a murder, it is not for you to kill the murderer, if you catch a thief, it is not for you to cut off his hand.
- Judgment in blood, property, and sexual offence may be exercised only by one who genuinely knows the cases and the law, which verses abrogate and which are abrogated, the conditions under which analogy is valid.
Annotation: the opponents grant that penalties belong to legitimate authority alone, and that judgment belongs to one who truly knows the law. Both concessions are about to be turned against them.

Annotated structure. The scriptural rebuke to those who punish the vices they love.

Whole: the devastating question, how men who incur penalties daily can be fit to administer them.
Citations:

> Do you enjoin virtue on people and forget yourselves? (chapter 2, verse 44)

> You are the best community brought forth for mankind, you enjoin good and forbid evil. (chapter 3, verse 110)

Annotation: how can someone command others to do good while forgetting to do it himself? Enjoining good and forbidding evil is the office of the imams, not of men who love the very vices they are meant to punish.

Process flow. The trap sprung fully closed, on the opponents' own admitted principles.

Start: the principles the opponents themselves hold.
  Principle: an unjust and immoral leader forfeits his authority.
  Principle: a judge who betrays his trust is removed.
  Principle: a sinner's testimony is inadmissible.
  Principle: as al-Shafi'i himself ruled, a sinner has no guardianship even over his own daughter's marriage.
  Apply them honestly to the leaders the opponents defend:
    unfit to judge.
    unfit to give a daughter in marriage.
    unfit to bear witness.
    unfit to carry out a single penalty.
End: the conclusion follows on the opponents' own terms. If their imamate is false, and the imamate is truly one of the foundations of religion, then it belongs to the pure progeny of Taha and Yasin, the family protected from corruption and falsehood, purified of every blame.

## What this chapter establishes

Process flow. The single shape that emerges from the ground crossed.

Start: the plainest fact about human beings, that we are born knowing nothing and must be taught.
  Step: follow it link by link up a chain of teachers to a final teacher who learns from no one but God, the prophet and the imam, God's standing proof over creation.
  Step: find that same figure hidden inside the ordinary acts of religion, the direction you face in prayer, the reason a congregation is worth twenty-seven solitary prayers.
  Step: the one the pilgrim's intention is secretly aimed at, the reason the Ka'ba sits in a hard and barren place.
  Step: his hands the ones the alms belong to, his banner the only one under which a war may lawfully be fought, his authority the only warrant for a punishment.
End: take him away and none of it stands, the prayer is broken, the alms misdirected, the war lawless, the penalty a crime.

Comparison matrix. The four surfaces that each prove the same figure.

Rows: Worship | Alms | War and the sword of the law | The opponents' jurisprudence
Columns: What is at stake | How it proves the imam

- Worship: prayer, congregation, sermon, pilgrimage | the imam is the direction faced, the source of the twenty-seven-fold merit, the object of the pilgrim's intention, without him the rites do not stand
- Alms: the zakat and its purifying prayer | God told the Prophet to take it, the recompense is the collector's intercession, the office must endure in every age
- War and the sword of the law: jihad, the treasury, the penalties | only the imam holds the treasury, the command, the knowledge of just war and the warrant for punishment
- The opponents' jurisprudence: their own principles honestly applied | an unjust leader forfeits authority, a sinner's testimony is void, so the leaders they defend are disqualified and the office is proven to belong elsewhere

Annotation: the second half turned a corner worth naming, al-Naysaburi did not merely assert the imam's necessity, he refuted his opponents out of their own mouths. It is the argument of a man so confident of his ground that he is willing to fight on his opponents' terms and still win.

Process flow. The necessity run backward through time, and the door the chapter opens.

Start: if the imam is required now, if the religion of this age cannot stand without him.
  Step: then the argument cannot stop at the present.
  Step: the treatise presses the claim into history itself, that there has never been a single age or generation, from the first human being onward, left without a guide at its peak.
  Contrast of the two proofs:
    The proof from worship and law showed the world needs an imam today.
    The proof from the prophetic cycles will show it has always needed one, and always had one.
End: an unbroken line of guidance reaching back, without a gap anywhere, to Adam.
