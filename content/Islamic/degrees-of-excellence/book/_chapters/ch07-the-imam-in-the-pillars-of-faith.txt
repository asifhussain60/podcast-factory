# The Imam in the Pillars of Faith

The ritual prayer is a powerful proof of the imamate — in how it is ordered, in the standing it prescribes, in its turning toward the qibla, and in the place of the prayer leader (imam) at the head of the rows. The Prophet (ع) first commanded the Muslims to face the qibla. The word qibla comes from qabala, which means "face to face": whoever faces the qibla turns his face straight toward it, and that direction is the imam. It is said that to be before (amama) a thing is to stand in front of it and so to precede it, which is the opposite of standing behind or after it. The word imam belongs to this same root, and the leader of the prayer is called imam precisely because he goes before the worshippers. To direct people toward the qibla is therefore to direct them toward the imam, and the Prophet commanded people to follow him, to obey him in every act of worship, and to accept his direction. When a person faces forward in prayer, he faces the qibla, and this mirrors, across the whole of his worship and obedience, his orientation toward the imam. When a person prays with his imam, the merit of that prayer comes precisely from its being performed with the imam — facing him, following him, and led by him in both intention and action — for the Prophet said that a prayer prayed in congregation equals twenty-seven prayers prayed alone. This shows that the whole of obedience lies in being led by the supreme imam (al-imam al-azam), guided by him, following him, and directed by him, so that obedience itself becomes excellent. The Prophet also commanded the worshippers to stand in even rows, so that there would be no unevenness among them as they stood. This signals that the hearts of those whom the imam guides and leads must not differ from one another, and neither must their actions nor their words.

The Prophet commanded the worshippers to follow the prayer leader even when the recitation, the rising, or the prostration is drawn out, and never to move against him in gesture or in intention. Indeed, if a worshipper matches the imam's physical action but not his intention, that prayer is not accepted; and if one who is being led falls short, the imam makes up for his shortcoming. All of this makes plain that if obedience to a partial (juzi) imam — one who is not an imam in the absolute sense — is made obligatory, and the reward of that worship is multiplied, then what must be said of obedience to the supreme (azam) imam, who leads in the whole of the revealed law (sharia) and its inner interpretation (tawil)? And if it is forbidden to move against the prayer leader in even one act of worship, how can it be allowed to move against the supreme imam in the commands and prohibitions that govern a believer's entire life? The believer stays under the care of the dawa in his prayer for as long as he remains within the allegiance (walaya) of the imam. If he moves against the imam in action, in intention, or in decision, he will have voided his prayers and stepped outside the way of the worshipper — just as when a worshipper sets himself to oppose the prayer leader, his bowing, prostration, standing, and sitting are made worthless, his act is empty, and he must begin the prayer again. How much more vital, then, is obedience to the supreme imam, which God has made absolutely obligatory. The Prophet said:

> "The imam is the guarantor for those who follow him."

This surely does not mean the prayer leader alone, for such a leader himself needs someone who can vouch for him. It means, in truth, the supreme imam (ع), who guarantees the purification of those he leads — freeing them from doubt, suspicion, confusion, and forgetfulness — and who guarantees their salvation as long as they are guided by him and follow him. The Prophet pointed to the imam's place as going before everyone, and he commanded them not to vie with the imam in reciting the Quran, saying:

> "When he prostrates you should prostrate, when he bows you bow, when he recites you listen, and when he says the salutations (taslim) then you should say it too."

All of this points to obedience to the imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ) and to their exaltation in every matter, to the permanence of their authority in their own age, to the duty of obeying them, and to the handing over (taslim) of the command (al-amr), at the close of each imam's term, to whomever he appoints. Even if there were no proof of the imam and of obedience to him in the sharia other than what is found in the prayer, that alone would be enough. For the Prophet explained that the imam clears the worshippers of their shortcomings in prayer — meaning that the imam covers their lapses and seeks forgiveness on their behalf — just as God, the Exalted, said:

> "If only when they wronged themselves, they had come to you seeking forgiveness of God and the Prophet, they would have surely found God Most Forgiving and Merciful."

If the imam were to withhold forgiveness and the acceptance of repentance from this community (umma), they would surely perish, for they stand in the greatest need of forgiveness. What endures, in every age and every time, is this: whoever comes to the imam, the imam seeks forgiveness for him, and God forgives him, just as He forgave those earlier people.

I say also that the Friday prayer is not valid without the sermon (khutba, اَلْخُطْبَة) delivered from the pulpits (manabir). People pray behind the imam so that they may understand that obedience to the imam is lifted from no creature anywhere. Furthermore, the pilgrimage in its entirety is a proof for the imamate. The pilgrimage begins with an intention, and an intention must be directed toward something known, so that when it is made with full knowledge (marifa) it becomes a clear and deliberate act needing no further explanation — which is itself a sign of the intention directed toward the imam. The Kaba, in some of the inner interpretations, is a proof for the imam. It was set in a desert empty of settlement, reachable only with great hardship, in a place without crops or trees and stripped of worldly gain, so arranged that worshippers may understand that what it stands for (mamthul) can likewise be reached only through great effort, hardship, and injury from enemies. They should understand too that turning toward the imam and seeking him must not be for worldly ends — out of greed, pleasure, or worldly standing — but that visiting the imam must arise from pure faith alone. If a person meets hardship, weariness, attacks from enemies, or exhaustion, his heart must not waver; he must bear it with patience. He will then see and know all of this in its symbol (mathal), which is the pilgrimage. Its circling of the Kaba, the touching and receiving of it (istilam), the pointing toward it, the running, the throwing of stones, the wearing of the white garments (ihram), and the giving up of perfume and marital relations — all of this points to the affirmation of the imamate and to obedience to him under every condition, to the seeking of guidance and the guarding of one's soul, and to the casting off of blameworthy doctrines and corrupt beliefs.

I say also that among the strongest proofs for the necessity of the imamate is the obligation of alms (zakat) — in its religious duties, its safekeeping, and its distribution. God, the Exalted, commanded the Prophet to take almsgiving (sadaqa) from the believers when He said:

> "Take alms (sadaqa) from their wealth, purify them and cleanse them through it and pray for them. Your prayers will indeed bring them tranquillity."

The Prophet then set out the duties of zakat, its amounts, and how it should be collected. He himself collected it until the community understood both how to give it and how to receive it. Whoever afterward sat in the place of the imams also collected zakat, claiming that the collection of alms was his and citing this verse and the practice of God's Messenger to justify it. For that reason he waged war against the Banu Hanifa and killed them. Those who sat in the place of the imams after him likewise collected the alms, until the matter at last reached their own leaders, who took offense at it, kept the alms money for themselves, and stopped it from being collected on behalf of the imam. Is it any wonder, then, to see the state of this community? They affirm that the verse in question abrogates (nasikh) in both ruling and recitation, that it is not itself abrogated (mansukh), and that the Prophetic sunna still requires the collection of alms. How, then, did they allow themselves to change the ruling of the verse and of God's command, and to change the sunna of the Prophet and the sunna of those whose imamate they claim? The only motive for that change was their denial of the imams' rightful standing and their rejection of the imamate within the Prophet's family (ahl al-bayt). God, the Exalted, had made plain, through His rulings on alms (zakat) and the manner of collecting them, the standing of the imamate and the imams: He forbade sadaqa to the Prophet so that no one could accuse him of collecting it for his own gain, and He likewise forbade it to the Prophet's family so that they would know that the imam among them is the one who holds the Prophet's place in collecting alms, and that they, the imams, are fully trustworthy in this.

Had the people been entrusted to carry out the duty of almsgiving and its distribution themselves, God would have commanded them to do so, just as He commanded them regarding prayer, fasting, and the other obligatory duties. But He did not order them to distribute the sadaqa; He commanded the Prophet to do so. If it had been allowed for the people to distribute it themselves, it would have been allowed in the Prophet's own time, when they were far steadier in their religion, more certain in their faith, and of greater integrity. How is it, then, that later generations have made themselves trustees of almsgiving? And why is it allowed for them that prayer — which is more meritorious than almsgiving — be performed behind their imams, and the other duties likewise, yet it is not acceptable to give their imams the alms? Why, further, are they held trustworthy in matters of bloodshed and sexual offenses and in every other matter of property, yet not reliable or trustworthy when it comes to alms? If the people are truthful in what they say about their imams being untrustworthy with alms, then those imams are not imams at all but embezzlers. And if they are embezzlers, it is not allowed to pray behind them, nor to wage jihad or make the pilgrimage under them, nor to accept their rulings on sexual offenses, bloodshed, or property. But if these people are lying in what they say and claim, then the scholars among them who issue religious opinions (fatwa) to that effect are liars as well, since they are the ones who bear witness to the leadership of those imams, and the testimony of liars is not acceptable. God, the Exalted, also commanded the Prophet to purify the believers and pray for them when He said:

> "...purify them and pray for them; your prayers will indeed bring them tranquillity."

He thus made the imam's purification of them and his prayer over them a source of their tranquillity, and that prayer was the recompense for the alms He took from them. If God had granted this recompense to those present in the time of the Prophet and withheld it from those who came after — even though the later generations need it more than those who lived in the Prophet's time — that would be an injustice against those who came after that age. God is far exalted above injustice toward His worshippers, and He, may His name be exalted, does not impose an obligation and assign a recompense (iwadan) for it, in this world or the next, without making that recompense universal. Rather, the purification, the prayer, and the tranquillity belong forever to whoever comes to the imam and gives alms (zakat) from his property, whether openly or in secret. The imam then purifies him and prays for him. Were the imam needed for this alone, that would be enough. His distribution of the alms to those entitled to it rests on his knowledge of who may rightfully receive it and who may not, and on his awareness of when a class of eligible recipients has no one in it and how the share meant for them should then be handled. The imam is thus the one who safeguards the treasury of the Muslims, distributing it according to the needs of religion and the demands of the time.

I say also that jihad is one of the pillars of religion, and that it is allowed to wage war only under the banner of an imam. No command to wage war can stand except from the imam, for the imam must first protect the treasury (bayt al-mal) of the Muslims, which is under his guarantee, and protect the border towns and the Muslim army, and ransom Muslim prisoners of war from the hands of the unbelievers. An army, moreover, may be sent out only under a commander appointed by the imam, or by someone the imam has himself appointed. The imam knows better the right times for peace, for agreements, and for treaties, and it is he who concludes them. When prisoners of war are taken, he knows whom to pardon, whom to ransom, and whom to put to death. He is better acquainted with the ways of gathering the land tax (kharaj) and the revenues, so that they serve as a means of benefit and support for the Muslims. The imam also identifies the rebel, and it is by standing with him that a party (fia) becomes the party of truth. The rebel is the one who leaves the obedience of the imam, and it then falls to the imam to decide whether to wage war, to make peace, or to conclude a truce. Al-Shafii said:

> "If it were not for the Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib (ع), we would not have known how to wage war against rebels, nor would we have been able to distinguish the rebel from the rightful believers."

There is also the fifth (khums, اَلْخُمْس), which God assigned to the Prophet's family and to the imams in place of the alms (sadaqa) that He had forbidden them. All the benefits of alms and zakat, together with all the duties tied to them, are bound to the imam. Beyond this there are the id feasts and the month of Ramadan, in which the imam keeps order and accepts the testimony of witnesses — whether opponents hold that the fasting and the breaking of the fast are set by the sighting of the moon or by the one the imam appoints. And the two feast prayers and the Friday prayer are not valid in congregation without a sermon (khutba) from the imam and his presence, or from someone he has appointed, even if the congregation is reduced from four to two on account of the imam's sermon.

As for the carrying out of legal punishments (hudud), only the imam, or someone the imam appoints, may carry them out. The cutting off of hands, flogging, stoning, imprisonment, the indemnity for injuries (arsh al-jirahat), and judgment concerning them can all be carried out only on the imam's orders. Were these rules the only proof for the imamate in the Messenger's family, that alone would be enough. For the opponents themselves agree that whoever holds no authority (walaya) may not rule on legal punishments. If a man witnesses another killing someone unjustly, it is not for him to kill that person. If he sees someone stealing, it is not for him to cut off his hand, stone him, or take any other such measure. This principle reaches so far that most people agree that not even a slave's owner may carry out legal punishments or chastise him; only the imam may do so.

Legal punishments cannot simply be abolished, for were they abolished, the people would perish and the lands would fall to ruin. Yet if everyone were allowed to carry them out, every person of violent or harmful intent would claim to be administering a lawful punishment, saying that the one he struck or killed had committed some act that deserved it. But how can a man who himself incurs twenty legal punishments every day rule on legal punishments and be allowed to carry them out? Were that allowed for such a person, it would be allowed for every oppressor and rebel, and the result would be the ruin of the people. Rather, when a rebellious party carries out legal punishments and destroys lives, and the imam has power over them, he fines them for whatever harm they did to the righteous party, and so keeps the wrongdoers from destroying the people and damaging their property.

Such people then claim that everything they did was in keeping with a legal rule laid upon them. But God, the Exalted, has spoken to this directly:

> "Do you command people to do good and forget it yourselves?"

God, the Exalted, made plain that whoever himself needs to be commanded to do good and restrained from evil is in no position to command others to good or forbid them evil. And God explained in another verse that the enjoining of good and the forbidding of evil (al-amr bil-maruf wa nahy an al-munkar) is a duty of the imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ), when He said:

> "You are the best community (umma) that has been raised for the people. You enjoin good conduct and forbid evil."

They are the equitable leaders (aimma al-wasat) who stand as witnesses over the people.

How can a man command good if he does not practice it himself? How can he forbid evil if he loves evil and commits it a hundred times a day? How is it allowed for their imams — when they commit daily the very acts that call for legal punishment, driven by lust and desire, and when they draw close to themselves those who love such acts and share in them — to forbid those same acts, all the while regarding those who do them as elegant, victorious, agreeable, and refined? Whoever will not join them in drinking and debauchery they neither draw close nor employ, for such a man is disagreeable to them. Is that not itself a lesson to those they lead, and an incitement toward corruption and debauchery?

We also say: it is agreed by all that legal judgment among the people in matters of bloodshed, sexual offenses, and property may be exercised only by one who knows the relevant cases and the legal opinions concerning them, who understands the legal norms, the Quranic revelation and its meaning — including the abrogating and the abrogated verses (al-nasikh wal-mansukh) — and the analogical deduction (qiyas) in their view, and who meets its conditions, such that he can be trusted in rulings on bloodshed, property, and sexual offenses. Does any of the usurpers who claim the imamate actually meet these conditions? On the contrary, every one of them leans on those so-called jurists who are said to have knowledge yet lack its substance. The usurpers shower these men with the petty goods of this world, appoint them governors of provinces, and let them preside over public sessions. When an incident arises, they turn to the jurists for answers, and what do they produce? One says, "According to Abu Hanifa, this man should be executed," while another says, "According to al-Shafii, he should not be executed." A third says, "According to Malik, this woman should be divorced," and a fourth says, "According to Ahmad b. Hanbal, the woman is not forbidden to him." The usurpers handed the leadership of religious affairs to these jurists so that the jurists would judge according to their own desires and opinions, and in return the jurists would leave worldly authority to the usurpers and bear false witness on their behalf. In doing so, the jurists disgraced themselves through their compliance. I do not know which of the two is more astonishing: the one who shamelessly claims the imamate while having no knowledge whatever of religion or its legal norms, and therefore needs such scholars, or the one who clings to his leadership and rewards their ignorance and their shame. If the jurists were true scholars, and authority truly rested on their pronouncements, then they would be the imams — not the man who falsely claims the title while being nothing more than an idol set up with no real authority, able neither to benefit nor to harm. Or should the greater wonder be kept for him who clings to such a leader and rewards his ignorance? In the end, their imam is the mirror image of the one he leads, and the one led is the mirror image of his imam.

If their imams have no right to exercise legal judgment and authority (walaya) — because they lack knowledge, fail to meet its conditions, and possess neither modesty nor integrity — then it must be noted that most adherents of the doctrine of choice (ikhtiyar) themselves hold that an imam who is unjust and immoral forfeits the imamate, and, in the view of some, ought to be removed from it. Likewise, a judge among them who betrays the people's trust should be removed from office, and a legal guardian should likewise lose his guardianship. Some go further, holding that the community is obliged to remove an imam who is unjust and immoral. Al-Shafii said: "A sinner has no legal guardianship; if he had a daughter, he would have no right to give her in marriage. That right would pass to the judge, not to him." And moreover, according to all these jurist-imams, the testimony of a sinner is inadmissible. Thus, by agreement (ittifaq) and consensus (ijma), their imams are not among those qualified to exercise legal reasoning (ijtihad) in religion, nor to give their daughters in marriage, nor to have their testimony accepted, nor to carry out legal punishments.

These men have been notorious and infamous offenders, and those who came after them walked the same path. How could anyone with such qualities be the Commander of the Faithful, the vicegerent (khalifa) of the Lord of the worlds, and an imam whose obedience is obligatory? If they hold no right of guardianship, legal judgment, or testimony, how can those they appoint exercise guardianship, and how can testimony be accepted from those they endorse? How can their recommendation of a man as fit for judgment, witnessing, and guardianship carry any weight at all? Is there anything in this world more remarkable than these people — than the contradictions in their rulings, the principles of their legal school (madhhab), and their doctrine? And is there anyone more infamous than their imam and the people he leads?

Were we to count every contradiction and disgrace lodged in their principles, this treatise would have to be stretched far beyond its intended scope. What we have already said is a sufficient refutation of their claim to the imamate and a clear proof that everything they have done and go on doing amounts to falsehood, injustice, and transgression. That alone is shame and disgrace enough for them. If their imamate is false, as we have shown, and if it is true that the imamate is one of the foundations of religion (usul al-din), an inevitable necessity to which all revealed laws and norms are bound, then the imamate truly belongs to the pure imams from among the progeny of Taha and Yasin — protected from corruption and oppression, from lying and transgression, and cleansed of all filth and blame. What we have set out through rational deduction and through the religious law is sufficient.

We shall now turn to establishing the necessity of the imamate on the basis of the past prophetic cycles, and to showing that there has never been an age or a period without a true imam whose obedience is obligatory.
