# The Imam in the Pillars of Faith

The ritual prayer, in its arrangement, its prescribed standing, its direction toward the qibla, and the standing of the prayer leader (imam) at the head of the rows, stands as a powerful indication of the imamate. The Prophet (ع) first commanded the Muslims to face the qibla. The word qibla is derived from qabala, meaning "face to face": whoever faces the qibla turns his face directly toward it, and that direction is the imam. It is said that to be before (amāma) something means to stand in front of it and thus to precede it, which is the opposite of being behind or after it. The word imām shares this same root, and the leader of the prayer is called imam precisely because he precedes the worshippers. The directive toward the qibla is therefore a directive toward the imam, and the Prophet commanded people to follow and obey him in every form of worship and to accept his direction. When a person faces forward in prayer, he is facing the qibla, and this mirrors, throughout all his worship and obedience, his orientation toward the imam. When an individual prays with his imam, its merit stems precisely from the fact that it is performed with the imam, facing him, following him, and being led by him in both intention and action, for the Prophet stated that a congregational prayer is equivalent to twenty-seven prayers performed alone. This shows that the whole of obedience consists in being led by the supreme imam (al-imām al-a'ẓam), guided by him, following him, and directed by him, so that obedience itself attains excellence. The Prophet also commanded that worshippers align themselves in rows, so that there would be no disparity among them in their standing. This indicates that the hearts of those guided and led by the imam must not differ from one another, and neither should their actions nor their speech.

The Prophet commanded worshippers to follow the prayer imam even when the recitation, the rising, or the prostration is prolonged, and never to act contrary to him in gesture or intention. Indeed, even if a worshipper agrees with the imam's physical action but not his intention, that prayer is not accepted; and if one who is led is negligent, the imam compensates for that negligence. All of this makes clear that if obedience to a partial (juz'ī) imam, one who is not an imam in the absolute sense, is made obligatory, and the reward of that worship is multiplied, then what must be said of obedience to the supreme (a'ẓam) imam, who leads in all of revealed law (sharī'a) and its esoteric interpretation (ta'wīl)? And if it is impermissible to oppose the prayer imam in any single act of worship, how can it be permissible to oppose the supreme imam in his commands and prohibitions governing a believer's entire life? The believer remains perpetually under the care of the da'wa in his prayer as long as he is included within the allegiance (walāya) of the imam. If he opposes the imam in action, intention, or decision, he will have annulled his prayers and departed from the norm of the worshipper, just as when a worshipper resolves to oppose the prayer imam, his bowing, prostration, standing, and sitting are rendered worthless, his action is futile, and he must begin the prayer again. How much more vital, then, is obedience to the supreme imam, which God has made absolutely obligatory. The Prophet said:

> "The imam is the guarantor for those who follow him."

This certainly does not refer only to the imam of the prayer, for such a leader himself requires someone who can vouch for him. The reference is, in truth, to the supreme imam (ع), who guarantees the purification of those he leads, freeing them from doubt, suspicion, confusion, and forgetfulness, and who guarantees their salvation as long as they are guided by him and follow him. The Prophet indicated the position of the imam as preceding everyone, and he commanded them not to compete with the imam in the recitation of the Qur'an, saying:

> "When he prostrates you should prostrate, when he bows you bow, when he recites you listen, and when he says the salutations (taslīm) then you should say it too."

All of this points to obedience to the imams (عليهم السلام) and to their exaltation in all matters, to the permanence of their authority in their own time, to the necessity of obeying them, and to the submission (taslīm) of command (al-amr) at the close of each imam's affair to whomever he designates. Even if there were no proof for the imam and for obedience to him in the sharī'a other than what is found in the prayer, that alone would suffice. For the Prophet explained that the imam absolves worshippers of their negligence in prayer, meaning that the imam covers their lapses and seeks forgiveness on their behalf, just as God, the Exalted, said:

> "If only when they wronged themselves, they had come to you seeking forgiveness of God and the Prophet, they would have surely found God Most Forgiving and Merciful."

If the imam were to withhold forgiveness and the acceptance of repentance from this community (umma), they would surely perish, for they stand in the greatest need of forgiveness. What endures, in every age and time, is this: whoever comes to the imam, the imam seeks forgiveness for him, and God forgives him, just as He forgave those earlier people.

I say also that the Friday prayer is not valid without the sermon (khutba) delivered from the pulpits (manābir). People pray behind the imam so that they may understand that obedience to the imam is lifted from no created being anywhere. Furthermore, the pilgrimage in its entirety is a proof for the affirmation of the imamate. The pilgrimage begins with an intention, and an intention must be directed toward something known, so that when it is made with full knowledge (ma'rifa) it becomes a clear and purposeful act requiring no further explanation, which is itself an indication of the intention directed toward the imam. The Ka'ba, in some esoteric interpretations, is a proof for the imam. Its placement in a desert devoid of civilization, reachable only with great difficulty, in a location without planting or trees and stripped of worldly benefit, is so arranged that worshippers may understand that what it symbolizes (mamthul) can likewise be reached only through great effort, hardship, and injury from enemies. They should also understand that turning toward the imam and seeking him must not be for worldly purposes, out of greed, pleasure, or worldly prestige, but that visiting the imam must arise from pure faith alone. If a person meets hardship, weariness, attacks from enemies, or fatigue, his heart must not falter; he must endure with patience. He will then see and recognize all of this in its symbol (mathal), which is the pilgrimage. Its circumambulation, the touching and receiving of it (istilām), the pointing toward it, the running, the casting of stones, the wearing of the white garments (ihrām), and the giving up of perfume and sexual relations, all of this points to the affirmation of the imamate and to obedience to him under every condition, to the seeking of guidance and the protection of one's soul, and to the discarding of reprehensible doctrines and corrupt beliefs.

I say also that among the most emphatic proofs for the necessity of the imamate is the obligation of alms (zakāt), in its religious duties, its safekeeping, and its distribution. God, the Exalted, commanded the Prophet to take almsgiving (sadaqa) from the believers when He said:

> "Take alms (sadaqa) from their wealth, purify them and cleanse them through it and pray for them. Your prayers will indeed bring them tranquillity."

The Prophet then explained the obligations of zakāt, its amounts, and how it should be collected. He himself collected it until the community understood both how to give it and how to receive it. Whoever subsequently sat in place of the imams also collected zakāt, claiming that the collection of alms belonged to him and citing this verse and the practice of God's Messenger as his justification. He waged war against the Banu Hanīfa for that reason and killed them. Those who sat in place of the imams after him likewise collected alms, until the matter eventually reached their own leaders, who took offence at it, kept the alms money for themselves, and prevented it from being collected on behalf of the imam. Is it any wonder, then, to see the condition of this community? They affirm that the verse in question abrogates (nāsikh) in both ruling and recitation, that it is not itself abrogated (mansūkh), and that the Prophetic sunna continues to require the collection of alms. How, then, did they permit themselves to alter the ruling of the verse and of God's command, and to change the sunna of the Prophet and the sunna of those whose imamate they claim? The only motive for that change was their denial of the imams' rightful status and their rejection of the imamate within the Prophet's family (ahl al-bayt). God, the Exalted, had made clear, through His rulings on alms (zakāt) and the manner of collecting them, the position of the imamate and the imams: He forbade sadaqa to the Prophet so that no one could accuse him of collecting it for personal gain, and He likewise forbade it to the Prophet's family so that they would recognize that the imam among them is the one who occupies the Prophet's position in collecting alms, and that they, the imams, are fully trustworthy in this regard.

Had people been entrusted to discharge the duty of almsgiving and its distribution themselves, God would have commanded them to do so, just as He commanded them with regard to prayer, fasting, and the other obligatory duties. But He did not order them to distribute the sadaqa; He commanded the Prophet to do so. If it had been permissible for people to distribute it themselves, that would have been permissible during the Prophet's own time, when people were far more steadfast in their religion, more certain in their faith, and of greater integrity. How is it, then, that later generations have made themselves trustees of almsgiving? And why is it permissible for them that prayer, which is more meritorious than almsgiving, be performed behind their imams, and the other duties likewise, yet it is not acceptable to give their imams the alms? Why, further, are they held trustworthy in matters of bloodshed and sexual offences and in every other aspect of property, yet not reliable or trustworthy when it comes to alms? If people were truthful in what they say about their imams being untrustworthy with regard to alms, then those imams are not imams at all but embezzlers. And if they are embezzlers, it is not permissible to pray behind them, nor to conduct jihād or pilgrimage under them, nor to accept their rulings on sexual offences, bloodshed, or property. But if these people are lying in what they say and claim, then the scholars among them who issue religious opinions (fatwa) to that effect are liars as well, since they are the ones who bear witness to the leadership of those imams, and the testimony of liars is not acceptable. God, the Exalted, also commanded the Prophet to purify the believers and pray for them when He said:

> "...purify them and pray for them; your prayers will indeed bring them tranquillity."

He thus made the imam's purification of them and his prayer upon them a source of their tranquillity, and that prayer was the recompense for the alms He took from them. If God had granted this recompense to those present in the time of the Prophet and had withheld it from those who came after, even though the later generations are more in need of it than those who lived in the Prophet's time, that would be an injustice against those who came after that age. God is exalted far above injustice toward His worshippers, and He, may His name be exalted, does not impose an obligation and assign a recompense ('iwadan) for it, in this world or the hereafter, without making that recompense universal. Rather, the purification, the prayer, and the tranquillity belong perpetually to whoever comes to the imam and gives alms (zakāt) from his property, whether in public or in private. The imam then purifies him and prays for him. Were the imam necessary for this alone, that would be sufficient. His distribution of alms to those entitled to it is grounded in his knowledge of who may rightfully receive it and who may not, and in his awareness of when a category of eligible recipients has no one in it and how the portion intended for them should then be handled. The imam is thus the one who safeguards the treasury of the Muslims, distributing it according to the needs of religion and the demands of the time.

I say also that jihad is one of the pillars of religion, and that it is permissible to wage war only under the banner of an imam. No command for waging war can be established except from the imam, for the imam must first protect the treasury (bayt al-māl) of the Muslims, which is under his guarantee, and protect the border towns and the Muslim army, and ransom Muslim prisoners of war from the hands of the infidels. Moreover, an army may only be sent out under a commander appointed by the imam, or by someone the imam has himself appointed. The imam is more knowledgeable about the right times for peace, for agreements, and for treaties, and it is he who concludes them. When prisoners of war are taken, he knows whom to pardon, whom to ransom, and whom to put to death. He is better acquainted with the ways of collecting the land tax (kharāj) and the revenues, so that they serve as a means of benefit and support for the Muslims. The imam also identifies the rebel, and it is by association with him that a party (fi'a) becomes the party of truth. The rebel is the one who departs from obedience to the imam, and it then falls to the imam to decide whether to wage war, to make peace, or to conclude an armistice. Al-Shafi'i said:

> "If it were not for the Commander of the Faithful, 'Alī b. Abī Tālib (ع), we would not have known how to wage war against rebels, nor would we have been able to distinguish the rebel from the rightful believers."

There is also the fifth (khums), which God assigned specifically to the Prophet's family and to the imams in place of the alms (sadaqa) that He had forbidden them. All the benefits of alms and zakāt, together with all the obligations connected to them, are tied to the imam. Beyond this there are the 'īd feasts and the month of Ramadan, in which the imam preserves order and accepts the testimony of witnesses, regardless of whether opponents hold that fasting and the breaking of the fast are determined by the sighting of the moon or by the one appointed by the imam. And the two feast prayers and the Friday prayer are not valid in congregation without a sermon (khutba) from the imam and his presence, or from someone he has appointed, even if the congregation is reduced from four to two on account of the imam's sermon.

[64] As for the execution of legal punishments (hudūd), only the imam, or someone the imam designates, may carry them out. The cutting off of hands, flogging, stoning, imprisonment, the indemnity for injuries ('arsh al-jirāhāt), and judgement concerning them can all be carried out only on the orders of the imam. Were these rules the only proof for the imamate in the Messenger's family, that alone would suffice. For the opponents themselves agree that whoever holds no authority (walāya) cannot be permitted to rule on legal punishments. If a man witnesses another killing someone unjustly, it is not for him to kill that person. If he sees someone stealing, it is not for him to cut off his hand, stone him, or take any other such measure. This principle extends so far that most people agree that not even a slave's owner may carry out legal punishments or chastise him; only the imam may do so.

Legal punishments cannot simply be abolished, for were they abolished, people would perish and countries would fall to ruin. Yet if everyone were permitted to carry them out, every person of violent or harmful intention would claim to be administering a lawful punishment, asserting that the one he struck or killed had committed some act that warranted it. But how can a man who himself incurs twenty legal punishments every day rule on legal punishments and be permitted to carry them out? Were that allowed for such a person, it would be permissible for every oppressor and rebel, and the result would be the destruction of the people. Rather, when a rebellious party carries out legal punishments and destroys lives, and the imam has power over them, he fines them for whatever harm they inflicted upon the righteous party, thereby preventing the wrongdoers from destroying the people and damaging their property.

Such people then claim that everything they did was in accordance with a legal rule enjoined upon them. But God, the Exalted, has addressed this directly:

> "Do you command people to do good and forget it yourselves?"

God, the Exalted, made clear that whoever is himself in need of being commanded to do good and restrained from evil is in no position to command others to good or forbid them evil. And God explained in another verse that the enjoining of good and the forbidding of evil (al-amr bi'l-maʿrūf wa nahy ʿan al-munkar) is a duty of the imams (عليهم السلام), when He said:

> "You are the best community (umma) that has been raised for the people. You enjoin good conduct and forbid evil."

They are the equitable leaders (a'imma al-wasat) who stand as witnesses over the people.

How can a man command good if he does not practice it himself? How can he forbid evil if he loves evil and commits it a hundred times a day? How is it permissible for their imams, when they commit daily the very acts that necessitate legal punishment, driven by lust and desire, and when they draw near to themselves those who love such acts and share in them, to forbid those same acts, all the while regarding those who perform them as elegant, victorious, agreeable, and refined? Whoever will not join them in drinking and debauchery they neither draw near nor employ, for such a man is disagreeable to them. Is that not itself a lesson to those they lead, and an incitement toward corruption and debauchery?

[65] We also say: it is universally agreed that legal judgement among people in matters of bloodshed, sexual offences, and property may be exercised only by one who knows the relevant cases and the legal opinions concerning them, who understands the legal norms, the Qur'anic revelation and its meaning, including the abrogating and the abrogated verses (al-nāsikh wa'l-mansūkh), and the analogical deduction (qiyās) in their view, and who fulfils its conditions, such that he can be trusted in rulings on bloodshed, property, and sexual offences. Does any of the usurpers who claim the imamate actually fulfil these conditions? On the contrary, every one of them depends on those so-called jurists who are said to possess knowledge yet lack its substance. The usurpers shower these men with the petty goods of this world, appoint them governors of provinces, and permit them to preside over public sessions. When an incident arises, they turn to the jurists for answers, and what do they produce? One says, "According to Abū Hanīfa, this man should be executed," while another says, "According to al-Shāfiʿī, he should not be executed." A third says, "According to Mālik, this woman should be divorced," and a fourth says, "According to Ahmad b. Hanbal, the woman is not forbidden to him." The usurpers handed the leadership of religious affairs to these jurists so that the jurists would judge according to their own desires and opinions, and in return the jurists would leave worldly authority to the usurpers and bear false witness on their behalf. In doing so, the jurists disgraced themselves through their compliance. I do not know which of the two is more astonishing: the one who shamelessly claims the imamate while possessing no knowledge whatsoever of religion or its legal norms, and therefore stands in need of such scholars, or the one who clings to his leadership and rewards their ignorance and their shame. If the jurists were true scholars, and authority genuinely rested upon their pronouncements, then they would be the imams, not the man who falsely claims the title while being nothing more than an idol set up with no real authority, capable neither of benefit nor of harm. Or should the greater wonder be reserved for him who adheres to such a leader and rewards his ignorance? In the end, their imam is the mirror image of the one he leads, and the one led is the mirror image of his imam.

[66] If their imams have no right to exercise legal judgement and authority (walāya), because they lack knowledge, fail to meet its conditions, and possess neither modesty nor integrity, then it must be noted that most adherents of the doctrine of choice (ikhtiyār) themselves hold that an imam who is unjust and immoral forfeits the imamate, and, in the view of some, ought to be removed from it. Likewise, a judge among them who betrays the people's trust should be removed from office, and a legal guardian should similarly lose his guardianship. Some go further, holding that the community is obliged to remove an imam who is unjust and immoral. Al-Shāfiʿī said: "A sinner has no legal guardianship; if he had a daughter, he would have no right to give her in marriage. That right would pass to the judge, not to him." And furthermore, according to all these jurist-imams, the testimony of a sinner is inadmissible. Thus, by agreement (ittifāq) and consensus (ijmāʿ), their imams are not among those qualified to exercise legal reasoning (ijtihād) in religion, nor to give their daughters in marriage, nor to have their testimony accepted, nor to carry out legal punishments.

These men have been notorious and infamous offenders, and those who came after them followed the same path. How could anyone possessing such attributes be the Commander of the Faithful, the vicegerent (khalīfa) of the Lord of the worlds, and an imam whose obedience is obligatory? If they hold no right of guardianship, legal judgement, or testimony, how can those they appoint exercise guardianship, and how can testimony be accepted from those they endorse? How can their recommendation of a man as fit for judgement, witnessing, and guardianship carry any weight at all? Is there anything in this world more remarkable than these people, than the contradictions in their rulings, the principles of their legal school (madhhab), and their doctrine? And is there anyone more infamous than their imam and the people he leads?

[67] Were we to enumerate every contradiction and disgrace embedded in their principles, this treatise would need to be extended far beyond its intended scope. What we have already said constitutes a sufficient refutation of their claim to the imamate and a clear demonstration that everything they have done and continue to do amounts to falsehood, injustice, and transgression. That alone is shame and ignominy enough for them. If their imamate is false, as we have shown, and if it is true that the imamate is one of the foundations of religion (uṣūl al-dīn), an inevitable necessity to which all revealed laws and norms are bound, then the imamate truly belongs to the pure imams from among the progeny of Tāhā and Yāsīn, who are protected from corruption and oppression, from lying and transgression, and purified of all filth and blame. What we have set out through rational deduction and through the religious law is sufficient.
