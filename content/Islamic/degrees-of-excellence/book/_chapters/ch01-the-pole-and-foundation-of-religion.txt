# The Pole and Foundation of Religion

In the Name of God, the All-Compassionate, the All-Merciful, from Whom we seek help in all matters.

Praise be to God, who sent messengers and prophets and appointed imams and caliphs as a blessing for His friends (awliyāʾ), a punishment to His enemies, a benefit for religion and the world, and an edifice ('imāra) for this present world and the world to come. I praise Him with the eulogy of one who is aware of the immense value of the bounty conveyed through them, who is grateful for it, and who journeys for God's sake, hastening toward it. I bear witness that there is no deity except God, with the testimony of a worshipper who understands the requirements of this profession and strives to fulfil its obligations. I bear witness also that Muhammad is His worshipper and Messenger, preferred above all His messengers and immune from all mistakes and errors, may God bless him with innumerable and infinite blessings. May God bless likewise his loyal and truthful legatee ['Alī], the adept in the clarification and the spiritual realities of the Qurʾan, and the imams descended from the martyr, to whom belong praise, glory, infallibility, and uprightness. May His most excellent salutations, His most bounteous blessings, and His purest greetings rest upon them, as long as tongues speak and darkness and light continue to alternate.

And now to the matter itself.

The imamate is the pole (quṭb) and foundation of religion (asās al-dīn) around which all religious and worldly affairs revolve, and it is a benefit for this present life and the life hereafter. Through it the affairs of God's servants are governed, the land prospers, and reward is accepted in the abode of return. It is through the imamate that knowledge of God's absolute unity (tawḥīd) and of the revealed Message is attained on the basis of proof, demonstration, and evidence, as is knowledge of the religious law, its foundations, its interpretation (taʾwīl), and its clarification.

I have said that the imamate is the pole and foundation of religion, and I have not given precedence to the messengership over the imamate, because in affirming (ithbāt) the imamate there is also an affirmation of the messengership. Whoever acknowledges the imam acknowledges the messenger, but not all who acknowledge the messenger acknowledge the reality of the imam. Proof of the messengership is needed for those who stand outside the domain of Islam, while affirming the imamate is necessary for Muslims, who are closer to the true believers. The struggle with them through debate and argument takes priority over the struggle against those who deny the messenger and the messengership. This is because the summons to Islam and to the messengership has already reached the polytheists and proof has been established for them, so that confronting them with the sword is more fitting. Moreover, the world can never be without an imam at any given moment, whereas the messenger is present at some times but not at others. Consequently, in affirming the imamate there is also an affirmation of the messengership.

God, the Exalted, called the imam a messenger when He said: "And remember in the Scripture, Ismāʿīl was indeed faithful in fulfilling his promise while he was a messenger, a prophet." Likewise, God called the messenger an imam when addressing Abraham: "Indeed, I am appointing you an imam for all humanity." This was after Abraham had completed his messengership, and God, the Exalted, said: "And when His Lord tried Abraham with His commands and he fulfilled them, He said: 'I am appointing you an imam for all humanity.'"

It is related from Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq (ع) that he said: "God chose Abraham first as a prophet (nabī), then as a friend (khalīl), then as a messenger (rasūl), and finally said: 'I am appointing you an imam for all humanity.'" God called Adam a vicegerent (khalīfa), and the title "vicegerent" is one of the names of the imams. God called David (ع) a vicegerent, while he was one of the imams of the past cycles, and He called the proofs (ḥujaj) "prophets." He called Muhammad and the other messengers "prophets," addressing them as prophets just as He addressed them as messengers. This is because the messenger first attains the rank of the imamate, and then, if the times require the establishment of a new religious law, he becomes the seventh imam of that cycle and undertakes the promulgation of that law. In this way the imam occupies the place of the messenger in his own age and time. Before arising to promulgate the religious law, the messenger is himself one of the imams, having assumed command from a previous imam; and after completing the religious law, he hands command over to the next imam. The imam is therefore always present in the world, and the world is never deprived of him. The imam upholds and maintains the religious law together with its esoteric reality, as I mentioned earlier. It is now clear that the progression of the religious cycle depends on the imam, and that it is the imam's prerogative how he acts upon the prophet's law during his time. No one can reach the prophet, attain his rank, or arrive at the proper understanding of the religious law, which is changed or replaced only by the imam, except through the imam. It is through the imam alone that one can attain the inner truth of the religious law, its interpretation, and its meaning.

Many works written by the elders of the daʿwa to affirm the imamate draw on deduction and on evidence from well-known Qurʾanic verses revealed concerning the imamate and the imams (عليهم السلام), as well as on traditions reported from the Prophet. Those who came before have left nothing in this matter for those who came after, and there is little point in simply restating the same ideas in different words. I therefore wish to write a treatise affirming the imamate by way of evidence (istishhād) and deduction (istidlāl) drawn from the outer horizons (al-āfāq) and our inner selves (anfus), from politics and governance (siyāsa) and from established norms (rusūm), by way of intellect ('aql), self-evidence (ḍarūra), innate constitution (al-fiṭra), natural disposition (al-jibla), and the agreement (al-ittifāq) of all religious communities (ahl kull milla), as well as from the philosophers (al-falāsifa) and the mathematicians (ahl al-ḥisāb). In this I follow the way of God, the Exalted, who said:

> سَنُرِيهِمْ آيَتِنَا فِى ٱلأٓفَاقِ وَفِىٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَهُمْ أَنَّهُ ٱلْحَقُّ ۗ أَوَلَمْ يَكْفِ بِرَبِّكَ أَنَّهُۥ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍۢ شَهِيدٌ

"We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and within themselves until it becomes manifest to them that it is the truth. Does it not suffice concerning your Lord that He is witness over all things?"

I shall further draw evidence from the structure of religious duties (farāʾiḍ) and practices in a manner that has not been attempted by the elders of the daʿwa. If what follows is found to be sound, it is owing to the excellence of support from the master of the age and the time (walī al-'aṣr wa'l-zamān). And if there is any error, it is due to my own weakness and shortcoming, and that is most fitting for one such as me.
