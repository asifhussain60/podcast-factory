# The Pole and Foundation of Religion

In the Name of God, the All-Compassionate, the All-Merciful, from Whom we seek help in all matters.

Praise belongs to God, who sent messengers and prophets and appointed imams and caliphs — a mercy to His friends (awliya), a punishment to His enemies, a benefit to religion and to the world, and an edifice (imara) upholding both this present world and the world to come. I praise Him as one who knows the immense worth of the bounty He conveys through them, who is grateful for it, and who travels for His sake and hastens toward it. I bear witness that there is no deity except God — the witness of a servant who understands what this profession requires and strives to meet its obligations. And I bear witness that Muhammad is His servant and Messenger, favored above all His messengers and kept free of every mistake and error; may God bless him with blessings beyond number and beyond end. May God likewise bless his loyal and truthful legatee [Ali], who is skilled in the clarification and in the inner realities of the Quran, and the imams descended from the martyr, to whom belong praise, glory, infallibility, and uprightness. May He likewise bless the master of the palace and the age [al-Hakim], upheld by conquest and victory. May God's finest salutations, His most generous blessings, and His purest greetings rest upon him, upon the heir-apparent of the Muslims (wali al-ahd al-muslimin), and upon the vicegerent (khalifa) of the Commander of the Faithful, for as long as tongues speak and as long as darkness and light continue to follow one another.

Now to the matter itself.

The imamate is the pole (qutb) and the foundation of religion (asas al-din) around which every religious and worldly affair turns, and it is a benefit to this present life and to the life to come. Through it the affairs of God's servants are governed, the land prospers, and reward is accepted in the abode of return. Through the imamate one attains, by proof, demonstration, and evidence, the knowledge of God's absolute oneness (tawhid) and of the revealed Message, together with knowledge of the religious law — its foundations, its interpretation (tawil), and its clarification.

I have called the imamate the pole and foundation of religion, and I have not placed the messengership ahead of it, because to affirm (ithbat) the imamate is already to affirm the messengership. Whoever acknowledges the imam acknowledges the messenger, yet not everyone who acknowledges the messenger acknowledges the true reality of the imam. Proof of the messengership is needed for those who stand outside the domain of Islam, while affirming the imamate is what Muslims require — and Muslims stand nearer to the true believers. To contend with them by debate and argument therefore takes priority over contending with those who deny the messenger and the messengership; for the call to Islam and to the messengership has already reached the polytheists and the proof has been established against them, so that meeting them with the sword is the more fitting course. Moreover, the world can never be without an imam at any moment, whereas the messenger is present at some times and absent at others. And so, to affirm the imamate is at the same time to affirm the messengership.

God, the Exalted, called the imam a messenger when He said: "And remember in the Scripture, Ismail was indeed faithful in fulfilling his promise while he was a messenger, a prophet." He likewise called the messenger an imam when He addressed Abraham: "Indeed, I am appointing you an imam for all humanity." This came after Abraham had completed his messengership, for God, the Exalted, said: "And when His Lord tried Abraham with His commands and he fulfilled them, He said: I am appointing you an imam for all humanity."

It is related from Jafar al-Sadiq (ع) that he said: "God chose Abraham first as a prophet (nabi), then as a friend (khalil), then as a messenger (rasul), and at last said: I am appointing you an imam for all humanity." God called Adam a vicegerent (khalifa), and "vicegerent" is among the names of the imams. He called David (ع) a vicegerent while David was one of the imams of the cycles that had passed, and He called the proofs (hujaj) "prophets." He called Muhammad and the other messengers "prophets," addressing them as prophets just as He addressed them as messengers. This is because a messenger first reaches the rank of the imamate, and then, should the age call for a new religious law to be set in place, he becomes the seventh imam of that cycle and takes up the promulgation of that law. In this way the imam stands in the place of the messenger in his own age and time. Before he rises to promulgate the religious law, the messenger is himself one of the imams, having received command from an imam before him; and once he has completed the religious law, he hands command to the imam who follows. The imam is thus always present in the world, and the world is never left without him. The imam upholds and preserves the religious law together with its inner reality, as I said earlier. It is now plain that the turning of the religious cycle depends upon the imam, and that how he acts upon the prophet's law in his own time is the imam's own prerogative. No one can reach the prophet, attain his rank, or arrive at a sound understanding of the religious law — which only the imam changes or replaces — except through the imam. Through the imam alone can one reach the inner truth of the religious law, its interpretation, and its meaning.

Many works composed by the elders of the dawa to affirm the imamate rely on deduction and on evidence drawn from the well-known Quranic verses revealed concerning the imamate and the imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ), and on the traditions reported from the Prophet. Those who came before have left nothing in this matter for those who come after, and there is little use in restating the same ideas in other words. I wish, therefore, to write a treatise that affirms the imamate by way of evidence (istishhad) and deduction (istidlal) drawn from the outer horizons (al-afaq) and from our own selves (anfus), from politics and governance (siyasa) and from established norms (rusum); by way of intellect (aql), self-evidence (darura), innate constitution (al-fitra), and natural disposition (al-jibla); and by the agreement (al-ittifaq) of every religious community (ahl kull milla), as well as of the philosophers (al-falasifa) and those who reckon with numbers (ahl al-hisab). In this I follow the way of God, the Exalted, who said:

> سَنُرِيهِمْ آيَتِنَا فِى ٱلأٓفَاقِ وَفِىٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَهُمْ أَنَّهُ ٱلْحَقُّ ۗ أَوَلَمْ يَكْفِ بِرَبِّكَ أَنَّهُۥ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍۢ شَهِيدٌ

"We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and within themselves until it becomes manifest to them that it is the truth. Does it not suffice concerning your Lord that He is witness over all things?"

I shall draw further evidence from the very structure of the religious duties (faraid) and practices, in a manner the elders of the dawa never attempted. If what follows proves sound, that is owing to the excellent support of the master of the age and the time (wali al-asr wal-zaman); and if any error appears in it, that is from my own weakness and shortcoming — and that is the most fitting thing for one such as me.

> **Editorial note (tradition-grounded).** 
> A related teaching preserved in this tradition holds that Adam was the first to draw the lines
> of religion in command and prohibition — a point that illuminates why this chapter can name Adam
> a vicegerent and count "vicegerent" among the names of the imams. That same tradition
> distinguishes the apparent laws the speaker-prophets set down from the inner meanings inferred
> beneath them, "the recommended meanings of the parables, called interpretation." This is the
> tawil the chapter says only the imam can open: the outer law is promulgated by the messenger in
> his cycle, while its interpretation and inner reality are preserved and unlocked through the
> imam who stands in the world at every moment. Read alongside that teaching, the chapter's claim
> that religion turns upon the imam is less a single assertion than the near end of a longer
> thread reaching back to the first command and prohibition.
