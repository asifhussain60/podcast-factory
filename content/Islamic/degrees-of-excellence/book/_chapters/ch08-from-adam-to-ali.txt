# From Adam to Ali

Section Seven


I say this: if it were acceptable for there to be creatures on earth without an imam or caliph, it would have been acceptable in the days of Adam (ع), when only the angels existed. Yet even then God, the Exalted, said:

> "Indeed, I am placing a vicegerent on earth. They said: Are You going to create one that will corrupt and shed blood on it while we glorify Your praises and regard You holy? He said: Surely, I know what you know not." (Quran 2:30)

In that passage God, may He be glorified, made plain that there would never be a time when the earth stood without an imam — not even if every one of its inhabitants were sinless and saintly. They too must recognize how indispensable the imam is, given their own ignorance, while most people, in truth, are corrupt. God, the Exalted, made something else plain in the same passage: that if it were acceptable for the imam to be chosen (ikhtiyar), the angels above all would have been granted that choice. God would not have overruled them, for they were immune from base inclinations, from false desires, and from any craving for worldly power and mastery. Since the choice was not granted to the angels, it cannot belong to those beneath them. This same meaning lies within the words of God, the Exalted, concerning the rights of parents:

> "Do not even say fie to them or tell them off." (Quran 17:23)

By forbidding the lesser wrong, God thereby forbade the harsher one of striking or abusing them.

God, the Exalted, has told the stories of past communities and their prophets so that people would see that the same pattern would run its course in the cycle of the Prophet Muhammad exactly as it had run in the cycles before him. The Prophet said: "My community will experience exactly what happened among the Banu Israil." He also said: "You will follow the footsteps of those before you, step by step in an identical manner (qudhdha bi-qudhdha), to the point that if they entered the hole of a lizard, you would enter it too."

In the story of Adam there is a sign that the imamate and the caliphate cannot be established by choice or by consultation. Had they been permissible by such means, the choice and consultation of the angels would have been the most fitting of all, for the angels were free of jealousy and rebellion. In that story there is also a sign that it is not for people to declare whether a given man is or is not fit for the imamate, or to set one candidate above another. God, the Exalted, showed those who objected to Adam how mistaken their position was; He reproached them, rebuked them, and exposed the falsehood of their words. He kept this account in the Quran so that people would take it to heart and would not dispute the imamate or contest the one whom God and His Messenger had chosen for it, as those angels had done. And in the appointment of Adam's son to the imamate there is a further sign that the world can never be without an imam. People accept the imams of Adam's age yet deny them in the age of Muhammad, may God bless him, even though God, the Almighty and Glorious, has declared:

> "Such was Our way (sunna) with the messengers We sent before you, and you will find no change in Our way." (Quran 17:77)

He also said:

> "You will find no change whatsoever in Our way (sunna)." (Quran 33:62)

What is meant here is the way of the imamate, not the way of ritual practice such as prayer, fasting, and the like. For the Prophet, may God bless him, did indeed change the practice of every prophet before him in the acts of worship (ibadat), yet he did not change the way of the imamate.

I say further: when Noah (ع) called the people to the ark out of compassion, he knew full well that those who stayed behind would perish and drown, by their own choice and by their conviction that their good lay elsewhere. The pattern is clear: every people whose prophet and imam summoned them to a matter, and who then refused it and turned away, were destroyed for that refusal. The ark was none other than the imam himself, and Noah's call to the people was a call to the imam — just as the Prophet said: "The likeness of my family (ahl al-bayt) among you is like Noah's ark: whoever boards it will be saved, and whoever stays behind will be drowned."

Noah (ع) appointed his son Shem (Sam) to the legateeship and the imamate by designation (nass) and appointment (tawqif), and after him the line ran on, one after another, until the time of Abraham (ع). Those who oppose us accept the imams of Noah's cycle yet deny them in the family of Muhammad, though the way has not been replaced or changed and people cannot do without an imam.

When Abraham (ع) arose, he commanded that fire be glorified. If what has been said of this is correct, then it was a symbol pointing to the glorification of the light of the imamate (nur al-imama), as I have already said. Then Abraham and Ismail (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ) built the House, the Kaba, and commanded the people to venerate it — itself a sign that the imamate was to dwell within Abraham's household and lineage. He urged the people to honor the imamate and to venerate and obey the imams of his family.

When Moses (ع) arose, he directed the people to face toward Jerusalem in prayer. Since Moses had no children or family in whom the imamate could continue, he pointed toward Jerusalem (bayt al-muqaddas), meaning that whoever among the Banu Israil possesses the sanctified spirit is your imam and your direction of prayer (qiblatakum). Then Jesus (ع) arose. He too had neither family nor children, and it is reported that he directed the people to face toward the east. If this is true, his command to face east meant turning toward the place from which the light of the imamate rises — that is, whoever radiates that light is your imam and your direction of prayer.

When the Prophet Muhammad, the seal of the prophets, may God bless him and his family, arose, he directed the people to turn away from Jerusalem toward the Kaba as the qibla of the Muslims. The Kaba had been built by Abraham and Ismail (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ). In giving this command, the Prophet showed that the direction had returned to the house of Abraham — that is, to the family of Ismail — and that the imamate had therefore returned to the children of Ismail, who are the children of the Prophet Muhammad, may God bless him. He commanded the people to venerate the House and to make the pilgrimage to it, a thing no prophet before him had commanded, so as to impress upon them the supreme excellence of the imams of Muhammad's family above every other imam of the cycles that had passed. Is there anyone in this world more iniquitous than this community? It accepted the imams of every prophetic age, affirmed that the world cannot be without an imam, and affirmed that no imam in any earlier cycle ever came to power by choice (ikhtiyar) — yet now it denies the imamate of Muhammad's family, rejects designation (nass), and calls instead for choice. God, the Exalted, has said:

> "Your Lord creates what He wills and chooses what is good for them." (Quran 28:68)

If the imamate were settled by choice, every people would simply choose whomever they pleased. The elders have treated the dispute between designation and choice at length. I have said that I mean to present what the elders left unaddressed in the books on affirming the imamate, and what I have said here is enough. I shall now establish that the imam must be the most excellent, the most knowledgeable, and the noblest of mankind.

I say that the virtues God, the Exalted, has named, and by which He has raised some people above others, are of three kinds (ashya): knowledge, jihad, and piety. God, the Exalted, said:

> "God has preferred those who strive and struggle over those who sit back by a great reward in degrees of rank." (Quran 4:95-96)

He also said:

> "Are those who know and those who do not, equal?" (Quran 39:9)

And:

> "Those who have been given knowledge have degrees of rank." (Quran 58:11)

And:

> "The most noble among you in the presence of God is the most pious among you." (Quran 49:13)

As for jihad, both the elite (khass) and the ordinary Muslims (amm) know well that these men never once fought in a single battle in person. The exploits of the Commander of the Faithful, Ali (ع), in war are famous and celebrated. The only mark these others left upon holy war was their flight at the battle of Hunayn, when Umar disobeyed the Prophet's plain order and counsel to hold the mountain pass and so drew the censure of the Muslims, while the Prophet and Ali (ع) fought on and cut the enemy down until they were routed.

As for piety (taqwa), where there is no knowledge there can be no piety, for piety comes through knowledge — through knowing what belongs to oneself and what belongs to others. It is reported that a God-fearing man (muhtasabin) once asked Muhammad b. al-Hasan, the companion of Abu Hanifa, to compile a book on asceticism. He compiled a book on trade instead, and when he was asked why, he replied: "Whoever has not mastered the law of buying and selling, how can he eat only what is lawful? And whoever does not eat only what is lawful has no asceticism." It follows, then, from the Quranic verses I have cited, that the imamate rightly belonged to the Commander of the Faithful (ع), for he had precedence (sabiq) in every virtue: he was the most knowledgeable, the most pious, and the one who fought most in the cause of Islam.

I maintain further that those who ruled never claimed excellence, knowledge, or piety for themselves. It was their companions who made those claims on their behalf. Indeed, Abu Bakr was the first to climb the pulpit and say: "You have appointed me, although I am not the best among you." If he spoke the truth, then those who claim greater excellence for him are liars. If he lied, then a liar cannot be an imam. And should it be said that he spoke out of humility, I answer that humility has its proper time and place. The Prophet himself, may God bless him, would speak openly of the rank and honor with which God had singled him out, and that was no arrogance. He said: "I am the master of the Arabs, without boasting"; "I am the master of humanity, without boasting"; and "I am the most eloquent of the Arabs, without boasting."

The Commander of the Faithful, Ali b. Abi Talib (ع), said: "Ask me before you lose me." He also said: "If a cushion were folded for me to sit upon, I would judge the followers of the Torah by the Torah, the followers of the Bible by the Bible, and the followers of the Furqan (Quran) by the Furqan." Many such sayings could be cited, and not one of them is arrogance or a denial of humility. Abu Bakr's words, "I have been put in charge of you although I am not the best among you," show that neither God nor His Messenger had granted him preference or designated him — not by ordering him to lead the prayer, nor in any other way. He confirmed of himself everything that was to be expected of him, and so he added: "Put me right." Correction of that kind is needed only in the case of grave and serious sins, since the lesser ones are wiped away by seeking forgiveness.

When Umar climbed the pulpit after Abu Bakr, he declared: "The oath (baya) to Abu Bakr was a falta, a hasty and unconsidered thing, but God has spared us its evil. Whoever repeats the like of it, kill him." In saying this, he himself tore down the literalists' claim that the Prophet's instruction to have Abu Bakr lead the prayer made his imamate binding and served as its designation. His statement also refutes what they cite from the traditions, such as: "Follow the two who come after me, Abu Bakr and Umar."
