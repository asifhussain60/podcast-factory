# From Adam to Ali

Section Seven

I say that if it were acceptable for there to be creatures on earth without an imam or caliph, this would have been acceptable in the time of Adam (ع), when only angels existed. Yet at that time God, the Exalted, said:

> "Indeed, I am placing a vicegerent on earth. They said: 'Are You going to create one that will corrupt and shed blood on it while we glorify Your praises and regard You holy?' He said: 'Surely, I know what you know not.'" (Qur'an 2:30)

God, may He be glorified, made plain in that passage that there would never be a time when the earth is without an imam, even if all its inhabitants were impeccable and saintly, so that they would recognise how indispensable the imam is even for them, given their ignorance, while most people are in fact corrupt. God, the Exalted, also made clear in that passage that if it were acceptable for the imam to be chosen (ikhtiyār), the angels would certainly have been granted that choice. God would not have overruled them, for they were immune from base inclinations, from false desires, and from cravings for worldly power and domination. Since choice was not acceptable for the angels, it is not acceptable for those beneath them. This meaning is implicit in the words of God, the Exalted, concerning the rights of parents:

> "Do not even say 'fie' to them or tell them off." (Qur'an 17:23)

By prohibiting the lesser, God thereby prohibited even the harsher treatment of striking them or abusing them.

God, the Exalted, has recounted the stories of past communities and prophets so that people would recognise that the same pattern would unfold in the cycle of the Prophet Muhammad as it did in earlier cycles. The Prophet said: "My community will experience exactly what happened among the Banū Isrā'īl." He also said: "You will follow the footsteps of those before you, step by step in an identical manner (qudhdha bi-qudhdha), to the point that if they entered the hole of a lizard, you would enter it too."

In the story of Adam there is an indication that the imamate and the caliphate cannot be established by choice or consultation. Had they been permissible by such means, the choice and consultation of the angels would have been the most fitting, since the angels were immune from jealousy and rebellion. In that story there is also an indication that it is not for people to declare whether a given person is or is not suitable for the imamate, or to prefer one candidate over another. God, the Exalted, informed those who objected to Adam of the error of their position, reproached them, and rebuked them, exposing the falsehood of their words. He preserved this account in the Qur'an so that people would take it as a lesson and would not dispute the imamate or contest the one whom God and His Messenger chose for it, as those angels had done. In the appointment of Adam's son to the imamate there is a further indication that the world can never be without an imam. All people accept the imams of Adam's period yet deny them in the period of Muhammad, may God bless him, even though God, the Almighty and Glorious, has declared:

> "Such was Our way (sunna) with the messengers We sent before you, and you will find no change in Our way." (Qur'an 17:77)

He also said:

> "You will find no change whatsoever in Our way (sunna)." (Qur'an 33:62)

What is intended here is the sunna of the imamate, not the sunna of ritual practices such as prayer, fasting, and the like. For the Prophet, may God bless him, did indeed alter the sunna of all previous prophets regarding acts of worship ('ibādāt), but he did not alter the sunna of the imamate.

I also say that when Noah (ع) called people to the ark out of compassion, he knew full well that those who stayed behind would perish and drown, by their own choice and by their conviction that their welfare lay elsewhere. The pattern is clear: every people summoned by their prophet and imam to a matter, who then refused and rejected it, were destroyed on account of that refusal. The ark was none other than the imam himself, and Noah's call to the people was a call to the imam, just as the Prophet said: "The likeness of my family (ahl al-bayt) among you is like Noah's ark: whoever boards it will be saved, and whoever stays behind will be drowned."

Noah (ع) appointed his son Shem (Sām) to the legateeship and the imamate by designation (naṣṣ) and appointment (tawqīf), and thereafter the line continued, one after another, until the time of Abraham (ع). Those who oppose us accept the imams of Noah's cycle yet deny them in the family of Muhammad, even though the sunna has not been replaced or altered and people cannot do without an imam.

When Abraham (ع) appeared, he commanded the glorification of fire. If what has been said about this is correct, then it was a symbolic indication of the glorification of the light of the imamate (nūr al-imāma), as I have already mentioned. Then Abraham and Ismā'īl (عليهم السلام) built the House, the Ka'ba, and commanded the people to venerate it, which was itself an indication that the imamate was to reside within Abraham's household and lineage. He urged the people to honour the imamate and to venerate and obey the imams of his family.

When Moses (ع) arose, he directed the people to face toward Jerusalem in prayer. Since Moses had no children or family in whom the imamate could continue, he pointed toward Jerusalem (bayt al-muqaddas), meaning that whoever among the Banū Isrā'īl possesses the sanctified spirit is your imam and your direction of prayer (qiblatakum). Then Jesus (ع) appeared. He had neither family nor children, and it is reported that he directed the people to face toward the east. If this is true, his command to face east meant turning toward the place from which the light of the imamate rises, that is, whoever radiates that light is your imam and your direction of prayer.

When the Prophet Muhammad, the seal of the prophets, may God bless him and his family, appeared, he directed people to turn from Jerusalem toward the Ka'ba as the qibla of the Muslims. The Ka'ba had been built by Abraham and Ismā'īl (عليهم السلام). In giving this command, the Prophet indicated that the direction had returned to the house of Abraham, that is, the family of Ismā'īl, and that the imamate had therefore returned to the children of Ismā'īl, who are the children of the Prophet Muhammad, may God bless him. He commanded the people to venerate the House and to perform the pilgrimage to it, something no previous prophet had commanded, as a way of impressing upon the people the supreme excellence of the imams from the family of Muhammad above all other imams in past cycles. Is there anyone in this world more iniquitous than this community, which accepted the imams of every prophetic period, affirmed that the world cannot be without an imam, and affirmed that there was never an imam in previous cycles who came to power by choice (ikhtiyār), yet now denies the imamate of Muhammad's family, rejects designation (naṣṣ), and calls for choice instead? God, the Exalted, has said:

> "Your Lord creates what He wills and chooses what is good for them." (Qur'an 28:68)

If the imamate were determined by choice, every people would simply choose whomever they wished. The elders have treated the debate between designation and choice at length. I have stated my intention to present what the elders have not addressed in the books on affirming the imamate, and what I have said here is sufficient. I shall now establish that the imam must be the most excellent, the most knowledgeable, and the noblest of mankind.

I say that the virtues God, the Exalted, has mentioned, and by which He has distinguished some people above others, are of three kinds (ashyā'): knowledge, jihād, and piety. God, the Exalted, said:

> "God has preferred those who strive and struggle over those who sit back by a great reward in degrees of rank." (Qur'an 4:95-96)

He also said:

> "Are those who know and those who do not, equal?" (Qur'an 39:9)

And:

> "Those who have been given knowledge have degrees of rank." (Qur'an 58:11)

And:

> "The most noble among you in the presence of God is the most pious among you." (Qur'an 49:13)

As for jihād, both the elite (khāṣṣ) and ordinary Muslims ('āmm) know well that these men never personally fought in a single battle. The military exploits of the Commander of the Faithful, 'Alī (ع), are well known and celebrated. The only legacy these others left in holy war was their flight at the battle of Hunayn, when 'Umar disobeyed the Prophet's explicit order and recommendation to hold the mountain pass and so earned the censure of the Muslims, while the Prophet and 'Alī (ع) continued to fight and cut down the enemy until they were routed.

As for piety (taqwā), where there is no knowledge there can be no piety, for piety comes through knowledge, through knowing what belongs to oneself and what belongs to others. It has been reported from Muhammad b. al-Hasan, Abū Hanīfa's companion, that a God-fearing man (muhtasabin) once asked him to compile a book on asceticism. He compiled instead a book on trade, and when asked to explain, he replied: "Whoever has not mastered jurisprudence, buying and selling, how can he consume only what is lawful? And whoever does not consume only what is lawful has no asceticism." It follows, therefore, from the Qur'anic verses I have cited, that the imamate rightly belonged to the Commander of the Faithful (ع), for he had precedence (sābiq) in every virtue: he was the most knowledgeable, the most pious, and the one who fought most in the cause of Islam.

I further maintain that those who ruled did not claim excellence, knowledge, or piety for themselves. It was their companions who made those claims on their behalf. Indeed, Abu Bakr was the first to ascend the pulpit and declare: "You have appointed me, although I am not the best among you." If he was speaking the truth, then those who claim superior excellence for him are liars. If he was lying, then a liar cannot be an imam. And if it is said that he spoke out of humility, I answer that humility has its proper time and place. The Prophet himself, may God bless him, would openly speak of the rank and status with which God had singled him out, and that was not arrogance. He said: "I am the master of the Arabs, without boasting"; "I am the master of humanity, without boasting"; and "I am the most eloquent of the Arabs, without boasting."

The Commander of the Faithful, 'Alī b. Abī Tālib (ع), said: "Ask me before you lose me." He also said: "If a cushion were folded for me to sit upon, I would pass judgement on the followers of the Torah according to the Torah, on the followers of the Bible according to the Bible, and on the followers of the Furqan (Qur'an) according to the Furqan." Many such examples could be cited, yet none of them constitutes arrogance or negates humility. Abu Bakr's words, "I have been put in charge of you although I am not the best among you," indicate that neither God nor His Messenger had granted him preference or designated him, whether by ordering him to lead the prayer or in any other way. He confirmed about himself all that was to be expected of him, and so he added: "Put me right." Correction of that kind is only necessary in the case of grave and serious sins, since lesser ones can be cancelled by seeking forgiveness.

When 'Umar ascended the pulpit after Abu Bakr, he declared: "The oath (bay'a) to Abu Bakr was a falta, a hasty, unconsidered decision, but God has averted its evil from us. Whoever repeats the like of it, kill him." In saying this, he himself demolished the claim of the literalists that the Prophet's instruction to have Abu Bakr lead the prayer made his imamate obligatory and served as a designation for it. Furthermore, his statement refutes what they cite in the traditions, such as: "Follow the two who come after me, Abu Bakr and 'Umar."
