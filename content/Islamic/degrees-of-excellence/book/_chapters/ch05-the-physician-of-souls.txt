# The Physician of Souls

We further say that human beings, at all times and in every moment, look after the welfare of animals, protecting them from what harms them and leading them toward what benefits them. A man guards their watering places and their grazing grounds, their seasons of mating and of bearing young; he shields them from destruction and from surroundings that would spoil their eating and drinking. Cattle often crave what would harm them, and a man holds them back, safeguarding their well-being even against the animals' own will.

In the same way, the imam (ع) protects the outcome of human affairs, their welfare, and all their conditions, their means of livelihood, their dealings, and their hidden interests, out of compassion for them. Were it not for the imam, a man would have no order in his worldly affairs, no safety or escape from those stronger than himself, no security from disputes, and no protection from punishment in the hereafter. What is more, a man endures the pain animals cause him and bears their bad behavior patiently, hoping they will improve; for the judgment of wisdom requires that one who possesses knowledge must not destroy, harm, or kill any living creature without good reason, but is bound instead to save it from destruction. In the same way, wisdom requires that property be protected from ruin and never be withheld from human use. So too the imam (ع) endures the misconduct of people and bears pain and hardship at their hands, since the principles of sovereignty and the wisdom of sound judgment require that the ignorant be guided, their welfare protected, and they themselves shielded from perdition and ruin, in the hope that they will one day return to good conduct and attain salvation. The imam does not look upon people with contempt for their ignorance or their disobedience; he looks upon them with an eye of mercy.

We also say that a man endures pain and hardship from his young children and prays for them, hoping they will grow into their full human potential and be rightly guided. In the same way, the imam (ع) endures pain and hardship from people out of mercy and compassion, a compassion like that of parents for their children. He provides for them in both worldly and religious matters, hoping they will be rightly guided and saved.

We say further that a teacher endures hardship for the sake of young boys, compelling them to learn the sciences and good conduct, while the child resists, resenting the teacher and preferring play and idleness. The teacher cares for the child and bears with him, knowing that if the child could see the benefit he stands to gain, he would not resent his teacher. The teacher also knows that when the boy comes of age he will recognize the worth of that discipline and feel grateful, regretting his earlier failings and disobedience. In the same way, the imam (ع) guides people toward their own worldly and religious good, even as they resist it and object to how he deals with them. In time, when the benefit becomes plain to them, they regret their disobedience.

We also say that because a man is made from various opposing natures, he is never wholly free of natural sickness, illness, and disease. The influence of time and place, of the celestial spheres and the stars, the effects of food and of other opposing elements in his nature, over-indulgence, too little eating, exhaustion, sexual intercourse, and other such things all give rise to illness and disease. Yet people had no knowledge of medicine. Had they stayed uninformed and ignorant in the face of so many illnesses, most of them would have died and perished, bringing ruin upon the world. But God, the Exalted, created a cure to match each illness, and He inspired the prophets and imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ), so that they came to understand the benefits of remedies and to recognize poisons and illnesses alike. They taught mankind, opening for them the way of medicine, so that people began to build on that foundation and to improve it through experience and practice, just as they made progress in every other trade, craft, and science, such as engineering, agriculture, navigation, and the rest, until physicians arose among mankind to treat the sick, gathering remedies from far and distant lands. They labored in that field, some for profit and some for the good of humanity, treating patients at times with wholesome and pleasant remedies and at other times with hot and bitter ones. A patient may loathe the bitter, foul-smelling substance that will cure him, yet the physician makes him drink it, willing or unwilling, assuring him of the good it will do, warning him that he faces death if he refuses, or gently coaxing him to endure the hardship. At times the physician forbids the patient to eat and drink, even while he craves both. He may forbid foods the patient enjoys and that agreed with him in health. He may give him something harsh that strains an already weakened body, all while the patient stands in danger of death. And if medicine alone fails, the physician may order lancing, the opening of a wound, cauterization, or the amputation of a limb, in the hope of saving the rest.

A person of sound mind will accept all of this, persuaded that the physician knows his condition better than he does himself and that the physician is a trustworthy guarantor who seeks only his welfare and his recovery. But a person lacking in reason will resent and despise the physician, while the physician meets him with understanding when he sees a man of cold constitution stricken with illness. Knowing that without treatment the patient will die, the physician, out of kindness and compassion, restrains him and forces him to drink the medicine. The patient may curse him, grow agitated, and threaten to strike him, but the physician bears it patiently, knowing that if the patient were aware and rational he would not resist treatment. The physician is not turned aside by the patient's bad behavior; he remains kind and continues treating him until his health is restored.

In the same way, spiritual diseases multiply. They arise from disputes among people; from their arbitrary desires and their leaning toward leisure, comfort, and self-display; from innovation, worldly ambitions, clashing doctrines, contradictory statements, and conflicting opinions; and from the lower self that goads a man toward evil, while the night is dark and the path obscure, and people know nothing of the medicine of the soul, nothing of its cure, of how to preserve its health, or of how to guard against the conditions and things that harm it, and nothing of the ways and paths of religion. Then humanity needs a guide and a physician to treat their illnesses, show them right action, and save their spirits. This must be done with gentleness, compassion, counsel, affection, forbearance, and encouragement, calling for many times more patience and compassion than the physicians of the body show. There must be no greed in their treatment, no seeking of personal gain, no warding off of harm for selfish ends. These guides must be the most reliable and trustworthy keepers of souls and of religion, surpassing even the most dependable physicians of the body in skill, experience, and knowledge.

For the risk and the danger in treating the soul are greater than those in treating the body, and errors in it can be far more severe. The need for such a guide is as inescapable to the intellect as the need for a physician is inescapable in practice. This knowing and compassionate physician of souls is the imam (ع) in every age and time. Were it not for the mercy, compassion, and kindness of the imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ) toward all of creation, people would perish in both their worldly and their religious affairs, in this life and the next. The imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ) treat created beings with delicate kindness, forbearance, and gentleness. They do not dwell on people's failure to obey, their bad manners, or their evil deeds; they look upon them instead with an eye of mercy, working to preserve their spiritual health and cure their illnesses, sometimes through a balanced and delicate diet, guarding their spiritual well-being through subtle sciences that strengthen the soul, at other times by withholding nourishment so that it neither sickens nor burdens. At still other times they compel people to drink a bitter, foul-smelling medicine so as to drive out the corrupting humors (al-kaymusat). At yet other times they impose a strict regimen until a person is brought to the utmost humility, all the while offering encouragement and warning, just as the Commander of the Faithful [al-Hakim] does with these people. Even as they go on desiring corruption and he sees no good in them, he remains patient with the pain they cause. We pray that He allows us to be among those who benefit from the treatment of the imam of the age (ع), and that He does not place us among those who require burning or the amputation of limbs through his turning away from us. Were it not for the mercy of the imams, not a single person would remain on the face of the earth, just as God has said:

> If God took humanity to task for what they deserve, He would not leave a [single] living creature on the surface of the earth ...
> (Quran, 35: 45)

We also say that wise men and men of insight, when they cultivate the world, regard their work as an image of God's own acts in creating the world, ordering its surroundings, and preserving human welfare. When they find good, fertile land, they know it will take seed and bring forth growth. When they discover water in distant deep valleys or beneath the ground, they labor to draw it out by digging canals and wells, directing that water to the fertile lands. They see no wisdom in leaving good, fertile land idle and wasted, or in leaving water untapped, for they are moved by compassion for living creatures and by concern for their welfare and the flourishing of the world.

In the same way, when the imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ) see people ready to receive the seed of wisdom and knowledge, even though they may be very far from truly being able to receive it, the imams pour great effort into digging channels through preparatory religious exercises on many occasions. People may not grasp the purpose of such an exercise until they come to see its wisdom and grow prepared and able to receive it. And when the imams cannot raise people to that level through religious exercise alone, they set wisdom within dense symbols, whether in words or in deeds, so that people may take in those symbols and gradually arrive at their meaning, and so be lifted to that level of understanding.

It is therefore necessary to believe in the supreme leadership, the imamate, of the imam of the age; to know that he is the head of the world and the vicegerent of God over His creation; and to raise no objection to any of the imams' commands, prohibitions, deeds, or teachings. One must know, in a general way, that all of this holds wisdom within it and is for the welfare of creation. The true meaning of a particular matter may at times be hard for a believer to grasp, but in the end its benefit will become clear, just as many things once unknown have in time become plain and clear. So too, in much of the sharia, the faithful do not at first understand the Lawgiver's purpose or the wisdom behind a ruling and may feel resistance to it; yet those to whom God grants recognition of its meaning come to perceive the wisdom within it. In the same way, many people meet things in the world whose wisdom they do not understand and wrongly conclude that no benefit lies in them, only to realize later that wisdom was there after all.

There are countless things whose very creation people count as harmful and destructive, seeing in them no wisdom and no benefit, yet when they come to understand their wisdom they grow calm and their objections fall away. Whoever recognizes the Wise One (al-Hakim) and grants that He is wise must inevitably grant that everything the Wise One does is full of wisdom, that He would do nothing devoid of wisdom, and that He would command, forbid, or say nothing except out of wisdom. A futile act is not permissible for the Wise One; and were it permissible for the Wise One to act, speak, command, or forbid without wisdom, that would be a vain act, and vain acts are not permissible for the Wise One. Whoever does a thing whose doing and whose leaving undone are entirely alike acts foolishly and steps outside the domain of wisdom.

Furthermore, if it is not permissible for the Wise One to perform an act whose existence and non-existence come to the same thing, how then could the imam perform an act when wisdom lies in something other than what he has done, or in its very opposite, and when the welfare of creation lies elsewhere? The imam is wise without qualification, and his bestowal of wisdom upon any of his servants makes them wise. Whoever acts without wisdom and goodness acts corruptly and in vain; and may God protect us from those who hold such a belief about the imam of the age. To hold such a belief is to say that this person has departed from wisdom, and whoever departs from wisdom has departed from the domain of his imamate, and that is a departure from the faith. May God protect us and all believers from any such doubt, notion, or belief, and may He grant us contentment and unquestioning submission (taslim).

We also say that free-grazing livestock, for all their own benefits and worth, cannot do without a shepherd to tend them, care for and protect them, choose their pasture and watering places, and guard them from thieves and beasts of prey. Without someone to take on that role, they would perish, unable to manage their own affairs, defend themselves against predators, or secure their own good and well-being.

People are likewise like sheep: without a shepherd to protect them, they would perish. The imam is their shepherd, safeguarding their worldly and religious affairs and protecting them from worldly and religious enemies alike. Were that role abandoned, not even two people would remain, nor would a single soul escape punishment. It is for this reason that people in every land are called subjects (raiya), because they live under the care of their shepherd, while the true shepherd is the imam, and all others who lay claim to that role are wolves devouring the flock.

We hold that the world is like the human body. If the body had no head, it would be a rotting corpse of no use to anyone. In the same way, if the world had no head, leader, or master (sais) to guide its worldly and religious affairs, people would turn into beasts devouring one another. The world would not last even a single day, and there would be no wisdom in its creation. The imam is indeed the head of the world, its leader and its master.

We say further that among the organs of the body there are disparities (tafawut) and differences in degrees of excellence (tafadul); the foremost and finest of them is the head. In the head resides the face, the face by which each human being is known and set apart from every other. Among the inner organs, the finest is the brain, their chief and the seat of the intellect. The imam holds a place in the world like that of the head among the limbs and like that of the brain among the inner organs. All the affairs of creation turn about him, just as all the actions of the body turn about the brain. Among all the faculties and spirits in a man, the finest is the intellect, and the imam is the Universal Intellect (aql al-kulli) in the world, the one in whom and through whom all the people of the world are made one.

As was said earlier, leadership in nature is present in everything. It is found among animals and birds: the stallion, for instance, protects and disciplines the mares. So too the rooster protects its fowl and disciplines it. Each species protects its young according to its capacity, defends its females, and guides the weak among them as each one needs, benefiting from and learning from those above it. The crane obeys its leader, and the bee obeys its queen.

God, the Exalted, created the world and ordered its creatures — as we have described in some measure earlier — according to this pattern of classification, disparity, and gradation in degrees of excellence, with varying levels of strength, nobility, and rank, so that it would be plain that within every species and genus there is a peak, a summit, and a chief in nobility and merit. By the same reasoning, all of mankind too must have a peak and summit in nobility and merit, to serve as their leader and guide in the world. It has thus been established that headship and leadership are necessary features of nature and creation, and of natural disposition and intuition as well, and that even those who deny revelation and religion cannot deny, whether by reason or by plain observation, that the world requires leadership, headship, and authority, for these are obligatory and necessary. It is therefore mandatory that this leader be the best of all people: the most noble, the most perfect, the most knowing, and the most pure, in keeping with the arguments we have drawn from creation and nature. The foremost must be the imam in all things, the chief and the head. The title supreme leader (al-imam) cannot be given to the one who is led (al-mamum), nor the title the excellent one (al-fadil) to the one who is preferred over others (al-mafdul), nor the title the one who supersedes (al-sabiq) to the one who is preceded (al-masbuq). Indeed, the people of every community and religion, philosophers, atheists, and those who deny God all attributes among them, affirm that the leader of the world ought to be the best of them, the most perfect in merit, and the most knowing of all people. And in all the world, no one fits this description except the imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ).
