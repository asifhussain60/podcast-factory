# The Physician of Souls

We further say that human beings, at all times and in every moment, look after the welfare of animals, protecting them from whatever harms them and leading them toward what benefits them. Man guards their watering places and grazing grounds, their seasons of reproduction and the bearing of offspring, shielding them from destruction and from environments that would harm their eating and drinking. Often cattle covet things that would harm them, and man prevents this, safeguarding their well-being even against the animals' own will.

Likewise the imam (ع) protects the outcome of human affairs, their welfare, all their conditions, means, transactions, and hidden interests, out of compassion for them. Were it not for the imam, man would have no order in his worldly affairs, no safety or escape from those stronger than him, no security from disputes, and no protection from punishment in the hereafter. Furthermore, man endures pain from animals and bears their bad behaviour with patience, in expectation of their improvement, for the judgement of wisdom requires that one who possesses knowledge must not destroy, harm, or eliminate any living creature without good reason. Rather, he is obliged to save them from destruction. Similarly, wisdom dictates that property must be protected from destruction and must not be rendered unavailable for human use. Likewise the imam (ع) tolerates the misconduct of people and bears pain and hardship from them, since the principles of sovereignty and the wisdom of sound judgement require that the ignorant be guided and their welfare protected, shielded from perdition and ruin, in the hope that they will eventually return to good conduct and attain salvation. The imam does not look upon people with contempt for their ignorance or their lack of obedience; rather, he looks upon them with an eye of mercy.

[35] We also say that a man tolerates pain and hardship from his young children and prays for them, hoping they will realize their human potential and be rightly guided. In the same way, the imam (ع) tolerates pain and hardship from people out of mercy and compassion, a compassion like that of parents for their children. He spends on them in both worldly and religious matters, hoping they will be rightly guided and saved.

[36] We say further that a teacher endures hardship on account of young boys, compelling them to learn the sciences and proper conduct, while the child resists this, resenting the teacher and preferring play and idleness. The teacher cares for the child and bears with him, knowing that if the child could see what benefit he would gain, he would not resent the teacher. The teacher also knows that when the boy reaches maturity, he will recognize the value of that discipline and feel gratitude, regretting his earlier failings and disobedience. Likewise, the imam (ع) guides humanity toward their own worldly and religious good, even as they resist it and object to how he deals with them. In time, when the benefit becomes clear to them, they regret their disobedience.

[37] We also say that because man is created from various opposing natures, he is never entirely free from natural sickness, illness, and disease. The influence of time, place, celestial spheres and stars, the effects of nourishment and other opposing elements in nature, over-indulgence, insufficient eating, exhaustion, sexual intercourse, and other such factors all give rise to illness and disease. Yet people were ignorant of medicine. Had they remained uninformed and ignorant in the face of so many illnesses, most people would have died and perished, bringing destruction upon the world. But God, the Exalted, created cures corresponding to each illness and inspired prophets and imams (عليهم السلام), so that they came to understand the benefits of medicines, recognizing poisons and illnesses alike. They taught mankind, opening up for them the way of medicine, so that people began to build upon that foundation and improved it through experience and practice, just as they made progress in all trades, crafts, and sciences such as engineering, agriculture, navigation, and others, until there arose among mankind physicians to treat people, collecting medicines from far and distant lands. They laboured in that field, some for profit and some for the welfare of humanity, treating patients at times with wholesome and pleasant remedies and at other times with hot and bitter ones. A patient may detest the bitter, foul-smelling substance that will cure him, yet the physician makes him drink it, willingly or unwillingly, assuring him of its beneficial outcome, warning him that he faces death if he refuses, or gently coaxing him to endure the difficulty. At times the doctor forbids the patient from eating and drinking, even while the patient craves both. He may prohibit foods the patient enjoys and that suited him in good health. He may administer something harsh that strains a weakened constitution, all while the patient is in danger of death. If medication alone fails, the physician may order lancing, the opening of a wound, cauterization, or the amputation of a limb in the hope of saving the rest.

A person of sound mind will accept all this, being persuaded that the doctor knows more about his condition than he does and that the doctor is a trustworthy guarantor who seeks only his welfare and recovery. But a person lacking in reason will resent and despise the doctor, while the doctor responds with understanding when he sees a man of cold constitution afflicted with illness. Knowing that without treatment the patient will die, the doctor, out of kindness and compassion, restrains the patient and forces him to drink the medicine. The patient may curse him, grow agitated, and threaten to strike him, but the doctor bears it with patience, knowing that if the patient were aware and rational he would not resist treatment. The doctor is not diverted by the patient's bad behaviour; instead, he remains kind and continues treating him until his health is restored.

[38] In the same way, when spiritual diseases multiply, arising from disputes among people, from their arbitrary desires, their inclinations toward leisure, comfort, and self-disclosure, from innovation, worldly ambitions, conflicting doctrines, contradictory statements, diverse opinions, and the lower self that incites men to evil, while the night is dark and the path obscure, and people are ignorant of medicine for the soul, ignorant of its cure, how to preserve its health, and how to guard against harmful conditions and things, ignorant too of the religious ways and paths, then humanity needs a guide and a physician to treat their illnesses, show them right action, and save their spirits. This must be done with gentleness, compassion, counsel, affection, tolerance, and encouragement, requiring many times more patience and compassion than that shown by physicians of the body. There must be no greed in their treatment, no seeking of personal gain, no deflection of harm for selfish reasons. These guides must be the most reliable and trustworthy custodians of souls and of religion, surpassing even the most dependable physicians of the body in their expertise, experience, and knowledge.

[39] For the risk and danger involved in treating the soul are greater than those of treating the body, and errors in it can be far more severe. The necessity of such a guide is as intellectually inescapable as the necessity of a medical doctor is practically inescapable. This knowledgeable and compassionate physician of souls is the imam (ع) in every age and time. Were it not for the mercy, compassion, and kindness of the imams (عليهم السلام) toward all of creation, people would perish in both their worldly and religious affairs, in this life and in the life to come. The imams (عليهم السلام) treat created beings with subtle kindness, tolerance, and gentleness. They do not dwell on people's deficiency in obedience, their bad manners, or their evil actions; instead they look upon them with an eye of mercy, working to preserve their spiritual health and cure their illnesses, sometimes through a balanced and delicate diet, protecting their spiritual wellbeing through subtle sciences that strengthen the soul, at other times by withholding nourishment so that it is neither nauseating nor burdensome. At still other times, they compel people to drink a bitter, foul-smelling medicine so as to expel the corrupting humours (al-kaymūsāt). At yet other times, they prescribe a strict regimen until the person is brought to a state of utmost humility, all the while offering encouragement and warning, just as the Commander of the Faithful [al-Hakim] does with these people. Even as they continually desire corruption and he sees no good in them, he remains patient with the pain they cause. We pray that He allows us to be among those who benefit from the treatment of the imam of the time (ع), and that He does not place us among those who require burning or amputation of limbs through his turning away from us. Were it not for the mercy of the imams, not a single person would remain on the face of the earth, just as God has said:

> 'If God took humanity to task for what they deserve, He would not leave a [single] living creature on the surface of the earth ...'
> (Qur'an, 35: 45)

[40] We also say that wise men and men of insight, in cultivating the world, consider their work analogous to God's actions in creating the world, arranging its environment, and preserving human welfare. When they find a good fertile land, they know it will receive seeds and bring forth growth. When they discover water in distant deep valleys or underground, they labour to bring it forth by digging canals and wells, directing that water to those fertile lands. They see no wisdom in leaving good fertile land unused and wasted, or in leaving water untapped, out of compassion for living creatures and concern for their welfare and the flourishing of the world.

Likewise, when the imams (عليهم السلام) see people ready to receive the seed of wisdom and knowledge, even though they may be very far from actually being able to receive it, the imams invest great effort in digging canals through preparatory religious exercises on many occasions. People may be unaware of the purpose of such an exercise until they come to perceive its wisdom and become prepared and capable of receiving it. When the imams cannot bring people to that level through religious exercise alone, they embed wisdom in dense symbols, whether in words or in actions, so that people may receive those symbols and gradually arrive at their meaning, and are thereby elevated to that level of understanding.

[41] It is therefore necessary to believe in the supreme leadership, the imamate, of the imam of the time, to know that he is the head of the world and the vicegerent of God over His creation, and not to object to any of the imams' commands, prohibitions, actions, or teachings. One must know, in general, that all of this embodies wisdom and is for the welfare of creation. The correct meaning of a particular matter may at times be difficult for a believer to grasp, but in the end its benefit will become clear, just as many things that were once unknown have eventually become apparent and clear. So too, in much of the sharīʿa, the faithful do not initially understand the Legislator's purpose or the wisdom behind a ruling and may feel resistance to it; yet those whom God grants recognition of its meaning come to perceive the wisdom within it. Similarly, many people encounter things in the world whose wisdom they do not understand and mistakenly conclude that no benefit lies in them, only to realize later that wisdom was indeed present, wisdom they cannot then deny.

There are countless things whose creation people regard as harmful and destructive, seeing no wisdom or benefit in them, yet when they come to understand their wisdom they grow calm and their objections fall away. Whoever recognizes the Wise One (al-Hakim) and acknowledges that He is wise must inevitably acknowledge that everything the Wise One does is full of wisdom, that He would not do anything devoid of wisdom, and that He would not command, prohibit, or say anything except out of wisdom. A futile action is not permissible for the Wise One; were it permissible for the Wise One to act, speak, command, or forbid without wisdom, that would constitute a vain act, and vain acts are not permissible for the Wise One. Whoever does something where the doing and the not doing are entirely equivalent acts foolishly and steps outside the domain of wisdom.

[42] Furthermore, if it is not permissible for the Wise One to perform an action whose existence or non-existence amounts to the same thing, how then could the imam perform an action when wisdom lies in something other than what he has done, or in its very opposite, and when the welfare of creation lies elsewhere? The imam is absolutely wise, and his bestowal of wisdom upon any of his servants renders them wise. Whoever acts without wisdom and goodness acts corruptly and in vain, and may God protect us from those who believe such a thing of the imam of the time. To hold such a belief is to say that the individual has departed from wisdom, and whoever departs from wisdom has departed from the domain of his imamate, and that is a departure from the faith. May God protect us and all believers from any such doubt, notion, or belief, and may He bestow upon us contentment and unquestioning submission (taslīm).

[43] We also say that freely-grazing livestock, though possessed of their own benefits and worth, inevitably require a shepherd to tend them, look after and protect them, select their pasture and watering places, and safeguard them from thieves and beasts of prey. Without someone to undertake that role, they would perish, unable to manage their own affairs, defend themselves against predators, or secure their own good and well-being.

People are likewise like sheep: without a shepherd to protect them, they would perish. The imam is their shepherd, safeguarding their worldly and religious affairs and protecting them from worldly and religious enemies alike. Were that role to be abandoned, not even two people would remain, nor would a single individual escape punishment. It is for this reason that people in all lands are called subjects (raʿīya), because they are under the care of their shepherd, while the true shepherd is the imam, and all other claimants to that role are wolves devouring the flock.

[44] We maintain that the world is like the human body. If the body had no head, it would be a decaying corpse of no use to anyone. Similarly, if the world had no head, leader, or master (sāʾīs) to guide its worldly and religious affairs, people would become beasts devouring one another. The world would not endure even a single day, and there would be no wisdom in its creation. The imam is indeed the head of the world, its leader and its master.

[45] We say further that there are disparities (tafāwut) and differences in degrees of excellence (tafāḍul) among the organs of the body; the foremost and finest among them is the head. It is in the head that the face resides, the face by which each human being is recognized and distinguished from all others. Among the internal organs, the finest is the brain, which is their chief and the seat of the intellect. The imam holds a position in the world analogous to that of the head among the limbs, and analogous to the brain among the internal organs. All the affairs of creation revolve around him, just as all the actions of the body revolve around the brain. Among all the faculties and spirits in man, the finest is the intellect, and the imam is the Universal Intellect (ʿaql al-kullī) in the world, the one in whom and through whom all people of the world become united.

[46] As mentioned earlier, leadership in nature is present in everything. It is found among animals and birds: the stallion, for instance, protects and disciplines the mares. Likewise, the rooster protects its fowl and disciplines it. Each species protects its young according to its capacity, defends its females, and guides the weak among them as each individual needs, benefiting from and learning from those above. The crane obeys its leader, and the bee obeys its queen.

[47] God, the Exalted, created the world and arranged its creatures, as we described to some extent earlier, according to this pattern of classification, disparity, and gradation in degrees of excellence, with varying levels of strength, nobility, and rank, so that it would be clear that within every species and genus there is a peak, a summit, and a chief in nobility and merit. By the same reasoning, all of mankind must likewise have a peak and summit in nobility and merit to serve as their leader and guide in the world. It has thus been established that headship and leadership are necessary features of nature and creation, as well as of natural disposition and intuition, and that even those who deny revelation and religion cannot deny, either rationally or by direct observation, that the world requires leadership, headship, and authority, for these are obligatory and necessary. It is therefore mandatory that this leader be the best among all people: the most noble, the most perfect, the most knowledgeable, and the most pure, in accordance with the arguments we have drawn from creation and nature. The foremost must be the imam in all things, the chief and the head. The title 'supreme leader' (al-imām) cannot be applied to the one who is led (al-maʾmūm), nor the title 'the excellent' (al-fādil) to 'the one who is preferred over others' (al-mafdūl), nor the title 'one who supersedes' (al-sābiq) to 'one who is preceded' (al-masbūq). Indeed, people of every community and religion, including philosophers, atheists, and those who deny God all attributes, affirm that the leader of the world ought to be the best among them, the most perfect in merit, and the most knowledgeable of all people. In all the world, no one fits this description except the imams (عليهم السلام).
