# Governance and the First Principle

The philosophers, I say, divided politics or governance (siyāsa) into three categories: governance of the individual (siyāsat al-khāṣṣa), governance of the family (siyāsat al-ḥāmma), and governance of the general public (siyāsat al-'āmma). Governance of the individual concerns a person's rule over himself. Governance of the family concerns a man's management of his household and dependents. Governance of the general public concerns the administration of cities and provinces. To govern himself properly, a person must restrain himself from all vices, bad traits of character, bestial habits, and blameworthy desires, ruling his own soul with firm resolve, reproaching and repenting when it errs, and praising it and holding it steady in good conduct when it does well. To govern his family is to manage his household and dependents by protecting their welfare, encouraging them to acquire virtues and praiseworthy character, correcting those who err through reprimand and similar means, and rewarding those who do well with praise and the like.

[49] Governance of the general public means that a man governs cities, safeguards the welfare of people's livelihoods, and keeps them from reprehensible morals and bad conduct. He protects everyone in their proper stations, corrects those who err through reprimand, rewards those who do well through praise, and oversees the affairs of their religion and their hereafter. This is what the philosophers call governance, and yet it is found nowhere in the world among those who falsely claim the imamate, nor among rulers, nor among their scholars. This quality exists only in the imams from the family of Muhammad (عليهم السلام), and no one else in the world is capable of bearing this responsibility.

The philosophers agree that whoever cannot govern himself has no business governing his family, and whoever cannot manage his own family well has no claim whatsoever to governance of the general public. Yet the literalists (ahl al-ẓāhir), their rulers, and their scholars do not possess this qualification. Those who have occupied the place of the imams and taken on their titles cannot govern themselves, their families, or the people. They are the ones we see today engrossed in amusement, moral depravity, and corruption. The qualities by which the philosophers define the political leader are found in none of these rulers. It seems evident that the author of this classification intended to encourage obedience to the imam, for he described the imam using the attributes of the ideal ruler, so that people would seek a ruler who met precisely this description. Whoever failed to meet it would not be accepted, until at last the people found the true imam.

[50] I also say that since human beings are born ignorant, possessing no knowledge, and since knowledge does not arise in them from nature, nor does speech, they stand in need of instruction. The proof that knowledge does not come from nature is this: if it did, people would naturally be scholars and would speak naturally, just as donkeys bray and dogs bark without any instruction, for it is plain that these animals require no teaching for those sounds. The braying of donkeys and the barking of dogs is identical whether in Hind, Sind, Rūm, or the land of the Arabs.

Further, if speech and knowledge were natural, as I have just noted, people would need no instruction for them, and all people would be equal in these respects with no differences among them, just as the braying of donkeys does not differ from one place to another. Since people do need instruction, each learns from another, in a chain that leads back to the one who received no instruction from any human being but received his knowledge through divine inspiration rather than teaching. This deficiency in human beings and their need for instruction necessitates a teacher, which in turn necessitates an ultimate end-point in the chain of teachers, namely the prophet and the imam (عليهم السلام). This is among the most compelling and convincing proofs for confirming the imamate and the prophethood.

[51] I also say that God, the Exalted, created human beings deficient and ignorant so that they would be receptive to knowledge and wisdom. Had He left them to themselves, there would have been no obligation upon them, no deeds to perform, no reward, and no punishment, and there would have been no wisdom in creating them at all. So when God inspired the prophets and imams with wisdom and knowledge, they in turn conveyed it to mankind, explaining their guidance and their path to the Truth, and laying upon them obligations, commands, and prohibitions.

Moreover, were it not for the imams, humanity would bear no responsibility, and there would be no guarantor or proof over them. The imams are therefore God's proofs to His creation at all times, from the very beginning of their moral responsibility and whenever they fall into disagreement. If people fell into sectarian conflict, each group citing a verse of the Qur'an, a hadith, or analogical reasoning, no proof would be established for any of them against the others. If there were no imam in the world to rescue them from disagreement, confusion, and perplexity and to guide them to the Truth, then God would have no proof against His creatures. Rather, the creatures would have a proof against God, for they would say: "We found no one to guide us from falsehood to the truth." It is through the imam, therefore, that God's proof is established over the people, just as God, the Exalted, said:

> "Say, to God belongs the most apparent proof." (Qur'an, 6:149)

[52] I also say that the rules and principles attributed to the sages are, in reality, the teachings of the imams (عليهم السلام). From them, people derived the foundations of every branch of knowledge and wisdom, including arithmetic and geometry. Each discipline has a purpose and an ultimate point that leads back to the imam. Among geometric shapes, the squares, triangles, arcs, and all the rest, the highest is the circle. The circle is the best and most perfect of all shapes, and it is the foundation from which all other shapes can be derived: the square, the triangle, and the arc alike. When a circle is divided, a square appears and arcs remain at the edges, and if we extend the horizontal and the vertical lines, four triangles emerge. All other shapes are derived from the circle, and all shapes are contained within it. The relation of shapes to one another mirrors the world and creation. Among shapes, the circle serves as a proof and symbol of the imam, who is the circle of religion (dā'irat al-dīn). All the ḥudūd, the ranks of the hierarchy, fall within his command and prohibition and draw their benefit from him. The circle has no need of any other shape, while every other shape needs it, just as the imam has no need of any other rank in the hierarchy in his age and time, while all the ranks need him, and he encompasses them all.

[53] I also say that the origin and source of numbers is the number one. It is the most perfect of numbers, independent of two and of everything above it up to a thousand, while the thousand and everything below it depends upon the number one. The imam is uniquely one in his age and time: all the ranks of the hierarchy need him, while he needs none of them. Some people may suppose that the number one points to the Creator of all creatures, but this is mistaken, for the number one is the cause of mathematics and the foundation of numbers, since two is one plus one, and three is one plus one plus one, and so on. Rather, the one truly signifies the imam in his rank and station, unique and unequalled. No other creature in his age and time shares his position.

[54] I also say that the dot, which the mathematicians describe as having no parts and as the origin of the line, the surface, and the body, is a symbol of the imam, whose rank no one can attain and who cannot be truly known. No one can adequately describe his attributes; people can only attest to their own inability to do so.

[55] Further, I say that among all the days Saturday holds the greatest distinction, and among all the months Ramadan is the finest, and within each day there is one hour in which supplication (du'ā') is answered. Every genus and species in the world, whether created or posited, has a peak and a summit in nobility and merit that none other can reach, and this points to the imam, as I have already explained. Were I to mention everything that deserves mention in this chapter, this treatise would far exceed its proper scope. What I have already set down should suffice for those who reflect carefully, enabling them to draw analogical conclusions from the principles and branches I have outlined. I shall, however, mention a few further points in brief (nuktatan 'alā al-ikhtiṣār), drawing on the obligatory religious duties (farā'id) and their structure.
