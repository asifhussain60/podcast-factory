# Governance and the First Principle

The philosophers, I say, divided politics, or governance, into three kinds: governance of the individual (siyasat al-khassa), governance of the family (siyasat al-hamma), and governance of the general public (siyasat al-amma). The governance of the individual is a person's rule over himself. The governance of the family is a man's management of his household and dependents. The governance of the general public is the administration of cities and provinces. To govern himself well, a person must hold himself back from every vice, every flaw of character, every beastly habit, and every blameworthy desire. He must rule his own soul with firm resolve, rebuking it and turning it to repentance when it errs, and, when it does well, praising it and holding it steady in good conduct. To govern his family is to manage his household and dependents: to guard their welfare, to urge them toward the virtues and praiseworthy character, to correct those who err by reprimand and the like, and to reward those who do well with praise and the like.

The governance of the general public means that a man governs cities, safeguards the welfare of the people's livelihoods, and keeps them away from base morals and bad conduct. He protects everyone in their proper stations, corrects those who err by reprimand, rewards those who do well by praise, and watches over the affairs of their religion and their hereafter. This is what the philosophers call governance, and yet nowhere in the world is it found among those who falsely claim the imamate, nor among the rulers, nor among their scholars. This quality belongs only to the imams from the family of Muhammad (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ); no one else in the world is able to carry this charge.

The philosophers agree that a man who cannot govern himself has no business governing his family, and that a man who cannot manage his own family well has no claim at all to the governance of the general public. Yet the literalists (ahl al-zahir), their rulers, and their scholars do not hold this qualification. Those who have taken the place of the imams and borne their titles can govern neither themselves, nor their families, nor the people. They are the very ones we see today sunk in amusement, moral corruption, and depravity. Not one of these rulers has the qualities by which the philosophers define the political leader. It seems plain that whoever devised this classification meant to lead people toward obedience to the imam, for he described the imam by the attributes of the ideal ruler, so that people would seek a ruler who fit exactly this description. Whoever fell short of it would not be accepted, until at last the people found the true imam.

I say also that human beings are born ignorant, holding no knowledge, and knowledge does not arise in them by nature, nor does speech; so they stand in need of instruction. The proof that knowledge does not come by nature is this: if it did, people would be scholars by nature and would speak by nature, just as donkeys bray and dogs bark with no instruction at all, for it is clear that these animals need no teaching for those sounds. The braying of the donkey and the barking of the dog are one and the same whether in Hind, in Sind, in Rum, or in the land of the Arabs.

Further, if speech and knowledge were natural, as I have just said, people would need no instruction for them, and all people would be equal in these matters with no differences among them, just as the braying of donkeys does not differ from one place to another. Since people do need instruction, each learns from another, in a chain that leads back to the one who received no instruction from any human being but received his knowledge by divine inspiration rather than by teaching. This deficiency in human beings and their need for instruction makes a teacher necessary, and this in turn makes necessary a final end-point in the chain of teachers, namely the prophet and the imam (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ). This is among the strongest and most convincing proofs for confirming the imamate and the prophethood.

I say also that God, the Exalted, created human beings deficient and ignorant so that they would be ready to receive knowledge and wisdom. Had He left them to themselves, no obligation would lie upon them, there would be no deeds to perform, no reward, and no punishment, and there would be no wisdom in creating them at all. So when God inspired the prophets and the imams with wisdom and knowledge, they in turn conveyed it to mankind, setting out their guidance and their path to the Truth, and laying upon them obligations, commands, and prohibitions.

Moreover, were it not for the imams, humanity would bear no responsibility, and there would be no guarantor and no proof set over them. The imams, therefore, are God's proofs to His creation at all times, from the very beginning of their moral responsibility and whenever they fall into disagreement. If people fell into sectarian strife, each group citing a verse of the Quran, a hadith, or an analogy, no proof would stand for any of them against the others. If there were no imam in the world to rescue them from disagreement, confusion, and bewilderment and to guide them to the Truth, then God would have no proof against His creatures. Rather, the creatures would have a proof against God, for they would say: "We found no one to guide us from falsehood to the truth." It is through the imam, then, that God's proof is established over the people, just as God, the Exalted, said:

> "Say, to God belongs the most apparent proof." (Quran, 6:149)

I say also that the rules and principles ascribed to the sages are, in truth, the teachings of the imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ). From them people drew the foundations of every branch of knowledge and wisdom, arithmetic and geometry among them. Each discipline has a purpose and a final point that leads back to the imam. Among the geometric figures — the squares, the triangles, the arcs, and all the rest — the highest is the circle. The circle is the best and most perfect of all figures, and it is the foundation from which every other figure can be derived: the square, the triangle, and the arc alike. When a circle is divided, a square appears and arcs remain at the edges; and if we extend the horizontal and the vertical lines, four triangles come forth. Every other figure is derived from the circle, and every figure is contained within it. The relation of the figures to one another mirrors the world and creation. Among the figures, the circle serves as a proof and a symbol of the imam, who is the circle of religion (dairat al-din). All the hudud (الْحُدُود), the ranks of the hierarchy, fall within his command and his prohibition and draw their benefit from him. The circle needs no other figure, while every other figure needs it, just as the imam needs no other rank of the hierarchy in his age and his time, while all the ranks need him, and he encompasses them all.

I say also that the origin and source of numbers is the number one. It is the most perfect of numbers, standing independent of two and of everything above it up to a thousand, while the thousand and everything below it depends upon the number one. The imam is uniquely one in his age and his time: all the ranks of the hierarchy need him, while he needs none of them. Some may suppose that the number one points to the Creator of all creatures, but this is mistaken, for the number one is the cause of mathematics and the foundation of numbers, since two is one plus one, and three is one plus one plus one, and so on. Rather, the one truly signifies the imam in his rank and his station, unique and without equal. No other creature in his age and his time shares his place.

I say also that the dot, which the mathematicians describe as having no parts and as the origin of the line, the surface, and the body, is a symbol of the imam, whose rank no one can attain and who cannot be truly known. No one can rightly describe his attributes; people can only confess their own inability to do so.

Further, I say that among all the days Saturday holds the greatest distinction, and among all the months Ramadan is the finest, and within each day there is one hour in which supplication (dua) is answered. Every genus and every species in the world, whether created or posited, has a peak and a summit in nobility and merit that none other can reach, and this points to the imam, as I have already explained. Were I to set down everything that deserves mention in this chapter, this treatise would run far beyond its proper bounds. What I have already put down should be enough for those who reflect with care, allowing them to draw conclusions by analogy from the principles and the branches I have outlined. I shall, however, mention a few further points in brief (nuktatan ala al-ikhtisar), drawing on the obligatory religious duties (faraid) and their structure.
