# The Disciplines of the Path

## Section 12 — The Call to Action and Night Prayer

If knowledge alone were enough — if understanding something were the same as becoming it — then the night call would be unnecessary. The herald who cries out in the dark, *Is there any who asks? Is there any who seeks forgiveness? Is there any who repents?* — why would that call go out, night after night, if the answer were already written in the books you read by day?

It would not. And it does.

The companions of the Prophet mentioned Abdullah ibn Umar in his presence. He spoke of this man with warmth — *an excellent man* — and then paused, and added the qualification that changes everything: *if only he would pray at night.* Not: if only he were more learned. Not: if only he had studied longer or understood more finely. If only he would pray at night. The night revealed something that the daylight hours did not require, and that the accumulated learning did not provide.

He also said to one of his companions: *O so-and-so, do not increase sleep at night — for much sleep at night leaves its owner poor on the Day of Resurrection.*

Poverty is a particular word. Not punished, not diminished. *Poor* — arriving at that accounting without the currency you needed, having spent your capital on sleep.

## Section 13 — The Voices of Dawn

God loves three voices: the voice of the cock, the voice of the one who recites the Quran, and the voice of those seeking forgiveness in the early morning.

I have thought about this for a long time — that the cock is in this list. The animal that cries at dawn because that is simply what it does, without deliberation, without a theory of why dawn is worthy of announcement. And the human being who oversleeps the crow of that same bird is, by the very comparison, revealed as having made a choice the animal has not.

Sufyan al-Thawri said that God created a breeze that blows at the time of dawn and carries the invocations and petitions for forgiveness upward to the Supreme King. Whether you understand this literally or as a description of something about that particular quality of attention that the pre-dawn hour makes possible — the image is the same. There is something happening at dawn that does not happen at noon. And the people who are awake for it receive something the people sleeping through it do not.

The herald's four calls mark the night's movement. At nightfall: *Up, let the worshippers arise.* At midnight: *Up, let the fully devoted arise.* At dawn: *Up, let those asking forgiveness arise.* And when day breaks, the final call: *Up, let the heedless arise* — and they rise from their beds as the dead are raised from their graves.

The heedless also rise. The night does not discriminate. But they rise last, having missed the three earlier calls, like latecomers to a gathering where the essential conversation has already taken place.

## Section 14 — Luqman's Admonition and the Lover's Lament

Luqman the Wise, in his testaments to his son, said this: *O my son, let not the cock be more clever than you — he cries at the time of dawn while you are sleeping.*

The cock does not know theology. The cock does not know the elaborate architecture of legal opinion or the subtle gradations of spiritual states. The cock simply responds to the light before it arrives. And the student who has spent years in the most sophisticated intellectual training available sleeps through the moment the cock has already answered.

Someone wrote a poem about this, and I have kept it:

*The pigeon cooed in the darkness of night*
*On a branch, in weakness, while I was asleep.*
*I lied — for I swear that were I truly His loved,*
*Not the pigeons alone, but I too would weep.*
*I think I am lovesick, excessively lovelorn*
*For my Lord — but I weep not, though animals weep.*

The self-indictment of that poem is precise. The speaker claims to love God. The speaker claims a special relationship, a spiritual intensity. And then the pigeon coos in the dark, and the speaker remains asleep, and the claim of love is exposed. An animal is a more faithful lover. That recognition — held honestly rather than defended against — is itself a beginning.

## Section 15 — The Essence of Obedience and Worship

Obedience is conforming to the law of the Prophet in commands and prohibitions, in word and in deed. Whatever you say and do and refrain from must be in emulation of the lawgiver. The form is not enough if the substance is absent. And the substance is not enough if it violates the form.

I offer two examples to make this concrete. If you fast on the day of Eid al-Fitr — the feast day — you are not worshipping: you are rebelling, because the fast on that day is prohibited regardless of your sincere intention. If you pray in a garment acquired through violence — the prayer has the outward form of worship, and yet you sin. Intention without lawfulness is insufficient. Lawfulness without correct intention is performance. The path requires both, simultaneously, held together.

This is harder than it sounds. Not because the rules are complicated — most people with adequate education know them — but because holding intention and form together across the length of a life, in the privacy of moments when no one is watching, is a discipline of the entire self rather than the application of a rule.

## Section 16 — The True Path of Sufism

It is essential that your words and deeds align with the law. Without that alignment, whatever you claim about your inner state is, at best, self-deception.

Do not be misled by the ecstatic utterances and the vehement cries of those who perform spiritual intensity rather than practicing it. Walking this road is by struggle — by the difficult, daily work of cutting off what your soul lusts after and refusing it what it desires, disciplining it repeatedly and without theater. It is not achieved through dramatic displays that impress observers. It is not accomplished through idle words about states you have not reached.

The loosened tongue paired with a veiled and negligent heart is a sign of misery, not attainment. You will not illuminate your heart by the lights of knowledge until you have silenced the noise the unreformed soul makes.

There are questions you have asked me that cannot be answered in writing. Not because the knowledge is being withheld, but because what you are asking about is only known by experience, and experience cannot be transcribed. The sweetness of sweetness is known by tasting it — not by reading a description of sweetness, however accurate. There is a kind of wisdom that waits on the other side of the doing, and no amount of reading about it can substitute for arriving at it. Work by what you know. What you do not know will be revealed to you.

## Section 17 — Four Essentials for the Seeker of Truth

The traveler on this path needs four things. Not many things — four specific things.

First: a true conviction with no heresy in it. A belief that is solid, examined, yours — not borrowed for convenience or performance.

Second: a sincere repentance after which you do not return to sin. Not a repentance that is really a pause. A genuine turning, after which the pattern changes.

Third: the satisfaction of adversaries — settling whatever claims others have against you so that you arrive at the final accounting without those debts outstanding.

Fourth: enough knowledge of the sacred law to perform your obligations correctly, and enough knowledge of what awaits you to make the journey seriously.

Shibli said that he had served four hundred teachers and read four thousand traditions, and then selected from all of them a single tradition by which he worked, and let the rest go — because he found his salvation in it, and all the wisdom of the ancients was included within it. The tradition was the Prophet's counsel to one of his companions: *Work for your world according to your position in it, and work for your other world according to the length of your remaining in it; and work for God according to your need of Him, and work for the fire according to your endurance in it.*

Four calibrations. The first two manage the proportion of worldly effort to otherworldly effort. The third is the one most likely to be skipped: work for God according to your *need* of Him — which is total. The fourth is the one most likely to produce a change in behavior on first hearing it: work for the fire according to your ability to endure it. Sit with that last phrase and feel what it asks.

## Section 18 — Hatim al-Asamm's Eight Benefits of Knowledge

Hatim al-Asamm spent thirty years with his teacher Shaqiq al-Balkhi. One day Shaqiq asked him: What have you actually gained? Hatim said he had gained eight things from science, and hoped his salvation was in them. Shaqiq said: What are they?

I want to give you Hatim's answer in his own words, as closely as the translation allows, because the eight observations are a complete structure and I would damage them by paraphrasing.

*The first: I observed mankind and saw that everyone had something they loved and longed for. Some of what they loved accompanied them to the illness of death; some accompanied them to the edge of the grave; then each turned and left the person alone. What entered the grave and gave comfort there was only good works. So I took good works as my beloved, to be a light for me in my grave.*

*The second: I saw people following their lusts and hastening toward the desires of their souls. I meditated on the verse: But as for whoever has feared the majesty of his Lord, and has restrained his soul from lust, truly the Garden shall be his dwelling. I was convinced the Quran was sincere truth, so I hastened to deny my soul its desires until it became trained in obedience.*

*The third: I saw everyone struggling to collect the vanities of the world. I reflected on the verse: What is with you vanishes, but what is with God abides. So I gave away my worldly possessions for God's sake and distributed them to the poor, as a treasure stored with God.*

*The fourth: I saw some people thinking their honor lay in the multitude of their family and kinsfolk; others in wealth and children; others in violence and power; others in squandering. I meditated on: The most worthy of you in God's sight is the one who fears Him most. So I chose reverent fear.*

*The fifth: I saw people censuring and slandering one another, and I traced this to envy over wealth and rank and knowledge. I meditated on: It is We who divide their substance among them in this world's life. I knew the division was from God, so I ceased envying anyone and was satisfied.*

*The sixth: I saw people treat one another with enmity for various motives. I meditated on: Truly Satan is your enemy; hold him as an enemy. And I knew that enmity was not permissible toward anyone but Satan.*

*The seventh: I saw everyone struggling excessively for provision, falling into doubt and forbidden things, debasing themselves. I meditated on: There is no moving thing on earth whose nourishment depends not on God. I knew my provision was guaranteed, so I busied myself with worship and cut off covetousness of everything else.*

*The eighth: I saw everyone relying on some created thing — wealth, property, trade, other people. I meditated on: Whoever puts his trust in God, He will be sufficient for him. So I relied on God, and He is my sufficiency and an excellent guardian.*

Shaqiq listened to all eight and then said: *Allah grant you success. I have considered the Torah and the Gospel and the Psalms and the Quran, and I find that the four books turn on these eight things. Whoever works according to them is working according to all four books.*

Eight observations. Four books. Thirty years of companionship reduced to this, and this turning out to be sufficient.
