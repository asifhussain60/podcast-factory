# Eight Admonitions and a Closing Prayer

*The letter now closes. The diagnosis was laid down in the first chapter (knowledge unaccompanied by righteous action does not save the scholar); the outline of the cure followed (the dawn watch, obedience to the Prophetic frame under inner struggle, the conditions of the sincere seeker); and the working rule of life was handed over in the third (Hatim ibn Ism's eight benefits, the figure of the* Shaykh al-Kamil *(perfected guide) at the center, the brief precise definitions of* Tasawwuf*, servitude,* Tawakkul *(reliance on God), and* Ikhlas *(sincerity)). What remains is the master's closing brief — eight admonitions, four to refrain from and four to actively take up — and the supplication he tells his disciple to recite after every prayer. The eight have one organizing claim under them: that knowledge will turn against the scholar on the Day of Judgment if it is not put to work in conduct, and that conduct has named contours the disciple can audit himself against. The supplication carries it out into the disciple's daily prayer-life. With these the letter ends.*

## The frame: four things to leave, four things to take up

Imam al-Ghazali opens the closing brief plainly. *Of these eight admonitions, there are four things you must do and four things you must not do — so that your knowledge does not turn against you on the Day of Judgment.* The frame is the same frame the whole letter has carried: knowledge, in itself, decides nothing. What it becomes in conduct decides everything. Each of the four to refrain from names a familiar trap of the religious life — a thing the scholar slides into because the religious surface looks intact. Each of the four to take up names a discipline the scholar mostly forgets because it has no audience. He begins with the four things not to do.

## First: refuse the habit of religious debate

The first admonition is bracing in its sweep. *As far as possible, do not engage in religious debates with everyone, and do not argue with anyone on any matter — religious or otherwise — for there are many hidden harms within such disputes, and the harms far outweigh any benefits.* Ghazali names the harvest the habit of disputation actually produces: *ostentation and show —* Riya *(performing the religious act for human applause rather than for Allah) — jealousy, arrogance, resentment, enmity, pride, and egotism.* The premise is not that argument is forbidden; it is that the soul cannot reliably distinguish, in the heat of it, between defending the truth and defending itself.

He then gives the disciple the test by which the rare exception can be recognized. *If an argument arises between you and another person over some issue, and your genuine desire is for the truth to prevail, then your intention to engage in that discussion may be considered sound. There are two signs of such right intention. The first is that whether the truth emerges from your own tongue or from the tongue of your opponent, it makes no difference to you — you are equally satisfied in either case, for the truth has come to light. The second sign is that you consider it preferable to discuss the matter in private, away from an audience.* Both signs are inward: indifference to whose mouth the truth comes from, and preference for the absence of spectators. A heart missing either sign is defending itself, not the truth.

**The categories of patients.** Ghazali then opens a long aside that gives the disciple a practical typology for one of the most exhausting parts of religious life — the question that comes from the wrong place. *Asking questions about religious and spiritual matters is like describing the diseases of the heart and their causes before a physician of the heart. And the efforts of that physician to cure those diseases are like the responses to the questions raised.* The ignorant are the patients; the scholars are the physicians; *imperfect scholars are incapable of curing these diseases of the heart, whereas a perfected scholar — a* Shaykh al-Kamil *— can cure them. Moreover, a perfected scholar can identify the root causes of the disease. But if the disease worsens and its causes cannot be identified, then a more expert physician must be sought — one who can determine which ailments are curable and which are not. To expend effort trying to cure an incurable disease is nothing but a waste of time.*

He then names the four kinds of questioners and tells the disciple which one alone is worth answering.

*The first kind of patient is one who asks questions out of jealousy or raises objections from envy.* Jealousy, Ghazali says flatly, is a lethal disease that has no cure. *Whatever answer you give, no matter how sound, such a person will regard you as an enemy, and the fire of envy within them will only burn more fiercely.* He cites a verse of poetry:

*Kullul 'Adawati Qad Turja Izalatuha / Illa 'Adawata Mun 'Aadaka Min Hasadin.* — *"Every kind of animosity can be remedied — except the animosity that is born of jealousy."*

And he gives the disciple a Quranic instruction for the response: *Fa 'AaRid 'Um Mun Tawalla 'Un Zikrina Walam Yurid Illal Hayatad Dunya.* — *"So turn away from the one who, out of jealousy, turns his face from Our remembrance and desires nothing but the pleasures of the life of this world."* Leave the jealous person to remain afflicted with the disease. There is no answer that helps.

*The second kind of patient is one whose disease arises from a lack of intelligence and foolishness.* Ghazali reaches for an extraordinary image: Prophet 'Isa (Jesus, peace be upon him) said that he was — *with the permission of God* — not incapable of restoring the dead to life, yet he was incapable of curing the fool and the ignorant. The ignorant fool, Ghazali specifies, is the one *who has spent only a brief time acquiring knowledge, has not even begun to grasp intellectual and transmitted religious learning, and yet raises objections against scholars who have devoted their entire lives to those very disciplines. The depth of knowledge and insight possessed by a great scholar is entirely beyond their comprehension. That incomprehension itself is their foolishness.* Keep away. Offer no response.

*The third kind of patient is one who, owing to restlessness and impatience, cannot absorb the teachings of the spiritually elevated, and who relies instead on their own deficient understanding, interpreting everything only according to their personal interest. This person is simple-minded, and their intellect is incapable of grasping the truth.* For this person too there is no answer. Ghazali grounds the instruction in a saying of the Prophet ﷺ:

*Nahnu Ma'aashirul Anbiyaee Umirna Un Natakallamun Nasa 'Ala Qadre 'Uuqulihim.* — *"We, the community of prophets, have been commanded by Allah to speak to people according to the capacity of their minds."*

The teacher cannot fight the listener's capacity; the teacher must respect it. To say more than the listener can carry is to harm both teacher and listener.

*The fourth kind of patient is one who is genuinely seeking the right path of guidance —* Sirat al-Mustaqeem* — obedient, sharp, and intelligent — and who is free from anger, the following of base desires of the lower self, jealousy, and the craving for wealth and fame. Whatever question this person asks, or whatever objection they raise, arises not from jealousy, fault-finding, or the desire to test, but from an earnest search for truth and correct guidance. This is the patient who can be cured. To answer the questions of such a person is therefore not merely permissible — it is obligatory* (Wajib *— religiously required).* One of four. The other three are not the teacher's failure; they are the limit of the medicine.

## Second: the reality of preaching

The second admonition is that the disciple should refrain from preaching and making speeches. The grounds are simple. The preacher cannot lecture others from a soul he has not first instructed himself. Allah said to Prophet 'Isa (Jesus):

*Ya Ibna Maryama A'azee Nafsaka Fa In At-ta'azta Fa Izin Nasa Fustahyee Minni.* — *"O Son of Mary! First admonish your own soul (Nafs); then, if it accepts your counsel, admonish others — otherwise have shame before Me."*

If, despite this, circumstances arise that compel the disciple to preach, then Ghazali names two things to guard against.

**Guard one — what a sermon must not be.** *In your sermon avoid colorful narrations, theatrical gestures, secretive language, flowery and embellished speech, pleasing poetry, any talk that transgresses the bounds of the* Shari'ah *(the law of Allah), and the fabrications of false claimants to Sufism.* Allah does not love the pretentious and the showy. When a preacher's *sole aim is to impress others with seemingly eloquent language — know that his inner being is corrupt and his heart is heedless of God's remembrance.* The purpose of a sermon is plain. *It is to speak of the punishment of the Hereafter. It is to describe one's own shortcomings in servitude to Allah and to express deep regret over wasted time and futile endeavors. It is to speak of the difficult stages of the life to come that stand as obstacles on the path ahead. It is to speak of how to pass through this world with one's faith — Iman — intact, of the awe-inspiring appearance of the Angel of Death — Malik ul-Maut — at the moment of dying, of the questioning in the grave by Munkar and Nakir (the two angels who appear after burial), and of the stations of the Day of Judgment. It is to speak of the fearsome spectacle of accountability on the plain of Hashr (hashr — where all of humankind will be assembled after resurrection); of the weighing of deeds in the Mizan (the mighty scale created by Almighty Allah for that purpose); of the crossing of the bridge called Sirat (a bridge over Hell, leading to Paradise, thinner than a hair and sharper than a sword, over which every human being will be made to walk); and of the other terrors of that Day.* The preacher sets these realities before the people, brings their own inward and outward faults to their minds, kindles fear of the Hereafter in their hearts, and moves them to grieve over the hours spent without God's remembrance.

To make all this concrete, Ghazali draws a striking domestic image. Consider the person whose house has been struck by a flood — water reaching the very door, threatening within seconds to engulf the house and drown everyone inside. *In that moment, the owner of the house will cry out urgently:* Al-Hazr! Al-Hazr! *("Beware! Beware! Run! The floodwater is here!") At such a moment of imminent catastrophe, no one would describe the flood in ornate sentences, with secretive language, embellished prose, or decorated poetry. A sermon should be precisely like this — rooted in genuine fear of one's own reckoning, and conveying that same urgency to others with the gravity that an impending disaster demands.*

**Guard two — what a sermon must not seek.** *While delivering sermons, one must never allow the thought to enter one's heart that after listening, people will shout praises, fall into ecstasy, tear their clothes, raise a clamor in the gathering, and declare that it was a wonderful sermon given by such-and-such a person. Such thoughts are pure ostentation — Riya — and to be pleased by them is proof of one's lack of wisdom.* The right intention is to summon God's creation *from this world toward the next; from sin toward servitude of God; from greed toward detachment of the heart from worldly attachment and the adoption of contentment; from miserliness toward generosity; from* Riya *toward sincerity — Ikhlas (doing everything solely for the countenance of Allah); from arrogance toward humility; from heedlessness of Allah's remembrance toward God-consciousness; and from pride toward piety.* Kindle love for the life of the Hereafter; loosen attachment to the world; cultivate piety where there is heedlessness and hope where there is overwhelming fear. A sermon that does not do these things, Ghazali writes, *becomes a source of ruin for both the preacher and the audience. Such a person is a Shaytan who, having become a slave of his own degraded lower self, imagines that the pulpit is a stage on which to display his abilities and harvest the fame and grandeur of the world.* Whoever has the authority to remove such a preacher from the *Minbar* (the pulpit), *it is obligatory upon him to do so deliberately and firmly,* so that they cannot distort the meaning of commanding the right and forbidding the evil — *'Amr bil Ma'roof Wa Nahi 'Anil Munkar*.

## Third: stay away from the affluent and rulers

The third admonition is unusually sharp. *You should not even greet any king, any person of wealth, or any ruler. Stay away from their company and gatherings, and beyond that, do not so much as lift your eyes to look at them.* In looking upon them and keeping their company lie *many hidden calamities*. If the disciple ever happens to find himself in their company by chance, he is to refrain from praising them. Ghazali grounds the rule in a hadith:

*Fa InnAllaha Yaghdib Iza Mudihal Faasiqu Waz Zalimu Wa Iza Madaha Wa Mun Da'aa Li Zalimin Bitoolil Baqaaii Faqad Ahabba Aye Ya'siyAllahu Fil Arde.* — *"Verily, Allah, the Exalted, is angered when any transgressor or oppressor is praised; and whoever prays for a long life for an oppressor has, in effect, expressed a desire that sin be perpetuated upon the earth."*

The logic of the second clause is the unforgiving heart of the rule. To love an oppressor's life is to love the prolongation of the oppression his life carries. The piety of the disciple cannot afford that affection.

## Fourth: do not accept gifts from rulers

The fourth admonition closes the *do not* set. *You should not accept any gifts from rulers, even if you are certain that what they offer comes from lawful — Halal — sources.* Ghazali names the corruption-mechanism precisely. *Harboring any desire for their possessions causes great damage and corruption in the Deen (deen — the path to God). The favors and rewards that come from rulers give rise to oppression, transgression, and sin, all of which damage the Deen. The minimum corruption that flows from this is that you will begin to love the oppressors, and when a person loves another, they pray for that person's long life. If an oppressor lives long, oppression endures longer, and there is greater turmoil and corruption in the world — and what could be worse than that?* The minimum is not minor.

He closes with the warning that this is exactly the territory where *Shaytan* baits the religious: *Beware! Beware!* Shaytan *will try to misguide you by planting this thought in your heart: "First take the money from these rulers, then distribute it among the poor, bring them comfort, and fulfill their needs." Beware — never accept this kind of counsel from any Shaytan, whether from among the* Jinn *(jinn — an invisible creation of God who, when inclined toward evil, become Satan's instruments for inciting harm among human beings) — or from among humankind.* The pious-sounding intention is itself the lure. *Satan has shed the blood of many through this very strategy and continues to do so.* For more, Ghazali refers the disciple to his own larger work *Ihya' 'Ulum ad-Din* (*Revival of the Knowledge of the Path to God*), where the calamities of court-money are laid out at length.

## Four matters worthy of our actions

*My dear beloved son! Refrain from the four things mentioned above. But there are also four things that you must actively attend to, and it will be fitting that you take full care of them.* The voice softens. The four to take up are quieter than the four to leave — none of them spectacular, none of them visible to a public — and that is the point.

**First — the method of developing an intimate relationship with Allah.** *Every matter between you and Allah should be conducted in such a way that, if your own servant were to do the same for you, you would not be saddened — rather, you would approve of it, commend it, and feel no anger over it. Likewise, whatever you would dislike your servant doing, realize that if you fall short in your own servitude to your Lord, your Creator will equally dislike it.* Ghazali drives the asymmetry that anchors the discipline. *Your servant was not created by you — you merely employed him. But you yourself were created, nurtured, and owned by your Real Creator and Lord. You are His slave in the fullest sense, and therefore your obligation not to displease your Lord — in thought, word, or deed — is immeasurably greater.* The household metaphor is precise: if you would resent in your servant what you do not resent in yourself before God, the discipline has already named the gap.

**Second — the method of relating to the slaves of Allah.** *Every matter between you and other slaves of Allah should be conducted in such a way that, if they were to do the same to you, you would be pleased and not aggrieved.* Ghazali grounds this in a sacred hadith — a tradition of the Prophet in which Allah speaks directly:

*Fala Yakmalu Eemanu 'Abdi Hatta Yuhibba Lisaa-irin Nasi Ma Yuhibba Li Nafsihi.* — *"My slave's faith — Iman — is never complete until he loves for all human beings what he loves for himself."*

The discipline is the same instrument the first one used (your treatment of others, audited as you would audit theirs to you), rotated outward.

**Third — an insistent advice to study and learn.** *If you want to increase your knowledge and wish to study scholarly works, then imagine that your lifetime will end in no more than a week. In that situation, consider carefully what kind of knowledge will truly benefit you, and devote yourself only to that.* The thought experiment ruthlessly clears the field. *If you knew with certainty that your life would last no more than a week, you would never spend those final days studying books on religious debate and argumentation, lexicography, linguistics and grammar, poetry, poetic collections, or the art of composition. You would recognize that such knowledge could offer you no benefit. Instead, you would devote that entire week to contemplation of the heart and to recognizing the faults within your* Nafs *— your own self — so that you might make amends in whatever time remained.* You would purify the heart, adorn it with the love of Allah and noble inner virtues, give yourself wholly to worship.

He sharpens it with a second image. *If you were told that the king would visit your home next week, you would spend the entire week consumed with a single concern: that the king's gaze should not fall upon anything unseemly. You would clean and adorn everything — your body, your clothing, the walls and floors of your house. You would leave nothing untended.* The application is not stated; the application is shown. The Prophet has said:

*InnAllaha La Yunzuru Ila Suwarikum Wa La Ila 'Aamalikum Wa Lakin Yunzuru Ila Qulubikum Wa Niyyatikum.* — *"Verily, Allah does not look at your faces or your outward actions, but He looks at your hearts and your intentions."*

When the gaze of the Supreme Ruler — *Ahkamul Hakimeen* (the Most Just of Judges, the Supreme Ruler over all rulers) — rests on the heart, why does the heart go unpurified? *If you desire to acquire knowledge of the matters of the heart, study my book* Ihya al-Uloom ad-Deen *— Revival of the Knowledge of the Path to God, also known as Revival of Religious Learnings — along with my other works, for such knowledge is personally obligatory —* Fard al-'ayn *(an obligation that falls on every individual Muslim) — upon every Muslim. Other forms of knowledge carry the status of communal obligation —* Fard Kifaya *(an obligation upon the whole community, discharged when at least one group within it fulfills it).* Even that knowledge should be acquired only to the extent necessary for obeying the commandments of Allah. *If Allah grants you the* Tawfeeq *(the divine enablement and inner motivation to perform righteous deeds), then you must necessarily pursue this knowledge.*

**Fourth — not stockpiling food.** *You should not accumulate more than one year's provision for your family from the goods of this world.* The Prophet set aside one year's worth of provisions for certain of his wives and said:

*Allahumma Aj'al Quta Aale Muhammadin Kafafun.* — *"O my Allah! Make this provision sufficient for the family of Muhammad."*

Ghazali notes the precision of even this. *The Prophet did not set aside provisions for all of his wives — the* Azwaj al-Mutahharat *(the Most Pure Wives). He reserved a year's supply only for those among the Azwaj al-Mutahharat whose* Tawakkul *was weaker. For those among the* Ummahatil Mu'mineen *(the Mothers of the Believers) whose certainty in Allah was perfect and whose Tawakkul was strong, the Prophet did not set aside even a single day's provisions. Among them was 'Aisha Siddiqa (may Allah be pleased with her), and others like her among the Mothers of the Believers.* The instruction does not flatten everyone to the same measure: one year is a ceiling on those who would otherwise hoard, not a floor on those whose hearts already rest.

## A supplication that may always be recited

*My dear beloved son! In this book I have answered all of your questions. Now gather your resolve, act upon everything within it, and do not forget me in your supplications — your* Du'a *(the personal prayer of asking).* The disciple had also asked the master for a supplication to recite at all times. Ghazali first widens the field. *Seek out supplications in the books of* Ahadith *(the traditions of the Prophet), particularly in the* Sahah Sitta *(the six most authenticated collections of his traditions), and commit them to memory. Similarly, many supplications have reached us through the* Ahl al-Bayt *(the family of the Prophet); seek them out there as well.* The disciple's prayer-life is to be sourced from the canon, not invented. Within that field, Ghazali hands him one in particular, to be recited *after every* Salaat *(the obligatory daily prayer), every prayer.*

*O my Allah! I seek from You the fullness and perfection of Your blessings; I seek the permanence of inward and outward purity; I seek the all-encompassing breadth of Your mercy; I seek the attainment of health; I seek increase in my sustenance; I seek happiness in my life; I seek true and lasting success in this life; I seek the completion of noble qualities; I seek from Your rewards the most abundant reward; I seek from Your plenitude the sweetest plenitude; I seek from Your favors the most plentiful favor; I seek from all deeds the deed that is best; I seek from all knowledge the knowledge that is most beneficial; and I seek from all sustenance the sustenance that is most wholesome.*

*O my Allah! Be on our side — grant us benefit — and do not burden us — protect us from harm. O my Allah! Grant us salvation at our death; reform our deeds; grant us safety in Your days and nights; extend Your mercy to us in our homes and in our possessions; cover our sins and faults with the veil of Your forgiveness; do us the favor of correcting all our shortcomings, inward and outward; and grant us confidence and reliance upon You.*

*O my Allah! Grant us steadfastness and perseverance in our Deen, our religion and path to God. Save us from deeds in this world that would become a source of shame and humiliation on the Day of Judgment. Lighten the burden of our sins; grant us the life of the pious; be sufficient for us in all our affairs; and protect us from the evil of the evildoers. Liberate us and our forebears from the Fire of Hell, from debt, and from oppression.*

*O You the Victorious One,* Ya Aziz *! O You the Forgiving One,* Ya Ghaffar *! O You the Generous One,* Ya Kareem *! O You the Coverer of Faults,* Ya Sattar *! O You the Forbearing One,* Ya Haleem *! O You the Compeller,* Ya Jabbar *! O You the Magnificent One,* Ya Azeem *! O You Who Subdues all things,* Ya Qahhar *! O Allah! O Allah! O Allah! O You the Compassionate One in this world,* Ya Rahman *! O You the Merciful One in the life hereafter,* Ya Raheem *! O You the Greatest of the Merciful,* Ya Arhamu ar-Rahimeen *! You are the Granter of boundless mercy. And may the mercy and blessings of Allah descend always upon Muhammad, the finest of all creation from eternity to eternity, and upon his descendants, and upon his companions. All praise belongs solely to Allah, the Lord of all the Universes —* Rabb ul-'Alameen.

The shape of the supplication is the shape of the letter itself. It begins with abundance asked of Allah, moves through the request for inward and outward correction, and closes with the names — each one a face of the mercy under which the whole letter has been written. The disciple is sent off with this on his tongue.

*This book comes to an end with the help of Al-Malik (the Absolute Sovereign), and Al-Wahhab (the Giver of all).*

The letter the disciple wrote has been answered. The diagnosis stands; the rule of life is in his hand; the eight admonitions audit his conduct against eight named contours; and the supplication carries him into his daily prayer-life with the master's own words. The rest is the soul actually offered. The pages can give no more than that, and they have given everything they can.
