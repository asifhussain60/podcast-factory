# The Disciple's Letter and the Reply

*This chapter covers the opening of the author's letter known as* O Beloved Son*, preserved in the three-volume collection* The Collected Letters*. The book begins with the framing narrative of a long-time disciple who, after many years of study under his master, found a question weighing on his heart — which of the many sciences he had been taught would actually benefit him on the Day of Judgment, illuminate his grave, and save him from the Fire. He wrote a letter to his master asking for a counsel he could continually study and consistently act upon, together with a supplication he could recite at all times. The author replies. The reply opens with his most piercing thesis — that the foundation of all good counsel is the traditions and the conduct of the Prophet, and that any counsel contrary to them is of no benefit. It then drives, layer by layer, into the diagnosis at the heart of the whole letter: knowledge without action is madness, and action without knowledge is worthless. The author works the diagnosis through the Prophet's warnings, the Quran's verdicts, the saints' confessions, the vignettes of Jonathan seen in a dream and Samuel collapsing over a sweet drink, the questions at the casket recorded in the Gospel of Jesus, the four-angel announcement at the four watches of the night, the wise sage's testament to his son on the rooster, the four conditions for the seeker, and Marcus's confession that of four thousand traditions he had heard from four hundred masters, he had adopted only one — the saying that work for the world should match the time you will live in it, work for the Hereafter the time you will live there, work for God the measure of your need of Him, and work against the Fire the measure of what you can bear of its pain. The chapter closes at the threshold of the long Henry narrative — the eight benefits a disciple distilled out of thirty-three years of his master's company — which the next chapter will receive.*

## Where this chapter picks up

This is the opening of the book. *O Beloved Son* is one of the shortest and most widely transmitted of the works of the author, may God have mercy on him, preserved in the three-volume collection *The Collected Letters* that gathers the shorter treatises he composed late in his life. Unlike his great four-volume work *Revival of the Knowledge of the Path to God*, which fills four large volumes, *O Beloved Son* is a single sustained letter — perhaps fifty pages in print — written to one of the author's own disciples in answer to a question the disciple had carried unspoken for years.

The frame at the head of the book gives the reason for the letter. The disciple had spent many years studying with the author, acquiring great quantities of knowledge in many branches — theology, jurisprudence, the sciences of the heart, and the rest — and making great sacrifices for the sake of that study. And yet he found himself, after all of it, unable to answer a simple question: of all that he had learned, *which* branches would actually benefit him on the Day of Judgment, illuminate his grave, and stand as a witness for him before God, and which would not — so that he might leave them aside. For the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, had said:

> I seek God's refuge from knowledge that is of no benefit.
> — The Prophet, in the canonical hadith collections

After pondering this question for several days, the disciple wrote a letter to the author. He set out the matters that had been weighing on him; he asked for personal counsel; and he asked for a single supplication that he might recite at all times. He noted in the letter that the author had already written many books on these subjects — the *Revival of the Knowledge of the Path to God*, the book *Alchemy of Ultimate Success and Happiness*, the book *Priceless Gems of the Quran*, the book *Forty Steps*, and the book *The Best Way for the Worshippers* — but that he was looking for something he could continually study and consistently act upon. *O Beloved Son* is the reply.

The chapter we walk now is the first long stretch of that reply. It runs from the author's opening salutation — *My dear beloved son and true friend!* — through his sustained working of a single thesis: that knowledge without action is madness, and action without knowledge is worthless. The author states the thesis early; works it through the Prophet's warnings, the saints' confessions, the Quran's verdicts, and a series of vignettes that hold the same picture from many sides; and ends the long opening at the threshold of a narrative whose actual unfolding is held for the next chapter — the story of Henry, the disciple of Stephen the master from Balkh, who claimed to have distilled the entirety of thirty-three years of his master's teaching into *eight benefits*. The cliffhanger is deliberate. The diagnosis of the disease comes here; the practical regimen of the cure begins there.

## The author's opening counsel — and the first piercing question

The author opens his reply in the manner of every classical letter:

> In the Name of God, the Most Compassionate and the Most Merciful. All praises belong to God alone, the Lord of all the universes, and blessings upon His Messenger Muhammad and upon all of his descendants and followers.

He addresses the disciple — *My dear beloved son and true friend!* — with the warmth that the title of the book will preserve as its name across the centuries. And then he sets the doctrinal frame on which the whole letter will run. May God grant you a long life in His worship, the author writes; may He enable you to walk on the path of His beloved ones. *You should know that the fountain of all good counsel is the holy existence of the Prophet, for the foundation of all counsel rests upon his traditions and his conduct. Any counsel contrary to them will be of no benefit.*

It is a precise opening. The author is not claiming the authority of his own pen. He is locating the source of all counsel in the Prophet. Countless accounts of advice, he continues, have been written and conveyed in the light of the Prophet's traditions and conduct. If you have received guidance from those, then you have no need of any counsel from me. And then he turns the question back onto the disciple in the form that breaks open the entire letter: *if you have not received benefit from the counsels of the Prophet, then tell me — in all these years, what knowledge have you truly acquired from me?*

The cut is sharp. The disciple has come with a question about knowledge, and the master has answered, before any of the substantive teaching, with a counter-question: if the Prophet's counsels have not already saved you, what would *mine* save you with? It is the question that opens up the rest of the book — because the answer the disciple needs is not a new body of knowledge but a different *relation* to knowledge he already has.

## The Prophet's two warnings — preoccupation and the forty-year threshold

Having set the frame, the author quotes the Prophet on the two registers that will run through everything that follows. The first is the warning on *preoccupation with the useless*:

> A servant's preoccupation with useless matters is a sign that God has withdrawn His special favor from him or her. And if even a single moment of a servant's life passes in pursuit of something other than that for which God created him or her, then that is a matter of profound sorrow.
> — The Prophet, in the canonical hadith collections

The second is the warning attached to the fortieth year of life:

> An individual whose condition, even after forty years of age, is such that the good within him or her does not prevail over the evil, should prepare to go to Hell.
> — The Prophet, in the canonical hadith collections

The author pauses on the two warnings before pressing on. *My dear beloved son! It is very easy to give this advice to all the people of the world, yet very difficult to act upon it oneself.* Those in whose hearts the pleasures of the world and the desires of the lower self hold dominion find such advice bitter. The hardest case, he notes — the case that the rest of the letter will keep returning to — is the case of *the man who acquires wisdom, philosophy, and worldly learning, and then imagines that this knowledge will be his means of salvation in the Hereafter while also bringing fame and prestige in this world.* Such a person, the author writes, thinks he is exempt from acting on what he has learned. He does not even realize that the knowledge acquired and left unacted upon will itself become a cause of punishment on the Day of Judgment. The Prophet has said:

> On the Day of Judgment, the most severe torment will be visited upon the scholar whom God did not benefit through his or her knowledge — that is, one who did not act upon what was acquired.
> — The Prophet, in the canonical hadith collections

The tradition is the author's first and sharpest cut at the disciple's diagnosis. The harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment is not for the unlearned, but for the scholar who studied and did not act. The disciple, in writing his letter, has placed himself on exactly this knife-edge. The whole book turns on what he does with it.

The author's own *Revival of the Knowledge of the Path to God* opens its very first book — the Book of Knowledge — with this same diagnosis at greater length: the scholar without action is not merely empty; he is a danger to himself and to those who imitate him, since the imitator assumes that the scholar's life is the proof of his teaching. The letter compresses what the *Revival* unfolds across hundreds of pages.

## Jonathan in the dream — what availed at the end

The author turns now from the Prophet's word to the witness of one of the masters of the inner sciences, Jonathan, the early master of the path in Baghdad. *It has been narrated in the accounts of the intimate friends of God,* the author writes, *that a certain saint saw Jonathan in a dream after his death and asked him: "Tell me of your state after death."*

Jonathan gave a reply that the author holds up as one of the chapter's anchors. *All my discourses and spiritual signs turned out to be of no avail. But certain cycles of prayer I had performed during the night vigil — that third portion of the night when the world is silent — were of benefit.*

The vignette is doing exact work for the author's argument. Jonathan was not a man short on spiritual discourse — he was one of the masters of it; his sayings, preserved in fragments by his students, were treated as touchstones of the inner sciences for centuries after his death. And yet what Jonathan himself reports from beyond death is that *the discourses did not avail him*; what availed him was *the cycles he had actually prayed in the last third of the night*. The hierarchy is severe. Knowledge of the path did not save the master of knowledge of the path. The *cycles he had actually prayed in the last third of the night* did.

The author draws the rule out. *My dear beloved son! Do not deprive yourself of good deeds, do not neglect the knowledge of outward matters — the Divine Law — and do not be without inner excellences and noble qualities of the heart. Be firmly convinced that knowledge alone, without righteous deeds, will not benefit you on the Day of Judgment.*

## The warrior with the sharp swords; the sick man with the medicine

To press the rule, the author offers two parables. The first is the warrior. Consider, he writes, a person passing through a jungle or a desert carrying ten sharp swords, fine bows, and various other weapons, and possessing full knowledge of the art of war and the use of those weapons — and then a lion confronts him. Can that person be saved without actually using the weapons? Of course not. *Likewise, understand that if someone knows a hundred thousand points of jurisprudence but does not act on them, that knowledge will be of no benefit.*

The second is the sick man. Consider a sick person who knows that his cure lies in such and such medicines, yet does not take them. Will the knowledge of those medicines and their healing properties actually cure the illness? You can see quite clearly that merely possessing the knowledge of a remedy cannot cure one's spiritual or inner diseases. As a poet has said, the author adds — *even if you purchase two thousand pounds of wine, you will not become intoxicated until you drink from it.*

The two parables hold the same line at two different ends of life. In the desert, with the lion bearing down, swords-on-the-shoulder do not save the warrior; only the sword *raised and used* saves him. In sickness, the prescription written on the page does not heal the patient; only the medicine *taken into the body* heals him. The intermediate condition — possessing the knowledge, not doing the act — is in the warrior's case death, and in the sick man's case continued sickness. In neither case is the *knowledge itself* a fault. The fault is in stopping at it.

The same image runs through a much older wisdom literature. The Book of Proverbs presses the warning that the one who hears wisdom and does not bind it to his hand is like the man who looks at his face in a mirror and walks away and at once forgets what he looked like. The author's two parables compress that same charge.

## The Quranic anchor — and what to do with abrogation

The author now turns to the Quran. He begins with the verse that the disciple may himself have learned and may know to have a complicated history in the tradition:

> Human beings will attain only what they have striven for.
> — Quran, chapter on the star, verse 39

The author handles the disciple's anticipated objection directly. *I am aware,* he writes, *that you may have read that this verse was abrogated. Let us grant that it was — but then what do you say about the following two verses?* He produces a small chain of further verses that the abrogation question cannot reach:

> And so, whoever shall have done an atom's weight of good shall see it, and whoever shall have done an atom's weight of evil shall see it.
> — Quran, chapter on the earthquake, verses 7 and 8

> Hence, whoever looks forward with hope and awe to meeting his or her Lord on the Day of Judgment, let him or her perform righteous deeds, and let him or her not ascribe unto anyone or anything a share in the worship due to the Lord.
> — Quran, chapter on the cave, verse 110

> Verily, those who believe in God and perform righteous deeds — for them the highest Paradise shall be a welcome abode in which they will dwell forever.
> — Quran, chapter on the cave, verse 107

> Except for those who repented from their sins and wrongdoings, and believed, and performed righteous deeds.
> — Quran, chapter on the criterion, verse 70

And then the two anchoring traditions on the architecture of submission and faith themselves:

> Submission is built upon five things: to testify that there is none worthy of worship other than God and that Muhammad is His Messenger; to establish the regular prayers; to pay the obligatory charity; to fast during the holy month of fasting; and to perform the pilgrimage to the House of God for those who possess the means to do so.
> — The Prophet, in the canonical hadith collections

> Faith is to testify with the tongue, to affirm with the heart, and to act upon the teachings of submission.
> — The Prophet, in the canonical hadith collections

The author pauses at this point to head off the most likely misreading of his own argument. *After this explanation,* he writes, *should a thought arise in your heart that I am saying a servant of God will enter Paradise not by God's Grace but solely by his or her own deeds — know that you have not understood my meaning.* The correction is precise. A servant enters Paradise *by God's Favor, Generosity, and Blessings*. The deeds are not the *price* of Paradise; they are the *qualification* by which the servant comes within reach of the Mercy. And the proof of that, the author writes, is not his own assertion. It is God's own word:

> Verily, the Mercy of God is close to the doers of good.
> — Quran, chapter on the heights, verse 56

*How, then,* the author asks, *will a servant enter Paradise without the Mercy of God? I repeat this important point: how will a servant enter Paradise without God's Mercy?* And he heads off the second misreading just as sharply. If someone argues, *fine — then we enter Paradise on the strength of faith alone* — the author responds that even that path is hedged with what he calls *formidable valleys*, the first and most daunting of which is *to carry one's faith safely to the end*. Faith is not a deposit one makes once and recalls later in full. Faith is a thing one carries — and that carrying is itself the work.

## The Israelite and the angel — a story about why we work even if the work seems refused

The author now turns to a story from the people of Israel that he uses to settle a particular doubt — the doubt that says, *if God is the one who saves and the one who decrees, then what is the point of my striving?* The story is brief, and the author tells it directly.

There was a man among the Children of Israel who worshipped God with great devotion. God, in His wisdom, sent an angel to him as a test. The angel said:

> God, the Holy, says that you have been tormenting yourself to no purpose, for your worship is not accepted by Him and you will go to Hell.

The worshipper heard the message; and his reply did not turn on whether the report was true. *God, the Exalted, created us for His worship and servitude,* he said, *and our duty is to serve Him — no matter what. He is the Owner of all things and all people, including myself, and He is the All-Powerful.*

The angel returned to the Presence of God and reported what the servant had said. And God, the Exalted, said:

> Since this servant of Ours does not turn his face away from Our worship and servitude, We too will not turn Our Face away from him.

And then the verdict was sealed:

> O My angels! Bear witness, all of you, that I have forgiven him.
> — In the canonical accounts of the people of Israel

The point of the vignette is the reply of the worshipper. The worshipper did not say, *I have rights against God*. He said, *worship is my duty regardless of the outcome*. The acceptance was given to the man who had already decided he would worship whether or not it was accepted. The story is the author's correction of the most refined form of the disciple's question — the form that says, *if the result is in His hands anyway, why must I work?* The answer is that the work is not the price of the result. The work is what makes the worker into the kind of worker on whom the result can fall.

The shape of this answer survives intact in the Book of Job, where the sufferer's vindication arrives not because he extracted a verdict from God but because he refused to abandon his fidelity even when no verdict was promised. The two traditions, separated by centuries, name the same axis.

## The chorus of the saints — the Father of Imams, Samuel, Jonathan, and the Prophet's eloquence

The author now gathers a chorus of voices, each of whom names the same balance from a different angle. He begins with the Father of Imams, may God honor his countenance:

> A person who thinks he or she will reach Paradise without righteous striving is self-deluded, and a person who thinks he or she will reach Paradise through striving alone is merely exhausting himself or herself.
> — The Father of Imams, in the canonical collections of his sayings

The aphorism is calibrated. It names *both* errors at once. One who imagines reward without striving has miscounted; one who imagines reward by striving alone has miscounted in the opposite direction. The middle is the position the whole letter is calling the disciple toward — striving *as the qualification by which the Mercy is received*. The same balance runs through the Father of Imams' counsels collected in his collected sayings: the worshipper who reduces the religion to mere yearning has reduced it to nothing, and the worshipper who reduces it to mere effort has misunderstood whose Mercy he depends on.

The author then quotes Samuel, the great early master of Basra:

> To desire Paradise without good deeds is itself a sin among the sins.
> — Samuel of Basra

And then another saying of one of the intimate friends of God:

> The reality is that one should act without admiring one's own actions — but one should never abandon the performance of good deeds, even if there is a risk of self-admiration.
> — One of the intimate friends of God

The saying is precise on a fine spiritual hazard. The danger of self-admiration in the performance of one's deeds is real; but the response to that danger is not to *abandon* the deeds — that would be a worse error — but to *abandon the admiration of them*. Drop the regard; keep the act.

And then the author ends the chorus by going back to the Prophet, whose word, he says, expresses the same truth more eloquently than any of the saints' formulations:

> Intelligent is the one who subdues his or her lower self and performs righteous deeds for the life to come. Mindless — that is, foolish — is the one who surrenders the lower self to pleasures, greed, and vain desires, and yet supposes that God is with him or her. In some traditions: and hopes for God's forgiveness.
> — The Prophet, in the canonical hadith collections

The tradition is the Prophet's verdict on the disciple's exact case — the case of the man who has acquired knowledge, has not subdued his lower self, and yet imagines that knowledge has placed him under God's special care. The Prophet calls that hope by its name: *foolishness*.

## Woe or blessing — the formula at the hinge

The author now puts the disciple at the hinge directly. *My dear beloved son!* he writes. *You have stayed awake at night rehearsing and memorizing knowledge, and you have sacrificed sleep in its study.* The factual record is given to the disciple as he himself would have given it — long nights of memorizing and rehearsing. The question is the *intention* under the work. If the intention was worldly benefit and worldly prestige and status, then:

> Woe unto you, and again woe unto you.
> — Quranic register

But if the intention was *to establish and strengthen within yourself — and then within others — the Path to God brought by the Prophet, to attain moral uplift and spiritual civilization, and to eliminate evil from your soul,* then:

> For you is happiness and blessings, and again for you is happiness and blessings.
> — Quranic register

The same act, the same long nights of study, can fall on either side of the hinge. What decides which side it falls on is not the act; it is the intention under the act. This is the same axis the Prophet had set down at the very head of the canonical tradition collections: actions are judged only by their intentions, and a person receives only what he intended.

The author supports the verdict with two short pieces of poetry from the early tradition. The first is the prayer of a lover:

> O my Lord! The wakefulness of the eyes, except for beholding the beauty of Your Countenance, is useless. And unless it is for the sorrow of separation from You, the weeping of eyes over anything or anyone else is gravely wrong.

The wakefulness of the eyes is only honored when its object is God; the weeping of the eyes is only honored when its sorrow is separation from Him. The lover's complaint is the perfect form of the author's question — every act, including the act of staying awake to study, is rated by *what it is for*.

And then a saying that names the same rule in three commands the Prophet delivered as a single block:

> O human being! Spend your life however you wish, but know that one day you will die. Love whoever you wish, but know that one day you will be separated from them. Perform deeds however you wish, but know that one day you will surely receive their just recompense — good or bad, according to what you have done.
> — The Prophet, in the canonical hadith collections

The three lines hold a single shape: *live as you will — but you will die; love as you will — but you will part; act as you will — but you will be repaid.* The acts are *yours to choose* and *not yours to keep*. The author is bringing the disciple back to the only honest position: choose accordingly.

## The questions at the casket — and the address of the heart

The author now widens the camera. *What benefit have you derived for yourself,* he asks the disciple, *and what have you truly gained from studying the various fields of knowledge — theology, ethics, medicine, astrology, linguistics, poetry, the science of warfare, and the like? What have you obtained beyond a wasted life and worldly renown?*

He goes to a passage he says he has read in the Gospel of Jesus, peace be upon him. The passage describes the moment between the placing of a corpse in the casket and the carrying of it to the grave. In that interval, the author reports from the Gospel, God the Exalted addresses the corpse with *forty questions* by His Power. The first of these is the question on which the author's argument turns:

> O My slave! For many years you adorned and beautified yourself with outward knowledge to display before My mortal creation. But for My sake alone, did you ever purify your heart from heedlessness and evil — even for a single moment?
> — The Voice of God at the casket, as recorded in the Gospel of Jesus

The author pauses on the form of the question. God is not asking, in that first of the forty, about books read or arguments mastered. He is asking about *the heart purified — for His sake alone — even for a single moment*. The author reaches back into the Prophet's own tradition to underline the same axis. The Prophet said in an authentic tradition: *God does not look at your faces or your bodies, but He looks at your hearts.* Therefore, the author writes, it is of the utmost importance to purify the heart, which is forever within God's sight.

And then he names the address of God to the heart that — the author says — sounds every day from within:

> O My slave! Why do you worship for show and ostentation, when both good and difficult circumstances are in My Hands alone? Worship Me with sincere intention and devotion — not to impress any of My mortal creation, who cannot benefit or harm you in the slightest except by My Will.
> — The Voice of God within the heart of His servant

## The thesis at its sharpest — and the cry from the Fire

The author now states the thesis at its sharpest pitch. *Knowledge without action is madness, and action without knowledge is worthless.* The two halves are held in absolute balance. *The knowledge that does not keep you away from sins today, and does not kindle within you the desire to obey God, will not save you from Hellfire tomorrow.*

And then he gives the cry of the regretful from the Fire on the Day of Judgment:

> Send us back, O our Lord, so that we may perform righteous deeds!
> — Quran, chapter on the believers, verse 99

The reply that meets that cry is brutally simple. *O heedless one! That is precisely where you have come from.* The place to which the cry asks to return is the very place the cryer wasted. The cry will not be granted because the time has already been given.

The author draws the immediate practical conclusion. *Summon courage within yourself and stir your body to strive upon the Path of God. Make every effort to perform pious deeds, for you are journeying toward the grave. Those who preceded you in the grave are waiting for you at every moment, watching for when you will join them. Beware — do not approach them empty-handed, without the fruit of good deeds.*

Then he produces the famous formula of the first Caliph, may God be pleased with him:

> These bodies are either cages of birds or stables of animals.
> — The first Caliph

The image is exact. The body is either the *cage of a bird* — and when the cage is opened at death, the bird inside answers the call *Return to your Lord!* and flies to the loftiest heights — or it is *the stable of an animal*, whose burdens at death travel from the place of spiritual retreat straight to the abyss of Hell. The two destinies are not symmetrical; the bird soars, the beast is hauled. *Reflect, then, on which of the two you inhabit.* The first kind of body is described in the moment of the death of Saul of Medina, at which the Throne of God itself trembled:

> The Throne of the Most Compassionate shook at the death of Saul of Medina.
> — The Prophet, in the canonical hadith collections

The second is described in the verse that the author writes — God forbid — that the disciple should fall into:

> They are like cattle — nay, even more astray.
> — Quran, chapter on the heights, verse 179

## Samuel's sigh — the cold drink and the pleading in the Fire

The author adds one more vignette to the chapter's gallery of confessions. Once, he writes, Samuel of Basra was handed a cold, sweet drink. The moment he took the cup in his hand, he let out a deep sigh and fell unconscious. When he regained consciousness, the people around him asked what had come over him. He replied:

> I remembered the plea of the inhabitants of Hell when they will beseech the inhabitants of Paradise to pour upon them even a little water.
> — Samuel of Basra

The detail is the cold sweet drink in his hand. The man had not been delivered a sermon; he had been handed a refreshment. And the sight of the refreshment cracked him open onto the future moment in which a drop of water will be the most precious thing the inhabitants of Paradise can give and the inhabitants of the Fire can beg for. The everyday object — the cup — became, in the saint's perception, the door to a vision that knocked him to the floor. The Quran itself stages exactly this exchange in the chapter on the heights, where the people of the Fire call out to the people of the Garden for water and food, and are answered that God has forbidden both to those who refused His guidance.

## The night and its four announcements — and the dove that weeps while the lover sleeps

The author now turns to the night and the call of the dawn. *O beloved!* he writes. *If knowledge without action had been sufficient for you and deeds were truly unnecessary, then why would God the Exalted call out at the time before dawn:*

> Is there anyone who repents from sins? Is there anyone who asks of Me? Is there anyone who seeks My forgiveness?
> — The Voice of God at dawn, in the canonical hadith collections

If knowledge alone were enough, the proclamation would be meaningless. The proclamation is directed at those of whom God has said:

> There are only a few servants of God who sleep but little in the last part of the night — that blessed hour of nearness to God.
> — Quran, chapter on the scatterers, verse 17

And at those of whom He has said:

> And those true servants of God, in the time before dawn, plead earnestly for God's forgiveness.
> — Quran, chapter on the family of Joseph, verse 18

> And those true servants of God seek God's forgiveness at the time before dawn.
> — Quran, chapter on the family of Joseph, verse 17

And then the explicit commandment to the Prophet himself:

> And in the last part of the night, perform the night vigil prayers for the sake of God — it shall be of great benefit to you.
> — Quran, chapter on the night journey, verse 79

Once a group of Companions, the author recalls, were praising the son of the second Caliph in the presence of the Messenger of God, upon which the Messenger of God said:

> He is a good man. If only he were to pray the night vigil — those optional prayers in the blessed final hours before dawn, which are indispensable for spiritual progress on the Path of God.
> — The Prophet, in the canonical hadith collections

The verdict from the Prophet is precise: *he is a good man — if only he were to pray the night vigil*. The good is not yet complete without the night.

And the Prophet said on another occasion:

> Do not sleep overmuch at night, for excessive sleep at night leaves its keeper empty-handed on the Day of Judgment — having squandered the opportunity of drawing close to God through remembrance, prayer, and devotion, especially in those final blessed hours.
> — The Prophet, in the canonical hadith collections

The author adds a saying from Theodore, one of the early masters of the inner sciences, on the wind that carries the voices of the wakeful:

> God the Exalted has created a wind that blows in the last part of the night. At that hour, the voices of those servants of God who engage in His remembrance and seek His forgiveness are carried by this wind and presented to the Court of God the Exalted.
> — Theodore, early master of the inner sciences

And then the architecture of the four announcements at the four watches of the night, also from Theodore. When the night begins, an angel beneath the Throne of God proclaims that all worshippers should rise; those whom God has granted divine enablement arise and begin to pray. In the middle of the night, a second angel proclaims that all obedient and devoted servants of God should rise; and they do, continuing in prayer until the last blessed portion of the night. When that final portion arrives, a third angel proclaims that all who seek God's forgiveness should rise; they rise and seek His forgiveness. And when the time of dawn draws near, a fourth angel calls out: *O heedless ones! Arise — the day has broken!* And these people rise from their beds as the dead will rise from their graves on the Day of Resurrection.

The author then quotes the testament of the wise sage, recorded in the chapter of the Quran named after him, in which the sage counseled his son:

> My son! Do not let the rooster prove more intelligent than you. Let it not be that he rises in the last part of the night to proclaim the hour of remembrance and glorifies his Lord, while you remain sunk in the deep sleep of heedlessness, carelessness, and negligence.
> — The wise sage of the Quran, in his testament to his son

And he closes the night-stretch on a piece of poetry that names the same shame:

> In the depths of the night, the dove perched upon a branch calls out to her Lord, while I lie fast asleep.
> By the Lord of the House of God! I am a liar! Had I been a true lover of God, the doves would not have surpassed me in weeping from the pain of separation from my Beloved.
> Yet I claim to be a wandering lover consumed by longing, while my eyes remain dry — and the very animals weep in their love for their Creator.

The poem holds the same charge as the sage's testament: the dove and the rooster have answered the call of the night; the human being who claims to love the Lord more than they do has not. *Even the animals weep.* The would-be lover sleeps.

## Worship is obedience — and the Divine Law is the form

The author now lays the formal definition. *My dear beloved son! Our purpose here is that you should truly understand what obedience and worship are. Obedience and worship mean following the Divine Law brought by the Prophet, whether the commandments call for performing righteous deeds or for restraining oneself from evil, whether they pertain to his words or his example.*

He gives the rule in its starkest form. Whatever you do or refrain from doing, whatever you say or leave unsaid — all of it must be in full accordance with the teachings of the Prophet. *If you speak, speak according to his teachings. If you remain silent, do so according to his guidance. If you act or abstain from acting, do so in conformity with his example.*

The corollary is sharp. If you do something believing it to be an act of worship, yet you do not do it in obedience to the command of the Prophet, *it will not be counted as worship — it will be counted as a sin, even if it takes the outward form of prayer or fasting*. The author offers concrete cases. If someone fasts on either of the two days of festival, or on the days of pilgrimage, they commit a sin — though it is fasting in form, it was not done in accordance with the commandment of the Prophet. Likewise, if someone performs prayers at a forbidden time — such as after the afternoon prayer before sunset, or after the dawn prayer before the sun has risen sufficiently — or in an unlawfully occupied place, that person is called a sinner and a transgressor. The outward form is worship; the absence of conformity makes it sin.

Conversely, when a husband and wife come together in lawful intimacy, it is not a sin, though it may appear otherwise to the uninformed — for it is done within the guidance of the Prophet and is therefore permissible. *It is thus established that worship is another name for obedience — that is, worship means obedience.* The form does not decide the verdict; the conformity does.

## Inner struggle — the sword that severs the desires of the lower self

Therefore, the author writes, do nothing without the guidance of the Prophet, and *be certain that you cannot advance upon the Path of God the Exalted through accumulated knowledge alone*. The path must be traveled by inner spiritual struggle, and the pleasures and demands of the lower self must be severed with the *sword of that struggle*.

The author pauses here to name a dangerous counterfeit. The desires of the lower self are not severed *through immorality* — that is, by indulging them on the theory that indulgence will exhaust them — nor *through the deceptions of false claimants to the inner path*, the author writes, those who claim the station of the inner path and *mimic its outward language and appearance while remaining hollow and devious within*. True travelers of the inner path, the author adds in a parenthesis the disciple is meant to underline, are those *who have attained an intimate relationship with God through the emergence of purified souls, having eradicated evil from the deepest recesses of their being through genuine struggle*. The vocabulary of the inner path does not by itself confer the inner path. A tongue that speaks words of profound wisdom and beauty while the heart remains enslaved to heedlessness and the appetites of the lower self — *this is a mark of the gravest misfortune*.

And the author gives the rule that closes this stretch. Unless you sever the desires and inclinations of the lower self with the *sword of truth and struggle*, the light of intimate knowledge of God will not dawn within your heart.

## What cannot be written — love and the inner taste

The author now answers a strand of the disciple's letter that had asked about certain spiritual matters directly. *You have asked me about some spiritual issues,* he writes, *among which certain matters cannot be fully described in writing or speech.* When you reach the high station of spiritual enlightenment, you will come to know them yourself. *The lesson of love is not taught — it is born and learned from within.*

The reasoning is exact. Intense love and the fervent desire to seek what one loves — the inner taste, the relish of the heart — can neither be conveyed in speech nor in writing; their true essence cannot be presented. Just as sweetness, sourness, and bitterness cannot be adequately described by anyone who attempts to put them into words, so too is it here. *If you reach this destination of high spiritual station, you will come to know all of this by yourself.* If you cannot reach that destination, the truth can be described in writing and speech *to some extent*.

Some of the disciple's questions, the author adds, have already been answered at length in the *Revival of the Knowledge of the Path to God* and in his other books; he refers the disciple back to those, and promises that in this letter he will only summarize, God willing.

## The four conditions for the seeker

Then the author begins the practical regimen. The disciple had also asked what is essential for those walking on the Path of God, the Exalted — that is, for the sincere seekers of God. The author gives four conditions.

The first condition is *the conviction of reverent fear of God* — to such a degree that the seeker does not become involved in innovation, defined as any matter contrary to the established principles of the Quran and the Prophet's conduct. That conduct, the author glosses, encompasses the teachings, actions, outward behavior, and inner states of the Prophet — including contentment, patience, gratitude, certitude in God, intimate relationship with God, intimate knowledge of God, intense love of God, and so forth.

The second condition is *true and complete repentance* — repentance made in such a way that the seeker will *never return to the humiliation of sins*.

The third condition is that *even one's enemies should be kept satisfied* to such an extent that none of God's creation holds any unfulfilled obligation against the seeker. The rights of the creatures are settled before the rights of the Creator are pursued.

The fourth condition is that the seeker should acquire enough knowledge of the Divine Law to follow and obey the commandments of God that are obligatory upon him or her. *It is not an obligation to learn more from the knowledge of the Divine Law than that.* From other branches of knowledge, one should learn only to the extent that is obligatory. The author is precise here in a way the disciple, given his complaint, is meant to feel directly. The condition is *sufficiency*, not *exhaustiveness*. The seeker is not obligated to read every book in every science. The seeker is obligated to know enough of the law to *obey* it.

## Marcus — four thousand traditions, four hundred masters, one practice

To press the point of *sufficiency*, the author reaches back to one of the most cited masters of the early sciences of the heart, Marcus, and reports his startling confession:

> I served four hundred spiritual masters and guides, and I adopted — that is, acted upon — only one out of the four thousand traditions of the Prophet that they narrated, and I left the rest of the traditions aside. This is because when I deliberated on this one tradition, I found my salvation within it. I found in this one tradition the knowledge of all who came first and all who came last.
> — Marcus, in the traditions of the intimate friends of God

The tradition Marcus adopted is the rule that fits the whole of life onto a single calibration:

> Work for this world as much as you will live in it, and work for the life hereafter as much as you wish to live there, and work for God, the Exalted, as much as you are in need of Him, and work toward avoiding the Fire as much as you can bear its pain and sufferings.
> — The Prophet, in the canonical hadith collections

The rule is four lines that hold the whole of a life. *Work for the world to the measure of your time in it.* The world is short; the work for it is small. *Work for the Hereafter to the measure of your time there.* The Hereafter is endless; the work for it is great. *Work for God to the measure of your need of Him.* Need of Him is total; work for Him is total. *Work to escape the Fire to the measure of what you can bear of its pain.* You can bear nothing of it; work to escape it must therefore be unceasing.

The author's commentary is exact. *It is evident from this tradition,* he writes, *that you do not need a vast quantity of knowledge.* Studying and acquiring knowledge beyond one's personal obligation is a communal obligation discharged when a sufficient number of people within the community fulfil it. If no one within the community discharges it, the whole community bears the sin of the neglect. But the individual seeker is not obligated to discharge it singly.

The author closes the long opening on a note that prepares the next chapter directly. *Pay special attention to the following tradition,* he writes, *so that you may attain a high level of certitude.*

## The threshold — the master from Balkh and the disciple of thirty-three years

The author begins the next vignette in the chapter's closing breath. *It is said that among the students and disciples of Stephen the master from Balkh was a student and disciple named Henry, who in turn later became a great spiritual master through the training and guidance of his teacher.* The disciples in question are seekers of spiritual elevation and enlightenment, who pursue the purification of the soul from all evil desires and tendencies under the guidance of a spiritual master who has himself completely attained that purification and high station before God through the training, guidance, and proximity of another spiritual guide.

One day Stephen the master from Balkh, having taught Henry for many years, asked him: *For how long have you been in my company — my spiritual proximity — and have been listening to what I have been teaching?*

Henry replied: *Thirty-three years!*

Stephen asked: *In that long period of time, what benefit have you derived from me?*

Henry responded: *I have obtained eight benefits from you.*

Stephen, the author reports, said:

> To God we belong and to Him shall we return.
> — Quranic supplication recited at times of loss and grief

And then the master said to his disciple: *I have spent my entire life in teaching and training, and you have gained only eight benefits from me!*

Henry replied: *O my teacher! If you ask me for the truth, then the reality is exactly what I have just told you. I do not need anything more, and these eight benefits from knowledge are quite sufficient, for I am certain that I will attain salvation and success in this life and in the life hereafter through them.*

Stephen the master from Balkh, the author says, said to him: *Very well — tell me, what are those eight benefits?*

And here the chapter holds. The recitation of the eight benefits — what Henry distilled out of thirty-three years of his master's company — is the labor of the next chapter. The author has spent the long opening of his letter setting up the diagnosis: knowledge without action is madness; the highest knowledge of the path will not avail at the end if it is not joined to the action that the path demands; and the action that the path demands is conformity to the conduct of the Prophet, severance of the desires of the lower self by inner struggle, and the four conditions of reverent fear, true repentance, the settlement of the rights of creatures, and the sufficient knowledge of the Divine Law. Marcus's one-tradition-out-of-four-thousand has set the *form* of sufficiency. Henry's eight-benefits-out-of-thirty-three-years is about to set the *content*.

The disciple, reading his master's letter, has been brought to the door. The next chapter opens it.
