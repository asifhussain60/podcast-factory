# The Marketplace: Honest Earning, Trade, and Property

The Marketplace: Honest Earning, Trade, and Property

Allah has commanded the believer to seek what He has set aside of His bounty. He says, ﴿وَاسْأَلُوا اللَّهَ مِن فَضْلِهِ﴾, "And ask Allah of His bounty," and He says, ﴿فَإِذَا قُضِيَتِ الصَّلَاةُ فَانتَشِرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَابْتَغُوا مِن فَضْلِ اللَّهِ﴾, "When the prayer is concluded, spread out in the land and seek the bounty of Allah." The pursuit of provision is therefore not a lapse from religion but a thing religion enjoins, provided it is pursued in the manner Allah has prescribed.

A man came to the Prophet, (ع), and said, "O Messenger of Allah, my soul has not been content with anything of this world, nor is it satisfied by it." The Messenger of Allah, (ع), said to him, "Say: O Allah, please me with Your decree, bless me in what You have given me, and make me content with what You have apportioned for me, so that I may not wish to hasten what You have delayed, nor to delay what You have hastened." Contentment, then, is the first discipline of the one who earns, for the soul that cannot be satisfied will not be steadied by any increase of wealth.

It is narrated through the Imams of his household, from al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad, upon him be the prayers of Allah, from his father, from his forefathers, upon them be the prayers of Allah, from the Messenger of Allah, (ع), that he said, "The faithful spirit breathed into my heart that no soul shall die until it has completed its provision. So be measured and at ease in seeking sustenance. There is no servant of Allah but that he has a provision, and between him and it there is a veil. If he is patient with restraint, Allah brings it to him lawfully; and if he is not patient, he tears the veil and so consumes it unlawfully." The same provision reaches a man either way, but his patience decides whether it reaches him as a blessing or as a sin.

He said, (ع), "Do not let the slowness of any provision drive you to seek it through disobedience to Allah, and be gracious in your seeking, for what is with Allah is not attained by disobedience." And he said, "If one of you becomes destitute, let him go out and seek his provision, strike out into the earth, and seek the bounty of Allah. Let him not sit in his house, grieving himself and his family and becoming a burden upon others." And he said, "Beneath the shade of the Throne, on the Day when there is no shade but His, will be a man who went out striking the earth, seeking the bounty of Allah, to restrain himself from begging and to provide for his dependents."

A man came and said, "O Messenger of Allah, I am skilled in no trade that I take up without finding it goes badly for me." He said, "Look at something you have once managed to earn from, and hold fast to it." The man said, "The cutting and selling of acacia for tanning." He said, "Then keep to the acacia trade."

The Messenger of Allah, (ع), once passed through one of the markets and said, "O company of merchants, the merchant is a licentious man, and the licentious are in the Fire." When they heard this, they shut the doors of their shops and abandoned trade. The next day he passed through the market and saw no one there, and asked, "What is the matter with them?" It was said, "They abandoned trade, O Messenger of Allah, when they heard what you said yesterday." He said, "I say the same again today, except for the one who takes what is rightfully his and gives others their right." The condemnation, then, fell not upon trade itself but upon the dishonesty that so often accompanies it.

He said, (ع), "I was sent as a mercy to people; I was not sent as a merchant, nor as a farmer. The worst of this nation are its merchants and its cultivators, except for those among them who guard their religion jealously." And he said, "Verily Allah loves the servant who is easy in selling, easy in buying, easy in settling what he owes, and easy in seeking what is owed to him."

The Messenger of Allah, (ع), saw a strong young man driving fat, well-laden camels. It was said to him, "O Messenger of Allah, how excellent is this man's strength and the strength of his camels; if only it were in the way of Allah." The Messenger of Allah, (ع), said to the youth, "What do you do with these camels of yours?" He said, "I labor with them and earn my living from them." He said, "And what do you do with your earnings?" He said, "I have a wife and children whom I support, sparing them from having to ask of others, and I pay off a debt I owe." He, (ع), said, "If he speaks truthfully, he has a reward like the reward of the one who fights in the way of Allah, and of the one who performs the Hajj and the Umrah." And Ali, upon him be the prayers of Allah, said, "The setting out of one of you in the way of Allah is no greater than his setting out to seek for his children and dependents that which will sustain them."

He said, upon him be peace, to a man who asked his permission to take up trade, "Have you gained understanding in religion?" The man said, "Some of it." He said, "Woe to you. Understanding first, then the marketplace; for whoever buys and sells without understanding falls into usury," and by usury here he meant that which is deferred. Knowledge of what is lawful must precede the transaction, or the trader sinks unknowingly into what is forbidden.

Muhammad ibn al-Munkadir said, "I saw Muhammad ibn Ali ibn al-Husayn, upon him be peace, on a day of severe heat. He had come in from a farm and was a heavy man, worn out with fatigue. I said to myself, I shall counsel a man of his nobility. I said, May I be your ransom. With your standing of honor, you exert yourself like this in seeking the world? Tell me, if your appointed hour came upon you in this state, what would you say of it? He said, Were my hour to come upon me in this state, it would find me in an act of obedience to Allah. By it I restrain myself from needing others and prepare provision for my Hereafter, striving to earn from what is lawful. What I would truly fear is that my hour should come upon me while I was in disobedience to Allah Almighty, and I seek refuge in Allah from that. So I said to him, May I be your ransom. I meant to counsel you, and you have counseled me."

A man said to Ja'far ibn Muhammad, upon him be the prayers of Allah, "O son of the Messenger of Allah, pray to Allah to grant me provision in ease, without effort." He said, "I will not pray to Allah for that. Seek as Allah Almighty has commanded you." And he said, upon him be peace, "It befits the Muslim to seek his provision until the heat of the sun reaches him."

Among the supplications of the Ahl al-Bayt, upon them be peace, is this: "O Allah, do not burden me with seeking what You have not apportioned for me, lest my occupation with it draw me away from obeying You and I be unable to attain any of it. O Allah, whatever You have apportioned for me of this, bring it to me in chastity and ease, and set me right by that which You have set right the righteous, for the righteousness of the righteous comes from You." It is reported, in the words of Abu Muhammad ibn Ali, (رض) and have mercy and blessing upon him, that this was among the supplications of Dawud, upon him be peace.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad, upon him be peace, said to Sufyan, who had come asking him, "O Sufyan, when a blessing appears upon you, praise Allah; and when something of your provision is slow in coming, seek the forgiveness of Allah." Then he recited, "Ask forgiveness of your Lord; verily He is ever Forgiving. He will send the sky upon you in abundant rain," up to His words, "and will make for you rivers." "And when something of the terrors of this world befalls you, say: There is no power and no strength save with Allah, the Most High, the Most Great." Sufyan said, "Grant me these three, and what are the three?"

He, upon him be peace, also said to a man among his Shi'a, counseling him, "When you go out, morning or evening, to seek your provision, and you desire success in it, pray two units of prayer. When you have finished the tashahhud, say: O Allah, I have gone forth, if I am one going forth, or set out, if I am one setting out, seeking of Your bounty as You commanded me. So grant me of Your bounty a lawful and wholesome provision as You commanded me, and grant me well-being in what You have provided for me. Say that three times. Then pray two more units, and after the tashahhud say: I have gone forth, if I am one going forth, or set out, if I am one setting out, by the power of Allah and His strength, with no power or strength of my own, but by Your power, O my Lord, and Your strength. I declare myself free, O my Lord, before You, of all power and strength. O Allah, I ask You for the good of this day, if it is day, or the good of this night, if it is night, and I ask You to provide me from Your bounty a lawful and wholesome provision, driven to me by Your power and Your strength, while I remain at ease, in chastity. Say that three times, then rise to your purpose."

He said, upon him be peace, "Allah Almighty has divided provision among His servants, and He has reserved beyond it a great surplus that He did not divide among them; so ask Allah of His bounty." And he said, "The one who sets out in pursuit of lawful provision is like the one who strives in the way of Allah." And he said to a man of his Shi'a who was engaged in trade, "When you seek something of this world and it escapes you, remember what Allah Almighty has singled you out for from His religion, and the favor He has shown you in turning others away from it. That is more fitting to lift your soul above the loss."

Ali ibn al-Husayn, upon him be the prayers of Allah, said, "Allah Almighty has made provision ten parts: nine of them in trade and one part in all other things."

A man said to Abu Abd Allah Ja'far ibn Muhammad, upon him be the prayers of Allah, to whom he was devoted, "O son of the Messenger of Allah, I have abandoned trade, waiting for the appearance of your cause." He said to him, "Do not do so. Open your door, spread out your mat, and seek provision from your Lord, for that is better for you. If you hold back from seeking your provision, your wealth will dwindle. So seek of the bounty of Allah, your Lord."

On What Is Forbidden to Sell

The Messenger of Allah, (ع), forbade the sale of free persons and declared it void, and likewise the sale of carrion and of wine. It is transmitted from the Imams of his household, upon them be the prayers of Allah, that everything unlawful may neither be sold, nor earned from, nor its price taken, such as filth, carrion, blood, swine, wine, every intoxicant, and all instruments of idle amusement. They forbade the making of such things, the earning from them, the selling and buying of them, the learning of them, and the lending of any assistance in them.

They permitted, however, the sale of grapes, pressed juice, raisins, honey, dates, and figs, even to one who will turn them into wine or an intoxicant, because the sale of these goods is itself lawful, and the sin falls only upon the one who renders them unlawful. They likewise permitted the taking of the price in every case where the sale of a thing is forbidden, when it is sold to one who will use it for himself, though it remains forbidden for that buyer to do so. As for the price received by one into whose hands a thing has passed by a lawful means, there is no harm in it.

There is no harm, either, in dealing with the polytheists and taking the price of what they buy from the Muslims, nor in what is in their hands from the price of things they have sold which are not lawful to sell. Most of their wealth is from usury and unlawful gain, yet it is taken from them as the jizya and as the price of what they purchase from the Muslims, and it becomes lawful for the one who takes it by its rightful means.

They, upon them be peace, disliked the price of the biting dog, but permitted the price of the hunting dog, and the sale of the herding dog is allowed. They disliked the price of the cat. They forbade buying what one knows to be stolen or taken by treachery until it is returned to its owners, who may then sell it. And they permitted the sale of copies of the Qur'an.

There comes from the Messenger of Allah, (ع), a general prohibition against the sale of water, of pasture (al-kala), and of fire. This prohibition falls upon the sale of what no one owns, such as water lying in the open wilderness or rainwater gathered there, and other such waters that are owned by none, and the flame of a fire from which a man lights his lamp or takes a brand without diminishing it. But if a man has a well he has dug on his own property, or a flowing spring, or a river he has developed, or a cistern, or the like in which rainwater settles upon his land and property, or water he has stored in a vessel, or carried upon his back or his mount or by any other means, then his sale of it is permitted. Likewise, if he has gathered pasture grass from the wild, or uprooted it, or tended and watered it upon land he owns, the sale of it is permitted. And so with fire, when it resides in something owned, such as firewood or charcoal or anything else in which fire is kindled, its sale is permitted, for it is property among the kinds of property.

There is no harm in buying from and selling to one known for treachery, theft, or other ill conduct, unless one knows that the particular thing being bought from him is itself stolen or taken by treachery. If one does not know this, there is no harm in dealing with him. We have already said that buying from and selling to the polytheists is permitted so long as one does not know that they took the goods by treachery or theft, and the Muslim is the more bound to inquire into the matter.

On the Prohibition of the Sale of Gharar

Allah Almighty has said, ﴿وَلَا تَأْكُلُوا أَمْوَالَكُم بَيْنَكُم بِالْبَاطِلِ﴾, "And do not consume one another's wealth among yourselves unjustly." The Messenger of Allah, (ع), as narrated from him by the pure Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt, upon them all be the prayers of Allah, forbade in general the sale of gharar. The sale of gharar is every sale concluded upon a thing that is unknown. The Messenger of Allah, (ع), explained several of its forms.

Among them, he forbade the sale of habal al-habalah, the offspring of an offspring. Of this kind was a trade the people of the time of ignorance practiced: one of them would sell a commodity against the price of the offspring of a she-camel once she had given birth and her young had itself produced young. Of this kind, too, is the sale of an animal's offspring before it is born, for they used to trade in what was still in the wombs of camels and other beasts.

Among them, he forbade, (ع), the sale of al-madamin and al-malaqih. The malaqih are the embryos we have mentioned, sold while still in their mothers wombs; the madamin are what is in the loins of the stallions, for they used to trade in what a stallion would sire over a stated period, once or a fixed number of times.

Among them, he forbade the sale of al-mulamasah, the sale by touch. This is when a man sells a garment or some article folded up, its inside unseen, or makes the mere touching of it with his hand take the place of looking at it, on the condition that the buyer has no choice in the matter once he sees it, unfolds it, or spreads it out; or when he says, "When you touch this commodity, the purchase between us is binding." But the contract of sale is made binding only by the spoken word and the parting of the two parties in mutual consent to it. Of this kind, too, is the touching of goods from behind a curtain so that the sale is concluded on that alone.

And among them, he forbade, (ع), the sale of al-munabadhah. This is when a man looks at a folded garment in another man's hand and says, "I buy this from you, and when you cast it to me the sale between us is complete, with no right of withdrawal for either of us"; or when each of the two casts to the other a garment or some article.

Among the sales the Prophet (may Allah bless him and his family) nullified were those in which a man would conclude a bargain by saying simply, "This for this," without turning the goods over or examining them. Among them too was the sale of pebbles, a practice of the pre-Islamic era in which the two parties sealed the contract by each throwing his pebble, making that throw the binding act of sale. He nullified all such sales.

He also prohibited the sale of al-wala and the giving of it as a gift, saying that al-wala belongs only to those who hold it by lineage and is neither to be sold nor given away. He prohibited the sale of a slave on condition of keeping him before he had come into one's possession, as well as the sale of a stray camel or a similar animal before it is found.

Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) said: If such a stray animal, or anything like it among absent goods, is sold together with something present, such as a slave, a beast, a garment, or any other permissible item that is at hand, then the sale of that combination is permissible, provided that both seller and buyer know the stray or absent item. Upon its arrival, the price falls upon the present item that the two parties have seen. Such a sale does not fall under the words of Allah Almighty, ﴿وَلَا تَأْكُلُوا أَمْوَالَكُمْ بَيْنَكُمْ بِالْبَاطِلِ﴾, "And do not consume one another's wealth unjustly" (Al-Baqarah 188).

Ali ((ع)) said: Likewise, if a man sells the milk in the udder of his sheep together with a quantity already drawn from it that is lawful to sell, the sale is permissible, because the price falls upon the present portion that the two parties have seen. Most sales are of this kind: the price rests upon what is seen, or upon the part that is seen and purchased while the greater portion remains concealed, as with houses, dwellings, garments, and animals. One sees only what is apparent and not what is hidden, yet the sale covers both the visible and the hidden, both what has been examined and what has not.

It is permissible to buy the dust of mines with gold and silver, hand to hand, even if upon extraction it yields more or less than the price paid, because at the moment of purchase it was neither silver nor gold but merely dust; selling it on deferred terms, however, is disliked.

It is narrated from Ali ((ع)) that he disliked the sale of fish still in the water when they can be reached only by fishing; but if a catch is already secured and can be had without hunting, selling it is permissible. The Prophet forbade the sale of wool upon the backs of sheep, and of their hides likewise, until the wool is shorn and the hide flayed. He forbade a man to sell his share of the spoils before he has received it, because he does not know what will come to him from it. And the sale of sukuk, that is, written certificates of debt, is forbidden.

### The Sale of Fruits

The Imams of the Prophet's household narrated that he forbade, in general terms, the sale of fruits until their goodness becomes apparent. Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) said that the ripening of fruits begins when they turn red or yellow. The Prophet meant their coming to soundness: if something of the fruit becomes good, it is permissible to sell the whole of it, and if none has yet ripened, the sale rests upon the principle already mentioned concerning present and absent goods. If nothing has appeared that is free of what is objectionable, the sale is not permissible unless both parties agree to it; and if either of them moves to annul it, it is annulled. If one kind among its varieties shows ripeness, that kind may be sold along with the others even if nothing of them has ripened, provided the whole is sold in a single transaction, all upon the principle already named.

The Imams ((عليهم السلام)) said: When the fruit blooms, or part of it does, it may be sold, together with what its trees will bear of such fruit for a year or two afterward, so long as this is named and stated. If nothing comes forth in the future, the price falls upon what was present at the time of sale. This too rests upon the principle already mentioned.

The generality of people do not permit selling fruit for years to come, claiming that it is gharar, an uncertain and deceptive sale, yet they permit selling it once some of it shows goodness. Their position is not consistent. The goodness that appears while the sale encompasses the remainder falls under the same ruling, and this is precisely what they denied and rejected, for they objected that part of the sale should fall upon what does not yet exist, namely that whose soundness has not yet begun, while they themselves allowed selling such fruit together with what had already become sound, with the price resting upon what had become sound. So likewise Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) said: It is permissible to sell the fruit once its goodness, or the goodness of part of it, has begun, for a year or two afterward; if it does not come forth in this year, it comes forth in the next.

It is the duty of the people to follow the saying of the Imams ((عليهم السلام)) and to abandon dispute with them, for what has come from them ((عليهم السلام)) is itself a proof, and whoever holds to it is spared the search for further evidence or witness. The most that has been urged by those who reject the sale of fruit for years is an undefined hadith they claim to have narrated from the Prophet (may Allah bless him and his family), that he forbade "the sale of years," without mentioning fruit or anything else. This hadith, if it is established, is an undefined one, and the interpretation of the Imams ((عليهم السلام)) makes plain that its intent is not what the generality have supposed. If it is taken as general, then they are bound to apply it to everything that falls within its scope, and it admits many meanings. Among them is that "years," in the speech of the Arabs, denotes times of hardship and adversity. They say, "The years have come upon us," meaning the hardships and trials, and they call a year of drought and distress "a year." On this reading, the prohibition would fall upon whatever is sold during times of hardship, so that every sale made in them would be void. It is also possible that, in the figurative usage of the language, "the sale of years" means selling by the year and for years to come, which itself admits many meanings. To follow what has come with explanation from the Imams ((عليهم السلام)) is more deserving of acceptance, especially what the foundational principles attest to and upon which the subsidiary rulings are built.

Whoever sells fruit whose soundness has not yet appeared, and the buyer accepts it, and neither of them moves to cancel the sale but both proceed with it, then the sale is permissible and not forbidden, despite what has been narrated of its prohibition. As Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) said: The Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family) forbade the sale of fruits before their soundness appeared only because people would buy them while they were still liable to perish, since blight might strike them before they were complete and good, and disputes over this would be brought before the Messenger of Allah. When such disputes grew many, he forbade them from selling until the soundness became clear; he did not make it unlawful for them.

He ((ع)) said: Whoever sells his fruit and reserves from it something known, that sale is permissible; and whoever makes a sale reserving from it something unknown, that sale is not permissible, because it was concluded over something unknown. He said: If the buyer sells the fruit before he gathers it, the sale is permissible.

The Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family) forbade al-muzabana, which is the sale of dates still on the heads of the palms for a measured quantity of dates, and of grapes still on the vine for raisins. He said that grain may not be sold before it can be reaped in the customary manner. If it is sold for wheat, or on the condition that it be left standing, and the buyer reaps it or leaves it until it ripens fully, there is no harm in that; and if it is bought for something other than wheat, then reaped or left until it ripens, there is no harm in that either. Thus it is permissible to sell standing crops and fresh herbage when that is known, the buyer taking it as one cutting or several where the plant grows back, while the root remains the seller's once the cuttings are spent.

The Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family) said: Whoever buys palm trees that already bear fruit which has appeared, its fruit belongs to the seller, unless the buyer stipulates that it is his. Under the same ruling falls the sale of trees bearing fruit that has reached the stage the fruit of the palm reaches at the time of pollination: if the buyer does not stipulate it, it belongs to the seller.

### The Prohibition of Cheating and Deception

The Imams from the household of the Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and them all) narrated that he said, "من غشنا فليس منا," that is, "Whoever cheats us is not one of us." He forbade cheating, treachery, deception, and beguilement in selling, and the breaking of pledges, and he commanded the fulfillment of contracts in selling, buying, marriage, and covenant.

His words, "Whoever cheats us is not one of us," mean that such a person is not among those who follow us in our command and belong to our company. They do not mean that whoever cheats thereby leaves the religion of Islam and apostatizes into unbelief; for were that so, he would incur what the apostate from Islam incurs, namely death. Allah Almighty said, telling of Ibrahim ((ع)), ﴿فَمَن تَبِعَنِي فَإِنَّهُ مِنِّي وَمَنْ عَصَانِي فَإِنَّكَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ﴾, "Whoever follows me is of me, and whoever disobeys me, then You are Forgiving and Merciful." So too the one who cheats has not followed the command of the Messenger of Allah and is therefore not of those who followed his command; yet if he is a Muslim, he is among those who followed him but disobeyed him in some of what he commanded, and his affair is referred to Allah Almighty: if He wills He forgives him, and if He wills He punishes him, as with other disobedient Muslims.

Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) said: Mixing two grades of food, or the like among goods, the good with the inferior, when it is done for the sake of sale, is cheating. He meant that it is cheating when the good is what is shown to the buyer while the inferior is concealed. But if the two are shown together, or the inferior is shown while the good is hidden, that is not cheating. Ali ((ع)) used to go about the markets of Kufa warning the people against cheating, saying, "Show the poorer part of your wares just as you show the good part of them."

He ((ع)) forbade blowing into meat and said, "إنما النفخ من الشيطان فلا تنفخوا في طعام ولا شراب," that is, "Blowing is from the devil, so do not blow into food or drink." He permitted blowing to separate the hide from the flesh in skinning, but forbade blowing into the flesh itself, for the reason mentioned, that it is a form of cheating: such blowing runs into the sinews and the thin membranes beneath the skin so that the flesh appears to be fat when it is not. He forbade mixing milk with water for one who means to sell it; but for one who means to drink it himself, there is no harm in that.

The Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family) forbade tatfif, the giving of short measure and short weight, taking more than one's due and giving less than is owed. Whoever gives full measure, or more, to the one he sells to, or takes less than his due from him, has done well; and whoever takes his right and renders the like has acted justly. So Allah, exalted be His mention, said, ﴿وَيْلٌ لِلْمُطَفِّفِينَ * الَّذِينَ إِذَا اكْتَالُوا عَلَى النَّاسِ يَسْتَوْفُونَ * وَإِذَا كَالُوهُمْ أَو وَزَنُوهُمْ يُخْسِرُونَ﴾, "Woe to the defrauders, who, when they take measure from people, take in full, but when they measure or weigh for them, give less" (Al-Mutaffifin 1-3); that is, when they receive they take their full due, and when they give they fall short. This is in matters of sale and exchange. As for the gift and the voluntary charity, whoever renders it in full has the greater reward, and if he falls short in it there is nothing against him; but if it is an obligation, he must render it in full.

Ali ((ع)) commanded that one be wary in buying and selling with dinars and dirhams, and ordered that nothing of the debased among them enter his treasury. This concerns coin whose badness is hidden, scarcely known except to those of expert eye, while it passes upon others. As for debased coin that is well known and scarcely hidden from anyone, taken at its proper rate, or known mintings that exceed one another in value, there is no harm in buying and selling with them, nor with coins of pure copper, nor with other things that are known and not obscure. It is narrated from Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) that he forbade differing measures and weights within a single town, because of the doubt and confusion that this admits.

The Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family) forbade tasriyah: that one who wishes to sell a ewe or a milking camel should leave it unmilked for a day or two so that the milk gathers in its udder, then sell it in that state so that the buyer, seeing the swollen udder, desires it. The buyer is thereby deceived and harmed. So the buyer has three days, that is, after he has milked it, within which, if he wishes, he may keep it, or if he wishes, return it together with a sa of dates in compensation for the milk he took from it; and if he found little milk in it, nothing more is owed. This is the option that belongs to the sale of animals, khiyar al-hayawan: the musarrat is returned even if the buyer has renounced the three-day option, provided the seller concealed the defect while knowing of it before the sale. But if the seller made it known to him before the sale, and disclosed it, and told him how many days the milking had been withheld, and the buyer was content with that, then he has no right to return it on account of the tasriyah, unless he finds in it some other defect.

The Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family) forbade najsh in transactions, saying, "لا تدابروا ولا تناجشوا," that is, "Do not turn your backs upon one another, and do not drive up bids against one another." Those who turn their backs fall into severance, enmity, harshness, and estrangement; it is as though he meant that none of you should turn his back upon his brother in this way. Najsh is that one who has no wish to buy approaches a commodity and raises its price, not desiring to purchase it, but so that another may hear him and raise his own bid upon that, intending thereby to inflate the price against the buyer. This is a kind of deception, for the bidder is taken as a guide and so others bid above him; therefore it was forbidden.

The Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family) forbade that a townsman sell on behalf of a Bedouin. The meaning is that the Bedouin brings a commodity and hands it to the townsman to sell for him, and the townsman sells it without letting it be offered openly in the market; this much is plain in the wording of the prohibition. But if the townsman cries the goods for him, or calls out their price and makes it known and gives his counsel, then informs the owner of what he has learned, and the owner orders him to sell, or comes and sells it himself, this does not fall under the prohibition, because a command issued for an act ascribes that act to the one who commanded it. As they say, the Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family) cut off the hand of a thief, stoned a married adulterer, and put to death those upon whom death was due, though all of this was carried out by his command; and as they say, "The prince killed so-and-so," or "struck so-and-so," even when it was another who performed the deed by his order. So when the Bedouin orders the townsman to sell his goods, it is the Bedouin who has sold them; but if the townsman or another sells them without having been ordered, and without the owner's leave, the sale is not permissible unless the owner of the goods authorizes it. This is what the prohibition addressed.

The Prophet ((ع)) forbade going out to meet the *rukban*, the caravans and traders arriving with their goods before they reach the city or the marketplace where they mean to sell. The prohibition guards against two harms: that the arriving merchants be wronged, since the one who rides out to meet them knows the prevailing prices and market values of which the sellers themselves are unaware, and that those townspeople who could not ride out be shut out from buying. It is also a kind of deception, because the one who goes to meet them holds knowledge of price and worth that the seller does not. Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) set its measure: whoever goes out to meet goods at a distance of a day's journey or less falls within the prohibition; beyond that distance it is no longer counted as meeting them. And whenever the goods are dressed up so that their price is raised with no benefit to the buyer, that is deceit (*ghishsh*) and is forbidden.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) was asked about a man who buys food and then sells the same measure of it in return. He said: "If he does not add to its price, there is no harm in it; but if he uses it to deceive the Muslims, there is no good in it, and it is not sound." He was also asked about a man who buys food by weight and finds in it more than he had paid for. He replied: "If the surplus is of the kind that people do not haggle over, the sort of variance that may ordinarily occur between two measurings, there is no harm in it. But if it is excessive, it is an error and must be returned to the seller, unless the seller is content to leave it with him."

From Ali ((ع)): he permitted the buyer to ask the seller for a little more, to tip the balance in the buyer's favor or to add to the measure, but only after the seller has given the buyer his full due, this being a request from the buyer. If the seller's heart is content with it, he may do it, and it is wholesome for the buyer; if his heart is not content, the buyer has no right to compel him. And if the buyer stipulates such an increase as a condition within the contract of sale while it remains unspecified, the sale is void; but if it is specified, the sale stands, and the known increase enters into the contract of measure and weight. Justice in this is what keeps to the truth, neither adding nor subtracting, and that is the fulfillment (*al-wafa*) that Allah Almighty commanded, where He says, and He is the most truthful of speakers:

﴿أَوْفُوا الْكَيْلَ وَلَا تَكُونُوا مِنَ الْمُخْسِرِينَ ۝ وَزِنُوا بِالْقِسْطَاسِ الْمُسْتَقِيمِ ۝ وَلَا تَبْخَسُوا النَّاسَ أَشْيَاءَهُمْ وَلَا تَعْثَوْا فِي الْأَرْضِ مُفْسِدِينَ﴾

"Give full measure and be not of those who cause loss. Weigh with the straight balance (*al-qistas*). And do not defraud people of their things, nor go about the earth spreading corruption." (al-Shu'ara 181-183)

Fulfillment is justice and truth, without increase or decrease. Shortchanging (*al-bakhs*) is to give less than is due, and skimping (*al-tatfif*) is to take in full for oneself yet give short to others, as Allah Almighty has said, and He is the most truthful of speakers:

﴿وَيْلٌ لِّلْمُطَفِّفِينَ ۝ الَّذِينَ إِذَا اكْتَالُوا عَلَى النَّاسِ يَسْتَوْفُونَ ۝ وَإِذَا كَالُوهُمْ أَوْ وَزَنُوهُمْ يُخْسِرُونَ﴾

"Woe to those who skimp, who, when they take by measure from people, take in full, but when they give to them by measure or by weight, give less." (al-Mutaffifin 1-3)

### Mention of What He Forbade Regarding Sales

The Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt ((عليهم السلام)) narrated that the Prophet ((ع)) forbade two conditions in a single sale, as when a man says, "I sell you this commodity for such a price in cash and for such a price on credit," leaving the choice to the buyer, so that whichever he takes, the lesser in cash or the greater on a deferred term, is his, and the contract is concluded upon this. Such a sale is not permissible, because the price is left indeterminate. Of the same kind is to sell a thing for dinars on the condition that the seller receive their value in dirhams at a set rate; or to sell on the condition that the other party in turn sell him some other thing, whether guaranteed or not, for immediate or deferred delivery; or to sell for a fixed term at a certain price and then raise the price whenever the term is extended. All of this and its like is two conditions in one sale, a corrupt sale when the two parties contract it and part upon it. But if what passed between them was only negotiation, and they then agreed afterward upon one of the two sales, annulling the other, and parted upon that, it is permissible, and the sale is whatever they settled upon.

They also narrated that he ((ع)) forbade profiting from what one does not yet guarantee (*ribh ma lam yudman*): something measured or weighed that the seller is owed by a debtor, or a provision due to come to him, which he sells before he has taken possession of it. Of this kind too is the craftsman who accepts a piece of work for a wage and then hands it on to another to do for less than he undertook it for, pocketing the difference, though he neither labored in it nor stood surety for it. Yet if he has done something to it, or contributed some part of the work himself, the surplus is lawful to him. Of the same kind is a man who rents a dwelling and then sublets it for more than he pays; this is not allowed. But they permitted him to sublet part of it for the like of what he himself pays and to live in the remainder. And if he has tended the property in some part, or kept it up, or seen to some of its affairs, the surplus becomes lawful to him. So it is with a *khan* that a man rents and then opens, for whose door he hangs the curtain, which he sweeps and whose affairs he attends to, and whose rooms he sublets at a profit: there is no harm in that surplus, in return for the care he has expended.

They narrated that the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) forbade a sale joined to a loan (*bay wa salaf*), as when a man says to another, "I will sell you such-and-such on the condition that you lend me such-and-such," or "Lend me a loan on the understanding that I owe you so much." If the sale is contracted on this footing, it is void; the borrower owes only what was actually lent, and the sale is annulled. But if the loan was given without being made a condition of the sale, the sale is not spoiled.

They narrated that he ((ع)) also forbade selling what one does not possess (*bay ma laysa indak*), as when a man bargains over something he does not have. If the sale is contracted, the thing belongs to the buyer, and the buyer keeps the option once he sees it. Of this kind is selling, for guaranteed future delivery, at a time when the like of that thing cannot be had, as with grapes or fruit in a season when there are none. This is the forbidden *kali* for *kali*, which is to say debt for debt, and it is not permissible. An example is a man who hands another a sum of dinars for a specified quantity of food to be delivered at a set time; when the time comes and the food is not to be found, he buys it from a man who owes him a debt payable at a later date, and lets that food stand against a deferred price. This is not permissible, for it is a debt within a debt. Or a man is owed a debt that has fallen due, and the two agree to extend the term in exchange for an increase in the debt: this is not lawful. But if the debtor is released from part of it and pays the rest early, before the term, there is no harm in that, for had the creditor remitted the whole of it to him, that would have been good.

They said: of *kali* for *kali* is that a man owes another a debt, and the creditor has him perform some work against it by an agreement between them; this is void. The worker is owed the wage of his like, when one or both of them acts upon it, and the surplus is returned, or taken if something remains owing to him. But if the debt was for a fixed term and it is collected early, before it falls due, against the forgoing of part of it, there is no harm. Of this kind also is that a man hires a riding beast and then transfers the hire onto another; this is not permissible. And of it is a debt owed to a group of men who divide it on the condition that each collects the share that falls to him; this is not permissible, and whatever each of them collects is shared among them all. Whatever falls within this meaning, entering into the category of debt for debt, is of the kind of *kali* for *kali*. But if two men each owe the other a debt, of food or other goods, of an amount neither knows, and they mutually agree that each release his fellow from what he owes in exchange for what is his, there is no harm in that, because it is something they have settled by agreement, unspecified, and is not of the category of *kali* for *kali*.

They permitted (may (عليهم السلام)) the sale of animals one for another, one for more than its like. It is narrated that Ali ((ع)) sold a camel of his named Usayfir for twenty camels to be delivered at a future term; and this is permissible when the camels are described. They forbade (may (عليهم السلام)) selling meat for a live animal. And they forbade a man's bidding over the bid of his brother (*sawm al-rajul ala sawm akhihi*). This applies once the seller has inclined to conclude the sale; but the mere raising of offers in a transaction still open in the public call, or one held open for bidding, is no harm. The Messenger of Allah ((ع)) himself sold by auction to the highest bidder some of what he was commanded to sell. They permitted the resale at cost (*tawliyah*) of food before taking possession of it, in the purchase of one who buys fruits upon the tops of the trees, and in the sale of food by the seller's own measure when the buyer knows this and trusts him in it.

They forbade the hoarding of food (*ihtikar*). It is narrated that the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) was severe and stern about it. Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) said: the Messenger of Allah was harsh in his reproof of Hakim ibn Hizam, a man of Quraysh of great wealth, who, whenever food entered Madinah, would buy all of it and hoard it to sell at his own price. He said: As for a man who buys food while it is plentiful among the people, in order to store it and trade in it, there is no harm in that, so long as the people can still find what to buy. But when none is to be found, he has no right to hoard it against them and leave them to perish; he must bring it out and sell it. They forbade (may (عليهم السلام)) pricing above the rate of the place (*al-tas'ir*). Whoever departs from the local price is told: either sell at the people's price, as they sell, or take your food up out of their market, so that you bring no inflation upon them.

Ali ((ع)) was asked about an unjust ruler (*sultan ja'ir*) who seizes a man for the payment of a sum and so drives him to sell his property: may he afterward reclaim it? He said: No. The buyer did not compel him; rather he did him a service, in relieving him of the harm, by what he bought from him, so long as he did not force him to the sale, and so long as it was that unjust ruler who drove him to it. The sale of one compelled is to be annulled only when he was coerced into the sale, driven to it and forced upon it; such a one may take action against the one who forced and compelled him. But if it was only the unjust ruler who drove him to sell, and, fearing punishment if he refused, he sold to one who neither compelled him nor was the one to whom that ruler forced him to sell in particular, then the sale is valid and there is no revoking it.

It is narrated that the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) commanded a man who had sold a house to put its price into the like of it. This was a command of discipline (*ta'dib*), for it has been related that when estates are sold and their price is not placed into the like of them, that wealth comes to nothing. And he ((ع)) forbade buying and selling in the mosque, during the time of the Friday prayer, and before the rising of the sun. Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) said: the second call to prayer on Friday, that is, the one called before the Imam while he is upon the pulpit, is what forbids buying and selling until the Friday prayer has been performed.

### On Currency Exchange (al-Sarf)

Allah Almighty said:

﴿يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَأْكُلُوا الرِّبَا أَضْعَافًا مُضَاعَفَةً وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ﴾

"O you who believe, do not devour riba, doubled and multiplied, and fear Allah, that you may prosper." (Al Imran 130)

The Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt of the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) narrated that he cursed the one who consumes *riba*, the one who pays it, the one who sells through it, the one who buys, the one who records it, and its two witnesses. He said: the consumer of *riba* will be raised on the Day of Resurrection as a madman. The Commander of the Believers, Ali ((ع)), said: *riba* is among the major sins.

Abu Abdullah al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) was asked about the words of Allah Almighty, ﴿يَمْحَقُ اللَّهُ الرِّبَا وَيُرْبِي الصَّدَقَاتِ﴾, "Allah blots out *riba* and causes acts of charity to grow." It was said to him: We have seen some who deal in *riba* grow great in wealth. He said: And what blotting could be more thorough than that of a single dirham of *riba*? If its owner repents of it, he returns it to its rightful owners and nothing of it remains to him; and if he withholds it and does not repent, He casts him into the Fire.

They said (may (عليهم السلام)) that the people of the covenant (*ahl al-dhimmah*) are to be prevented from dealing in *riba* and from practicing it openly. Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali ((ع)) said that the Messenger of Allah ((ع) and his family) did not accept the *jizyah* from the people of the covenant until he had stipulated upon them that they would not consume *riba* nor deal in it, nor eat the flesh of swine, nor marry their sisters, the daughters of their brothers, or the daughters of their sisters. Whoever of them does so today has no covenant of protection. For He, exalted be the Speaker, has said:

﴿فَبِظُلْمٍ مِنَ الَّذِينَ هَادُوا حَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ طَيِّبَاتٍ أُحِلَّتْ لَهُمْ وَبِصَدِّهِمْ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ كَثِيرًا ۝ وَأَخْذِهِمُ الرِّبَا وَقَدْ نُهُوا عَنْهُ وَأَكْلِهِمْ أَمْوَالَ النَّاسِ بِالْبَاطِلِ وَأَعْتَدْنَا لِلْكَافِرِينَ مِنْهُمْ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا﴾

"For the wrongdoing of those who became Jews We forbade them good things that had been lawful for them, and for their barring many from the way of Allah, and their taking of *riba* though they had been forbidden it, and their consuming people's wealth wrongfully. And We have prepared for the disbelievers among them a painful punishment." (al-Nisa 160-161)

So *riba* is forbidden upon them in their own Law, even if they deem it lawful for themselves. *Riba* in the language means increase. It is said, the wealth grew (*raba al-mal yarbu*), meaning it increased, and its owner makes it grow (*yurbi*) when he adds to it; from this the raised ground is called *rabwah*, for its rising above the land around it. Yet not every increase in wealth from a lawful source, even though it follows this same pattern, falls under the *riba* that is forbidden. Rather, the *riba* that is forbidden is what the Imams (may Allah bless them) have related from the Messenger of Allah ((ع)): the sale of what is measured or weighed at a disparity, each kind for its own kind. That is not permissible except like for like, hand to hand, with no increase and no decrease, whether from one of the two parties or the other.

From the Messenger of Allah ((ع)), he said: "Gold for gold, and silver for silver, hand to hand and weight for weight; whoever increases or seeks increase has dealt in *riba*. And wheat for wheat, barley for barley, flour for flour, dates for dates, raisins for raisins, corn for corn, basket for basket, and rice for rice, measure for measure; whoever increases or seeks increase has dealt in *riba*." Muhammad ibn Ali ((ع)) said: no copper coin (*fulus*), nor anything else, may be brought into the exchange of gold for gold or silver for silver as an increase upon the weight. Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) said: no increase is permissible in that, even if one of the two kinds is finer than the other. That is, when gold is sold for gold, or silver for silver, or any measured or weighed thing for its like, it is permissible only weight for weight if it is among what is weighed, or measure for measure if it is among what is measured. And if a man trades refined gold (*ibriz*) of the purest sort for gold of the lowest, it is permissible only weight for weight, so long as the name of gold still applies to it; until it becomes pure copper, copper predominating in it so that it is reckoned as copper and the name of gold no longer falls upon it, whereupon it may be bought with gold as copper is bought, at a disparity. And the same holds for silver against silver in this sense, and likewise for everything that is measured or weighed.

When two different metals are joined in a single object, such as a decorated sword or a ring adorned with one upon the other, al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (may Allah's blessings be upon him) taught that it should be sold against the opposite kind: gold against silver, or silver against gold. It is sold at the higher value, until it is known that the price exceeds the worth of the precious metal in it, so that gold is matched against gold and silver against silver, weight for weight, while the remaining portion of the price falls upon the sword, the ring, or whatever the object may be.

He also said that it is permissible to take dirhams in place of dinars, and dinars in place of dirhams. When a man is owed dinars, whether as an advance, from a sale, or from any rightful claim, he may receive dirhams instead at their value or at whatever the two agree upon; and likewise, when he is owed dirhams, he may receive dinars in their place.

He said that exchange (sarf) is lawful only hand to hand. If a man buys dinars for dirhams, dirhams for dinars, gold for silver, silver for gold, or any portion of one for the other, he must not part from his counterpart until he has taken delivery. If the other says, "Send a messenger and he will settle it for you," he should not accept this. Rather he should say, "Give and take," or remain with the man and not leave him. And if he does send a messenger along with him, he should instruct that messenger himself to take delivery on the spot before parting. Should either of them depart before delivery is taken, the exchange between them is void.

He said that whoever repays a loan of dirhams or dinars with something better than what was owed has done nothing wrong, provided it springs from the willing goodness of the borrower and was not stipulated as a condition at the time of the loan.

### On the Sale of Food to One Another

The pure Imams of the household of the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) narrated that he forbade any disparity in the exchange of goods of the same kind that are measured or weighed, as has already been mentioned. Whoever buys something for another of its own kind may take no excess on either side. But when the two kinds differ, disparity between them is permitted, just as it is permitted between silver and gold, or iron and similar metals whose kinds differ, so long as they belong to what is weighed or measured.

Accordingly al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (may Allah's blessings be upon him) said: it is not permissible to sell a sa of almonds for two sa's of almonds, nor a sa of dates for two sa's of dates; but it is permissible to sell a sa of wheat for two sa's of dates, and a sa of dates for two sa's of raisins. Foodstuffs and the dried fruits of the land follow a single rule: when the two kinds differ, there is no harm in disparity, hand to hand, though it is not lawful on deferred terms.

He said: barley and wheat are a single kind, and disparity between them is not permitted. Likewise wheat, flour, and sawiq may be exchanged only measure for measure. It was said to him: but there is a surplus in it, meaning in the grain. He answered: does it not carry a cost, meaning the milling and the other labor of its preparation? It was said: yes. He said: then the one is set against the other.

He said: the Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family) forbade the sale of fresh, moist dates for dried dates, because when the moist dates dry they diminish in measure. He permitted the sale of garments one for another, one garment for more than one. And he said: the Messenger of Allah forbade the sale of food for food by guesswork, without measure, but permitted the exchange of bread for flour, and vinegar for vinegar even when their kinds differ, and the molasses of sugar cane for the honey of bees, like for like, hand to hand.

### On the Option of the Two Parties to a Sale

The pure Imams of the household of the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) narrated that the two parties to a transaction retain the right to withdraw until they part by mutual consent. Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) explained that this parting is the parting of their bodies from the place in which they concluded the sale. He related that his father ((رض)) bought a piece of land, and when he wished to make the purchase binding upon himself he rose, walked a few steps, and then returned, intending by this to render the sale binding. He said: the Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family) said that the Muslims are bound by their conditions, except a condition that contradicts the Book of Allah, mighty and majestic.

He recalled the account of Barirah, who was a bondwoman belonging to a people who had granted her a contract of emancipation. She came to one of the wives of the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) to seek help with her emancipation payment, and that wife said to her: I will pay what you owe and set you free. But Barirah's masters said: we will not sell her to one who frees her unless her right of patronage (al-wala) remains with us. When this was reported to the Messenger of Allah ((ع)), he said: buy her and free her, for patronage belongs to the one who frees. Then he addressed the people and said: what is the matter with people who stipulate conditions not found in the Book of Allah? Every condition that contradicts the Book of Allah is void. Whoever makes a sale and stipulates in his contract a permissible condition, it is binding; but if he stipulates what is not permissible, his condition is void, just as the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) did not allow the condition of patronage made by those who sold Barirah, for he had declared that patronage belongs to the one who frees.

People differed concerning a sale made with a condition. Some held that whoever sells something and stipulates a condition in his contract renders the condition void, arguing from a report transmitted in general terms from the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) that he forbade a sale together with a condition. Others held that both the sale and the condition stand, citing that the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) bought a camel from Jabir ibn Abdullah al-Ansari during one of his expeditions on the condition that Jabir might ride it back to Madinah. Still others held that the sale stands but the condition is void, citing the account of Barirah that has been mentioned.

The teaching that has come from the People of the House (may Allah's blessings be upon them) settles all of this, establishes the sound position, and makes it clear. The condition stipulated by Barirah's masters was voided only because they stipulated what is not permissible: the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) had ruled that patronage belongs to the one who frees, yet they stipulated patronage for themselves apart from the one who freed her. As for the Messenger of Allah's allowing Jabir ibn Abdullah the condition of riding the camel he had sold him, that was a permissible thing, containing no forbidding of the lawful nor making lawful of the forbidden. And as for the report that he forbade a sale together with a condition, if it is sound, it is a general statement whose meaning is a condition that is unlawful and impermissible. Upon this the People of the House ((عليهم السلام)) said: a sale containing a condition is to be examined; if the condition is lawful, it holds and is binding, and if it is unlawful, it is neither binding nor enforced, while the sale itself stands valid in both cases.

Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (may Allah's blessings be upon him) made this plain: if a man sells a bondwoman to another and stipulates in the contract of sale that the buyer shall not sell her, nor give her away, and that she shall not be inherited from him should he die, then the conditions that he not sell her and not give her away are binding, because that is his to grant. But the condition that she not be inherited from him is not binding, because Allah, mighty and majestic, has ordained inheritance for a man's heirs; so whoever stipulates that they shall have no share, his condition is void. This position of the People of the House ((عليهم السلام)) differs from what the generality have said, and its proof is evident.

Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad ((ع)) said: whoever buys something on the understanding that he holds the option over it for a fixed term, and the thing perishes within that term before the buyer has made his choice, the loss falls upon the seller's property, and the buyer must swear that he had neither chosen it nor accepted it. If he refuses to swear, it becomes binding upon him.

He said: the Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family) granted the buyer of an animal an option of three days, whether or not it was stipulated, and there is no option for the seller once the two have parted. If the buyer brings about some new act upon the animal before the three days are out, the purchase becomes binding upon him. It was asked: what is meant by such an act? He said: as when it is a bondwoman and he touches her, or kisses her, or looks upon what had been forbidden to him before the purchase; and likewise if he has intercourse with her. If he offers her for sale, he is made to swear that he did not offer her intending to keep her, but only wishing to inspect the matter before returning her if he should choose.

He said: if the buyer sells what he purchased while his option still stands, then, if he had called a witness before selling it that he had chosen it and was satisfied with it, the surplus of the price is his; but if he called no witness, he must swear to it, and if he refuses the oath, the surplus belongs to the seller.

He said: whoever buys a commodity and goes off to bring the price but does not bring it until three days have passed, the seller may, if he wishes, demand the price, or, if he wishes, sell the goods or hold them back and rescind the sale; and after the three days the buyer has no standing claim upon it.

He said: whoever buys something under an option for a term, then spends upon it and draws profit and use from it, and then chooses to return it, must return what he gained from it, while what he spent upon it is his to recover. And if the buyer purchases a commodity on the understanding that the option in it belongs to another, then the option belongs to the one for whom it was made.

### On the Rulings Concerning Defects

The saying of the Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family), "Whoever deceives us is not of us," together with its explanation and his prohibition of deceit, has already been mentioned. Whoever knows of a defect in what he sells and hides it has committed deceit. Just so, no one who knows of such a defect should conceal it from the buyer, for the Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family) said: sincere counsel is part of religion. And he said: it is not lawful for a Muslim to sell to his brother goods in which he knows there is a defect without making it plain, nor is it lawful for one who knows of it to keep it hidden.

Whoever would sell free of liability for a defect must disclose that defect to the buyer and make it plain. It does not suffice for him to say, "I sell to you with disavowal (bara'a)," without naming the defect from which he disclaims liability, informing the buyer of it and showing it to him. If this is not done, and he only says, "I sell to you with disavowal," and the buyer then meets with a defect, the buyer may hold him to it, provided he had not seen and accepted it.

He ((ع)) said: if a group buy a commodity and divide it among themselves, and one of them finds a defect in the portion that fell to him, he may take its diminished value from the seller. If the commodity still stands undivided, or if a single person bought the whole and finds a defect in part of it, he takes the value of the defect, unless that defective part is the very face of the commodity, in which case he may return the whole if he wishes. And if the commodity is of what is weighed or measured or is otherwise uniform, and a defect is found in part of it, such as cotton, linen, silk, or food and the like, the seller replaces that part with its equal from the same kind as the purchased goods.

When the buyer finds a defect in the goods and the seller says to him, "Keep it with its defect if you wish, or return it if you wish," the buyer has nothing beyond this: he may keep it or return it. But if he asks for the value of the defect, he has no right to that unless the seller agrees with him upon it.

He ((ع)) said: whoever buys a commodity and brings about some new act upon it, or causes a defect in it, and then discovers in it an old defect from which the buyer had not been released, and holds the seller to it, must pay the seller the value of what he himself brought about in the goods, and may then return them if he wishes, or keep them with their defect. But if the seller had practiced deception upon him with the defect and hidden it from him, the buyer may return them and owes nothing for what was diminished in them. This is established only by clear evidence bearing witness to the deception; and if there is no such evidence, the seller swears that he did not know of the defect and did not deceive concerning it.

When the buyer holds the seller to a defect, the seller may require him to swear that he did not know of the defect before the purchase nor at the time of it, and that since learning of it he has neither put the purchase to use, nor profited from it, nor allowed another the use of it. If he swears, he is entitled to return it, or to the value of the defect where they agree upon that or where the matter is one in which value is due. But if he declines the oath, or had become aware of the defect, or profited from the goods after seeing it, or offered it for sale, then it is binding upon him and he has no claim in it.

It is related from the Commander of the Believers, Ali (may Allah's blessings be upon him), and from Muhammad ibn Ali ibn al-Husayn and Ja'far ibn Muhammad (may Allah's blessings be upon them), concerning a man who buys a bondwoman, has intercourse with her, and then finds a defect in her, that he may not return her, but may take the value of the defect from the one who sold her to him, unless he finds her to be pregnant, in which case he returns her and returns with her half of a tenth of her value.

They said ((عليهم السلام)): the purchased slave is returned under the warranty (al-uhda) for whatever arises within the span of a year, of madness, leprosy, vitiligo, and al-qarn, should any of these arise in the buyer's possession within that year; and likewise the calamity of death falls upon the seller. This applies to slaves in particular, not to the rest of the animals. From Ali (may Allah's blessings be upon him) it is reported that he said: the warranty in a slave covers the gravest disease for a year, and the calamity of death for three days.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad (may Allah's blessings be upon him) said: if the seller, at the time of concluding the sale, disclaims to the buyer the year's warranty and the option of three days for all animals, and the buyer releases him from these, the release is binding upon him and he has no recourse to any of them. Upon this, people today write into the deeds for the purchase of slaves the disclaimer of the year's warranty and of the three days option, and into the deeds for other animals the disclaimer of the three days option. Al-Mahdi (may Allah's blessings be upon him), according to what has reached us, ordered that this be written down so that people would not turn back upon one another over it and prolong their quarrels. Whoever consents to it is bound by it, and whoever dislikes it is not compelled. And if the seller is content to leave out the mention of the disclaimer, he is not compelled to it, and what has been described before applies to him. Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (may Allah's blessings be upon him) said: there is no warranty in a sale made with disavowal, nor in the sale of an inheritance, neither the warranty of the year nor the option of three days.

The Marketplace: Honest Earning, Trade, and Property

On the sale of Murabaha

Selling by Murabaha, in which a price is set as the cost of purchase together with a stated profit, is permissible when it is stated plainly as purchase and profit. Yet the pure Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt of the Messenger of Allah, (ع), disliked the practice of selling by Murabaha on the condition that the profit be fixed as a proportion of the price, because that resembles riba. For if a man says, "I will sell you this commodity at a profit of one-tenth of its price," or any similar formula, then the increase is no longer simply a profit but has become an addition folded into the price itself, and so it comes to resemble riba. This is the form of sale practiced among the merchants of Iraq, who say, "I will sell you this commodity for dowzadeh," or "for yazda," Persian words whose meaning is "for every ten, twelve," or "for every ten, one."

Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad, (ع), related that his father received goods from Egypt. He prepared food and invited the merchants to his home to purchase the goods. He ordered that they be fed, and they ate; then he ordered the goods to be displayed to them. They said, "We will take them from you at dowzadeh." He replied, "No. Rather, I bought these goods for ten thousand dinars, and I will sell them to you for twelve thousand." They said, "We accept," and he handed the goods over to them.

He said, (ع), that whoever buys goods and then incurs expenses upon them, such as freight, the cost of containers, and the like, may sell them by Murabaha and reckon what he has spent into the price. He should say that the goods stand him at such and such in total. He must not say that he bought them for a sum that already includes his added expenditure, for that would be a lie and would draw him toward the boundary of riba, unless he sets out the purchase price, the profit, and the expenditure separately, or combines them openly by saying, "This stands me at such and such in total." He must not claim he bought a thing for more than he actually paid for it.

He was asked about a man who bought a quantity of merchandise, divided it into parts, and wished to sell each part by Murabaha for what it had cost him within the whole. He said: No, unless he makes clear to the buyer that he himself apportioned the cost in this way.

He was asked about a man who bought a commodity upon a deferred term of payment, that is upon nazira, and then sold it on by Murabaha without informing the buyer of that deferment, and the buyer afterward came to learn of it. He said: the buyer is entitled, if he wishes, to the same term of deferment that the seller had enjoyed, unless the seller had said to him at the time of the contract, "On this day it stands me at such a price, and I sell it to you for such a price," and did not phrase it as a fixed rate of profit.

He said: if a man buys a garment for one dinar and pays for it in dirhams, or agrees a price in dirhams and pays it with a dinar, he may sell it by Murabaha for what he bought it for, without mentioning the currency he actually tendered. This rests upon his teaching, (ع), that it is permissible to discharge a debt of gold with silver and a debt of silver with gold. For once he has bought the thing with the gold or silver agreed upon, and the price between him and the seller has been settled as an obligation, what he afterward pays in discharge of it is a lawful settlement, and the purchase remains binding upon the one who made the sale.

He said, (ع), that one who buys his own slave and then sells him by Murabaha may do so, because such use does not diminish the slave's value and is not a thing for which compensation is owed. Likewise if a man buys a beast and rides it, or a slave and puts him to work, or a garment and wears it, provided this causes no wear that lessens its worth. To disclose such use to the buyer is the safer course for the seller in his religion; and if he does not disclose it, no liability falls upon him, as the narrations have come down.

On al-salam

Allah, mighty and majestic, said: ﴿يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا تَدَايَنْتُمْ بِدَيْنٍ إِلَى أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى فَاكْتُبُوهُ﴾, "O you who believe, when you contract a debt for a specified term, write it down" (al-Baqarah: 282). The pure Imams of the family of the Messenger of Allah, (ع), narrated that he said: Whoever enters into a sale for an unknown term, or for an unspecified thing, has not made a true sale.

They said, (عليهم السلام), that al-salam has four conditions, and if any one of them is missing or violated, the contract is void. The first is that the thing delivered be described by a known description. Its measure must be named, by volume or by weight according to its nature, the place of delivery must be specified, and the term at which delivery falls due must be stated. They said that a salam contract is not concluded except by payment of the price before the two parties part from the place in which they contracted. As was set out before, if the price is not handed over at the session, the transaction becomes a debt exchanged for a debt, and the Messenger of Allah, (ع), forbade that.

Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad, (ع), held that whoever enters a salam contract for the produce of a particular village or a named locality may not do so until its soundness becomes apparent; but if no locality is specified, the salam is permissible and stands as an obligation upon the one who received the advance. He permitted a salam contract with one who does not yet possess what he has undertaken to deliver, provided that, when he guarantees it, it lies within his means at the time it falls due, so that he may then acquire it, pay for it, and hand it over. The Imams also permitted salam contracts in animals when the animal is known and precisely described.

He was asked about a man who enters a salam contract for a known thing and then receives either less than it or more than it. He said: if this is by mutual consent, there is no harm in it. They permitted salam in well-known, described garments, and held that if a man takes back his capital, in whole or in part, without adding anything to it, that is permissible; but if he takes back something more, it is not.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad, (ع), was asked about the holder of a salam obligation who pays the price to the one entitled to it, and that one then says, "Buy it for yourself and take your due." He disliked this, and said that the man should have another carry out the purchase, and should not himself undertake both the buying for his own benefit and the taking of delivery from himself.

He was asked about one who holds a salam obligation that falls due, who cannot find the very thing owed but finds others of its kind, such as beasts or goods, and so hands these over at their value. He said there is no harm in that. Likewise, if a man sold food for dirhams, and when the term arrived said, "I have no dirhams, but take food from me instead," there is no harm in the creditor taking food from him, for his right is in dirhams and he may take with them whatever he wishes. The Imams held it reprehensible to enter salam contracts for things that do not keep, such as fresh fruit, meat, and the like.

On the conditions and rulings governing sale contracts

Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad, (ع), said: Whoever buys a slave and finds money with him, that money belongs to the seller, unless the buyer stipulated otherwise in the contract; if he did stipulate it, the seller has no claim to it, even if it exceeds the price of the slave. For whatever a slave possesses and holds in his hand, when he is sold, is reckoned as part of his price, and this does not fall under the riba that is forbidden, particularly when the exchange involves goods, or where the thing is a tangible object and the slave was bought with goods, or the slave was himself an object bought with currency, or was himself currency bought with a tangible object. In all such cases the contract of sale is valid, there being no disagreement upon this.

The form by which a sale is soundly contracted, concerning which there is no dispute, is that a man says to another, "Sell me this slave of yours," or "this commodity of yours, for such and such," naming something lawful to sell by, and the other answers, "I have sold it to you." Or the seller begins, saying, "I have sold you this for such and such," and the buyer answers, "I have bought it," or "I accept this purchase." By this the sale is concluded between them. Thereafter the two retain the right of option until they part bodily from the place of their contract and each withdraws from the other. Whoever of them retracts before they have parted has the right to do so, and the sale is annulled. The sale is completed only by their parting, as has been described.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad, (ع), said concerning two contracting parties where the buyer claims, "I have handed you the price," while the commodity is still in his hand, and the seller says, "You handed me nothing": if the transaction is of a kind in which witnesses are customarily called and people are accustomed to take testimony, the buyer's claim of payment is not accepted without proof, and the seller must swear that he did not receive the price and obtained nothing of it from him in any way. But if it is of a kind in which witnesses are not customarily called, then the word is the word of the one in whose hand the thing lies, together with his oath.

He said: Whoever makes a sale and then claims he erred in it, that he did not know its price at the time of the sale, or intended otherwise and mistook the matter, one looks to its value. If the difference between his selling price and its true value lies within the range by which people commonly differ in transactions of that kind, the sale stands and his claim is set aside. But if there is a great disparity, beyond what people ordinarily tolerate, it is returned to him; and the buyer may require him to swear that he did indeed err, as he claims.

From Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali, (ع): he was asked about a man who appointed an agent to sell his goods, and the agent claimed to have sold them at an undervalue, and the owner sought to undo the sale and recover the goods. He said: the matter is examined. If the value on the day of sale matched the price for which it was sold, or the difference fell within the range by which people commonly differ, the sale is valid and is not undone. But if the value was greater by a margin beyond what people ordinarily tolerate, the sale is returned. And if the buyer wishes to put the owner who seeks its return to his oath, that he did not consent to his agent's sale nor ratify it after coming to know of the loss, that is his right; and if the owner will not swear, the buyer has no claim.

Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali, (ع), was asked about two agents jointly appointed to sell a thing, where one of them sells it without the other. He said: his sale is not valid, unless authority had been given to whichever of them might act alone, when alone, or to them together, when together.

Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad, (ع), was asked about a man bargaining over a slave girl, whose owner sold her to him at his discretion, saying, "I have sold her to you for whatever price you wish." The buyer accepted the sale, then offered a price the seller was not content with and thought too little. He said: her value on the day of sale is examined. If the price offered is less than her value, the buyer must add what falls short of it. For though the seller left the price to the buyer's judgment, it is not permitted for that judgment to be exercised against him unjustly.

He said, (ع): whatever a judge or a ruler sells in settlement of an obligatory debt, or on behalf of a child, or in matters resembling these, carries no warranty or liability of recourse upon the one who ordered the sale. The sale is made from the property of the one against whom it is ordered, as his obligation, even if he has no other wealth.

He said, (ع): whoever trades with the property of an orphan, whether as executor, guardian, or otherwise, if he gains a profit the profit belongs to the orphan; and if he suffers a loss, the loss falls upon the one who traded with it, and he must make it good from the orphan's funds, because he overstepped in it.

He said, (ع), concerning a slave whose master gave him money, with which he bought and freed himself, and whose master afterward learned of it: the master takes back the money the slave received, and the slave remains his, according to how the case stood. This is when the money had been earned by the slave and had come to him without his master's permission. But if his master had released that money to him, or made him a gift of something, or it had come to him from a lawful source, and he bought his own freedom with it, then the purchase is valid. A slave may also enter into a contract of manumission, mukataba, paying its installments from his earnings; and when he has paid what was stipulated upon him, he is freed.

It has been narrated from the Imams, (عليهم السلام), that one who dies leaving no heir but a slave whom he owns: the slave is bought out of the estate, that is, if his master consents to sell him, and the remainder of the estate is then given to him as inheritance. But if his master will not sell him, he does not inherit, and the inheritance passes to the public treasury if there is no other heir but him.

He said, (ع), concerning the sale of a house for a house, a share for a share, a quarter for a quarter, and the sale of part of such things for part: all of this is permissible when the parties know what they are exchanging.

From the Commander of the Faithful, Ali, (ع): he gave judgment in the case of two men who bought a commodity from a man. After they had concluded the sale, they departed to bring the price. One of them came back with it, and Ali ruled that he might take the commodity from the seller; then, when the second came and paid to the one who had taken the goods half of the price, he became his partner in it.

It was also narrated that certain people did business with a woman of Quraysh who held a large sum of money in trust. Two men came to her with money and deposited the whole of it with her, saying, "Do not release it to either one of us without the other." Then one of them came to her bearing a sign that had been agreed between him and his companion, told it to her, and said, "He has sent me to receive the money, and this is the token between him and you." So she released the money to him, and he went off with it. Afterward the other man came and demanded the money. She said, "Your companion has already taken it. He brought your message and the token, such and such, that was agreed between me and you." He said, "I sent nothing to you, and I gave him no token." The matter was brought before Umar in his day, and he gave judgment against her for the money. Then she came to Ali, (ع), seeking his help, and he said to Umar, "Did these two not instruct her that the money was not to be released to either one of them without the other?" He said, "Yes." Ali said, "Then he has no right to hold her liable until he brings his companion with him." So the woman was released, and the device contrived against her was undone.

It is related from Ja'far ibn Muhammad (may Allah's blessings be upon him) that an emissary of the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) came from Yemen bringing captives. When they reached al-Jahfa their provisions ran out, so they sold a slave girl from among the captives, a girl whose mother was with her, and brought the mother forward. When word of this reached the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) he disapproved of it and ordered that the girl be returned, saying: "Do not separate kindred by sale" (لا تفرقوا بين ذوي الأرحام بالبيع).

Ja'far ibn Muhammad (may Allah's blessings be upon him) spoke also of a man who buys a commodity from another and then seeks to return it, but the seller refuses to accept its return, so the buyer forgoes part of the price in order that the seller will take it back. The Imam said that the buyer may reclaim from the seller whatever he had given up, provided the seller has genuinely granted the return, for a rescission is not brought about by remitting part of the price. If, however, the seller buys from the buyer some other, separate commodity for less than the price at which he had originally sold to him, that is permissible.

**On Debts**

The pure Imams from the household of the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) related that he said: Allah the Almighty is with the debtor until he discharges his debt, so long as he intends to discharge it and the debt is not for something Allah hates. And he said: whoever frees a captive, or relieves one burdened by debt, and grants respite to one in hardship or makes him a remission, will be beneath the shade of the Throne on a Day when there is no shade but His shade.

He said: "Whoever extends a loan has the reward of charity for it." Then afterward he said: "Whoever extends a loan has the reward of charity for it every day." Ali (may Allah's blessings be upon him) said: "O Messenger of Allah, before this you said that whoever extends a loan has the reward of charity, and now you say every day?" He replied: "If a man lends to a fixed term, he is as one who has given it in charity; and when the term has passed and he grants further respite, it is for him the reward of charity every day until he collects it."

A man bound by a contract of manumission once stood before Ali ((ع)) seeking his help, but Ali found at that moment nothing to give him. So he said to him: "Shall I direct you to something better than that?" The man said: "Yes, what is it?" Ali said: "Something I heard from the Messenger of Allah ((ع) and his family). He said: were there upon you a debt as great as Sabr, Allah the Almighty would discharge it for you." And Sabr is a mountain in Yemen. The man said: "And what is it?" Ali said: "Say: O Allah, suffice me by what You have made lawful so that I need not what You have made unlawful, and enrich me by Your bounty so that I need none besides You" (اللهم اكفني بحلالك عن حرامك واغنني بفضلك عمن سواك).

The Messenger of Allah ((ع)) forbade a loan that draws a benefit. Ali (may Allah's blessings be upon him) said: none of you should accept the riding of a beast or the loan of goods from a man to whom he has given a loan. Ja'far ibn Muhammad (may Allah's blessings be upon him) likewise disapproved of a creditor lodging with his debtor and eating his food, drinking his drink, or feeding from his fodder. And Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali (may Allah's blessings be upon him) said: every loan that draws a benefit is riba. This holds when the benefit arises from a stipulation or an intention, as in the case of the creditor who lodges with his debtor and eats his food and feeds from his fodder, for it may be that the debtor does this only on account of the loan. But if the benefit is freely offered, and was something already passing between them and customary to them apart from the loan, there is no harm in it. It has also come from them that one who advances dirhams or dinars and is repaid in better coin commits no wrong, provided he did not stipulate it.

Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (may Allah's blessings be upon him) was asked about a man whose regular customer or business partner comes to him and borrows from him, the lender lending because he profits from the borrower through buying and selling with him; and about brokers who lend to people against their crops until they sell on their behalf and recover the loan from them, for otherwise they would not lend to them at all. He permitted this when it is well-known and customary between them, but disapproved of any part of it from which the lender draws a direct benefit, on account of the Prophet's prohibition ((ع)) of a loan that draws a benefit. And it is related of Ali, of al-Husayn ibn Ali, of Muhammad ibn Ali ibn al-Husayn, and of Ja'far ibn Muhammad (may Allah's blessings be upon them all) that they permitted the bills of exchange known as al-safatij: money that a man borrows in one land on the understanding that he, or another on his behalf, will repay it in another land. They saw no harm in this, because it is a loan and a debt.

It is related of them (may Allah's blessings be upon them) that dealing on credit is permitted, provided the contract is properly formed. So if a man owes a debt that has fallen due and says to his creditor, "I cannot find what I might pay you with, but sell me something on a deferred term, and I will sell it and pay you from the proceeds," and the creditor does so, there is no harm in it. But if the creditor instead transacts with him in mere words, in a manner not lawful, there is harm. An example is his saying, "Lend me one hundred dinars for two hundred," or any like increase set to a named term, then dressing it as a sale in which he sells him something at a valid sale whose value he raises by that same increase and sets to a term. Such bargaining over the increase is disliked. To leave it aside and intend a plain sale with no mention of any increase is safer. A sale contract validly concluded after such bargaining is, however, permissible. This is like a man and a woman who exchange words bargaining over an unlawful union and then abandon it and contract a sound marriage between them: the bargaining and the back-and-forth over immorality is disliked and forbidden, yet it does not invalidate the marriage they contract afterward, nor anything that proceeds from it. Rather, their returning to what is lawful, with repentance from what they had bargained over, is well, if Allah wills.

Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (may Allah's blessings be upon him) was asked about a man who says to another, "Buy me such-and-such goods for so much, and I will buy them from you to a term for so much, with such-and-such an increase." He said: there is no harm in that, for he buys them only after he has come to own them. And he permitted that a man who is owed a debt to a term, and who comes to need it before its time, may remit part of it so that the debtor pays it early, or may ask the debtor to pay him part of it on condition that he extend the remainder to a further term, and there is no harm in that, so long as he adds nothing above his capital.

**On Transfer of Debt and Suretyship**

A transfer of debt (al-hawala) is that a man who is owed a debt by another assigns that debt onto a third party. Suretyship (al-kafala) is the bearing of a burden on another's behalf; the bearer is called surety, guarantor, pledge, warrant, and underwriter, and whoever takes on such a burden is obliged to discharge what he has undertaken. The Messenger of Allah ((ع)) said: "The underwriter is liable" (الزعيم غارم), and the underwriter, as we have said, is the bearer of the burden. As Allah says: "They said, while turning toward them: What is it you are missing? They said: We are missing the king's measuring-cup, and for whoever brings it there is a camel's load" (Yusuf 71-72), and then, "and I am underwriter for it" (وأنا به زعيم), that is, the bearer of the burden.

Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali and Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (may Allah's blessings be upon them) said: when a man transfers onto another a debt he is owed, the creditor may pursue whichever of the two he wishes, either the original debtor or the one onto whom the debt was transferred, unless the creditor discharged the original debtor. If the discharge was made at the time of the transfer, then the creditor may pursue none but the one onto whom the debt was transferred, and he has no claim upon the original debtor whom he discharged.

And when two men together stand surety for one man's debt, the one owed may take from whichever of the two sureties he wishes, unless they stipulated that the suretyship is divided equally between them. If they made no such stipulation and each gave an unrestricted suretyship, he may take the whole from whichever of them he can reach. If they stipulated in the contract of suretyship that each of them is surety for the other, he may take from whichever he wishes, and the one from whom he takes recovers from his fellow surety half of what he paid. If one surety is added after another, they are all sureties together; he may take from whichever of them he wishes, unless he discharges one.

If the surety guarantees a sum of money or that for which he stood surety, he must discharge it unless the one owed releases him from it, and it does not suffice him to produce the person on whose behalf he stood surety. But if the suretyship was a suretyship of the person, and he produces him to the one for whom he stood surety according to the agreed condition and is quit of him by that, then he is discharged and nothing remains upon him. And if he undertook the bearing of the person unrestrictedly, then whenever he is asked to produce him he must do so. If the suretyship is to a fixed term, the one owed has no recourse to the surety until the term has passed; and if the surety does not produce the person then, he is liable to pay what is owed, unless the person has died. If the person has died and the surety had only borne responsibility for his appearance, nothing remains upon the surety. If the person is absent and the surety says, "I will pay what is owed upon him," and what is owed is unknown, then nothing is upon him beyond what is proven; and if the amount is known, only that is upon him. If the suretyship concerns a matter in which the person must of necessity be produced, for an oath that has become due upon him, or a vow of abstention, or his being held, or the like, then he must of necessity be produced, unless he dies or the one owed releases the surety.

And he ((ع)) said: there is no suretyship in a prescribed penalty. Whoever has become liable for a hadd, or retribution, or execution, or amputation cannot be released against a surety in that matter; rather it is exacted from him. If witnesses testify to it and it is required that he be held until the testimony is established, he is detained, and no surety is accepted for him, because were he to slip away the retribution could not be exacted from the surety. And suretyship does not bind a slave to stand as surety, even if he is permitted to trade, unless his master grants him leave in it.

**On Interdiction and the Spendthrift**

Interdiction (al-hajr) is restraint. Allah the Almighty has said: "And test the orphans" (al-Nisa 6), meaning try them, "until, when they reach marriageable age," meaning puberty, "then, if you perceive in them sound judgment, hand over to them their wealth." And the Messenger of Allah ((ع)) forbade the wasting of wealth, and Allah the Almighty has said: "And do not squander wastefully; indeed the squanderers are the brothers of the devils" (al-Isra 26-27). The child is therefore barred from his wealth by the ruling of the Book until he comes of age and sound judgment and care for his property are recognized in him, whereupon his wealth is handed over to him. He was barred only because of the danger of foolishness and squandering; so if he comes of age and is still foolish and wasteful, he remains under interdiction, and likewise if this arises in him after maturity, he is placed under interdiction.

Ali ibn Abi Talib (may Allah's blessings be upon him) placed Abdullah ibn Ja'far ibn Abi Talib (may Allah's blessings be upon him) under interdiction when his squandering of his wealth was established, and brought the matter for that purpose to Uthman during his caliphate and called upon him to act. Uthman hesitated. Then afterward it came to pass that Abdullah had bought a salt marsh for sixty thousand, and Uthman said: "By Allah, it would not please me to own it even for these two sandals of mine." And Ali (may Allah's blessings be upon him) came to him and said: "Will you not interdict your nephew?"

The insane person is likewise placed under interdiction. Whoever is under interdiction has his maintenance met from his own wealth in a fitting measure, and one is appointed for him to guard his wealth and to spend upon him from it. When the foolish one becomes upright, the squanderer becomes careful, and the insane one regains his reason, and those whose testimony is acceptable bear witness to it, the interdiction is lifted from them; and if they relapse, it is reimposed upon them. And whatever they disposed of their wealth while under interdiction is likewise void.

**On Bankruptcy**

As for bankruptcy: a man who is in debt and becomes insolvent and finds nothing with which to pay, having nothing to sell to discharge his debt from it and no wealth to pay with, when this is established of his condition, is not to be imprisoned for it. If he is insolvent and owes debts to a group of creditors and has in hand wealth that does not cover their debts, what is in his hand is divided among the creditors by shares: each takes in proportion to his debt, and the shortfall falls upon each in the same proportion. Thus a man who goes bankrupt owing one man a hundred dinars and another two hundred, and there is found in his hand only thirty dinars, the owner of the hundred receives ten and the owner of the two hundred receives twenty; and upon that reckoning is whatever is more or less, however few or many the creditors.

If one of the creditors finds in the bankrupt's hand something he had sold him, or part of it, he has the better right to that very thing over the rest of the creditors until he recovers his whole debt; and if there is in its price a surplus and profit after he has recovered his debt, that belongs to the rest of the creditors. It is not for the bankrupt to sell what is in his hand apart from the creditors until he has paid them their due. They are to scrutinize the sale of his assets and bid against one another among themselves, and when the price stands firm and no further increase is found in it, and they are content and he too consents to the sale, the assets are sold and divided among them by shares. If the bankrupt refuses to sell, or one of the creditors refuses, and they bring the matter before the authority, and it is testified before him that the sale was conducted with the utmost scrutiny for the best price, he orders the assets sold and apportioned among the creditors. If they do not cover the debts, they are distributed; and if they cover them and leave a surplus, the surplus is returned to him. If part of the assets suffices to meet their dues and he does not wish all of them sold, only so much of them is sold against him as covers the debts that are upon him.

The bankrupt's acknowledgment of a debt is not accepted once the creditors have come forward, unless the one to whom he acknowledges it brings clear evidence, or the creditors affirm its truth. But what he acknowledged while he was still solvent is valid. And if there comes into his hand something in which he has a wage owed to him, and he stands forward for it alongside the rest of the creditors, he has the better right to it until he receives his wage from it.

When a man buys a slave and frees him, or takes a concubine who then bears him a child, or gives something away as charity, and afterward falls into bankruptcy, Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad, (ع), was asked how these acts stand. He said that if the man made the purchase at a fair price while he was still solvent, before any bankruptcy had been established against him, and only later became insolvent, then the manumission and the charity hold. The seller takes his place among the creditors and is treated like them with respect to whatever remains in the man's hands. But if the man was poor and had nothing to give, neither the manumission nor the charity is valid, for he was disposing of what was not truly his.

It has been narrated from the People of the House, (عليهم السلام), that the Commander of the Faithful, (ع), held that the mother of a child, the umm walad, may be sold for the price of her own freedom. That is, if a man buys her and she bears him a child, and he owns nothing else, she stands as the price of what he owes. But if he has means, the seller takes his share alongside the other creditors, and whatever remains of the debt continues as an obligation upon the freed person, who is asked for it whenever he is able and must then pay it. Whichever of the two, the freed man or the freed woman, finds it easier to discharge may be asked for it, and the easiest path is taken. The one who has been freed, however, may not be compelled back into service, neither as a concubine nor as one who had been owned, nor may what remains be demanded of such a person in the manner it would be demanded of one still enslaved. If the one who freed them owns nothing, and there is value in them beyond what was owed, preference is given to freeing them at their assessed worth. Where a charitable gift is concerned, preference is given to the charity reaching its recipient, and the remainder is returned.

Whoever carries debts and owns slaves whose sale would settle those debts may not free them; they are sold and the debt is paid, provided he has no other funds. But if he does have other funds, then what passed through his hands stands: what he spent, what he sold, and what some creditors received while others did not, all of it holds, and it is lawful for whoever received it. A man may not discharge a debt, nor sell, nor buy, except by lawful means. So long as a solvent man is still trading openly and his creditors have not risen against him, what he pays out and what he sells is valid for those who receive it; he is restrained from such dealings only once his creditors have come forward and his bankruptcy is declared. The wife, for her part, contends among the creditors for her dowry and is not deprived of her right.

When a creditor or claimant comes forward and the man against whom the claim is made acknowledges it, or proof is established against him, and then, once judgment has been given, he pleads that he has nothing while the claimant calls him a liar, the question of proof turns on the nature of the obligation. If it arose from a debt or from something that actually reached his hands, the burden falls on him to prove that he lost it and that it left his possession. But if what was owed never reached his hands, such as a liability arising from an offense, or from standing as surety, or from a transfer of debt by hawala, or from the maintenance of a dependent, or from a woman's dowry, then his plea of insolvency is accepted upon his oath, and it falls to the one with proof to contest it. As for property he claims to own, if he can prove any of it he is held to it; if he cannot, it stands as a debt against him, to be collected whenever he becomes able, and whenever it appears that he has something in hand, it is sold and his debts are paid from the proceeds.

If witnesses testify that he is in fact wealthy and that the means to satisfy his debt are merely hidden, he is imprisoned and compelled until he pays. If, when confronted, he says to his opponent, "I will settle your right and give you what I owe," and offers no plea of bankruptcy, that word is taken at face value and he is held to it, and he is imprisoned until he discharges it. When a debtor who has visible property turns obstinate, the judge imprisons him and presses him to sell, and disciplines him should he delay; if he persists in his obstinacy, the sale is carried out on his behalf. And if he acknowledges the debt, or it is proven against him that he has funds, and he still withholds payment, he is imprisoned at his opponent's request and compelled until he renders what he owes.

On tenant farming and musaqah. Tenant farming, muzara'ah, is a partnership in cultivation in which each party holds a defined share of the produce, or in which a man sows another man's land using his own oxen or his own seed in return for a known portion of the yield. Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad, (ع), was asked about this and permitted it. He said that the Messenger of Allah, (ع) and his family, gave the land of Khaybar to the Jews on the condition that they cultivate it and receive half of what it produced, and he apportioned it with them on that basis. The land held palms and trees, and whoever worked it took a third or a quarter. Whatever the two parties agree upon, whether little or much, is permissible. It is likewise permissible to rent land for a fixed term in order to cultivate it for a known and understood consideration. He disapproved, however, of renting land for wheat on the condition that wheat be grown in it. And the tenant farmer may undertake whatever the parties stipulate as to labor, plowing, oxen, and the rest, and according to whatever they agree, whether the land belongs to both of them or to one alone. What he disliked was that they fix a separate known share for the seed, a share for the land, and a share for the oxen; rather, each party should stipulate for himself a known portion of the whole yield.

Musaqah is work upon land that bears trees, carried out by a worker in return for a defined share of what is produced. Ja'far ibn Muhammad, (ع), said that if a man is given land to plow, water, and tend, and it holds pomegranates, palms, fruits, legumes, or the like, on the condition that he receive half of what it yields, or whatever the two agree upon, then it is permissible. The same holds if the land is handed over to be built up and cultivated. If the worker plants it and then claims that the owner had given him leave, while the owner denies it, the word is the owner's, confirmed by his oath, unless it was known at the time of planting that the worker had permission. In that case the worker swears, and he is entitled to the equivalent of a tenant's wage for that land, unless he advances a claim that does not fit the circumstances, in which case he owes the customary rent of the land. The same applies if the owner had no knowledge of the planting and has no proof against the worker, and the worker swears that he did not rent the land on the terms claimed against him; then the cultivator is liable for the equivalent of the land's rent. The owner, for his part, has no right to tear up the standing crop.

If the cultivators plowed the land as trespassers and this is proven against them, and neither plaintiff nor defendant will take the oath after it has been offered, and it is not shown that the owner was the one in the wrong, then the owner is given a choice. He may either take the equivalent of the land's rent from them, or pay their expenses and the cost of their planting and take the crop for himself. He may not uproot it, for tearing up a standing crop is an act of corruption. And he said that if a man plows a piece of land and another asks to share with him what he has plowed, on the condition that he pay half the seed and half the costs, and the two agree, it is permissible. He said too that there is no harm in a man giving his ruined land to one who will restore it for a fixed period on the condition that all it produces during that time is his; when the agreed term has passed, the land returns to its owner now improved. Likewise, if the land carries a tribute charge and another man takes it on together with that charge, there is no harm in it. He may sow it; and if he leaves it unsown while the owner neither prevents him nor stands between him and it, then he owes the rent for the term he contracted for, whether he chooses to plow it or to leave it, unless it was stipulated that he owes no rent except when he actually plows. In that case the choice between plowing and leaving the land is his, and he is bound by the condition placed upon him; if such a condition was made, the two are held to what they contracted.

He was asked about the people of a village burdened with a tribute that the ruler pressed upon them harshly, who then assigned the matter to a man on the condition that whatever he paid on their behalf, little or much, would discharge their obligation, and that any surplus beyond what was demanded would be his. He said that if they agreed to this willingly, it is permissible, and the one who took the burden from them is entitled to the surplus they agreed upon.

On rents and hire. The Imams of the household of the Messenger of Allah, (عليهم السلام), narrated that there was found in the sheath of a sword a writing that bore the curse of Allah, of the angels, of the prophets and the messengers, and the curse of the Prophet Muhammad, (ع), upon whoever claims as his father one who is not his father, or claims as his patron one who is not his patron, or wrongs a hired laborer of his wage, or moves the landmark boundaries of the road. Ali, the Commander of the Faithful, (ع), said, "I found this writing in the sheath of the sword of the Messenger of Allah, (ع) and his family, and I know it." This is a long matter whose full treatment belongs elsewhere. And Allah, mighty and majestic, said: ﴿قُل لَّا أَسْأَلُكُ عَلَيْهِ أَجْرًا إِلَّا الْمَوَدَّةَ فِى الْقُرْبَى﴾, "Say: I do not ask you for any reward for it except affection in kinship" (Al-Shura 23). In this verse too there is a long discourse that belongs to its own place.

The consensus of what has come from the Imams, (عليهم السلام), is that a man may lease out himself, and those under his charge, and whatever he owns, whether a beast, a tool, a ship, land, a dwelling, or anything else, in all its lawful forms. The wage for such a thing is lawful, and it is permitted to earn it, so long as the hire is contracted for a fixed term and an understood purpose and upon what is right and allowed. None of this is permitted if it concerns something unlawful to earn from or unlawful to do. This is a foundational principle from which many particular rulings follow. So one who has no authority over himself cannot validly hire himself out, nor hire out what he does not own and has no right to. One who holds what it is not lawful for him to own, such as a man who has seized a free person, or who keeps instruments of forbidden amusement, may not rent these out, nor may anyone lawfully hire them from him. Nor may a man lease even what he rightfully owns to one who will use it for an unlawful end, as when a man hires out himself, his beast, his tools, or his ship for the making of wine, or for carrying it, or for anything of that kind.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad, (ع), was asked about taking a wage for teaching the Quran, and he found it objectionable. He related that a man once said to the Commander of the Faithful, Ali, (ع), "I love you, O Commander of the Faithful." He replied, "But I hold something against you." The man asked why, and he said, "Because you take a wage for teaching the Quran, and I have heard the Messenger of Allah, (ع) and his family, say that whoever takes the teaching of the Quran as a livelihood will have that as his only portion on the Day of Resurrection." Yet they permitted, (عليهم السلام), the taking of wages for teaching writing, literature, and the other things that teachers impart to children, and they themselves paid the wage for such instruction; had it been forbidden or even disliked, they would not have paid it. What was disliked was solely the wage for teaching the Quran by itself. So when a teacher instructs children in the Quran together with other subjects for which a wage may lawfully be taken, there is no harm in it, and the wage falls upon what is lawful and not upon what is not.

It is narrated that the Messenger of Allah, (ع) and his family, married a woman to one of his companions on the condition that he teach her a surah of the Quran. The report on this is given only in summary and does not state that he made the teaching her dowry; and a marriage may be valid even without a named dowry, as will be treated in the matters of marriage. Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali, (ع), was asked about a man over whom the Quran is recited, who, when he has completed it, prepares food as people customarily do and invites those who recited with him, including the one who recited to him. He said there is no harm in that, so long as it is not done for the sake of the Quran itself.

They also permitted, (عليهم السلام), the taking of a wage for ruqyah by means of the Book of Allah and His names. It is narrated that the Messenger of Allah, (ع) and his family, sent out a company who passed by one of the encampments of the Arabs and lodged there. The chief of that encampment was stung, and its people came asking whether any among the company could perform ruqyah. One of them recited the opening chapter of the Book, al-Fatihah, over him, and he recovered. They were given thirty sheep, which they accepted. When they returned to the Messenger of Allah, (ع) and his family, and told him what had happened, he allowed them to keep what they had taken.

Abu Abdullah al-Sadiq, Ja'far ibn Muhammad, (ع), was asked about a man who pays another a wage on the condition that he teach him a craft and let him work alongside him, and he permitted it. This falls under what was said before: if the craft is lawful, the arrangement is permitted. But whoever hires, or is hired, to teach what is unlawful, such as singing, idle entertainment, and the like, this is not permitted.

Concerning the muezzin, they related that the Commander of the Faithful, (ع), said that the wage of the muezzin is among unlawful gain, suht, yet he said there is no harm in his receiving a stipend from the public treasury. It is as though he disliked only the muezzin taking a wage directly from those for whom he calls the adhan. In the same way it was held undesirable for an imam to take a wage from those he leads in prayer, or a judge from those between whom he adjudicates, while it was permitted that they be sustained from the Bayt al-Mal, the House of Wealth.

Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad, (ع), was asked about the wage of the broker, the samsar, whom either the seller or the buyer engages, and he permitted it, provided that the buying and selling on which he is hired is itself lawful. He was also asked about a broker who is given a commodity to sell and is set a price for it, on the condition that whatever he obtains above that price is his as his fee, and he permitted that as well. And they said, (عليهم السلام), that the hired worker and the craftsman who spoils or destroys what he was engaged to do is liable for it, as with the fuller who tears the garment, or the tailor or the dyer who ruins it.

The Marketplace: Honest Earning, Trade, and Property

A hired worker who spoils the task entrusted to him, who cuts a garment wrongly, or who departs from what he was instructed, and likewise a porter or carrier who breaks what he was paid to transport, bears responsibility for the loss whenever the goods were surrendered into his keeping and he himself took charge of them. But if a man merely lets out his animal while the renter is the one who accompanies his own goods, and they are broken or damaged while in the renter's charge, there is nothing upon the owner of the animal. If the hired man or porter claims that what was handed to him was lost from his hands, and the one who delivered it denies this, the proof is upon the claimant and the oath is upon the one who handed over the goods, swearing that he does not know it to have been lost. The worker or porter is held liable for it unless he can produce proof of the loss; yet if the proof establishes that it was stolen or wrested from his hands, then nothing is upon him.

Leasing and hiring are permitted, (عليهم السلام), for a price in specie or in goods. Ja'far ibn Muhammad (prayers of Allah be upon him) was asked about taking the use of one house in exchange for the use of another, and he said, "There is nothing wrong with that." He was asked about a man who rents dwellings by the month on the understanding that if he occupies them for a single day he is bound for the whole month, or for the whole year, and he said that the man is held to whatever he has bound himself to.

He was asked about two parties who dispute the payment of the rent (kara). The owner of the house wishes to take a prepayment, while the renter says, "I will not pay you until the end of the month or the year." He said: if they made this a stipulated condition, then it stands as they contracted it; but if they laid down no condition, the owner may take the rent day by day, each day as it falls due.

He was asked about a man who rents a house in which there are trees and stipulates their fruit as part of the rental contract. He said: if the fruit was already in blossom at the time of the contract, or some of it was, or there was some present yield together with it, then that is permissible; otherwise it is not, and the rental is void if they conclude it on that footing together.

He was asked about a man who rents a house that has been partly demolished, or that fell to him through inheritance, or whose rooms leak when the rain comes, and the renter presses the owner to repair it. He said: the owner cannot be compelled to do so; but if the damage harms the renter, he may reduce the rent and vacate, owing only for what he has already occupied, while if the damage works no harm, the rental is not dissolved. He said: even where the renter has not stipulated that the owner repair what has decayed and clean out its drains, that obligation still falls upon the owner without any stipulation, because it is among the things known to be incumbent upon the owner of a house. And he said, (ع), that it is not permissible for the renter to bring into the house anything that would harm it, unless he has stipulated that in the rental contract.

He was asked about a dispute between renter and owner over the rent, the owner claiming the greater amount and the renter the lesser, whether or not the renter has occupied the house. He said: they both take an oath, and whichever of them shrinks from the oath, the word of the other prevails, provided that other has sworn; but if they both swear, or both refuse the oath together, the rental is dissolved.

He was asked about the hire of undivided property (kira al-musha), and he said: the hire of undivided property is permissible just as its sale is permissible.

He was asked about a man who is dwelling in another man's house, who claims he was given lodging in it without any rent, while the owner of the house says, "Rather I let it to you for such and such," and neither has proof. He said: the owner of the house swears that he did not lodge him without charge, and the resident owes him the fair value of the rent, unless that value should exceed what the owner claimed he let it for, in which case the owner takes only what he himself acknowledged and adds nothing to it. Likewise, if the renter claims that the owner ordered him to spend on the house and to mend it, and he did so, but the owner denies it, then where the renter has no proof the owner swears that he gave no such order, and the renter has the right to take back the materials of the repair.

He was asked about a man who rents from another a house in which goods have been stored, taking it on the condition that the owner clear them out, and after the contract is made the owner is slow to remove the goods. He said: the owner is owed rent only to the extent the renter was actually able to dwell in the house.

He was asked about a house that is damaged, in whole or in part, through some act of the renter that he had not stipulated in the rental contract. He said: he guarantees whatever was damaged on that account, unless it is something that tenants customarily do, in which case there is no liability upon him even without stipulation, so long as it falls within what is commonly understood; for not everything the renter does must be set down as a condition.

He was asked about an animal or a ship that is damaged before reaching the destination for which it was hired. He said: whoever hired a particular animal or ship, having seen it and inspected it and taken it into his charge, the hire is dissolved when it is damaged, and he owes the proportional rent for the distance already traveled. But if he hired carriage without naming any particular animal or ship, then the carrier (makari) must furnish a substitute equal to the one damaged to carry the load to the agreed destination, and the full rent is due.

He was asked about one who hires an animal for a known number of days to ride it, or load it, or grind with it, or thresh with it, fixing no precise term but only naming the use he intends. He said: this is permissible, and he may put it to such use within just bounds. He must not press it beyond proper use, and the owner of the animal may not cut short the agreed period. If the renter did not name the use he intended, and then wishes to put it to some labor that would harm or diminish it, he has no right to do so.

He was asked about one who hires an animal or a ship from a man to carry a known load on the condition that he deliver it within a stated and fixed period, and he fails to deliver within that period and exceeds it. He said: a hire on these terms is void, and the hirer owes only the equivalent wage.

It was related of Ali (prayers of Allah be upon him) that he gave judgment concerning a man who hired an animal to a named destination and then took it beyond, so that it perished: he held him liable for the animal itself but did not also charge him rent for the distance beyond the agreed limit. And al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (prayers of Allah be upon him) said: if the animal did not perish, and the renter took it beyond the stipulated limit, then when its owner calls him to account for the excess he may take from him a wage commensurate with that excess if he wishes, or take compensation for the diminishment caused by it, whichever of the two he chooses, but he may not take both together. Likewise, if a man hires it for a day or several days and then keeps it beyond that, the owner has the choice between taking the proportional rent for the time held after the term expired or the equivalent hire rate; and if it perishes while he has held it past the stipulated term, he is a guarantor of its value.

He was asked about a dispute between two parties over the distance, the renter claiming a far destination while the owner of the animal says it is near, both being agreed that there was a hire. He said: the proof lies upon the one claiming the greater, who is the renter; and if he has no proof, the oath falls upon the carrier (makari). If they agree upon the destination but differ over the route, the proof lies upon the one who claims the more arduous of the two roads, and the oath is upon the other. If the two routes are equal and they differ over which was contracted, then where the renter has neither ridden nor set out, both take an oath and the contract is dissolved if neither has proof, and whoever refuses the oath is bound by his fellow's claim; but if the renter has already ridden or set out, the word is the owner's, and the renter stands as the claimant.

He was asked about a man who hires carriage to a country, and the carrier, reaching the first settlement of that country, wishes the renter to alight there, while the renter insists on reaching its farthest part, no particular place within that country having been named in the contract, as when a man hires carriage to Iraq, or to Khurasan, or to the Levant (al-Sham), or to Africa (Ifriqiya), or to the like. He said, (ع): the obligation in such a case is fulfilled by reaching the chief city that is the mother of that region, the one renowned for it, the one by whose name it is known, and the one that people customarily mean when they set out for it.

He was asked about land taken on hire that is then struck by a calamity, by flooding, or the receding of water, or the drying of a spring or the cutting off of a river by which it was watered. He said: if the renter wishes to spend upon it out of that year's rent, he may do so, and the cost falls upon the owner of the land; otherwise he owes only in proportion to what he has cultivated and benefited from, and the hire is dissolved thereafter.

He was asked about a hire in which an option (khiyar) is stipulated. He said: whatever is stipulated of what is lawful and permissible is valid, and whatever is of what is neither lawful nor permissible is not valid, and the hire itself remains in force as it stands.

He was asked about a man who delivers wheat to the miller, or sesame to the oil-presser, stipulating a known measure to be rendered back to him in return for a wage set for the work. He said: to fix it in that manner is not permissible. The miller and the oil-presser are entitled to the fair wage for their labor, and the owner of the wheat or the sesame takes back whatever each of them actually produces.

He was asked about the earnings of the cupper (al-hajjam), and he saw no harm in them. He said: the Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and his family and grant them peace) paid the cupper, and the Imams, (عليهم السلام), continue to pay them to this day; and had it been unlawful, they would never have given it to them.

He was asked about the craftsman approached by one who wants work done, who promises what he cannot afterward provide, such as a tailor who is shown a garment and says he will clothe the man with the like of it and cuts the cloth accordingly, then finds it will not clothe him; or a goldsmith who says, "Such a piece of work will serve you for this much gold," and after he has begun and cast the gold and silver finds himself unable to complete it; or one who shows gold or silver and declares them sound, and they prove to be poor. He said, (ع): if each of them spoke according to the extent of his knowledge and the utmost of his effort and then erred, there is nothing against him; but if it is established against him, or known of him, that he intended thereby to deceive his client, he guarantees whatever he has spoiled.

On Pledges

﴿يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا تَدَايَنْتُمْ بِدَيْنٍ إِلَى أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى فَاكْتُبُوهُ﴾, "O you who believe, when you contract a debt for a specified term, write it down" (al-Baqarah: 282), and ﴿وَلَمْ تَجِدُوا كَاتِبًا فَرِهَانٌ مَقْبُوضَةٌ﴾, "and you do not find a scribe, then a pledge taken in hand" (al-Baqarah: 283). This shows that a pledge (rahn) is valid only when it is taken into possession; so whatever has not been received is no pledge at all. Whatever may be sold may be pledged. Whoever takes an undivided share in pledge receives possession of it in the same way an undivided share is received in a sale. The one who holds a pledge may not derive benefit from it. If the pledge perishes, the loss falls upon the wealth of the pledgor (rahin), unless the pledgee (murtahin) brought about its destruction. And if the pledge is destroyed, the pledgee has no right to demand another pledge to stand in its place; he has nothing but the right to claim his debt when it falls due.

If the pledgor and the pledgee disagree over what the pledge secures, the proof lies upon the one claiming the surplus, who is the pledgee, and the oath is upon the pledgor as to the excess claimed against him. And if the pledge has perished and they then differ over its value, the proof likewise lies upon the one claiming the surplus, who here is the pledgor, since he is the one who held it, and the pledgee swears as to the excess he claims.

The pledgee may not sell the pledge when its term falls due except by order of a judge, and only after the debt has been established before him. If the pledgor is absent, the judge orders that it be sold on his behalf and his due paid from the proceeds, just as an absent debtor's property is sold for his debt, the judge acting in the pledgor's stead. If the pledgee sells the pledge, whether at the time of maturity, after it, or before it, without the pledgor having committed that to him, he guarantees it; and whatever he has enjoyed or profited from of the pledge belongs to the pledgor. The pledgor, for his part, may not sell the pledge, nor give it away, nor donate it as charity, nor free it if it is a slave, nor remove it from his ownership by any means whatever, until he has redeemed it. The pledgee does not sell it; rather the matter is referred to the judge, who sells it on behalf of the absent party once the debt is established before him, appointing one to sell it and settle the debt from it, just as is done with property that is not held in pledge. But if the pledgor has authorized the pledgee to sell the pledge at maturity, and he sells it, calling witnesses to that, striving to get the highest price, and conducting the sale to the fullest, then nothing is held against him in that, even should the pledgor suspect him in the sale and have him swear to it if he wishes. And if the pledgee himself wishes to buy the pledge, there is no harm in his doing so when they are agreed upon it, the price being set off against his debt, and the surplus settled between them.

On Partnership

Partnership in wealth (al-shirka), according to the teaching of the Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt (prayers of Allah be upon them), is permissible when two men, or a group, contract it, each of those forming it contributing wealth of his own and mingling it with the wealth of his fellow until it becomes a single fund, no longer distinguishable, that they may buy and sell with. Whatever profit arises from it is shared between them, and whatever loss is incurred falls upon them in proportion to what each contributed. Neither of them may sell, nor buy, nor bring about any new act in what they hold in common save with the knowledge of his partner and at his bidding; though if each grants the other leave to do in it whatever he sees fit, then it is permitted, and his partner has no recourse against him for it, unless he should wrong him. In that case the partner may say to him, "You have betrayed me," or "You favored another in buying and selling against me," or "in what I committed to your charge," or "you set out to squander my wealth," or the like, and he then has the right to put him to his oath that he did no such thing and intended none of it. And if the wealth is held equally between the two partners and they stipulate that the profit and the loss are likewise to be shared equally between them, then they are held to their stipulation; but if they stipulate a disparity, the profit is divided as they have stipulated, while the loss is borne in proportion to each one's capital.

When two partners contribute capital and labor in equal measure, the profit and the loss are both divided according to the terms they have agreed upon. If they stipulate a different arrangement, the profit follows what they have stipulated, while the loss remains proportional to the capital each has put in. The same holds when their contributions differ, so that one partner brings more than the other: the profit is shared according to their agreement, but the loss is borne in proportion to the capital.

When the money belongs to only one of them, the arrangement becomes mudaraba, that is, speculation, and the one who has no money is the mudarib, the working partner. The terms may stipulate that he alone shall labor with the money and trade in it, or that both shall work together, and they hold to whatever they have stipulated. The profit between them is divided according to their contract, but the loss falls upon the owner of the money alone and not upon the mudarib. If the owner of the money guarantees the mudarib against any shortfall or loss he might incur, that guarantee voids the arrangement: it is no longer a valid mudaraba or partnership but becomes a loan. The borrower then keeps whatever gain he earns, and he must guarantee the principal as he would for himself, since he has taken it upon himself.

If the mudarib violates what the owner of the money stipulated, whether in what he buys, what he trades in, where he travels, or any other condition set in the mudaraba contract, the profit between them is still divided according to their agreement, but should a loss occur or the money pass out of his hands, he is its guarantor and answerable for whatever is lost, because he has transgressed the terms.

If a man gives money to another to work with, trade in, and return a specified, fixed profit, this is not permissible and is not binding upon the one to whom it was given. It is riba, which is unlawful for him. He must return whatever he took and repent to Allah Almighty for it. The single exception is when the one who hands over the money is the master and the recipient his slave, for then what is paid is lawful and permitted to him. There is no riba between a master and his slave, because the slave's wealth belongs to his master in any case.

If one of the partners dies, the partnership is dissolved, and any partner may annul and dissolve it whenever he wishes. If a partner or a mudarib is accused of wrongdoing, he clears himself by oath. Partnership is disliked when it is formed over goods that remain distinct and are not mingled, each contribution kept separate. When two parties share in what each holds apart, whatever each one holds remains his own, whether it increases or decreases. But if they wish to enter a genuine partnership, each may sell to the other half of the separate goods in his possession at a known price, commit to that sale, and on that basis form a valid partnership.

It is disliked for a Muslim to enter into partnership with a dhimmi, and for a believer to partner with one who differs from him in religion, since such a partner may buy and sell in ways the believer does not permit and cannot prevent, thereby drawing what is unlawful and riba-tainted into his wealth. Yet if the believer or Muslim himself oversees all the buying and selling, and the dhimmi or the one who differs has no hand in it, there is nothing wrong with such a partnership.

When a mudarib dies leaving a debt he owes, speculation funds entrusted to him, and deposits in his keeping, and none of these is found intact within his estate, the ruling distinguishes the debt from the trust. The debt is binding, for it rests upon his liability. But as for the speculation funds and the deposit, he was a trustee over them. If they are not found in his estate by their own substance, and no proof is established against him that he consumed them, was compensated for them, destroyed them, mingled them with his own wealth, or did anything that would make him liable, then nothing is required of his heirs except an oath that they do not know him to have done any such thing, nor have they done it themselves, nor is any of it in their hands. If the holder of such a claim names, before he dies, the party to whom a particular asset belongs, it goes to the one he named; but if he did not hand it over, then the debt takes precedence and is satisfied first. When the funds are already fully committed, they may not be shared with an absent partner, and partnership with one who is absent is not valid.

If a man is owed a debt by another and, before recovering it, attempts to enter that debt into a partnership or a mudaraba with the debtor, this is not valid, and no partnership or mudaraba is contracted by it, until the creditor takes possession of the debt and hands it over; only then may he form a partnership or mudaraba with it.

When two partners separate and settle accounts while a debt remains owed to them from their partnership, they may not divide that outstanding debt on the condition that each recovers only the share assigned to him, if afterward they fall into dispute over it. What is required is that whatever each recovers belongs to both of them in common, and whatever of the debt is lost falls upon both alike. If, however, one says to the other, "Give me my capital," or whatever sum they had agreed upon, "and yours is whatever exceeds it, and upon you falls whatever is lacking from it," and both agree to this, then it is permissible for them to act upon what they have agreed.

On Pre-emption

Pre-emption, shuf'ah, applies to dwellings and real property when an undivided share or shares are sold, common portions not yet partitioned between the seller and his partner or partners. In such a case the partner is more entitled to the sale, the worthier pre-emptor, over a stranger who has no share. If one partner exercises his right while the others do not, he is more entitled than those who held back. If they all come forward, they share it equally, each taking according to his portion in the partnership; where their shares differ, the pre-emption follows those shares, and where they are equal, it is divided equally among them.

There is no pre-emption for a neighbor or anyone else in property already divided, nor over a river, a ship, an animal, or anything other than real estate and undivided shares, which is to say land and what stands upon it of building or planted trees. If a property is owned jointly between two people and one sells his share, his partner is the worthier to it by pre-emption. Pre-emption holds on behalf of a child until he comes of age, and if his guardian or whoever is charged with his affairs is able to exercise it for him and it would benefit the child, it should be taken on his behalf. It holds likewise for an absent partner until he returns or word of the sale reaches him, whereupon he either takes it up or lets it pass within the span of a year. The one who is present has the same span: from the time the sale reaches him until a year has passed, unless he surrenders his right of pre-emption.

The right belongs to the pre-emptor by virtue of his partnership in what is sold, whether his share in it is small or large, and whether he is a partner in the whole of it or only in part, as with a wall standing between two houses that belongs to both owners, or in which one of them holds a common share, or any other right they own that the sale falls upon, be it slight or considerable. Pre-emption in such a thing belongs to whoever holds a share in it, however small or large that share may be.

Pre-emption is established for the people of a private lane, al-Raighah, who do not have direct participation with one another but share the lane, provided no barrier separates them from the property in question. There is no such right in a public, commonly traveled street, because the people whose dwellings open onto that street do not own it as the people of a private lane own theirs. They merely benefit from it through their courtyards, and in this they stand on equal footing with the passers-by upon the road. So the people of the street have no pre-emption over what is sold along it by right of the street; that right belongs to the people of the private lane among themselves.

If a house lies within a private lane and contains a dwelling, and that dwelling is sold, the people of the houses are worthier of pre-emption than the buyer, and they may exercise it over what is sold within the lane, because they hold ownership of their own courtyards while the people of the lane own nothing of those houses. Likewise, if there is a lane within a lane, the people of the inner lane may pre-empt what lies along their own path and passage. The people of the outer lane, and those in its upper reaches, may not pre-empt what lies below them, in which they hold no right and to which they have no path, beyond the boundaries of their own dwellings into the lane. The matter is to be examined in the light of shared rights, for it is the sharing of rights that governs.

Whoever holds a right in what is sold, or in any of the rights upon which the sale falls, is a partner and therefore a pre-emptor, and this reflects the principle of shared rights among the people of the lane. For if a man owned outright the houses that lie within his lane, it would be his right to open the lane from the outside, to possess the whole of it, and to bar anyone from entering or passing through it without his leave. So too, when there is a lane within a lane and the owner of the inner lane owns all the houses that open onto it, he may take possession of the whole and withhold it from others. The same applies to everything within the lane, except what is bordered by a wall belonging to others among the people of the lane, for there his ownership reaches only to its boundary.

There is no pre-emption for a Jew or a Christian against a Muslim. Muslims may pre-empt in the sale of what they share with Muslims and with dhimmis alike. Likewise the dhimmis may pre-empt against one another in what they trade among themselves where pre-emption is due, but they have no right of pre-emption against Muslims. There is no pre-emption in a gift, in charity, in a grant of residence, in a life grant, or in a grant for the lifetime of the recipient. Nor is there pre-emption in what is sold against goods rather than a price, as when a house is exchanged for another house, or for a riding animal, or for any other livestock or merchandise. In such a case the pre-emptor may not say, "I will pay the value of the goods that the sale was concluded upon," and so take the house. So too, if a man marries a woman with a house as her dowry, and pre-emption would ordinarily be due in such a house, the pre-emptor may not say, "I will pay a dowry equal to this woman's and take the house."

But if the house was sold for a price, and a quantity of goods was received in settlement of that price, the pre-emptor is offered the house at the price upon which the sale was concluded. Likewise, if the owner of a house marries a woman for a known dowry and then hands her a house in settlement of it, this stands as a sale, and the pre-emptor may take the house for the dowry by which the woman received it. And if what is subject to pre-emption is sold for something that has a known equivalent, the pre-emptor may take it for that equivalent, as when it is sold for measured wheat, for dates, or for the like of what is measured or weighed and has a known equivalent. Pre-emption is then due, and the pre-emptor is given that equivalent.

The pre-emptor may press his claim against either the seller or the buyer, whichever the obligation falls upon. When a sale is concluded and completed between buyer and seller, and the pre-emptor comes forward against the seller before the price has been collected from the buyer and pays it to him, what he receives in return is rightfully his, and the buyer may neither prevent him nor say, "I will pay what I bought it for and take it from you, since the pre-emption stands against me." For the pre-emptor's claim rests upon the seller, and the buyer is bound to deliver. If, on the other hand, the buyer is left to pay the price first, and the pre-emptor then presses his claim against him within the period in which the claim is valid, that too is his right.

When property subject to pre-emption is taken by a buyer who then sells it on, and it passes from hand to hand one after another, whether the pre-emptor knows of it or not, he may press his claim against whichever of them he chooses, seller or buyer, paying that party the price he himself paid to the one from whom he bought, whether more or less, so long as the period of pre-emption has not expired and he has not relinquished his right. He is a pre-emptor at the moment of every sale, and his failure to act upon any one of them does not forfeit his right while the period still runs. This is like a man who buys what is subject to another's pre-emption for a price, then sells it to a second for less or more, who sells it to a third, and so on until a whole succession of people have owned it. The pre-emptor may, if he wishes, press his claim against any one of them he chooses, unless the period from the time of the original purchase has elapsed or he has relinquished his right. If he presses his claim against the first buyer, who within the period of pre-emption had sold what was subject to it for more than he paid, the pre-emption entails the unwinding of that sale and all that followed it, and the pre-emptor takes the property at the price the buyer paid, delivering it to him. The same course follows for each of those who came after.

If a single house has an upper and a lower dwelling, divided and sold separately, there is no pre-emption for the owner of the upper over the owner of the lower, nor of the lower over the upper, when the division took place before the sale. Even though one part stands above the other, the relation between them is merely that of neighbors. But if they share a common entrance, or anything that remains undivided between them, and the sale falls upon the seller's share in that common property, then pre-emption is due in it.

When undivided and divided property is sold together in a single transaction, the partner in the jointly held portion may take the whole transaction, both the common and the divided parts, and he may not take one part without the other, unless both parties agree. If a buyer purchases the common share at a price and the sale is concluded between him and the seller, and the seller then withdraws part of the price so that the sale comes to rest upon what was received after the reduction, and the pre-emptor then comes forward demanding to take it at the first, higher price, that is not his to claim. He is entitled only to what was actually paid, since the sale finally rested upon that lesser sum.

If a seller sells the property for a price and then gives back to the buyer some or all of that price as a gift or as charity, and the pre-emptor then comes forward wishing to exercise his right, he must pay the price upon which the sale was contracted. He has no claim to deduct what the seller returned to the buyer, for that return was a device and a manipulation meant to cut off the pre-emption. Should the seller inflate the price and then hand the surplus back to the buyer, or should the buyer pay part as profit and part as price in order to circumvent the pre-emptor by such a stratagem, then the contract and the true intention are what govern. The sale is held to rest upon the whole price, or upon what was genuinely received, and the gifts or charity used to disguise it do not invalidate the pre-emptor's right.

So if the price has been inflated and the pre-emptor still wishes to proceed, he pays what the buyer actually paid and receives all that the two parties truly agreed upon. This is established and confirmed by the acknowledgment of the buyer against whom the claim is pressed, or by clear proof brought against him that the matter was indeed a contrivance. But if the pre-emptor merely alleges this without proof, and the other party denies it, then the most that can be required of the one who denies is an oath answering the claim made against him. Such manipulation to cut off pre-emption is unlawful for both seller and buyer, for it is a conspiracy to nullify one of the established rights. And when a sale has been concealed, the pre-emptor keeps his right of pre-emption from the moment the sale reaches him until a year has passed, just as he does when the sale is open and known.

If a sale is concluded in secret, the holder of the right of pre-emption (the *shafi*) may make his claim from the moment the sale reaches his knowledge, and that right remains open to him for one year, even if the sale was in fact publicly known and he was present when it took place.

If he lets the year pass and only then claims that he was never informed of the sale, he is not believed, because the sale was a matter of common knowledge. The duty to claim does not arise until the sale has been concluded and finalized. Once it is concluded and finalized and the pre-emptor is bound to his claim, the claim is binding upon him even if the seller has not yet received the price, and the pre-emptor may pay whichever of the two he wishes, the seller or the buyer.

If the pre-emptor proceeds against the buyer, the buyer's obligation is only to deliver the property to him. The warranty for it rests upon the original seller, and no warranty falls upon the buyer when the pre-emptor proceeds against him, the delivery of the property tracing back to the seller.

If the buyer builds upon what he has purchased that is subject to pre-emption, or sows it, that act counts as a completed taking of possession. If a man claims that he bought the property from someone who is absent, the pre-emptor may come forward, yet neither the claim of sale nor the pre-emption is established against the absent party, and the pre-emptor may not exercise his right until the sale is shown to be valid. Likewise, if a man claims to have purchased from someone present who denies the sale, the pre-emptor may not press his claim until the sale is validated, and his right of pre-emption then runs from the time of that validation.

If a house is held as an endowment for particular named persons, and one of them builds within it and then dies, so that his portion passes to his heirs, and some of the heirs sell their inherited share, the pre-emption belongs to the heirs. Whoever relinquished his pre-emption before the sale, and then comes forward after the sale has taken place, is free to press it or to leave it, for the relinquishment is not binding until after the sale has been concluded.

If the pre-emptor and the buyer disagree over the price, the buyer's word is accepted under oath that he bought at the price he names, and that price is what is paid, unless clear evidence is established to the contrary. In the same way, if the pre-emptor accepts the buyer's word, or the buyer swears and the pre-emptor pays him the price upon that oath, and it afterward emerges through evidence that the buyer in fact purchased for less than that, or the buyer himself admits it, the pre-emptor may recover from him whatever was charged above the true price.

If the pre-emptor exercises his right, then exchanges the property and sells it onward, and afterward turns to the buyer demanding that the buyer pay him the price and return what was bought from him, making that his claim of pre-emption, he is bound to carry it through. If he does not press the claim, nothing is required of him.

This is, in its entirety, the position of the Ahl al-Bayt, may (عليهم السلام).

**On the preservation of oaths and covenants**

Allah, Mighty and Majestic, said: "And guard your oaths" (Al-Ma'idah 89). And: "And those who are keepers of their trusts and their covenant" (Al-Mu'minun 8). And He said: "Fulfill the covenant of Allah when you have made a covenant, and do not break your oaths after their confirmation, when you have made Allah a surety over you" (Al-Nahl 91). And He said: "Indeed, those who exchange the covenant of Allah and their own oaths for a small price will have no share in the Hereafter; Allah will not speak to them, nor look upon them on the Day of Resurrection, nor purify them, and theirs is a painful punishment" (Al Imran 77).

The Messenger of Allah, (ع), forbade swearing by any other than Allah, Mighty and Majestic, and said: "Whoever swears, let him swear by Allah; whoever swears by Allah, let him be truthful; and whoever has another swear to him by Allah, let him accept it; and whoever does not do so is no Muslim." And he, (ع), said: "Three are those at whom Allah will not look on the Day of Resurrection, whom He will not purify, and for whom there is a painful punishment: a man who pledges allegiance to an imam for a portion of this world, so that if he is given it he keeps faith, and if he is not given it he withholds it; a man who has water beside the road and denies it to the traveler; and a man who swears after the afternoon prayer that he was offered such-and-such for his goods, so that another takes him at his word and accepts it, while he is lying."

Ali, may the blessings of Allah be upon him, said: "Allah, Mighty and Majestic, does not hallow one who swears by His name falsely." And it is related from him, (ع), that he said: "Beware the false oath, for it makes the merchandise sell but wipes away the blessing; and whoever swears a false oath has been audacious against Allah, so let him await His punishment." And he, the blessings of Allah be upon him, said: "Whoever breaks his pledge of allegiance will meet Allah on the Day of Resurrection maimed," meaning he will have no hand.

**On what is binding in oaths and what is not**

Allah, Mighty and Majestic, said: "Allah will not take you to task for the thoughtless word in your oaths" (Al-Baqarah 225). Ja'far ibn Muhammad, the blessings of Allah be upon him, said: Allah will not call you to account for the thoughtless word in your oaths. The thoughtlessness meant here is a man's saying "No, by Allah" and "Yes, by Allah" while his heart is not set upon the oath. He, (ع), means what a person utters of this in the midst of his speech, swearing by Allah within it while he does not intend to swear. This is confirmed by the words of Allah, Mighty and Majestic, following that saying: "Allah will not take you to task for the thoughtless word in your oaths, but He will take you to task for the oaths your hearts have deliberately bound" (Al-Ma'idah 89), meaning what the swearer has resolved in his heart to swear upon.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad, the blessings of Allah be upon him, made this plain, saying of a man who declares, in the course of his speech, "By Allah, I did not do it," concerning something he did in fact do, while his conscience is not set upon the oath: "It is a lie he has told; let him seek Allah's forgiveness for it." And he, (ع), said: "The oath is according to what the claimant administers it upon." And it is related that he forbade equivocation in oaths, and said that if a man is wronged, the oath goes by the intention of the one who swears, and if he is a wrongdoer, it goes by the intention of the one who administers it. Equivocation is the use of likeness in speech: that a man intends one thing but couches it in the likeness of another, so that he leads the listener to suppose that the thing he has likened it to is what is meant, while he intends and conceals something else. The People of the Book are made to swear by Allah and by whatever they hold in reverence within their own faith.

Abu Abd Allah Ja'far ibn Muhammad, the blessings of Allah be upon him, said concerning the words of Allah, Mighty and Majestic, "And remember your Lord when you forget" (Al-Kahf 24): this concerns the oath. If you say, "By Allah, I will surely do such-and-such," and you forget to make the exception, then say, when you remember, "if Allah wills." And he related that Ali, the blessings of Allah be upon him, said: a company of Jews came to the Messenger of Allah, (ع), and asked him about a matter, and he said, "Tomorrow I will tell you of it," and he waited for the revelation of Allah, Mighty and Majestic, and did not make the exception in his words. So the revelation was withheld from him forty days, after which there came down upon him: "And never say of anything, Indeed I will do that tomorrow, except that Allah wills; and remember your Lord when you forget" (Al-Kahf 23 to 24).

They, (عليهم السلام), commanded the exception in the oath, and said: put the will of Allah first; and they held this to be commendable. The Commander of the Believers, the blessings of Allah be upon him, said: whoever swears and then says "if Allah wills" incurs no perjury. Ja'far ibn Muhammad, the blessings of Allah be upon him, said: if he moves his tongue with the exception, he has made the exception, even if he does not say it aloud. And he related that the Messenger of Allah, (ع), said: a man's oath admits of an exception, and the exception holds when it is joined to the oath, that he swears and then says "if Allah wills" before he breaks off his speech. This is a permissible exception by consensus, and one who swears and excepts in this way does not fall into perjury.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad, the blessings of Allah be upon him, said that the exception is permissible even after forty days, because the Messenger of Allah, (ع), made the exception after the revelation had been withheld from him, following his words to the Jews who questioned him, "Tomorrow I will tell you," when Allah, Mighty and Majestic, said, "And remember your Lord when you forget" (Al-Kahf 24). The like of this is related from Ali, the Commander of the Believers, the blessings of Allah be upon him. And it is related from Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali, the blessings of Allah be upon him, that he said: the exception is permissible even after a year.

The exception in an oath applies only to what the swearer swears that he will do or will not do. But one who is put to an oath over a right claimed against him, and swears to the claimant, and makes an exception, whether secretly or aloud, his exception there counts for nothing. The oath in such a case rests upon intentions, and upon the apparent words, and upon the intention of the one who administers it. If the swearer intends or conceals something by which to drop the oath, or contrives some trick in it, or equivocates, nothing of it is lifted from him; the oath and the perjury both stand. He must discharge what he swore to the one to whom it is due, and repent thereafter and seek forgiveness. There is no expiation for it, if he has perjured himself, except that he carry out what he swore for the one entitled to it, and then repentance and the seeking of forgiveness afterward.

There is no oath over what the swearer does not possess, as when a man swears that he will free a slave he does not own, or that he will give away something that is not in his possession. There is no oath by divorce, nor by manumission, nor by Hajj, nor by Umrah, nor by anything else among oaths sworn by other than Allah, Blessed and Exalted. The oath is only by Allah, Mighty and Majestic.

Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali, the blessings of Allah be upon him, was asked about the words of Allah, Mighty and Majestic, "And when the Prophet confided to one of his wives a certain matter" (Al-Tahrim 3), to the end of the verse. He said: the Messenger of Allah, (ع), had been alone with his bondwoman Mariyah the Copt at the beginning of her coming to him, before he had settled her in a house, and A'ishah came upon him. She questioned him about it, and he, wishing to please her, swore to her that he would not return to Mariyah, and forbade her to himself, and charged his wife to keep the matter secret. She did not do so, but told it to Hafsah, and the two of them joined together against him. So Allah, Mighty and Majestic, sent down upon him: "O Prophet, why do you forbid yourself what Allah has made lawful for you?" (Al-Tahrim 1), and commanded him to expiate the oath he had sworn (Al-Tahrim 5).

He did not make anything binding upon him in his forbidding to himself what was lawful, but rebuked him for it. So whoever forbids to himself what Allah, Mighty and Majestic, has made lawful for him, it is not thereby forbidden to him; it remains lawful for him, and let him partake of it, and nothing is upon him. If he has sworn, he expiates his oath and partakes of what Allah, Mighty and Majestic, has made lawful for him. The Messenger of Allah, (ع), said: "Whoever swears an oath upon a matter and then sees that what is contrary to what he swore is better for him, let him do that which is better and expiate his oath."

Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali, the blessings of Allah be upon him, said: every oath sworn in disobedience counts for nothing; there is neither perjury nor expiation in it. Whoever swears that he will not do what is obligatory upon him, or that he will do what is permitted to him, and then refrains from what is not permitted and does what is obligatory, incurs neither perjury nor expiation. And whoever swears, in an act of obedience, that he will do it, must do what he swore; and if he does not do it, he expiates his oath.

**On vows**

Allah, Mighty and Majestic, said: "Indeed the righteous will drink from a cup whose mixture is camphor" (Al-Insan 5), up to His words, Mighty and Majestic: "They fulfill their vows and fear a Day whose evil is widespread" (Al-Insan 7). And the Messenger of Allah, (ع), forbade the vow to other than Allah, Blessed and Exalted, and the vow made in disobedience and in the severing of kinship.
