# The Marketplace: Honest Earning, Trade, and Property

The Marketplace: Honest Earning, Trade, and Property

Allah has commanded His servants to seek what He has apportioned and to ask Him for His bounty. He says, وَسْأَلُوا اللَّهَ مِن فَضْلِهِ, "And ask Allah of His bounty" (Al-Nisa': 32), and He says, فَإِذَا قُضِيَتِ الصَّلَاةُ فَانتَشِرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَابْتَغُوا مِن فَضْلِ اللَّهِ, "When the prayer is concluded, spread out in the land and seek the bounty of Allah" (Al-Jumu'ah: 10). Yet the seeking is to be measured and calm, never grasping, for provision is already assured to its owner.

A man came to the Prophet (ص) and said, "O Messenger of Allah, my soul has not been content with anything of this world, nor is it satisfied by it." The Messenger of Allah (ص) said to him, "Say: O Allah, please me with Your decree, bless me in what You have given me, and make me content with what You have apportioned for me, so that I may not wish to hasten what You have delayed, nor to delay what You have hastened."

Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) narrated from his fathers (عليهم السلام), from the Messenger of Allah (ص), that he said, "The faithful spirit (روح الأمين) breathed into my heart that no soul shall die until it has completed its provision. So be at ease in seeking sustenance, for there is no servant of Allah but that he has a provision with a veil between him and it. If he is patient with restraint, Allah brings it to him lawfully; and if he is not patient, he tears the veil and consumes it unlawfully." And he said (ص), "Do not let the slowness of any provision drive you to seek it through disobedience to Allah. Be gracious in your asking, for what is with Allah is not attained through disobedience." And he said (ص), "If one of you falls into hardship, let him go out and seek sustenance, strike out across the earth, and seek the bounty of Allah. Let him not sit in his house and so bring grief upon himself and his household and become a burden upon others." And he said (ص), "In the shade of the Throne, on the Day when there is no shade but His, is a man who went out striking across the earth, seeking the bounty of Allah, to restrain himself from need and to provide for his family."

A man came and said, "O Messenger of Allah, I take up no trade but that I am thwarted in it." He said, "Look for something in which you once succeeded, and hold fast to it." The man said, "Gathering qaraz (القرظ)." He said, "Then keep to the qaraz."

The Messenger of Allah (ص) once passed through a marketplace and said, "O company of merchants, the merchant is a transgressor, and the transgressor is in the Fire." When they heard this, they shut the doors of their shops and abandoned trade. The next day he passed through the market and saw no one there. He asked, "What is the matter with them?" It was said, "They abandoned trade when they heard what you said yesterday, O Messenger of Allah." He said, "I say the same today, except for the one who takes his due and gives what is owed."

He said (ص), "I was sent as a mercy to people; I was not sent as a merchant or a farmer. The worst of this nation are its merchants and its cultivators, except for those among them who are sparing of their religion." And he said (ص), "Verily Allah loves the servant who is easy in selling, easy in buying, easy in repaying, and easy in requiring repayment."

The Messenger of Allah (ص) saw a sturdy young man driving well-fed camels. It was said to him, "O Messenger, how fine is this man's strength and the strength of his camels; were it spent in the way of Allah!" The Messenger of Allah (ص) said to the young man, "What are you doing with these camels of yours?" He said, "I labor with them and earn from them." He said, "And what do you do with the earnings?" He said, "I have a wife and children whom I support; I keep them by it from having to beg of others, and I pay off a debt I owe." The Messenger of Allah (ص) said, "If he speaks truthfully, then he has a reward like the reward of the one who goes out to fight in the way of Allah, and of the pilgrim performing Hajj and 'Umrah."

'Ali (ع) said, "The going out of one of you in the way of Allah is no greater than his going out to seek for his children and household what will set them aright." And he (ع) said to a man who asked his leave to take up trade, "Have you gained understanding in religion?" The man said, "Some of it." He said, "Woe to you! Understanding first, then the marketplace; for whoever buys and sells without understanding is mired in usury, and then is mired the deeper."

Muhammad ibn al-Munkadir said: I saw Muhammad ibn 'Ali ibn al-Husayn (ع) on a day of severe heat, coming back from an estate, and he was a heavy-set man, weary with fatigue. I said to myself, "I shall counsel him." So I said, "May I be your ransom, a man of your standing in nobility, to exert yourself like this in seeking the world! Tell me, if your appointed time came to you in this state, what would you say of it?" He said, "If my death came to me while I was in this state, it would come to me while I was in obedience to Allah, laboring to earn from what is lawful, restraining myself from need of others, and laying up for my Hereafter. What I would fear is that my death might come to me while I was in some disobedience to Allah, and I seek refuge in Allah from that." I said, "May I be your ransom; I meant to counsel you, and you have counseled me."

A man said to Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع), "O son of the Messenger of Allah, pray to Allah that He grant me provision in ease, without my having to seek it." He said, "I will not pray to Allah for that. Seek, as Allah has commanded you." And he (ع) said, "It befits the Muslim to seek his provision even until the heat of the sun strikes him." And he (ع) related from the supplications of the Ahl al-Bayt (عليهم السلام): "O Allah, do not burden me with seeking what You have not apportioned for me, lest my occupation with it keep me long from Your obedience while I am unable to attain any of it. O Allah, whatever You have apportioned for me of that, bring it to me in chastity and ease, and set me right by that with which You set right the righteous, for the righteousness of the righteous is through You." Abu Muhammad ibn 'Ali (ع) said that this was from the supplication of Dawud (ع).

Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said to Sufyan, who had come to ask of him, "O Sufyan, when a blessing appears to you, praise Allah; and when some provision is slow in coming to you, seek Allah's forgiveness." Then he recited, اسْتَغْفِرُوا رَبَّكُمْ إِنَّهُ كَانَ غَفَّارًا, "Ask forgiveness of your Lord; He is ever Forgiving," up to His words, وَيَجْعَل لَّكُمْ أَنْهَارًا, "and He will make rivers for you." He said, "And when some trial of this world befalls you, say, 'There is no power nor strength except with Allah, the Most High, the Most Great.' O Sufyan, take these three." Sufyan said, "And which are the three?"

He (ع) also said to a man of his Shi'a, advising him: "When you set out in the morning, or depart at any hour, to seek provision, and you desire success in that, pray two rak'ahs. When you have finished the tashahhud, say: 'O Allah, I have gone forth, if I am one who goes forth this morning, or I have set out, if I am setting out, seeking of Your bounty as You have commanded me. So grant me of Your bounty a lawful, wholesome provision, as You have commanded me, and grant me wellness in what You have provided me.' Say that three times. Then pray two more rak'ahs, and after the tashahhud say: 'I have gone forth, if I am going forth this morning, or I have set out, if I am setting out, by Allah's might and power, not by any might of mine nor power, but by Your might, O Lord, and Your power. I disavow before You, O Lord, all might and power. O Allah, I ask You for the good of this day if it be day, or the good of this night if it be night, and I ask You to provide me from Your bounty a lawful, wholesome provision that You drive to me by Your might and Your power, while I am at ease and in chastity.' Say that three times, then rise to your purpose."

And he (ع) said, "Allah has distributed provision among His servants, and He has favored some over others with great bounty. So ask Allah of His bounty." And he (ع) said, "The one who goes out in search of lawful provision is like the one who strives in the way of Allah." And he (ع) said to a man of his Shi'a who dealt in trade, "If you seek something of this world and it eludes you, remember what Allah has singled you out for from His religion, and what He has favored you with, turning it away from others; for that is more fitting that your soul rise above it." 'Ali ibn al-Husayn (ع) said, "Allah has made provision ten parts: nine of them in trade, and one part in all other things."

A man who was devoted to Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said to him, "O son of the Messenger of Allah, I have given up trade, awaiting the appearance of your command." He said to him, "Do not do so. Open your door, spread your mat, and seek provision from your Lord, for that is better for you. If you hold back from seeking provision, your wealth will drain away. So seek from the bounty of Allah, your Lord."

On What Is Forbidden to Sell

The Messenger of Allah (ص) forbade the sale of free persons and declared it void, and likewise the sale of carrion, of wine, and of unlawfully taken things. It is transmitted from the Imams of his household (عليهم السلام) that everything forbidden may neither be sold, nor earned from, nor may its price be taken, such as filth, carrion, blood, swine, wine, and every intoxicant, and instruments of amusement, all of them. They forbade making such things, earning from them, selling them, buying them, learning them, and lending any aid in them.

But they permitted the sale of grapes, pressed juice, raisins, honey, dates, and figs, even to one who would make wine or intoxicant from them; for the sale of these commodities is lawful, and the sin lies only upon the one who turns them to what is forbidden. They also permitted taking the price for anything whose sale is forbidden, from one who takes it for his own use, though the forbidden use remains upon him.

As for a price that has passed lawfully into another's hand, there is no harm in it. Nor is there any harm in transacting with polytheists, or in taking the price of what they buy from Muslims. What is in the hands of polytheists that they have sold and taken possession of belongs to them, though it is not permitted to sell to them what may not be sold. Most of their wealth is usury and unlawful gain, yet it is taken from them as jizya and as prices for what they purchase from the Muslims, and it is lawful for the one who takes it by its right.

They (عليهم السلام) disliked the price of the biting dog, but permitted the price of the hunting dog, and the sale of the herding dog is allowed. They disliked the price of the cat (السنور). They forbade buying what is known to be stolen or taken by betrayal, until it is returned to its owners, and then one may buy it from them. And they permitted the sale of copies of the Qur'an (المصاحف).

The Messenger of Allah (ص) forbade, in general terms, the sale of water, pasturage (الكلأ), and fire. This prohibition falls upon selling what no one owns, such as pasture in the open wilderness or the rainwater within it and the like, of waters that no one owns; and the flame of fire, such as that from which one lights a lamp or a brand for no other benefit. But whoever has a well that he has dug in his own land, or a flowing spring, or a river he has cut, or a cistern, or the like into which rainwater settles within his property and ground, or a vessel in which he has stored it, or has carried it on his back or his mount or by whatever means, then his sale of it is permitted. Likewise, if a man gathers herbage from the wild, or uproots it, or tends it and waters it in land he owns, its sale is permitted. And so with fire, when it belongs to one who owns it, such as firewood or charcoal or anything else in which fire is kindled: its sale is permitted, for it is wealth among the kinds of wealth.

There is no harm in buying from and selling to those known for betrayal, theft, and other wrongdoing, unless one knows that what is bought from them is itself stolen or taken by betrayal; if one does not know this, there is no harm in dealing with them. We have already said that buying from and selling to polytheists is permitted where one does not know that they have betrayed or stolen; and the Muslim is all the more fit to be careful in this.

On the Prohibition of Selling Gharar

Allah has said, وَلَا تَأْكُلُوا أَمْوَالَكُم بَيْنَكُم بِالْبَاطِلِ, "And do not consume one another's wealth unjustly" (Al-Baqarah: 188). The Messenger of Allah (ص), as the pure Imams of the household (عليهم السلام) have transmitted from him, forbade in general terms the sale of gharar (الغرر). A sale of gharar is any sale contracted upon something unknown. The Messenger of Allah (ص) made plain several of its forms.

Among them, he forbade the sale of ḥabal al-ḥabala (حبل الحبلة). Of this kind was a sale the people of the Jahiliyya used to practice: one of them would sell a commodity against the term of a she-camel's bringing forth, then against what her offspring would in turn bring forth. And among them was the sale of offspring before it is born; they used to trade the load in the bellies of camels and other beasts.

Among them, he (ص) forbade the sale of al-maḍamin and al-malaqih. Al-malaqih are the embryos we have mentioned, sold while still in their mothers' wombs; and al-maḍamin are what is in the loins of the stallions, for they used to trade what a stallion would sire over a stated term, whether once or a fixed number of times.

Among them, he (ص) forbade the sale of al-mulamasa (touch). This is that a man sells a garment or an object folded up, its inside unseen, or makes the mere touching of it with the hand stand in place of inspection, on the condition that the buyer has no choice once he sees it, unfolds it, or spreads it out; or that he says, "If you touch this article, the sale between us is binding." Yet the contract of sale is made binding only by its being spoken, and by the parting of the two parties in bodies after mutual consent to its terms. Of the same kind is the touching of goods from behind a curtain, so that the sale is concluded on this alone.

And among them, he (ص) forbade the sale of al-munabadha (throwing). This is that a man looks at a folded garment in another's hand and says, "I buy this from you, and when you throw it to me the sale between us is complete, with no right of withdrawal for either of us"; or that each of them throws to the other a garment or some object.

The Marketplace: Honest Earning, Trade, and Property

Among the transactions the Messenger of Allah (ص) nullified was the sale concluded by a pledge given over a thing without turning it over or examining it, the buyer simply saying, "This for that." Of these too was the sale of pebbles, a practice of the pre-Islamic era in which the two parties sealed the contract by each throwing his share, making that throw itself the binding act of sale. He (ص) abolished all such sales.

He also prohibited the sale of al-wala' and its gift, declaring that al-wala' belongs only to those who confer it by lineage and is not to be sold. He forbade the sale of a slave held on the condition of keeping him before he has come into possession, as well as the sale of a stray camel and animals like it. Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: If such a stray animal or its like is sold together with something present, such as a slave, a beast, a garment, or any other permissible item that is at hand, then the sale of that combination is valid, provided the seller and buyer both know the stray or absent item. The price, upon the absent thing's arrival, falls upon the present item that the two parties have seen. This does not fall under the words of Allah Almighty, ﴿وَلَا تَأْكُلُوٓاْ أَمْوَٰلَكُم بَيْنَكُم بِٱلْبَٰطِلِ﴾, "And do not consume one another's wealth unjustly."

Similarly, he said, if a man sells the milk in the udder of his sheep along with a quantity already drawn from it that is permissible to sell, the sale is valid, because the price falls upon the present portion the two parties have seen. Most sales are of this kind. They fall upon what the parties have seen, or upon what some of it is seen and purchased while the greater part remains concealed, as in houses, dwellings, garments, and animals. One sees only what is apparent and not what is hidden, yet the sale covers both the visible and the hidden. It is permissible to buy metal dust with gold and silver, hand to hand, even if upon extraction it yields more or less than the price paid, because at the moment of purchase it was neither silver nor gold but merely dust. It is disliked, however, to sell such dust by its like. It is narrated from 'Ali (ع) that he disliked the sale of fish still in the water when it can be reached only by fishing; but if a portion of it is secured and can be had without hunting, its sale is permissible. He forbade the sale of wool upon the backs of sheep and of their hides in the same way, until it is sheared or the animal is skinned. He forbade a man to sell his share of the spoils before he has received it, since he does not know what will come to him from it. And the sale of sukuk, that is, written certificates of debt, is likewise forbidden.

Mention of the Sale of Fruits

The Imams from the household of the Prophet narrated that he forbade in a general way the sale of fruits until their goodness becomes apparent. Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said that the ripening of fruit begins when it turns red or yellow. The Messenger of Allah (ص) meant its coming into goodness, so that once something of it has become good it is permissible to sell all of it. If none has yet ripened, the sale is conducted on the basis of the principle already mentioned regarding present and absent goods. If nothing of the fruit has appeared that is free of what is objectionable, the sale is not permissible unless both parties consent to it; and if either of them annuls it, it is annulled. If some kind of the fruit has come into goodness, that kind may be sold together with the other kinds, even if none of those others has yet ripened, provided all is sold in a single transaction. They (عليهم السلام) said: If the fruit has blossomed, or a part of it, its sale is permissible, and the sale may include what its trees will bear of such fruit for a year or two afterward, so long as that is named and stated. If nothing comes forth in the future, the price falls upon what was present at the time of sale, this too being founded upon the same principle.

The general public do not permit the sale of fruit for years to come, claiming that it is deceptive, yet they permit it once some of it shows goodness. Their position is not consistent, for the goodness that appears while the sale still encompasses the remainder falls under the very same ruling. This is what they denied and rejected, since they refused that any part of the sale should fall upon what had not yet come to be, namely the portion whose goodness had not begun, while still permitting the sale of that portion together with what had ripened, with the price resting on what had ripened. So too Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: It is permissible to sell the fruit once its goodness, or the goodness of some of it, has begun, for a year or two afterward; if it does not come forth this year, it comes forth the next. Upon this they founded their ruling in the matters we have described concerning sales and the consuming of wealth. Those of the general public who opposed this held otherwise, though it was their duty to follow the saying of the Imams (عليهم السلام) and abandon dispute with them. What has come from them (عليهم السلام) is itself a proof, so that whoever holds to it is spared the search for further evidence and witness.

The strongest thing relied upon by those who reject the sale of fruit for years is a single undetailed hadith, which they claimed to narrate from the Prophet (ص), that he forbade "the sale of years," without mentioning fruit or anything else. This hadith, if it is established, is a general and undetailed report, and the interpretation of the Imams (عليهم السلام) makes clear that its intent is not what the general public suppose. If it is general, they are bound to rule by it in everything that falls under its scope, and it admits of many meanings. Among them is that in the speech of the Arabs "years" refers to times of hardship and affliction; they say, "the years have come upon us," meaning adversities and trials, and they call a year of drought and hardship "a year." On this reading it would follow that what is sold in times of hardship is forbidden, so that every sale transacted during them is void. It is also possible that, in the figurative usage of the language, "the sale of years" means selling by the year and for years, which again admits of many meanings. Following what has come explained from the Imams (عليهم السلام) is more worthy of acceptance, above all where the foundational principles bear witness to it and the derived rulings branch out from it.

Whoever sells fruit whose goodness has not yet appeared, and the buyer accepts it, and neither cancels the sale but both proceed with it, the sale is valid and not forbidden, notwithstanding what has been narrated of its prohibition. For Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: The prohibition of the Messenger of Allah (ص) against selling fruit before its goodness appeared was only because people used to buy it while it might still perish, since it was liable to blight before it ripened and became sound, and they used to bring their disputes over this to the Messenger of Allah (ص). When such disputes grew many, he forbade them to sell until the goodness became clear; he did not make it unlawful for them. He said: Whoever sells his fruit and reserves from it a known portion, that sale is valid; and whoever sells while reserving an unknown portion, that sale is not valid, because the contract fell upon something unknown. And he said: If the buyer sells the fruit before he harvests it, that sale is valid.

The Messenger of Allah (ص) forbade al-muzabanah, which is the sale of dates upon the heads of the palms for a measured quantity of dates, and of grapes on the vine for raisins by measure. He said it is not permissible to sell standing grain before it may be harvested in the customary manner, when it is sold for wheat. But if it is sold on the condition that it be left until it is reaped, and the buyer then harvests it or leaves it until it stands full, there is nothing wrong with it. So too it is permissible to sell the harvest of a crop, and green fodder, when that is known, by purchasing it as a cutting or several cuttings from what regrows, the root remaining the seller's once the cuttings are done. The Messenger of Allah (ص) said: Whoever buys a palm tree that already bears fruit which has appeared, its fruit belongs to the seller, unless the buyer stipulates that it is his. Under this ruling falls the sale of trees bearing fruit that has reached the stage the fruit of palms reaches at the time of pollination: if the buyer does not stipulate it, it belongs to the seller.

Mention of the Prohibition Against Cheating and Deception

The Imams from the Ahl al-Bayt of the Messenger of Allah narrated that he said, "Whoever cheats us is not one of us" (من غشنا فليس منا), and that he forbade cheating, treachery, deception, and beguiling in trade, and forbade breaking of faith. He commanded fulfillment of contracts in selling, buying, marriage, and covenant. The Messenger of Allah's words, "Whoever cheats us is not one of us," mean that such a person is not among those who follow us in our command and belong to our company; not that whoever cheats thereby leaves the religion of Islam and apostatizes as an unbeliever, for were that so he would incur what one who apostatizes from Islam incurs, namely execution. Allah Almighty said, telling of Ibrahim (ع): "Whoever follows me is of me, and whoever disobeys me, then You are indeed Forgiving and Merciful." Likewise the one who cheats has not followed the command of the Messenger of Allah (ص) so as to be counted among those who followed his command; yet if he is a Muslim, he is among those who followed him in part and disobeyed him in part of what he commanded, and his affair is referred to Allah, who forgives him if He wills and punishes him if He wills, as with other disobedient Muslims.

Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: Mixing two grades of food, or the like of it among wares, the good with the poor, when done for the sake of the sale, is a kind of cheating. He meant that if a man shows the buyer the good and conceals the inferior, this is cheating; but if both grades are shown together, or the good is hidden while the inferior is shown, that is not cheating. 'Ali (ع) used to go about the markets of Kufa warning against cheating and saying, "Show forth of the poor of what you sell what you show of its good." He forbade blowing into meat, saying, "Blowing is from the devil, so do not blow into food or drink." He permitted (ع) blowing to loosen the skin from the flesh in skinning, but forbade blowing into the flesh itself for the reason mentioned, that it is a form of cheating, since such blowing runs into the sinews and thin membranes beneath the skin so that they appear to be fat when they are not. And he forbade (ع) mixing milk with water for one who intends to sell it; as for one who mixes it to drink it himself, there is nothing upon him in that.

The Messenger of Allah (ص) forbade tatfif, which is shortchanging in measure and balance, taking in excess when receiving and giving short when handing over. Whoever gives generous weight and adds for the one he sells to, or takes less from the one he receives from, has done well; and whoever takes his due in full and gives it in full has stood by justice in it. So Allah Almighty said, ﴿وَيْلٌ لِّلْمُطَفِّفِينَ ٱلَّذِينَ إِذَا ٱكْتَالُواْ عَلَى ٱلنَّاسِ يَسْتَوْفُونَ﴾, "Woe to the mutaffifin, those who, when they take measure from people, take in full," meaning when they receive from them; ﴿وَإِذَا كَالُوهُمْ أَو وَّزَنُوهُمْ يُخْسِرُونَ﴾, "and when they give by measure or weight to them, give less," meaning when they give it out to them. This concerns only sale and exchange for a counter-value. As for a gift or a voluntary charity, whoever gives it in full has the greater reward, and if he falls short there is nothing upon him; but if it is an obligation, he must render it in full. The deception here lies in adding to the measure when taking and diminishing it when giving, which is the shortchanging that Allah Almighty forbade and warned against in His Book.

They (عليهم السلام) forbade buying and selling with debased dinars and dirhams, and 'Ali (ع) commanded that none of such coin should enter his treasury. This applies to what is debased in a hidden way, scarcely recognized as poor except by the experts, so that its poor quality is lost upon others and works to their loss. But as for coin whose condition is well known and hardly concealed from anyone, taken at its own rate, or a recognized mint whose pieces differ from one another in value, there is nothing wrong in buying and selling with it, nor with copper coin that is pure copper, nor with anything else that is known and not obscure. It is narrated from Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) that he forbade differing measures and weights within a single town, because of the doubt and confusion that this brings.

The Messenger of Allah (ص) also forbade tasriyah, which is that one who wishes to sell a ewe or a milk-camel leaves it unmilked for a day or two so that the milk gathers in its udder, then sells it in that state, so that the buyer, seeing the swollen udder, is drawn to it. The buyer of a musarrat is thereby given a choice for three days after he has milked it: if he wishes he may keep it, and if he wishes he may return it, returning with it a sa' of dates in place of the milk he took from it; and if he found little milk in it, he returns it and owes nothing. This choice is the choice pertaining to the animal: the musarrat may be returned even though the buyer has waived the three-day option, so long as the seller concealed the withholding of milk. But if the seller made it known before the sale and disclosed how many days he had withheld the milking, and the buyer was content with that, then the buyer has no right to return it on account of the tasriyah, unless he finds in it some other defect.

The Messenger of Allah (ص) forbade najsh in transactions, saying, "Do not turn your backs upon one another, and do not practice najsh," for najsh breeds severance, enmity, harshness, and estrangement; as though he meant that none of you should turn his back upon his brother in the manner of severance. Najsh is that one who has no wish to buy comes up to a commodity and raises its price, not desiring to purchase it, but so that another may hear him and raise his own bid above it, intending thereby to inflate the price for the seller. This is a form of deception, because the one who bids up is taken as a model and others raise their bids above his, and so it was forbidden.

The Messenger of Allah (ص) forbade a townsman to sell on behalf of a Bedouin. This means that a Bedouin brings a commodity and hands it to a townsman to sell it for him, and the townsman sells it without letting it be offered openly in the market; the sense of the prohibition is plain. Either the townsman is to sell it for him as instructed, or he is to cry it and make known the price it stands at, then inform the owner of what was offered, so that the owner may order it sold or come and sell it himself. This last case carries no prohibition, for a command over an act that belongs to the one who commands it is attributed to him as his own deed. As they say that the Messenger of Allah (ص) cut off the hand of a thief, and stoned a married adulterer, and put to death those upon whom death was due, when in truth all of this was carried out by his command. So too they say that the commander killed so-and-so or struck so-and-so, when another performed the act by his order. Thus, when the Bedouin instructs the townsman to sell his goods, it is the Bedouin who has sold them. But if the townsman, or anyone else, sells them contrary to what he was ordered, without the owner's leave, the sale is not valid unless the owner of the commodity authorizes it, and this is what the prohibition addressed.

The Prophet (ص) forbade the practice of going out to meet the caravans, that is, intercepting the traders and travelers as they approach with their goods before they reach the city or the marketplace where they intend to sell. This was forbidden out of concern that the arriving sellers would be wronged, and out of concern for the townspeople who were unable to go out to that place to buy from them. It is also a form of deception, because the one who rides out to meet the caravan knows the prevailing prices and market values of which the arriving seller is ignorant. Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) fixed the limit at this: whoever goes out to receive goods at a distance of a day's ride or less, and no more than that, does not fall under the prohibition; beyond that distance, he must not go out to meet them. And whenever goods are dressed up so that their price is inflated with no benefit to the buyer, that is fraud and is forbidden.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about a man who buys food and then sells the same measure of it in return. He said: "If it involves no increase, there is no harm in it; but if it is used to cheat the Muslims, there is no good in it, and it is not sound." He was also asked about a man who buys food by weight or by measure and finds in it a surplus beyond what he paid for. He replied: "If it is the kind of surplus by which people are not defrauded, of the sort that may reasonably vary between two measurings, there is no harm in it. But if the surplus is gross, it is a mistake and must be returned to the seller, unless the seller is content to let him keep it."

From Ali (ع) it is related that he permitted the buyer to give the seller more than his due, an extra measure or an added sum, after the buyer has received his full right, this being a voluntary gift from the buyer. If the seller is glad to accept it, let him take it, and it is wholesome for the buyer; but if the seller is not glad of it, the buyer must not compel him to it. If, however, this increase is stipulated as a condition within the purchase contract and its amount is unknown, the sale is void; if the amount is known, the sale is not void, and the known increase enters into the contract of measure and weight. Justice in all of this is what is true, neither in excess nor in deficiency, which is the fulfillment that Allah, mighty and majestic, commanded, where He, the most truthful of speakers, says: ﴿أَوْفُوا الْكَيْلَ وَلَا تَكُونُوا مِنَ الْمُخْسِرِينَ * وَزِنُوا بِالْقِسْطَاسِ الْمُسْتَقِيمِ * وَلَا تَبْخَسُوا النَّاسَ أَشْيَاءَهُمْ وَلَا تَعْثَوْا فِي الْأَرْضِ مُفْسِدِينَ﴾ (al-Shu'ara 181 to 183). Fulfillment is justice and truth, with neither increase nor decrease. Underpaying (al-bakhs) is shortfall, and giving short measure (al-tatfif) is a false taking, as Allah, mighty and majestic, the most truthful of speakers, said: ﴿وَيْلٌ لِلْمُطَفِّفِينَ * الَّذِينَ إِذَا اكْتَالُوا عَلَى النَّاسِ يَسْتَوْفُونَ * وَإِذَا كَالُوهُمْ أَوْ وَزَنُوهُمْ يُخْسِرُونَ﴾ (al-Mutaffifin 1 to 3).

### What He Forbade Regarding Sales

The Imams of the household of the Messenger of Allah (ع) narrated that the Prophet (ص) forbade combining two conditions in a single sale. That is, a man saying, "I sell you this commodity for cash at one price and on credit at another, the choice being mine," so that if the buyer pays the lesser sum in cash, or the greater on deferred terms, he may. A sale contracted on this basis is not permissible, because it is left undetermined. Of the same kind is selling something for dinars on the condition that the buyer hand over dirhams in their place at a stated rate; or selling on the condition that the other party sell something back in a guaranteed exchange, whether at once or later; or selling for a fixed term with the price rising if the term is extended. All of this and its like is two conditions in a single sale, and it is a corrupt contract when the two parties conclude it and part upon it. If, however, they merely negotiated between themselves and then agreed afterward upon one of the two sales, annulling the second, and parted on that basis, it is permissible, and the sale is whatever they settled upon.

They also narrated that the Prophet (ص) forbade profiting from what one does not yet guarantee. This is something measured or weighed that a seller is owed by a debtor, or a provision due to come to him, which he sells before he has taken possession of it. Of this kind is work that a craftsman accepts for a wage and then passes on to another for less than he agreed to do it for, pocketing the difference, when he neither labored in it nor bore its liability. But if he improved something in it or did part of the work himself, the surplus is lawful to him. Of this kind, too, is renting a dwelling and then subletting it for more than one paid. They permitted a man to sublet part of it for the like of what he paid and to live in the rest; and if he tended the dwelling in some measure, or maintained it, or saw to some of its affairs, the surplus is lawful to him. It is like a caravanserai (khan) that a man rents, then opens, curtains, sweeps, and looks after, renting out its rooms and making a profit thereby: there is no harm in that surplus, because of the labor he put into it.

They narrated that the Messenger of Allah (ص) forbade a sale combined with a loan. This is a man saying to another, "I sell you such and such on condition that you lend me such and such," or "Lend me a loan on the understanding that I owe you so much." If the sale is contracted on this basis, it is void, and the borrower owes only what was lent to him; the sale is nullified. But if the loan carries no condition within the sale contract, the sale is not spoiled.

They narrated that the Prophet (ص) forbade selling what one does not possess, such as a man bargaining over something he does not own. If he contracts the sale, the thing is his to deliver, and the buyer retains the option once he sees it. Of this kind is selling a guaranteed item for delivery at a time when no such thing exists, like grapes or fruit in a season when there are none. This is the prohibition of al-kali' bi'l-kali', that is, debt for debt, which is not permissible. An example is a man who advances a sum of dinars to another for a specified quantity of food to be delivered at a set time; when the time comes and the food is not to be found, he buys it from someone who owes him a debt payable at a later date, and hands over that food on condition the price too be deferred. This is not permissible, for it is a debt within a debt. Likewise, a man is owed a debt that has fallen due, and the two agree to extend the term in exchange for increasing the debt; this is not lawful. But if the debtor is allowed to pay it off early, before its term, there is no harm in that, for if a man forgoes a claim for his fellow's sake, he has done well. They said: part of al-kali' bi'l-kali' is that a man owed a debt takes labor from the debtor in settlement of it by mutual agreement; that is void, and the worker is due the wage of his like if either of them acted upon it, returning the surplus or taking what is still owed him. If the debt had a set term and is paid off in part before it falls due, there is no harm in that. Of this kind, too, is a man's renting a mount and then transferring it onto another; this is not permissible. And if a debt is owed to a group who divide it on the condition that each collect only the portion that falls to him, this is not permissible unless each takes only what is genuinely between them; whatever any one of them collects is shared among them. All of this, and whatever carries the same sense of debt for debt, falls under al-kali' bi'l-kali'. But if two men each owe the other a debt of food or the like whose amount is unknown, and they mutually agree that each release his fellow from what he owes in return for his own claim, there is no harm in that, because it is a settlement over what is unknown and undetermined, and it is not of the category of debt for debt.

They (عليهم السلام) permitted the exchange of animals for one another, one for more than one. It is narrated that Ali (ع) sold a camel of his named Usayfir for twenty camels to be delivered at a future time, and this is permissible when the camels are described. They (عليهم السلام) forbade selling meat for a live animal. They (عليهم السلام) forbade one man's bidding over another's bid: this means that if the seller has already inclined toward a sale, then raising the offer is unlawful, but if the transaction is still open in the crier's call, or is being held out for further bids, there is no harm in it. The Messenger of Allah (ص) himself sold to the higher bidder some of what he was asked to sell. They permitted the transfer of purchased food to another before taking possession of it in the sale of fruit still on the tops of the trees, and in selling food by the seller's own measure when the buyer knows of it and accepts his word for it.

They forbade the hoarding of food, and it is narrated that the Messenger of Allah (ص) was severe and stern about it. Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: the Messenger of Allah (ص) was harsh in this matter only against Hakim ibn Hizam, a man of Quraysh of great wealth, who, when food entered Madinah, would buy the whole of it and hoard it to sell it at his own price. He said: as for a man who buys food while it is plentiful among the people, in order to hold it and trade in it, there is no harm in that, so long as the people can find what they need to buy. But if it cannot be found, he has no right to hoard it against them and let them perish; he must bring it out and sell it. They (عليهم السلام) forbade the setting of prices above the going rate: to the one who departs from the local price it is said, "Either sell at the people's price as they sell, or take your goods out of their market," so that inflation is not brought down upon them.

Ali (ع) was asked about a tyrannical ruler who seizes a man for a fine and thereby forces him to sell his property: does the seller have the right to reclaim it afterward? He said: No. The buyer did not compel him; rather he benefited him by relieving him of the harm through what he bought from him, since the buyer did not force the sale, and it was the unjust ruler who compelled him, not the buyer. The sale of one compelled is to be annulled only when he was forced and coerced into selling by the one who had power over him; that one has the right to pursue his claim against the one who compelled him. But if the tyrannical ruler drove him to sell, and he, fearing punishment if he refused, sold to a party who neither coerced him nor had been the cause of forcing the sale upon that particular buyer, then the sale stands and there is no revoking it.

They narrated that the Messenger of Allah (ص) commanded a man who had sold a house to put its price into the like of it. This was an instruction of discipline, for it has been transmitted that when dwellings are sold and their price is not placed into the like of them, that wealth does not endure. And it is confirmed that he (ص) forbade buying and selling in the mosque, during the time of the Friday prayer, and before sunrise. Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: the second call to prayer on Friday, that is, the one given before the Imam while he is upon the pulpit, is the one that forbids buying and selling until the Friday prayer has been performed.

### On Currency Exchange (al-Sarf)

Allah the Exalted said: ﴿يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَأْكُلُوا الرِّبَا أَضْعَافًا مُضَاعَفَةً وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ﴾ (Al Imran 130). The Imams of the household of the Messenger of Allah (ص) narrated that he cursed the one who consumes usury, the one who feeds it to others, the one who sells through it, the one who buys through it, its scribe, and its two witnesses. He (ع) said: the consumer of usury will be raised on the Day of Resurrection as a madman. The Commander of the Believers, Ali (ع), said that usury is among the major sins. Abu Abdullah al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about the saying of Allah, mighty and majestic, "Allah blots out usury and causes charity to grow." It was said to him, "We have seen some who deal in usury amass great wealth." He (ع) said: What blotting out could be more thorough than this: a dirham of usury, if its holder repents of it, he must return it to its owners and nothing of it remains to him; and if he keeps it and does not repent, it drives him into the Fire.

They (عليهم السلام) said that the people under covenant are to be prevented from dealing in usury and from displaying it. Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali (ع) said that the Messenger of Allah (ص) accepted the jizya from the covenanted peoples only after stipulating upon them that they neither consume usury nor deal in it, nor eat the flesh of swine, nor marry their sisters, nor the daughters of their brothers, nor the daughters of their sisters. Whoever among them does any of this today has no covenant. And He who is exalted above all speakers said: ﴿فَبِظُلْمٍ مِنَ الَّذِينَ هَادُوا حَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ طَيِّبَاتٍ أُحِلَّتْ لَهُمْ وَبِصَدِّهِمْ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ كَثِيرًا * وَأَخْذِهِمُ الرِّبَا وَقَدْ نُهُوا عَنْهُ وَأَكْلِهِمْ أَمْوَالَ النَّاسِ بِالْبَاطِلِ وَأَعْتَدْنَا لِلْكَافِرِينَ مِنْهُمْ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا﴾ (al-Nisa 160 to 161). Usury is thus forbidden to them in their own law, even though they permit it to themselves.

Usury (al-riba) in the language means increase. It is said, "the wealth grows (raba al-mal yarbu)," meaning it increases, and its owner "makes it grow" when he adds to it. From this, raised ground is called rabwa, on account of its rising above the land around it. But an increase in wealth from a lawful direction, even though it runs in this same channel of increase, does not fall under the meaning of the forbidden usury. The forbidden usury, in what the Imams narrated from the Messenger of Allah (ص), is the sale of what is measured or weighed at unequal amounts; that is not permissible except like for like, hand to hand, with no increase and no decrease, neither in the item itself nor in anything else exchanged between the two parties.

It is related from the Messenger of Allah (ص) that he said: gold for gold, silver for silver, hand to hand and weight for weight; whoever gives more or takes more has dealt in usury. Wheat for wheat, barley for barley, flour for flour, dates for dates, raisins for raisins, sorghum for sorghum, measure for measure, and rice for rice, measure for measure; whoever gives more or takes more has dealt in usury. Muhammad ibn Ali (ع) said: copper coins (fulus) do not enter into gold for gold and silver for silver, nor does anything else that would be added over the weight. Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: no increase is permitted in that, even if one of the two is of finer quality than the other. That is, if one sells gold for gold, or silver for silver, or anything measured or weighed for its like, it is permissible only weight for weight if it is among what is weighed, or measure for measure if it is among what is measured. If a man buys pure gold of the finest sort with gold of the lowest, this is permissible only weight for weight, so long as the name of gold still applies to it. This holds until it becomes pure copper, copper predominant in it and reckoned as copper, so that the name of gold no longer applies to it; then he may buy it with gold as one buys copper, at unequal amounts. Silver for silver is the same in this sense, and likewise everything that is measured or weighed, however great the difference in quality between the two.

When like is exchanged for like, no increase is permissible, even though one variety may be finer than another. That is, if gold is sold for gold, or silver for silver, or anything that is measured or weighed is sold for its own kind, it is not lawful except weight for weight where the thing is weighed, or measure for measure where it is measured. If a man buys pure gold, ذهب إبريز of the finest quality, with gold of a lower grade, that too is not lawful except weight for weight, so long as the name "gold" still applies to it, until it becomes pure copper, نحاس, dominated by copper and ascribed to it, so that the name "gold" no longer falls upon it. At that point it may be bought with gold as copper is bought, at a differential. Silver for silver follows the same principle, as does everything measured or weighed, however greatly the two varieties may differ in worth.

Concerning goods in which gold or silver is joined to something else, al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: with a decorated sword, a ring, and their like, where one metal is set together with other material, it is more agreeable to me that he sell it for what differs from it, gold for silver or silver for gold, unless it is known that the price exceeds the metal's worth, in which case the gold is set against gold and the silver against silver, weight for weight, while the remainder of the price stands against the sword, the gemstone, or whatever else accompanies it.

He also said that it is permissible to take dirhams in place of dinars and dinars in place of dirhams. He meant that when a man is owed dinars, whether as a loan advanced, or from a sale, or from any rightful claim, he may receive dirhams for them at their value or at whatever the two agree upon; and likewise if he is owed dirhams he may receive dinars for them. He said further that exchange, الصرف, is not lawful except hand to hand. If you buy dinars for dirhams, or dirhams for dinars, or gold for silver, or silver for gold, or any part of one for the other, do not part from him until you have taken delivery. If he says, "Send a messenger and he will settle it for you," do not do so. Rather say, "Give and take," or remain with him and do not leave him. If you send your messenger with him, instruct him to be the one who concludes the exchange at the moment of mutual delivery. If he parts from you before you have taken delivery or he has, the exchange between you is void. And he said that whoever is repaid in dirhams or dinars better than what he lent has done nothing wrong, provided it is by the free choice of the borrower and was not stipulated upon him as a condition of the loan.

As for the sale of foodstuffs one for another, all the pure Imams of the household of the Messenger of Allah (ص) narrated that he forbade a differential in what is measured or weighed when the two are of one kind. Whoever buys something of a kind against its own kind may not take a differential; but when the two kinds differ, a differential is permitted between them, just as it is permitted between silver and gold, or brass and iron, and the like, whose kinds differ, when they belong to what is weighed or measured. So al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: it is not lawful to sell a sa' of almonds for two sa's of almonds, nor a sa' of dates for two sa's of dates; but it is lawful to sell a sa' of wheat for two sa's of dates, and a sa' of dates for two sa's of raisins. Grains, foodstuffs, and dried fruits run upon a single course: when the two kinds differ there is no harm in a differential, hand to hand, though it is not sound on deferred terms. He said: barley and wheat are one kind, and a differential between them is not lawful; likewise wheat, flour, and sawiq are lawful only measure for measure. It was said to him that there is an added worth in the grain, and he answered: does it not carry a cost, meaning the milling and the other handling of it? They said yes. He said: this against that. The Messenger of Allah (ص) forbade the sale of ripe fresh dates for cured dates, because the fresh diminish in measure once they dry. He (ع) permitted the sale of garments one for another, one for more than one. And he said that the Messenger of Allah (ص) forbade selling food one for another by guesswork, without measure, yet permitted bread for flour, and vinegar for vinegar even where the kinds differ, and cane-sugar for the honey of bees, like for like, hand to hand.

As for the option retained by the two parties to a sale, the pure Imams of the household of the Messenger of Allah (ص) narrated that the two parties to a transaction keep their option until they part by mutual consent. Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said that separation here means the parting of their bodies from the place in which they concluded the sale. He said: my father (رض) bought a parcel of land, and when he had confirmed it he rose, walked a few steps, and then returned, intending by this to make the sale binding. And he said that the Messenger of Allah (ص) said, "المسلمون عند شروطهم إلا ما خالف كتاب الله عز وجل," the Muslims are held to their conditions, save a condition that contradicts the Book of Allah, the Mighty and Sublime.

Then he recounted the affair of Barirah. She was a slave to a people who had granted her a writ of emancipation, and she came to one of the wives of the Messenger of Allah (ص) seeking help with her writ. The wife said to her, "I will pay what you owe and set you free." But her masters said, "We will not sell her to one who frees her unless her wala', her bond of patronage, remains ours." This was reported to the Messenger of Allah (ص), and he said, "Buy her and free her, for الولاء لمن أعتق, patronage belongs to the one who frees." Then he addressed the people and said, "What is the matter with people who lay down conditions not found in the Book of Allah? Every condition contrary to the Book of Allah is rejected. Whoever makes a sale and stipulates within his contract a permissible condition, it is valid; but if he stipulates what is not permissible, his condition does not stand, just as the Messenger of Allah (ص) did not allow those who sold Barirah the wala' they stipulated for themselves, for he said patronage belongs to the one who frees."

People differed over a sale accompanied by a condition. Some said that whoever sells a thing and stipulates a condition in his contract, the condition is void, arguing from a report they related in general terms from the Messenger of Allah (ص), that he forbade a sale with a condition. Others said that both the sale and the condition are valid, arguing that the Messenger of Allah (ص), in one of his expeditions, bought a camel from Jabir ibn Abdullah al-Ansari and stipulated that Jabir ride it to Madinah. Still others said that the sale is valid but the condition void, arguing from the report of Barirah already mentioned. The word that has come from the People of the House (عليهم السلام) settles all of this, establishes the sound view, and makes it plain. The voiding of the condition laid down by Barirah's owners was only because they stipulated what is not permissible: the Messenger of Allah (ص) had ruled that patronage belongs to the one who frees, and they stipulated patronage for themselves rather than for the emancipator. As for the Messenger's allowing Jabir ibn Abdullah the condition of riding the camel he had sold him, that was a permissible thing, in which there is no making forbidden what is lawful nor lawful what is forbidden. And as for what they related, that he forbade a sale with a condition, if it is authentic it is a general saying whose meaning is a condition that is neither lawful nor permitted. On this the People of the House (ص) said that in a sale carrying a condition, the condition is examined: if it is of what is lawful, it holds and is binding; if it is of what is not lawful, it is neither binding nor enforced, while the sale remains valid in both cases. Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) illustrated this: if a man sells another a slave girl and stipulates in the contract that he shall not sell her, nor give her away, and that she shall not be inherited from him if he dies, then the condition that he not sell or give her away binds him, for that is his to stipulate; but the condition that she not be inherited from him does not bind, because Allah, the Mighty and Sublime, has ordained the inheritance for a man's heirs, so whoever stipulates that it is not theirs has stipulated what does not stand. This position of the People of the House (عليهم السلام) is contrary to what the generality hold, and its proof is clear.

Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said that whoever buys a thing on the terms of an option for a set period, and it perishes within that time before the buyer has exercised his choice, bears the loss upon the seller's property; the buyer must swear that he neither chose it nor was content with it, and if he does not swear it becomes binding upon him. He said that the Messenger of Allah (ص) granted the buyer of an animal an option of three days, whether or not it was stipulated, and that there is no option for the seller once the two have parted. If the buyer brings about a new circumstance within the three days, the sale binds him. When asked what such a circumstance is, he said: for instance, if the purchase is a slave girl and he touches her, kisses her, or looks upon what was forbidden to him before the purchase, and likewise if he has relations with her; and if he offers her for sale, he is made to swear that he did not offer her meaning to keep her and then return her afterward if he wishes. He said that if the buyer sells what he purchased while the option still stood, and had witnesses before the sale that he had already chosen and accepted it, the surplus of the price is his; but if he had no witnesses he swears to it, and if he will not swear, the surplus belongs to the seller. He said that whoever buys a commodity and sets out to bring the price but fails to bring it before three days pass, then the seller may, if he wishes, demand the price, or sell the thing, or hold it and rescind the sale, and the buyer has no standing over it after the three days. He said that whoever buys a thing under option for a period, spends upon it and draws income from it, then chooses to return it, must give back the income he drew and keeps what he spent. And he said that if the buyer purchases a commodity on the terms that the option in it belongs to another, then the option belongs to the one for whom it was appointed.

As for the rulings upon defects, the saying of the Messenger of Allah (ص) has already been mentioned in the discussion of fraud, "من غشنا فليس منا," whoever cheats us is not one of us, together with its explanation and his prohibition of fraud. Whoever knows of a defect in what he sells and hides it has committed fraud, and it is likewise not fitting to conceal from the buyer what the seller knows, for the Messenger of Allah (ص) said, "النصيحة من الدين," sincere counsel is part of the religion. He said that it is not lawful for a Muslim to sell his brother something in which he knows there is a defect without making it plain, and it is not lawful for one who knows of it to keep it hidden. So whoever would disclaim a defect must show it to the buyer and make it plain; it does not suffice him to say, "I sell it to you free of liability," without naming the defect he disclaims, and letting the buyer see it and showing it to him. If he fails in this and merely says, "I sell it to you free of liability," and then the buyer strikes upon a defect, the buyer may hold him to it, so long as he had not seen it and accepted it.

He (ع) said that if a group buys a commodity and divides it among themselves, and one of them meets a defect in the share that came to him, he may take its diminished value from the seller. If the commodity still stands undivided, or one man bought it whole and finds a defect in part of it, he takes the value of the defect, unless that part is the very face of the commodity, in which case he may return the whole if he wishes. If the commodity is of what is weighed or measured, or is fungible, such as cotton, linen, silk, and food, and a defect is found in part of it, the seller replaces that part with its like from the same kind of the goods purchased. When the buyer finds a defect and the seller tells him, "If you wish, take it with its defect, and if you wish, return it," the buyer has nothing beyond these two: he may take or he may return; and if he seeks the value of the defect, that is not his unless the seller agrees to it. He said that whoever buys a commodity and works some change into it, or a defect arises in it while in his hands, and then he discovers an old defect that the seller did not disclaim to him, and he raises it, must pay the seller the value of what he introduced into the commodity and then return it if he wishes, or else keep it with its defect. If the seller had practiced deception upon him and hidden the defect, the buyer may return it and owes nothing for what has diminished in it; but this is established only by evidence bearing witness to the deception. Absent such evidence, the seller swears that he did not know of the defect and did not deceive. When the buyer raises the defect against the seller, the seller may put him to his oath that he did not know of the defect before the purchase or at its time, and that since knowing it he has neither used the purchase, nor drawn benefit from it, nor made it available to another. If he swears, he has either the return or the value of the defect where they agree upon it or where it is a thing in which value is due. But if he refuses the oath, or had already seen the defect, or benefited from the commodity after seeing it, or offered it for sale, then it is binding upon him and he has no claim in it.

It came from the Commander of the Believers, Ali (ع), and from Muhammad ibn Ali ibn al-Husayn and Ja'far ibn Muhammad (عليهم السلام), concerning a man who buys a slave girl, has relations with her, and then finds a defect in her, that he may not return her but may take the value of the defect from the one who sold her to him, unless he finds her to be pregnant, in which case he returns her and returns with her half of a tenth of her value. They said (عليهم السلام) that the purchased slave is returned under the guarantee, العهدة, within one year for newly arising madness, leprosy, baras, and al-qarn, should any of these arise with the buyer within that year; and likewise the calamity of death falls upon the seller. This holds for slaves in particular, apart from the rest of animals. From Ali (ع) it is related that he said the guarantee in slaves runs for a year against the gravest disease, and for three days against the calamity of death. Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said that if the seller disclaims to the buyer the year's guarantee and the three-day option in all animals at the time of concluding the sale, and the buyer accepts that disclaimer, the release binds him and he has no return upon any of it. On this basis people today write, in the deeds for purchasing slaves, the disclaimer from the year's guarantee and the three-day option, and in the deeds for other animals, the disclaimer from the three-day option. Al-Mahdi (ع), in what has reached us from him, ordered this to be written so that people would not turn back upon one another over it and prolong their quarrels. Whoever consents to it is bound by it, and whoever refuses it is not compelled. If the seller is content to leave the disclaimer unmentioned, he is not compelled to it, and there falls upon him in that matter what has already been described. Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: there is no guarantee in a sale of disclaimer, nor in a sale of inheritance, neither the year's guarantee nor the three-day option.

As for the sale by murabahah, it is permissible when the purchase price and the profit are both named. But the pure Imams of the household of the Messenger of Allah (ص) disliked the murabahah sale in which the profit is made a proportion of the price, for that is a species of riba. When a man says, "I sell you this commodity at a profit of a tenth of its price," or names some like fraction, the added profit is folded into the price itself, and so it comes to resemble riba. This is a sale the merchants of Iraq trade by, saying, "I sell you this commodity at بده دوازده or at بده يازده," a Persian expression whose sense is two upon every ten, or one upon every ten. Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: goods came to my father (ع) from Egypt, so he prepared food and summoned the merchants to his house to buy them. He ordered that they be fed, and they ate; then he ordered the goods displayed before them. They said, "Shall we take it from you at بده دوازده?" He said to them, "No, but this is merchandise whose purchase cost ten thousand dinars; if you wish it for twelve thousand, take it." They said, "We have taken it," and so he made the sale binding for them. And he (ع) said that whoever buys goods and spends upon them, in such things as transport and fulling and the like, may sell them by murabahah and carry what he spent into their price, meaning that he says, "This stands me at such and such."

The pure Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt of the Messenger of Allah (ع) permitted selling by *Murabaha*, profit-disclosed sale, when the seller states plainly both what he paid to acquire the goods and the profit he seeks. What they disliked was a different practice: selling by *Murabaha* on the condition that the profit be made a fixed proportion of the price, because that resembles *riba*. For when a man says, "I will sell you this commodity for a profit of one-tenth of its price," or any similar formula, the increase is no longer merely a profit but an addition folded into the price itself, and so it comes to resemble *riba*. This is the very form of sale practiced by the merchants of Iraq, who say, "I will sell you this commodity for *dowzadeh*," or "for *yazda*," Persian words meaning "for every ten, twelve," or "for every ten, one."

Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) related that his father received goods from Egypt, prepared food, and invited the merchants to his home to buy. He ordered that they be fed, and they ate. Then he ordered the goods to be presented, and they said, "We will take them from you at *dowzadeh*." He replied, "No. Rather, I bought these goods for ten thousand dinars, and I am selling them to you for twelve thousand. If you wish, take them." They said, "We have taken them," and he made the sale binding upon them.

He (ع) said that if a man buys goods and then incurs expenses upon them, such as freight, containers, and the like, he may sell them by *Murabaha* and include what he spent within the price. That is to say, he may declare, "This stands me at such and such in total." What he must not do is claim that he bought the goods for a sum that already includes his added expenditure, for that would be a lie and would carry him to the very boundary of *riba*, unless he sets out the purchase price, the profit, and the expenditure separately, or gathers them together openly by saying, "This costs me so much in all." He must never say he bought a thing for more than he actually paid for it.

He was asked about a man who bought a single lot of merchandise, divided it into parts, and then wished to sell each part by *Murabaha* for what he had reckoned it to cost him. He said: No, unless he explains to the buyer that he apportioned the cost among the parts himself, as indeed he did. He was likewise asked about a man who bought a commodity by direct inspection and then sold it by *Murabaha* to a buyer who had not witnessed that inspection, the buyer only afterward learning of it. He said that the buyer has, if he wishes, the same benefit of that inspection that the seller had, unless the seller says to him at the moment of contract, "This day it stands me at such a price, and I sell it to you for such a price," and does not phrase it as a stated rate of profit.

He (ع) said: If a man buys a garment for a dinar and settles the payment in dirhams, or buys it for dirhams and settles the payment with a dinar, he may sell it by *Murabaha* according to what he bought it for, without mentioning the currency he actually tendered. This follows from his principle that one may discharge a debt owed in gold with silver, and one owed in silver with gold. Here the man bought with the gold or silver he bought with, the price between him and the seller was settled, and the debt thereby fell due upon him; whatever he later paid in discharge of it, being a permissible medium, does not alter that the purchase price is what the contract fixed. He must include in the stated cost whatever he lawfully spent upon the goods, and the sale is binding upon the one who transacted it.

He (ع) permitted a man who buys a slave girl and lies with her to then sell her by *Murabaha*, because that does not diminish her value and is not a thing for which compensation is owed. So too if a man buys a beast and rides it, or a slave and puts him to service, or a garment and wears it, unless doing so causes wear. To disclose such use to the buyer is the safer course for the seller in his religion, and if he does not disclose it, no liability rests upon him, as the narrations have come down.

**On *al-salam*.**

Allah, Mighty and Majestic, said: ﴿يَٰأَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ ءَامَنُواْ إِذَا تَدَايَنتُم بِدَيْنٍ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى فَٱكْتُبُوهُ﴾, "O you who believe, when you contract a debt for a specified term, write it down" (al-Baqarah: 282). The pure Imams (عليهم السلام) narrated that he (ع) said: Whoever enters a sale for an unspecified term, or for something unspecified, has made no true sale. They said (عليهم السلام) that *al-salam* has four conditions, and if any one of them is wanting or is broken, the contract is void. The first is that the item to be delivered be described by a known description. The measure must be stated, whether by volume or by weight according to its nature; the place of delivery must be named; and the term at which delivery falls due must be fixed.

They said (عليهم السلام) that a *salam* contract is not concluded except by payment of the price before the two parties part from the place where the contract was made. It has been said before that if the price is not received in the session, the transaction becomes a debt exchanged for a debt, and the Messenger of Allah (ع) forbade that.

Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) held that whoever enters a *salam* contract for the produce of a named village or a particular locality is not permitted to do so until that produce may reasonably be expected to be sound and available. If no locality is named, the *salam* is permissible, and the obligation is secured against the one who received the advance. He (ع) permitted a *salam* contract with one who does not yet possess what he has undertaken to deliver, provided the item lies within his means when the term comes; at that point he acquires it, pays for it, and delivers it. The Imams (عليهم السلام) also permitted *salam* in animals when the animal is known and precisely described.

He (ع) was asked about a man who enters a *salam* for a known item and then receives either less than it or something more; he said: if this is by the consent of both, there is no harm in it. The Imams (عليهم السلام) permitted *salam* in well-known, described garments, and they held that if the man takes back his capital, or a part of it, without adding anything, that is permissible, but if he adds anything to it, that is not allowed.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about the one who owes a *salam* obligation and pays its price to the one entitled, who then says, "Buy it for yourself and take full satisfaction of your due." He disapproved of this and said that the creditor should assign the purchase to someone else, and should not himself undertake both the buying for his own benefit and the taking of delivery from himself. He was asked about one who owes a *salam* that has fallen due but cannot find the specific item at its term, yet finds others of the same kind, such as animals or wares, and so pays their equivalent value; and this is without harm. Likewise, if a man sold food for dirhams and, when the term came, said, "I have no dirhams, but take food from me instead," there is no harm in the creditor taking food from him, for it is dirhams that are owed to him and with them he may take whatever he wishes. The Imams (عليهم السلام) regarded *salam* as reprehensible in things that do not keep, such as fresh fruit, meat, and their like.

**On the conditions and rulings governing sales.**

Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: Whoever buys a slave and finds money in his possession, that money belongs to the seller, unless the buyer stipulated it in the contract; if he did stipulate it, the seller has no claim to it, even if it exceeds the price of the slave. This is because whatever a slave holds and possesses in his hands, when he is sold, is counted as part of his price, and so this does not fall within the *riba* that is forbidden, particularly when the money is in wares, or is coin and the slave was bought with wares, or the slave was himself a thing bought with a document, or a document exchanged for a thing. In all these cases the sale is valid, there being no disagreement upon it.

The sound form by which a sale is concluded is this: one man says to another, "Sell me this slave of yours," or "this commodity of yours, for such and such," naming a lawful price, and the other says, "I have sold it to you." Or the seller begins, saying, "I have sold you this for such and such," and the buyer says, "I have bought it," or "I accept this purchase." The sale is thereby concluded between them. Yet they retain the option to withdraw until they part from the place of contract with their bodies, each passing out of the other's sight. Whichever of them wishes to retract before they part may do so, and the sale is undone. The sale is completed only by their separation, as has been said.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said concerning two parties to a sale where the buyer claims he paid the price while the goods are still in his hand, and the seller denies receiving anything: if the transaction is of a kind that people commonly call witnesses to record, the buyer's claim of payment is not accepted without proof, and the seller must swear that he did not receive the price and obtained it in no way from him. But if it is of a kind not customarily witnessed, then the word is the word of the one in whose hand the thing lies, together with his oath.

He (ع) said: Whoever makes a sale and then claims he erred in it, that he did not know the price at the time of sale or meant a different sum, his claim is examined against the value. If the difference between what he sold it for and its true value falls within the range in which people commonly under-reckon one another in such dealings, the sale stands and his claim is set aside. But if the difference is great, beyond what people ordinarily overlook, the thing is returned to him, and the buyer may put him to his oath that he erred as he claims.

From Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn 'Ali (ع): he was asked about a man who appointed an agent to sell his goods, and the agent claimed to have sold them, but the owner rose to undo the sale and reclaim the goods, alleging the agent had sold at an underprice. He said: the matter is examined. If the value on the day it was sold equals the price it fetched, or exceeds it within the margin by which people commonly under-reckon one another, the sale stands and holds good. But if the value exceeds the price beyond what people ordinarily accept, the sale is returned. Then, if the buyer wishes to bind by oath the owner of the goods who stands to reclaim them, that he did not consent to his agent's sale and did not ratify it after learning of this loss, he has that right; and if the owner will not swear, the buyer has no recourse against him.

Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn 'Ali (ع) was asked about a man who appointed two agents together on the condition that they sell a thing for him, and one of them sold it without the other: the sale is not valid, unless the man had granted that either one, acting alone, might do it, along with their acting jointly.

Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about a man negotiating over a slave girl, whose owner sold her to him saying, "I have sold her to you for whatever price you please." The buyer accepted the sale and then tendered a price the seller was not content with and thought too little. He said: her value on the day of sale is examined; if the price offered is less than her value, the buyer must add what falls short of it, for even where a man has committed the price to another's judgment, it is not lawful for that other to judge against him unjustly.

He (ع) said: Whatever a judge or a ruler sells in discharge of an obligatory debt, or on behalf of a minor, or in a case resembling these, no warranty or liability rests upon the one who was ordered to carry out the sale; the burden lies upon the estate of the one on whose behalf it is sold, that is, upon his liability, even if he has no wealth. And he said: Whoever trades with an orphan's wealth, whether an executor, a guardian, or another, if he makes a profit the profit belongs to the orphan, and if he suffers a loss the loss falls upon the one who traded, who must restore it to the orphan's wealth, because he transgressed in it.

He (ع) said concerning a slave whose master had given him money, with which the slave bought and freed himself, the master learning of it afterward: the master takes back whatever money was taken, and the slave remains his according to the case, this being where the money had been earned by the slave and had come to him without his master's leave. But if the master had released that money to him, or given him something, or it had reached him from a rightful source, and he then bought himself with it, that is valid. A slave may also enter a written manumission contract, paying from his earnings what is set upon him; and when he has paid what he was contracted for, he is freed.

It was narrated from the Imams (عليهم السلام) that if a man dies leaving no heir but a slave he owns, the slave is bought out of the estate, that is, if his master consents to sell him, and the remainder of the estate is given to him as inheritance. If his master will not sell him, he does not inherit, and the inheritance passes to the public treasury, there being no other heir.

He (ع) said of selling a house for a house, a share for a share, a quarter for a quarter, and selling part of such things one for another, that all of it is permissible when the parties know what they are trading.

From the Commander of the Faithful, 'Ali (ع): he gave judgment concerning two men who bought a commodity from a man. After concluding the sale they went off to fetch the price; then one of them came and 'Ali (ع) said he might take the commodity from the seller, and that if the second man then came and paid to the one who had taken it half the price, he became his partner in it.

It was also narrated that certain people used to entrust their affairs to a woman of the Quraysh who managed a great deal of money. Two men came to her with a sum and deposited it all with her together, saying, "Do not release it to either one of us without the other." Then one of them came to her with a token that had been agreed between him and his companion, told her of it, and said, "He has sent me to receive the money, and this is the sign between him and you." So she handed him the money and he went off with it. Afterward the other man came and said, "The money." She replied, "Your companion has already taken it; he came with your message and with such a sign that had been agreed between me and you." He said, "I sent nothing to you, and I gave him no token." The matter was brought before 'Umar in his day, and he gave judgment against her for the money. She came to 'Ali (ع) seeking his aid, and he said to 'Umar, "Did the two not tell her not to release the money to either one of them without the other?" 'Umar said yes. 'Ali (ع) said, "Then this man has no right to hold her liable until he brings his companion with him." 'Umar said to the man, "So it is. Bring your companion," and he set the woman free, and the trick worked against her came to nothing.

The Marketplace: Honest Earning, Trade, and Property

From Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) it is related that an emissary of the Messenger of Allah (ص) came from Yemen. When the party reached al-Jahfa their provisions gave out, so they sold a slave girl from among the captives, a girl who had her mother with her, and they brought the mother forward for sale. When word of this reached the Messenger of Allah (ص) he disapproved of it and ordered that she be returned, saying: "Do not separate kindred by sale" (لا تفرقوا بين ذوي الأرحام بالبيع). Thus the first law of the marketplace is that gain may never be purchased at the price of a bond of blood.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) also spoke of a man who buys a commodity from another and then wishes to return it, seeking its rescission, but the seller refuses to take it back. So the buyer remits to the seller part of the price in order to induce him to accept the return. The Imam ruled that the buyer may take back from the seller whatever the seller had remitted to him, if the seller has indeed granted the rescission, for a true rescission restores the sale as it was. If, however, the seller purchases some other commodity from the buyer, distinct from what he had first sold him, and for less than that first sale, this is permissible as a fresh transaction. But rescission itself (الإقالة) is never effected by reducing anything from the price.

On Debts

The pure Imams from the household of the Messenger of Allah (ص) have narrated that he said: Allah the Almighty is with the debtor until he discharges his debt, so long as he intends to repay it and the debt was not incurred in something Allah hates. And he said: Whoever frees a captive, or relieves one burdened by debt, and grants respite to a man in hardship or remits his debt for him, shall stand beneath the shade of the Throne on a Day when there is no shade but His shade.

He said: "Whoever grants a loan, for him is the reward of charity." Then afterward he said: "Whoever grants a loan, for him is the reward of charity every day." So it was asked of him, O Messenger of Allah (ص), "You said before that whoever grants a loan has the reward of charity, and now you say every day?" He answered: "If a man grants a loan to a stated term, he is as one who has given it in charity; and once its term has come and he still grants his debtor respite, it is for him the reward of charity every day until he collects it."

A man under a contract of manumission once stood before 'Ali (ع) asking his help, and 'Ali found at that moment nothing to give him. He said to the man, "Shall I direct you to something better than that?" The man said, "Yes, what is it?" 'Ali (ع) said, "Something I heard the Messenger of Allah (ص) say: if there were owed against you the like of Sabr in debt, Allah the Almighty would settle it for you." And Sabr is a mountain in Yemen. The man asked, "And what is it that I am to do?" 'Ali (ع) said, "Say: اللهم عفني بحلالك عن حرامك وأعنّي بفضلك عن فضل من سواك: O Allah, suffice me by what You have made lawful so that I have no need of what You have made unlawful, and enrich me by Your grace so that I need not the grace of any besides You."

The Messenger of Allah (ص) forbade a loan that draws a benefit to the lender (سلف يجرّ منفعة). 'Ali (ع) said: let none of you take the use of a beast to ride, nor the loan of goods, from a man to whom he has advanced a loan. Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) likewise disapproved that a creditor should lodge with his debtor, or eat his food, or drink his drink, or feed from his fodder. Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn 'Ali (ع) said: every loan that draws a benefit is usury (كل قرض جرّ منفعة فهو ربا). This holds when the benefit arises from a stipulation or from an intention, as in the case of the man who lodges with his debtor and eats his food and feeds from his fodder, for perhaps he does so only on account of his loan. But if the benefit is freely given, a customary courtesy that already passes between the two of them and that they extend to one another apart from the loan, there is no harm in it. It has also come from them (عليهم السلام) that whoever advances dirhams or dinars and is repaid with better coin in cash, there is no harm in that, provided he did not stipulate it beforehand.

Abu 'Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about a man whose regular customer or business associate comes to him and asks a loan, and he lends to him because of the benefit he draws from dealing with him, from buying and selling with him. He was asked, too, about a people who have a broker who sells their crops for them, and who advances them loans until he has sold on their behalf and recovers what he lent, for otherwise he would not lend them anything at all. He permitted this where the arrangement is well known and established between them, while disapproving of any loan by which the lender contrives to draw a benefit, on account of the Prophet's prohibition (ص) of a loan that draws a benefit. And it is related of 'Ali (ع), al-Husayn ibn 'Ali (ع), Muhammad ibn 'Ali ibn al-Husayn (ع), and Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) that they all permitted bills of transfer (السفاتج): money that a man borrows in one land on the understanding that he, or another on his behalf, will repay it in another land. They saw no harm in this, since it is but a loan and a debt.

It is related from them (عليهم السلام) that dealing on credit is permitted, so long as the contract is properly framed. If a man owes another a debt already fallen due, and says to his creditor, "I have nothing with which to repay you, but sell me some goods on a deferred term, that I may sell them and repay you from the proceeds," and the creditor does so, there is no harm in it. But if instead he engages him only in words, in a form that is not lawful, saying, "Lend me a hundred dinars for two hundred," or any like increase set to a named term, and then dresses this up as a valid sale whose price is raised by just that increase and set to that term, such haggling is reprehensible. To leave aside all mention of the increase and to turn instead to an honest sale is the safer course, and a sale contract soundly concluded after such negotiation is permissible. This is like a man and a woman who first discuss between them an unlawful union and then abandon it and contract instead a valid marriage: the negotiation and exchange of words about immorality is reprehensible and forbidden, yet it does not corrupt the marriage they afterward contracted, nor anything that flows from it. Rather all is well in their turning back to what is lawful, with sincere repentance from what passed, if Allah wills.

Abu 'Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about a man who says to another, "Buy me such-and-such goods for such a price, and I will buy them from you after a stated term at such an increase." He said: there is no harm in this, but only that the man buy the goods after he himself has come to own them. He also permitted that a man owed a debt to a fixed term, who then has need of it before its due date, may forgo part of it in exchange for its being paid early, or may ask that a portion be paid now against an extension of the remaining term, provided he adds nothing above his original capital.

On Transfer of Debt and Suretyship

Transfer of debt (الحوالة) is when a man is owed a debt by another and transfers his claim onto a third. Suretyship (الكفالة) is the bearing of a burden on another's behalf; the one who bears it is called guarantor, warrant, surety, pledge, and undertaker, and whoever takes on such a burden is bound to discharge what he has undertaken. The Messenger of Allah (ص) said: "The undertaker is liable" (الزعيم غارم), and the undertaker, as we have said, is the one who bears the burden. So it is in the words of Scripture, when they said, "We are missing the goblet of the king; and for whoever brings it there is a camel's load, and I am guarantor for it" (وأنا به زعيم) (Yusuf: 71–72), that is, a bearer of the burden. And both Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn 'Ali and Abu 'Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (عليهم السلام) said: I am a guarantor.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: when a man transfers onto another the debt owed him, the creditor may pursue whichever of the two he wishes, the original debtor or the one to whom he has been referred, unless the creditor released the original debtor. If he released him at the moment of the transfer, then he may pursue none but the one to whom he was referred, and he has no claim upon the original debtor whom he released. And when two men jointly stand surety for a third, the one to whom the surety is owed may take from whichever of them he wishes, unless the two stipulated that the suretyship is divided between them in halves. If they made no such stipulation and each gave an unqualified surety, he may take the whole from whichever of them he can reach. If it was stipulated in the contract of surety that each is guarantor for his fellow, he may take from whichever he wishes, and the one from whom he takes may recover half of what he paid from his co-guarantor. If one surety is added after another, they are all guarantors together, and he may take from whichever he wishes, unless he has released one.

When a guarantor stands surety for money, or for whatever he has guaranteed, he must discharge it, unless the one to whom it is owed releases him; and it does not suffice him merely to produce the person guaranteed. But if the surety was for the person, and he produces that person to the one for whom he guaranteed him, upon the agreed condition, and disavows himself of him thereby, then he is quit and nothing lies against him. If he bore the burden of the person absolutely, so that whenever he is asked to produce him he must produce him, that obligation rests on him whenever he is called upon. If the suretyship of the person is set to a term, the one to whom it is owed has no recourse to the surety until the term expires; and if the surety then fails to produce the person, he is liable to pay what falls due, unless the person has died. If he has died, and the surety had only guaranteed his person, nothing lies against the surety. If the person is absent and the surety says, "I will pay what he owes," and what he owes is known, nothing more than that lies against him. But if the suretyship concerns a matter in which the person guaranteed must be produced, for an oath that has fallen due, or a formal detention, or the like, then he must be produced, unless he dies or the one to whom the surety is owed releases the guarantor.

And he (ع) said: there is no suretyship in a prescribed penalty (لا كفالة في حدّ). Whoever incurs a legal penalty, whether execution, retaliation, killing, or amputation, no surety may be taken from him for it; rather the penalty is exacted from his own person. If witnesses testify to such a matter and it becomes necessary to hold him until the testimony is confirmed, he is imprisoned, and no surety is accepted for him, because were he set at liberty and to flee, the penalty could not be exacted from the surety. Nor is a slave bound to stand as surety, even if he has been given leave to trade, unless his master gives him leave for that as well.

On Interdiction, the Spendthrift, and Bankruptcy

Interdiction (الحجر) is restraint. Allah the Almighty has said, "And test the orphans" (al-Nisa': 6), that is, try them, "until, when they reach the age of marriage" (al-Nisa': 6), that is, maturity, "then, if you perceive in them sound judgment, hand over to them their wealth" (al-Nisa': 6). And the Messenger of Allah (ص) forbade the squandering of wealth, and Allah the Almighty has said, "And do not squander wastefully; indeed the squanderers are the brethren of the devils." The child is therefore restrained from his wealth by the ruling of the Book until he comes of age and sound management of his affairs is recognized in him, whereupon his wealth is delivered to him. That restraint was on account of folly and prodigality; so if he comes of age and is yet foolish and prodigal, he remains under interdiction; and if such conduct arises in him after he is grown, interdiction is imposed upon him.

'Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع) imposed interdiction upon 'Abdullah ibn Ja'far ibn Abi Talib when his squandering of his wealth was established, and he brought the matter before 'Uthman in the days of his rule and instructed him concerning it. 'Uthman held back. Then, some while afterward, 'Abdullah bought a salt marsh for sixty thousand, and 'Uthman said, "By Allah, it would not please me that it were mine, even for the worth of these sandals of mine." So 'Ali (ع) came to him and said, "Will you not place your nephew under interdiction?"

The insane man, likewise, is placed under interdiction. Whoever is placed under interdiction has his maintenance met from his own wealth in a fitting measure, and one is appointed to guard his wealth and to spend upon him from it. When the foolish man mends, and the prodigal becomes careful, and the madman recovers his reason, and those whose testimony is admissible bear witness to it, the interdiction is lifted from them; and if they return to their former state, it is reimposed. Whatever dispositions of their wealth they made while under interdiction are void.

As for bankruptcy: a man who owes a debt and becomes insolvent, finding nothing with which to pay, having nothing to sell to settle his debt and no wealth to render, is not imprisoned once this is established of his condition. But if he becomes bankrupt while owing debts to a body of creditors and holds wealth that does not suffice for their debts, what is in his hands is divided among the creditors in proportion, each taking according to the measure of his debt and each falling short in the same measure. So if a man goes bankrupt owing one man a hundred dinars and another two hundred, and only thirty dinars are found in his hands, the owner of the hundred takes ten and the owner of the two hundred takes twenty; and by that same reckoning it goes, whether the sum be more or less and the creditors few or many. If one of them finds in the bankrupt's hands the very thing he had sold him, whole or in part, he has the better right to that particular thing, before the rest of the creditors, until he recovers the whole of his debt; and if there be, after he has recovered his debt from its price, any surplus or profit, that belongs to the body of creditors.

It is not for the bankrupt to sell what is in his hands apart from the creditors until he has rendered them their due. They are to press for the best price in the sale of his assets, bidding among themselves; and when the price settles and no further increase is to be had, and they are content, and he too consents to the sale, the goods are sold and the proceeds shared out. If the bankrupt refuses to sell, or one of the creditors refuses, and they carry the dispute before the ruler, and it is attested before him that the sale has been fully pressed to its highest, he orders the sale and the distribution among the creditors if the proceeds do not meet their debts; and if the proceeds meet them and a surplus remains, it is returned to him. If a part of his goods suffices to satisfy their claims, only so much is sold as answers to the debts, and the whole is not sold against him.

The bankrupt's own acknowledgment of a debt is not accepted once the creditors have come forward, unless the one to whom he acknowledges it brings proof, or unless the creditors affirm its truth. Whatever he acknowledges while still solvent and of standing is valid. And if there is found in his hands anything upon which one of them has a wage owing to him, and that man comes forward together with the rest of the creditors, he has the better right to it until his wage is paid to him from it.

Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about a man who buys a slave and frees him, or a concubine who then bears him a child, or a thing that he gives away as charity, and who afterward falls into bankruptcy. He said: if he bought that at a fair price while he was solvent, and his insolvency had not yet been established, and only later did he suffer loss and fall bankrupt, then the manumission and the charity stand. The seller is treated as one creditor among the man's creditors with respect to whatever remains in his hands. But if the man was poor and had nothing, then neither his manumission nor his charity is valid, for he was giving away what was not his to give, and it is returned. Concerning this it has come from the People of the House (عليهم السلام) that the Commander of the Faithful (ع) held that the umm al-walad, the mother of a child, may be sold for the price of her own freedom, meaning that if a man buys her and she bears him a child, and he has nothing else, she is his only recourse. If he does have means, the seller takes his share along with the other creditors, and what remains of the price stays as an obligation upon the one who manumitted, to be sought from him whenever he becomes able, and he must discharge it. Whichever of the two, the freed man or the one who freed, is the more able is the one who may be pursued; if the freed man himself comes into ease, he has no claim to go back upon his umm al-walad or upon the one who freed him for anything, and he is asked instead for whatever remains upon him from his own person, if he wishes. If there is a surplus in the value of the slave or the umm al-walad, and the one who freed them has nothing, they are sold; if there is a surplus in the price, a portion of the slave is freed to the extent of it; and if there is a surplus in what was given as charity, that surplus passes to the one to whom the charity was given and the remainder is returned.

Whoever has a debt and owns slaves whose price would cover that debt, and who frees them, his manumission is not valid; they are sold and his debt is settled, provided he has no other property. But if a man is in debt and, while still solvent and buying and selling with his affairs upright, sells what is in his hands and settles some of his creditors and not others, before the creditors have risen against him, then what he has paid out and what he has sold in that state is valid for the one who bought from him and the one whom he paid. Were this not so, no one under debt could ever discharge a debt, or buy, or sell. He is barred from all that only once the creditors rise against him and his bankruptcy is established. The wife shares with the creditors for her dowry. When the creditors, or a single creditor, rise with a claim of debt, and the one against whom they rose acknowledges it, or proof of it is established against him, and then, once judgment has gone against him, he pleads that he has nothing and the claimant contradicts him, the ruling turns on the nature of the obligation. If it arose from a debt or from something that reached his hands, the burden falls on him to prove that he has become destitute of it and that it has left his possession. But if what fell due upon him was something that never reached his hands, such as a liability from a felony, a surety, a hawala, the maintenance of one whose maintenance is his duty, the dowry of a woman, or the like, then his plea of destitution is accepted upon his oath, and the burden of proof falls upon the holder of the right as to whatever he claims the man possesses. If he proves any of it, the man is held to it; if he cannot prove it, it remains a debt, sought from him whenever he comes into ease, and whatever is found to be in his hands is sold and his debts paid from the proceeds.

If witnesses testify that he is solvent and that the means to pay his debt lie concealed in his hands, he is imprisoned and beaten until he pays. If, when confronted, he says to his creditor, "I will settle your right and give you your due," or something of that sort, and does not declare himself bankrupt, he is held to that word and imprisoned for it until he pays. When the debtor is obstinate and has property that is apparent, the judge imprisons him over it until he sells, and beats him; and if he persists in his obstinacy, the judge sells on his behalf. When a man acknowledges the property, or proof of it is established against him, and he refuses to hand it over, he is imprisoned and beaten, at the demand of his adversary, until he discharges what is owed.

As to tenant farming and musaqah: tenant farming, al-muzara'ah, is a dealing over cultivation in which partners share, on the terms that each of them contributes something to it, or that a man sows in another's land with his own oxen or his own seed on the condition that he give the owner a known portion of what the land yields. Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about this and permitted it. He said that the Messenger of Allah (صل الله عليه وآله وسلم) gave the land of Khaybar to the Jews on the condition that they cultivate it and have half of what it produced, and he apportioned it with them upon that, and in it were palms and trees and whatever was sown. Tenant farming for a third, or a quarter, or whatever the two farmers agree upon, whether little or much, is valid. He (ع) said likewise that it is permissible to hire land for a known term to be cultivated, for a stated payment in cash or in kind, but he disapproved of hiring it for wheat on the condition that wheat be grown in it. He (ع) permitted tenant farming on whatever the farmers stipulated of labor, of plowing, of the work of the oxen and the like, and on whatever they agreed upon, whether the land belonged to both of them or to one of them. He disapproved of their stipulating a set share for the seed, a share for the land, and a share for the oxen; rather they should stipulate the shares for themselves, each of them having a known portion of the whole.

Musaqah is labor upon land that has trees on it, worked by a laborer on the condition that he has what it yields, according to what he stipulates. Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: if a man gives another a piece of land to plow, water, and build up, and in it are pomegranates, palms, fruit, herbs, or the like, on the condition that the laborer has half of what it produces, or whatever the two agree upon, that is valid. Likewise if he gives him his land to build up and to sow. If the man then sows it and claims that he leased it, while the owner denies the lease, the word is the owner's upon his oath, unless it was known at the time of sowing that the sower had leave, in which case the sower swears and the rent of the like of that land is due from him. Unless he brings some claim that does not fit, in which case what is due from him is the rent of the like of the land; and the same holds if the owner of the land had no knowledge of it and had no proof, and swore that the man had not leased it from him on the terms he claimed, so that the sower is charged the rent of the like of the land. The owner has no right to tear up the crop. If the sower plowed it as a usurper and a transgressor, and this is established against him, and he did not claim that he had leased it, or if he had made that claim and then withheld from the oath after it was returned to him, and the owner's own transgression was not established, then the owner is given the choice either to take from him the rent of the like of the land, or to pay him his outlay and the cost of his sowing and take the crop himself; but he has no right to tear it up, because tearing up a standing crop is an act of corruption. He (ع) said, concerning a man who plows land and whom another man asks to be made his partner in what he has plowed, on the condition that he pay half the seed and half the cost, and the two agree upon that, that it is valid. He (ع) said there is no harm in a man giving his ruined land to one who will build it up for a known term on the condition that the one who develops it has all that it yields; when the term they agreed upon has passed, the land returns, developed, to its owner. Likewise, if there is a land tax upon it and a man takes it on together with its tax, there is no harm in that. He said: whoever leases land in order to sow it and then leaves it unsown, without the owner preventing him or coming between him and it, owes its rent for the term for which he leased it, whether he chose to plow it or to leave it, unless he had stipulated that no rent is due from him except when he plows, so that the choice to plow or to hold back is left to him and the condition is sealed upon that; if he seals it thus, it is according to what they contracted. He (ع) was asked about the people of a village who owed a tribute, upon whom the ruler pressed unjustly, so they assigned it to a man on the condition that he would relieve them of whatever was exacted from them, little or much, and he relieved them, and afterward a surplus remained. He said: whatever they agreed upon is valid, so long as they consent to it, and the one who bore that burden from them has whatever surplus remains.

As to rents, al-ijarat: the Imams of the household of the Messenger of Allah (عليهم السلام) narrated that there was found in the sheath of a sword a writing in which was the curse of Allah, and of the angels, the prophets, and the messengers, and the curse of Muhammad the Prophet (صل الله عليه وآله وسلم), upon whoever claims as father one who is not his father, or attaches himself as client to other than his patrons, or wrongs a hired man of his wage, or steals the landmarks of the road. Ali, the Commander of the Faithful (ع), said: I found this writing in the sheath of the sword of the Messenger of Allah (صل الله عليه وآله وسلم), and I know it. This is a matter whose telling is long and not for this place; it is set down in the Book of the Imamate. And Allah, mighty and majestic, said: ﴿قُل لَّا أَسْأَلُكُمْ عَلَيْهِ أَجْرًا إِلَّا الْمَوَدَّةَ فِى الْقُرْبَىٰ﴾ (Al-Shura 23). In this too there is a long discourse, and it likewise belongs in the Book of the Imamate.

The sum of what has come from the Imams (عليهم السلام) is that a man's letting out of himself, and of those under his charge, and of whatever he owns of beast, tool, ship, land, dwelling, or anything else, in all its forms, is lawful, provided that the wage for it is of what is lawful and permitted and that the thing itself is of what it is lawful and permitted for him to earn by. The lease in such a case is valid when the two contracting parties conclude it for a known term, upon a matter that is understood, and upon what is due and allowed. None of that is valid if it is in something whose earning is not lawful, or in work that is not lawful to do. This is a root from which many branches issue, and whatever springs from it takes this same ruling. So one who is under another's charge cannot validly contract a lease over himself, nor over anything he does not own and has no right to. It is not lawful for one who holds what it is not lawful for him to own to let it out, as with a man who has usurped a free person, or has taken up instruments of frivolity or things whose ownership is not lawful; he is owed no wage for it, and it is not lawful for another to hire it from him. Nor is it lawful for him to let out what he does own and may lawfully own to one who will use it in what is unlawful, as with a man who lets out himself, his beast, his tool, or his ship for the making of wine, or for carrying it, or for the like of what is not lawful.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about taking a wage for teaching the Quran, and he disapproved of it. He said that a man once said to the Commander of the Faithful, Ali (ع), "I love you, O Commander of the Faithful," and he replied, "But I hold you in dislike." The man asked, "And why?" He said, "Because you take a wage for teaching the Quran, and I heard the Messenger of Allah (صل الله عليه وآله وسلم) say that whoever takes the teaching of the Quran as his livelihood, that will be his whole portion on the Day of Resurrection." Yet they (عليهم السلام) allowed a wage for teaching writing, letters, and the other things that teachers teach to children; they hired for that and gave the wage for it, and had it been forbidden or disapproved they would not have given it. What they disapproved was the wage for teaching the Quran purely and alone. So when the teacher teaches the children the Quran together with other things for which it is permissible to take a wage, there is no harm in it, and the wage falls upon what is lawful and not upon what is not lawful to be paid for. It is narrated that the Messenger of Allah (صل الله عليه وآله وسلم) married a woman to one of his companions on the condition that he teach her a surah of the Quran. The report on this is left general and does not state that he made this her dowry, and the marriage may be valid without a named dowry, as we shall mention in the matters of marriage.

Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali (ع) was asked about a man to whom the Quran is recited, and who, when the reciter completes it before him, prepares food as people do and invites his companions who recite with him, and invites the one who recited to him. He said: there is no harm in that, so long as it is not on account of the Quran. And they (عليهم السلام) permitted the taking of a wage for ruqyah by the Book of Allah and His names. It is narrated that the Messenger of Allah (صل الله عليه وآله وسلم) sent out a company that passed by one of the encampments of the Arabs and lodged with them, and the chief of that encampment was stung. They came to the company and asked whether any of them could perform ruqyah, and one of them recited over him the Opening of the Book, and he recovered; they were given thirty sheep, which they took. When they came to the Messenger of Allah (صل الله عليه وآله وسلم) and told him of it, he permitted them what they had taken.

Abu Abdullah al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about a man who gives another a wage on the condition that he teach him his craft and take him as a partner in it, and he permitted that. This falls under what was mentioned before, when the craft is of what is lawful. As for one who hires, or is hired, to teach what is not lawful, such as singing, instruments of frivolity, and their like, that is not permitted. They narrated that Ali, the Commander of the Faithful (ع), said that the wage of the muezzin is of unlawful gain, and he said there is no harm in his being provided for from the Bayt al-Mal. It seems that what he (ع) disapproved was only the muezzin's taking the wage from those for whom he calls the adhan; and likewise the disapproval came regarding the imam taking a wage from the people he leads in prayer, and the judge from the people between whom he judges, and the like of that, while they permitted their being provided for from the Bayt al-Mal. Abu Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about the wage of the broker, the simsar, hired by one who has him buy or sell, and he permitted it, provided the buying and selling for which he is hired is of what is lawful. He (ع) was asked about a broker given a commodity to sell, who secures a price for it on the terms that whatever he gains above that is his as his wage, and he permitted it. And they (عليهم السلام) said, concerning the hired man and the craftsman who spoils or destroys what he was hired to do, that he is liable for it, as with the fuller who tears the cloth, or the tailor or the dyer who ruins it.

The Marketplace: Honest Earning, Trade, and Property

A worker who is entrusted with another man's property bears responsibility when the loss issues from his own hand. If a craftsman cuts a garment wrongly, or departs from what he was instructed, or a porter or carrier breaks what he was paid to transport, and the goods were surrendered into his custody and he himself was handling them, then he is liable for whatever he caused to be broken or damaged while it lay in his possession. Nothing, however, falls upon the owner of an animal on a mere claim. If the hired man or porter says that what he was carrying was wrenched from his hands, and the one who handed it over denies this, the proof rests upon the one who makes the claim, and the oath rests upon the one who denies that he knew it had left him. The worker or porter is held liable only where there is evidence against him. If the evidence shows that the thing was stolen or seized from his hands, nothing is upon him.

The Imams (عليهم السلام) permitted leasing and hiring (kara') by a specified object and by goods. Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about this and said, "There is nothing wrong with that." He was asked about a man who rents by the quarter: if he stays for a single day, is he bound to the month or the year? He answered that a man is bound by what he has bound himself to. He was asked about two parties who dispute the payment of the rent, the owner of the house wishing to take payment in advance while the renter says, "I will not pay until the end of the month or the year." He said, "If they stipulated this as a condition, it stands as they contracted it; and if they made no stipulation, the rent may be taken day by day."

He was asked about a man who rents a house that has trees in it and stipulates the fruit as part of the contract. He said that if the fruit was in blossom at the time of the contract, or some of it was already present, this is permissible; otherwise it is not, and the rental is void if the two conditions are concluded together in that manner. He was asked about a man whose rented house was partly ruined, or whose rooms leaked in the rain, and the renter carried out repairs at the owner's charge. He said the owner cannot be compelled to repair; but if the ruin causes the renter harm, the renter may reduce the rent and vacate, owing what he has already occupied, and if there is no harm the rental is not dissolved. Even where the renter has not stipulated that the owner repair what has decayed or clean out its drains, that duty still lies upon the owner without stipulation, since it is among the things known to be incumbent upon the owner of a house. He said (ع) that it is not permissible for the renter to bring into the house anything that would harm it, unless he stipulated as much in the contract.

He was asked about a dispute over the rent, the owner claiming more and the renter claiming less, whether the renter has occupied the house or not. He said, "They both take an oath; whichever of them shrinks from the oath, the word of the other prevails once he has sworn upon it. If they both swear, or both refuse the oath together, the rental is dissolved." He said that leasing an undivided, jointly held share (mushā') is permissible just as its sale is permissible.

He was asked about a man who dwells in another man's house and claims he was allowed to live there without rent, while the owner says he leased it to him for such and such, and neither has proof. He said the owner swears that he did not give it free of rent, and the resident is then liable for the value of the rent, unless that value exceeds what the owner claimed as the agreed sum, in which case the owner takes only what he acknowledged and nothing beyond it. Likewise, if the renter claims that the owner ordered him to spend upon the house and repair it, and he did so, and the owner denies it, and the renter has no proof, then the owner swears that he gave no such order, and the renter has the right to remove what he added.

He was asked about a man who rents from another a house part of which is taken up by stored goods, on the condition that the owner clear them out, and then the owner is slow to remove them. He said the renter owes rent only to the extent that he was actually able to dwell in the house. He was asked about a house, or part of it, damaged by something the renter did that was not stipulated in the contract. He said he guarantees what was damaged on that account, unless it is something that occupants customarily do; in that case there is no liability even without stipulation, so long as it falls within what is commonly understood, for not everything a renter does must be written into the contract.

He was asked about an animal or a ship that fails before reaching the destination for which it was hired. He said that whoever hired a particular animal or ship, inspecting it and taking it into his charge, has the rental dissolved when it fails, and owes only the proportional rent for the distance already covered. But if he engaged a carrier without naming a particular animal or ship, the carrier must furnish an equivalent to carry the load through to the stipulated destination, and the full rent is due. He was asked about one who hires an animal for a known number of days to ride it, load it, grind with it, or thresh with it, mentioning only what he means to use it for. He said this is permissible, and the man may put it to fair use of that kind. He must not exceed proper use, nor may the owner cut the hired period short. If the renter did not name what he meant to do with the animal, and then wishes to put it to a use that would harm or diminish it, he has no right to that.

He was asked about one who hires an animal or a ship to carry a known load on the condition that he deliver it within a stated and appointed time, and then fails to deliver within that time and overruns it. He said the rental on such terms is corrupt, and the hirer owes the fair hire for its like. It was related of 'Ali (ع) that he ruled concerning a man who hired an animal to a named destination and carried it beyond that point, so that it perished: he made the man liable for the animal, and imposed no rent upon the excess beyond the agreed limit. Al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said that if the animal did not perish and the renter had carried it past the stipulated limit, the owner may take from him the fair hire for the excess, or take compensation for whatever diminishment the excess caused, whichever he chooses, but he may not take both together. Likewise, if a man hires it for a day or several days and then keeps it beyond that, the owner may choose between the proportional rent for the time held after the term expired and the fair hire for the like; and if it perishes while held past the stipulated term, the renter guarantees its value.

He was asked about a dispute over distance, the renter claiming a far destination and the owner of the animal saying it was near, though both agree that a hire took place. He said the proof lies upon the one who claims the greater, who is the renter, and if he has no proof the oath falls upon the carrier (Makari). If they agree on the destination but differ over the route, the proof lies upon the one claiming the more difficult of the two roads, and the oath upon the other. If the two roads are equal and they differ over which was named in the contract, and the man has neither ridden nor set out, they both take an oath and dissolve the contract if neither has proof, and whoever refuses the oath is bound by his fellow's claim. If he has already ridden or set out, his own word is taken and the renter is the claimant. He was asked about a man who hires transport to a country, and the carrier, reaching the first settlement of that land, wishes the renter to alight there, while the renter insists on reaching its farthest part, no particular place within the country having been named in the contract. This is like a man who hires to Iraq, or to Khurasan, or to the Levant, or to Africa, or the like. He said (ع) that the destination in such a case is the mother-city of that region, the one meant by its name, famous by it, and known to people as the place they intend when they set out for it.

He was asked about land that suffers a calamity, flooding, the receding of water, the drying of a spring, or the failing of a river that watered it. He said that if the renter wishes to spend upon it out of his year's rent, he may, and the cost falls upon the owner of the land; otherwise he owes only in proportion to what he cultivated and benefited from, and the rental is dissolved thereafter. He was asked about a rental in which an option is stipulated. He said that whatever is stipulated of what is lawful and permissible stands, and whatever is of what is not lawful and not permissible does not stand, while the rental itself remains in force.

He was asked about a man who delivers wheat to a miller, or sesame to an oil-presser, stipulating a fixed measure to be returned to him for a wage set upon it. He said, "Fixing the yield in that way is not permissible." The miller and the presser are entitled to the fair wage for their labor, and the owner of the wheat or sesame takes whatever each of them in fact produces. He was asked about the earnings of the cupper and saw no harm in them. He said, "The Messenger of Allah (ص) paid him, and the Imams (عليهم السلام) go on paying them to this day; had it been unlawful, they would never have given it to them." He was asked about the craftsman whom a customer approaches wanting work he cannot in fact deliver: the tailor who is shown a cloth and says he will make its like and cuts it accordingly, then cannot find enough to clothe him; or the goldsmith who says a certain amount of gold will suffice for such and such work, and after beginning and melting down the gold and silver finds himself unable; or one who shows gold or silver saying it is of good quality, and it proves to be poor. He said (ع) that if each of them spoke to the measure of his knowledge and the utmost of his effort and then erred, nothing is upon him; but if it is established against him, or known of him, that he meant thereby to deceive his client, he guarantees what he spoiled.

On Pledges

﴿يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا تَدَايَنتُم بِدَيْنٍ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى فَاكْتُبُوهُ﴾ (al-Baqarah: 282), ﴿وَلَمْ تَجِدُوا كَاتِبًا فَرِهَانٌ مَّقْبُوضَةٌ﴾ (al-Baqarah: 283). This shows that a pledge is a pledge only when it is taken into possession; whatever is not received in hand is no pledge at all. Whatever may lawfully be sold may lawfully be pledged, and whoever takes an undivided share (mushā') in pledge receives possession of it in the same manner as an undivided share is received in a sale. It is not permissible for the one holding the pledge to draw benefit from it. If the pledged thing perishes, the loss is from the pledgor's wealth, unless the pledge-holder himself brought about its ruin. When it perishes, the holder has no right to demand another pledge in its place; he has only the right to claim his debt when it falls due. If the pledgor and the pledge-holder dispute over what the pledge secures, the proof lies upon the one claiming the surplus, who is the pledge-holder, and the oath lies upon the pledgor as to the surplus claimed against him. If the pledge has perished and they dispute over its value, the proof likewise lies upon the one claiming the surplus in it, who is now the pledgor, and the oath upon the pledge-holder as to the surplus he claims. The pledge-holder may not sell the pledge when the term matures except by order of a judge, after the debt has been established before him.

If the pledgor is absent, the judge, having verified the debt, orders the pledge sold on his behalf and the creditor's due paid from it, just as an absent debtor's property is sold for his debt, the judge standing in for him in the matter. If the pledge-holder sells the pledge, whether at maturity, after it, or before it, without the pledgor's authorization, he is liable, and whatever benefit or yield he took from the pledge belongs to the pledgor. The pledgor, for his part, may not sell the pledge, nor give it away, nor make it a charity, nor set it free if it is a slave, nor remove it from his ownership by any means whatsoever until he redeems it. Nor may the pledge-holder sell it in order to recover the debt for which it was pledged when the term falls due, whether the pledgor is present or absent; rather the pledgor is the one who sells it and settles his debt. Even if the pledgor granted the holder leave to sell it at maturity but did not appoint him as agent, so that the holder would stand in his place in the sale, the holder may not sell it on his own behalf; he refers the matter to the judge, who sells on behalf of the absent party once the debt is established and appoints someone to sell it and settle the debt, just as is done with property that is not pledged. But if the pledgor did appoint the pledge-holder as his agent to sell the pledge at maturity, and the holder sold it, calling witnesses to the sale and striving to obtain the fullest price and to sell it well, then nothing is upon him, even if the pledgor suspects him in the sale and puts him to an oath upon it, should he wish. And when the pledgor wishes to sell the pledge, there is no harm in the pledge-holder buying it from him if they agree upon that, setting it off against his debt, and settling any surplus between them.

On Partnership

Partnership in wealth (sharikah), according to the word of the Imams of the People of the House (عليهم السلام), is permissible when two men or a group contract it, each of them bringing money of his own and mingling it with the money of his partner in the contract, until it becomes a single, indistinguishable fund to be bought and sold with. Whatever gain arises from it is shared between them, and whatever loss is incurred is borne by them in proportion to what each has contributed. Neither of them may sell, buy, or undertake any new dealing in what they jointly hold except with his partner and by his leave. If each grants the other leave to act in it as he sees fit, then he may do so, and he owes his partner no accounting for it, unless he betrays him. If a man says to his partner, "You have wronged me," or "You gave an advantage to another against me in buying and selling," or "In what I entrusted to you," or "You meant to squander my wealth," or the like, then he may put him to an oath that he did no such thing and intended none of it. When the money is held equally between the two partners, and they stipulate that gain and loss be shared equally between them likewise, they hold to what they stipulated.

When two partners contribute equally to a venture, the profit and the loss are both according to their agreed terms. If they stipulate a different arrangement, then the profit follows what they stipulated, while the loss remains proportional to the capital. Even when their capital contributions differ, so that one contributes more than the other, the profit is still divided according to what they have agreed upon, and the loss falls in proportion to the capital each has put in.

If the money belongs to only one of them, the arrangement is no longer an ordinary partnership but mudaraba, speculation, and the one who has no money is the mudarib, the working partner. The two stipulate that he shall be the one who works with the money and trades with it, or they agree together upon whatever terms they set. The profit between them is based on what they have contracted, and the loss falls upon the owner of the money alone and not upon the mudarib. If the owner of the money guarantees the mudarib against any shortfall or loss he incurs, this guarantee removes the arrangement from the category of a valid mudaraba or partnership altogether; it becomes instead a loan. The one who took the money keeps whatever gain he earns from it, and he must guarantee it as he would guarantee his own, exactly as he took the liability upon himself.

If the mudarib violates what the owner of the money has stipulated, whether in what he buys, what he trades in, where he travels, or any other condition set in the mudaraba contract, the profit between them is still divided according to what they agreed. But if a loss occurs or the money passes out of his hands, he is its guarantor and answerable for whatever is diminished, because he has transgressed the terms.

If a man gives money to another to work with, trade in, and earn from as a specified fixed sum set upon him, this is not permissible and is not lawful, nor is it binding upon the one to whom it was given. It is riba, which is not permissible for him. He must return whatever he took, and repent to Allah Almighty from it. The one exception is where the man who paid it over and fixed the profit upon the other is his own slave, for then what was paid is halal and lawful to him. There is no riba between a master and his slave, because the slave's wealth belongs to his master.

If one of the partners dies, the partnership is dissolved, and any partner who wishes to annul it may dissolve it whenever he wills. If the partner or the mudarib is accused, he is put to his oath. Partnership is disliked when it is made in goods that remain distinguished and unmixed. If two partners join in what each holds separately, then what is held apart belongs to its own owner, whether it increases or decreases. But if they wish to form a genuine partnership, each of them sells to the other half of the separate goods in his possession at a known price, so that the goods pass into shared ownership, and upon this they subscribe together. Such a partnership is valid.

It is disliked for a Muslim to enter into partnership with a dhimmi, and for a believer to partner with one who differs from him in religion, since such a partner may engage in buying and selling that the believer does not permit and cannot prevent, and this introduces what is unlawful and riba-tainted into his wealth. If, however, the believer or Muslim himself oversees the buying and selling, and not the dhimmi or the one who differs, then there is nothing wrong with such a partnership.

If the mudarib perishes owing a debt, holding speculation funds, and keeping deposits in trust, and none of these is found intact within his estate, and it is not established against him that he consumed them, was compensated for them, destroyed them, mixed them with his own wealth, or did anything else that would make him liable, then nothing is required of his heirs except an oath that they do not know he did any of these things, that they did not do them, and that none of it is in their hands or against them. As for the mudaraba funds and the deposit, he is a trustee over them; when they are not found in his estate by their own substance and no proof stands against him, the debt alone remains binding, because it is a liability upon his person.

If the man who is owed a debt, holds a deposit, and carries a trust names, before he dies, one of these parties as the one to receive what is due, then it belongs to the one he named. If he did not hand it over, the debt takes precedence and is settled first, and whatever remains after that is shared among the body of creditors. It is not permissible to form a partnership with an absent partner. If a man is owed a debt by another and enters into mudaraba or partnership with him on the strength of that debt, this does not hold, and neither company nor mudaraba is contracted by it, until the debtor brings the debt and pays it over; only then may he take him as a partner or a mudarib with it.

If two partners separate and settle accounts, and a debt owed to them from their partnership still remains outstanding, and they divide that debt on the condition that each recovers what fell to him by the division, this is not binding upon them should they later dispute it. Rather, whatever each of them collects is shared between them both, and whatever of the debt is lost falls upon them jointly. But if one says to the other, "Give me my capital," or whatever sum they had agreed upon, "and yours is whatever exceeds it, and upon you is whatever falls short of it," and they agree to this, then it is permissible for them to act upon what they have agreed.

On Pre-emption

Pre-emption applies to dwellings and real estate when a share or shares are sold that are still held in common and undivided between the seller and his partner or partners. The partner has the greater claim and is more entitled to the sale than an outsider who has no share in the property. If one of the partners exercises pre-emption while the rest do not, he is more entitled to it than those who held back. If they all exercise it, they share it, each taking a portion matching his share in the partnership; and if their shares differ, so does their portion, while if their shares are equal, it is divided equally between them.

There is no pre-emption for a neighbor or anyone else in property that has already been divided, nor is there pre-emption over a river, a ship, an animal, or anything other than real estate and undivided shares, which is to say land and what stands upon it of building or of trees growing in it. If a property is owned jointly between two people and one of them sells his share, his partner is more entitled to it by pre-emption.

Pre-emption is exercised on behalf of a child until he comes of age. If his guardian or the one who stands in his place rises to claim it, and doing so benefits the child, it is taken for him. It is likewise held for the absent until he returns or word of the sale reaches him, whereupon he either submits the pre-emption or lets his claim stand for the period between him and the completion of one year. So too the one who is present has that same period to act, from the time the sale reaches him until a full year has passed from that moment, unless he relinquishes the pre-emption.

Pre-emption is owed to the pre-emptor by virtue of his partnership in the thing sold, whether his share of it is small or large, and whether he is a partner in the whole of the sale or in only part of it. An example is a wall standing between two houses whose two owners share it, or in which one of them holds a common share, or any other of the rights they own upon which a sale can fall, be it small or large. The pre-emption in such a case belongs to whoever has a share in it, however slight or however great.

Pre-emption is owed to the people of the private lane, the raighah, that does not open to a through-road, because of their shared ownership of it and the absence of any barrier between them. This does not extend to a public thoroughfare that is commonly walked, because the people of the dwellings along such a street do not own it as the people of the raighah own their lane. They merely benefit from it through their courtyards, and in this they and the passers-by on the road stand alike. Therefore the people of a public street have no pre-emption in what is sold along it by right of the street; that right belongs to the people of a raighah among themselves.

If within a raighah there is a house containing a dwelling, and one dwelling of it is sold, the people of the houses have a stronger claim to pre-emption than the people of the raighah, and they may also exercise pre-emption in what is sold within the raighah, because they own the ground of their own courtyard, whereas the people of the raighah own nothing of their houses. Likewise, if there is a lane within a lane, or a lane leading deeper in, the people of the inner lane exercise pre-emption in whatever lies along their path and passage, while the people of the outer and upper part of the lane have no pre-emption in what lies below them, in that to which they have no right and no path, beyond the bounds of their own dwellings into the interior of the lane.

All of this must be examined with regard to shared rights, for the sharing of rights is what is taken into account. Whoever holds a right in what is sold, or in any of the rights upon which the sale falls, is a sharer and therefore a pre-emptor. This shows the shared right the people of the raighah hold in it, for if a man were to own outright all the houses the lane contains, it would be his and within his right to wall it off from the outside and possess the whole of it, and none would have the right to make a way through it with him or to enter it without his leave. So too, if there is a lane within a lane and the owner of the inner lane owns all the houses opening onto it, he may enclose the whole of it and bar others from it. The same holds for whatever lies within the interior of the raighah, except what is bounded by a wall belonging to another of the people of the raighah, for there it stops at its own boundary.

There is no pre-emption for a Jew or a Christian against a Muslim. Muslims exercise pre-emption in the sale of what they share, whether with fellow Muslims or with the people of the dhimmah. Likewise the people of the dhimmah exercise pre-emption against one another in what they trade among themselves that carries the right of pre-emption, but they have no pre-emption against Muslims.

There is no pre-emption in a gift, in charity, in a grant of residence, in a life-grant, or in a grant reverting upon death, nor in what is sold for goods in exchange, as when a house is sold for a house, for a riding animal, or for other livestock or wares. In such a case the pre-emptor may not say, "I will pay the value of that exchanged article upon which the sale fell, and take the house." Likewise, if a man marries a woman and settles upon her a house that would ordinarily carry the right of pre-emption, the pre-emptor may not say, "I will pay a dowry equal to this woman's and take the house." But if the house was sold for a price and that price was received, the pre-emptor is offered it at the very price upon which the sale was concluded. And if the owner of the house married the woman for a known dowry and then handed her a house in settlement of it, this is like a sale, and the pre-emptor may take it for the dowry at which the woman received it.

If what carries the right of pre-emption is sold for something that has a known equivalent, the pre-emptor may take it for that equivalent, as when it is sold for measured wheat, for dates, or for the like of what is measured or weighed and has a known equivalent. The pre-emption then holds, and the pre-emptor is given that equivalent.

The pre-emptor may press his claim upon either the seller or the buyer, against whichever of the two the obligation falls. When the sale is concluded and completed between buyer and seller, and the pre-emptor rises against the seller before the price has been received from the buyer and pays it to him, then what the seller has received from him stands, and the buyer may not prevent him, nor say, "I will pay for what I bought and take it from you, since the pre-emption has been raised against me." For the pre-emptor's right rests upon the seller, and the buyer is bound to deliver. If instead the pre-emptor leaves the buyer until the buyer has paid the price, and then rises against him within the period in which he is entitled to act, that too is his right.

If what carries the right of pre-emption is sold and taken by the buyer, and he then sells it on, and it passes by sale from one hand to the next, whether the pre-emptor knew of this or not, the pre-emptor may press his claim upon whichever of them he chooses, whether seller or buyer, at the price at which that party bought it, be it more or less, so long as the period of pre-emption has not lapsed and he has not relinquished it. He is a pre-emptor at the moment of every sale, and his failure to press the claim does not forfeit his pre-emption so long as the period has not passed.

This is like a man who buys what another had a right of pre-emption in for a price, then sells it to another for less or for more, and it passes by sale from hand to hand until a whole group has owned it in turn. If the pre-emptor wishes to press his claim, he may do so against whichever of them he chooses, unless the period from the time of the original purchase has elapsed or he has relinquished his right. If he rises against the first buyer, and that buyer had sold it within the pre-emption period for more than he paid, the pre-emption entails returning the sale from the seller and all that came after it, and the pre-emptor takes the property at the price the first buyer paid, delivering that price to him. The same runs through to whoever came after him, should the pre-emptor press his claim against him.

If a single house has an upper and a lower dwelling, already divided and sold separately, there is no pre-emption for the owner of the upper against the owner of the lower, nor for the owner of the lower against the owner of the upper, so long as the division took place before the sale. Even though one stands above the other, this is treated as a neighbor relationship. But if the two share a common entrance, or anything that remained held in common between them, and the sale falls upon the seller's share in that common portion, then pre-emption in it is obligatory.

If common-share property and divided property are sold together in a single transaction, the partner in the jointly held portion may take the whole transaction, both the common and the divided parts, and he may not take one part without the other unless the parties agree. If the buyer purchases the common share for a price and the sale is concluded between him and the seller, and the seller then withdraws part of the price so that the sale settles upon what was received after that reduction, and the pre-emptor then rises and asks to take it at the first price, this is not his right. He is entitled only to what was actually paid, because the sale settled at the later figure.

If the seller sells the property for a price and then gives back to the buyer some or all of that price as a gift or as charity, and the pre-emptor then rises wishing to exercise his right, he must pay the price upon which the sale was concluded. He has no right to deduct what the seller gave back to the buyer. This holds unless the whole was done as a device and a contrivance to cut off the pre-emption, the seller inflating the price and then returning the surplus to the buyer, or the buyer paying part as sale price and part as profit in order to defeat the pre-emptor by such a stratagem. For the contract and the true intention are what govern, and where the sale was in truth concluded upon the whole, or upon what was actually taken as price rather than upon what was returned as gift or charity, this does not void the pre-emptor's right nor raise the price against him. If he wishes to exercise pre-emption, he pays what the buyer actually paid and receives all that they contrived over.

This is established and made valid by the acknowledgment of the buyer against whom the pre-emption is pressed, or by evidence standing against him that the matter was indeed a device and a contrivance. But if the pre-emptor claims this and has no evidence, while the party against whom he presses it denies it, then the most that falls upon that party is the oath answering the claim made against him. Such contrivance to cut off pre-emption is not lawful for the seller or the buyer, for it is a scheme to nullify one of the established rights.

The right of pre-emption turns on when the sale becomes known. If a sale is kept secret, the pre-emptor's right to claim runs for one year from the time the sale reaches his knowledge, and this holds even where the sale had in fact been made public and he was present. But if he delays past the year and then claims he had not been informed of a sale that was itself well known, he is not believed, precisely because the sale was public knowledge. The obligation of pre-emption does not arise until the sale has been concluded and finalized. Once it is concluded and the pre-emptor is bound by his claim, it becomes obligatory upon him even if the seller has not yet received the price, and he may pay whichever party he chooses, the seller or the buyer.

If the pre-emptor proceeds against the buyer, the buyer must hand over the property to him together with its custody, while the seller bears no custody obligation when the pre-emptor's claim is pursued through the buyer and the property is delivered from the seller. If the buyer builds upon or sows what he has purchased that is subject to pre-emption, that act constitutes his acceptance and delivery, and his own pre-emptive standing in the thing is thereby cut off.

Certain claims do not yet establish a right to pre-empt. If a man claims that he bought from an absent party, the pre-emptor may rise, but neither the claim nor the pre-emption is established against the absent one, and the pre-emptor may not exercise his right until the sale is valid. Likewise, if the buyer claims to have purchased from a present party who denies it, the pre-emptor may not proceed until the sale is validated, and his right then holds from the time of that validation.

Where a house is held in trust for certain named notables, and some of them built within it and then died, leaving it to their heirs, and some of those heirs sold their inherited share, the pre-emption belongs to the heirs. And whoever relinquished his pre-emption before the sale, then rose to claim it after the sale was carried out, may still do so or not, for the obligation of relinquishment is not binding until after the sale has been concluded.

If the pre-emptor and the buyer disagree over the price, the buyer's word is accepted under his oath that he bought at the price he states, and that price is what is paid, unless the pre-emptor offers him charity or takes an oath against him, in which case the price is paid to him accordingly. If it is later established that the buyer in fact purchased for less than that, or the buyer himself acknowledges it, the pre-emptor is entitled to recover the excess.

If the pre-emptor exercises his right, then substitutes the property and sells it, and afterward rises against the buyer demanding that the buyer pay him the price and return what was bought, making that his claim, he is obliged to carry it through. If he does not proceed, no obligation falls upon him.

This is all the position of the Ahl al-Bayt (عليهم السلام).

On the Preservation of Oaths and Covenants

Allah, Mighty and Majestic, said: "And guard your oaths" (al-Ma'idah 89), and He described the believers as "those who are faithful to their trusts and their covenants" (al-Mu'minun 8). He said: "Fulfill the covenant of Allah when you have made a covenant, and do not break oaths after their confirmation, when you have made Allah a surety over you" (al-Nahl 91). And He said: "Those who purchase with the covenant of Allah and their own oaths a small gain will have no share in the Hereafter; Allah will not speak to them, nor look at them on the Day of Resurrection, nor purify them, and for them is a painful punishment" (Al Imran 77).

The Messenger of Allah (ع) forbade swearing by anything other than Allah, Mighty and Majestic, and said: "Whoever swears, let him swear by Allah; whoever swears by Allah, let him speak the truth; and whoever has an oath sworn to him by Allah, let him accept it. If he does not do so, he is not a Muslim." He (ع) also said: "There are three whom Allah will not look at on the Day of Resurrection, will not purify, and for whom there is a painful punishment: a man who pledges allegiance to an imam only for a portion of this world, so that if he is given it he stays loyal and if he is not given it he withholds his loyalty; a man who has water on the roadside and denies it to the traveler; and a man who swears after the afternoon prayer that he was offered such-and-such a price for his goods, so that another takes them, believing him, when he is lying."

'Ali (ع) said: Allah, Mighty and Majestic, does not sanctify one who swears falsely in His name. And it is reported from him (ع) that he said: "Beware the false oath, for it makes goods sell and wipes out blessing. Whoever swears a false oath has been insolent toward Allah, so let him await His punishment." And the Messenger (ع) said: "Whoever breaks his pledge of allegiance will meet Allah on the Day of Resurrection maimed," meaning he will have no hand.

On What Is and Is Not Binding in Oaths

Allah, Mighty and Majestic, said: "Allah will not take you to task for what is thoughtless in your oaths" (al-Baqarah 225). Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: The thoughtlessness referred to here is a person's saying "No, by Allah" and "Yes, by Allah" without his heart being fixed upon the oath. He meant what a person lets fall in the course of ordinary speech, swearing by Allah while intending no oath at all. This is confirmed by the words that follow: "but He will take you to task for what your hearts have earned" (al-Ma'idah 89), meaning what the swearer has resolved in his heart to swear upon.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) made this plain, saying of a man who says something in the midst of his speech, "By Allah, I did not do it," while not binding his conscience to the oath: "It is a lie he has told, so let him seek Allah's forgiveness for it." And he said: "The oath is measured by what the one who demands it seeks it upon." It is reported that the Messenger (ع) forbade equivocation in oaths and said that if the swearer is wronged, the oath follows his own intention, and if he is the wrongdoer, it follows the intention of the one administering it. Equivocation is the use of likeness in speech, whereby a man intends one thing but couches it as another, so that the listener is led to suppose the likeness is what is meant, while the speaker inwardly intends something else. As for the People of the Book, they are made to swear by Allah and by whatever they hold sacred in their own faith.

On the words of Allah, "And remember your Lord when you forget" (al-Kahf 24), Abu 'Abdullah Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: This concerns the oath. If you say, "By Allah, I will surely do such-and-such," and forget to add the exception, then say "In sha' Allah" when you remember. He related that a group of Jews came to the Messenger of Allah (ع) and asked him about a matter, and he said, "Tomorrow I will tell you," waiting upon the revelation of Allah without making an exception in his word. The revelation was withheld from him for forty days, after which it came down: "And never say of anything, 'I will surely do that tomorrow,' except that Allah wills; and remember your Lord when you forget" (al-Kahf 23 to 24). So they were commanded to make the exception in their oaths, and to put the will of Allah first, and this was held commendable.

The Commander of the Faithful (ع) said: Whoever swears and then says "In sha' Allah" has not perjured himself. Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said: If a man moves his tongue with the exception, he has made it, even if he does not speak it aloud. He related that the Messenger (ع) said a man's oath admits of an exception, and the exception is valid when joined to the oath, that is, he swears and then says "In sha' Allah" before he breaks off his speech. Such an exception is permissible by consensus, and one who has sworn and thus excepted incurs no perjury. Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع) said the exception is permissible even after forty days, because the Messenger (ع) made his exception after the revelation was withheld from him following his word to the Jews who had questioned him, "Tomorrow I will tell you," when Allah revealed, "And remember your Lord when you forget" (al-Kahf 24). The like of this is narrated from 'Ali, the Commander of the Faithful (ع).

Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn 'Ali (ع) said the exception is permissible even after a year. But the exception applies only to an oath a man swears to do or not do something. As for one made to swear over a right claimed against him, who swears to the claimant, his exception counts for nothing whether he makes it privately or aloud. The oath there is governed by intention, and what stands is the outward wording taken according to the intention of the one who administers it. If the swearer intends or conceals something to void the oath, or works a stratagem in it, or equivocates, nothing is lifted from him; both the oath and its perjury stand. He must fulfill what he swore, and there is no expiation for the perjury except to render what he swore to the one to whom it is owed, then to repent and seek forgiveness. There is no oath over what the swearer does not possess, as with one who swears to free a slave he does not own or to give away what is not his. There is no oath by divorce, no oath by manumission, no oath by pilgrimage or by 'Umrah, and no oath sworn by anything other than Allah, Blessed and Exalted. The oath is only by Allah, Mighty and Majestic.

Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn 'Ali (ع) was asked about the words of Allah, "And when the Prophet confided a matter to one of his wives" (al-Tahrim 3), to the end of the verse. He said: The Messenger of Allah (ع) had been alone with his bondwoman Mariyah the Copt in the first days after she came to him, before he had settled her in a house of her own, and 'A'ishah came upon him. She questioned him about it, and wishing to please her, he swore to her that he would not go back to Mariyah, forbade her to himself, and charged 'A'ishah to keep the matter secret. She did not, but told Hafsah, and the two of them backed one another against him. So Allah, Mighty and Majestic, sent down: "O Prophet, why do you forbid what Allah has made lawful for you?" (al-Tahrim 1), and commanded him to expiate the oath he had sworn. He was not held to have wronged himself by forbidding what was lawful to him; rather he was gently reproached for it. Whatever Allah has made lawful remains lawful and is not thereby forbidden. So whoever forbids to himself what Allah has made lawful has not truly made it forbidden; it stays lawful for him, and he may take to it. If he bound it with an oath, let him expiate the oath and take to what Allah has made lawful for him. The Messenger of Allah (ع) said: "Whoever swears upon a matter and then sees that its opposite is better for him, let him do what is better and expiate his oath."

Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn 'Ali (ع) said: Every oath sworn in an act of disobedience counts for nothing; there is neither perjury nor expiation in it. Whoever swears not to do what is obligatory upon him, or not to do what is permitted him, and then refrains, has done no wrong, and there is neither perjury nor expiation upon him. But whoever swears to perform an act of obedience must do what he swore, and if he does not, he must expiate his oath.

Mentioning Vows

Allah, Mighty and Majestic, said: "The righteous will drink from a cup whose mixture is camphor" (al-Insan 5), to His words, "They fulfill their vows and fear a Day whose evil is widespread" (al-Insan 7). The Messenger of Allah (ع) forbade the vow made to other than Allah, Blessed and Exalted, and the vow made in an act of disobedience or in the severing of kinship.
