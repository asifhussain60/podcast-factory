What you have here is a compendium of transmitted reports — short, gathered accounts of practice and ruling — arranged by subject rather than told as a continuous story. Its very title marks it as an abridgement: a condensed collection of *asar*, the received reports, and the numeral signals that it is a second gathering, a companion to a first. It reads the way such collections read, moving topic by topic through the ordinary business of a life lived under law.

Because the book is built from transmitted reports, no single author speaks to you in his own voice. Where a first person appears, it belongs to a figure inside a report — someone whose words or conduct are being relayed — not to a narrator confiding in the reader. Keep that distinction in mind: the "I" of a passage is a person being quoted, and the book itself stands at a step's remove, carrying the account forward. Nothing here has been added or augmented; the edition presents the reports as they come, faithfully.

The nine chapters map the terrain of everyday obligation. They begin in the marketplace, with honest earning and property, then take up the weight of a sworn word — oaths and vows and how they are answered for. From there the book turns to the body's needs: what may be eaten and drunk and used to heal, what may be worn and how one is adorned, and how an animal is lawfully hunted, slaughtered, or offered. The later chapters move into the household — marriage and its ordering, and then its ending in divorce — before closing with the freeing of slaves, the giving of gifts, the dividing of estates, and finally the work of the judge in setting wrong to right.

The figures who recur are not named heroes but roles: the trader, the one who swears, the spouse, the heir, the judge. Read them as the standing parties to the situations the reports address.
