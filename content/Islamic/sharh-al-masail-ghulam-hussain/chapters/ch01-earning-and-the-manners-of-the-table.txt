# Lawful Earning and the Table

## Where this episode opens

What if the place where a person's religion is actually settled is not the prayer mat at all, but the market stall, the dinner table, and the block where the animal is slaughtered? That is the inversion this book opens with, and it does not apologise for it. It does not begin with the nature of God, or the ranks of the spiritual hierarchy, or the mysteries of the soul. It begins with a man carrying a rope over his shoulder to earn a day's wage, and it treats that man's morning as a matter of religious consequence — not a lesser thing he must get out of the way before religion begins, but the very ground on which religion is either kept or lost.

This is an explanatory treatise, written to answer questions of practice for a community that needed rulings it could use. Its author gathered the issues bearing on daily life and set aside those that no longer touched anybody, along with technical matters like usury and mortgage. What remains is a working handbook, and this first stretch of it takes up the two most ordinary acts in a person's day: getting money, and eating.

Three teachings hold this episode together, best heard as a single descending line. The first is that earning a lawful living is not a concession to worldly necessity but a form of devotion in its own right, ranked alongside striving in the way of God. The second is that the table — how a person eats, how he feeds another, how he behaves as guest and as host — is a sustained discipline of humility, treated with the seriousness usually reserved for ritual. The third is the boundary itself: what may lawfully be eaten, how an animal must be killed, what may be hunted, and what may be worn. That third teaching is the driest, and it is where the logic of the first two becomes visible. Earning is devotion, and eating is discipline, precisely because the substance passing through a person's hands and mouth is not neutral.

Hold onto the tension as we go. Everything here pulls two ways at once: the world is to be worked in, traded in, eaten from and dressed for — and every one of those acts carries a limit the person is expected to know and to keep himself.

## The dignity of lawful earning

The section opens by quoting the command to travel the earth and seek of God's bounty, and everything that follows is commentary on that single instruction. The bounty is real, it is out in the world, and a person is told to go and find it.

The Messenger of God, may God's blessings and peace be upon him, is reported as saying: trade, and keep away from laziness. The pairing is the whole argument in miniature. Trade is not merely permitted; its opposite is not piety but idleness. And the reward attached to it is stated in an image of startling brightness: whoever earns his living through lawful means, and seeks by that earning to provide for his children and to spare them from having to depend on anyone else, will meet God with a face like the full moon.

Notice what the reward is attached to. Not to wealth, and not to success, but to the lawfulness of the means and the direction of the intention — that the earning exists so that dependants need not beg. The tradition then presses the point from the other side. It is better for one of you, the Messenger said, to take his rope, carry a load on his back, and earn by it, than to come to a man whom God has blessed with wealth and ask him for something — whether that man gives or withholds. The last clause is careful. Whether the rich man gives or withholds, the asking was the loss; carrying the load was the better thing either way.

A further saying is sharper still, because it is sworn upon. By God, the Messenger said, it is better for a young man to drive his needle through leather in the way of God than to leave it. Someone raised the obvious objection — that if a man has strength and means, surely he should be exerting himself in the way of God rather than sitting at a workbench with an awl. The answer refuses the distinction the question depends on: this, in this year, is the most tender path in the way of God. The leather and the needle are not a detour from striving. They are a form of it.

That same equivalence is stated outright elsewhere: if a man is steadfast in earning lawfully, he earns a reward like that of one who strives in God's path. And the one who labours and provides for his dependants will be rewarded for it. The best thing a man can earn, another saying holds, is what suffices for his wife and his children; and if he takes on a debt for their sake and then discharges it, he has done well, and there is no fault upon him in it. That last clause matters. Debt incurred to feed a family, and honoured, is not treated here as a failure of trust in providence. It is treated as a man doing his job.

And when a person cannot manage — the counsel is not to sit still with the difficulty. Let him go out of his difficulty and strike the earth seeking God's bounty. He should not grow faint, because this is not the end. The image behind the phrase is a man going out and working the ground, and the "bounty" he seeks is spelled out plainly as the sustenance of his family. There is no romance in it. It is a man in trouble, told to get up and go out.

Alongside the encouragement there is a distinct strand about how trade should actually be conducted. Al-Dahhak is quoted for the principle: people should make buying easy, selling easy, judgement easy, and the satisfying of needs easy. Four ordinary things, each of which can be made hard by a person who wants the advantage — and the instruction is to make each of them easy. Trade, described this way, is where a person's character shows in small increments.

There is a memorable moment behind this. The Prophet of God, may God's blessings and peace be upon him, once passed a group of men who in that time were called brokers, and told them: I do not call you brokers; I call you merchants. And in the same breath the boundary is drawn — the swindler is in the Fire. The renaming is a dignifying act and a warning in one gesture: the trade is honourable, and the honour of the name does not survive the cheating.

The relationship between trade and religious knowledge is settled by a short exchange. A man told the Commander of the Believers that he wished to go into trade. The answer came first: learn the jurisprudence of the religion of God. Then trade. The order is not decorative. A man who does not know the rulings will break them without intending to, because the breaking happens inside ordinary transactions that look like nothing at all.

Against all this, one saying stands as a guard on the other side, and the treatise includes it deliberately: do not hate a man for being occupied with the world, because if he is negligent in a worldly matter, he only becomes more preoccupied with it. The observation is shrewd. Contempt for the working man does not free him from the world; neglect of his affairs is what binds him to it. And the tradition goes further still: there is no one striving in the way of God greater in reward than the man who strives to earn, for his son and his children, that which is good for them.

The Imams sharpen this into personal instruction. A man came to Imam Ja'far ibn Muhammad, may God's peace be upon him, asking him to pray for him and to give him a supplication. The answer he received was a refusal. I will not pray for you as you have asked. Rather, the Muslim ought to go out and seek his sustenance until the heat of the midday sun strikes him. A supplication was requested; work was prescribed instead. The refusal is the teaching.

The same Imam's counsel to his companions turns toward attentiveness rather than effort. Do not sit idle in the markets, buying small things yourself that you have no business buying — not for yourself, and not on behalf of those poor in religion. A wise man ought to look into small matters himself, and look carefully, day after day. The small transaction, examined personally and repeatedly, is where a livelihood is either kept honest or quietly ruined.

And a final note of proportion closes the section, from the same Imam, who saw a man from among the Companions carrying something in his hand and remarked that God dislikes that a noble man should carry the lowest of things, lest he diminish himself in the eyes of others. Read beside everything preceding it, this is not a retraction of the rope and the load; it is a matter of fitness. A person carries what is his to carry, and his public bearing is part of what he owes to the position he holds. The dignity of work and the dignity of the worker are held together, and neither is allowed to cancel the other.

## The manners of the table

From how a person gets his food, the treatise moves directly to how he eats it, and the same seriousness carries across. Food is described from the outset as the blessing of God upon those who receive it, and the first instruction attached to it is not to waste it.

The pattern is set by the Messenger of God, may God's blessings and peace be upon him, and it is a pattern of deliberate lowness. He would eat sitting on the earth. He would say that he ate as a servant eats, and sat as a servant sits. He sat cross-legged, ate plainly in the manner of the virtuous, and did not arrange his eating to be seen.

The particulars are given in detail. He ate with his right hand, with water beside him. He did not eat from the rim of the bowl, nor from the middle of the dish, but from what was directly in front of him. He took small mouthfuls and chewed them thoroughly, and would not rush toward the next bite. He never found fault with the food; if something displeased him, he left it and said nothing. He did not drink water while food was still in his mouth. And when the meal was done, he cleaned his fingers.

Each of these is small enough to seem trivial, and that is why they are collected. Eating from what is in front of you is a rule about the other people at the table; not faulting the food is a rule about whoever cooked it. The treatise states the underlying principle plainly: what is being practised at the table is humility before God and the glorification of His provision. And so the people of religion ought to imitate these acts of the Prophet and carry them out in their own lives — without showing off, and without arrogance. That last qualification is essential. A person can perform every one of these details and turn the whole discipline inside out by performing them visibly.

Even the placement of the food is given a rule, and the rule adapts. If a person is on the ground, let him put his food on the ground if he is able; if he is on a bed, he may place it before him; and he should keep away from reclining at his table. If it is his custom to sit on a chair, let him set the table at a height suited to him — so that it becomes evident that he honours his sustenance. The aim stays fixed while the arrangement flexes: the food is a gift, not fuel.

More is narrated from Ja'far ibn Muhammad, may God's peace be upon him. A person should not eat or drink with his left hand, nor take meat with it. When drinking water, let him say: in the name of God; and when he sets the vessel down: praise be to God. Let him look into the vessel first, and drink the water clean and pure, pausing to breathe three times rather than draining it in one go. If he is thirsty, let him draw the water with his hand and sip it. And the food itself must be lawful and clean — not sought out of pleasure and desire, but out of piety. That last clause ties the eating back to the earning: the lawfulness of what is on the plate and the lawfulness of the money that bought it are the same question asked twice.

Then the subject turns outward, from eating to feeding, and the register lifts. The verse is quoted: and they give food, in spite of their love for it, to the poor, the orphan, and the captive, saying — we feed you only for the sake of God; we desire from you neither reward nor thanks. Two things are asked for at once. The food is given while it is still wanted, not after it has become surplus; and every return, including the return of gratitude, is deliberately renounced.

The reward described is architectural. It is narrated that in Paradise there is a palace whose outer face can be seen from within and whose inner face from without — a dwelling with no concealment in it at all — and that it belongs to those who feed others, and who pray at night while everyone else is asleep. A palace with no hidden side, for a life with no hidden side.

There is a narration from the Commander of the Believers concerning a day when the Messenger of God brought seven captives and asked him to prepare a meal that would bring comfort to those who were burdened. Some were dealt with according to the judgement passed on them, and one was released. And the Messenger of God told him that Jibril had informed him about the hand that is withheld from food, and assured him he would be helped in the task.

The instruction to feed extends into the ordinary case. If your believing brother comes to you, feed him the best of what you have; if he is fasting, still honour him with what you can. And from Imam Muhammad al-Baqir, may God's peace be upon him: feeding a believer is equivalent to freeing a slave, and among the most beloved of deeds to God is bringing joy to a believer — whether by satisfying his hunger or by paying off his debt.

Hospitality is then named for what the tradition takes it to be: one of the moral qualities of the believer, and one of the noble virtues. The scholars agreed on the guest's right as three days; past that, what is given becomes charity rather than the discharge of a duty. Generosity is counted among the most noble qualities of the prophets. A person should draw close to his brother as far as he is able — and if he has only water, let him offer that water in the best way he can. Whoever is invited should attend: the host owes hospitality and the guest owes his presence. There is a scene involving al-Husayn, may God's peace be upon him, who saw feet turning toward the food while a man remained standing at prayer, and an exchange of apologies passed between them — even prayer, held at the wrong moment, can separate a person from the table.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad's rulings on the guest's side of the meal are practical and warm. If a person arrives while fasting and is offered food, he should break his fast — unless the fast is an obligatory one. If your brother comes to eat, do not hold back until he has taken his share with you, because what he wants from you is your honour, not your food. Give him what you have, invite him, do what you can; speak with him, sit with him at the table, treat him warmly — because a man's affection is shown by how generously he welcomes a guest. I love it, the Imam said, when a man eats my food with enjoyment; that gladdens me. And when a meal is shared with parents and children, one should not sit in silence but talk, because there is blessing in the conversation and the reward is multiplied by the stories shared among those gathered. A meal is not a feeding station; it is fellowship, and silence is a failure of it.

The manners of the guest are then set out as their own body of rules. It is not in keeping with the Sunnah to enter a house uninvited at a time when people are eating; walking toward food to which one was not called is a transgression, and eating in those circumstances is forbidden. If a person may properly enter while food is being prepared, he should wait until the household has finished their prayers and then ask whether they would like him to stay. If he knows they sincerely want his company, he may accept. But if he senses that the invitation is offered only out of courtesy, out of obligation rather than genuine desire, he should decline. Reading the room honestly is harder than following a rule.

There are relationships where the question does not arise. The permission is grounded in the verse allowing a person to eat in the homes of fathers, mothers, brothers, sisters, paternal and maternal uncles, paternal and maternal aunts, in homes whose keys one holds, or in the homes of one's friends — with no blame whether people eat together or separately. The Messenger of God, peace be upon him, came to Barira's house and found food there in her absence; it had been given to her as charity. When she returned and was told, she confirmed that it had reached her lawfully, and he ate it with full knowledge of that and with gladness. Charity given to its rightful recipient becomes her property, and what she then offers is a gift, not charity a second time.

Two narrations about al-Hasan sit side by side as a pair. He would enter a house, see food, sit and eat from it, saying that this was what they used to do — and he would not allow anyone to enter his own house without permission. Entirely at ease in another's home, entirely firm about his own threshold, and the treatise sees no contradiction there. Passing through the market one day he took something from a vendor's goods and ate it, and Hisham challenged him — do you eat a man's property without his permission, Abu Sa'id? The answer given is that the man's own conscience is his guide in what he eats. And asked who his close friend was, he named the one in whom his soul found rest and his heart found peace. Such is the nature of deep familiarity, the intimacy of two close companions.

Among the good manners of receiving a guest: if the finest food in the house is available, bring it out and do not stint. A man holding two invitations should not weigh them by the wealth or closeness of those inviting him — he honours the invitation that reached him first, gives priority to the poor over the rich, and does not neglect those absent or far away. An invitation from someone unjust or oppressive should be declined. The purpose of accepting an invitation at all is not to satisfy an appetite; it is to bring joy to the heart of the host. The remaining guest rules are small and exacting: do not enter a house without permission; do not crowd a gathering, since tranquillity and dignity must remain possible in it; do not look around the room as though searching for what is missing; be considerate of the health and comfort of those present.

The host's side is stated with equal precision. The Messenger of God, peace be upon him, said that the last of the guests to depart should not linger, that a person should act graciously toward his guest, and he urged promptness in hospitality. When a woman of considerable wealth offered something, he did not overpraise it; when a woman of lesser means passed by, he did not belittle what she offered. Look to the character, he said, for noble character is a gift from God, granted in full measure to whomever He wills. The refusal to grade hospitality by its cost is itself the lesson.

The host's intention is then examined directly. He must not intend by his hospitality to show off or to boast, but to gladden the hearts of his brothers in faith, to follow the Sunnah in feeding others, and to give particular consideration to those who possess merit and knowledge. He should humble himself rather than seek out only the wealthy, act for the dignity of those he hosts, and keep to the times he appointed, so nobody is left waiting. If the food is ready and most of the guests have arrived, he should not hold the meal for the rest — though a brief wait is acceptable. And he honours his guests with generous speech, acknowledges any shortcoming in his service rather than concealing it, and sees them to the door.

## The lawful and the forbidden

Having settled how a person should eat, the treatise turns to what may be eaten at all — and the tone changes. This is the ruling section, and it is deliberately unadorned.

The prohibition is stated first in its scriptural form. What has been made forbidden is carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and what has been slaughtered for other than God — together with what has been strangled, beaten to death, killed by a fall, gored, or partly devoured by a wild beast, unless one is able to slaughter it properly before it dies, and what has been slaughtered on altars. Two grounds are folded into one list: some things are forbidden by what they are, others by how they died or by what was invoked over them.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad, peace be upon him, was asked about a case and answered that a thing is not forbidden outright, but that it is not permissible for a person who is already full to go on eating it — a ruling about the eater rather than the food. He then set out categories. The first is the grains: wheat, barley and the like. The second is the pulses and plants, of which it is said that they are not by nature a food for human beings, though they may give benefit and energy, and that if there were harm in them they would be forbidden — the condition attached being that they be taken medicinally, or that they be less harmful than the flesh of animals. Then the flesh of camels and sheep is permitted, and likewise the meat of certain wild animals.

The distinctions among animals are given by physical mark rather than by name-list. Among land animals, those with neither tusks nor claws are permitted — deer and rabbits are the examples. Among birds, those without a crop are permitted. From the sea, what has no scales is forbidden. On eggs, a rule of discernment: if the two ends of the egg can be told apart, one indicating an animal that may be eaten and the other one that may not, the egg may be eaten; otherwise it may not. And the insects of the earth — snakes, scorpions and the like — are forbidden.

On the food of the People of the Book there is a difference of opinion, and the position recorded here is restrictive: the flesh of polytheists, Jews, Christians and Magians is forbidden. But necessity is admitted immediately. If a person is driven by severe hunger, he may eat only enough to keep death away.

The consensus of the Imams, peace be upon them, permits the glands, the spleen and the liver, and likewise the turtle and the crab once taken out of their shells. The horse, the mule and the donkey are subject to restrictions. In genuine necessity during a shortage of food, the donkey and the mule may be eaten — but one may not ride them afterwards. And where a person fears for his life and is compelled to eat carrion or something otherwise prohibited, he may eat until he is satisfied; but if the necessity continues, he must then judge what is strictly needed to sustain himself, and hold to that. Necessity relieves the first crisis fully and then tightens.

Wine is treated as doubly harmful, and the only recourse is not to return to it. Then a set of cases about contamination. If a creature that bleeds dies in oil and the oil is liquid, the whole is spoiled; if the oil is solid, what surrounds the dead thing is spoiled and the rest may be eaten. As for food cooked in a defiled vessel, the person has a choice — to eat what was cooked, or to leave it.

Then a ruling of considerable practical breadth: Muslims may eat what is sold in the markets by way of meat, whether or not the slaughterer's background is known, provided it is sold in the general Muslim marketplace — a deliberate release from an impossible investigation. On water: everything sent down from the sky is pure — rainwater, river water, spring water, well water — and all of it is permissible to drink. Fruits and their juices are permissible unless they have been boiled to the point where the liquid changes and becomes intoxicating; and whatever causes intoxication is forbidden. Cheese is noted as widely produced and sold in the markets, with an explanation of how it may be made lawfully.

Slaughter is given its own treatment, and it is the most exacting passage in the section. Eat of what has been slaughtered, and mention the name of God over it. On the animal itself the instruction concerns patience: even if a limb is left hanging after the cut, the animal is fully lawful — but if it is severed and cut apart before life has fully departed, it is forbidden to eat.

The method is described step by step. When a person slaughters an animal, he should treat it gently, lay it on its side on the ground, say in the name of God, God is the Greatest, and cut quickly across the throat with a sharp blade. He should not press down on the animal until it has died. The blood should be allowed to flow while he watches it through its final convulsions until it collapses. Only then should the head be severed and the animal skinned. Every element of that sequence is a restraint on haste, because the animal is still alive through most of it.

Failures are then named. Forgetting to mention the name of God, or mentioning it in a manner that falls short of proper intention, or cutting off the head outright without the proper method — none of these produce a permissible animal. There is no validity to a slaughter performed in ignorance, nor to one done deliberately contrary to the rulings. But an allowance is made for the uncontrollable case: if cattle, buffalo or similar animals become agitated at the time of slaughter and fall into a pit where the throat cut cannot be performed, the animal may be struck and stabbed with a knife wherever one can reach, and it is permissible to eat.

By consensus, the fetus of an animal whose mother has been lawfully slaughtered belongs to the one who slaughtered her, and is itself lawful. A Jew, a Christian or a Magian may slaughter, provided the name of God is mentioned; but whoever omits the name — whether from among them or from among the Muslims — and is accused of having done so deliberately, what he slaughtered is not permissible to eat. Whoever apostatises from the religion of Islam, man or woman, may not perform a lawful slaughter. The blind man, the woman, and the boy who has not yet reached adulthood may all slaughter if they do it properly — and likewise the mute, if he forms the intention and holds the name of God in his heart. What qualifies a slaughterer, in other words, is intention and competence, not status.

The market ruling is then repeated for the wider range of goods: it is acceptable to buy and eat what is sold in Muslim markets — meat, eggs, yogurt, clarified butter, what has been rendered from fat — as well as fish from the sea and locusts, regardless of whether the one who caught them was a Muslim, a Jew, a Christian or a Magian. As for the catch of polytheists, one should be cautious.

Hunting follows. It is forbidden during the sacred months for those in a state of ihram, and permitted outside those times. It is sufficient to eat what trained hunting animals catch — dogs, cheetahs, falcons of various kinds, and other trained birds — as long as the name of God was mentioned when they were sent out. If the prey dies from the predator's impact before the hunter can reach it, and he knows the animal to be lawfully trained, what it killed may be eaten. Where the dog belongs to a non-Muslim but is known to have been sent by a Muslim, a Jew or a Christian, everything it catches is lawful. And if a man kills his quarry with a sword, or wounds it anywhere on its body with an arrow or a spear, it is permissible to eat.

Particular cases are then settled. The zebra's meat is lawful, and distinct from the domesticated donkey. If a hunted animal disappears and then falls from a height or into water and dies, its flesh may be eaten — because it died from the wound inflicted on it, not from the fall or the drowning. But if the hunter does not know whether the wound was what killed it, he should not eat it until he confirms that the killing blow came from an iron-tipped weapon before the animal died. What a polytheist or a Magian has caught should not be eaten. Locusts taken alive by Muslims or Jews are lawful.

The section closes by moving from what enters the body to what surrounds it, opening with the assurance that by the grace of the Lord these things are made lawful. A saying is given about each vessel according to its capacity, running through the ordinary furnishings of a household and the blessing attached to each, and ending on the line that gives the passage its shape: the dignity of the household is its sustenance.

Ja'far ibn Muhammad was asked about a man of standing who gathers young men around him to honour them, so that they protect one another in dignity — and whether there is excess in that. A man said to him that he loved to see people generous toward those who are not coarse, who eat without burdening anyone else, and in whom the mark of reverence can be seen. The Imam's answer reached for the case of Yusuf, a prophet and the son of a prophet, who used to recline on brocade couches gilded with gold, and who sat in the council of Pharaoh in order to bring benefit to people, because the people were weak. The example does what a rule could not: it puts fine surroundings and service to the powerless in the same sentence, and refuses to let the first cancel the second.

The most direct exchange on this comes from Sufyan al-Thawri, who visited Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq and observed that he was wearing fine clothing. Your forebears, he said, wore rough garments — and you wear finer ones than they did. The reply turns the objection around. In former times, God's provision was straitened, and coarse clothing was fitting for straitened circumstances; now God has expanded His provision, and it is proper for those He has blessed with abundance to show the effect of that blessing. Simplicity is a response to conditions, not a permanent uniform; and concealing a blessing is not humility.

The rulings on dress follow. All clean things of the earth are lawful to wear and to move about in. Every kind of animal hide is lawful — whether from an animal whose meat is permitted or forbidden — as are silk, wool and hair. But if the animal's meat is not permissible, its hide may be worn only outside prayer; prayer is not valid in such a garment, for the Messenger of God prayed only in cotton. He used to say: wear cotton, and shroud your dead in it. As for a man who wears silk and prays in it with no intention of boasting or pride, there is nothing wrong in that. And Ja'far ibn Muhammad was once seen sitting on a carpet patterned with figures; when it was mentioned to him, he said the Sunnah permits it.

Finally, adornment. Jewellery for women — necklaces, bracelets and anklets — is a lawful adornment, and women should not abandon it. The one restriction is on display: a woman must not strike the ground with her foot so that the sound of her hidden ornaments is heard. For men, gold ornament and gold rings are not permissible, though gold is permitted for young girls. The pattern that has governed the whole episode holds to the last line. The thing itself is permitted; what is regulated is the announcement of it.

## What this episode lands

Three things have been settled here, and they belong together.

Earning a lawful living has been placed inside religion rather than beside it — ranked with striving in God's path, measured by the lawfulness of the means and by whether the family is spared from having to ask, and guarded by the instruction to learn the rulings before entering the market at all.

The table has been made a discipline. How a person sits, which hand he uses, whether he faults the food, whether he speaks over the meal, how he weighs two invitations against each other, whether he keeps his guests waiting — each has been given a ruling, and every ruling points the same way: away from the self and toward the person across the table.

And the boundary has been drawn, from what may be eaten to how an animal must be killed to what may be worn. The rulings are dense and often dry, but the logic under them is the one this episode opened with: nothing passing through a person's hands or mouth is neutral, and a religion that begins in the marketplace has to end at the butcher's block. What has not yet been touched is the transaction itself — the contract, the price, the debt. That is where the treatise goes next.
