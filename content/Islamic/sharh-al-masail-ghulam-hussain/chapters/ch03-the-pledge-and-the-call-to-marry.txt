# Pledge, Shared Wall, and Marriage

## Where this episode opens

Which loan is safer — the one secured by something the lender can hold in his hand, or the one secured by nothing but a piece of paper? Every instinct answers the first. This treatise answers the second, and then goes further: it says that the pledge held in the hand without a document is the very form the sacred law condemns, and that the paper everyone regards as the weaker security is the thing that actually protects both men. That inversion is the doorway into this stretch of the book, and it turns out to be the same idea running underneath everything else here.

The treatise has already settled how an agreement comes into existence and what happens to property a person holds without owning it. This chapter takes up what was left of that material — the thing handed over as security for a debt, and the wall two households share — and then turns a corner the treatise has been walking toward from the beginning. Having spent chapter after chapter on obligations over things, it begins on obligations between people, and the first of them is marriage. More than half of this stretch is an argument for marrying, addressed to a reader the author plainly suspects of thinking that piety and celibacy have something to do with each other.

Three teachings carry the episode. The first is the doctrine of the pledge, where the whole weight of the ruling shifts off the object and onto the written record, and where a class of pledge in common use is declared forbidden outright. The second is the law of the shared wall — what two neighbours owe each other when a structure between them falls, how jointly held land is divided, and who repairs a roof that is one man's ceiling and another man's floor. The third is the case for marriage: why the text treats abstention as an injury a person does to himself, why it insists that the home is the couple rather than the building, how it reads the verse permitting more than one wife as a restriction dressed as a permission, and what it says about choosing well.

One note before we begin. This portion of the manuscript has come down badly damaged: names appear that connect to nothing, sentences break and resume elsewhere, and in places only the shape of a ruling survives. Where the teaching can be recovered it is given plainly; where it cannot, the gap is left as a gap rather than filled with invention.

## The pledge and the written record

The section opens where the treatise usually opens, with practice rather than theory. A group of guests was mentioned to the Messenger of God one day, and he sent his servants to buy flour from a certain Jewish man of the town. The man had said earlier: give me something to hold in pawn. And something was given. Placing this narration first is not incidental. The pledge is not a concession wrung out of the law by hard commercial reality; it is a transaction the Prophet himself entered, with a man outside his own community, over an ordinary household need. Whatever restrictions follow are restrictions inside a practice already established as lawful.

Our master Judge al-Nu'man, may God sanctify his exalted soul, gives the definition the chapter turns on. The pledge is called the received pledge — so the thing must actually be received, taken into the hand of the one who holds it, and it is not otherwise. A pledge that has not passed into the holder's possession is not a defective pledge. It is not a pledge.

[Editorial note: this paragraph is recovered from the Urdu edition of this treatise, pages 37–39 and 43–47, and carries no corresponding citation in the Arabic source's own apparatus.] Beside the pledge stands a second form of security, distinct from it rather than a variety of it: the guarantee, where a third party takes on responsibility for another man's debt with the creditor's consent. Once that consent is given, the creditor may pursue either the original debtor or the guarantor for what is owed — the two liabilities stand beside each other rather than one replacing the other. The treatise works the point through a case: if Zayd guarantees Amr's debt to Bakr, and Amr later repays Zayd directly, Zayd's guarantee to Bakr is unaffected. What passed between Zayd and Amr settles what Amr owed to Zayd; it says nothing about what Zayd promised to Bakr. The two debts remain distinct, and discharging one does not touch the other.

Then comes an unusually sharp complaint, and it is aimed at the jurists. The rulings on mortgage and pledging, the treatise says, had become confused in their minds. They failed to distinguish between what is not a legally recognised mortgage and what is a coercive or merely general one. They did not see that the legal mortgage is a specific category sitting inside the much broader category of the absolute mortgage — that the arrangement the books of jurisprudence discuss is one species of a wider practice, not the whole of it. And so, the charge continues, they never worked through the pledges properly, and never recognised that not all pledges are legally recognised at all.

The complaint is not that the jurists got a rule wrong. It is that they mistook a subset for the whole set, and therefore had nothing to say about the pledges most people were actually making. Correct that error and the distinction at the heart of the teaching appears immediately.

There are two forms of mortgage. The blameworthy one — the forbidden one — is where a man pledges something of his own when taking a loan, and no written record is made of the transaction at all. The treatise names the outcome it fears with unusual bluntness. The pledged thing is now in the creditor's possession. Nothing on paper records what was lent or against what. And when the moment comes to acknowledge the debt, it can be denied. The man who handed over his security to obtain a loan can be told that no loan was ever made, and he has no instrument with which to answer.

The second form is the one where a document is written for the loan the mortgagee advances, and the pledge is recorded in that writing as security. Here the treatise makes a remark that is easy to read past. What distinguishes the documented arrangement from the undocumented one, it says, is nothing but an increase in formality. Nothing has changed about the goods, the debt, or the intentions of the parties. All that has been added is a piece of paper — and that addition is what moves the transaction from the forbidden category into the lawful one.

From that single move everything else follows. Where the pledge comes after the writing, recourse is available to the mortgagee if he needs it, because the written record makes plain the very thing the undocumented pledge left obscure. And the converse is put with equal force: if a man pledges after writing and then destroys the document, his position collapses back to that of someone merely holding a thing in another man's grip. The document, not the object, was what he actually had.

There is a further consequence, and it is the strongest argument the chapter makes. Suppose the mortgaged thing is damaged or destroyed while it is held. If the debt rests on the object, it is now in ruins along with the object and the parties are back to argument. But where the loan is documented, the destruction of the pledged thing diminishes nothing: the deed records what was lent, and goes on recording it after the goods are gone. The writing survives the object it describes. That is the whole case for formality in a single ruling.

The treatise also permits what a stricter instinct would forbid. Once the writing exists, it is permissible to benefit from the mortgaged property — to rent it out, to live in it — as is common, the text says, among people in civilised lands. The procedure it sketches is recognisably a modern one. A man who wishes to arrange a pledge first writes a document for the loan he is about to take. He acknowledges the arrangement openly, may appoint a witness, and confesses before them that he received a stated sum at a stated time. The loan and the mortgage then cover the thing for a period, or the courts hold it securely. And if the other party later denies the arrangement, the judge compels him and forces the repayment. The instrument, the witness, and the court replace the grip of a hand on an object.

The prevalence of writing, the treatise concludes, is the foundation of the mortgage — it is what carries the mortgage to its proper term. And what the sacred law prevented is not this arrangement at all. Both the mortgage and the pledge aim at the same thing, ease of payment; and where writing became prevalent, the means of trade widened and losses in acquisition fell, because the terms now bind both sides. That is a claim about commerce, not merely about religion, and the author makes it as one.

[Editorial note: this paragraph is recovered from the Urdu edition of this treatise, pages 43–47, and carries no corresponding citation in the Arabic source's own apparatus.] The insistence on writing is not the author's own preference dressed up as law. He grounds it directly in scripture, in the verse instructing believers that when they contract a debt for a fixed term, they are to write it down. A debt deferred to a future date, in other words, is to be put in writing and witnessed as a matter of course, not merely as a precaution for the unusually careful. And the same verse's protection is carried over intact: neither the scribe who records the debt nor the witnesses who attest it should be made to suffer harm on account of the part they have played. The document exists to protect the two parties to the debt — not to expose the men who helped bring it into being.

The rulings on what may be pledged come from Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, who said that it is permissible to pledge everything pure, from places and lands — markets, shops, sweets, clothing, animals, and grain in abundance — to be held in the grip of the one who advances the loan. The list is deliberately wide. Nearly anything a working household possesses can stand as security, provided it is not itself of the forbidden kind.

But the same narration guards the other side, and here the text holds a tension it does not resolve. The pledged thing belongs to its owner. It is therefore not permissible for the holder to benefit from it in any form, to press it into use, or to make free with it while the debt runs against the pledgor. Set that beside the earlier permission to rent out and live in a mortgaged property once the writing exists, and the two do not sit flush. The treatise does not reconcile them. What can honestly be said is that the permission is attached to the documented arrangement and the prohibition to the bare holding of another man's property — the same distinction that runs through the whole section, applied now to what the holder may do rather than to whether the pledge is valid at all.

On disputes the ruling is short and one-sided. The holder should not accept a statement without evidence, even if the amount differs by a single dirham; and where the accounts conflict, the saying is the saying of the pledgee — the word of the man holding the security is taken. Against that, one protection for the debtor: when the mortgage falls due, the pledgor cannot be pressed beyond his capacity. The section closes on a phrase carried over from scripture as a warning to the creditor who would push further: God does not love the aggressors.

The doctrine of the loan itself, already settled earlier in the treatise, is not reopened here. What this section adds is the mechanism by which an honest loan is made provable — and its verdict is that the mechanism, not the merchandise, is what the law was protecting all along.

## The shared wall and divided land

The next stretch begins from a principle rather than a case: a saying on harm and harm, which is the standard the whole section applies. Not ownership. Not who paid for what. Harm — whether an act injures a neighbour, and whether a refusal to act injures him too.

The governing case is a wall. Two walls belonging to two houses of different owners have fallen, and one owner refuses to build a drainage channel from which his neighbour benefits. If leaving it unbuilt would harm the neighbour, the judge is obliged to compel him to build it. The ruling is stronger than it first appears: a man is being compelled, by the court, to construct something on his own property, for the benefit of the man next door, on the ground that not building it is itself an injury. The right at stake is not the neighbour's right to be left alone. It is his right not to be damaged by another man's inaction.

Where the wall itself is broken, the owner may call someone to partner with him in rebuilding it. The manuscript is thin here, but the shape of three outcomes survives. He builds it in partnership, each party bearing half the cost and half the responsibility. Or another builds it against the harm, and in that case places his beam in it — the man who paid for the structure acquires the right to use it. Or the matter is settled along with the road and the other shared things, by common consent. The road belongs to the same family of problems: things no single household owns and every household needs.

From the shared structure the section moves to the shared boundary, and here the concern becomes privacy. Half of a shared wall belongs to one party — and no one may open a door in the shared wall of a market, because a door in that wall is a deceptive means of looking inside a house. The reasoning is worth noting. Nothing has been taken and no ground has been trespassed. The injury is entirely a matter of sight: an opening that lets one household see into another has removed something from that household even though it removed no property. The same instinct produces the ruling that a barrier may be placed between the sun and a neighbour's house, and the qualification that a neighbour's agreement does not override the claim of the one who was there first.

On doorways the rule turns on whose ground is being used. It is permissible to open one of a house's exits onto a public thoroughfare, since the street belongs to everyone. But where the way passes through what belongs to the people themselves rather than to the public, a man has no right to alter it without permission. The passage is damaged at exactly this point, but the distinction — public road, permission not required; private way, permission required — comes through intact.

The rulings on dividing jointly held land come from Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, who said that division is of three types.

The first is the division ordered by the judge when the partners are already in agreement and each is assigned his portion, with no oath required from anyone. The agreement then stands for them and for their heirs. This is the division by evaluation, and the detail about heirs is the substantive part: a division reached amicably is not a private arrangement between two living men but a settlement of the property itself, which the next generation inherits already divided.

The second is the division of experiment, where the divider proceeds by planting, in cases where the land can be divided into comparable parts. Where the ground is uniform, it can be split by measure and the parts tested against each other.

The third is division by valuation, used when the parts are not comparable — when things differ and must be distinguished, and one must account for what the parts actually require in order to be usable. The requirements the treatise lists are the practical heart of the ruling: pathways, doors, the street shared between the partners, the well, the courtyard. A share of land that cannot be reached, entered, or watered is not a share of anything, so the valuation must price the access along with the ground.

The section ends with the case that most obviously touches ordinary life: the division of a building into an upper and a lower storey, and the question of who is responsible when the structure between them needs repair. The answer given is that the responsibility belongs to the owner of the lower portion. The reasoning offered is that the benefit here is like the ground itself — the upper owner stands on it and benefits from it, in the way any owner benefits from the earth beneath his house, which is to say that the ceiling of the lower dwelling is a structural part of the lower dwelling and not a convenience the upper owner has borrowed. And should the owner of the upper level come to him about it, the treatise adds a note of temper rather than law: he should think well of the man, and need not fear that the ruling is a burden laid unfairly on him. The passage that follows, on ceilings at the time of sale and distribution, has not survived in usable form, and nothing is supplied in its place.

What holds this section together is that none of these rulings is settled by asking who owns the thing. The wall, the drainage channel, the doorway, the shared street, the well, the ceiling — in each case the answer is reached by asking who is harmed and who benefits. That is the same question the treatise is about to ask about people who share not a wall but a life.

## The call to marry

The turn is made with a verse: and among His signs is that He created for you from yourselves spouses, that you may dwell in tranquility with them, and He placed between you affection and mercy — indeed in that are signs for a people who give thought. Beside it stands a saying of the Prophet: whoever loves to meet God pure and complete, let him marry. And the plainest statement of all, which the rest of the argument keeps returning to: it is of my Sunnah to marry.

A narration from 'Ali is then quoted on the practice of the Prophet's companions and on the Prophet's own manners — the list that survives runs to shaving, trimming, kindness, and generosity, and the passage around it has broken down. What comes through the damage is the connection the treatise wants drawn between marriage and ordinary human decency. Then a line stated as a measure: the more the servant believes, the more he loved his wife. Affection for a wife is offered as an index of faith, not as a distraction from it.

From here the argument becomes polemical, because the author is answering someone. Whoever abandons marriage out of fear of the hardship a family brings has wronged himself — because God promised His worshippers that the poor would be enriched from His bounty, and God is Vast and Knowing. And the principle behind that: there is no monasticism in Islam. Celibacy is forbidden, and forbidden for men and women alike. The person imagined here is not a libertine. He is a scrupulous man who has calculated that he cannot afford a household and has concluded that abstaining is the safer, humbler course. The treatise tells him he has committed an offence against himself and doubted a promise.

The general argument runs through the ordering of appetite. A person is pulled in conflicting directions by forces that operate on him whether he consents or not — hunger, thirst, sleep, wakefulness, and others of the same kind. Desire is one of these, appearing at puberty as the others appear in their season. The sacred law does not pretend the impulse is absent and does not undertake to abolish it. It calls for its regulation, and in that a man follows the path of the prophets, who were not men without appetites but men whose appetites were placed under order. Man was created to extend life by nature, the text adds, and not to spend all his hours in transactions.

Then the practical case. A man who leaves his house each day to exert his strength cannot also keep the house he has left. Someone must hold the household and its affairs in order — the provisioning, the ordering of the day, the care of the children and the accounting of what they need. And the treatise is blunt that this is not a menial supplement to the man's work but a distinct competence: the house is not sound without the woman.

What follows is the passage that carries the section's real weight. The real house, it says, is the school of feeling — the place of upbringing, the place of moral ease, where children are first raised. The teacher there is the mother, and especially the devoted mother, and it is in that house that children receive the highest human lessons of education. The house is with the woman and the woman is with the household. So it is not the intention of the devout Muslim to abandon marriage.

The treatise then insists on a distinction it will not let go of. The real home is the man and the woman together; they are the ones responsible for it. The rented house and its provisions are not the purpose and not the aim. There is the figurative home — a building, a lease, the visible apparatus of domestic life — and there is the real home, a relation between two people that exists whether they live under a tree in the desert or in a high place of hardship. The building was never the thing.

The argument for chastity is made through an etymology, and the derivation is made to carry the doctrine. The word for immunity comes from the root meaning fortress and fortification; in the language, a fortress is what secures a good, as though it immunised the body against disease. The intention of marriage, then, is not what the ascetics claim, that chastity consists in eliminating desire like an animal. It is fortification. And the diseases it guards against are diseases of character rather than of the body: bad temperament, hardness of nature, agitation, inertia. Against them, marriage is said to increase composure, tranquility, forbearance, restraint, patience, and gentleness — which the treatise calls the origin of sound conduct and of life itself.

Marriage is then urged along two paths, one religious and one worldly.

The religious argument is aimed at the ascetic, since he is the hardest case: the man walking the path to the Hereafter, absorbed in longing for it, who has become detached from worldly attachments and concludes that a wife is the first of them. 'Uthman ibn Maz'un is the example. He came to the Messenger of God, peace be upon him, having complained of his condition and wishing to renounce the world entirely. The reply was flat: do not do it, for I dislike that. And a reassurance about provision followed — that if he asked God for his daily sustenance, God would grant it. The rest of the exchange is fragmentary, but its close is preserved: the Prophet embraced him warmly and said, do not abandon my way; whoever desires my way, the angels will intercede for him on the Day of Resurrection — and whoever turns away from my path, that turning away is its own answer.

The same case comes again through our master Ja'far al-Sadiq, asked about a man in whom the fear of God had grown so great that he abandoned women and good food and would not raise his head toward the sky out of reverence. The answer takes the man's piety apart point by point. Abandoning women is something the Messenger of God never did. As for leaving good food — the Prophet of God ate meat and honey. And as for not raising the head: true humility is in the heart. Then the question that ends the matter: who was more God-fearing than the Messenger of God? Yet he did not abandon marriage, and it was never forbidden to him. The scriptural line is quoted to close it — the Messenger of God is a fine example for those who hope in God and the Last Day. The argument is not that the ascetic aims too high. It is that he has invented a standard the Prophet did not meet, which means it is not a standard.

The worldly argument answers the fear of poverty, and it concedes the fear before answering it. Narrations from Mawlana Ja'far al-Sadiq are cited on the lack of a bride-price. A man from a district who teaches well but has no provision should turn to God — that the days are from Him, that He does not wish the righteous among His servants to remain poor, that God will enrich them from His bounty. And for the man who has not yet found a wife and is waiting: let him live in chastity and restraint until he finds what he hopes for, and there is nothing wrong in that. The treatise is not counselling recklessness. It is drawing a line between waiting and renouncing.

To this it adds a plain economic exhortation. Self-sufficiency, and not depending on others for one's livelihood, is worthy of a Muslim — and in the Arabian Peninsula there is no disgrace in seeking provision through agriculture. Even in a good year, when a man's house is full, he and his children must still exert themselves in cultivation from sowing to harvest. They must not be idle. A household is being described as a working unit, and the argument for marriage has quietly become an argument about labour.

Then the chapter takes up the license for more than one wife, and it takes it up defensively, because critics are in view. Some, it says, use this door to criticise Islam — claiming that the Qur'an cheapens desire, that the age of such marriage has passed, that husbands are not necessary.

The reply begins from circumstance. In conditions of tribal rivalry, doctrinal division, and enmity carried from one generation to the next, killing a man was counted as no more than killing an animal, with no thought for the rights of the vulnerable; and when war broke out and men fell on both sides, no one remained to protect the women and orphans from whoever chose to dominate and plunder them. The author then makes the argument contemporary, and this is where his own century enters the book. He points to the beginning of the twentieth century and to the war in South Africa between the Boers, in which multitudes of men, leaders and prominent figures fell, and the men on both sides were reduced to a fraction. He then turns to census figures from the European states of his own day. Among the French, by his reckoning, women exceeded men by twenty-three thousand four hundred; in Germany, by eighty-seven thousand six hundred and eight; and in another year of counting, by eighty-two thousand six hundred and nineteen, with a further figure of some twenty-four thousand for which no policy or law has produced any answer at all. These are the author's own numbers from his own period and are reproduced as he gives them.

His question to the critics is then simple: what do you propose for those women? Either it must be said that they do not want what men want, or the consequences must be faced — and he points to what he takes to be the visible result, houses ruined and people harmed, occurring every year in those same kingdoms. The polygamy of the sacred law is being defended not as an indulgence but as a provision for a demographic emergency, and he insists it is not restricted to wartime, since the same imbalance arises from many causes.

Having made that defence, the treatise turns to the verse itself and reads it with real care. Look at it fairly, it says, and it is not what the critics think. It is one of the highest degrees of eloquence, because it combines restriction with permission — the license granted at the outset and its limitation supplied immediately after. If from among women you take two, three, or four, the permissibility is closed off by what follows: the condition of treating them equitably, which reduces the man to one. And if you fear you will not be just, then one only — or what your right hand possesses — so that you will not incline wrongfully. So give equitably and fear God; He is Ever-Forgiving. The verse is from Surah al-Nisa'. The reading being urged is that the sentence permits with one clause and withdraws with the next, and that a man who reads only the first half has not read the verse.

Then the image the section is built on. This license is a remedy for improvement and a mercy for a pressing need. And medicine is not taken for its own sake; it is taken so that a condition does not worsen, and it is not for healthy people whom no necessity compels. So the man who marries more than one wife without necessity is like a man dosing himself while in perfect health. The permission has not been denied him. It has been described to him as a treatment, which is a way of asking whether he is ill.

Our master Judge al-Nu'man, may God have mercy on his soul, is quoted to the same effect: the merit in marrying more than one belongs to those who are capable of providing for them and who possess the power of justice. It does no good to men who cannot be fair among their children and cannot avoid doing them harm. Where a man does have several wives, he must fulfil each one's right, treat each one well, and be good to each of them. If they can live in one house, that is permitted to him; otherwise the practice to follow is that of the Messenger of God, peace be upon him, and they are housed separately.

[Editorial note: this paragraph is recovered from the Urdu edition of this treatise, page 80, and carries no corresponding citation in the Arabic source's own apparatus.] Four categories of marriage are named as unlawful outright, whatever provision or capacity the man in question has: marrying a woman who has been divorced three times and has not yet completed an intervening marriage to another man, which the law requires before she may return to her first husband; marrying a woman already known, at the time of the marriage, to be forbidden to him; marrying a woman still within her waiting period after widowhood; and marrying a woman while the man himself is in the state of pilgrim consecration. Each rests on the same principle as the shared wall and the divided field, met earlier in this same chapter: a condition unmet at the outset is not repaired by anything that follows it.

The counsel on remarriage is emphatic. A man whose wife has died should not sit on afterward alone in his house; he must marry again. There is good in this for the people and for the community, and it increases the bonds between them — and even if there are children on one side or the other, he should not refrain on that account. Let him look to the widows among the people and marry one of them when he finds a woman who suits him, because sitting without the protection of a marriage is an evident deficiency. An account follows of a widow with sons who remarried, of a jealous objection raised against the match, and of the matter being brought to the Prophet, who reassured the household — the objector afterward apologising and admitting the worry had come from unfamiliarity rather than from any fault. The manuscript here is badly broken and the names in it no longer connect to one another, so the ruling is given and the story is not reconstructed.

On the conduct of the marriage itself, bringing in resources from outside belongs to the man's side of the arrangement and the ordering of the household to the woman's, and each of them owes the other things which must be done and not merely intended. And then the recommendation that raises the whole discussion above a division of chores: because love grows between them, it is wise that they consult each other on matters belonging to each — so that even the figurative home becomes a paradise, and they two its inhabitants.

That word is not chosen loosely, and the treatise proves it by quoting the address to Adam: enter, O Adam, and settle — you and your wife — in paradise, and eat from it wherever you wish, and do not approach this tree. The reading given is unusual and worth stating carefully. The forbidden tree is taken as a figure for whatever belongs to jealousy: let neither of you approach the thing that would make the other jealous, lest you be among the wrongdoers. The garden is the marriage, the prohibition is mutual protectiveness, and the couple are being told that they hold their paradise on exactly one condition.

On children, the counsel is that whoever has them should look after them as a wise guardian looks after family wealth — keeping them close, and guaranteeing good character before anything else. The passage detailing this is damaged and is not pressed further here.

The chapter closes on the choosing, and it is Judge al-Nu'man who is quoted. The governing image is an ornament: when the wife is a necklace for the husband, he must choose his necklace well. And the warning that gives the image its force is that the choice is harder than it looks, because the differences do not show. Women may appear alike in body and limb; but look within them, observe the differences of their families and their upbringing, and between them lies a vast distance in morals, in temperament, in conduct, in pride, and in disposition. A verse of poetry is quoted on two women who might be taken for equals: the fingers differ in rank like the rungs of a ladder, so that the discerning eye finds the difference many times over. The likeness is on the surface; the ranking is real.

The Prophet's own instruction is given as the standard — choose for your seed. She should be suited to bearing and raising children, well-mannered, brought up with the finest discipline, combining purity with beauty, and these qualities should come first, in that order. Beauty is not excluded. It is placed after character, and the ordering is the ruling.

The section ends on a saying that gathers the whole chapter into a list: five things bring happiness — a good wife, good children, good neighbours, a livelihood in one's own land, and holding to the way of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him.

## What this episode lands

Three things have been settled here.

The pledge was moved off the object and onto the paper. The form of security everyone trusts — a valuable thing surrendered into a creditor's hands — is the form the treatise condemns, because it leaves the debtor with nothing to answer a denial with. The form that looks weaker survives fire, theft, and the destruction of the goods themselves, because a written record goes on saying what was lent after the pledged thing has ceased to exist. The addition that makes the difference is, in the text's own accounting, nothing but an increase in formality, and that modest phrase carries the entire ruling.

The law of the shared wall was decided by harm rather than by title. A man can be compelled by a judge to build on his own land because not building injures his neighbour. A door cannot be opened in a shared wall, though nothing would pass through it but a line of sight. Land is divided by what makes a portion usable — the path, the door, the well, the courtyard — and not by area, because a share nobody can reach is not a share. And the ceiling between two households belongs to the man below, who stands under it, rather than to the man above, who stands on it.

And the case for marriage was made as a case against a particular kind of piety. The opponent here is not the man who wants too much but the man who has decided that wanting less is holier, and the answer is that the Prophet did not meet the standard such a man has invented. From there the argument becomes concrete: the household as a working unit and the home as a school; the home relocated from the building to the couple; the license for more than one wife read as a permission that closes itself in its second clause, prescribed like a medicine and therefore addressed only to the ill; and the choosing governed by an ordering — character first, beauty afterward — because the differences that matter are the ones that do not show.

What the treatise has not yet done is say how a marriage is actually made. It has argued that a man should marry and told him what to look for; it has said nothing about who may marry whom, on what terms the agreement is struck, what each party owes the other once it is, or which relations place a woman permanently out of reach. Having spent this stretch urging the reader toward the door, the book turns next to the contract on the other side of it.
