# Sale, Debt, and the Trust

## Where this episode picks up

Why would a book of religion care whether you sold a fish that was still swimming? Not whether you cheated anyone — whether the fish was in the water at the moment you named your price. That is the level at which this stretch of the treatise operates, and the strangeness of it is the doorway in. The previous episode established that lawful acquisition and lawful consumption are religious matters rather than merely practical ones; this one takes the next step and asks what actually happens in the moment two people agree on something. It turns out that a great deal of religion is decided in that moment, and that most of it is decided before anyone has had the chance to be dishonest.

This is a working lawyer's material, and it covers the transaction in all its forms: the sale, the loan, the debt that cannot be repaid, the rented house, the hired animal, the borrowed tool, the money left with a friend for safekeeping. What holds it together is worth naming at the outset, because the rulings are dense and the thread is easy to lose among them. A transaction is valid when nothing in it is left indeterminate, and a transaction is righteous when the stronger party declines to press his advantage. The first idea governs whether an agreement exists at all. The second governs what kind of person you are inside an agreement that does.

Three teachings carry the episode. The first is the anatomy of a sale — its three pillars, and the five conditions deciding whether the thing being sold can be sold at all. The second is the doctrine of the loan, where the treatise becomes unexpectedly severe: it divides all lending into two kinds by intention alone, forbids the lender of the second kind any benefit whatever, and reads the scriptural war against usury as a demand for the cancellation of debt rather than merely its regulation. The third is everything else people do with property they do not own outright — hire it, share it, borrow it, leave it with someone — where the governing category stops being ownership and becomes trust.

One warning about the text itself. The further this material travels from the chapter on sale, the more damaged its transmission becomes, until whole passages survive only in fragments. Where the teaching is recoverable it is given; where it is not, it is left alone rather than repaired with invention.

## The three pillars of sale

The section on sale opens by quoting the command: do not consume one another's wealth wrongfully, unless it be through trade conducted with mutual consent. That single sentence carries the whole legal architecture that follows. Wealth moves between people in exactly one legitimate way — by an exchange both parties actually agreed to — and everything else is consumption of what is not yours. Beside it stands a prophetic prohibition of three practices that were evidently common enough to name: the sale of pebbles, deceptive transactions, and sales involving uncertainty. That last category is the one to keep in mind, because nearly every technical rule that follows is a defence against it.

A valid sale, the treatise says, stands on three pillars. The first concerns the contracting parties themselves. A buyer must not be a minor, nor insane, nor blind — none of these, in the text's own phrase, is regarded as a serious transactor. The reasoning is not contempt. It is that a contract binds a person to consequences he must be capable of grasping when he consents, and where that capacity is absent or the object cannot be examined, consent is not what it appears to be.

The second pillar concerns the object of the contract — the thing being sold — which must be capable of passing from the possession of the one who releases it into the possession of the other. Five conditions attach to it, and they are the substance of the chapter.

The first condition is that the thing sold must not be something whose consumption is forbidden. But the treatise immediately widens the gate: if the thing can be put to some use other than consumption, buying and selling it is permitted. The examples are vivid and practical. A dog or a cheetah kept for guarding, hunting, or tracking may be sold, because the animal is being bought for what it does, not for what it is. The dung of animals whose meat is not eaten may be sold for fuel or for the treatment of wounds. Elephants may be sold for riding. Snakes and scorpions may be sold for use in medicine. Livestock and birds whose meat is eaten may be sold, whether they have come to their owner on their own or been acquired; parrots and peacocks and creatures of that kind may be sold. Then the exception that clarifies the principle: a dog kept for no purpose whatsoever may not be sold. Purpose is the whole test. Where there is a use, there is a price; where there is no use, the transaction has no object.

The same logic is then pushed to its most uncomfortable case: idols may be sold if they are reshaped into something of sound use, or kept as relics — once the thing has ceased to be what made it prohibited. Impurities may be sold for use in agriculture. Throughout, the object is assessed for its function rather than its category, which is a far more flexible instrument than a list of forbidden goods.

The second condition is ownership: both the object being sold and the person disposing of it must stand in their rightful relation — the goods must belong to their owner, or the owner must have granted permission. A sale by someone with no title to what he is selling is not a bad sale; it is not a sale.

Here the treatise pauses over a division that matters for everything downstream: property is of two kinds, and legal liability attaches differently to each. Movable property — Persian goods, books, and the like — can be carried from place to place. Immovable property, such as land, cannot be moved from where it lies. The distinction is not academic, because it determines what taking possession actually means. For movable property, the seller's right passes to the buyer through inspection, testing, and receipt: you look at it, you try it, you take it. For immovable property, possession transfers when the buyer clears it of whatever remained there at the time of sale — the house becomes yours when what was in it is out of it. And from this follows a hard rule: anything bought or sold before possession has been taken is an invalid sale. You cannot trade on a thing that has not yet arrived in your hands.

The fourth condition is that the object of sale must be capable of actual delivery, and if it cannot be delivered, the sale is void. Now the striking examples arrive. It is not valid to sell air. Nor fish still in the water. Nor a fetus still in the womb. Nor grass still growing in a field. Nor something merely depicted on an animal's back. Nor a defective limb. The treatise then gives the reason binding these together, and it is a genuinely elegant piece of legal thinking: each of these is a thing that cannot be clearly separated from what is not being sold, and so it remains indeterminate. The fish is not distinguishable from the pond. The grass is not distinguishable from the field. Sell one, and neither party can say afterwards what changed hands.

An exception is drawn where the impure and the pure are pledged together: the impure part is set aside and the pure part sold — as with milk in an udder sold together with milk already drawn. Even there, complete delivery may be impossible where a pledge or a suspended transaction is involved, because the term of a pledge keeps the property in the creditor's hands and an arrested asset stays with whoever holds it, which prevents the seller from delivering what he promised.

The fifth condition follows directly: the object of sale must be specifically identified and described. If it is not identified by inspection, then a seller who says take one animal from this herd, or ten cubits from this land, or any garment you like from among these garments, has made a corrupt transaction — for precisely the same reason the fish in the water was disallowed. Which animal? Which ten cubits? The seller must describe the object with enough detail that the buyer knows what he is receiving. A sale is valid when its object is clearly specified by description, characterising the thing and teaching the buyer about it where it is not immediately apparent, so that the buyer's mind is at rest concerning it. And when the object is plainly present at the moment of contract, the sale is valid and permitted without further ceremony.

The third pillar is the wording — the offer and the acceptance by which the contract comes into being and the object passes from one party to the other. It may take many forms: a pledge, an exchange, an outright sale, or other arrangements, all of them governed by whether the offer and the acceptance actually correspond. The seller says, I sell you this, and the buyer agrees — whether in explicit terms of sale or in figurative expressions that are accepted as conveying the meaning. And then the rule that gives the pillar its teeth: if the terms are ambiguous or the meaning obscure, the sale is invalid. A form of words that leaves either party able to claim something different afterwards has not concluded anything. If the seller wishes to reserve the right of option in the sale, he may raise the price accordingly; that option is his to sell.

The remaining rulings in the section are the practical residue, and several of them are memorable. Dates, raisins, grapes, and other fruits may be sold while still on the vine or the tree, until they wither. Something may lawfully be bought from a thief, an oppressor, or a betrayer, provided one knows with certainty that the particular item in question did not come from theft, oppression, or betrayal — a ruling about the goods rather than the character of the man selling them.

The Messenger of God, may God's blessings and peace be upon him, forbade outbidding: artificially inflating the price of a commodity by feigning interest in it with no genuine intention to buy, while someone else actually wants to purchase it. No one should outbid the offer of a fellow Muslim. Note how precisely this is aimed. The bidder has not lied about the goods and has not broken any term. He has corrupted the price by pretending to be a party to a transaction he never intended to enter, and the market's own instrument is turned into the weapon.

On the option period: where a buyer comes within an agreed period to make an offer, any increase in the goods' value during that period belongs to the owner and any decrease falls on the buyer. On defects: the buyer is expected to take the item with its defect, but once he becomes aware of it he may return it if he chooses. And on disputes — the buyer says he has paid, the seller denies it — people rely on documentation. Where documentation is absent, the treatise offers a rule of thumb: if the item is in the buyer's possession, the buyer's word is taken; if there is a means by which the seller can be cleared, the seller's word is taken; and neither need swear an oath so long as neither has been hasty. For transactions people ordinarily commit to writing — a horse, a piece of land — the ruling is well settled in the jurisprudence.

## Riba and the two loans

The chapter on lending opens in a completely different register, and the shift is abrupt enough to notice. The section on sale was technical throughout — conditions, pillars, validity. The section on loans begins with God described as the Lord of charity, the One who grants that a loan made with the sincere intention of earning reward in the Hereafter is itself charity. We have moved from what makes an agreement binding to what is in the lender's heart, and from here intention does all the legal work.

The verse quoted is the pivot: whatever you give as riba so that it may increase through other people's wealth does not increase with God; but whatever you give in zakah, seeking God's face, it is they who will receive manifold reward. The one who lends seeking the benefit of the Hereafter has it multiplied for him in the next life. And the principle is restated in the language of farming — whoever wishes to cultivate the harvest of the Hereafter will have it increased for him, and whoever wishes to cultivate the harvest of this world will be given of it, though he will have no share in the reward of the world to come. Whatever God accepts with goodwill is doubled in the recompense of the next life.

Then comes the definition, and it is more careful than the reputation of the subject would suggest. Riba means increase. But not every increase is forbidden — the increase that accrues in ordinary sales and comparable dealings is not what the prohibition is about. Riba in the technical sense of the sacred law refers specifically to the increase that accrues on a loan when the lender's intention is to gain a worldly benefit, and when the principal returns to him along with that increase. The blessings of God descend only upon the one whose intention in lending is sound.

[Editorial note: the primary Arabic source is damaged from this point through the remainder of the loan doctrine; the following material has been recovered by cross-reference to a secondary Urdu edition of this same treatise and carries no citation in the primary source's own apparatus.] A hadith is cited in the same register: every loan that draws a benefit to the lender is riba. The gloss the treatise attaches to it is careful rather than sweeping — what is forbidden is a worldly benefit made a condition of the loan, something stipulated in advance and owed in return for the lending. A borrower's gratitude is not that. Nor is a gift he chooses, unprompted, to return afterward, with no such condition ever having been set. The line the treatise draws is between what was demanded and what was freely given.

From this the whole structure follows. A loan falls into one of two categories: one where the lender's purpose is worldly benefit, and one where the lender's purpose is the reward of the Hereafter. Each category depends entirely on the intention of the one who lends — for actions are judged by their intentions — and both fall within the domain of legally regulated transactions. That last clause matters, and it is easy to skim past. The worldly loan is not thereby placed outside the law. It is a recognised category with its own rulings.

Those rulings are, in a word, lenient toward the borrower. Where a loan has been extended for worldly benefit and the borrower is in a position to repay, the lender has the right to reduce the repayment or excuse it, to extend the time allowed while reducing the principal slightly or substantially, or to forgive the debt entirely — and there is reward in that. Note the direction every one of those options runs. The lender's rights, as the treatise enumerates them, are all rights to take less.

The loan intended for the reward of the Hereafter is treated with a precision that borders on the arithmetical. The reward is eighteen-fold for maintaining the bond of brotherhood with a fellow believer, and twenty-four-fold for maintaining the bond of kinship — the latter extending the lender's own lifespan. And with the reward comes the severity: the owner of such a loan has no right to benefit from the principal in any way, whether small or great, nor to impose any condition that burdens the principal. The reason given is precise and worth holding onto, because it explains the whole prohibition. God has designated every loan made purely for His sake as an act of devotion. Once a thing is an act of devotion, every worldly benefit derived from it is forbidden — not because profit is dirty, but because you cannot be paid twice, once by God and once by your brother, for the same act.

Then the treatise turns to the case that gives the doctrine its human weight: the borrower who cannot pay when the time comes. Sometimes, even when the term has arrived and the borrower is in hardship, the lender should cancel the arrangement for that period, accept the principal as agreed, and — if the borrower is genuinely and visibly in difficulty — grant him more time and even remit the principal itself. Two names are attached to this as precedent, 'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib and 'Uthman ibn 'Affan. The scene the text then sketches survives only in fragments, but its shape is clear enough: a creditor is told that taking his right in full will ruin a household of children, and is offered half now against a doubled share of the following season's harvest, with the other half left to the family. When the term arrived and repayment was demanded, the matter reached the Messenger of God, may God's blessings and peace be upon him.

[Editorial note: recovered from the secondary Urdu edition, filling a gap where the primary source survives only in fragments here; it has no primary-source citation of its own.] The clearer shape of the episode, as that edition preserves it, is this: the verse below was revealed to address Muslim creditors who were still holding riba debts contracted in the pagan period, and the Prophet, may God's blessings and peace be upon him, instructed them to take only their principal and forgive the rest. 'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib and 'Uthman ibn 'Affan are named as men who were ordered to write off exactly such interest owed to them from before Islam.

And the revelation that came is the passage the whole section is built to reach: O you who believe, fear God and give up whatever riba remains outstanding, if you are truly believers. If you do not, then be warned of war from God and His Messenger. But if you repent, you shall have your principal — do no wrong and you will not be wronged. If the debtor is in hardship, grant him time until he is at ease; and if you remit it as charity, that is better for you, if only you knew.

The treatise's commentary on that verse is the theological centre of this episode. If you abandon the dispute and return only the principal of the loan, it says, you will not be wronging the borrower, nor will you be wronged by forgoing the increase. Both halves of that sentence are doing work: the cancellation of the increase is not presented as a loss the lender absorbs, because the increase was never his. The one being pressed for repayment who cannot pay the principal is to be given the status of those granted respite. And if you remit the entire loan, that is better for you — you know the secret of brotherhood. The text then makes a remarkable admission: this question has been confused for most of the jurists, and it asks God's help for them and for itself in grasping what is intended. A rulings manual conceding that the scholars have generally misread a verse is not a small gesture.

What follows is the historical account, and it explains why the ruling arrived when it did. Both categories of lending were in use among the Arabs, the Christians, and the Jews and their associates before the coming of Islam, and afterwards in the trade conducted before the conquest of Mecca. Their transactions were held in the grip of documented contracts, and the Muslims were oppressed by them — particularly in the matter of ready cash, where they were given a paltry sum and enormous amounts were taken from them in return, with buying and selling at usurious rates, until the situation became straitened and there was no stratagem by which to escape it. The Prophet was waiting on divine guidance in the matter. Then God opened the conquest of Mecca in the eighth year, and the strength of the Muslims increased, and their capacity to resist exploitation grew with it.

In the ninth year came the further revelation: the polytheists are indeed impure, so let them not approach the Sacred Mosque after this year of theirs; and if you fear poverty, God will enrich you from His bounty if He wills — God is All-Knowing, All-Wise. The Commander of the Believers conveyed the message of disavowal from the Prophet, delivering the warning to the polytheists. The economic reordering that follows is hard to reconstruct in its details — the trade routes, the outside merchants bringing money in and taking grain out, the market regulated under the community's own authority — but its outcome is stated plainly. From that year onward the Muslims could put their own money into the field and deal in both categories: with fellow Muslims according to the reward of the Hereafter, and with ordinary buyers and sellers according to worldly benefit. Those dealing on worldly terms were to forgive the increase in cases of hardship and to stop pressing for the principal, in accordance with God's command.

Notice what has just been described. The prohibition is not presented as a timeless abstraction handed down in a vacuum. It is presented as arriving at the moment the community was finally strong enough to live by it. Guidance waited on capacity.

The remaining material is more scattered. Dealings with non-Muslims were largely abandoned by those who prized their honour, once the precedent of how the polytheists had treated the early community became known; yet the treatise is careful to say such dealings are not forbidden by the sacred law outright. A narration is given from Mawlana Ja'far al-Sadiq, peace be upon him, that a man should not enter partnership with a protected non-Muslim, nor keep his accounts, nor deposit with him, nor cultivate affection for him — a counsel of distance in commerce rather than a prohibition of trade.

Then the exchange hadith, in its old paired form: gold for gold, silver for silver, barley for barley, date for date, a full measure for a full measure — like for like. Whoever increases or takes more sins in the taking and the giving alike; both parties are implicated. The treatise observes that in its own day such direct exchange had become widespread — in the lands of Yemen people exchange goods as circumstance requires, and where the road offers no alternative people manage their needs by exchange, as in India and elsewhere — and that the well-known reply concerning interest was given in that context.

The section closes on orphans, which belongs here because an orphan's property is a debt of a different kind. Attend to them until they reach the age of marriage, and if you find them to be of sound judgement, hand over their property to them. The guardian is expected to act for the orphan and spend on him with kindness, under the supervision of the ruler; and where a guardian spends another's money in the wrong place, the text turns sharp toward those who administer such funds and pronounce on their necessities. The restriction is meant for the one who cannot yet manage his own affairs — an orphan of sound sense is not to be held under it, while a man who squanders is the one properly called restrained.

## Hire, partnership, and the trust

The last stretch of this chapter is the most badly damaged in transmission and also, in a way, the most interesting, because it is where the treatise stops talking about buying and starts talking about holding. In every arrangement that follows, someone has property that is not his: a tenant in a house, a rider on a hired animal, a working partner with another man's capital, a neighbour with a right over a shared wall, a borrower with a lent tool, a friend with a bag of money left for safekeeping. Ownership was the question in the first section. Trust is the question here.

The section opens on the insolvent merchant — the man whose debts have consumed his wealth or risen beyond it. Claims against him are established by the knowledge of the trusted agent and by what his creditors bring, and his assets are gathered so that he may pay what he owes, down to the house he lived in and let to a tenant. Then the decisive act: the ruler declares his bankruptcy publicly, so no one else loses money lending to him. That declaration is what the word means here — the man prohibited from dealing and made publicly known as such is the bankrupt. The first to be paid are those with the greatest claim.

Two refinements follow, and both turn on intention again. If a man was still dealing with people, and his debts exceeded his assets, but no one had yet declared him bankrupt, then gifts he makes to his children or to others stand — unless it becomes clear to the judge that he acted out of corrupt intent, moving property beyond his creditors' reach while the reckoning approached, in which case the judge should invalidate those transactions. And a bankrupt man is not stopped from marrying or from the other affairs of a household. Bankruptcy restricts his dealings; it is not a sentence on his person.

Then hire, and here the treatise is unusually systematic. The rental of a house, a ship, or an animal is a recognised contract. What makes it valid is specification, exactly as in the chapter on sale: the amount and the duration must be stated, along with the purpose for which the thing will be used and the circumstances in which harm to it may occur. Once that is done, the choice of whether to let the property at all belongs to its owner.

The tenant's obligations are drawn tightly. He is bound to do what is necessary without harming the structure or weakening it; work that damages the building is not permitted to him, because the owner did not consent to it. Anything beyond the ordinary use — alterations, service arrangements, and the like — requires the owner's permission. Where permission was given, the tenant must prove it: if he establishes his case, it stands; otherwise the owner takes the oath and the tenant is held responsible. And there is no recourse for the one who goes beyond what was agreed.

The rulings that follow soften that in the tenant's favour, which is the pattern of the whole chapter — the party with less power gets the benefit of the doubt. A man renting a house may occupy part of it and leave part empty if he does not wish to share everything he sees; if there are fruit trees on the property, he may eat from them; if he has taken benefit from what is inside and lived there a day or two, the month's rent stands. He is not required to put in writing what he has promised. And in a dispute between owner and tenant where the owner has no evidence, the word of the tenant — the one actually occupying and warming the house — is what carries weight. Most striking of all: if the owner promised to hand the property over and failed to do so, no rent is owed for however long the tenant was kept out of it. You are not charged for a house you were not let into.

For a specific animal or a specific ship, the ruling turns on how much of the journey was actually delivered. If the animal proved unfit, or the conveyance was given up before the journey began, the hirer loses his claim to the hire; and if the failure came after he had already mounted and covered part of the road, the reckoning is made according to the portion of the road he had covered. Even where the journey was only partly completed, responsibility and compensation are apportioned on the same basis. Within a set period the hirer may use the animal within customary limits; if he goes beyond them, he is liable. And where a man hires to a known destination and then drives the animal past it through his own fault, and the animal perishes, the owner has the choice: he may keep what remains to him, or he may take compensation from the hirer — and if he is satisfied, that is the end of it. Guarantee, the treatise adds, is itself a recognised contract, and its fee may be waived.

On partnership, a narration is given from Mawla Ja'far ibn Muhammad, from his father and his forefathers, from the Messenger of God, may God's blessings and peace be upon him, that he permitted partnership in profit. The mechanics are recoverable even where the text is broken. Capital is mixed until it becomes a single pool drawn from both sides, and shares are reckoned according to what each contributed. Distinct from this is the arrangement in which the money comes from one side and the work from the other, with the division between them counted according to the agreement they made and the terms appointed. The working partner does not bear the burden of the loss, because the burden belongs to the one who carries the capital — unless he departs from what he was instructed to do, in which case he guarantees what is missing. And a partnership is not valid where a man is committed to supplying something he can no longer supply.

[Editorial note: recovered from the secondary Urdu edition to fill a gap in the primary source at this point.] One stipulation is enough to void the whole arrangement: if a partner insists on a fixed sum off the top regardless of what the venture actually earns, the partnership is corrupted into a loan wearing a partnership's clothing, and the fixed sum is riba by another name.

Pre-emption comes next: the right of a partner or neighbour to step into a sale of adjoining property and take it on the same terms as the buyer. It is narrated from Jabir, and the treatise holds that the pre-emption right of a partner is obligatory where the partnership is between Muslims. The right does not run in ships and animals. It attaches to what is genuinely shared — a common road, a common wall, something held jointly in a building, a shared door or gateway within one locality. And it lapses the moment sharing does: once the property has been divided so that no shared stake remains between the owner of the upper portion and the owner of the lower, pre-emption no longer applies. The right exists to protect a person from having a stranger inserted into something he holds in common, and where nothing is held in common there is nothing to protect.

[Editorial note: recovered from the secondary Urdu edition to fill a gap in the primary source at this point.] The Commander of the Believers is quoted narrowing the right by faith as well as by property: it can be held only by a Muslim against a Muslim seller. One narration extends what counts as genuinely shared beyond the wall, the road, and the gateway to a jointly held well or boat — though the treatise notes this stands apart from the general rule already given, that pre-emption does not run in ships and animals, and should be read as the minority view rather than a correction of it. And where the buyer has understated what he actually paid, the pre-emptor is not bound by the false figure: once the true price becomes known, he may still exercise his right at that price.

It may be exercised on another's behalf — by parents for their child, by a guardian for an orphan, by the judge for one who has no guardian. The entitled party may be present or absent, and whether or not he knew of the sale, he may exercise the right when he learns of it. For farmers the period within which it may be claimed is a year; for others it turns on the claimant's circumstances.

Then the loan for use — 'ariya, the lending of a thing to be used and returned. The treatise sets out an order of precedence for anyone wishing to do such a favour, and the order is a small piece of moral architecture: begin with one's own children, then the closest among the believers, then the brothers in religion, then the needy and the destitute, to the point of relieving real hardship and making the relief known to the one who needs it. This is presented as the practice of the Messenger of God, may God's blessings and peace be upon him, in which the loan is given, the naked clothed, the guest received. The governing rule for the borrower is narrow: he may use the borrowed thing only for what the lender authorised. Damage from transgression or misuse falls on him; damage arising in the ordinary course of the permitted use does not.

Which brings the chapter to the deposit — property left with someone purely for safekeeping — and to the principle the treatise states in its own voice: trusts are restored to their people. The rule of liability is the gentlest in the chapter. If a man is given something to hold and it perishes without any wrongdoing on his part, with no misuse and no fraud, no liability falls on him at all, because he is a trustee and the whole nature of the arrangement is trust. The burden of proof follows: it is not for the depositor to accuse the one holding the deposit until he produces evidence. Suspicion is not a claim.

The chapter closes with the case that shows this doctrine being applied under pressure, and it is the finest thing in the slice. A wealthy woman of Quraysh was given a hundred dinars to hold by two men, on the express condition that they would come to her together to collect it. Some time later one of them came alone, saying his companion had sent him with a sign and instructed her to hand over the money. Trusting him, she paid it. Then the second man came and asked for the money. She told him his companion had already come and she had paid him. He replied that he had given no such instruction.

The matter was brought before 'Umar, who found it a heavy affair, and he sent them to the Commander of the Believers. The judgement, when it came, did not need to establish who was lying, or to make the woman bear a loss for having believed a plausible man. It simply held the parties to the condition they had themselves set. You deposited the money on the terms that all of you would come together for it. Go, bring your companion, and take what is yours.

Read that beside the opening of the chapter and the two ends of this episode close on each other. The sale of a fish still in the water was void because nobody could say afterwards what had changed hands. The deposit here was safe because the parties had said at the outset exactly how it would change hands — and when one of them departed from that, the answer was not to apportion blame but to return everyone to the terms. The condition was the protection. It had been there the whole time.

## What this episode lands

Three things have been settled here.

The anatomy of the transaction has been laid out: three pillars, five conditions, and one question running under all of them — can either party say afterwards precisely what was exchanged? Air, unborn animals, uncaught fish, unnamed cubits of land and unchosen garments all fail for the same reason, and it is not superstition about the goods: an agreement about something indeterminate has not agreed on anything. The rulings on defect, on dispute and on the false bidder are the same instinct applied to people rather than objects.

The doctrine of lending has been divided by intention alone, and the division is severe in one direction and generous in the other. A loan made for God's sake is an act of devotion, and no benefit whatsoever may be drawn from it, however small. A loan made for worldly benefit is lawful and regulated, and every right the treatise lists on the lender's side is a right to take less than he is owed. Where the borrower is in hardship, the instruction runs from delay to remission, and the text reads the scriptural declaration of war on usury as pointing there — with the frank admission that most jurists have read the passage otherwise.

And the long tail has turned on trust rather than title. In hire, in partnership, in pre-emption, in the borrowed tool and the deposited purse, someone holds what belongs to another, and the treatise's instinct is consistently to protect the weaker position: the tenant's word against an owner without evidence, no rent for a house never handed over, the working partner shielded from a loss he did not cause, the trustee free of liability for what perished in his honest keeping. The question stops being who owns this and becomes who was trusted with it, and on what terms — the same question the chapter on sale asked, arriving from the other direction.

What remains of the property material — the pledge, the written record of a debt, the wall two neighbours share — is taken up next, and it is there that the treatise turns from obligations over things to obligations between people, and opens its argument for marriage.
