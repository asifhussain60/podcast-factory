# The Marriage Contract and Its Bonds

## Where this episode picks up

How much milk does it take to make two people permanently unmarriageable to each other? The answer this stretch of the treatise gives is: one swallow, provided it falls inside the child's first two years. A woman neither of them will remember, a quantity too small to measure — and a barrier as absolute as the one between brother and sister, standing for the rest of their lives. That ruling is the far edge of this episode, and everything before it is the same idea approached from nearer in: that a marriage is built out of bonds nobody chooses, and that all the choosing happens inside a frame already drawn.

The treatise has already spent considerable length pressing the reader toward marriage and telling him what qualities to look for in a wife. That argument is finished and is not reopened here. What opens instead is the machinery: at what age, chosen by whom, contracted through whom, on what terms, with what payment, owing what to each other, and — at greatest length of all — with which women the question can never arise.

Three teachings carry the episode. The first is the choosing and the look, which is far less about one man's preference than a modern reader expects. The second is the contract and its terms: who may lawfully conclude a marriage, which stipulations are void the moment they are spoken, where the husband's authority stops, and what the marriage payment actually is. The third is the forbidden degrees — kinship by blood, kinship by marriage, and kinship created by nursing.

One note before we begin, as in the last episode. This portion of the manuscript is damaged: sentences break and resume elsewhere, a list is announced and never given, names appear that connect to nothing. Recoverable rulings are stated plainly, and the rest is marked as missing rather than filled in.

## The choosing and the look

Marriage is not merely a contract between a man and a woman in the legal sense, the treatise begins; it is meant to keep the lineage alive through what is right and just on every side. That phrase governs the section, because no ruling in it is permitted to serve one party's convenience alone.

The first question is the plainest one. Someone who wished to marry a woman asked about her face and her hair. The answer is compressed and partly broken, but its logic survives: if he is able to look, he should catch a glimpse of her before others do, because it is sight that falls into the trap of hearing. Sincerity between the two does not hold unless they are warm to each other; discord replaces sincerity, the house is then wet with misery, and what should have been a heaven becomes its substitute. A bad match ruins a life, and the stakes are set that high on the first page.

Because the stakes are that high, it was chosen and stipulated that a man and a woman should not marry until the wise and discerning of their age have made the choice, so that each may live well with the other. The choosing is lifted out of the hands of the two people most affected by it and given to those judged capable of doing it well. And nothing achieves the purpose, the text says, except finding the meeting point between his age and hers — named at around eighteen years — so that the marriage brings pleasures, purity and goodwill between them, and in due time for their children. The argument against marrying earlier is from evidence rather than principle: marriage does not flourish among those who marry young, and at a young age it confines itself to the bare necessities.

Then the treatise suspends its own rule where the rule would get people hurt. Where the order of the state is beyond control and families are not secure, where some oppress others as in the age of ignorance and as under certain rulers now, there are evildoers who descend on a household whenever they hear of a young woman in it and loot it from its people. In such conditions the marriage of a girl becomes a necessity even if she is young, and it requires a large number of people in the arrangements; for a young man, likewise, marriage is then the first and most appropriate course. But in civilised countries, where affairs are organised and the government governs its people, there is no fear of wealth or children being plundered and no cause to fear the corruption of morals — so no need to rush into marriage before maturity and age.

Here the author places himself in his own text. In a small situation such as ours, he writes, in the land of India, we are safe for ourselves and our children, and the benefits of community life are more abundant than can be stated; grief and the need for assistance fall upon everyone, and so people help one another as though they were limbs of one body. One benefit carries the pages that follow: if a man wishes to marry a woman of the community, what grew from her upbringing is not hidden from him in the choosing, because she is not among the women who come from outside. That makes the selection easier and doubles the likelihood of righteous children.

The conclusion drawn is a programme for education rather than a rule about marriage. The daughters of the community come raised by good women; they are, in the text's phrase, the cooks of goodness. It is therefore necessary to broaden proper upbringing among those who understand the affairs of the community, so that they fulfil what they must of the rights of husbands, of sisters, and of children in their rearing — with right beliefs and knowledge established in their hearts, especially for girls before they enter family life. If each woman sets her household in order, is good to her husband and helps him in his livelihood, she will be the first school for the children: the argument for teaching girls rests on their being the earliest teachers anyone has.

The mirror image is a harsh warning against marrying outside. The stranger who enters at his own request will not find a welcome, and neither will a marriage contracted outside; for a time, disagreement and hatred will arise. Such a man lowers his standing among the community, especially when a woman of the community marries a common man, and more so when a man marries a common woman; and if there are children, those children will find no rest. The facts of the past twenty years, the author says, are sufficient as lesson. He then turns a proverb against his own people rather than against outsiders: the shark enters your house with a sniff until you open the door for him — and doors were opened, secretly, in some of the houses of the brotherhood. This is his reckoning with his own period, presented as his reckoning and not as a ruling.

The rulings on modesty follow from the same concern, because temptation lies in ambush. The word given is the instruction to tell one's wives and daughters and the women of the believers that they should draw their outer garments close about them. The women of the believers in the time of the Prophet, peace be upon him, were of the finest character, unlike those who used to harm them by mocking them, and the covering was affirmed as a protection for the believers in that time and after it. The hijab is a ruling among the rulings of Islam, made a general ruling in the Quran. What happened instead, the author says, is that those who did not care about the morals of these times found fault with the Quran on the veil while hiding this beneath the garb of Islam and faith — a criticism dressed in belief.

Three rulings follow, two of them answers to questions put to Abu Ja'far al-Salih. He was asked about a believing man, known in his place, where there is nobody available other than those lawful for him to marry, and who therefore married among them. He was asked about a dissolute woman marrying a man, and answered that he must not do that: the people of concealment and chastity are his choice. And a saying of a prophet of God is given on marrying a non-Muslim — that he should seek a Muslim woman, even if she is a convert to Islam.

On looking, the precision is the teaching. The Messenger of God, peace be upon him, said that a man who wishes to marry may look at the woman he intends to marry if he is able, for God commanded believing men and women alike to lower their gaze. Then the ration: it is narrated from the Father of Imams that the Messenger of God, peace be upon him, was asked about a man looking at the woman he intends to marry, and said — look once, and that is for you; the second is against you; and the third is an arrow from the arrows of Satan. Whoever abandons it for the sake of God, God will grant him in its place faith whose taste he will find in his heart. Mawlana Ja'far ibn Muhammad, peace be upon him, asked about a man who looks past what is before him, answered that a man should occupy his gaze with his own family.

The treatise then names the setting those narrations assume: the marketplace and similar places where people mix openly. There are communities among the faithful — as today among some Arab peoples — where women are found alone in shops with nobody to manage their affairs, and there is then no alternative but for them to go out. But the established practice among noble peoples is that women of religion and honour do not leave the home except under necessity, and it is a deficiency for such a woman to be seen by unrelated men.

The rule then turns inward, and the narration is remarkable for the reason it gives. A man asked the Messenger of God, peace be upon him, whether he should seek permission before entering upon his own family. He replied: yes, for I would not wish you to see anything unpleasant. The man said: she is my sister — sometimes her hair or her hands are uncovered. He said: remain modest before her. And he added a reason that is not about the man at all: I fear that if you enter upon her suddenly it may startle her and diminish some of her good deeds toward you. He then said that if a man has a female relative who has reached puberty, let him refrain from extended conversation with her.

The section closes on the etiquette of the proposal. There is a hadith, which the text itself calls weak, that a man may not make a proposal over his brother's proposal — meaning after an agreement has been reached. A woman is either married or she is not. If married, her cases are three: under a husband, in the waiting period after divorce, or having completed it. Under a husband, nobody may propose to her at all; in the time of divorce the matter may extend a little; and if her husband has died, a man may not show words openly that she will understand — no proposal by direct statement, and the same holds for approaching her mother. If the unmarried woman is not forbidden to him, proposing is permissible, and as long as nobody else has proposed seriously there is no objection. A woman's weeping is not by itself an answer, nor is her breaking off the conversation a sign of consent.

Two damaged accounts illustrate the line between an indirect approach and a proposal. Sakina bint Handhala came to Abu Ja'dh Muhammad ibn Ali and said her husband had died; she was his cousin. He spoke to her of her kinship from the Messenger of God, peace be upon him — and she answered: may God raise you, you have proposed to me while I am still in the waiting period. In the second, the Messenger of God, peace be upon him, entered upon Umm Salamah, the daughter of Abu Umayyah ibn al-Mughirah, who had been widowed, and spoke to her softly; and the text is explicit that this was not a formal proposal. Kindness to a widow is not a proposal; a proposal during the counted days is what the ruling forbids.

## The contract and its terms

Who may actually conclude a marriage is a small closed list of people. Guardianship remains in force until the ward reaches puberty, and the age at which maturity arrives differs from one person to another. The rule itself is stated in the negative: nobody denies guardianship in marriage except the father, where a father can be found, or his duly appointed agent. And then the consequence — if a woman marries through someone other than these four guardians, the marriage is invalid. Not irregular, not in need of repair, but void. The passage enumerating the four did not survive in this manuscript, but it can now be supplied. [Recovered from a second, Urdu-language edition of this same treatise, pp. 85-86:] Where a minor's marriage must be contracted out of necessity, that authority belongs to four persons alone — the father; the grandfather; the father's appointed agent, the wasi, whom the father deputized for this purpose during his own lifetime; or the wasi to whom the father's will gave authority over the children's marriage after his death. Beyond naming them the Urdu edition goes dark at the same place the Arabic one does: what becomes of a marriage contracted by someone outside this circle, beyond the bare verdict of invalidity already given above, is itself illegible in it, and nothing further is supplied here. It is equally impermissible for any of them to act on verbal consent alone.

What follows is more accommodating than the invalidity rule suggests. An adult woman who wishes to arrange her own marriage may appoint as her agent one of those four, or a genuine son, or a reliable relative, or her paternal brother, with two witnesses present. One guardian may delegate authority to another among them who is living, so that the one chosen may be appointed by the judge to act on her behalf; and nobody outside this circle should interfere. If the guardian refuses without valid cause, the judge steps in, and the agent he appoints conducts the marriage on her behalf — the path, the treatise says, narrated from the Imams of guidance. Two procedural notes close the passage: the judge may himself serve as witness to the contract of two men, and there is no objection if the men who witnessed the appointment of the agent also witness the marriage.

With the contract in place, the question becomes what may be written into it, and the governing rule is stated with a verse: do not say what your tongues describe, that this is lawful and this is forbidden, inventing lies against Allah. Mawlana Ja'far al-Sadiq, may Allah have mercy on him, gives it in operative form: any condition stipulated by the people is neither valid nor enforceable if it violates the Quran or contradicts the Sunnah. The parties may agree to a great deal, and whatever they agree to that cuts against the Book is not a bad bargain but a nullity.

The husband is then given authority over four things: intercourse, divorce, maintenance by kindness, and the contracting of further marriages. If the time of the contract is divided among several wives, each receives her due share, and he must fulfil the rights of each whether his wife agrees with the arrangement or not. But every clause is immediately qualified: all of it must be conducted with fairness and kindness.

And the qualification is given teeth. If either party is wronged, the wife may bring the matter before the judge, whose powers are then bounded with unusual candour — he does not have the power to impose a ransom divorce, nor to remove the husband from the home. But the husband must not strike his wife for anything that is not legally obligatory. The verse on discord is quoted as the boundary of what he may do: as for those women from whom you fear nushuz, admonish them, then forsake them in bed, and then strike them — but if they return to obedience, seek no further means against them. The treatise brings the verse in under the heading of what contradicts the Quran, and reads the closing clause as the operative one: the sequence ends the moment its purpose is met. And a Muslim man is entitled to certain prerogatives, except where he acts in a state of intoxication or moral filth.

On the ransom divorce, the couple must be given a choice, and the matter comes before an arbitrator. Whoever wished to proceed with a marriage would do so with the proper documentation recorded in a book designated for that purpose — the doctrine of the written record established in the last episode, now doing its work on a marriage register rather than on a debt. A man must deal equitably with his wife in good treatment, fulfilling the right of raj'ah, providing maintenance, and observing the waiting period. What comes next is broken: if none of the conditions are met and a directive is issued on the side of the woman, her divorce rests in the hand of the one who has taken authority over the matter, so as to bring about reconciliation or to finalise it. The number named in the middle of that sentence connects to nothing that survives.

Where the man wishes to divorce and the woman objects, she may ransom herself by returning her portion of the division to him, or some part of it, and they may agree on a settlement. Reconciliation by mutual agreement is permissible, and the verse given for it is that if a woman fears ill-treatment or desertion from her husband, there is no blame on either of them if they come to a settlement. The essential addition the treatise makes is that they reconcile on good terms — the settlement is meant to restore something, not merely to be permitted. Seeking pain through divorce is blameworthy, except where it is necessary to complete a separation; and a man may divorce his wife in order to marry another. As for defects that neither party knew of before the marriage but which became apparent afterward, each of them has the right either to remain or to separate. The text names nine such defects and then, rather than listing them, sends the reader elsewhere — to Da'a'im al-Islam, Qadi al-Nu'man's own compendium, for the details. A second, Urdu-language edition of this treatise does the same thing at the same point, which settles that this is not a passage the manuscript lost but a chapter the author chose not to write twice. The principle survives without the list because the author never meant to give the list here: a concealed defect gives the right of exit to whichever party did not know of it, and gives it to both equally.

The rulings on the marriage payment begin with two narrations pulling the same way. A man whose bride-price was provided by his people came forward, and the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him, said: make peace, and do not exaggerate in the matter of bride-prices, lest enmity result. And a man came saying he wished to marry a certain woman, and was told to proceed; he answered that he had nothing to offer as a marriage gift, and was told: this ring is your gift. A ring stands as a lawful mahr, and inflating the payment is named a cause of enmity rather than a mark of honour.

The figures are the author's own conversion into the currency of his readers. Twelve and a half uqiyyas of silver, he notes, is roughly forty rupees. The Prophet, peace be upon him, used to set the mahr for his wives at a moderate amount, and it is preferable to settle it at that level; the brotherhood mahr of forty rupees became customary among the people, and the practice descends from the Mothers of the Believers.

And then the ruling that shows what kind of thing the mahr actually is. Entering into marriage without naming a specific amount is valid, because the mahr is not one of the essential pillars of the marriage contract — though it is recommended to name it before the marriage is consummated. Decrees issued about the mahr beforehand remain in effect afterward, and where the wife delegates it to the husband before the contract is finalised, it is established as obligatory upon him after consummation.

Whose judgment sets the amount is not the husband's. The judge considers the woman's standing among her peers, her circumstances and her wealth, and calculates accordingly; if the amount is high, a comparable mahr is set, equal to the mahr of the wives of the Prophet, peace be upon him, and even if it falls below that he may give her less in proportion to his means. If the husband is assigned the responsibility of setting it, she may accept whatever he gives, and a husband who does not have much should give what he can before separating from her. Two rules then guard the payment from being intercepted. The mahr does not belong to the parents or the guardian, nor to the agent, nor to the one exercising jealous guardianship over the woman, unless it was given specifically to settle a matter pertaining to that. And gifts are divided by their timing: all gifts that reached the wife before the marriage belong to the husband, while gifts that came at the time of the marriage or after it belong to her as her right.

The section ends where the treatise puts its weight, on what the two of them owe each other, and it opens on a verse: they are a garment for you and you are a garment for them. The hadith transmitted alongside it is one the text says people went on repeating until their tongues grew weary of it. Heed my words, the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him, said: prayer, and right conduct toward those whom your right hand possesses — do not neglect them. Fear Allah regarding women, for they are in your care like captives. You took them in trust by the word of Allah, and it is impermissible to use them as fuel for the Fire.

The purposes of marriage are restated in one line, as the ground of these mutual rights rather than as an argument for marrying, already made earlier in the treatise: the continuation of offspring, and cooperation in the good of this life and the next. Two verses are given — that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them, and placed between you affection and mercy; and the prayer of those who ask their Lord for comfort to their eyes from among their spouses and offspring, and to be made leaders of the righteous. The bond of marriage, the treatise says, is like a garden that delights the eyes with its varied beauty, each spouse a faithful companion and a source of good for the other. Garments give protection and dignity, and so neither of them shall be harmed nor burdened beyond capacity. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that the best among men is the one who is best to his family.

A few particular rulings close the passage. It is not permissible for a woman to dress like a man, nor for a man to dress like a woman. On the husband's rights over his wife it was said: that she not dispose of his wealth without his permission, that she not refuse him herself, that she not fast voluntarily without his permission, and that she not leave his home without his permission — and this, it was said, is among the greatest of his rights. Asked about the greatest of the wife's rights over her husband, the answer given is a single clause: that he treat her with dignity and honour. The asymmetry of those two lists is on the page, and the treatise does not comment on it.

## The forbidden degrees

The last and longest movement answers a different question. The rulings so far concern how a marriage is made well; these concern the marriages that cannot be made at all, and they do not turn on anyone's choice or conduct. A relationship exists, and the door is shut.

The section is headed by a verse — O Prophet, We have made lawful for you your wives — and opens on a case rather than a principle: a hadith recording that a man was swept away by his emotions and married his father's wife, with two witnesses present. The witnesses are the point. Every formality was in place and the marriage was still impossible, because what forbids here is not a defect in procedure.

The categories are then named. Kinship falls into two kinds: blood relation, which is nasab, and affinity through marriage, which is musahara. Blood relation on the father's side brings relatives into close relationship with one another; they are joined by lineage and counted as kin, and one born into that kinship is bound on both sides by ties of blood. Where there is no such blood connection, the relationship is musahara — the bond marriage itself creates — and musahara, like blood kinship, carries its own rulings on choice and permissibility. A marriage manufactures relatives, and the relatives it manufactures are as real for these purposes as the ones a person is born with. A narration from Salih is given in the same passage on a father and son and the marrying of slave girls and daughters where the girls are young, with the girls holding a choice in the matter once they reach puberty.

The list of the protected women is where the manuscript is at its worst. The passage enumerating them survives as a sequence of broken clauses — the sanctity of a man's own household, his father and brothers, his uncle and his uncle's wife, the daughters of his sister and her mother, the women related to him through nursing, and the mothers of his women. It reads as a fragment of the scriptural list of forbidden degrees, and the text closes it by saying that these prohibitions are affirmed by the Book of God. The clauses can no longer be attached to individual rulings with confidence, and no attempt is made here to reassemble them. What the passage does establish is that the list is scriptural and closed.

[Recovered from a second, Urdu-language edition of this same treatise, pp. 77-78:] The verse itself can now be given whole. Forbidden to you in marriage are your mothers, your daughters, your sisters, your paternal aunts, your maternal aunts, your brother's daughters, your sister's daughters, your foster-mothers who nursed you, your foster-sisters through nursing, your wives' mothers, your step-daughters under your guardianship from wives you have consummated marriage with — if the marriage was not consummated there is no blame — the wives of your own begotten sons, and the combining of two sisters in marriage at once, except what has already occurred before this prohibition was revealed; for God is forgiving, merciful. And married women are forbidden to you, except those your right hand possesses. The Urdu edition prints a genealogy chart alongside this verse, mapping the relations by name, but the chart itself did not survive its own scanning — a table collapsed into an unreadable run in transcription — and it is not reconstructed here.

Where the manuscript recovers, it recovers on the rulings about combining two relatives in one marriage. A question is put about a man ignorant of the prohibition on combining two sisters. First, the treatise says, he should know this. The second woman he speaks to is the sister of the wife he already has, and then he knows that no separation is obligatory upon them other than divorce. If intercourse has occurred the dowry is due to her; if it has not, nothing is due. The author then gives his own positions in his own voice: he does not hold the combining of the paternal aunt and the niece to be permissible, nor the combining of the wife of the paternal uncle with his daughter without her consent. And a case is put of a nomad whose wife is his wife's sister, who divorces his wife and seeks another — where, if it falls within our practice, marrying her is permissible. A short cluster of invalid cases follows, named rather than argued: the marriage of a man who marries a woman after knowing she was forbidden to him is invalid, and so is that of the woman a man marries after her husband's death when he has counted her waiting period and married her while in the state of ihram. The four divorced women the passage announces are not enumerated in what survives. A second edition of this treatise, in Urdu, gives only one "four women" ruling anywhere in its own marriage chapter, and it may be what this passage originally pointed to — though the fit is not exact, since only the first of the four actually concerns a divorced woman. [Possible match, recovered from the Urdu edition; offered here as probable rather than confirmed:] it is unlawful to bring together in marriage, at the same time, a woman who has been triple-divorced and still requires an intervening marriage before she may return to the same husband; a woman married while her being unlawful to the groom was already known; a woman contracted in marriage during her waiting period of widowhood; and a woman married while in the state of pilgrim consecration. Whether this is the very list the damaged passage once gave, or simply the nearest ruling either edition has to offer, cannot be settled from the manuscripts alone.

Then the treatise turns to nursing, and gives it more room than anything else in this stretch of the book. The verse it opens on concerns the arrangement rather than the prohibition: if you wish to have your children breastfed, there is no blame on you, provided you pay what you have promised in a fair manner. Parents may arrange for their children to be nursed by whoever is willing, and a mother may say: I will nurse him, my brother, for I am satisfied.

The principle is then stated in the form that governs everything after it. A hadith establishes that breastfeeding creates the same prohibitions as birth. Not similar prohibitions and not lesser ones — the same. And the treatise immediately narrows the ruling by consent: it applies where the nursing takes place with the consent of the one responsible for the newborn, whether wealthy or not.

The rulings on consent are a short series, and the surviving text is uneven. If a woman breastfeeds a child without the permission of the one responsible for him, that is the first case; if permission is granted, the ruling falls on the one who pays for it. A third case turns on milk lost or spilled within the two-year period, even by a single swallow. Where the newborn inevitably swallows the milk, breastfeeding does not require the consent of the one responsible; where the feeding was unintentional, it should not be considered as establishing a prohibition. And where the nursing is done through a legitimate arrangement, the treatise reaches the ruling that matters most: such breastfeeding does not establish prohibited degrees of kinship merely by the route the milk travelled — not by dripping through the nose or the ear, nor by tube in the case of injury, nor by injection into the throat. What creates the bond is a nursing relationship, not the mechanical delivery of milk.

The second principle is the time limit. The bound of permissible nursing is two full years from the day the child is born, calculated as complete months, and all who nurse the child within that period fall under the prohibition. Two qualifications follow. If the woman has never given birth, or is infertile, milk sometimes appears from her even in small amount, and the prohibition still operates — with the condition that the one who nurses must not be born of unlawful relations. And where nursing is desired past that threshold, it is permitted when undertaken out of necessity.

What the prohibition creates is a full parallel family. Those who share milk from a single woman become forbidden to one another as foster-siblings. If a man has several wives and a boy is nursed by one of them, the boy becomes the milk-son of that wife only and not of the others — yet all of those co-wives are forbidden to one another's milk-children by virtue of the shared household bond. And even if the husband himself did not intend to establish milk-kinship, the suckling alone is sufficient to create the prohibition. Intention has no part in it. If a single woman nurses several children, whether they were brought to her from infancy or not, all of them become milk-siblings to one another and are forbidden to marry each other.

The lines then run outward through the generations, and the treatise walks them patiently. When a woman nurses a man's child she becomes forbidden to him, and so do her mother and her female ancestors, her daughters and her granddaughters; and if she is a slave the prohibitions apply in full just the same. However the milk-kinship arises, the treatise says, the one who has nursed you is forbidden to you. A man's foster-daughters and foster-granddaughters are forbidden to him, as are his foster-sister, his foster-nieces through brother or sister, and his foster-aunts on both sides — and nursing does not deprive a natural daughter of her own prohibition. Nursing also prohibits the combining of two foster-sisters in one marriage, and the combining of a woman with her foster-aunt on either side, whether the relation arose through nursing or through direct marriage: the rule on combining two sisters, now applied to the family that milk created.

Having drawn the circle, the treatise is careful about its edge. A man whose son was nursed by a certain woman may marry a different woman who was not nursed by her, because she is not a milk-sister and is otherwise unrelated to him. They may also marry the woman who nursed their son under certain conditions, and likewise the daughter of a nurse or wet-nurse, as long as those daughters did not themselves suckle the son or his children. Where no Muslim woman is available it may be permissible to arrange nursing with a lawful non-Muslim woman under conditions, provided no harm results — for nursing does not create prohibition where the conditions are not met.

The last question is one of proof, and it arrives at the worst possible moment in a marriage: nursing discovered after the contract but before consummation. The judge may confirm the prohibition between them. Complete nursing, for the purpose of proof, is defined precisely — three consecutive days, or ten separate sucklings. If intimacy had already been established before the matter was proven, the dowry remains owed and the consequence stands. After the first and second cases the husband has no further claim, and the two are to be separated. The testimony of a foster-brother is acceptable in this matter, and credible testimony is not to be rejected merely on suspicion.

## What this episode lands

Three things have been settled here.

The choosing was taken out of the hands of the two people choosing. The age is set at a rough meeting point of about eighteen and suspended where disorder makes waiting dangerous; the pool is narrowed to the community on the argument that a known upbringing is worth more than an unknown face; and the look a man is allowed before he commits himself is rationed to one, with the second counted against him and the third named an arrow of Satan. Even the knock before entering a room where a sister sits is justified not by what he might see but by the startle it would cost her.

The contract was made a real instrument with real limits. A marriage concluded through the wrong person is void rather than defective, and a guardian who refuses without cause is not a veto but an obstacle a court removes. A stipulation that cuts against the Book is not a hard bargain but a nullity. The husband is handed authority over four named things and every one of them is qualified in the same breath by fairness and kindness. And the marriage payment was deliberately made small and deliberately made hers: a ring will serve, inflating it is named a cause of enmity, and the amount is set by a judge rather than by the man paying it. That it is not one of the pillars of the contract is the clearest statement of what it is — not the price of the marriage, but a debt inside it.

And the forbidden degrees work on a different principle from everything else here. Every other ruling can be complied with. These can only be discovered: a marriage with two witnesses standing by is impossible because of who the woman is, and a nursing arranged by adults for reasons of health builds a family tree that will constrain children who never consented to it and will not remember it. The husband's intention is declared irrelevant, and one swallow inside two years is enough. Three consecutive days or ten sucklings is what it takes to prove — a threshold set with such care because it decides whether two people already married are relatives.

What the treatise has not yet done is say what happens while a marriage runs, or when it ends. It has settled who may marry, who may not, on what terms and for what payment, and has said almost nothing about what a wife is owed day to day, how a divorce is pronounced and counted, or who keeps the children. Having built the bond, the book turns next to maintaining it and to taking it apart.
