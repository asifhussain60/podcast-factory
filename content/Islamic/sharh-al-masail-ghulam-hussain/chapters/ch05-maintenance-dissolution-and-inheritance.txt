# Maintenance, Dissolution, and What a Person Leaves Behind

## Where this episode picks up

What is a woman supposed to do when her husband simply stops existing? Not dead, not divorced, not away on business — gone, with no address, no letter, no word from anyone who might have seen him. The treatise opens this stretch of its material on exactly that case, and its first move is not consolation but a definition: a man whose whereabouts are known is not missing, he is absent. From that one distinction the whole apparatus follows — who investigates, what property gets opened up, where the judge writes, how long she waits, and at what point the marriage she is still technically inside stops being a marriage at all.

The previous episode built the bond, and stopped at the moment the contract exists. This one takes up everything afterwards: the daily obligation the contract creates, the several ways it can be undone, and then — because the treatise treats a person's wealth as one continuous subject — what happens to what a person owns when he gives it away in life and when he leaves it behind at death.

Three teachings carry the episode. The first is what a household is owed, from the missing husband through the standard of provision to the maintenance of a woman already divorced. The second is dissolution and the waiting period: the five recognized forms of undoing a marriage, the procedure that makes a divorce count, the ransom a wife may pay for her release, and the counted months before anyone can move on. The third is wealth that leaves your hands — the gift, the endowment, the will, and the fixed fractional shares the law imposes whether a person planned for them or not.

One warning. This stretch of the manuscript is damaged, at its worst at the end, where the inheritance grid dissolves into strings of words that no longer parse. Where the text is recoverable it is rendered here; where it has broken down, that is said plainly and nothing is invented to cover the gap.

## What a household is owed

Start with the man who has vanished. The absent man has a recognized last place of residence and is known to be somewhere; the missing man's location is entirely unknown. That is not a nicety — it decides what a judge can do next.

When a wife comes forward complaining that a missing husband has left her without maintenance, the judge does not begin with the marriage. He begins with the property. His first duty is to investigate whether the man left anything behind, because if he did, that property is used to provide for her — it is not frozen pending his return. If a guardian has been appointed to administer it, that guardian may spend from it on her behalf. Only when the property is exhausted and she is still without support does the judge take the second step: he writes to the place where the husband was last known to be, to inquire about him. Feed her from his money, then go looking for him — the ordering tells you what the law thinks it is protecting.

Then the timing. If the husband returns before the waiting period is complete and wishes to reclaim his wife, he may do so, and the time he was absent counts toward the period. If he returns after it has elapsed, her right in the matter stands. And on maintenance the treatise gives the missing man a startling status: he is treated as being among those present, from the day he went missing. His absence does not suspend the obligation. What his wife does not have is any automatic entitlement beyond what can be established.

From the vanished husband the text turns to the ordinary one, and states the obligation plainly: his duty is to provide for his family and to fulfill his duties toward them. A hadith is offered as sufficient for people to understand the matter — that leaving those who rely on you without provision is a serious failing. A man should be kind to his family. The standard is named: the median standard of living, one that is neither wasteful nor miserly. That middle line rules out two failures at once — the man who ruins his household by extravagance and the man who starves it by hoarding — and refuses to fix a number.

The provision is not only material. The people of the household are entitled to their religious knowledge and to their upbringing in generosity; a man who feeds his family and teaches them nothing has not discharged the obligation.

Then a principle that reframes the whole ledger. Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn 'Ali al-Salim, commenting on the words of God Almighty — "and no age is diminished" — stated that nothing spent in obedience to God is to be considered wasteful. Money spent on a duty is not money lost. It is from this principle, the treatise says, that the harder cases follow — the man who finds he cannot spend on what has passed while he is still responsible for someone, whether his wife is in Egypt or anywhere else.

What happens when he has nothing? Here the text gives a choice rather than a rule. Between the two parties there is an option: if she accepts the situation, she remains; if she does not, standing without maintenance entitles her to seek relief. A husband's poverty is not a defense that binds her. It is a condition she may consent to live under.

The entitlement is drawn broadly. A woman of any age — young or old, and even one who does not observe prayer — has a right to maintenance from her husband, in accordance with her proximity to him. Her piety is not a condition of being fed.

One circumstance suspends it. If the husband is a man of means who has left without his wife's permission and has been staying elsewhere at his own expense, and there is rebellion on her part, she is not entitled to maintenance until she returns. Nushuz — a wife's withdrawal from the obligations of the marriage — is what breaks the claim. The trigger is her withdrawal, not his displeasure, and the suspension ends the moment she returns.

The most consequential rulings here concern the woman already divorced, because that is where an obligation is most likely to be quietly dropped. Her maintenance is the husband's responsibility — the exception being an irrevocable divorce — unless he fails to provide and she remains silent about it until she gives birth. And she is entitled to maintenance if she breastfeeds her child. Nursing is work done for the child, and the man responsible for the child pays for it. Which is the reasoning the treatise anchors in the verse: a mother should not be harmed on account of her child, nor a father on account of his child.

Then the address that governs the whole subject: O Prophet, when you divorce women, divorce them at the beginning of their waiting periods. And the command that follows is given to the believers — provide for them, and let their matter be dealt with honorably, so that their provision is known and witnessed, and that it is just. Every word is a constraint. Known, so the amount is not left vague. Witnessed, so it is not deniable later. Just, so it is not merely technically paid.

Bear witness before God, the passage closes. A letter was accordingly written to the judge of the matter, and he wrote back regarding divorce and its proper care — a ruling addressed, the treatise says, to those who have not taught the Muslims the way of marriage and its proper conduct, because people must follow their established ways. That is the author's diagnosis of why any of this needs writing down.

## Dissolution and the waiting period

The treatise now turns to how a marriage comes apart, and opens by refusing a definition it has already refused once. Marriage is not merely a contract between two spouses. Each side carries obligations — for the husband, the dowry, maintenance, and good companionship; for the wife, chastity, affection, and obedience — all of which the previous episode set out and none of which is reopened here. What is new is the consequence: when the covenant is broken, and one of the parties inflicts serious harm by neglecting the duties assigned, the marriage contract may be dissolved.

The procedure mirrors the way a marriage is made. Just as a marriage is not generally contracted by the woman without a representative or agent, its dissolution is initiated by the representative of the aggrieved party — the one who complains on her behalf and seeks the protection of his wife from harm. The judge's first duty is investigative: he must determine whether the complaint is true. If it is, he compels the husband either to cease the harm or to remain silent.

If the husband does not respond, the judge may issue a divorce on his behalf; he does not need the husband's cooperation. But the door back is left open — if news of the husband's willingness reaches the court, and the situation on the wife's side allows it in the balance, he may be given her back into his care, with renewed kindness toward her and the members of the household. After that the husband has a choice: he may keep her on the condition he has described, or divorce her, and that divorce is revocable. Look, the treatise says, to the holy law in this chapter, and see how carefully it takes account of the rights of the couple and the ordering of their lives.

Then it defends the institution. Divorce was recognized in earlier revealed laws and among the prophets, and it accords with the law of nature — because marriage touches the living relations of the couple under the purified covenants between them, and if one party violates those covenants, the government of justice may deprive him of that bond. That deprivation, in the language of the Shari'ah, is what is called divorce: not a private exit but a sanction the order of justice imposes when a covenant has been broken.

And having defended it, the treatise immediately limits it. The benefit of divorce is sufficient for both parties, to spare each other further harm — but it should not be said that divorce is a final cure for everything. It falls on the one who wastes it through abuse, or on the one who seeks to remarry with humiliation. As the wise have said: patience in the face of hardship is better than separation. And then the image the chapter is remembered for — the bitterness of separation after intimacy is, in Islam, like the parable of nakedness after being clothed. Deeply disliked; not forbidden.

According to the jurists there are five forms of marital dissolution: divorce — that is, 'iddah divorce — khul'a, ila', zihar, and li'an, with the divorce of the rebellious wife alongside them. Having named all five, the author narrows his scope deliberately: he states that he will treat only divorce, the waiting period, and khul'a, since these are generally what practice requires, and he does not return to ila', zihar, or li'an anywhere else in the treatise. What follows here is accordingly limited to the three he chose to explain.

Take 'iddah divorce first, because it is the procedure everything else is measured against. When a husband wishes to divorce a woman, he should consider three things: that she is in a state of purity from menstruation, that he has not had relations with her after that purification, and that he record it. He writes something to the effect that "my wife, the daughter of so-and-so, is divorced by me today," and he calls two witnesses, all of it in the presence of the proper authority. The written record, whose doctrine the treatise established in its chapter on debt, is applied here to the ending of a marriage as it was to the making of a loan.

But writing alone does not do it. It is not a substitute for verbal pronunciation; a sound and explicit utterance of the word of divorce is required. And even where there is an intention to leave the woman in doubt, a wink or a gesture does not constitute divorce. A marriage cannot be ended by implication.

Two harder cases follow. The first concerns death during the counted days: if a husband dies during the first or second divorce period, and the other party is still alive — even if the husband fell ill during those days — and death came before the 'iddah was complete, then the instrument of that dissolution is complete. The second is the sequence of divorces. If the man divorces the woman once and then a second time, and she then marries another man, and the 'iddah of that second marriage ends and she is released from it, she may return to the first husband — who retains one more divorce with her, that being the second of his remaining. Divorcing a wife after she has returned to him from a second husband does not give him the furthest three full separations from her. The count is not reset by the intervening marriage.

Certain conditions are dispensed with in specific cases. A pregnant woman who has not reached full term, a woman who is not an adult, and a woman not yet touched by her husband: for such a woman, if three months pass from the divorce, her waiting period is complete.

Then khul'a — the dissolution the wife buys. The treatise sets it under the verse: "It is not permissible for you to take back anything of what you have given them" — unless both parties fear that they cannot maintain the limits of God, in which case there is no blame upon either of them. And before any of that, where there is discord between them, an arbitrator from his family and an arbitrator from hers should be appointed; if they seek reconciliation, God will bring them together.

The mechanism is a negotiated exchange. If union between the couple is not possible, and the woman says, "I cannot fulfill your rights as a returning wife — take this amount from me in exchange," then she is giving something in return for her release. It may be an unspecified amount or a known sum; her dowry returned to him, or her service for a period, or something similar. If the husband agrees and says, "I accept your satisfaction," and the judge accepts this with witnesses and a written document — recording that on such a date the husband is pleased and the wife has offered an amount acceptable to both — then the khul'a is valid, and neither will be judged from it unjustly.

The arbitration the arbitrators settle upon must be agreed between the two spouses, and once agreed it is binding. The treatise notes that this procedure was set down by the author in his legal compilation so that neither spouse transgresses the rights of the other. Whoever calls them before a judge to agree between them, or declares an explicit divorce, should have her observe her 'iddah in her husband's house or elsewhere — because there are no courts standing ready to catch them before they fight the matter out. And it is not permissible to marry another until the waiting period is completed.

Which brings the section to the waiting period, where the treatise becomes as precise as only a counting rule can be. Those of you who die and leave behind wives — they shall wait four months and ten days. Divorced women shall wait three menstrual cycles. As for women past the age of menstruation, if three months have passed since the divorce, that is their waiting period. Those who are pregnant wait until they give birth. The widow's four months and ten days apply whether she is an adult or a minor.

Inside a revocable divorce the marriage is not yet over. The divorced woman's intention, where possible, is to observe her 'iddah in her husband's house; the husband may take her back; and maintenance and housing come from his resources throughout. In a first or second revocable divorce both parties retain their options — the waiting period may be reviewed, and if both wish to reconcile, the husband may return to her with witnesses and a written document of return. If instead the parties renew their intentions after the period has run out, that requires a new marriage contract, following the exception of a sound marriage after an irrevocable divorce.

The third divorce is where everything changes. After it, the wife has no maintenance and no housing from the husband. Both still have their two choices — she may return by way of their religion's rules if they see fit — but after the third divorce the husband has no right to her except by her marrying another husband who consummates the marriage with her, and who then divorces her or dies. And the treatise closes that door against the obvious workaround with unusual sharpness: this is not for those living in the world today who merely go through the motions of remarriage. The dissolution must be genuine, without any manipulation or arrangement. It is not permissible for a man who has issued the third divorce to return to her through a sham arrangement.

For the woman who has not reached puberty and for those who have, the situation is similar; and during the 'iddah she is not required to travel or attend gatherings of women, even in the most honored and virtuous of nights.

## Wealth that leaves your hands

Having spent its length on what a man owes his household while he is present and after he has gone, the treatise turns to the whole question of wealth leaving a person's hands — first by choice, then by instruction, and finally by law.

It opens on the most famous image the subject has. "The example of those who spend their wealth in the way of Allah is like a grain that sprouts seven ears, each ear containing a hundred grains. And Allah multiplies for whom He wills, and Allah is Vast and Knowing." The meaning, the treatise says, is that those who spend their wealth for the sake of God — not following it with reminder of their gift, nor with harm — have their reward with their Lord, and there is no fear upon them, nor shall they grieve. The two disqualifiers are named as carefully as the reward.

On whom to give to, the treatise is blunt. The encouragement is to give to one's children and to those most in need among them. The philanthropist begins with his own family and those close to him, then extends outward, and gives guidance to whoever accepts the favor without denying it — even where the material given does not ultimately benefit the recipient. And the giver does not ask anyone afterward for acknowledgment, because he is certain that in giving he will receive no blame.

Around that principle the surviving text gathers a cluster of sayings on the character such giving grows out of. When a greeting is given, one should return it equal to or better than it was offered. Ja'far al-Sadiq said that being a good neighbor is better than merely having a good dwelling. Reward is stirred within a person by the qualities of the people of faith; and when Allah wills good for a servant, He grants him both the will and the capacity to act upon it. The people of good deeds in this world are likewise the foremost in the Hereafter. Our master al-Nu'man, whenever he is mentioned in the narrations concerning the virtues of service and reward, is described as one whose deeds were for Allah and for the sake of Allah, aligned with the benefits of religion and its people. And concerning Ja'far al-Sadiq it is related that a man continues to receive wages after his death, so long as good works continue in his name — through prayers offered for him, or righteous deeds that carry on as though he were still alive.

That saying is the hinge of the section: if a man's account keeps earning after his death, the structure of what he leaves behind is the shape of his continuing deed.

So the treatise starts sorting. First, motive: there is a distinction between the man who spends his wealth to be seen by people, believing neither in Allah nor in the Last Day, and the man who gives sincerely. Second, category: goodness may be expressed in word or in deed, for a short time or a long one, while charity is more specific in its conditions. Third, and this is where the law begins, instrument: wealth — whether land, livestock, or other property — divides into the gift and the endowment.

A gift is the transfer of something from one person's ownership to another's, either for a set period or indefinitely, and where a term was set the giver may reclaim it once that term has elapsed. An endowment is the perpetual dedication of an asset such that it cannot be inherited or sold after the founder's death. The intended benefit continues for as long as the asset remains — which is why the treatise notes that it works best when the asset is real property rather than something subject to loss or damage, and observes that risk of loss weighs differently depending on the nature of the asset and the degree of the holder's knowledge.

Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq is the authority on the dividing line: whoever gives a gift in perpetuity, seeking the face of Allah and the abode of the Hereafter, has severed the bond of return between himself and that gift — it cannot revert to him. A gift made for a defined period may be reclaimed once that period has ended, provided the asset remains intact; if it has perished, there is nothing to return.

Between the outright gift and the formal endowment sit the arrangements hardest to classify. The scholars distinguished between a man permitting someone to live in his house for an unspecified period without compensation — which resembles an endowment in name even if it does not meet the full definition — and a formal waqf. It is recorded that the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him, designated certain property as a waqf, drawing from its yield for his hospitality while the principal remained dedicated. A lifelong grant allows the recipient to live in the property for the remainder of his life, or in some arrangements for the lifetime of either party; upon death the ruling that had governed the property is lifted and it returns to its original owners or their heirs.

Among the narrations, it is related that the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him, said that the one who gives charity is like one who is delivered and set free — and this applies to the living believer who wishes to draw closer to Allah through his wealth. Several questions of completion follow. It was asked about a man who gives charity and then the recipient dies before accepting it: the scholars said that if the recipient accepted it the charity is valid, and if he did not, it is as though it was never completed. Questions were also put about charitable gifts made in a state of illness, and about accepting charity on behalf of a minor. Each turns on whether proper receipt occurred. A charity is not complete because it was intended, but because it was received.

On the waqf proper, the treatise is administrative and thorough. It is permissible to establish a waqf over any property suitable for endowment — animals, land, jewelry, clothing, or other goods — for the benefit of oneself or of others, including one's own heirs, whether equally among them or with distinctions by gender or lineage. The conditions should be recorded in writing so that no ambiguity remains. If the founder stipulates a right to draw upon the endowment for a particular need, that right belongs to him within those conditions, and the endowment does not cease to exist on that account. After his death the waqf is administered among its beneficiaries according to its terms. The administrator takes charge of it and serves those entitled, drawing a reasonable compensation if needed; if some portion has deteriorated he may address it; a trustee may be appointed to oversee both positions; and if assets wear out, their value is assessed. Where a specific term is stipulated, those restrictions are observed; and once the endowment is functioning, there is no objection to dividing the benefit among the beneficiaries equally or proportionally, according to what the founder specified.

Then the treatise stops explaining and produces a model. It records the testament of the Father of Imams, as mentioned in the foundational texts, for greater clarity in this chapter — presenting it as a summary of what he stipulated rather than as his address word for word.

He dedicated his wealth for the sake of Allah — what was his of properties and what surrounded them — allowing no one a path against it. His freed servants were to work under five specified conditions, and from the endowment's yield they were to receive their expenditures and their sustenance. He did not intend it as something to be sold or given away; it was charity in the way of Allah, as he had written to those close to him. The charity is binding even after his death, so that all of it continues to be spent seeking the face of Allah, for the near and the distant relatives from Banu Hashim and Banu Abd al-Muttalib.

The administration then descends by name. Al-Hasan ibn Ali was to eat from it in the proper manner and to spend it as Allah shows him, without embarrassment, so long as it remains in its place as charitable wealth; and if he wishes to derive a wholesome benefit from it, he may do so without reproach. If al-Hasan ibn Ali passes away and al-Husayn is still living, the matter transfers to al-Husayn ibn Ali, who is to act in it as well as the one who came before him. Whoever is of the sons of Fatima has the same standing in this charity as was written for the sons of the Father of Imams — for it is dedicated to the face of Allah. Then honor and glorify the noble and the blessed, and be satisfied with what has come to pass in goodness. Each successor is to look after this matter to its end.

That document is the doctrine working at once: a perpetual dedication, written conditions, named administrators in succession, a defined class of beneficiaries, and a clause forbidding sale or gift.

From the endowment the treatise moves to the will, the same act performed later and under more constraint. The best will is for the parents and the nearest of kin, as a right upon the God-fearing. Where people are present at the time of death, the testament must be made justly between them, two witnesses are required, and it is to be written clearly. Imam Abu Ja'far said that the will is a right upon every Muslim.

The reasoning is given plainly: a human being inevitably departs this world, and during life he will have had dealings over property — buying, selling, incurring losses — so that what remains at his death is what is called the estate. And the estate does not belong solely to the deceased. Two-thirds belong to the heirs, if they accept.

Two conditions apply. The heirs must be of sound mind, and the testator may direct his wealth only within what is lawful. The heirs divide two-thirds among themselves according to the shares prescribed by the Sharia. Whatever the testator stipulated in a state of health or of illness, while his senses were sound, is to be honored; and whatever has already been fulfilled stands, even if sworn to. If a person confesses during illness to a debt not previously established by proof, the matter is considered carefully, and the heirs may permit it. Our master the judge said that making a will of one-third is recommended and permissible, without diminishing the rights of the heirs. If the guardian is present he is to be advised, and if he permits something within the will it stands without liability upon him, provided he has not overstepped what is lawful.

The order of deductions is the practical heart of the matter. What is first taken from the wealth of the deceased is the money for the shroud and the expenses for preparing the body; then the debt, if he had one that was limited. What remains is divided: one-third goes to his bequest and to the charities, if he was disposed to that. If he recommended nothing, no third is taken out at all — all of it is divided among the heirs. Insolvency is not to fall upon him. And his inheritance is purely his own, without the deduction of khums.

The heirs are arranged in layers by their proximity to the deceased, and the rule of the layers is exclusionary rather than proportional: where those of the first class of relatives are united, there is no need to look beyond them; and so to the second layer, and only then to the third. They are taken strictly in order.

The prescribed shares are carried by the verses the treatise quotes at length. What God commands you regarding your children — for the male is the equivalent of two females. If the heirs are women above two in number, they receive two-thirds; if she is one, she has one-half. For his parents, each of the two receives one-sixth of what he left, if he had a son. If he had no son and his parents are his heirs, then his mother receives one-third; and if he has brothers, then his mother receives one-sixth — after a bequest he may have made, or a debt. Your parents and your children: you do not know which of them is closer to benefiting you. This is an obligation from God; indeed, God is ever Knowing and Wise.

And for you is one-half of what your wives leave if they have no child; but if they had a child, then for you is one-quarter of what they leave, after any bequest they had made or any debt. And for the wives is one-quarter of what you leave if you have no child; but if you have a child, then for them is one-eighth of what you leave, after any bequest you had made or any debt. And if a man or a woman leaves neither parents nor children but has a brother or a sister, then for each of the two is one-sixth; but if they are more than two, they share in one-third — after any bequest made or debt, causing no harm. This is an ordinance from God, and God is Knowing and Forbearing.

Around those verses the source lays out a working grid: pages of tabulated cases setting shares for combinations of heirs — sons, daughters, father, mother, husband, wife, then the cases of a single daughter and of brothers and siblings — in the fractions the verses name: a half, a third, two-thirds, a quarter, a sixth. That material is schematic in the original, a legal table rather than prose, and is not reconstructed here.

Beyond the tables the manuscript deteriorates badly. The section headed as the eighth class — the sons of the deceased and the paternal relatives — survives only in fragments, and the later material on the uncle and the aunt alongside the grandfather and grandmother is worse still, with strings of words that no longer parse into rulings. What can be recovered is a set of principles rather than a table. That for the male the share is doubled, stated over and over as the governing ratio. That a uterine sibling on the mother's side takes a sixth as an additional share. That a paternal brother, whether one or many, may take all the wealth or divide it equally with his brothers. That the shares of the husband or the wife are deducted first, along with the debts and the remaining obligations, before any division among the wider heirs. That the mother receives a sixth in one configuration and a third in another. That a paternal grandmother takes her share when present, and that a later class gathers the uncle and the aunt together with the grandfather and grandmother from whichever side, with the one whose connection is stronger receiving the better portion. And that a guarantor stands for what is lost of wealth, and a guardian for the orphan.

The individual cases those principles were meant to resolve are not recoverable, and nothing has been supplied in their place.

## What this episode lands

Three things have been settled, and each takes a private matter and hands it to a public authority.

Maintenance was made a debt rather than a kindness. It runs at a median standard that is neither wasteful nor miserly; it covers instruction and upbringing as well as food; it does not lapse when a man disappears, because the missing husband is counted among the present for exactly this purpose; and it does not depend on the wife's piety. The one thing that suspends it is her own withdrawal from the marriage. And the divorced woman was deliberately kept inside the obligation, right through to the nursing of the child, on the ground that neither parent may be harmed on account of that child.

Dissolution was made a procedure with a public record. A judge, not a husband, decides whether a complaint of harm is true, and where the husband will not act the judge may issue the divorce over his head. The pronouncement must be spoken explicitly — a written note will not do it, and neither will a gesture — and it must be witnessed before the proper authority. The wife has her own instrument in khul'a, buying her release with her dowry or her service. The waiting periods are counted precisely, because everything hangs on them: maintenance, housing, the right to take her back, and the right of either party to move on. And the third divorce is final in a way the treatise refuses to let anyone engineer around.

And wealth was traced all the way out of a person's hands. In life it leaves by gift, which can be timed and reclaimed, or by endowment, which severs the bond of return permanently — proved not with a definition but with the testament of the Father of Imams. At death it leaves by will, which is a right upon every Muslim but reaches only a third of the estate, because two-thirds already belong to the heirs before anyone writes anything. What the will does not govern, fixed fractions do: the shroud, then the debts, then the third, then the prescribed shares in strict order of proximity.

The idea running under all three is the one the treatise stated in passing and then spent the chapter demonstrating — that a man goes on receiving wages after his death, so long as good works continue in his name. Maintenance, dissolution, and inheritance are all ways of making sure that what a person owes does not depend on his being there to pay it.
