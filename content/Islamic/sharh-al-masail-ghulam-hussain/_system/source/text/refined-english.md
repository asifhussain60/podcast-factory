Praise be to God for the blessings of early and later times.

This is an explanatory treatise. It sets out the place of Fallāḥ Ḥusayn and his brother, and records the care taken over its printing by Muḥammad Ibrāhīm, may God pardon him.

<!-- page 6 -->

In the name of God, the Most Merciful, the Most Compassionate.

All praise is due to God alone, and misguidance falls upon whoever strays after guidance has come to him.

The pillars of this work rise from Hind — that is, India — in response to the need for its rulings. From the available sources, the author summarised the issues translated in al-Muhannadī and left aside matters that had been set aside in earlier times, such as questions relating to enslaved persons, certain difficulties, and other topics. He likewise omitted some issues such as those of usury, mortgages, and the like.

This was accomplished in the presence of the supporter Saʿd ʿAlī ibn Ḥasan, may God preserve him, in the thirty-seventh year, when the review was undertaken alongside the judge and the scholar until the first of the month.

Taḥsīn ibn — carried this work when he travelled to the Indian subcontinent, concerning the conditions of the mission and the brotherhood in the thirty-eighth year. He was pleased to present it and engage with it, so the book was set out and rendered into Arabic. It was then brought forward, and God's provision was permitted for it; he was instructed to take it with him to the Arabs so that whoever was of them might benefit from it. And God is our sufficiency for success.

A Reminder on the Virtues of Earning One's Living

God Most High says: *"Travel the earth and seek of God's bounty."*

The Messenger of God — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — said: Trade, and keep away from laziness. Whoever earns his living through lawful means and seeks thereby to provide for his children and to spare them from depending on others will meet God with a face like the full moon.

He also said: It is better for one of you to take his rope, carry a load on his back, and earn thereby, than to come to a man whom God has blessed with wealth and beg from him — whether that man gives or withholds.

He further said: By God, it is better for a young man to drive his needle through leather in the way of God than to leave it. And it was said: O Messenger of God, if one has the strength for this and the means, should he not rather exert himself in the way of God? He replied: Indeed, the most tender path in the year of the Messenger of God — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — is this. And he said: I have seen that the best thing a man can earn is what suffices for his wife and children. He who takes on a debt for their sake and fulfils it has done well, and that is no fault upon him. The Messenger of God — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — said: If a man is steadfast in earning lawfully, he earns a reward like that of one who strives in God's path. He who labours and provides for his dependants will be rewarded; and he said to me: Make peace, for if one is unable to do so —

<!-- page 8 -->

— let him go out of his difficulty and strike the earth seeking God's bounty. He should not grow faint, for it is not the end. One day a man went out, working the earth, seeking of God's bounty — that is, of his family's sustenance.

Al-Ḍaḥḥāk said: People should make buying easy, selling easy, judgement easy, and satisfying needs easy. He described trade in this way and advised people accordingly.

Blessings are many. Once the Prophet of God — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — passed by some people who at that time were called brokers, and he said to them: "I do not call you brokers; I call you merchants." The swindler is in the Fire. So they closed the doors and the slave girl came. The Messenger of God — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — departed from the gathering, and the son of the people said: Tell me, O Messenger of God — what I heard yesterday, hold to what he said — and I say it today: security came when the truth was given. In this sense, a man said to the Commander of the Believers that he wished to trade. He said: Learn the jurisprudence of the religion of God. He said: Some of that — and you are the jurisprudence.

<!-- page 9 -->

Then trade. He — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — said: Do not hate a man for being occupied with the world, for if he is negligent in a worldly matter, he becomes more preoccupied with it. This was among his sayings.

He also said: There is no one striving in the way of God greater in reward than the man who strives to earn for his son and his children that which is good for them.

A man asked Imam Jaʿfar ibn Muḥammad — may God's peace be upon him — to pray for him and give him a supplication. He replied: I will not pray for you as you have asked. Rather, the Muslim ought to seek his sustenance until the heat of the midday sun strikes him. He advised some of his companions: Do not sit idle in the markets buying small things yourself that you ought not to buy — not for yourself, nor for those poor in religion. The wise man ought to examine small matters himself and look carefully, day by day. He saw a man from among the Companions carrying something in his hand and said: God dislikes that a noble man carry the lowest of things, lest he diminish himself before others.

Etiquette of Food

What are the courtesies of wholesome food and the conduct of the people of this age?

He — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — said: There is no honour for Muḥammad in it. They prepared their food, so the first food is called the blessing of God upon them; and do not waste it, for the Messenger — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — used to eat on his mount and sat upon its back, a distance of about a hundred, and he lifted his leg. He sat upon the earth and would say: "Food in its simplicity — I am a servant." He ate as a servant eats. He sat cross-legged in the masjid and would pray the first prayer, or recline or eat in the manner of the virtuous.

He ate with his right hand, and beside him was water; he would save it and would not eat from the edge of the bowl nor from the middle of the food, but would eat from what was in front of him. The food was small and he chewed it well, without rushing to the next bite before the first was finished. He did not fault the food; if something displeased him, he would leave it. And it is good to save something from the best of the food in obedience to the one who is patient until all the water is obtained. He did not drink water during the meal if there was still food in his mouth, or if he sneezed.

<!-- page 11 -->

He — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — would purify his fingers after the food was finished, so his hand remained in the manner of the Prophet of God — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — in eating and drinking: humility before God and glorification of his provision. Therefore the people of religion ought to imitate the acts of the Prophet and carry them out in their lives, without showing off or arrogance.

If one is on the earth, let him place his food on the ground if he is able. If he is on a bed, he may place it before him. Let him keep away from reclining at his table. If it is his custom to sit on a chair, he may place the table at a height suited to him, so that it becomes evident that he honours his sustenance.

It is narrated from our master Jaʿfar ibn Muḥammad — may God's peace be upon him — that he said: A man should not eat or drink with his left hand, nor take meat with it. When drinking water, let him say: "In the name of God," and when he sets it down: "Praise be to God." Let him look into the vessel first, and drink it pure and clean, pausing in three breaths. If you are thirsty, draw the water with your hand, sip it and pass the milk. The food itself must be lawful and clean, not sought out of pleasure and desire but out of piety and righteous deeds.

On the Merit of Feeding Others

God Most High says: *"And they give food, in spite of their love for it, to the poor, the orphan, and the captive, saying: 'We feed you only for the sake of God; we desire from you neither reward nor thanks.'"*

It is narrated in hadith that in Paradise there is a palace whose outer face is visible from its inner face, and its inner face from its outer — and it belongs to those who feed others, who pray at night while the people sleep. How great is the blessings of food! Food is guaranteed to satisfy those who partake of it, and their needs are met.

It is narrated from the Commander of the Believers — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — that the Messenger of God — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — brought seven captives and said to him: O ʿAlī, prepare a meal for us — a meal that will bring comfort to those burdened. He said: With the lamb, strike the necks of these, and release this one. The Messenger of God — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — said to him: Jibrīl has told me that the hand withheld from food — meaning: I will help you. And it is narrated from Aḥmad that he said: If your believing brother enters, feed him the best of what you have. If he is fasting, anoint him with something. And it is narrated from Imam Muḥammad ibn Bāqir —

<!-- page 13 -->

— may God's peace be upon him — that he said: Feeding a believer is equivalent to freeing a slave, and among the most beloved of deeds to God is bringing joy to a believer — whether by satisfying his hunger or by paying his debt.

Hospitality is one of the moral qualities of the believer and one of the noble virtues. The scholars of the guest's right agreed that hospitality is due for three days; beyond that it becomes charity. Among the most noble of the moral qualities of the prophets is generosity. One must draw close to his brother as much as he is able. If one has only water, let him offer it in the best way he can to his brother. If he cannot even offer a morseful, then God's abhorrence falls on day and night alike. Whoever humbles his brother will find closeness to him in a time of need; and whoever is invited must attend and say: He has the right — the one who called a fool who came and the one who called for less. Whatever they do not pray, the Messenger of God — may God's blessings and peace be upon him — said: Whoever eats the food of a bright man, eating in an atmosphere where fire is present, and then denies it — with the taste of the man who has other food to which he has been invited — he said: As for al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAlī — may God's peace be upon him — he saw legs turning toward the food and said to the one who was praying: Forgive me. He said: Why is al-Ḥusayn standing to pray — excuse me, I was arrogant.


<!-- page 14 -->
If you are fasting, Mawlana Ja'far ibn Muhammad said: if someone entered while fasting and was offered food, he should break his fast — unless it was an obligatory fast. He said further: if your brother comes to eat, do not restrain him until he has taken his share with you. He desires your honor. He also said: if someone comes to you, give him what you have, invite him, and do what you can. Speak with him, sit with him at the table, and treat him warmly — for a man's affection is shown by welcoming his guest with generosity. He said: I love it when a man eats my food with enjoyment, for that gladdens me. He used to say that when sharing a meal with parents and children, one should not sit in silence but rather speak and converse, for in that there is a blessing. The reward is multiplied through the shared stories of those gathered, because this gathering is one of food and fellowship with one's children.

On the Manners of a Guest

It is not in keeping with the Sunnah to enter a house uninvited at a time when people are eating, for that would be an intrusion — walking toward food to which one was not called is a transgression, and eating in such circumstances is forbidden. However, if it is agreed that a person may enter and the hosts are preparing food, he should wait until they have finished their prayers and then ask if they would like him to join them. If he knows that they sincerely wish for his company, he may accept. But if he senses that their invitation is made merely out of courtesy, out of a sense of obligation rather than genuine desire, he should decline.

If, however, a person enters the homes of those whose close relationship with him is well established, then his entering is permissible. As Allah Almighty said — permitting one to eat in the homes of one's fathers, mothers, brothers, sisters, paternal uncles, maternal uncles, paternal aunts, maternal aunts, or in homes whose keys you hold, or in the homes of your friends — there is no blame upon you whether you eat together or separately. The Messenger of Allah — peace be upon him — came to Barira and found food there while she was absent; the food had been given as charity to her. When she returned and was told about it, she confirmed that the charity had been given to her as such, and he ate with knowledge of that.

<!-- page 16 -->
In her reply, the charity had lawfully reached her, and he ate it with full knowledge of that and with happiness in it. It is narrated of al-Hasan that he would enter a house and, upon seeing food, would sit and eat from it, saying: "This is what we used to do." It is also narrated that al-Hasan would not allow anyone to enter his house without permission. One day he passed through the market and saw a vendor; he took something from his goods and ate it, and Hisham said to him: "What are you doing, O Abu Sa'id? Do you eat a man's property without his permission?" He replied: "The man's own conscience is his guide in what he eats." He then leaned back, composed himself, and said: "O Almighty — be mindful, be alert. Who is your close friend?" He said: "My uncle," meaning the one in whom his soul found rest and his heart found peace. Such is the nature of deep familiarity and the intimacy of two close companions.

Among the good manners of receiving a guest: if the finest food in the house is available, one should bring it out and present it, and one should not stint on hospitality. And from good manners is that a man, if he finds himself between two invitations, should not show preference between the poor and the rich, or between the near and the far.

<!-- page 17 -->
Rather, he should honor the first invitation received. He should give priority to the invitation of the poor over that of the rich, and he should not neglect those who are absent or far away.

One should refrain from accepting an invitation if the one who extends it is unjust or oppressive. The purpose of accepting an invitation is not to satisfy one's appetite, but rather to bring joy to the heart of the host.

One should not enter a house without permission, and one should not crowd the gathering through excessive numbers — a gathering should be one in which tranquility and dignity are possible.

One should not look around the room as though searching for what is missing. One should be considerate of the health and well-being of those present, and if one is seated among people, observe the proper manners of a guest.

The Messenger of Allah — peace be upon him — said: the last of the guests to depart should not linger. He also said: act graciously toward your guest. The Messenger of Allah — peace be upon him — urges promptness in hospitality.

He said — peace be upon him — look to the character of the mother, for noble character is a gift from Allah, and to whomever He wills, He grants it in full measure.

On What is Forbidden

What has been made forbidden to you are carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and that which has been slaughtered for other than Allah, as well as that which has been strangled, beaten to death, killed by a fall, gored, or partly devoured by a wild beast — unless you are able to slaughter it properly — and that which has been slaughtered on altars.

From Mawla Ja'far ibn Muhammad — peace be upon him — it is narrated that he was asked about something and said that it is not forbidden outright, but it is not permissible for one who is already full to eat it. He explained that foods fall into categories: the first category includes all grains, such as wheat and barley and the like; the second is the group of all pulses and plants, and everything in this class is not a natural food for human beings but may provide benefit and energy; and if there were no harm in it, we would not forbid eating it. The condition for permissibility is that it be used medicinally, or that it be less harmful than animal flesh. Also permitted is the flesh of camels, sheep, and similarly the meat of certain wild animals.

<!-- page 20 -->
Among those are animals that have neither tusks nor claws, such as deer and rabbits. Among birds, those without a crop are permitted. From sea creatures, everything that has no scales is forbidden to eat. Regarding eggs: if both sides of the egg are distinguishable — one side indicating an animal that may be eaten and the other an animal that may not — then the egg may be eaten; otherwise it may not. Insects of the earth, such as snakes, scorpions, and the like, are forbidden to eat.

As for the food of the People of the Book, there is a difference of opinion. The flesh of polytheists, Jews, Christians, and Magians is forbidden. If one is driven by severe hunger, one may eat only enough to stave off death.

The consensus of the Imams — peace be upon them — permits eating glands, the spleen, the liver, and similarly the turtle and crab after they have been removed from their shells. However, the horse, mule, and donkey are subject to restrictions. As for the lamb, if it is a matter of necessity during a shortage of food, the donkey and mule may be eaten but one may not ride them afterward. If one is in dire need and fears for one's life and is compelled to eat carrion or a prohibited thing, one may eat until satisfied; but if the necessity persists, one must assess what is strictly needed to sustain oneself.

<!-- page 21 -->
As for one's thirst for wine — it is doubly harmful until it is quenched, and one has no recourse but to avoid returning to it. If one falls into a situation involving oil that has become impure —

if it dies in the oil: if it is an animal that bleeds and the oil is liquid, it is spoiled; if the oil is solid, then what surrounds the dead thing is spoiled, but the rest may be eaten. As for what is cooked in a defiled vessel — the one in that situation has a choice: either to eat what was cooked or to leave it.

The first gift goes to the one who makes use of it. Muslims may eat what is sold in the markets by way of meat, whether or not it comes from a slaughterer whose background is unknown, provided it is sold in the general Muslim marketplace.

As for water: We have sent down from the sky pure water — including rainwater, river water, spring water, and well water — everything is permissible to drink. Fruits and their juices are permissible to consume unless they have been boiled to the point that the liquid has changed and become intoxicating; if one drinks it and becomes intoxicated, then whatever causes intoxication is forbidden by the Shari'a. As for cheese — much of it is produced and some of it is sold in our markets.

On Ritual Slaughter

As for what has been slaughtered: eat of it and mention the name of Allah over it, if you believe. The Messenger of Allah — peace be upon him — said regarding the animal: it is clear to us that from the animal — and concerning the patience required in slaughtering beasts — even if a limb is hanging after the cut, it is considered fully lawful. But if it is severed and cut before life has fully departed, it is forbidden to eat it.

He said — peace be upon him — that when one slaughters an animal, one should treat it gently, lay it on its side on the ground, and then say: "In the name of Allah, Allah is the Greatest," and quickly cut across the throat with a sharp blade; one should not press down on it until it has died. The blood should flow as one observes the animal in its final convulsions until it collapses and dies; only then should the head be severed and the animal skinned.

As for forgetting to mention the name of Allah, or mentioning it in a manner that falls short of proper intention — or cutting off the animal's head outright without the proper method — that does not render it permissible.

<!-- page 23 -->
There is no validity to a slaughter performed in ignorance, or one done deliberately contrary to the rulings — Allah does not accept such a sacrifice. If cattle, buffalo, or similar animals become agitated and uncontrollable at the time of slaughter, and fall into a pit from which it is not possible to perform the proper throat cut, then one may strike and stab the animal with a knife wherever one is able to reach it — it is permissible to eat it.

The consensus — peace be upon them — is that the fetus of an animal whose mother has been lawfully slaughtered belongs to the one who slaughtered it and is lawful. It is also permissible for a Jew, a Christian, or a Magian to slaughter if they mention the name of Allah. But whoever omits the name of Allah — whether from among them or from among the Muslims — and was accused of doing so deliberately, the animal they slaughtered is not permissible to eat.

Whoever apostatizes from the religion of Islam — whether he was a man or a woman — it is not permissible to eat what he slaughters. The blind man, the woman, and the boy before he reaches adulthood may slaughter if they do so properly. Likewise the mute, if he forms the intention and imagines the name of Allah in his heart. It is acceptable to purchase and eat what is sold in Muslim markets — whether meat, eggs, yogurt, clarified butter, or

<!-- page 24 -->
what has been rendered from fat — as well as fish caught from the sea, and locusts, regardless of whether the one who caught them was a Muslim, a Jew, a Christian, or a Magian. As for the catch of polytheists — one should be cautious.

On Hunting and What is Permitted and Forbidden

What is resolved from it: that which is weighed in the balance of the general principle

Hunting at sea is forbidden during the sacred months for those in a state of ihram, but it is permitted outside of those times.

The hadith of al-Tabarani and his narration — with Allah's safety in it — is on this point: he said that he will be glad if he says — the rain of the earth is lawful for whoever takes it, whether or not the animal was named over.

It is sufficient to eat what trained raptors catch — such as dogs, cheetahs, falcons, falcons of various kinds, and other trained hunting birds — as long as the name of Allah Almighty was mentioned when they were sent out.

<!-- page 25 -->
If the prey dies from the impact of the predator before one is able to reach it, yet one knows it to be a lawfully trained animal, then it is permissible to eat what it has killed. As for the dog of a non-Muslim — if one knows that it was sent by a Muslim, a Jew, or a Christian — then everything it catches from the hunt is lawful. And if a man intends to hunt and kills the animal with a sword, or wounds it anywhere on its body with an arrow or a spear, then it is permissible to eat it.

The zebra's meat is lawful, and it is distinct from the domesticated donkey. If one hunts an animal and it disappears and then falls from a height or into water and dies, one may eat its flesh — for it died from the wound inflicted on it, not from the fall or drowning. If one does not know whether it was killed by the wound, one should not eat it until one confirms that the killing blow came from an iron-tipped weapon before the animal died. One should not eat what a polytheist or a Magian has caught from hunting. Locusts: if they are taken alive by Muslims or Jews, they are lawful; if they die on their own, that has been mentioned.

He used to recline on brocade couches gilded with gold, and he would sit in the council of Pharaoh to bring benefit to people, for the people were weak."

It is recorded that Sufyan al-Thawri once visited Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq and observed that the Imam was wearing fine clothing. Jewelry for women — necklaces, bracelets, and anklets — is a lawful adornment, and women should not abandon it. However, a woman must not strike the ground with her foot so that the sound of her hidden ornaments is heard. **On the Rules of Sale**

God Most High says: ⟪ar:وَلَا تَأْكُلُوا أَمْوَالَكُم بَيْنَكُم بِالْبَاطِلِ إِلَّا أَن تَكُونَ تِجَارَةً عَن تَرَاضٍ⟫ — "Do not consume one another's wealth wrongfully, unless it be through trade conducted with mutual consent."

There is also a hadith forbidding the sale of pebbles, deceptive transactions, and sales involving uncertainty. A valid sale has three pillars. The first concerns the contracting parties, and from the contract of sale it is understood that the first condition is that the contracting party — the buyer — must not be a minor, nor insane, nor blind, for none of these is regarded as a serious transactor. The second pillar concerns the object of the contract — that is, the thing being sold — which must be capable of being transferred from the possession of the one who releases it to the other. There are five conditions for this.

<!-- page 29 -->
The first condition is that the thing sold must not be something whose consumption is forbidden. If it can be put to a use other than consumption, then buying and selling it is permissible — for instance, a dog or a cheetah kept for guarding, hunting, or tracking; the dung of animals whose meat is not eaten, for use as fuel or for treating wounds; elephants for riding; snakes and scorpions for use in medicine; and livestock and birds whose meat is consumed, whether they have come to their owner or been acquired. Items such as parrots, peacocks, and similar creatures may be sold. The exception is a dog kept for no purpose whatsoever — selling such a dog is not permissible. It is also permissible to sell idols if they are reshaped into something of sound use, or as relics. Impurities may be sold for use in agriculture. The second condition is that both the object being sold and the one who disposes of it must belong to their rightful owners, or that the owner has granted permission. The third pillar is that the sale be one of two kinds, since legal liability attaches to one of two categories: movable property and immovable property. Movable property — such as Persian goods, books, and similar items — can be transferred from place to place. Immovable property — such as land — cannot be moved from its location.

<!-- page 30 -->
As for movable property, possession is transferred when the seller's right passes to the buyer through inspection, testing, and receipt. For immovable property, possession is transferred when the buyer vacates it of whatever remained there at the time of sale. Accordingly, anything bought or sold before possession is taken — the sale is invalid. The fourth condition is that the object of sale must be capable of actual delivery; if it cannot be delivered, the sale is void. Thus it is not valid to sell air, or fish still in the water, or a fetus still in the womb, or grass still in a field, or something depicted on an animal's back, or a defective limb, and similar things — for all of these are things that cannot be clearly separated from what is not being sold, and so they remain indeterminate. An exception is made for the case where someone mortgages a thing together with something impure: the impure part is set aside and the pure part is sold — as in the case of milk in an udder sold together with milk already drawn out. In such a case, however, complete delivery may not be possible for the mortgagee or for a suspended transaction, since the period of the mortgage keeps the property in the creditor's hands, and an arrested asset remains in the hands of its holder — thus preventing the seller from delivering it. The fifth condition is that the object of sale must be specifically identified and described; if it is not identified by inspection, then for example if the seller says to the buyer, "I sold you one animal from this herd,"

<!-- page 31 -->
or "ten cubits from this land," or "a garment from among these garments — take whichever you like," this is a corrupt transaction. The seller must instead describe the object with sufficient detail. A sale is valid when its object is clearly specified by description — characterizing and teaching the buyer about the thing if it is not immediately apparent — so that the buyer's mind is put at ease regarding the described item. When the object of sale is clearly present at the time of the contract, it is valid and permissible to sell it.

The third pillar concerns the wording — the offer and acceptance by which the contract of sale is established and the object is transferred from one party to the other. This may take many forms: mortgage, exchange, outright sale, or other arrangements, all governed by the correspondence between offer and acceptance. For example, the seller says, "I sell you this," and the buyer agrees, whether in explicit terms indicating sale, or in accepted figurative expressions that convey the meaning. If the terms are ambiguous or the meaning obscure, the sale is invalid. If the seller wishes to exercise the right of option in the sale, he may increase the price accordingly — that option is his. It is permissible to sell dates, raisins, and grapes and other fruits while they are still on the vine or tree until they wither. He said: look once, and that is for you; the second is against you; and the third is an arrow from the arrows of Satan. Whoever abandons it for the sake of God, God will grant him in its place faith, whose taste he will find in his heart. Our master Ja'far ibn Muhammad, peace be upon him, was asked about a man who is watering and looks beyond it, and the boys say: a man should occupy his gaze with his own family — accept this and do not accept your temptation. It is no secret that these narrations, in which I mentioned the permissibility of looking, concern the marketplace and similar places where people mix openly.

There are communities among the faithful — as is the case today among some Arab peoples — where women are found alone in shops, with no one available to manage their affairs on their behalf. In such circumstances, there is no alternative but for them to go out, and this is permissible. However, the established practice among noble and distinguished peoples is that women of religion and honour do not leave the home except under necessity. It is a deficiency for a woman of religious standing to be seen by unrelated men. A man once came to the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him, and said: "O Messenger of Allah, should I ask permission before entering upon my own family?" On Lawful and Unlawful Women in Marriage

"O Prophet, We have made lawful for you your wives…" The hadith states that a man was swept away by his emotions and married his father's wife, with two witnesses present. It is transmitted that Salih said: a father and son may permissibly marry slave girls and daughters if the girls are young. Upon reaching puberty, however, the girls have a choice in the matter. It is well established that kinship falls into two categories: blood relation (*nasab*) and affinity by marriage (*musahara*). Blood relation on the father's side brings relatives into a close relationship with one another — they are joined by lineage and are considered kin. One born into this kinship is bound on both sides by ties of blood. When there is no such blood connection, the relationship is called *musahara* — affinity through marriage. Those who belong to this category of kinship are not strangers to the marriage bond. The relationship of *musahara*, like blood kinship, carries its own rulings regarding choice and permissibility.

<!-- page 85 -->
When those under guardianship reach adulthood — as if they had come into the fullness of life — the authority of the guardian may appear to be coercive. Yet these two forms of guardianship remain in force until the ward reaches puberty. The furthest point from puberty is the age of *musahara*, and the age of maturity differs among them. No one denies guardianship in marriage except the father — where one can be found — or his duly appointed agent. If a woman marries through someone other than these four guardians, the marriage is invalid. It is equally impermissible for these guardians to act on mere verbal consent alone. If an adult woman wishes to arrange her own marriage, she may appoint as her agent one of these four, or a genuine son, or a reliable relative, or her paternal brother to act in this matter, with two witnesses present. One of the guardians may also delegate authority to another among them who is living, allowing the one chosen to be appointed by the judge to act on her behalf. The matter should not be left unresolved, and no one else should be allowed to interfere. If the guardian refuses without valid cause, the judge steps in —

<!-- page 86 -->
— that is, the appointed agent conducts the marriage on her behalf. This is the path narrated from the Imams of guidance. The judge, moreover, may serve as a witness to the contract of two men, and there is no objection if those who served as witnesses to the agency act also as witnesses to the marriage itself.

On Conditions Stipulated in the Marriage Contract

Allah the Almighty said: "Do not say what your tongues describe — that this is lawful and this is forbidden — inventing lies against Allah." Mawlana Ja'far al-Sadiq, may Allah have mercy on him, said: "Any condition stipulated by the people is neither valid nor enforceable if it violates the Quran or contradicts the Sunnah." It is established that Allah has given the husband authority in certain matters: intercourse, divorce, maintenance by kindness, and the contracting of further marriages. If the time of the contract is divided between several wives, each receives her due share. The husband is obliged to fulfil the rights of each, whether his wife agrees with the arrangement or not —

<!-- page 87 -->
— but all of this must be conducted with fairness and kindness. If either party is wronged, the wife may bring the matter before the judge, though the judge does not have the power to impose divorce through payment (*khul'*) or to remove the husband from the home. However, the husband must not strike his wife for anything that is not legally obligatory. As for what contradicts the Quran, Allah says: "As for those women from whom you fear *nushuz*, admonish them, then forsake them in bed, and then strike them" — but if they return to obedience, seek no further means against them. Maintenance, the provision of what is necessary, and divorce each have their own proper place. In any case, if a man is a Muslim, he is entitled to certain prerogatives, except if he acts in a state of intoxication or moral filth. As for *khul'* divorce, the couple must be given a choice. The matter comes before the arbitrator, or a dispute arises — as has occurred in various cases over the years. Whoever wished to proceed with marriage would do so, with the proper documentation recorded in a book designated for that purpose. A man must deal equitably with his wife in the matter of good treatment, fulfilling the right of *raj'ah*, providing maintenance, and observing the waiting period. If none of the aforementioned conditions are met and a directive is issued — that is, one that is on the side of the woman — then the man's right regarding the couple, if he wishes to reconcile them when the number exceeds thirteen —

<!-- page 88 -->
— is that her divorce rests in the hand of the one who has usurped authority, so as to bring about reconciliation between them or to finalise the divorce. Both outcomes are subject to moderation. If the man wishes to divorce his wife but the woman objects, she may ransom herself by returning her portion of the division to him, or some part of it, and they may agree on a settlement. Reconciliation through mutual agreement is permissible, as Allah the Almighty says: "If a woman fears ill-treatment or desertion from her husband, there is no blame on either of them if they come to a settlement." The essential addition is that they reconcile with one another on good terms. Seeking pain through divorce is blameworthy except where it is necessary to complete the separation. It is also established that a man may divorce his wife in order to marry another. As for defects that neither husband nor wife knew of before the marriage but that became apparent afterward, each of them has the right either to remain in the marriage or to separate. These defects are enumerated in nine pillars —

<!-- page 89 -->
A man whose bride-price was provided by his people came forward. The Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him, said: "Make peace, and do not exaggerate in the matter of bride-prices, lest enmity result." It is also narrated that a man came to the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him, and said: "O Messenger of Allah, I wish to marry this woman." He replied: "Proceed." The man said: "I have nothing to offer as a marriage gift." He said: "This ring is your gift." The man replied: "Yes," and so he gave the woman in marriage to him. It is also said that a marriage should not be contracted without a proper *mahr*. It is known that twelve and a half *uqiyyas* of silver is the equivalent of roughly forty rupees. The Prophet, peace be upon him, used to set the *mahr* for his wives at a moderate amount, and it is preferable to settle it at that level. The brotherhood *mahr* of forty rupees became customary among the people, and this practice descends from the Mothers of the Believers. It is stated that entering into marriage without naming a specific *mahr* is valid, because the *mahr* is not one of the essential pillars of the marriage contract — it is permissible to contract a marriage without specifying it, though it is recommended to name it before the marriage is consummated. Decrees issued before marriage regarding the *mahr* —

<!-- page 90 -->
— remain in effect afterward. If the wife delegates the *mahr* to the husband before the contract is finalised, and it is subsequently established as obligatory upon him after consummation, this is the more widely accepted position. The husband does not simply determine the amount himself. Rather, the judge considers the woman's status among her peers, her circumstances and wealth, and calculates accordingly. If the amount is high, a comparable *mahr* is set — equal to the *mahr* of the wives of the Prophet, peace be upon him. Even if it falls below that, he may give her less, in proportion to his means. If the husband is assigned the responsibility of setting the *mahr*, she may accept whatever he gives. As for the husband who does not have much to offer, he should give what he can before separating from her, or make some suitable arrangement. The *mahr* does not belong to the parents or the guardian, nor to the agent or the one exercising jealous guardianship, unless it was given specifically to settle a matter pertaining to that. All gifts that reached the wife prior to the marriage belong to the husband; gifts that came at the time of the marriage or after it belong to her as her right. If it becomes clear that he did not use the gift well, she is entitled to reclaim that thing from others.

On the Verse of Mutual Rights

"They are a garment for you and you are a garment for them." A hadith recommended by the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him, has been transmitted — a matter that people continued to speak of until their tongues grew weary of it. He said: "Heed my words: prayer, and right conduct toward those whom your right hand possesses — do not neglect them. Fear Allah regarding women, for they are in your care like captives. You took them in trust by the word of Allah, and it is impermissible to use them as fuel for the Fire."

It is no secret that the original purpose of marriage is twofold: the continuation of offspring and cooperation in the good of this life and the next. Allah has made this clear in His words: "And He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them, and He placed between you affection and mercy." And those who say: "Our Lord, grant us from among our spouses and offspring comfort to our eyes, and make us leaders of the righteous" — this applies both to the believers and to the worshippers devoted to Him. Association through the bond of marriage —

<!-- page 92 -->
— is like a garden that delights the eyes with its varied beauty and the lushness of its blessings. Each spouse is a faithful companion and a source of good for the other. Allah has affirmed this in the Quran by saying that they are like garments upon one another — providing protection and dignity — and that they shall not be harmed, nor shall they be burdened beyond their capacity. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that the best among men is the one who is best to his family, and that a man who is married should conduct himself well from the day of his marriage — on that day or another, or twice — according to his circumstances, even to the point of sharing what is hidden and what is pleasant. This serves the benefit of maintaining the household and the wellbeing of those who dwell within it.

It is not permissible for a woman to dress like a man, nor is it permissible for a man to dress like a woman. A man should not be compelled to forgo adornment or clothing. On the question of a husband's rights over his wife, it was said: he has the right that she not dispose of his wealth without his permission; that she not refuse him herself; that she not fast voluntarily without his permission; and that she not leave his home without his permission. This, it was said, is one of the greatest rights of the husband. And as for the greatest rights of the wife, he replied: that he treat her with dignity and honour.

On Breastfeeding

"If you wish to have your children breastfed, there is no blame on you, provided you pay what you have promised in a fair manner." Parents may arrange for their children to be nursed by whoever is willing. A mother may say: "I will nurse him, my brother, for I am satisfied."

A hadith establishes that breastfeeding creates the same prohibitions as birth. It is noted that the ruling of breastfeeding applies specifically — the language here means that if breastfeeding takes place with the consent of the one responsible for the newborn, whether wealthy or not, it carries legal consequences.

The relevant rulings are as follows:

(1) If a woman breastfeeds a child without the permission of the one responsible for him.

(2) If the permission is granted by him, the ruling falls on the one who pays for it — whether it reaches the child through his ears or nose.

(3) If the woman's milk has increased in her breast and —

<!-- page 94 -->
— it causes her discomfort, and a milk vessel is lost, or what was placed in the child's mouth or given to a cousin is lost — within the two-year period, even with a single swallow — and the newborn inevitably swallows the milk in the first scenario, then breastfeeding does not require the consent of the one responsible. In the second scenario, the breastfeeding is unintentional and therefore should not be considered as establishing a prohibition. If the breastfeeding is done through a legitimate means — that is, through a proper nursing arrangement — then such breastfeeding does not establish prohibited degrees of kinship, even if the milk reaches the child by dripping through the nose or ear, or by tube in the case of injury, or by injection into the throat.

The second principle is that the limit of permissible nursing is two full years from the day the child is born, calculated as complete months. All who breastfeed the child within this period fall under the relevant prohibition. If the woman has never given birth or is infertile, it sometimes happens that milk appears from her — even a small amount — but the condition for the prohibition to apply is that the one who nurses must not be born of unlawful relations. If breastfeeding is desired after the child's bones have developed further, this falls around —

— a specific threshold, and such breastfeeding, when undertaken out of necessity, is permitted. The Imam, after a period of illness, is not considered to be nursing in a lawful manner under these circumstances.


<!-- page 95 -->
Milk-kinship creates a prohibition on marriage: those who share milk from a single woman become forbidden to one another as foster-siblings. If a man has multiple wives and a boy is nursed by one of them, the boy becomes the milk-son of that wife only — not of the other wives. Yet all of those co-wives are forbidden to one another's milk-children by virtue of the shared household bond. Even if the husband himself did not intend to establish milk-kinship, the suckling alone is sufficient to create the prohibition.

If a single woman nurses multiple children — whether they were brought to her from infancy or not — all of those children become milk-siblings to one another and are forbidden from marrying each other.

When a woman nurses a man's child, she becomes forbidden to him, as does her mother and her female ancestors, her daughters and her granddaughters. His mother may not be raised as a wife to her son's milk-brother, and her daughters are likewise forbidden in this way.

If the nursing woman is a slave, the prohibitions still apply fully: her daughters and the daughters of those above her in lineage are all forbidden, whether they came from the same husband or from another. No matter how the milk-kinship arises, the one who has nursed you is forbidden to you.

A man's foster-daughters, his foster-granddaughters — by way of example — are all forbidden to him through nursing. Nursing does not deprive a daughter of the natural prohibition; she remains forbidden. So too are his foster-sister, his foster-nieces through brother or sister, and his foster-aunts on both sides.

<!-- page 96 -->
Nursing also prohibits the combining of two foster-sisters in one marriage, and similarly prohibits combining a woman with her foster-aunt on either side, whether through nursing or through direct marriage.

A man who has nursed a son through a woman may also marry a different woman — one who was not nursed by that same woman — without any prohibition applying to her, because she is not a milk-sister but is otherwise unrelated to him. However, the foster-brother who was nursed is not guilty of anything regarding a woman who is not in this prohibited circle, nor is that woman his father's wife in any sense.

They may also marry the woman who nursed their son, in certain conditions, and similarly they may marry the daughter of a nurse or a wet-nurse — as long as those daughters did not themselves suckle the son or his children and no kinship was thereby established. If no Muslim woman is available, it may be permissible with a lawful non-Muslim woman under conditions, provided no harm results — for nursing does not override natural kinship, and nursing does not create prohibition in the absence of Islam's conditions being met.

On the question of whether milk-kinship is proven when the nursing occurs after the marriage contract but before consummation: the judge may confirm the prohibition between them. Complete nursing is defined as three consecutive days or ten separate sucklings. If friendship or intimacy had already been established before this was proven, the dowry remains owed and the retribution stands. After the first and second cases, the husband has no further claim, and they are to be separated. The testimony of a foster-brother is acceptable in this matter, and credible testimony is not to be rejected merely on suspicion.

<!-- page 97 -->
On the ruling regarding a missing person: if one knows the whereabouts of a missing person, that person is not treated as missing but as absent. The distinction is that the absent person is known to be somewhere and has a recognized last place of residence, whereas the missing person's location is entirely unknown.

If a wife complains about lack of maintenance from a missing husband, the judge should first investigate whether he left any property. If property is found, it should be used to provide for her. If a guardian is appointed to administer it, that guardian may spend from it on her behalf. If all the property is exhausted and she remains without support, the judge writes to the place where the husband was last known to be, in order to inquire about him.

If the husband returns before the waiting period is complete and wishes to reclaim his wife, he may do so — and the time he was absent counts accordingly. If he returns after the period has elapsed, then her right in that matter stands. As for the missing person, he is treated among those who are present from the day he went missing with respect to his wife's maintenance. The wife of a missing person, however, has no automatic entitlement beyond what can be established.

<!-- page 98 -->
It is known that his obligation is to provide for his family and to fulfill his duties toward them. Regarding maintenance and the related rulings:

There is a hadith sufficient for people to understand that neglecting those who depend on them is a grave matter — leaving those who rely on him without provision is a serious failing. A man should be kind to his family, and the median standard of living is one that is neither wasteful nor miserly.

May God provide, and from the sanctity of God's limits: if God wills, the people of the household are entitled to their religious knowledge, their upbringing in generosity, and what that encompasses. What is meant here is that Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn 'Ali al-Salim stated, regarding the words of God Almighty — "and no age is diminished" — that nothing spent in obedience to God is to be considered wasteful. It is from this principle that when a man finds he cannot spend on what has passed and he is responsible for someone — whether his wife in Egypt or otherwise —

<!-- page 99 -->
— and if he finds nothing, there is a choice between the two parties: if she accepts the situation, she remains; if she does not, then standing without maintenance entitles her to seek relief. It is also said that a woman of any age — whether young or old, or one who does not observe prayer — has a right to maintenance from her husband in accordance with her proximity to him. If the husband is a man of means who has left without his wife's permission and has been staying elsewhere at his own expense, then if there is rebellion on her part, she is not entitled to maintenance until she returns. As for the divorced woman, her maintenance is the husband's responsibility — except in the case of an irrevocable divorce — unless he fails to provide, and she remains silent about it against the husband until she gives birth. She is entitled to maintenance if she breastfeeds her child, or if she was divorced as the Almighty said — a mother should not be harmed on account of her child, nor a father on account of his child.

O Prophet, when you divorce women, divorce them at the beginning of their waiting periods. The command is given to the believers: provide for them and let their matter be dealt with honorably. That their provision is known and witnessed, and that it is just.

<!-- page 100 -->
Bear witness before God — so a letter was written to the judge of the matter, and he wrote back regarding divorce and its care. This applies to those who have not taught Muslims the way of marriage and its proper conduct, because people must follow their established ways.

On the subject of khul'a:

Marriage is not merely a contract between two spouses. Rather, the husband has obligations — the dowry, maintenance, and good companionship — and the wife has obligations — chastity, affection, obedience — along with other terms of the covenant. When this covenant is broken and one of the parties inflicts serious harm by neglecting the duties assigned to the husband, the marriage contract may be dissolved.

Just as marriage is not generally contracted by the woman without a representative or agent, the dissolution too is only initiated by the representative of the aggrieved party — specifically by the one who complains on her behalf and seeks protection of his wife from harm. The judge must investigate whether the complaint is true, and if so, compel the husband to cease the harm or remain silent.

<!-- page 101 -->
If the husband does not respond to the judge's intervention, the judge may proceed to issue a divorce on his behalf — though if news of his willingness reaches the court, and the situation on the wife's side allows it in the balance, the husband may be given her back into his care with renewed kindness toward her and the members of the household. After that, the husband has a choice: he may keep her on the condition he has described, or he may divorce her — and that divorce is revocable. So one must look to the holy law in this chapter to see how carefully it takes into account the rights of the couple and the proper ordering of their lives.

Divorce was recognized in earlier revealed laws and among the prophets as well, and it is in accordance with the law of nature — as we mentioned — because marriage touches the living relations of the couple under the purified covenants between them. If one party violates those covenants, the government of justice may deprive him of that bond, and this deprivation in the language of the Shari'ah is called divorce.

The benefit of divorce is sufficient for both parties, to spare each other further harm. And it should not be said that divorce is a final cure for everything — divorce falls on the one who wastes it through abuse, or on the one who seeks to remarry with humiliation, as the wise have said: patience in the face of hardship is better than separation.

<!-- page 102 -->
The bitterness of separation after intimacy is in Islam like the parable of nakedness after being clothed — it is something deeply disliked.

According to the jurists, there are five forms of marital dissolution: divorce ('iddah divorce), khul'a, ila', zihar, and li'an. Each has a meaning that we are obliged to clarify in its proper context, and there is also the divorce of the rebellious wife.

Regarding the divorce of 'iddah: when a husband wishes to divorce a woman, he should consider three things — that she is in a state of purity from menstruation, that he has not had relations with her after that purification, and the correct procedure is for him to record it, writing something to the effect that "my wife, the daughter of so-and-so, is divorced by me today," and to call two witnesses. All of this is done in the presence of the proper authority.

On the question of what happens if a husband dies during the days of the first or second divorce period: if the other party is still alive — even if the husband fell ill during those days — and death came before the 'iddah was complete, then the tool of that dissolution is complete. If the period was one of sitting in marriage and the man divorces the woman first, then a second time, and she marries another man —

<!-- page 103 -->
— the 'iddah of the second marriage ends, and she is released from that second marriage also, through completion or separation. She then returns to the first husband, who retains one more divorce with her — this being the second of his remaining — and the divorce of one's wife after she has returned to him from the second husband does not give him the furthest three full separations from her.

What is noted is that these five conditions are dispensed with accordingly. A pregnant woman who has not yet reached the full term, one who is not an adult or who has not yet been touched by her husband — for such a woman, if three months pass from the divorce, the count of her waiting period is complete. The writing is not a substitute for verbal pronunciation; a sound and explicit utterance of the word of divorce is required. Even if there is an intention to doubt the woman, a wink or a gesture does not constitute divorce.

On khul'a:

"It is not permissible for you to take back anything of what you have given them" — unless both parties fear that they cannot maintain the limits of God. If that is the case, there is no blame upon either of them. When there is discord between them, an arbitrator from his family and an arbitrator from hers should be appointed.

<!-- page 104 -->
If they seek reconciliation, God will bring them together. Regarding khul'a: if union and agreement between the couple are not possible, and the woman says, "I cannot fulfill your rights as a returning wife — take this amount from me in exchange," then she is giving something — whether it is an unspecified amount or a known sum — in exchange for her release. This may be her dowry, returned to him, or her service for a period of time, or something similar. If the husband agrees and says, "I accept your satisfaction," and the judge accepts this with witnesses and a written document — on such a date, the husband is pleased and the wife has offered an amount acceptable to both — then if both accept, the khul'a is valid and neither will be judged from it unjustly.

The arbitration that the arbitrators decide upon must be in agreement between the two spouses, and it is binding on them. This procedure was set down by the author in his legal compilation so that neither spouse transgresses the rights of the other. The one who calls them before a judge to agree between them, or who declares an explicit divorce, should have her observe her 'iddah in her husband's house or elsewhere — because there are no courts that await them before they fight over the matter if they seek to break off entirely, and it is not permissible to marry another until the waiting period is completed.

On the waiting period ('iddah) and its rulings:

"Those of you who die and leave behind wives — they shall wait four months and ten days." And divorced women shall wait three menstrual cycles. As for women past the age of menstruation, if three months have passed since the divorce, that is their waiting period. Those who are pregnant wait until they give birth. If the birth occurs shortly after the divorce, the number of divorces — three — corresponds to three menstrual cycles; three months for those past menstruation; and the waiting period of a woman whose husband has died is four months and ten days, whether she is an adult or a minor, extended or otherwise.

The intention of the divorced woman — if it is possible — is to observe her 'iddah in her husband's house. During a revocable divorce, the husband may take her back, and maintenance and housing come from the husband's resources. In a first or second revocable divorce, both parties retain their options —

<!-- page 106 -->
— the waiting period may be reviewed, and if both wish to reconcile, the husband may return to her with witnesses and a written document of return, and she remains with him. If the parties renew their intentions, that is another matter — one that requires a new marriage contract after the exception of a sound marriage following an irrevocable divorce.

As for the third divorce: the wife has no maintenance and no housing from the husband. Both have two choices — either she returns by way of their religion's rules if they see fit — but after the third divorce, the husband has no right to her except by her marrying another husband who consummates the marriage with her and then divorces her or dies. This is not for those living in the world today who merely go through the motions of remarriage — the dissolution must be genuine, without any manipulation or arrangement. It is not permissible for a man who has issued the third divorce to return to her through a sham arrangement.

For the woman who has not yet reached puberty and for those who have: her situation is similar, and she is not required to travel or attend gatherings of women during the 'iddah in the most honored and virtuous of nights.

On charity and spending in the way of God:

"The example of those who spend their wealth in the way of Allah is like a grain that sprouts seven ears, each ear containing a hundred grains. And Allah multiplies for whom He wills, and Allah is Vast and Knowing."

The meaning is that those who spend their wealth for the sake of God — not followed by reminder of their gift, nor by harm — their reward is with their Lord, and there is no fear upon them, nor shall they grieve.

Regarding the encouragement to give to one's children and to those most in need among them: those who sought help — a brother more beloved to God than spending two months for a cause — and 'Ali al-Salih, the philanthropist in the hall of benefit to the free, from a student, because he is certain that in doing so he will receive no blame. He does not ask anyone afterward for acknowledgment, and the philanthropist begins with his own family and his close ones, then extends outward and gives guidance to whoever accepts the favor without denying it — even if that raw material does not ultimately benefit him.


<!-- page 108 -->
The narrations record that when a greeting is given, one should return it in a manner that is equal to or better than it was offered. Ja'far al-Sadiq said that being a good neighbor is better than merely having a good dwelling. Rewards are stirred within a person by the qualities of the people of faith. When Allah wills good for a servant, He grants him the will and the capacity to act upon it. The people of good deeds in this world are likewise the foremost in the Hereafter. Our master al-Nu'man, whenever he is mentioned in narrations concerning the virtues of service and reward, is described as one whose deeds were for Allah and for the sake of Allah, aligned with the benefits of religion and its people. Concerning Ja'far al-Sadiq, it is related that he said a man continues to receive wages after his death, so long as good works continue in his name — through prayers offered for him, or righteous deeds that carry on as though he were still alive.

<!-- page 109 -->
There is a distinction between the one who spends his wealth to be seen by people, not believing in Allah or the Last Day, and the one who gives sincerely. The difference between general goodness and charity is that goodness may be expressed in word or deed, for a short or long time, while charity is more specific in its conditions. Wealth — whether in land, livestock, or other property — is divided into two broad categories: the gift and the endowment. A gift is the transfer of something from one person's ownership to another's, either for a set period or indefinitely; the giver may reclaim it once the term has elapsed. An endowment, by contrast, is the perpetual dedication of an asset such that it cannot be inherited or sold after the founder's death. The intended benefit continues for as long as the asset remains — particularly when the asset is real property rather than something subject to loss or damage.

<!-- page 110 -->
The risk of loss weighs differently depending on the nature of the asset and the degree of the holder's knowledge. Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq said: whoever gives a gift in perpetuity, seeking the face of Allah and the abode of the Hereafter, has severed the bond of return between himself and that gift — it cannot revert to him. A gift made for a defined period, whether short or long, may be reclaimed once that period has ended, provided the asset remains intact. If it has perished, there is nothing to return. Questions arise concerning lifelong grants and dwelling-rights. The scholars distinguished between a man permitting someone to live in his house for an unspecified period without compensation — which resembles an endowment in name even if it does not meet the full definition — and a formal waqf. It is recorded that the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him, designated certain property as a waqf, drawing from its yield for his hospitality while the principal remained dedicated.

<!-- page 111 -->
A lifelong grant allows the recipient to live in the property for the remainder of his life, or in some arrangements for the lifetime of either party. Upon death, the ruling that had governed the property is lifted and it returns to its original owners or their heirs. Among the narrations, it is related that the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him, said that the one who gives charity is like one who is delivered and set free — and this applies to the living believer who wishes to draw closer to Allah through his wealth. It was asked concerning a man who gives charity and then the recipient dies before accepting it: the scholars said that if the recipient accepted it, the charity is valid; if he did not, then it is as though it was never completed. Questions were also asked concerning charitable gifts made by those in a state of illness, and concerning the acceptance of charity on behalf of a minor — the ruling in each case depends on whether proper receipt occurred.

It is permissible to establish a waqf over any property suitable for endowment — whether animals, land, jewelry, clothing, or other goods — for the benefit of oneself or others, including one's heirs, whether equally among them or with distinctions by gender or lineage. The conditions of the waqf should be recorded in writing so that no ambiguity remains. If the founder stipulates the right to draw upon the endowment for a particular need, that right belongs to him within those conditions, and the endowment does not cease to exist on that account. After the founder's death, the waqf is administered among its beneficiaries according to its terms. The administrator takes charge of the endowment and serves those entitled to it, whether relatives or others, drawing a reasonable compensation for his role if needed. If the administrator sees that some portion of the endowment has deteriorated, he may address it appropriately. A trustee may be appointed to oversee both positions, and their satisfaction should be sought. If assets of the endowment wear out, their value is assessed and the matter handled accordingly.

Where a specific term and arrangement are stipulated in the waqf, those restrictions are to be observed. Once the benefit is restored and the endowment is functioning, there is no objection to dividing the benefit among the beneficiaries either equally or proportionally, according to what the founder specified. We record here the testament of our master, the son of Talib, may Allah be pleased with him, as mentioned in the foundational texts, for greater clarity in this chapter. This is a summary of what he stipulated: he dedicated his wealth for the sake of Allah — what was his from properties and what surrounded it — allowing no one a path against it. His freed servants were to work under five specified conditions, and from the endowment's yield they were to receive their expenditures and sustenance. He did not intend it as something to be sold or given away; it was charity in the way of Allah, as he had written to those close to him. The charity is binding even after his death, so that all of it continues to be spent seeking the face of Allah, for the near and distant relatives from Banu Hashim and Banu Abd al-Muttalib.

The best will is for the parents and the nearest of kin, as a right upon the God-fearing. As for those present at the time of death, the testament must be made justly between them, and two witnesses are required. The will is to be written clearly, and Imam Abu Ja'far said that the will is a right upon every Muslim. The reasoning is that a human being inevitably departs this world, and during life one may have had dealings over property and wealth — buying, selling, or incurring losses — so that what remains at death is what is called the estate. It does not belong solely to the deceased; rather, two-thirds belong to the heirs if they accept.

<!-- page 116 -->
Two conditions apply: the heirs must be of sound mind, and the testator may direct his wealth as he wishes within what is lawful. The heirs divide two-thirds of the estate among themselves according to the shares prescribed by the Sharia, and they are satisfied with that. Whatever the testator stipulated in a state of health or illness, while his senses were sound, is to be honored; and whatever has already been fulfilled stands, even if sworn to. If a person confesses during illness to a debt not previously established by proof, this is to be considered carefully, and the heirs may permit it. Our master the judge said that making a will of one-third is recommended and permissible, without this diminishing the rights of the heirs. If the guardian is present, he is to be advised, and if he permits something within the will, it stands without liability upon him, provided he has not overstepped what is lawful.
