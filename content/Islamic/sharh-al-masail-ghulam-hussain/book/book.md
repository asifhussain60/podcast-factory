# Al-Muhannadi: A Guide to Lawful Living

## 1. The Dignity of Earning Your Bread

Praise belongs to God for the blessings of earlier and later times.

The treatise opens with the words of its own beginning:

> بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ

> In the name of God, the Most Merciful, the Most Compassionate.

> الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ وَحْدَهُ وَالصَّلَاةُ عَلَى مَنْ لَا نَبِيَّ بَعْدَهُ

> All praise is due to God alone, and misguidance falls upon whoever strays after guidance has come to him.

The work that follows is an explanatory treatise. It sets out the standing of Fallah Husayn and his brother, and records the care taken over its printing by Muhammad Ibrahim, may God pardon him.

Its foundations lie in Hind — that is, India — composed in answer to the need there for its rulings. Drawing on the sources available to him, the author summarized the issues translated in al-Muhannadi, setting aside matters that earlier times had already laid to rest: questions relating to enslaved persons, certain technical difficulties, and topics of that kind. He likewise omitted issues such as those of usury, mortgages, and the like.

This was accomplished in the presence of the supporter Sa'd 'Ali ibn Hasan, may God preserve him, in the thirty-seventh year, when the review was undertaken alongside the judge and the scholar until the first of the month.

Tahsin ibn carried the work with him on his journey to the Indian subcontinent — concerning the conditions of the mission and the brotherhood — in the thirty-eighth year. He was pleased to present it and engage with it. The book was set out and rendered into Arabic, then brought forward, and God's provision was permitted for it. He was instructed to take it to the Arabs, so that whoever among them wished might benefit from it. God is their sufficiency for success.

God Most High says:

> فَٱنتَشِرُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَٱبْتَغُوا۟ مِن فَضْلِ ٱللَّهِ
>
> "Travel the earth and seek of God's bounty."

The Messenger of God said:

> اَلتَّاجِرُ الصَّدُوقُ يحشر يوم القيمة مع الصِّدِّيقِينَ والشهداء
>
> "The truthful merchant — اَلتَّاجِرُ الصَّدُوقُ — is raised on the Day of Resurrection with الصِّدِّيقِينَ and the martyrs."

And he said:

The Master said:

> مَنْ طَلَبَ الرِّزْقَ حَلَالًا وَتَعَفُّفًا عَنِ الْمَسْئَلَةِ وَسَعْيًا عَلَى عِيَالِهِ وَتَعَطُّفًا عَلَى جَارِهِ لَقِىَ اللَّهَ وَوَجْهُهُ كَالْبَدْرِ
>
> "Whoever seeks his provision lawfully, keeping himself from begging, striving for his dependents, and showing kindness to his neighbor, will meet God with a face like the full moon."

The Master said:

> اِتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَاَجْمِلُوا فِى الطَّلَبِ
>
> "Fear God, and be measured in seeking."

> لَإَنْ يَأْخُذَ اَحَدُكُمْ حَبْلَهُ فَيَحْطِبَ عَلَى ظَهْرِهِ خَيْرٌ مِنْ اَنْ يَأْتِىَ رَجُلًا اَعْطَاهُ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ فَيَسْأَلَهُ اَعْطَاهُ اَوْ مَنَعَهُ
>
> "That احدكم takes his rope and carries firewood upon his back is better for him than that he come to a man whom اَعْطَاهُ اللَّهُ of His bounty and beg from him, whether that man gives him or refuses him."

It is related of him that during the expedition of Tabuk, he passed by a strong young man driving his beasts and laboring hard. Those present said: "O Messenger of God, if only this man's strength, vigor, and soundness were spent in the way of God — that would be better." The Messenger of God heard them and asked: "What is this work of yours, and for what do you toil at it?" The young man said: "I have a wife and dependants. I earn by this labor, I spend upon my dependants, I keep them from having to beg, and I discharge a debt that lies upon me." He said: "Perhaps it is for other than that." The young man said: "No." When the young man had turned away, the Messenger of God said: "If he is truthful, then he has a reward like the reward of the fighter, and the reward of the pilgrim, and the reward of the one who performs the lesser pilgrimage."

He said: When a man's circumstances become straitened,

> فَلْيَخْرُجْ مِنْ بَلَدِهِ وَيَضْرِبْ فِى الْاَرْضِ يَبْتَغِى مِنْ فَضْلِ اللَّهِ وَلَا يَغُمَّ نَفْسَهُ وَاَهْلَهُ
>
> "let him go out from his town — بلده — and strike out into الارض seeking of الله's bounty, and let him not bring grief upon himself and his household."

It is also related of him that he spoke of those whom God shelters in His shade on the day when no shade exists but His:

> رَجُلٌ خَرَجَ ضَارِبًا فِى الْاَرْضِ يَطْلُبُ مِنْ فَضْلِ اللَّهِ مَا يَكُفُّ بِهِ نَفْسَهُ وَيَعُودُ بِهِ عَلَى عِيَالِهِ
>
> "a man who went out striking into the earth, seeking of God's bounty what would suffice him and what he might bring back for his dependants."

And he said:

> اِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْعَبْدَ اَنْ يَكُونَ سَهْلَ الْبَيْعِ وَسَهْلَ الشِّرَاءِ وَسَهْلَ الْقَضَاءِ وَسَهْلَ الِاقْتِضَاءِ
>
> "God loves the servant who is easy in selling, easy in buying, easy in settling what he owes, and easy in claiming what is owed to him."

That is how he described trade — he loved it and urged people toward it, for in it lie much good and much blessing. The Messenger of God once passed by the traders, those whom people of that time called brokers, and said to them:

> اَلَا اِنِّى لَا اُسَمِّيكُمُ السَّمَاسِرَةَ وَلَكِنِّى اُسَمِّيكُمُ التُّجَّارَ وَالتَّاجِرُ الْفَاجِرُ فِى النَّارِ
>
> "I do not name you brokers; rather I name you merchants. And the merchant who deals falsely is in the Fire."

At that, they shut their doors and withdrew from trading. The Messenger of God came out and asked: "What is the matter with the people?" He was told: "O Messenger of God, they heard what you said yesterday, and they have held back." He said: "And I say it again today: unless a man takes what is rightfully his and gives what is rightfully due."

A report has also come down of a man who said to the Commander of the Believers: "O Commander of the Believers, I wish to enter into trade." He said:

> اُفْقِهْتَ فِى دِينِ اللهِ

"Have you gained understanding in the religion of God?" The man said: "Something of that." He said: "Understanding first, then trade."

And he used to say:

> اِنِّى لَاَبْغَضُ الرَّجُلَ يَكُونُ كَسُولًا فِى اَمْرِ دُنْيَاهُ لِاَنَّهُ اِذَا كَانَ كَسِلًا فِى اَمْرِ دُنْيَاهُ فَهُوَ عَنْ اَمْرِ آخِرَتِهِ اَكْسَلُ
>
> "I dislike the man who is slothful in the affair of his world, for if he is slack in the affair of his world, he is slacker still in the affair of his Hereafter."

Among his sayings also was:

> مَا مِنْ غَدْوَةِ اَحَدِكُمْ فِى سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ بِاَعْظَمَ مِنْ غَدْوَتِهِ يَطْلُبُ فِيهِ لِوَلَدِهِ وَعِيَالِهِ مَا يُصْلِحُ هُمْ
>
> "No morning setting-out of one of you in the way of God is greater than his setting-out to seek for his child and his dependants what will set them right."

A man once asked Imam Jafar ibn Muhammad (عليه السلام) to call upon God to grant him provision and to give him a supplication. He said:

> لَا اَدْعُو لَكَ اَطْلُبُ كَمَا أُمِرْتُ

"I will not supplicate for you. Seek, as you have been commanded." Then he said:

> يَنْبَغِى لِلْمُسْلِمِ اَنْ يَلْتَمِسَ الرِّزْقَ حَتَّى يُصِيبَهُ حَرُّ الشَّمْسِ

"It befits the Muslim to seek his رزق until the heat of الشمس reaches him." ان

He then counseled one of his companions, saying:

> لَا تَكُنْ دَوَّارًا فِى الْاَسْوَاقِ وَلَا تَكُنْ شَرَّاءً دَقَايِقَ الْاَشْيَاءِ بِنَفْسِكَ فَاِنَّهُ لَا يَنْبَغِى لَكَ وَلَا لِلْمَرْءِ الْمُسْلِمِ اَهْلِ الدِّينِ وَالْحَسَبِ اَنْ يَشْتَرِىَ دَقَايِقَ الْاَشْيَاءِ بِنَفْسِهِ
>
> "Do not be one who circles about in the markets, and do not be one who buys trifling things himself; for it does not befit you, nor does it befit the Muslim man of religion and standing, that he purchase trifling things himself."

One day the Master noticed a man among his companions carrying a bundle of greens, and said:

> انه يكره للرجل السرى ان يحمل الشئ الدنى لئلا يجترىء عليه احد
>
> "It is disliked for a man of honor to carry a lowly thing, lest anyone become emboldened against him."

## 2. At the Table: Eating, Feeding, and Being Fed

Food is a matter of religion before it is a matter of appetite, and the Prophet's conduct at his meals was itself a teaching. He ate as a servant eats, and he said of himself:

> أَنَا عَبْدٌ آكُلُ كَمَا يَأْكُلُ الْعَبْدُ وَأَجْلِسُ كَمَا يَجْلِسُ الْعَبْدُ
>
> "I am a servant: I eat as the servant eats, and I sit as the servant sits."

He disliked eating while reclining, and he disliked the posture of tyrants — stretched out or leaning back at ease. He would sit upon the ground, drawing one leg up and settling upon the other, and take his food in that posture.

At the table his practice was consistent:

- He ate with his right hand and began with water.
- He did not eat from the crown of the dish or from the middle of the food, but from what lay nearest him.
- He kept each morsel small and chewed it well, and did not reach for the next until the first was swallowed.
- He never faulted a food: if it pleased him he ate it, and if it did not, he left it.
- He forbade haste and gulping, and remained patient until each mouthful was fully swallowed.
- He did not drink water in the course of a meal unless a morsel had caught in his throat or he had sneezed.

When the food was finished he cleaned his fingers and washed his hand. This was the way of the Messenger of God in eating and drinking: humility before God and honor shown to His provision. The people of religion ought therefore to follow the Messenger's acts and courtesies in their own living, free from any display of greatness or vanity.

As for how the food is set out: if a man is seated on the ground, he may place his food upon a cloth if he is able. If he is upon a bed, he may set it apart from where he sits. If his custom is to sit upon a chair, he may spread the table upon something raised above him, so that his honoring of the sustenance is made plain.

It is narrated from Jafar ibn Muhammad (ع) that he said:

> لَا يَأْكُلُ الرَّجُلُ بِشِمَالِهِ وَلَا يَشْرَبُ بِهَا وَلَا يُنَاوِلُ بِهَا
>
> "A man should not eat with his left hand, nor drink with it, nor hand anything with it."

When drinking water he should say بسم الله, and when he sets the vessel down, اَلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ. He should look into the vessel first and drink water that is pure and clean, pausing over it in three breaths. If no vessel is at hand, he may drink from his cupped hands, sipping the water; milk, however, may be taken in a draught. The food itself must be lawful and clean, and the intention behind eating it must not be mere pleasure and indulgence, but strength for righteous deeds.

The chapter turns next to the merit of feeding others. God Most High says:

> وَيُطْعِمُونَ الطَّعَامَ عَلَى حُبِّهِ مِسْكِينًا وَيَتِيمًا وَأَسِيرًا إِنَّمَا نُطْعِمُكُمْ لِوَجْهِ اللَّهِ لَا نُرِيدُ مِنْكُمْ جَزَاءً وَلَا شُكُورًا
>
> "And they give food, in spite of their love for it, to the poor, the orphan, and the captive, saying: 'We feed you only for the sake of God; we desire from you neither reward nor thanks.'"

And in the hadith:

> إِنَّ فِي الْجَنَّةِ غُرَفًا يُرَى ظَاهِرُهَا مِنْ بَاطِنِهَا وَبَاطِنُهَا مِنْ ظَاهِرِهَا لِمَنْ أَلَانَ الْكَلَامَ وَأَطْعَمَ الطَّعَامَ وَصَلَّى بِاللَّيْلِ وَالنَّاسُ نِيَامٌ
>
> "In Paradise there are chambers whose outside is seen from within and whose inside is seen from without, prepared for whoever speaks gently, feeds others, and prays by night while the people sleep."

It is also said that the best of people are those who provide food. Whoever feeds a hungry man until he is satisfied and gives him drink until he is quenched, God removes him far from the Fire.

It is narrated from the Commander of the Believers (ع) that the Messenger of God was brought seven captives. He said: "O 'Ali, rise and strike their necks." Then Jibril descended and said: "O Muhammad, strike the necks of six of these and release this one." The Messenger of God said: "O Jibril, what is his state?" He said: "He is open-handed, generous with food." He said: "Is this from you or from your Lord?" He said: "Rather from your Lord, O Muhammad."

Also from 'Ali (ع), that he said:

> إِذَا دَخَلَ أَخُوكَ الْمُؤْمِنُ فَأَطْعِمْهُ مِنْ أَطْيَبِ مَا فِي بَيْتِكَ وَإِنْ كَانَ صَائِمًا فَادْهُنْهُ
>
> "When your believing brother comes in, feed him from the best of what is in your house; and if he is fasting, anoint him."

It is also narrated from Imam Muhammad al-Baqir (ع) that feeding a believer is equal to freeing a slave, and that the most beloved of deeds to God is bringing gladness to a believer — whether by satisfying his hunger or by settling his debt.

Hospitality belongs to the character of the believer and to the noble virtues. Honoring the guest is among them, and the limit of hospitality is three days; what is given beyond that is charity. Among the noblest manners of the prophets is generosity with food. It falls upon the one visited to offer his brother whatever is easy for him, even if it is only a draught of water. Whoever disdains to offer his brother what is easy for him remains in God's displeasure by day and by night; and whoever belittles what his brother offers him remains so likewise.

'Ali (ع) would attend invitations and say: "It is a right upon whoever is invited that he answer it. But whoever comes uninvited has come to what does not befit him."

And the Messenger of God said:

> مَنْ أَكَلَ الطَّعَامَ لَمْ يُدْعَ إِلَيْهِ فَإِنَّمَا يَأْكُلُ فِي جَوْفِهِ شُعْلَةَ نَارٍ
>
> "Whoever eats food to which he was not invited eats only a flame of fire in his belly."

He also forbade a man to feed another from food (اَلطَّعَام) to which he himself had been invited, unless given leave to do so.

It is related of al-Husayn ibn 'Ali (ع) that he saw a man who had been invited to a meal say to the one inviting him: "Excuse me." Al-Husayn (ع) said: "Rise and answer the invitation. If you are not fasting, then eat; and if you are fasting, then bless them."

Jafar ibn Muhammad (ع) said: when one of you enters upon his brother while fasting and his brother asks him to break the fast, let him break it — unless the fast is a make-up fast or a vow, or unless the sun has already passed its meridian. And he said: when your brother says to you "eat," then eat, and do not oblige him to swear upon you, for he desires only to honor you. And he said: when your brother comes to you, offer him what is easy for you; and if you invite him, take trouble for him as far as you are able.

He said to one of his companions who was eating with him: a man's affection for his brother is known by the goodness of his eating (أَكْل) from his food. It pleases me that a man should eat of my food and eat well — that gladdens me. And he used to say: when you gather over food with family and children, do not sit in silence, but speak of what is good and relate the stories of the righteous.

*On the manners of the guest.*

It is not of the Sunnah to enter a house uninvited at the time of eating, for that is an intrusion. It is reported that whoever walks toward food to which he was not invited walks as a transgressor and eats what is unlawful. Yet if a man enters and finds those inside at their food (اَلطَّعَام), he should hold back until they finish. If they call him to share with them, he should consider: if he knows they call him sincerely, wishing his company, let him share with them; but if he perceives that the call was directed only toward إليه — a gesture of politeness and embarrassment on their part — he should decline.

As for entering the houses of those whose closeness is established, God Most High has said:

> وَلَا عَلَى أَنفُسِكُمْ أَن تَأْكُلُوا مِن بُيُوتِكُمْ أَوْ بُيُوتِ آبَائِكُمْ أَوْ بُيُوتِ أُمَّهَاتِكُمْ أَوْ بُيُوتِ إِخْوَانِكُمْ أَوْ بُيُوتِ أَخَوَاتِكُمْ أَوْ بُيُوتِ أَعْمَامِكُمْ أَوْ بُيُوتِ عَمَّاتِكُمْ أَوْ بُيُوتِ أَخْوَالِكُمْ أَوْ بُيُوتِ خَالَاتِكُمْ أَوْ مَا مَلَكْتُم مَّفَاتِحَهُ أَوْ صَدِيقِكُمْ لَيْسَ عَلَيْكُمْ جُنَاحٌ أَن تَأْكُلُوا جَمِيعًا أَوْ أَشْتَاتًا

The Messenger of God entered the house of Barira in her absence and found food there — food that had come to her as charity. When she returned and was asked about it, she said that the charity had reached where it belonged; he had eaten knowing this, and she was glad of it.

The companions of al-Hasan (ع) used to eat whatever they found in his house without asking leave. Al-Hasan (ع) would come in, see this, and be gladdened by it, saying: "Thus we used to do with our friends."

It is narrated that one day al-Hasan (ع) passed through the market, taking now from this basket and now from that, and eating as he went. Hisham said to him: "What has come over you, O Abu Said, in your scrupulousness — eating a man's goods without his leave?" Al-Hasan (ع) said: "Recite to me the verse of eating." Hisham recited until he reached the words of God Most High, أَوْ صَدِيقُكُمْ. The verse struck him, and he asked: "And who is the friend?" Al-Hasan (ع) said: "The one in whom the soul finds rest and in whom the heart is at ease." Many narrations and the practice of the righteous run in this way.

The guest, if offered a choice among the foods, should choose freely and not count it a breach of the bond between them. Among the marks of noble character: when a man is pressed by invitations from two quarters, he does not prefer one over the other on grounds of wealth or poverty, or of distance and proximity — he answers the first invitation. He should give precedence to the invitation of the poor and the stranger, since such a person may not be able to extend hospitality a second time. He declines when the one inviting is a tyrant, a transgressor, or a man of evil.

Beyond this, of the guest's conduct:

- He should not intend, by his acceptance, the satisfying of his own appetite, but the gladdening of the heart of the one who invited him.
- He should not enter the house without permission.
- He should not crowd those gathered there, but sit wherever tranquility and dignity are possible for him.
- He should not fix his gaze on the place from which the food is brought out.
- When seated among people, he should give particular attention to those nearest him, in courtesy and well-being.

**On the manners of the host.**

The Messenger of God said: "لَا خَيْرَ فِيمَنْ لَا يُضِيفُ" — "There is no good in one who does not offer hospitality." And he said: "Take trouble for the guest."

The Messenger of God passed by a woman who had abundant flocks and herds and did not extend hospitality to him; then he passed by a woman who had only a few small ewes, and she slaughtered for him. He said: "Look at her. These characters are in the hand of God: whoever He wishes to grant a good character, He grants it."

- The host should not intend by his invitation display and boasting; he should intend by it the gladdening of the hearts of the brethren, and the following of the Sunnah of the Messenger of God in feeding food and bringing joy to the hearts of the believers.
- He should give particular regard, in his invitation, to people of merit and knowledge and to the chosen among hearts, even if they be of modest means rather than wealthy.
- He should clean the house in which he receives his guests and attend to whatever belongs to the honoring of guests.
- He should observe the appointed times of those he has invited, so as not to weary them with waiting. If some of those invited are late beyond the appointed hour, it is the right of those present that the food be set before them without awaiting the rest; if only a short while passes in waiting for the remainder, there is no harm in it.

And of the completeness of honoring:

> وَطِيبُ الْحَدِيثِ وَإِظْهَارُ التَّقْصِيرِ فِي الْخِدْمَةِ وَالْوَدَاعُ إِلَى بَابِ الدَّارِ حِينَ الْانْصِرَافِ

a cheerful face, pleasant speech, the acknowledgment of one's own shortcoming in service, and the farewell taken at the door of the house at the time of departure.

## 3. Clean and Unclean: Meat, the Knife, and the Hunt

### What is Lawful and What is Forbidden of Food

God Almighty said:

> حُرِّمَتْ عَلَيْكُمُ ٱلْمَيْتَةُ وَٱلدَّمُ وَلَحْمُ ٱلْخِنزِيرِ وَمَآ أُهِلَّ لِغَيْرِ ٱللَّهِ بِهِۦ وَٱلْمُنْخَنِقَةُ وَٱلْمَوْقُوذَةُ وَٱلْمُتَرَدِّيَةُ وَٱلنَّطِيحَةُ وَمَآ أَكَلَ ٱلسَّبُعُ إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ وَمَا ذُبِحَ عَلَى ٱلنُّصُبِ

It is related from Mawla Jafar ibn Muhammad (ع) that he set out what is lawful and what is forbidden in a concise summary. The lawful foods from the earth's produce fall into three classes:

1. Grains in all their varieties — wheat, barley, and the rest;
2. Fruits, of every kind;
3. Vegetables and growing plants.

Whatever among these nourishes, benefits, and strengthens a person is lawful; whatever causes harm is forbidden, except when taken as medicine for treatment.

Of animal flesh, what is lawful includes the cow, the camel, and the sheep, together with what comes from them. Among wild animals, everything that has neither fang nor claw is lawful — the gazelle and the hare, for instance, both of which are cloven-hoofed. Among birds, everything that has a crop. Among the game of the sea, everything that has scales. Whatever falls outside these classes is forbidden. As for eggs: an egg whose two ends differ in shape is lawful; an egg whose two ends are alike belongs to a creature whose flesh is not eaten. The creeping things of the earth — the snake, the scorpion, and their like — are forbidden as food.

A dispensation exists concerning the food of the People of the Book and other sects, provided the food contains nothing slaughtered by an idolater. The slaughter of Jews, Christians, and Magians is lawful to eat when it has been carried out according to the conditions of proper slaughter.

The Imams (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ) considered it disagreeable to eat the glands, the marrow of the spine, the spleen, and the gall bladder, and likewise the tortoise, the crab, and creatures that come out of shells. The horse, the mule, and the donkey are animals for riding and for carrying loads; yet the horse, if slaughtered out of necessity, is lawful to eat. The donkey and the mule, however, are forbidden.

A person in genuine need, fearing for his life, who is compelled to eat carrion or some other forbidden thing, may eat until he is satisfied. One compelled by thirst, finding only wine available, may drink until his thirst is quenched — but he may not return to it unless compelled a second time.

If a creeping thing without flowing blood — a fly, for instance — falls into a condiment or into oil, removing it leaves no harm, and the food may be eaten. If a creature that has blood dies in it, such as a mouse, the ruling divides: if the substance is liquid, it is wholly spoiled; if it is solid, what surrounds the dead creature is spoiled and the remainder may be eaten. That is the ruling of the Sharia. If someone finds this distasteful, the choice is his — he may eat, or he may refrain, or he may give it to another Muslim who will use it.

Concerning drink, God Almighty said:

> وَأَنزَلْنَا مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ مَآءًۭ طَهُورًۭا
>
> "And We sent down from the sky pure water."

This covers the water of the sky, of rivers, of springs, and of wells. Likewise lawful are the milk of any animal whose flesh is permitted, and the juice of fruits — whether boiled or unboiled — so long as neither has altered in nature or become intoxicating. If a drink intoxicates, it has become wine, and drinking it is forbidden in the Sharia.

### How the Slaughter is to be Performed, and Whose Slaughter is Eaten

God Almighty said:

> فَكُلُوا۟ مِمَّا ذُكِرَ ٱسْمُ ٱللَّهِ عَلَيْهِ إِن كُنتُم بِـَٔايَتِهِۦ مُؤْمِنِينَ
>
> "So eat of that over which the name of God has been mentioned, if you are believers in His signs."

It is related in hadith that the Prophet forbade cruelty to animals and forbade the maiming of beasts. If a limb remains hanging after being cut through and the slaughter then follows, the animal is wholly lawful. If, however, a limb is broken off and severed before the slaughter, eating that limb is forbidden.

Ali (ع) said:

> إِذَا ذَبَحَ أَحَدُكُمُ الْبَهِيْمَةَ فَعَلَيْهِ أَنْ يَسْتَقْبِلَ بِهَا الْقِبْلَةَ وَيَضَعَهَا عَلَى الْأَرْضِ ثُمَّ يَقُوْلُ بِسْمِ اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ
>
> "When one of you slaughters a beast, let him turn it toward the qibla and lay it upon the ground, then say: In the name of God, and God is the Greatest."

The slaughterer cuts through the windpipe with a sharp blade, taking care not to sever the spinal cord, then waits until the animal has died. He lets the blood flow until death is certain, waits for the animal to grow still, and only then removes the head and strips away the hide.

If a man does any of the following through ignorance or forgetfulness, there is no harm in eating the animal:

- He forgets to mention the name of God.
- He mentions God with glorification and declaration of His unity rather than the prescribed formula.
- He forgets even that.
- He slaughters without facing the qibla.
- The knife runs ahead and severs the head.
- He cuts below the proper place of slaughter.

If, however, he deliberately abandons any of these requirements — in defiance of God's rulings — his slaughter may not be eaten.

When cattle, buffalo, or other animals bolt at the time of slaughter and resist so that the slaughterer cannot reach the throat — or when an animal falls into a well, cannot be retrieved, and its throat cannot be reached — he may strike or stab it with a spear or sword wherever he is able. If the animal dies from that blow, eating it is permitted.

The Prophet considered it disagreeable to slaughter a milk-giving animal or one bearing young, provided another animal is at hand.

The slaughter of a Jew, a Christian, or a Magian is permitted when they mention the name of God over the animal. Whoever among them — or among the Muslims — deliberately abandons that mention and is under suspicion of having done so, his slaughter may not be eaten. Whoever apostatizes from Islam, whether by becoming a Jew, a Christian, or by joining one of the sects, his slaughter is likewise forbidden.

A dispensation covers the slaughter performed by a blind person, a woman, or a boy who has not yet reached maturity, provided it is done correctly. It also covers the slaughter of a mute person who understands, holds the mention of the name in his mind, and gestures toward it.

There is no harm in buying and eating meat, eggs, milk, or clarified butter sold in Muslim markets without investigating the one who slaughtered. Whatever is taken from the sea — fish and locusts — is lawful to eat regardless of whether the one who caught it was a Muslim, a Jew, a Christian, or a Magian. What idolaters hunt and bring alive is also lawful to eat.

### Hunting, and What is Lawful and Forbidden in It

God Almighty said:

> أُحِلَّ لَكُمْ صَيْدُ الْبَحْرِ وَطَعَامُهُ مَتَاعًا لَكُمْ وَلِلسَّيَّارَةِ وَحُرِّمَ عَلَيْكُمْ صَيْدُ الْبَحْرِ مَا دُمْتُمْ حُرُمًا وَإِذَا حَلَلْتُمْ فَاصْطَادُوا
>
> "Lawful to you is the game of the sea and its food, a provision for you and for travelers; and the game of the sea is forbidden to you so long as you remain in the state of ihram; and when you have left that state, then hunt."

Game taken within God's sanctuary is held to be undesirable. Yet should such game take flight and leave the sanctuary, it may lawfully be taken. The Prophet said that a bird caught and then released, and afterward taken again, is lawful for whoever takes it.

There is no harm in eating what trained hunting animals seize — dogs, hunting leopards, falcons, hawks, and other trained beasts and birds — provided:

- the name of God Almighty was spoken at the moment of releasing them, and
- it is established that the quarry died from the hunting animal's blow.

If the quarry is found while still alive, it must be slaughtered before it is eaten. If the name of God was omitted at the moment of release, there is still no harm in eating what the dogs or other trained animals have killed. As for a Magian's dog: when a Muslim, a Jew, or a Christian trains it, takes possession of it, and releases it, eating whatever game it seizes is permitted.

When a hunter cannot reach the quarry in time to slaughter it, and kills it instead with a sword, an arrow, or a spear — wounding it in any part of the body — there is no harm in eating it. The flesh of the wild ass is lawful, and its slaughter differs from that of the domestic donkey.

If a man shoots at game, strikes it, and the animal then disappears and falls from a height and dies, its flesh may be eaten only if it is known that it died from the shot. If this is not known, it may not be eaten. What is struck with a stone or a pellet may not be eaten unless something with an iron edge reaches it for slaughter before it dies.

There is no harm in eating the fish and locusts that an idolater or a Magian has caught, when he brings them alive to a Muslim, a Jew, or a Christian and they die in the hands of those persons.

### Dress and Ornament

God Almighty said:

> وَأَمَّا بِنِعْمَةِ رَبِّكَ تُحِلُّ
>
> "And as for the favor of your Lord, by it is the lawful made lawful for you."

And in the hadith:

> لِيُنْفِقْ كُلُّ ذِي سَعَةٍ مِنْ سَعَتِهِ
>
> "Let every man of means spend out of his means."

- Whoever keeps hair, let him tend it well.
- Whoever takes a wife, let him honor her.
- Whoever takes a sandal, let him keep it in good repair.
- Whoever takes a riding beast, let him give it سعته — its full measure.
- Whoever takes a garment, let him keep it clean.

The honor due to a garment is its cleanliness; the honor due to a house is its sweeping.

Mawlana Jafar ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about a man, and the questioner said: "O son of the Messenger of God, is it counted as extravagance that a man should keep many garments — adorning himself with them, and preserving some by means of others?" He replied: "No, that is not extravagance. God loves to see the mark of His favor upon His servant. The blameworthy thing is that a man of سعة should constrict himself, for God Almighty said concerning the man ذي سعة: he ought to spend from what God has given him."

A man then said to him that the person he held most dear was one who wore coarse cloth, ate food without seasoning, and made himself humble, so that the mark of humility showed plainly upon him. He said:

> وَيْحَكَ إِنَّمَا الْخُشُوعُ فِي الْقَلْبِ لَا فِي اللِّبَاسِ، وَمَا عَلِمْتَ اَنَّ نَبِيَّنَا يُوسُفَ نَبِىَّ ابْنَ النَّبِىِّ كَانَ يَلْبَسُ أَقْبِيَةَ الدِّيبَاجِ مُزَرَّرَةً بِالذَّهَبِ وَيَجْلِسُ مَجْلِسَ فِرْعَوْنَ يَحْكُمُ بَيْنَ النَّاسِ وَإِنَّمَا كَانَ النَّاسُ يَحْتَاجُونَ إِلَى عَدْلِهِ وَقِسْطِهِ
>
> "Woe to you!

Sufyan al-Thawri entered upon Imam Jafar al-Sadiq (ع) and saw fine garments upon him. He said: "O son of the Messenger of God, you relate to us that Ali (ع) used to wear coarse garments, and you wear better than that." The Imam said:

> وَيْحَكَ يَا سُفْيَانُ إِنَّ عَلِيًّا كَانَ فِي زَمَانٍ ضَيِّقٍ وَإِنَّ اللَّهَ قَدْ وَسَّعَ عَلَيْنَا وَيُسْتَحَبُّ لِمَنْ وَسَّعَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ أَنْ يُرَى أَثَرُ ذَلِكَ عَلَيْهِ
>
> "Woe to you, Sufyan. Ali lived in a time of straitness, and God has widened His provision upon us; and it is fitting for one upon whom God has widened His provision that the mark of it be seen upon him."

Imam Jafar ibn Muhammad (ع) laid down the governing principle on lawful dress in a single statement. Everything the earth produces may be worn without harm, and prayer may be performed in it. From any animal whose flesh is lawful to eat, the hide, wool, hair, and fur are all permissible to wear, provided the animal was slaughtered according to the rite. If it was not, wearing it outside of prayer carries no harm, but prayer may not be performed in it.

The Messenger of God loved white cotton garments, and he used to say:

> اِلْبَسُوا الْبَيَاضَ وَكَفِّنُوا فِيهِ مَوْتَاكُمْ
>
> "Wear white, and shroud your dead in it."

If a man wears silk and prays in it — not intending thereby any display or boasting before his companions — there is no harm in it. It is related of Jafar ibn Muhammad (ع) that he was seen seated upon a carpet bearing figures; when this was put to him, he said that the Sunnah is that such things be trodden upon.

For a woman, the following rules govern adornment:

- The neck, the hand, and the foot are the places of adornment.
- It is not fitting for her to go without ornament, whether of gold or of silver.
- She is forbidden to strike the ground with her foot so that the sound of her anklet is heard.

For men, adorning oneself with gold is forbidden, though for young girls and for women there is no harm in it.

## 4. The Anatomy of a Sale

God Most High said:

> ⟪ar:وَلَا تَأْكُلُوا أَمْوَالَكُم بَيْنَكُم بِالْبَاطِلِ إِلَّا أَن تَكُونَ تِجَارَةً عَن تَرَاضٍ⟫
>
> "Do not consume one another's wealth wrongfully, unless it be through trade conducted with mutual consent."

There is likewise a hadith in which the Prophet (صَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلَّمَ) forbade the sale of pebbles, and forbade deception (الْخَدِيعَة), fraud (اَلْغِشّْ), and sales involving uncertainty (اَلْغَرَر).

A valid sale rests upon three pillars: the contracting parties, the object contracted over, and the wording or act by which the contract of sale is established.

The first pillar is the contracting party. The buyer must not be a minor, insane, or blind, for none of these is regarded as a competent transactor.

The second pillar is the object of the contract — the thing sold — which must be capable of being transferred from the possession of the one who releases it to the other. Five conditions govern it.

1. The thing sold must not be something whose consumption is forbidden. If it can be put to a use other than consumption, buying and selling it is permissible:
   - a dog or cheetah kept for guarding, hunting, or tracking;
   - the dung of animals whose meat is not eaten, for use as fuel or for treating wounds;
   - elephants, for riding and bearing heavy loads;
   - snakes and scorpions, for medical use;
   - livestock and birds whose meat is consumed, whether they have come to their owner or been acquired.

Creatures such as parrots, peacocks, and the like may also be sold. The exception is a dog kept for no purpose whatsoever — its sale is not permissible. Idols may be sold if they are reshaped into something of sound use or kept as relics. Impurities and refuse may be sold for use in agriculture.

2. Both the object sold and the one who disposes of it must belong to their rightful owners, or the owner must have granted permission.

3. The object must be in possession, and possession takes two forms, since property falls into two categories — movable and immovable. Movable property, such as a horse or a book, can be transferred from place to place; possession passes to the buyer when the seller's right and discretion transfer through inspection, testing, and receipt. Immovable property, such as land, cannot be moved from its location; possession passes when the buyer vacates it of whatever remained there at the time of sale. Accordingly, anything bought or sold before possession is taken constitutes an invalid sale.

4. The object must be capable of actual delivery, both in law and in fact. What cannot plainly be delivered may not be sold: air, fish still in the water, a fetus still in the womb, the stud of a male animal, something depicted on an animal's back, milk still in the udder, and things of that kind. All of these are indeterminate because they cannot be clearly separated from what is not being sold. An exception is made where the impure or excluded portion is set aside and the remainder is sold — as when milk in an udder is sold together with milk already drawn out. Delivery may also be impossible in law, as with mortgaged or suspended property: the period of the mortgage keeps the property in the creditor's hands, and an arrested asset remains in the hands of its holder, leaving the seller unable to deliver it.

5. The object must be specified in its essence, its quantity, and its description. If it is not identified — as when the seller says to the buyer, "I sold you one animal from this herd," or "ten cubits from this land," or "a garment from among these garments, take whichever you like" — the transaction is corrupt. The seller must instead mark out and describe the object with sufficient detail. A sale is valid when its object is clearly specified by description, the seller characterizing and informing the buyer about the thing if it is not immediately present, so that the buyer's mind is put at ease regarding the described item. When the object of sale is plainly present at the time of the contract, the sale is valid and permissible.

The third pillar is the wording and the act by which the contract of sale is established and the object transferred from one party to the other. Such transfer may take many forms: mortgage, exchange, loan for use, and others. What must exist between the two persons is offer and acceptance, whether explicit or figurative. The seller says, "I sell you this," and the buyer says, "I have taken it" — whether in explicit terms indicating sale, or in accepted figurative expressions and customary usages that convey the meaning of a contract of sale. Where the terms convey nothing intelligible, the sale is not valid. If the seller wishes to exercise the right of option in the sale and to add to the base price, that option is his.

There is no harm in selling dates, raisins, grapes, and other fruits while they are still on the vine or tree, until they wither. There is no harm in buying something from a thief, an oppressor, or a betrayer, provided one knows with certainty that the item in question did not come from betrayal, theft, or oppression.

The Messenger of God (صَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلَّمَ) forbade النجش, which is the artificial inflating of the price of a commodity by one who has no genuine intention to buy, but who hears of another's interest and drives him on to purchase. No one should outbid the offer of his brother.

If a man sells his house or other property on the condition that, should the buyer bring the price within a known period, the property be returned to him, then during that period any increase and any decrease belong to the buyer. It is incumbent upon the seller, if there is a defect in the sold item, to disclose it to the buyer; otherwise the buyer may return the item, if he wishes, once he becomes aware of the defect.

If a dispute arises between seller and buyer — the buyer claiming to have paid the price and the seller denying it — and the dispute concerns property of a kind over which people do not ordinarily draw up documents, then the ruling is this: if the item is in the buyer's possession, the buyer's word is taken; if it has not left the seller's hands, the seller's word is taken, along with an oath that he did not receive the price. Neither party need take an oath if the sale is plain. If the sale concerns property of the kind that people ordinarily document — such as a horse, a house, or land — and the two differ over payment of the price, then the burden of proof falls upon the buyer to show that he paid what was due; otherwise the seller swears that he was not paid, as he denies it, and the price is handed over to him.

## 5. Lending Without Riba

God Most High, the Lord of charity, has granted that a loan made with the sincere intention of earning reward in the Hereafter is itself charity. He said:

> ⟪وَمَا آتَيْتُم مِّن رِّبًا لِّيَرْبُوَ فِي أَمْوَالِ النَّاسِ فَلَا يَرْبُو عِندَ اللَّهِ وَمَا آتَيْتُم مِّن زَكَاةٍ تُرِيدُونَ وَجْهَ اللَّهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُضْعِفُونَ⟫
>
> "Whatever you give as riba so that it may increase through other people's wealth does not increase with God; but whatever you give in zakah, seeking God's face, it is they who will receive manifold reward."

The one who gives a loan seeking the benefit of the Hereafter will have it multiplied for him in the next life. It is also said: whoever wishes to cultivate the harvest of the Hereafter, We shall increase it for him; and whoever wishes to cultivate the harvest of this world, We give him of it, though he will have no share in the reward of the Hereafter. It is said as well that whatever God accepts with goodwill is doubled for the giver — multiplied, that is, in the recompense of the next life.

Riba means increase. Not every increase, however, is forbidden; increases arising from sales transactions and similar dealings are not. In the technical sense of Islamic law, riba refers specifically to the increase that accrues on a loan when the lender's intention is worldly benefit and the principal is returned to him together with that increase. The blessings of God descend only upon the one whose intention in lending is sound.

A loan therefore falls into two categories:

- One in which the lender's purpose is worldly benefit.
- One in which the lender's purpose is the reward of the Hereafter.

Each category depends entirely on the intention of the one who lends, for actions are judged by intentions, and both categories fall within the domain of legally regulated transactions.

A loan intended for worldly benefit, and extended on that basis, means that if the borrower is in a position to repay, the lender has the right to reduce or excuse the repayment, to increase the time allowed while reducing the principal slightly or substantially, or to forgive the debt entirely — and there is reward in each of these.

As for a loan intended for the reward of the Hereafter, that reward is eighteen-fold; twenty-fold for maintaining the bond of brotherhood with a fellow believer; and twenty-four-fold for maintaining the bond of kinship — the latter also extending one's lifespan, as is related in the chapter on the virtue of charity from the Messenger of God (صلى الله عليه وآله وسلم). The owner of such a loan has no right to benefit from the principal in any way, whether small or great, nor to impose any condition that places a burden on the principal. God Most High has designated every loan made purely for His sake as an act of devotion, and every worldly benefit derived from it is therefore forbidden. Transactions were conducted among the Muslims for God's sake — that is, as a legitimate loan. Sometimes, even when the time for repayment arrives and the borrower is in hardship, the lender should cancel the arrangement for that period and accept the principal as agreed; and if the borrower is genuinely and visibly in hardship, the lender should grant him additional time and even remit the principal.

Such was the case with 'Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib and Uthman ibn Affan, who lent a Muslim man dirhams on the condition of repayment at the coming season. When the agreed time came, the Muslim man said: "If you take your right in full, you bring ruin upon these wretched children. If you take half and leave the other half for my children, I shall give you at the following season twice the remaining half — which would be satisfactory." When the appointed time came and they demanded their right in full, the man had nothing to give them, and the matter reached the Messenger of God (ص). Then this was revealed:

> يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَذَرُوا مَا بَقِيَ مِنَ الرِّبَا إِن كُنتُم مُّؤْمِنِينَ فَإِن لَّمْ تَفْعَلُوا فَأْذَنُوا بِحَرْبٍ مِّنَ اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ وَإِن تُبْتُمْ فَلَكُمْ رُءُوسُ أَمْوَالِكُمْ لَا تَظْلِمُونَ وَلَا تُظْلَمُونَ وَإِن كَانَ ذُو عُسْرَةٍ فَنَظِرَةٌ إِلَىٰ مَيْسَرَةٍ وَأَن تَصَدَّقُوا خَيْرٌ لَّكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

The meaning is this: the believers are called to fear God and to relinquish whatever immediate benefit they have been extracting from a borrower who cannot render it — this, if they are truly people of faith. The goal is that the lender become beloved to God and His Messenger, since the opposite course is nothing less than opposition to God and His Messenger. If the dispute is abandoned and the lender takes only the principal of the loan, he wrongs neither the borrower nor himself by forgoing the increase. A debtor pressed for repayment who cannot render the principal is to be granted respite until ease arrives. And if the entire loan is remitted as charity, that is better — for those who know the secret of brotherhood. Most of the jurists have confused this question. May God grant us and them the strength to grasp its intended meaning.

Both forms of lending were in use among the Arabs — and among the Christians, the Jews, and their associates — before the appearance of Islam and after it. Before the conquest of Mecca, trade and its prices lay in the grip of the People of the Book, who wronged the Muslims, particularly in transactions involving goods sold on credit or for cash: they gave little money and took much in return, in buying and in selling, until the situation became straitened for the Muslims and they had no stratagem by which to escape it. The Messenger (ص) waited for the appointed time and for the ruling from God. God gave him the conquest of Mecca, which he opened by God's leave in the eighth year of the Hijra, and with it the strength and standing of the Muslims grew.

Then, in the ninth year, this was revealed:

> ⟪إِنَّمَا الْمُشْرِكُونَ نَجَسٌ فَلَا يَقْرَبُوا الْمَسْجِدَ الْحَرَامَ بَعْدَ عَامِهِمْ هَٰذَا وَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ عَيْلَةً فَسَوْفَ يُغْنِيكُمُ اللَّهُ مِن فَضْلِهِ إِن شَاءَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ⟫
>
> "The polytheists are indeed impure, so let them not approach the Sacred Mosque after this year of theirs. If you fear poverty, God will enrich you from His bounty if He wills. God is All-Knowing, All-Wise."

'Ali ibn Abi Talib conveyed it to them, as the Messenger (ص) had commanded him regarding the chapter of Baraa, and recited it to the polytheists. The Messenger (ص) then commanded the Muslims to take up trade in grain and other goods themselves and to bring wealth in from outside the community. They continued to bring it through the port of Jidda — the nearest port to Mecca — until trade passed into their hands and their affairs grew strong through it. From that year they were able to pay the zakah on their wealth, and they dealt in both domains: the worldly and the otherworldly. With Muslims in straitened circumstances, they dealt for the benefit of the Hereafter; in buying and selling, they dealt for worldly benefit. When they saw that someone with whom they had a worldly transaction was in hardship, they were lenient regarding any increase owed, or gave the principal of the loan outright as charity, as God Most High had commanded. From such conduct, brotherhood and mutual generosity became firmly established among them, along with a disposition to prefer others over themselves; dealings and bonds of brotherhood multiplied in their hands.

They abandoned dealings with non-Muslims out of indignation, remembering how the polytheists had formerly treated them. Their aversion grew to the point that some would not permit dealings with them at all, nor drink water a polytheist had touched, nor eat their food. Yet the Sharia forbids none of that. Even gold received from them is not forbidden in general — only what they slaughter for idols or according to their own creed is what the Sharia forbids, not what polytheists merely handle. Where they are employed outside the governance of Islam, taking a wage from that is permitted, for their creed has no entry into the transaction — as the Shaykh said — and the same applies to what is offered to other than God.

The narration transmitted with general application from Mawlana Jafar al-Sadiq (ع) is that he said: it is not fitting for a man among you to enter partnership with a dhimmi, nor to entrust him with goods, nor to leave a deposit with him, nor to give him pure affection. This rests upon the custom that prevailed among the Muslims in earlier times, and is not a declaration that such things are forbidden in the Sharia.

A further category of riba is recorded in the hadith: "الذَّهَبُ بِالذَّهَبِ وَالْفِضَّةُ بِالْفِضَّةِ وَالْبُرُّ بِالْبُرِّ وَالشَّعِيرُ بِالشَّعِيرِ وَالتَّمْرُ بِالتَّمْرِ وَالْمِلْحُ بِالْمِلْحِ مِثْلًا بِمِثْلٍ وَيَدًا بِيَدٍ فَمَنْ زَادَ أَوِ اسْتَزَادَ فَقَدْ أَرْبَى الْآخِذُ وَالْمُعْطِي فِيهِ سَوَاءٌ" — "Gold for gold, silver for silver, wheat for wheat, barley for barley, dates for dates, salt for salt, like for like, hand to hand; whoever gives more or asks for more has dealt in riba, the taker and the giver alike." The reason such commodity-for-commodity exchange existed is that coinage was not yet in general circulation to meet people's needs — a situation that persisted, and still persists, in parts of Yemen — and so people traded one good directly for another.

Where coinage is current and people's needs are met by it — as in India and other lands — barter falls away, and with it this form of riba ceases.

Regarding the property of orphans, God said: "Test the orphans until they reach the age of marriage; and if you find in them sound judgment, hand over their property to them." It is related from Mawlana Jafar (ع) that he said: "The guardian of the orphan, when the orphan reads the Quran, has reached maturity, and sound judgment is discerned in him, hands over his property to him. But if he has reached maturity and has neither sound judgment nor a mind that can be trusted, his property is not handed over to him, and the guardian spends upon him from it with kindness."

The ruler withholds the power of disposal from one who squanders his wealth in the wrong place, and transfers it to whoever will administer it properly and meet his necessities. This withholding — whether from an orphan not yet of age or from an adult who squanders — is called الْحَجْر.

When a merchant's debts consume or exceed his wealth and his creditors press their claims, the ruler must place him under restraint and take charge of his property. From that property he is given whatever remains due to him: the rent of the house in which he lives, the hire of a mount, or the wage of a hired man. What remains is then divided among the creditors in proportion to their loans. Any outstanding dowry owed to his wife is reckoned alongside the creditors' claims. If something specifically belonging to a particular claimant is found among the property and that person seeks it, the matter rests with the ruler: if the item is of small value it is given to him; if not, it is kept with the property. The ruler and the lender then make his insolvency publicly known, so that no one may lose money by lending to him. This announcement is called تفليسا, and the one placed under restraint and so declared is called a مُفْلِسَا.

If the borrower continued dealing with people while his debts exceeded his wealth, and no complaint had yet been raised against him — so that he sold his estate or gave it to his children or to others — those dispositions are valid. But if it becomes clear to the judge that he acted with corrupt intent, the judge may annul them.

Neither the judge nor the ruler may compel the insolvent debtor at the creditors' demand; he is instead granted respite until ease comes.

A related case: a man incurred debt, was declared insolvent to his creditors, and afterward others lent to him — yet he did not prosper. Those who lent after the declaration of insolvency hold a prior claim over the earlier creditors. The earlier creditors lent while the man was still dealing among people, with some hope of benefit from their wealth, since he was buying and selling. Those who lent afterward, knowing he was destitute, gave only as support and assistance. Whoever lent after the declaration of insolvency therefore has the stronger claim.

One further matter: the insolvent is not prevented from marrying, nor from other such matters involving dirhams, nor from riding a mount along with his wife. In these the choice remains his, if he wishes.

## 6. Trusts, Pledges, and Shared Walls

The rental of a house, a ship, or a riding animal is a permitted contract, whether the animal hired is ridden upon another conveyance or put to some other use. In every case the amount and the term must be specified.

If a tenant brings into a rented house something harmful to the property, the choice rests with the owner: he may let the lease stand, or he may put the tenant out. The tenant may do within the house whatever his ordinary occupancy requires, provided it does not harm the building or weaken its structure. Work that damages the house and its fabric is not his to undertake without the owner's consent. If he carries out such work without permission and damage follows, he bears the liability. If he claims the work was done with the owner's permission, the burden of proof lies with him: if he establishes it, his word stands; if not, the owner takes the oath and the tenant is held responsible.

One who has rented a house that has since fallen into ruin or collapsed has no claim to compel the owner to repair it, but he has a choice: he may remain, or he may depart. There is no objection to a man renting a dwelling, living in part of it, and subletting the rest at a higher rate than he himself pays. Whoever rents a house in which there are trees, and stipulates their fruit, may lawfully eat of it. If the owner stipulates that should the tenant dwell there even a day or two the full month's rent falls due, that stipulation is valid. Whatever has been agreed must be written down. If nothing is written and a dispute arises between owner and tenant with no supporting proof, the word of the owner prevails. If a man rents a house in which goods belonging to the owner remain, and the owner promises to remove them but fails to do so, rent is owed only for the portion of the house the tenant actually occupied.

Whoever hires a particular animal or a particular ship for travel to a known place, and that animal dies or that ship is disabled before the journey begins, the hire is void. If this occurs after he has set out and covered part of the road, he owes according to what he has already traveled. If the hire was contracted for delivery and conveyance without any particular animal or vessel being designated, the contract holds for whatever animal or vessel is provided and the full wage is due. Whoever hires an animal for a fixed period may use it for the purpose for which he hired it, to the degree customary for such use; if he exceeds that degree, he is liable.

A further question: when a man hires an animal or a ship on the condition that it bring him to a certain place by a certain day or hour, and stipulates that a later arrival shall reduce the hire, that condition renders the hire contract void. The hirer nonetheless owes the wage originally contracted.

A further question: whoever hires an animal to reach a known place and then travels beyond it, and the animal perishes, is liable to the owner for its value. If the animal does not perish but the hirer has exceeded the agreed limit, the owner has a choice: he may hold the hirer liable for whatever diminution occurred during the excess period, or he may claim from him the equivalent of the hire for that period.

As for suretyship: when a man stands guarantor for another, or for a person's safety — undertaking to protect that person and his property and to deliver him unharmed to a known place, or guaranteeing a person's soundness — such guarantee and suretyship are permitted, and he may take a wage for it.

It is narrated from Mawlana Jafar ibn Muhammad (ع), from his father, and from his forefathers, that the Messenger of Allah (ع) permitted partnership in profit and in lands. When two men, or three, or more wish to enter into partnership in wealth, each brings out capital equal to that of his partners, or more, or less; they combine it so that it becomes one undivided property, no part distinguishable from another, and then buy and sell in whatever trade they see fit. Profit accrues to each according to his share, and any loss falls upon each according to the measure of his capital.

The partnership called مضاربة is likewise permitted: the capital comes from one party and the labor from the other, and the profit is divided between them according to the proportion and arrangement they have agreed upon. No portion of any loss falls upon the worker — that is, the مضارب — because the loss returns to the owner of the capital. If, however, the worker departs from his instructions and transgresses them, he is liable for whatever is lost. No valid partnership exists in which a man provides capital on condition that he receive a fixed predetermined portion. Nor should a believer take as a partner one who is not a believer, for that is the field of the believer.

Concerning pre-emption — الشفعة: an absent neighbor is waited upon in his right of pre-emption when their road is shared. From Jabir (رضي الله عنه), it is related that he said:

> «قَضَى النَّبِيُّ بِالشُّفْعَةِ فِي كُلِّ مَا لَمْ يُقْسَمْ، فَإِذَا وَقَعَتِ الْحُدُودُ وَصُرِّفَ الطَّرِيقُ فَلَا شُفْعَةَ حِينَئِذٍ»
>
> "The Prophet decreed pre-emption in everything that has not been divided; but when the boundaries have been set and the road turned aside, there is no pre-emption then."

He (ع) said:

> «لَيْسَ لِلْجَارِ شُفْعَةٌ»
>
> "The neighbor has no right of pre-emption."

And he said also:

> «شُفْعَةُ الشَّرِيكِ وَاجِبَةٌ إِذَا كَانَ بَيْنَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ، وَلَيْسَ لِلذِّمِّيِّ وَالْكَافِرِ شُفْعَةٌ، لِأَنَّ الشُّفْعَةَ حَقُّ الْمُسْلِمِينَ»
>
> "The pre-emption of the partner is binding when it is among Muslims; the protected non-Muslim and the unbeliever have no pre-emption, for pre-emption is a right of the Muslims."

Pre-emption is reckoned and apportioned by shares. It does not apply to a ship, to livestock, or to movable goods, but it does apply to things held in common:

- a shared road
- a shared wall
- a shared portion of a building
- a shared door
- a tree
- houses within a single quarter

When division has fallen between these things, no pre-emption remains. Likewise, after division, no pre-emption exists between the owner of the upper story and the owner of the lower, unless something shared remains between them.

The right of pre-emption belongs to the absent and to the minor just as it belongs to the one present and to the adult. A father may therefore exercise pre-emption on behalf of his infant child, a guardian on behalf of the orphan, and the judge on behalf of one who has no guardian. A claimant of pre-emption — whether present or absent, whether or not he knew of the sale — retains his right so long as its time has not passed. In the Sharia, the time of pre-emption is one year for cultivators; for others it follows the custom of the locality in which the claimant resides. Once that time has passed, pre-emption is extinguished.

The seller has no standing against a party if he failed to obtain that party's written testimony and mark at the time of the contract, for without it the one entitled to pre-emption may rise after the sale to press his claim. If the one entitled to pre-emption surrendered his right before the sale and then rose to claim it, he may still do so, because pre-emption is not established until the moment of sale itself. If the claimant comes to the buyer and the buyer says, "I bought it for such and such," and the claimant surrenders his right on that basis, and it then becomes known that the buyer paid less, the claimant may return upon him if he wishes. And if the seller reduced the price for the buyer at the time of the contract in the customary manner between contracting parties, that same reduction is granted to the one entitled to pre-emption.

Concerning العارية, the loan for use. God Almighty said:

> «وَلَا تَنسَوُا۟ ٱلْفَضْلَ بَيْنَكُمْ»
>
> "And do not forget generosity between yourselves."

When a man wishes to do a kindness and a good deed, he should begin with those nearest to him, in this order:

1. his children
2. the nearest of the believers
3. those next to them
4. the brethren in religion
5. the needy — and the most needy among them is the most deserving of alms and of kindness

Among the Sunnah of the Messenger of Allah (ع) was that he would offer a loan, grant the article on loan, and receive the guest hospitably. The loaned article remains the property of the one who lent it, and the borrower may use it only for what the lender permitted him.

A further question. If the borrower stood as guarantor for the loaned article and then destroyed it — or destroyed some part of it, or damaged it — he is liable for what he damaged and destroyed. If he did not stand as guarantor, nothing falls upon him, unless the lender establishes proof that the article was destroyed or damaged through the borrower's transgression and poor care of it.

A further question. If the borrower claims the loss of the loaned article and has no proof of that, and he is among those suspected of dishonesty and treachery, he is not believed, and he is liable. If the article had been given in pledge, the lender takes it back from the one holding it in pledge.

Concerning trusts. God Almighty said:

> «إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَأْمُرُكُمْ أَن تُؤَدُّوا۟ ٱلْأَمَنَتِ إِلَىٰٓ أَهْلِهَا»
>
> "Indeed, God commands you to render back the trusts to those to whom they are due."

And He said also:

> «فَإِنْ أَمِنَ بَعْضُكُم بَعْضًۭا فَلْيُؤَدِّ ٱلَّذِى ٱؤْتُمِنَ أَمَنَتَهُۥ وَلْيَتَّقِ ٱللَّهَ رَبَّهُۥ»
>
> "And if one of you entrusts another, let the one entrusted render back his trust, and let him fear God his Lord."

Mawlana Jafar al-Sadiq (ع) said in his counsel to a group of his Shia:

> «اللَّهَ اللَّهَ، وَأَدُّوا الْأَمَانَةَ إِلَى الْأَبْيَضِ وَالْأَسْوَدِ، وَإِنْ كَانَ حَرُورِيًّا أَوْ شَامِيًّا، عَدُوًّا كَانَ أَوْ صَدِيقًا»
>
> "God, God! Render the trust back to the white and the black, even if he be a Haruri or a Syrian, whether he be an enemy or a friend."

A further question. If a man receives a deposit for safekeeping and it is lost or perishes before he has secured it — through no wrongdoing of his own and without his having consumed any of it — then no liability falls upon him and no guarantee is owed by him. The one who holds a deposit and the partner in trade are alike entrusted persons. If the deposit is gone, the one who left it may not accuse the one who held it without establishing proof.

A further question. If Zayd handed a deposit to Amr, and Amr later said, "You did indeed deposit it with me, but you instructed me to hand it over to Bakr, and I handed it to him," while Zayd denies having given any such instruction, then the burden of proving that instruction rests upon Amr — it must be shown that Zayd commanded it. If no proof is produced, Zayd takes the oath. If Amr acknowledged that he handed the deposit to Bakr but Bakr denies having received it from him, then the burden of proving the handover rests upon Amr; if no proof is produced, Bakr takes the oath, and the deposit is recovered from Amr.

It is related that during the caliphate of Umar two men came to a wealthy woman of Quraysh and deposited a hundred dinars with her, saying: "Hand it back to us only when we come to you together." They then departed. After a short while, one of them returned alone and told her that his companion had been prevented from returning with him, that he had instructed him to come on his behalf, and he offered her an agreed sign between them. The woman, being of an unsuspecting nature, handed the money to him, and he departed with it. When the second man came and asked her to return his money, she told him that his companion had come with their agreed sign and that she had handed the money over to him. The second man said that he had given no such instruction. They brought the matter before Umar, who did not know how to judge between them, and so he sent them both to Ali (ع). The Master said to the claimant: "If you both instructed her to hand the money back only when you came to her together, then go and bring your companion and take your right."

A further question. It is not proper for the one holding a trust to spend anything of what has been deposited with him. A child who has not reached the age of discernment does not bear liability for a deposit.

Concerning the pledge, الرهن. God Almighty said:

> «يا أيها الذين آمنوا إذا تداينتم بدين إلى أجل مسمى فاكتبوه» ... «وَإِن كُنتُمْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍۢ وَلَمْ تَجِدُوا۟ كَاتِبًۭا فَرِهَنٌۭ مَّقْبُوضَةٌۭ»

"O you who believe, when you contract a debt for a stated term, write it down," to His words, "And if you are upon a journey and find no scribe, then a pledge taken in hand."

A hadith runs:

> «لَأَمِينٌ فِي السَّمَاءِ أَمِينٌ فِي الْأَرْضِ، وَلَوْ أَسْلَفَنِي لَأَدَّيْتُهُ، وَاذْهَبْ بِهِ عَنِّي وَأرْهَنْهُ عِنْدَهُ»
>
> "One trusted in heaven is trusted on earth; had he lent to me I would have repaid him. Go on my behalf and pledge it with him."

The occasion behind this hadith was as follows: guests arrived one day at the Messenger of Allah, and he (ع) said to his servant: "Go and borrow flour from such a Jew, to be repaid at a stated term." The Jew said: "I will give nothing until you pledge something with me." The Messenger (ع) then spoke the words of the hadith recorded above.

The master al-Qadi al-Numan, may God sanctify his soul, said in Kitab al-Daaim:

> «إِنَّ اللَّهَ سَمَّى الرِّهَانَ مَقْبُوضَةً، فَإِذَا لَمْ يَكُنِ الرَّهْنُ مَقْبُوضًا فَلَيْسَ هُوَ بِرِهَانٍ»
>
> "God named pledges as taken in hand; so if the pledge is not taken in hand, it is no pledge at all."

The jurists had conflated the rulings of the pledge, failing to distinguish the legal pledge — the particular form — from the customary pledge — the general form. They did not recognize that the legal pledge is a specific category within the broader concept of pledge, nor did they grasp that the pledge questions laid down in the books of jurisprudence concern the legal pledge alone. The result was a failure to tell the two apart, with the mistaken assumption that all pledges are of the legal kind — whereas the legal pledge and the customary pledge are distinct things.

The legal pledge — from which the Sharia forbids deriving benefit — is that in which the pledger offers something of his own as security when taking a loan from the pledge-holder, with no written document at all. The pledged thing must then pass into the holder's physical possession; without that, if the debtor denies the debt, the lender has no evidence of his loan except that thing.

The customary pledge is that a document is drawn up for the loan the pledge-holder extends to the pledger. If, alongside the writing, property or other goods are pledged — whether handed over into the holder's possession or not — and their description is recorded in the document, this constitutes additional security only. When a pledge accompanies a written document, the pledge-holder may lawfully derive benefit from the pledged thing if he wishes, because deriving benefit from a pledge that comes with a written document is not what the Sharia forbids.

If a pledge is made after the writing, and the document is then lost or destroyed, what remains is only the thing held in the pledge-holder's possession — as in the legal pledge, where there is no writing and the pledged thing stands as the lender's proof of his claim against the pledger. Where there is a written document, however, the writing governs the relationship between pledger and pledge-holder. Benefit from the pledged thing after the writing is therefore permitted, whether by way of rent or by dwelling in it. This is the practice current among people in the settled lands: when a man wishes to transact by pledge, the pledger first writes a document for the loan he receives from the pledge-holder, then acknowledges it in clear and unambiguous terms. He may even appoint witnesses and acknowledge before them that he took from such a person such a sum by way of loan, pledging with him such a thing for such a term. The courts record this faithfully in the register of transactions, and if the debtor later denies it, the judge holds him liable and compels repayment. And even if the pledged thing given alongside the document is lost or destroyed, the pledge-holder may still claim his loan from the debtor by that document.

Through the spread of writing in the customary pledge, the legal pledge — from which the Sharia forbade benefit — has largely fallen away. This practice brings ease to both pledger and pledge-holder in payment and in profit, widening the avenues of trade and multiplying the means of gain, since the benefit lies on both sides. The pledge-holder holds, in place of the sum advanced, a property or other pledged thing from which benefit accrues until the sum is repaid, just as the pledger gains from what he has received. Set down below are the rulings of the legal pledge as recorded from the earliest times.

It is narrated from Imam Jafar al-Sadiq (ع) that he said: "Pledging is permitted in everything — dwellings and lands, whether held purely or in partnership, and ornaments, clothing, animals, grain, and the like — by depositing it in the grip of the one from whom the dirhams are taken, so that it may remain with him as proof against the pledger should he deny the debt. The thing belongs to its owner, so it is not permitted for the holder of the pledge to derive benefit from it in any manner, nor to make away with it. If the pledge perishes, the word is the word of the pledger, and the debt remains upon him for its value. If the holder claims that he returned it, his word is not accepted without proof. If they differ over the amount of the dirhams, the word is the word of the pledger, and upon the holder of the pledge is the proof of the excess he claims. When the pledge reaches its term, it may not be sold except in the presence of the pledger or his agent."

Concerning walls held in common. God Almighty said:

> «وَلَا تَعْتَدُوٓا۟ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُعْتَدِينَ»
>
> "And do not transgress; indeed God does not love the transgressors."

And the hadith:

> «لَا ضَرَرَ وَلَا ضِرَارَ»
>
> "There shall be no harm and no reciprocating harm."

If the wall between two houses falls, and it stands within the property of one of the two owners, and that owner refuses to rebuild it, the neighbor has no right to compel him. But if it was that owner who demolished it, and the demolition brings harm upon the neighbor, the judge compels him, and rebuilding becomes obligatory upon him. If the wall is held in common, and one of the two calls upon his partner to build it and the other refuses, the one who refuses is compelled to one of three things:

- That he build it in partnership and pay half its cost.
- That he permit the other to build it in the place of the wall and consent to that building — in which case he may not place in it any timber, beams, or other things except by mutual consent.
- That he pay half its cost.

It is not permitted for anyone to open a window in a wall — shared or unshared — through which the interior of a neighbor's house may be overlooked, except by mutual consent. Nor may a man be prevented from raising his own wall, even if it should come between the sun and his neighbor's house. There is no objection to a man changing the road to his house or the door of his dwelling if the house opens onto a public thoroughfare; but if it lies within the quarter of a group of people, he may not change it without their permission.

It is narrated from Mawlana Jafar al-Sadiq (ع) that he said: "The division of land and dwellings is of three kinds. The first is the division made by the judge: when the partners are content and competent in the matter, and each of them knows what has been apportioned to him and is satisfied with it, the division is binding among them. The second is the division by trial, which is that what is divided is apportioned by planting when its parts are equal. The third is the division by estimation, which is that it be divided by value when the portions differ and are unequal. Those things which they all require — such as the passageway, the door, the public road among the partners, the well, the courtyard, and the like — remain shared among the partners."

Imam al-Sadiq (ع) was asked about the division of the upper story and the lower, and upon whom the repair of the roof falls. He said: "Upon the owner of the lower story — for the roof is as ground for the owner of the upper story, who takes benefit from it. If the owner of the upper story causes damage, the repair falls upon him. This ruling holds where the matter has been left unspecified; but where the matter of the roof has been made plain and set down at the time of sale and purchase, then what is written on the document is what governs."

## 7. Why Marry at All

Marriage itself is among the signs of God. God Almighty said:

> ﴿وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ أَنْ خَلَقَ لَكُمْ مِنْ أَنْفُسِكُمْ أَزْوَاجًا لِتَسْكُنُوا إِلَيْهَا وَجَعَلَ بَيْنَكُمْ مَوَدَّةً وَرَحْمَةً إِنَّ فِي ذَلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ﴾
>
> "And among His signs is that He created for you from yourselves spouses, that you may dwell in tranquility with them, and He placed between you affection and mercy. Indeed in that are signs for a people who give thought."

And the hadith:

> «مَنْ أَحَبَّ أَنْ يَلْقَى اللهَ طَاهِرًا مُطَهَّرًا فَلْيَسْتَعْفِفْ بِزَوْجَتِهِ، وَمَنْ أَحَبَّ أَنْ يَكُونَ عَلَى سُنَّتِي فَإِنَّ مِنْ سُنَّتِي النِّكَاحَ»
>
> "Whoever loves to meet God pure and purified, let him keep himself chaste through his wife; and whoever loves to be upon my Sunnah, then of my Sunnah is marriage."

It is narrated from 'Ali (ع) that he said none of the companions of the Messenger of God (صَلَّع) married without the Messenger of God saying to him that he had completed his religion and his faith. Among the manners of the prophets are cleanliness, the trimming of hair, the use of perfume, and frequent recourse to spouses — meaning women — for the more a servant grows in faith, the more his love for his wife grows.

He (صَلَّع) said that whoever abandons marriage out of fear of the burden of a household has thought ill of his Lord, for God has promised His servants and said:

> ﴿إِنْ يَكُونُوا فُقَرَاءَ يُغْنِهِمُ اللهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ وَاللهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ﴾
>
> "If they are poor, God will enrich them from His bounty, and God is Vast and Knowing — وَٱللَّهُ وَسِعٌ عَلِيمٌۭ."

And he used to say:

> «لَا رَهْبَانِيَّةَ فِي الْإِسْلَامِ»
>
> "There is no monasticism in Islam."

He forbade celibacy for men and for women alike, and he said:

> «تَنَاكَحُوا تَكَاثَرُوا فَإِنِّي أُبَاهِي بِكُمُ الْأُمَمَ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ»
>
> "Marry and increase, for I shall boast of you before the nations on the Day of Resurrection."

God Almighty, having deposited in the human being manifold interests, faculties, and drives — hunger, thirst, sleep, wakefulness, and others — likewise deposited in him the desire for marriage, which appears at the time of puberty. Just as the Messenger of God (صَلَّع) set down a governing law for each of those drives, so too did he set for this drive its regulating limits, so that a person might be preserved from resembling the beasts and might travel upon the road of the prophets. The human being was created social by nature and cannot manage the whole of his affairs without the help of another. The nearest of those who assist him is his wife. When he goes out to earn his sustenance and to store away a portion of it against a time when illness, weakness, or some other cause prevents him from going out, and when he seeks to guard his children, he must have someone who keeps his house and his furnishings. For that purpose no one is suited except the woman. The mother is the helper in the man's living, in the ordering of his household, in the sponsorship of his children, their maintenance and their upbringing. As the soundness of the house is not achieved without the woman, so the soundness of the woman is not achieved without the man.

These two — husband and wife — are the house in truth, whether they dwell beneath a tree in the desert or in some lofty dwelling among the buildings. What is commonly called a house is so named only by figure of speech. The true house is the school of feeling and of upbringing, the place where human character is acquired, where children receive their first nurture, and where the greatest part of their formation comes to them from their mother. In this true house the children obtain the highest foundations of humanity and the lessons of upbringing, and what they attain in the season of childhood accompanies them ever after. It falls upon a man, therefore, to choose from the community the best woman for this school, so that she may be fit for the teaching of children; and none is fit for that except one sound in her body, sound in her soul, and correct in her knowledge. If a woman is without a husband, or a man without a wife, then neither of them has a household in truth, even though they dwell in lofty chambers.

What follows from this is that the house is with the woman and the woman is with the house. The aim of the religious Muslim in marriage is nothing other than the obtaining of children and chastity against what is forbidden, as the Quran indicates in His words:

> ﴿مُحْصِنِينَ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ﴾
>
> "Taking them in chaste wedlock, not in licentiousness."

The aim of marriage, then, is not the mere discharge of appetite as an animal discharges it, but entry into the fortress of devotion and chastity. The word مُحْصِنِين derives from الإحصان, the verbal noun of الحصن, and الحصن in the language means the fortress. One who marries with a good intention has thereby fortified and protected himself from bodily diseases and from deficiency alike. Even a man who is free in purpose from whisperings and the dangers of appetite is not, while unmarried, free from bodily ailments such as ill temper, coarseness of nature, derangement, constriction, and torpor. Through marriage, moreover, the good qualities of character increase:

- gravity
- tranquility
- forbearance
- devotion
- patience
- gentleness
- and others besides

A question arises here. Since the appetite for marriage was placed in human beings by nature — as the other natural drives were placed in them — a person is compelled from within toward its satisfaction. This is evident in the state of the corrupt and dissolute, who find in themselves a compulsion even after committing a vile act, despite its being forbidden. What, then, is the purpose of the encouragement to marry that comes from the Lawgiver, as it is recorded in the Daaim?

That encouragement is built upon two causes: one religious, one worldly.

The religious cause concerns those who travel the road of the Hereafter and practice renunciation in this world. When they are absorbed in longing for the Hereafter, they turn away from worldly attachments, and for this reason they are inclined to forgo marriage entirely, since marriage is the first step upon the stairway of worldly attachments. It is narrated from 'Ali (ع) that he said: Uthman ibn Mazun came to the Messenger of God (صَلَّع) and said, "O Messenger of God, the promptings of my soul have overcome me, and I would do nothing until I consult you." The Messenger of God (صَلَّع) said, "With what has your soul prompted you, O Uthman?" He said, "I wished to wander the earth." He said, "Do not wander in it, for the wandering of my community is to the mosques." He said, "Then I resolved to forbid meat to myself." The Messenger of God (صَلَّع) said to him, "Do not do so, for I desire it, and by God, were I to ask God to feed it to me every day He would do so." He said, "And I resolved to castrate myself." He said, "O Uthman, he is not of us who does that to himself. The castration of my community is fasting." He said, "And I resolved to forbid myself Khawla my wife." He said, "Do not do so, O Uthman," and he mentioned the benefits of living with a wife which are set down in the Daaim. Then the Messenger of God (صَلَّع) placed his hand upon his breast and said, "O Uthman, do not turn away from my Sunnah, for whoever turns away from my Sunnah, the angels shall turn away from him on the Day of Resurrection and shall turn his face from my Pool."

In this same meaning, a narration is recorded from Mawlana Jafar al-Sadiq (ع). He was asked about a man in whom the fear of God had so taken hold that he had abandoned women and wholesome food and would not raise his head to the sky, out of reverence for God Almighty. He said, "As for your words concerning the abandonment of women, you know what the Messenger of God had of them. As for your words concerning the abandonment of wholesome food, the Messenger of God (صَلَّع) loved meat and honey. As for your words that the fear of God so entered him that he cannot raise his head to the sky, humility is in the heart. And who was more fearful of God than the Messenger of God (صَلَّع)? Yet he did not do this." Then he recited the words of God, mighty and majestic:

> ﴿لَقَدْ كَانَ لَكُمْ فِي رَسُولِ اللهِ أُسْوَةٌ حَسَنَةٌ لِمَنْ كَانَ يَرْجُو اللهَ وَالْيَوْمَ الْآخِرَ﴾
>
> "Indeed there is for you in the Messenger of God a fair example, for whoever hopes in God and the Last Day."

The Lawgiver's encouragement toward this worldly cause is directed especially at those who are destitute, indigent, or poor — those who forgo marriage because of the narrowness of their provision and livelihood. For this reason it was said that whoever cannot afford a dower should give at least a small portion of one. On this point, narrations from Mawlana Jafar al-Sadiq (ع) speak to the smallness of the dower: if a believer finds nothing, then let her dower be a dirham, or a garment, or some small thing; and if he cannot find even these, then let him teach her a sura of the Quran. As for the burden of household and the straitness of livelihood, God Almighty said:

> ﴿وَأَنْكِحُوا الْأَيَامَى مِنْكُمْ وَالصَّالِحِينَ مِنْ عِبَادِكُمْ وَإِمَائِكُمْ إِنْ يَكُونُوا فُقَرَاءَ يُغْنِهِمُ اللهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ وَاللهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ﴾
>
> "And marry off the single among you and the righteous among your male and female servants. If they are poor, God will enrich them from His bounty, and God is Vast and Knowing."

Whoever cannot find a wife, and awaits ease from God in His own time, is to live — man and woman alike — in chastity and devotion until each finds what is hoped for; there is no blame in that.

There is no doubt that a destitute person standing alone — whether man or woman — cannot drive poverty away without the help of another in earning a living. This is especially so on the Arabian Peninsula, where the only livelihood is agriculture and where years of drought leave houses bare of food and provision. A man there must labor alongside his dependents from the time of sowing to the season of harvest, and the household does not manage with one woman alone; it may require two, or up to four.

This need becomes greater still when hostilities break out among the many tribes, whose enmities run from generation to generation and whose differing creeds deepen the divisions between them. In such conditions a human life is reckoned worth no more than an animal's, for there is no true government to protect the rights of subjects. When war comes and the men of both sides are killed, widows and orphans are left with no one to guard them from those who overpower and plunder. They are content, then, to enter under a man's protection and to preserve themselves through marriage, even to one who already has a wife.

Another reason is particular to that land. Because the Arabian Peninsula lies close to the equator, its heat is intense, and women there lose their beauty and their strength before men do. Moreover, when a woman is with a man, the days of her menstruation, her pregnancy and delivery, and her nursing require that he keep apart from her; or some other cause of separation arises, of the kind to be discussed in what follows. A village man, therefore — whether he seeks chastity or seeks rest amid the toil of earning a living in a state of destitution — cannot easily manage with one wife alone. From the time of the Prophet (صَلَّع) to this day, the necessity that calls for plurality of wives has remained standing among them.

That necessity, however, is not confined to the Arab world, nor to war alone. Many other causes arise among other peoples as well. From the opening of the recent twentieth century alone, several major conflicts may be counted: the war between the English and the Boer people in South Africa, in which great numbers of men and leaders perished; the war involving Japan, in which five hundred thousand men were lost from both sides combined; and many further wars, between small states and great, fought for differing purposes. Each left behind, in its wake, a scarcity of men with no escape.

The condition of two or three of these states — the English, the French, and the Germans — makes the point plainly, as their own population records show:

- In France, women outnumbered men by 423,409.
- In Germany, after the point at which men and women stood equal in count, women exceeded men by 887,648, with no men remaining to marry them, since each man already had a woman his equal in number.
- In England, women outnumbered men by 182,619.

The total of women in these three states alone, setting aside all others, for whom no marriage partner exists reaches two million four hundred thousand. Those who forbid plurality of wives should be asked what arrangement they have made for these women, and what law they have set down for them — for a law must accord with nature. Either they claim that these women feel none of what other women feel, or they permit them fornication. The children of the bathhouses in those kingdoms run into the thousands every year, and by such vile acts the homes of men afflicted with immorality toward those not their wives have been ruined. Yet for all this want of sight, the opponents censure Islam and say that the Quran gives license to the riding of appetites by permitting plurality of wives.

Had they looked at the noble verse with any fairness — the very verse they suppose grants Muslims such license — they would have found the matter entirely otherwise. The verse stands at the highest degree of eloquence precisely because it forbids plurality with the very words of licensing. Had it granted outright license for plurality, it would have stopped at His words:

> ﴿فَانْكِحُوا مَا طَابَ لَكُمْ مِنَ النِّسَاءِ مَثْنَى وَثُلَاثَ وَرُبَاعَ﴾
>
> "Marry such women as seem good to you, two, three or four."

But it closed the opening after this with His words:

> ﴿فَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ أَلَّا تَعْدِلُوا فَوَاحِدَةً﴾
>
> "And if you fear you will not deal justly, then one."

And after that:

> ﴿وَلَنْ تَسْتَطِيعُوا أَنْ تَعْدِلُوا بَيْنَ النِّسَاءِ وَلَوْ حَرَصْتُمْ فَلَا تَمِيلُوا كُلَّ الْمَيْلِ فَتَذَرُوهَا كَالْمُعَلَّقَةِ وَإِنْ تُصْلِحُوا وَتَتَّقُوا فَإِنَّ اللهَ كَانَ غَفُورًا رَحِيمًا﴾
>
> "And you will never be able to deal justly between women, however much you desire it; so do not incline with total inclination, leaving her as one suspended. And if you make amends and fear God, then God is Ever-Forgiving, Most Merciful."

Surat al-Nisa.

Any fair-minded person who examines the meaning and intent of this verse can see at once that the permission it grants is not an approval or encouragement toward plurality — it is a remedy, a treatment. Medicine is properly used only by those who are ill, so that disease may not worsen but be checked by the cure. The license of treatment does not address the healthy; they are not required to burden themselves with it where there is no necessity. A man who marries beyond one without necessity is therefore like a man who takes medicine in a state of health and only wearies himself by it. This is what our master al-Qadi al-Numan, may God sanctify his soul, explains in the *Daaim*, where he said: the merit of taking many women belongs to one who is able to undertake their maintenance, who has been given such strength and capacity as will suffice them, and who is able to refrain from inclining toward one over another and leaving any of them as one suspended, as God has forbidden. A man who has several wives must observe the right of each one, satisfy them, and act well toward each one of them. If it is possible to house them in one house, there is no harm in that — though it is difficult — otherwise he should follow the example of the Messenger of God (صَلَّع) and house them separately.

The counsel: a man whose wife has died should not remain alone without a wife after her death, but should marry again and not hesitate in it, for in this lies the good of the people and the community and the increase of affection among them. If either of the two had children, that should be no reason for hesitation. It falls to a man's kin and companions to look after the widows among the people and give thought to marrying them off, considering what is suitable for each one; for a woman living without the shelter of a husband is a more shameful and more grievous situation than a man living without a wife.

The Messenger of God (صَلَّع) already had two wives, 'Aisha and Sawda, when he proposed to a Muslim woman whose husband had died, so that she might be under his care. She replied that she was old, had dependents, and was prone to jealousy, and asked how she could marry again. He (صَلَّع) told her that he was older than she; that if she had dependents, they would live alongside the dependents of the Messenger of God (صَلَّع); and that as for her jealousy, he would supplicate God Almighty to lift it from her. The second marriage proceeded on those terms. When she entered the chamber in the house of the Messenger of God and conversation resumed, she excused herself and said, "I said what I said, O Messenger of God, before the marriage, and there was nothing in it from me except that my state might be known beforehand."

What has gone before makes plain that the true house is the man and the woman together, and that a house of stone and timber is a house only in a figurative sense. The management of that figurative house's affairs is in the woman's hands, since it is bound to her, just as the bringing in of provision from outside is bound to the man. As the woman has no entry into affairs that fall to the man, so the man has no entry into affairs that fall to the woman. It is for him to provide for her according to his capacity, and for her to act within her charge according to her own judgment.

## 8. Choosing Well, Looking Rightly

Within that division of charge stands the truth that the real home is not the building. The rented house and its furnishings are neither the purpose nor the aim. The real home is the man and the woman together — they are the two who answer for it. The affairs of the household are many and are not exhausted by walls and provisions. Bringing resources in from outside belongs to the man, and such matters do not enter the woman's domain except where they touch her charge; in the same way, there are affairs belonging to the woman that the man must leave to her, and he provides for her according to his means.

What is needed between them is affection, and where love is strong, the mark of two sensible people is that they consult one another in the matters each holds in charge. The household becomes a garden for them, and they are its dwellers — addressed by the words of God Almighty:

> يا آدم اسكن أنت وزوجك الجنة وكلا منها رغدًا حيث شئتما ولا تقربا هذه الشجرة فتكونا من الظالمين
>
> "O Adam, dwell you and your wife in the Garden, and eat from it freely wherever you wish, and do not approach this tree, lest you become of the wrongdoers."

The tree stands for the affairs belonging to each: that neither draw near to what belongs to the other, lest each become a wrongdoer against the other.

Since the true home is the two spouses, whoever marries off his children must look to both of them. If they prove sensible in managing the household's means, he keeps them with him. If they are sensible in what benefits them and each stands firm in the duty binding upon him, he provides them a dwelling separate from his own, so that they may live by their own soundness. If both are weak and their parents are left to bear the burden, those parents must not withdraw from their children's affairs. But two sensible parents, experienced in the ways of this world, do not thrust themselves into the affairs of their children — for their children now constitute a real home of their own, and the parents are among those attached to it.

Concerning the choice itself, God Almighty said:

> ٱلْخَبِيثَتُ لِلْخَبِيثِينَ وَٱلْخَبِيثُونَ لِلْخَبِيثَتِ وَٱلطَّيِّبَتُ لِلطَّيِّبِينَ وَٱلطَّيِّبُونَ لِلطَّيِّبَتِ
>
> "Corrupt women are for corrupt men, and corrupt men for corrupt women; and good women are for good men, and good men for good women."

And there are the sayings:

- "Marry those who are equal to you, and marry among them, and choose well for your seed."
- "Beware of a marriage of ruin, for it is a marred creation."
- "Marry a woman who is dark and fertile rather than one fair and beautiful and barren, for I shall vie with the other nations through your numbers on the Day of Resurrection."
- "Take hold of the one who has religion."
- "Do not marry a woman for her wealth and her beauty, for her wealth and her beauty may be her ruin."
- "Among the happiness of the Muslim man is a righteous wife, a spacious dwelling, an easy mount, and a righteous child."
- "The best of your women is the chaste one."
- "Beware of marrying the foolish woman, for love of her is an affliction and her children are a loss."
- "Marry the fair-eyed, for in them there is blessing."
- "The virgin woman is sweeter of speech, quicker to learn, and firmer in affection and steadier in hope."

Our master the Judge al-Numan, may God cover him with mercy, said that the saying of the Messenger of God — "choose well for your seed" — is a comprehensive word. Its meaning is that a believing man should marry only into a house of purity, should marry one born of lawful lineage, and should keep away from people of debauchery and doubt. The wife is a necklace upon her husband, and it is fitting that he choose that necklace well. Women, though they resemble one another in body and limb, differ greatly when their inward natures are examined and their characters compared. The distance between them in morals, temperament, good compliance, pride, refractoriness, and other qualities besides is far. As the poet said:

> لَيْسَ النِّسَاءُ سَوَاءً
> إِنَّ الْأَصَابِعَ لَيْسَتْ بِمُسْتَوِيَاتٍ
>
> "Women are not all alike, for even the fingers are not of one measure."

The point singled out and prized in the saying "choose well for your seed" is that the woman be fit to bear children and well mannered, shaped by the finest upbringing. If that is joined to purity and beauty, so much the better. For this reason the Prophet loved for a man the woman who is fertile and affectionate, and said: "Five things are of happiness: a righteous wife, righteous children, choice companions, a man's provision in his own land, and the rope of Muhammad."

Marriage is not merely a legal contract between a man and a woman. Its intent is the continuance of lineage together with affection and good character, each party fulfilling what is owed to the other. With that intent in view, the saying was transmitted: "When one of you wishes to marry a woman, let him ask about her hair as he asks about her face, for the hair is one of the two beauties." Where it is possible to see the woman, a glance should be taken — not with evil intent, but as honest acquaintance. Sight falls first, and hearing follows; and where no such acquaintance exists, no bond and no sincerity arise between the two. Instead, aversion takes the place of sincerity, the house is afflicted with misery in place of happiness, and fire in place of the Garden.

From the same saying — "choose well for your seed" — it follows that a man and a woman should not marry until they have reached the age of discernment, right guidance, and understanding, so that each may choose for himself a partner with whom he can live. That point is reached at roughly eighteen years of age. Marriage at that age brings joy and sincerity between the two, and soundness to their children. Marriage is not good where the one marrying is young. As for the narrations cited among the scholars concerning the marriage of a woman while still young in years, these are particular to the places and times in which such a thing was a necessity — where the order of authority was not firm and people were not secure from one group overpowering another, as happened in the age of ignorance and as is still found in the outlying regions of the Arab lands. There, the wicked, upon hearing of a girl approaching womanhood in a certain household, raid that household and carry her off from her family — as is recorded in the books of those people and in their verses. In such places, marrying off a daughter while she is still young becomes obligatory, and it requires the multiplication of those bound by ties of marriage. Marriage under those conditions is more fitting and more suitable.

In settled countries, where affairs are ordered and the government holds firm over its people, there is no fear of children being plundered. Children remain under the guardianship of their households, and there is no fear for the corruption of their morals. In such lands, parents are not required to marry their daughters before maturity and discernment — not until judgment is settled and the young have striven in earning a livelihood, so that they may maintain their spouses without need of others. Early marriage brings no benefit in a land such as India, where by the praise of God the community lives secure, with security for its children.

With that security, the benefits of the community are beyond counting. Wherever joy or grief falls, and wherever the need for mutual help arises, its people rise to aid one another, standing as the limbs of a single body — in the affairs of religion and the affairs of the world alike. Among those benefits is this: when a man wishes to marry one of the women of the community, whether young or grown, her upbringing, her habits, and her rearing are not hidden from him as he chooses, as they would be hidden in a woman brought in from outside. The selection for marriage thus becomes easy. The righteous among the young do not act presumptuously before their parents and the guardians of their religion; rather, they hold one another in regard. By the praise of God, whoever seeks a spouse finds among the daughters of the community women whose upbringing and companions have been among righteous women, so that they are of purity and soundness.

Necessary teaching should be broadened among them, so that they are versed in the management of the household and fulfill what is owed from them — the rights of husbands and of sisters, and the rights of children in good rearing, in fixing sound beliefs, and in planting correct knowledge in their hearts. This need is greatest for girls before they enter into household charge. Were such teaching common among them all, each one would be fit to order the house, would be the best companion to her husband and a support to him in the affairs of his livelihood, and would be, by her religion, the first school for the children and their first training ground, before the schools outside.

Whoever among the men and women of the community seeks, for himself or for his children, a marriage from outside the community will find — by reason of the great mixing and the keeping of company in the passing affairs of this world — that no long stretch of time passes before the matter turns to discord and mutual hatred. Beyond that, such a person's standing within the community is lowered as well. This holds especially when a woman of the community marries a common man, and more so when a man marries from among common people. When such couples have children, no rest remains for them. The events of these past twenty years, apart from what occurred before them, stand as sufficient example and lesson for believing men and women. Nor is the saying far from the truth: "Evil does not enter your house of itself unless you open the door to it." The doors have been opened to evil in some of the houses of the brethren. It is the meeting of believing women with strangers from among common people — even strangers of good standing — that has opened the way toward marriage with them.

The keeping of women to the house, and the keeping of the veil, have been among the marks of the believers and among the traits of chastity and modesty — especially while women are unmarried — and stand as a stronger safeguard against what is disliked and shameful. Upon this, the word of God Almighty gives evidence:

> يا أيها النبي قل لأزواجك وبناتك ونساء المؤمنين يدنين عليهن من جلابيبهن ذلك أدنى أن يعرفن فلا يؤذين
>
> "O Prophet, say to your wives and your daughters and the women of the believers that they draw their outer garments close about them. That is more fitting, that they be known and not molested."

Before this verse came, the believing women in the time of the Prophet could not be distinguished from others in the eyes of the common people, and some were harassed with mockery. The command from God was therefore that they draw their outer garments close about them. The believing women of that age, and of every age after it, kept to the veil, and the veil became one of the marks of the people of faith. Through the constancy of its practice, it took its place among the rulings of Islam, recognized as a general ruling established in the Quran.

When the Quran was seen to carry a general ruling for all believing and Muslim women, those who cared nothing for the moral character of the people of faith began searching for grounds to permit the free mixing of women with their friends and associates from among the general people, and leveled objections at the Quran over the veil. Yet the veil is a command of the Law, and one of the things that sets apart Islam and faith and their qualities.

It is related of Abu Jafar (ع) that he was asked about a believing woman of sound understanding living in a place where no one shared her religion: may she marry among them? He said: "She may not marry except one whose religion is known." He was also asked about a dissolute woman — whether a man may marry her. He said: "That is not fitting for him. The people of concealment and chastity are his choice."

It is further related from the Messenger of God that a Muslim should not marry a woman who is not Muslim while a Muslim woman is available to him. If a woman comes to know Islam, and it happens that she was married to a Muslim while she had previously been an associator, the choice belongs to her: if she wishes, she remains with him, and if she wishes, she returns to her family. When the days of her waiting period are complete, she is released from the contract. And if the believer divorces her and wishes to take her back before those days are complete, the right to do so is his.

God Almighty said:

> حُرِّمَتْ عَلَيْكُمْ أُمَّهَاتُكُمْ وَبَنَاتُكُمْ وَأَخَوَاتُكُمْ وَعَمَّاتُكُمْ وَخَالَاتُكُمْ وَبَنَاتُ الْأَخِ وَبَنَاتُ الْأُخْتِ وَأُمَّهَاتُكُمُ اللَّاتِي أَرْضَعْنَكُمْ وَأَخَوَاتُكُمْ مِنَ الرَّضَاعَةِ وَأُمَّهَاتُ نِسَائِكُمْ وَرَبَائِبُكُمُ اللَّاتِي فِي حُجُورِكُمْ مِنْ نِسَائِكُمُ اللَّاتِي دَخَلْتُمْ بِهِنَّ فَإِنْ لَمْ تَكُونُوا دَخَلْتُمْ بِهِنَّ فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ وَحَلَائِلُ أَبْنَائِكُمُ الَّذِينَ مِنْ أَصْلَابِكُمْ وَأَنْ تَجْمَعُوا بَيْنَ الْأُخْتَيْنِ إِلَّا مَا قَدْ سَلَفَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ غَفُورًا رَحِيمًا وَالْمُحْصَنَاتُ مِنَ النِّسَاءِ إِلَّا مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ كِتَابَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ وَأُحِلَّ لَكُمْ مَا وَرَاءَ ذَلِكُمْ

A question: if a man marries a woman in ignorance of the prohibition against joining two sisters — not knowing that this second woman he has married is the sister of his existing wife — and he then comes to know, separation between them is obligatory without the form of divorce. If he has consummated the marriage, the dowry is owed by him and the waiting period is owed by her; if he has not consummated it, nothing is owed by him. There is no objection to joining a maternal aunt's daughter with a paternal aunt's daughter in marriage, nor to joining the wife of a paternal uncle with his daughter by another woman, nor to marrying a daughter born of other than one's own parents. A man may not marry the sister of his wife when the wife's divorce is still revocable; but if the divorce was irrevocable, there is no objection to the marriage.

A question: four women are forbidden forever.

1. The woman divorced by a divorce that ends the marriage completely.
2. The woman a man marries after knowing her prohibition.
3. The woman a man marries during her waiting period following the death of her husband.
4. The woman he marries while he is in the state of consecration.

God Almighty said:

> وَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِيمَا عَرَّضْتُم بِهِۦ مِنْ خِطْبَةِ ٱلنِّسَآءِ أَوْ أَكْنَنتُمْ فِىٓ أَنفُسِكُمْ عَلِمَ ٱللَّهُ أَنَّكُمْ سَتَذْكُرُونَهُنَّ
>
> "And there is no blame upon you in what you hint at of a proposal to women, or conceal within yourselves. God knows that you will be mindful of them."

It is related that the Messenger of God forbade a man from proposing over the proposal of his brother — meaning after mutual agreement between the two parties has been reached.

A woman's standing in relation to men is of two kinds: married and unmarried. If she is married, her situation falls under one of three cases: she is under a husband; she is in the waiting period of a divorce, whether irrevocable or revocable; or she is in the waiting period of a woman whose husband has died. If a woman is under a husband, or in the waiting period of a revocable divorce, no one may propose to her openly or hint at it. But if she is in the waiting period of an irrevocable divorce, or in the waiting period of a woman whose husband has died, there is no objection to speech by which she may come to know the man's intent, though a proposal in the manner of open declaration remains impermissible. As for the unmarried woman, if she is not among the forbidden, proposing to her is permissible, and one man's proposal does not bar another's until she gives her answer. That answer must be an express acceptance; weeping or silence does not constitute evidence of consent.

Jafar al-Sadiq (ع) said concerning the meaning of hinting: "It is not fitting for a man to propose to a woman in her waiting period; but the hinting that God has permitted is that he speak words by which the woman may come to know his intent."

Abu Jafar Muhammad ibn Ali (ع) entered upon Sakina bint Hanzala, whose husband — her cousin, who had married her — had died. He greeted her and said: "How are you, daughter of Hanzala?" She said: "Well, may I be made your ransom." He then said: "You have known my kinship to the Messenger of God, and my kinship to Ali (ع), and my standing in Islam, and my house among the Arabs." She said: "May God set you right, Abu Jafar. Do you propose to me in my waiting period?" He said: "I did not do so. I only informed you of my kinship and my station. The Messenger of God entered upon Umm Salama, daughter of Abu Umayya ibn al-Mughira, when she had been widowed of Abu Salama — who was her cousin — and he did not cease to mention to her his station and his place with God until the mark of the mat appeared in his palm from the force of his leaning upon his hand. And that was not a proposal."

It is related from the Messenger of God that he said: "When one of you wishes to marry, there is no objection to his stealing a glance at the woman he intends to marry, if he finds the means." God commanded believing men to lower their gaze from believing women, and believing women likewise to lower their gaze from believing men. It is related from Ali (ع) that he said the Messenger of God was asked about a man past whom a woman passes and who looks at her, and he said: "The first glance is for you, the second is against you, and the third glance is an arrow from the arrows of Iblis. Whoever leaves it for the sake of God, God will requite him with a faith whose sweetness he finds in his heart." Jafar ibn Muhammad (ع) was asked about a man past whom a woman passes and who looks after her, and he said: "Would he love that men should look at his own household? Accept for people what you accept for yourselves."

These narrations, in which the permissibility of a woman's going out to the market is mentioned, belong to places where there is a single people or a single tribe of the people of faith, as is the case now in some of the outlying parts of the Arab lands, where women remain among their own quarters. A woman who has no one to undertake her affairs has no remedy but to go out, and there is no objection to it. But in those places where many kinds of people live together — the noble and the base among them — it is not for the women of noble houses and the people of religion to go out from the house, even veiled, for that is counted a fault among the people of religion.

It is related that a man came to the Messenger of God and said: "O Messenger of God, shall I ask permission of my sister when I wish to enter upon her?" He said: "Yes. Would it please you to see her uncovered?" The man said: "No." He said: "And my sister, O Messenger of God, uncovers her hair at times before me." He said: "No." The man said: "And why?" He said: "I fear for you that if something of her beauty appears to you, Satan may take hold of you." And he said: "When one of you kisses a woman of his close kin who has reached the age of menses, let him kiss between her eyes or upon her head, and let him refrain from her cheek and from her mouth."

It is related that he forbade a virgin being given in marriage until she is consulted, and that there is no marriage except with a guardian and two witnesses. Ali (ع) said: "The giving in marriage by the father and the grandfather is permitted for sons and daughters when they are small, and they have no option after they reach maturity."

Kinship is of two kinds. What comes by way of the father is called kinship of lineage, and the relatives connected through the father are bound to a man by the tie of blood. The one born into this kinship is not there by his own choice; it is necessary and unchosen. What comes by way of marriage is called kinship of affinity, and the relatives connected through this tie are bound to the man and to the woman by the tie of marriage; the two spouses enter this kinship by their own choice, and so they are praised or blamed for it. Kinship by affinity is the kinship that a man and a woman are able to acquire for themselves.

## 9. The Contract: Forbidden Degrees, Guardians, and the Bride-Price

On Lawful and Unlawful Women in Marriage

God Almighty said:

> "يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِىُّ اِنَّا اَحْلَلْنَا لَكَ اَزْوَاجَكَ"
>
> "O Prophet, We have made lawful for you your wives."

The hadith states that a man was swept away by his passions and married his father's wife, with two witnesses present. It is transmitted that Salih said: a father and son may lawfully marry slave girls and their daughters if the girls are young; upon reaching puberty, however, the girls have a choice in the matter.

Kinship falls into two categories:

- Blood relation (نسبية, *nasab* (نَسَبِيَّة)): relatives joined by lineage on the father's side, bound to one another by a shared line of descent. One born into this kinship is bound on both sides by ties of blood.
- Affinity by marriage (صِهَارِيَّة, *musahara* (صِهَارِيَّة)): where there is no blood connection, the relationship is formed through the marriage bond. Those in this category are not strangers to marriage.

The relationship of *musahara*, like blood kinship, carries its own rulings regarding choice and permissibility.

When those under guardianship reach adulthood and come into the fullness of their years, the authority of the guardian may appear coercive. Yet both forms of guardianship remain in force until the ward reaches puberty. The furthest point from puberty is the age of *musahara*, and the age of maturity differs among individuals. Guardianship in marriage belongs to no one other than the father — where one can be found — or his duly appointed agent.

> "فَاِنْ اَنْكَحَهَا غَيْرُ هَؤُلَاءِ الْاَرْبَعَةِ فَالنِّكَاحُ بَاطِلٌ"
>
> "If she is given in marriage by other than these four, the marriage is void."

It is equally impermissible for these guardians to act on verbal consent alone. If an adult woman wishes to arrange her own marriage, she may appoint as her agent one of these four, or a genuine son, or a reliable relative, or her paternal brother, to act in the matter, with two witnesses present. One of the guardians may also delegate authority to another living guardian, allowing the one chosen to be appointed by the judge to act on her behalf. The matter should not be left unresolved, and no one else should be permitted to interfere. If the guardian refuses without valid cause, the judge steps in and the appointed agent conducts the marriage on her behalf. This is the path narrated from the Imams of guidance (عَلَيْهِمُ السَّلَامُ). The judge, moreover, may serve as a witness to the contract of two men, and there is no objection if those who witnessed the agency also serve as witnesses to the marriage itself.

On Conditions Stipulated in the Marriage Contract

God Almighty said:

> "وَلَا تَقُولُوا لِمَا تَصِفُ اَلْسِنَتُكُمُ الْكَذِبَ هَذَا حَلَالٌ وَهَذَا حَرَامٌ لِتَفْتَرُوا عَلَى اللهِ الْكَذِبَ"
>
> "Do not say what your tongues describe, that this is lawful and this is forbidden, inventing lies against God."

Mawlana Jafar al-Sadiq (ع) said:

> "شَرْطُ اللهِ قَبْلَ شُرُوطِهِمْ"
>
> "The condition of God comes before their conditions."

That is, any condition the parties stipulate is neither valid nor enforceable if it violates the Book or contradicts the Sunnah.

God has granted the husband authority in certain matters: intercourse, divorce, maintenance given in kindness, and the contracting of further marriages. Where time is to be divided among several wives, each receives her due share. The husband is obliged to fulfill the rights of each wife, whether she accepts the arrangement or not, and all of this must be conducted with fairness and kindness. If either party is wronged, the wife may bring the matter before the judge — though the judge does not have the power to impose divorce through payment (الْخُلْعِ) or to remove the husband from the home. The husband must not strike his wife over anything that is not legally obligatory. God Almighty said:

> وَاللَّاتِى تَخَافُونَ نُشُوزَهُنَّ فَعِظُوهُنَّ وَاهْجُرُوهُنَّ فِى الْمَضَاجِعِ وَاضْرِبُوهُنَّ فَاِنْ اَطَعْنَكُمْ فَلَا تَبْغُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ سَبِيلًا
>
> "As for those women from whom you fear *nushuz* (نُشُوز), admonish them, then forsake them in bed, and then strike them; but if they obey you, seek no further means against them."

Maintenance, necessary provision, and divorce each have their proper place. A Muslim man holds certain prerogatives, except when he acts in a state of intoxication or moral filth. In the case of الْخُلْعِ divorce, the couple must be given a choice. The matter is brought before the arbitrator, or a dispute arises — as has occurred in various cases over the years. Whoever wished to proceed with the marriage would do so, with proper documentation recorded in a designated register. A man must deal equitably with his wife in matters of good treatment, fulfilling the right of *rajah*, providing maintenance, and observing the waiting period (مُحْسِبُ الزَّمَانِ). If none of these conditions are met and a directive is issued — one that falls on the side of the woman — then the man's right concerning the couple, should he wish to reconcile them when the number exceeds thirteen, is that her divorce rests in the hand of the one who has assumed authority, so as to bring about reconciliation or to finalize the divorce. Both outcomes are subject to moderation.

If the man wishes to divorce his wife but she objects, she may ransom herself by returning her portion of the division to him, or some part of it, and they may agree on a settlement. Reconciliation through mutual agreement is permissible, as God Almighty said:

> اِنِ امْرَاَةٌ خَافَتْ نُشُوزًا اَوْ اِعْرَاضًا فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا اَنْ يُصْلِحَا بَيْنَهُمَا صُلْحًا وَالصُّلْحُ خَيْرٌ
>
> "If a woman fears ill-treatment or desertion from her husband, there is no blame on either of them if they come to a settlement between themselves, and settlement is better."

The essential condition is that the two parties reconcile on good terms. Seeking harm through divorce is blameworthy except where separation is necessary to complete the matter. It is likewise established that a man may divorce his wife in order to marry another. As for defects that neither husband nor wife knew of before the marriage but that came to light afterward, each party has the right either to remain in the marriage or to separate. These defects are nine, and they are enumerated in the *Daaim*.

**On the Bride-Price**

God Almighty said:

> وَآتُوا النِّسَاءَ صَدُقَاتِهِنَّ نِحْلَةً
>
> "And give the women their bride-gifts as a free gift."

And the hadith:

> اِنَّ اللهَ غَافِرٌ كُلَّ ذَنْبٍ اِلَّا رَجُلًا اغْتَصَبَ امْرَاَةً مَهْرَهَا اَوْ اَجِيرًا اَجْرَتَهُ اَوْ رَجُلًا بَاعَ حُرًّا
>
> "God forgives every sin except that of a man who seizes a woman's bride-price, or a hired man's wage, or a man who sells a free person."

A man whose bride-price had been provided by his people came forward. The Messenger of God (صَلَّع) said:

> "لَا تُغَالُوا فِى مُهُورِ النِّسَاءِ فَتَكُونَ عَدَاوَةٌ"
>
> "Do not exaggerate in the matter of the bride-prices of women, lest enmity result."

It is also narrated that a man came to the Messenger of God (صَلَّع) and said: "O Messenger of God, I wish to marry this woman." The Prophet asked what he would give her as her bride-gift. The man said: "I have nothing." The Prophet saw a ring on the man's hand and asked:

> "اَهَذَا الْخَاتَمُ لَكَ"
>
> "Is this ring yours?"

The man replied: "Yes." The Prophet said:

> "فَتَزَوَّجْهَا عَلَيْهِ"
>
> "Then marry her upon it."

A marriage should not be contracted without a اَلْمَهْر. The Prophet (صَلَّع) did not set for any of his wives more than twelve and a half *uqiyyas* — the equivalent of roughly forty rupees — and upon that he married Fatima (رض). The *Daaim* states that there is no fixed limit upon the اَلْمَهْر; this was simply the اَلْمَهْر the Messenger of God (صَلَّع) set for his wives, as though he loved equality among them in the matter. The brotherhood settled upon forty rupees as the اَلْمَهْر, so that equality might obtain between the wealthy among the people and the poor among them. What has come from the Commander of the Faithful is that consummation is not to take place without a اَلْمَهْر — consummation being what is meant by entering upon her — for the اَلْمَهْر is not among the conditions of the contract itself. It is permissible to contract a marriage without naming the اَلْمَهْر and without paying it in advance, though it is fitting that the customary observances of the اَلْمَهْر precede the marriage, named and specified.

> وَمَنْ فَوَّضَ اَمْرَ الْمَهْرِ اِلَى الزَّوْجَةِ قَبْلَ الْعَقْدِ فَكَذَلِكَ بَعْدَ الدُّخُولِ لِاَنَّهُ قَدْ وَجَبَ الْاَدَاءُ عَلَيْهِ بَعْدَ الدُّخُولِ
>
> "Whoever delegates the matter of the bride-price to the wife before the contract, it is likewise after consummation, for payment has become obligatory upon him after consummation."

The husband does not simply determine the amount himself. Rather, the judge considers the woman's standing among her peers — her circumstances and her wealth — and calculates accordingly. If her station is elevated, a comparable اَلْمَهْر is set for her, equal to the اَلْمَهْر of the wives of the Prophet, which is forty rupees. If her station is lower, he gives her less, in proportion to it. If the setting of the اَلْمَهْر has been assigned to the husband, she may accept whatever he gives. As for a husband of limited means, he should give her something before he enters upon her — a *dirham*, or whatever he is able. The اَلْمَهْر is the wife's right alone; neither her parents, nor her guardian, nor her agent, nor anyone else has any share in it, unless she herself chooses to make a gift of it to one of them, which is entirely within her power. Likewise, everything that comes to her as gifts at the time of the marriage — whether from the husband or from the relatives — is hers and belongs to her by right, unless it is made plain that a given item was lent for her use only, in which case she must return that item and nothing more.

**On Mutual Rights in Marriage**

God Almighty said:

> وَعَاشِرُوهُنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ
>
> "And live with them in kindness."

And He said:

> هُنَّ لِبَاسٌ لَكُمْ وَاَنْتُمْ لِبَاسٌ لَهُنَّ
>
> "They are a garment for you and you are a garment for them."

A hadith has also been transmitted from the Messenger of God (صَلَّع) on a matter he continued to press upon the people until his tongue grew weary and his speech faltered. He said:

> الصَّلَاةَ الصَّلَاةَ وَمَا مَلَكَتْ اَيْمَانُكُمْ لَا تُكَلِّفُوهُمْ مَا لَا يُطِيقُونَ، اللهَ اللهَ فِى النِّسَاءِ فَاِنَّهُنَّ عَوَانٍ فِى اَيْدِيكُمْ اَخَذْتُمُوهُنَّ بِاَمَانَةِ اللهِ وَاسْتَحْلَلْتُمْ فُرُوجَهُنَّ بِكَلِمَةِ اللهِ

The original purpose of marriage is the continuation of offspring and mutual assistance in the welfare of this life and the next. God Almighty made this clear in His words:

> "وَخَلَقَ لَكُمْ مِنْ اَنْفُسِكُمْ اَزْوَاجًا لِتَسْكُنُوا اِلَيْهَا وَجَعَلَ بَيْنَكُمْ مَوَدَّةً وَرَحْمَةً"
>
> "And He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them, and He placed between you affection and mercy."

And He said:

> "وَالَّذِينَ يَقُولُونَ رَبَّنَا هَبْ لَنَا مِنْ اَزْوَاجِنَا وَذُرِّيَّاتِنَا قُرَّةَ اَعْيُنٍ وَاجْعَلْنَا لِلْمُتَّقِينَ اِمَامًا"
>
> "And those who say: Our Lord, grant us from among our spouses and offspring comfort to our eyes, and make us leaders of the God-fearing."

It falls to believers — and to the righteous among God's servants — to make their married life like a garden beneath which springs flow, delightful to the eye in its meadows and its greenness, each spouse seeking the good of the other. The Quran makes this plain in God's words:

> "وَلَهُنَّ مِثْلُ الَّذِى عَلَيْهِنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ"
>
> "And they have rights similar to those due from them, in kindness."

And in His words:

> "وَعَاشِرُوهُنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ"
>
> "And live with them in kindness."

And in His words:

> "وَلَا تُضَارُّوهُنَّ لِتُضَيِّقُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ"
>
> "And do not harm them so as to straiten them."

The Prophet (صَلَّع) said:

> "خَيْرُكُمْ خَيْرُكُمْ لِاَهْلِهِ"
>
> "The best of you is the best of you to his family."

It is fitting that the one who marries hold a wedding feast on the day of the marriage or the day after it — once or twice, according to his means — so that the matter be made known. The same applies at the time of the عَقِيقَة, at circumcision, and upon a traveler's return from a journey, so that the benefits of travel become plain to those who have remained at home.

It is not permissible for a woman to wear the dress of a man, nor for a man to wear the dress of a woman, nor may either be compelled to jewelry or garments beyond what their means allow.

A woman asked the Prophet (صَلَّع), saying: "What is the right of the husband over the wife?" He said:

> "لَا تَتَصَدَّقْ مِنْ مَالِهِ اِلَّا بِاِذْنِهِ وَلَا تَمْنَعْهُ نَفْسَهَا وَلَا تَصُومْ تَطَوُّعًا اِلَّا بِاِذْنِهِ وَلَا تَخْرُجْ مِنْ بَيْتِهِ اِلَّا بِاِذْنِهِ"
>
> "She is not to give in charity from his wealth except by his leave, nor to refuse him herself, nor to fast voluntarily except by his leave, nor to leave his house except by his leave."

The woman then said: "Then who among people has the greatest right over a man?" He said: "His mother." She said: "Then who among people has the greatest right over a woman?" He said: "Her husband."

## 10. Milk, Maintenance, and the Untying of a Marriage

God Almighty said:

> وَإِنْ أَرَدْتُمْ أَنْ تَسْتَرْضِعُوا أَوْلَادَكُمْ فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ إِذَا سَلَّمْتُمْ مَا آتَيْتُمْ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ
>
> "If you wish to have your children breastfed, there is no blame on you, provided you pay what you have promised in a fair manner."

Parents may arrange for their children to be nursed by whoever is willing. A mother may say: "I will nurse him, my brother, for I am satisfied."

God Almighty said:

> وَالْوَالِدَاتُ يُرْضِعْنَ أَوْلَادَهُنَّ حَوْلَيْنِ كَامِلَيْنِ لِمَنْ أَرَادَ أَنْ يُتِمَّ الرَّضَاعَةَ
>
> "And mothers shall nurse their children two complete years, for whoever wishes to complete the nursing."

And He said:

> وَأُمَّهَاتُكُمُ اللَّاتِي أَرْضَعْنَكُمْ وَأَخَوَاتُكُمْ مِنَ الرَّضَاعَةِ
>
> "And your mothers who have nursed you, and your sisters through nursing."

The hadith establishes that breastfeeding creates the same prohibitions as birth:

> يَحْرُمُ مِنَ الرَّضَاعَةِ مَا يَحْرُمُ مِنَ النَّسَبِ
>
> "What is forbidden through nursing is what is forbidden through lineage."

The ruling of breastfeeding applies in a specific way. The verse indicates that when breastfeeding occurs with the consent of the person responsible for the newborn — wealthy or not — it carries full legal consequences.

The relevant rulings are as follows:

1. If a woman breastfeeds a child without the permission of the person responsible for him.
2. If that permission is granted, the ruling falls on the one who pays for it, whether the milk reaches the child through his ears or nose.
3. If the woman's milk has increased to the point of causing her discomfort, and a milk vessel is lost — or what was placed in the child's mouth, or given to a cousin, is lost within the two-year period — even a single swallow suffices, since the newborn inevitably swallows the milk.

In the first case, breastfeeding does not require the consent of the responsible party. In the second, the breastfeeding is unintentional and therefore does not establish a prohibition. When breastfeeding is conducted through a proper nursing arrangement, such breastfeeding does not establish prohibited degrees of kinship, even if the milk reaches the child by dripping through the nose or ear, by tube in cases of throat injury, or by injection.

The second principle drawn from the verse is that permissible nursing is limited to two full years from the day of birth, calculated as complete months. All who breastfeed the child within this period fall under the relevant prohibition. If the woman has never given birth or is infertile, it sometimes happens that a small amount of milk appears from her nonetheless, but the condition for the prohibition to apply is that the nursing woman must not be born of unlawful relations. If breastfeeding is desired after the child's bones have developed further — which falls around a specific threshold — such breastfeeding is permitted when undertaken out of necessity. The Imam, after a period of illness, is not considered to be nursing in a lawful manner under these circumstances.

Concerning the milk of the sire, Ali (ع) said: لَبَنُ الْفَحْلِ يُحَرِّمُ, "the milk of the sire creates prohibition." Milk-kinship prohibits marriage: those who share milk from a single woman become forbidden to one another as foster-siblings. If a man has multiple wives and a boy is nursed by one of them, the boy becomes the milk-son of that wife alone, not of the others. Yet all those co-wives are forbidden to one another's milk-children by virtue of the shared household bond. Even if the husband did not intend to establish milk-kinship, the suckling alone is sufficient to create the prohibition.

If a single woman nurses multiple children — whether brought to her from infancy or not — all of those children become milk-siblings to one another and are forbidden from marrying each other.

When a woman nurses a man's child, she becomes forbidden to him, as do her mother, her female ancestors, her daughters, and her granddaughters. His mother may not be given in marriage to her son's milk-brother, and her daughters are likewise forbidden in this way.

If the nursing woman is a slave, the prohibitions still apply in full: her daughters and the daughters of those above her in lineage are all forbidden, whether they came from the same husband or from another. However milk-kinship arises, the woman who has nursed a man is forbidden to him.

A man's foster-daughters and foster-granddaughters are all forbidden to him through nursing. Nursing does not diminish a daughter's natural prohibition; she remains forbidden. So too are his foster-sister, his foster-nieces through brother or sister, and his foster-aunts on both sides.

Nursing also prohibits the combining of two foster-sisters in one marriage, and similarly prohibits combining a woman with her foster-aunt on either side, whether through nursing or through direct marriage.

A man who has a son nursed by a woman may marry a different woman — one who was not nursed by that same woman — without any prohibition applying to her, since she is not a milk-sister and is otherwise unrelated to him. The foster-brother who was nursed bears no restriction regarding a woman outside the prohibited circle, nor is that woman his father's wife in any sense.

They may also marry the woman who nursed their son, under certain conditions, and similarly they may marry the daughter of a nurse or wet-nurse, provided those daughters did not themselves suckle the son or his children and no kinship was thereby established. If no Muslim woman is available, it may be permissible with a lawful non-Muslim woman under conditions, provided no harm results — for nursing does not override natural kinship, and nursing does not create prohibition unless Islam's conditions are met.

On the question of whether milk-kinship is proven when nursing occurs after the marriage contract but before consummation, the judge may confirm the prohibition between them. Complete nursing is defined as three consecutive days or ten separate sucklings. If friendship or intimacy had already been established before this was proven, the dowry remains owed and the retribution stands. After the first and second cases, the husband has no further claim, and they are to be separated. The testimony of a foster-brother is acceptable in this matter, and credible testimony is not to be rejected merely on suspicion.

**On the Missing Person**

Ali (ع) said that if the whereabouts of a missing man are known, he is not treated as missing but as absent. The distinction is this: an absent person is known to be somewhere and has a recognized last place of residence, whereas a missing person's location is entirely unknown.

If a wife complains of lack of maintenance from a missing husband, the judge should first investigate whether the husband left any property. If property is found, it should be used to provide for her. If a guardian has been appointed to administer it, that guardian may spend from it on her behalf. If the property is exhausted and she remains without support, the judge writes to the place where the husband was last known to be, in order to inquire about him.

If the husband returns before the waiting period is complete and wishes to reclaim his wife, he may do so, and the time of his absence counts accordingly. If he returns after the period has elapsed, her right in that matter stands. As for the missing man, his obligations toward his wife's maintenance are reckoned among those of persons present from the day he went missing. The wife of a man merely absent, however, has no automatic entitlement beyond what can be established.

**On Maintenance**

The husband's obligation is to provide for his family and to fulfill his duties toward them. God Almighty said:

> وَارْزُقُوهُمْ فِيهَا وَاكْسُوهُمْ وَقُولُوا لَهُمْ قَوْلًا مَعْرُوفًا
>
> "And provide for them from it, and clothe them, and speak to them a kindly word."

And He said:

> وَعَلَى الْمُوسِعِ قَدَرُهُ وَعَلَى الْمُقْتِرِ قَدَرُهُ
>
> "Upon the man of means according to his means, and upon the straitened according to his."

The hadith makes plain that neglecting those who depend on one is a grave matter:

> كَفَى بِالْمَرْءِ إِثْمًا أَنْ يُضَيِّعَ مَنْ يَعُولُ، وَأَنْ لَا يَشْبَعَ الرَّجُلُ وَيُجِيعَ أَهْلَهُ، الرِّفْقُ نِصْفُ الْعَيْشِ، مَنِ اقْتَصَدَ فِي مَعِيشَتِهِ رَزَقَهُ اللَّهُ وَمَنْ بَذَّرَ حَرَمَهُ اللَّهُ، إِذَا أَرَادَ اللَّهُ بِأَهْلِ بَيْتٍ خَيْرًا فَقَّهَهُمْ فِي الدِّينِ وَرَزَقَهُمُ الرِّفْقَ فِي مَعِيشَتِهِمْ وَالْقَصْدَ فِي شَأْنِهِمْ
>
> "It is enough sin for a man that he neglect those he supports, and that a man eat his fill while his family goes hungry. Gentleness is half of living. Whoever is moderate in his livelihood, God provides for him, and whoever squanders, God withholds from him. When God intends good for a household, He gives them understanding in religion, gentleness in their livelihood, and moderation in their affairs."

A man should be kind to his family, and the appropriate standard of living is one that is neither wasteful nor miserly. Abu Jafar Muhammad ibn Ali (ع) spoke concerning the words of God Almighty — وَلَا تُبَذِّرْ تَبْذِيرًا — "and do not squander wastefully." He said: لَيْسَ فِي طَاعَةِ اللَّهِ تَبْذِيرٌ — "there is no squandering in obedience to God."

From this principle it follows that when a man finds he cannot provide for what has already passed and he bears responsibility for someone — whether his wife in Egypt or elsewhere — and he has nothing, the matter rests with the two parties: if she accepts the situation, she remains with him; if she does not, the absence of maintenance entitles her to seek relief.

It is also held that a woman of any age, whether young or old, or one who does not observe prayer, is entitled to maintenance from her husband commensurate with her proximity to him. If the husband is a man of means who has left without his wife's permission and has been living elsewhere at his own expense, then if there is rebellion — نشوز — on her part, she is not entitled to maintenance until she returns.

As for the divorced woman, maintenance remains her husband's responsibility, except in the case of an irrevocable divorce — unless he fails to provide and she keeps silent about it against him until she gives birth. She is entitled to maintenance if she breastfeeds her child, or if she was divorced, as God Almighty said:

> وَلَا تُضَارَّ وَالِدَةٌ بِوَلَدِهَا وَلَا مَوْلُودٌ لَهُ بِوَلَدِهِ
>
> "Let no mother be harmed on account of her child, nor a father on account of his child."

And God Almighty said:

> يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ إِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَطَلِّقُوهُنَّ لِعِدَّتِهِنَّ وَأَحْصُوا الْعِدَّةَ ...

> فَإِذَا بَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ فَارِقُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ وَأَشْهِدُوا ذَوَيْ عَدْلٍ مِنْكُمْ وَأَقِيمُوا الشَّهَادَةَ لِلَّهِ
>
> "O Prophet, when you divorce women, divorce them at the beginning of their waiting periods, and count the waiting period... And when they reach their term, either retain them honorably or part from them honorably, and call to witness two just men among you, and establish the testimony for God."

A letter was written to Rufaah, judge of Egypt, on the matter of divorce: خُذْ حَذَرَكَ فِيهِ — "take care in it." The admonition applies wherever Muslims have not been taught the proper conduct of marriage, for people are bound to follow the established ways of their community.

On the Dissolution of a Marriage

Marriage is not merely a contract between two spouses. The husband bears obligations — the dowry, maintenance, and honorable companionship — and the wife bears her own: chastity, affection, and obedience, together with the other terms of the covenant. When that covenant is broken and one party inflicts serious harm by neglecting the duties assigned to the husband, the marriage contract may be dissolved.

Just as a woman does not generally enter marriage without a representative or guardian, the dissolution of a marriage is likewise initiated only by the representative of the aggrieved party — the one who complains on her behalf and seeks her protection from harm. The judge must investigate whether the complaint is true. If it is, he may compel the husband to cease the harm or hold his peace.

Should the husband fail to respond to the judge's intervention, the judge may proceed to issue a divorce on his behalf. Yet if news of the husband's willingness to comply reaches the court, and the wife's circumstances allow it, he may be restored to her care — on the condition that he treats her and the members of the household with renewed kindness. After that, the husband faces a choice: he may retain her on the terms he has acknowledged, or he may divorce her, and that divorce is revocable. The sacred law in this matter takes careful account of the rights of both parties and the proper ordering of their lives together.

Divorce was recognized in earlier revealed laws and among the prophets before Islam, and it accords with the law of nature. Marriage governs the living relations of a couple under the purified covenants between them; when one party violates those covenants, the authority of justice may deprive him of that bond. This deprivation, in the language of the Shariah, is called divorce.

Divorce serves both parties by sparing each from further harm. It should not, however, be regarded as a final remedy for every difficulty. Its ruin falls upon the one who wastes it through abuse, or upon the one who pursues remarriage in humiliation. As the wise man said:

> اَتَيْتُ لِلْوَصْلِ لَا لِلْفَصْلِ
>
> "Patience in the face of hardship is better than separation."

The bitterness of parting after intimacy is, in Islam, like the image of nakedness after clothing — something the discerning find deeply grievous.

The jurists identify five forms of marital dissolution:

1. طَلَاقُ الْعِدَّةِ, the divorce of the waiting period
2. الخلع, khula
3. الإيلاء, ila
4. الظهار, zihar
5. اللعان, lian

Each carries a meaning clarified in its proper place. The divorce of the rebellious wife is also recognized.

When a husband wishes to divorce his wife, he must attend to three things:

1. She is in a state of purity from menstruation.
2. He has not had relations with her since that purification.
3. The correct procedure: he records the divorce in writing — to the effect that "my wife, the daughter of so-and-so, is divorced by me today" — calls two men as witnesses, and does all of this in the presence of the proper authority.

If a husband dies during the first or second waiting period while the other party is still alive — even if the husband fell ill during those days and death came before the waiting period was complete — the dissolution is valid and complete.

Where the marriage was of the kind in which the couple lived together, and the man divorces the woman once and then a second time, and she then marries another man, and the waiting period of that second marriage ends, and she is released from it — whether through its natural completion or through separation — she returns to the first husband, who retains one further divorce with her, this being the second of his remaining. The divorce of a wife who has returned to him from a second husband does not grant him three fresh separations from her, except after consummation and the fulfillment of the three conditions already named.

Five categories of wives are exempt from these conditions:

- the pregnant woman who has not yet reached full term
- the one who has not been touched by her husband
- the one who is not an adult
- the one for whom three months have passed since the divorce, completing the count of her waiting period

The written record does not substitute for a spoken pronouncement. A sound and explicit utterance of the word of divorce is required, so that no doubt remains with the woman. Even where there is an intention to cast doubt, a wink, a gesture, or the words of one who is intoxicated do not constitute divorce.

**On Khula**

God Almighty said:

> وَلَا يَحِلُّ لَكُمْ أَنْ تَأْخُذُوا مِمَّا آتَيْتُمُوهُنَّ شَيْئًا إِلَّا أَنْ يَخَافَا أَلَّا يُقِيمَا حُدُودَ اللَّهِ فَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ أَلَّا يُقِيمَا حُدُودَ اللَّهِ فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا فِيمَا افْتَدَتْ بِهِ ...

> God Almighty said: وَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ شِقَاقَ بَيْنِهِمَا فَابْعَثُوا حَكَمًا مِنْ أَهْلِهِ وَحَكَمًا مِنْ أَهْلِهَا إِنْ يُرِيدَا إِصْلَاحًا يُوَفِّقِ اللَّهُ بَيْنَهُمَا
>
> "It is not permissible for you to take back anything of what you have given them, unless both fear that they cannot maintain the limits of God. If you fear that they cannot maintain the limits of God, there is no blame upon either of them for what she gives in ransom… And if you fear discord between them, appoint an arbitrator from his family, أهله, and an arbitrator from her family, أهلها. If they seek reconciliation, إصلاحا, God will bring them together."

Ali (ع) said: اَلْخَلْعُ طَلَاقٌ, "khula is divorce." Where union and agreement between the couple are no longer possible, and the woman says, "I cannot fulfill your rights as a returning wife — take this amount from me in exchange," she is offering something in return for her release, whether an unspecified amount or a known sum. That offering may be her dowry returned to him, or the use of her mount for a period, or her service for a period of time, or something of a similar kind. If the husband agrees and says, "I accept your satisfaction," and the matter is brought before the authority with witnesses and a written document — recording that on such a date the husband is content and the wife has offered a sum acceptable to both — then, if both parties accept, the khula is valid, and neither will be judged unjustly on account of it. It is fitting for the husband, if he is content with a sum at all, to be content with less than the dowry he originally gave her, or with whatever the two have agreed upon.

Each spouse may appoint an arbitrator from his or her own side, or they may agree upon a single person. Whatever the arbitrators decide must rest on the agreement of both spouses, and it is binding upon them. This procedure was recorded by the author in his legal compilation so that neither spouse transgresses the rights of the other. Whoever brings them before a judge to reach agreement, or who declares an explicit divorce, must have the wife observe her waiting period — in her husband's house or elsewhere — since it is not permissible for her to marry another until the waiting period is complete. Should the two wish to marry each other again, whether during the waiting period or after it, that remains open to them.

On the Waiting Period

God Almighty said:

> وَالَّذِينَ يُتَوَفَّوْنَ مِنْكُمْ وَيَذَرُونَ أَزْوَاجًا يَتَرَبَّصْنَ بِأَنْفُسِهِنَّ أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ وَعَشْرًا، وَالْمُطَلَّقَاتُ يَتَرَبَّصْنَ بِأَنْفُسِهِنَّ ثَلَاثَةَ قُرُوءٍ، وَاللَّائِي يَئِسْنَ مِنَ الْمَحِيضِ مِنْ نِسَائِكُمْ إِنِ ارْتَبْتُمْ فَعِدَّتُهُنَّ ثَلَاثَةُ أَشْهُرٍ وَاللَّائِي لَمْ يَحِضْنَ، وَأُولَاتُ الْأَحْمَالِ أَجَلُهُنَّ أَنْ يَضَعْنَ حَمْلَهُنَّ وَلَوْ كَانَ بَعْدَ الطَّلَاقِ بِسَاعَةٍ

God Almighty said: *"Those of you who die and leave behind wives, they shall wait four months and ten days. And divorced women shall wait three menstrual cycles. As for those of your women who have despaired of menstruation, if you are in doubt, their waiting period is three months, and likewise those who have not menstruated. And those who are pregnant, their term is that they deliver, even if it be an hour after the divorce."*

The waiting period for a divorced woman is three menstrual cycles; for a woman past menstruation, three months; and for a woman whose husband has died, four months and ten days — whether she is an adult or a minor, whether the marriage was consummated or not.

The divorced woman's intention, where it is possible, is to observe her waiting period in her husband's house, since he may take her back. In the case of death, maintenance and housing come from the deceased husband's estate.

In a first or second revocable divorce, the husband retains his option: at any point within the waiting period, he may return to her if he wishes and she is content — the return set down in a document before witnesses and in the presence of the proper authority. If he wishes to reunite after the waiting period has expired, that is no longer open to him except by a new marriage contract, after which she remains with him under the count of a first and then a second divorce.

In a third divorce, the wife has no claim on her husband for maintenance or housing. He has no right of return, and no reunion between them is possible unless she marries another man who genuinely consummates the marriage with her and is then released from him — by divorce or by death. A marriage arranged merely to enable her return to the first husband does not serve this purpose; the dissolution must be genuine, without manipulation or prior arrangement.

A woman whose marriage was never consummated has no waiting period. She may marry immediately — even on the day her first husband divorced her — and in her case there is no right of return. During the waiting period, no man may enter upon a woman except those forbidden to her in marriage, or a servant, or her daughter, or those below the age of puberty and their like. She is permitted the pilgrimage, necessary travel, and attendance with other women for prayer on the virtuous nights and at the noble shrine.

## 11. What Outlives You: Charity, Endowment, and Inheritance

Wealth passes out of a person's hands in three ways that reach beyond the span of his life:

- what he gives away while he lives
- what he ties up so that it can never be given away again
- what he leaves behind for others to divide

Each has its own law, and each is treated in turn.

God Almighty said:

> مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ كَمَثَلِ حَبَّةٍ أَنْبَتَتْ سَبْعَ سَنَابِلَ فِي كُلِّ سُنْبُلَةٍ مِائَةُ حَبَّةٍ وَاللَّهُ يُضَاعِفُ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

The verse reads: "The example of those who spend their wealth in the way of God is like a grain that sprouts seven ears, in every ear a hundred grains. And God multiplies for whom He wills, and God is Vast and Knowing." God Almighty also said:

> الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ ثُمَّ لَا يُتْبِعُونَ مَا أَنْفَقُوا مَنًّا وَلَا أَذًى لَهُمْ أَجْرُهُمْ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ وَلَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ

The verse establishes that spending must be purely for God's sake — free from any reminder of the favor given and free from any harm done to the one who received it.

On the same matter it is related:

> الْخَلْقُ عِيَالُ اللَّهِ، أَحَبُّهُمْ إِلَيْهِ أَنْفَعُهُمْ لِعِيَالِهِ وَأَوْسَعُهُمْ خُلُقًا، مَنْ سَعَى فِي مَعَاوَنَةِ أَخِيهِ كَانَ أَحَبَّ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ مِنْ أَنْ يَعْتَكِفَ شَهْرَيْنِ
>
> "Creation is God's household; the most beloved among them to Him is the one most useful to His household and the most generous in character. Whoever exerts himself in aid of his brother is more beloved to God than one who keeps a retreat of two months."

Ali (ع) said that the doer of good acts for another's benefit asks nothing in return, because he is certain that he acts solely for God's sake and therefore seeks acknowledgment from no one. He also said that the doer of good begins with his own household, his near kin, and his brothers, then extends his generosity beyond them. If anyone offers him a gift, he receives it graciously and does not refuse it, even if the thing is of no use to him — he places it where others may benefit from it, and compensates the giver with something in return, as God Almighty said:

> وَإِذَا حُيِّيتُمْ بِتَحِيَّةٍ فَحَيُّوا بِأَحْسَنَ مِنْهَا أَوْ مِثْلَهَا
>
> "And when you are greeted with a greeting, greet with what is better than it, or return it."

Jafar al-Sadiq (ع) said:

> لَيْسَ شَيْءٌ أَفْضَلَ مِنَ الْخَيْرِ إِلَّا ثَوَابَهُ
>
> "There is nothing more excellent than good, except its reward."

Preferring others over oneself is among the qualities of the people of faith. When God intends good for a servant, He joins to the will the power to act. The doer of good among the people of faith is counted among those who do good; the people of good works in this world are the people of good things in the Hereafter, and they are the foremost to reach the Garden. The doer of good is he who deals openly with his brethren in goodness, aids them in their affairs, and benefits them.

Al-Qadi al-Numan said that whatever the narrations report concerning the merits of service and its reward pertains to what was done for God and for the face of God — or for the benefit of religion and its people — with no worldly purpose in it.

Jafar al-Sadiq (ع) is reported to have said that nothing continues for a man after his death by way of reward except three things:

1. A charity he set flowing during his lifetime
2. A righteous child who prays for him
3. A good practice he established — for it works on after his death

God Almighty said:

> يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تُبْطِلُوا صَدَقَاتِكُمْ بِالْمَنِّ وَالْأَذَى كَالَّذِي يُنْفِقُ مَالَهُ رِئَاءَ النَّاسِ وَلَا يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ فَمَثَلُهُ كَمَثَلِ صَفْوَانٍ عَلَيْهِ تُرَابٌ فَأَصَابَهُ وَابِلٌ فَتَرَكَهُ صَلْدًا

The distinction between goodness and charity is this: goodness is general — it may reside in word or deed, in any matter, whether brief or extended. Charity, by contrast, is set for a known term, short or long; it applies to lands, property, livestock, or other things, and all of it is to be written down and made clear.

Charity falls into two kinds: the gift and the endowment.

- The **gift** is the transfer of something from one person's ownership into another's, either for a known term or in perpetuity; the giver has no right of return before the term is complete.
- The **endowment** is the setting aside of an estate — or of something a man customarily gave — so that after his death it is withheld from the heirs.

The founder may draw benefit from what he has endowed for as long as he lives, since he is the one who established it, provided the endowed thing belongs to the category of real property rather than something liable to loss. In a perishable thing there is the risk of diminution for those to whom it is endowed.

Imam Jafar al-Sadiq (ع) said: "Whoever gives a gift in perpetuity, intending by it the face of God and the abode of the Hereafter and the joining of kinship — recorded, severed, and made plain — has no return to it. Whoever gives a gift for a known term, whether short or long, has the right of return after the term is complete if the thing still stands; but if it has perished, he has no such right."

He was asked about the lifelong grant, al-umra, and about the right of dwelling, al-sukna, and he said that people are bound by their own stipulations. The dwelling-right, the lifelong grant, and al-ruqba are of one rank; the condition is what distinguishes them:

- The **dwelling-right** is that a man settles another person — or several, whether women or men — in his house for a known term, granting that use without compensation. A grant of this kind is also called charity, even though it is not drawn from the estate. The reports record that the Messenger of God gave in charity from his own wealth, making it an endowment, and used to spend from it on his guests.
- The **lifelong grant** is that he settles a person there for the whole of that person's life, or for the lives of his descendants as well.
- **Al-ruqba** is that he settles another person there until one of the two dies; when one dies, the ruling of al-ruqba lapses with that death, and the house returns to its rightful people — either to the owner or to whoever inherits from him.

As for what is related from the Messenger of God:

> إِنَّ الَّذِي يَتَصَدَّقُ ثُمَّ يَرْجِعُ كَالَّذِي يَقِيءُ ثُمَّ يَرْجِعُ فِي قَيْئِهِ
>
> "The one who gives charity and then takes it back is like the one who vomits and then returns to his vomit."

That was said at a time when writing was not yet in common use and the giver did not make his intention plain — whether the charity was in perpetuity or for a specified time. Writing is what clarifies the matter, so that nothing is done contrary to his intention.

Al-Sadiq (ع) was asked about a man who gave in charity a share held in common, and he said it is valid. He was asked about charity before possession is taken, and he said: if the one given the charity accepted it — or if it was accepted on his behalf because he was a child — it is valid whether possession was taken or not; but if it was not accepted, it counts for nothing until accepted. He was asked about charity of undivided property, and he said it is valid and is received in the way that such property is customarily received.

An endowment may be made in anything the founder wishes to dedicate — livestock, land, a house, jewelry, clothing, a sum of money, or a usufruct — so that whoever is endowed to may benefit from it, whether one person or many, whether from among the heirs or from others, and whether the benefit is distributed equally or with distinctions, among men or women or both. Writing distinguishes all of this so that nothing falls into doubt. If the founder stipulates that should he have need of something from the endowment he may take what he requires, that right belongs to him; yet such an endowment remains in force only during his lifetime, and after his death it is divided among the heirs.

The founder may administer the endowment himself, or appoint another over it, whether from his relatives or from others. The administrator may take a fee for his service if he has need of it, just as he might pay a fixed allowance to someone else; there is no harm in that. When the administrator sees that an animal has wasted away, a garment worn out, or jewelry or a house fallen into ruin, and there are beneficiaries, he may act with their consent and exchange it for something of easier use, drawing on the value of those things without incurring debt against them. If the beneficiaries themselves wish to contribute to repairs as an act of charity from their own means, the administrator may accept it.

If the founder specified the channels of benefit from the endowed sum — fixing an amount and a time, and stipulating an order — that order is observed. If the benefit is exhausted before all channels are satisfied, there is no harm in it; otherwise the benefit is distributed among those channels equally or with distinctions, out of that benefit, according to the terms of the endowment.

Recorded here, for greater clarity in this matter, is the testament of our master Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع), as it is mentioned in the *Daaim*, in summary. This is what he enjoined and endowed and settled upon his property:

> This is what Ali ibn Abi Talib enjoined, seeking the face of God: what belongs to me at Yanbu of property, and what is known to be mine of it and what lies around it, is charity, except that Riyah and Abu Nayzar and Jubayr are free, no one has any way against them, and they are my freedmen who work in the property for five years, and in it is their maintenance and their sustenance and the sustenance of their families. And with that, what belongs to me in Wadi al-Qura, a third of it, and the property of Fatima and its servants, is charity. And what belongs to me at Daymah and its people is charity, except that Zurayq has for him the like of what I have written for his companions. And what belongs to me at Udhayna and its people is charity. And what I have written of these my properties is a binding, irrevocable charity, so that, whether I live or die, it is spent in every expenditure by which the face of God is sought, in the way of God and for His face, and upon those of kinship from Banu Hashim and Banu Abd al-Muttalib, the near and the far. And in charge of it after that is al-Hasan ibn Ali, eating from it in the proper manner and spending it where God shows him, in what is lawful and made lawful, no blame upon him in it. If he wishes to make property of the charity serve in place of other property, he may do so, no blame upon him in it. If he wishes to sell a portion of the property and discharge a debt with it, he may do so if he wills, no blame upon him in it. And the children of Ali and their property revert to al-Hasan after him. And if al-Hasan ibn Ali has any dwelling other than the house of charity and it seems good to him to sell it, let him sell it if he wills, no blame upon him in it; and if he sells it, then its price is in three parts: a third he places in the way of God, and a third among Banu Hashim, and a third among Banu Abi Talib, placing it among them where God shows him. And if something befalls al-Hasan while al-Husayn is living, then it passes to al-Husayn ibn Ali, and al-Husayn ibn Ali does in it the like of what I commanded al-Hasan, and he has the like of what I have written for al-Hasan and upon him is the like of what is upon al-Hasan. And what belongs to the sons of Fatima of my charity is the like of what belongs to the sons of Ali. And I have appointed what I have appointed to the sons of Fatima seeking the face of God, and honoring the sanctity of Muhammad, and magnifying it and ennobling it and being well pleased with it. And if something befalls al-Hasan and al-Husayn, then the offspring of the survivor of the two shall look into that.

The remainder of the testament is as it is recorded.

God Almighty said:

> كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمْ إِذَا حَضَرَ أَحَدَكُمُ الْمَوْتُ إِنْ تَرَكَ خَيْرًا الْوَصِيَّةُ لِلْوَالِدَيْنِ وَالْأَقْرَبِينَ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ حَقًّا عَلَى الْمُتَّقِينَ

God Almighty said: "It is prescribed for you, when death approaches أَحَدُكُمْ إِذَا and he leaves behind wealth, that he make a bequest for parents and near relatives in the proper manner, a duty upon the God-fearing."

And He said:

> يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا شَهَادَةُ بَيْنِكُمْ إِذَا حَضَرَ أَحَدَكُمُ الْمَوْتُ حِينَ الْوَصِيَّةِ اثْنَانِ ذَوَا عَدْلٍ مِنْكُمْ أَوْ آخَرَانِ مِنْ غَيْرِكُمْ
>
> "O you who believe, testimony among you when death approaches one of you, at the time of bequest, shall be by two just men from among you, or two others from outside."

And it is related:

> لَيْسَ يَنْبَغِي لِلْمُسْلِمِ أَنْ يَبِيتَ لَيْلَةً إِلَّا وَوَصِيَّةٌ مَكْتُوبَةٌ عِنْدَ رَأْسِهِ
>
> "It is not fitting for a Muslim to pass a night except that a written bequest is at his head."

And Abu Jafar (ع) said: الْوَصِيَّةُ حَقٌّ عَلَى كُلِّ مُسْلِمٍ — "The bequest is a duty upon every Muslim."

A bequest records the wishes a person intends to have carried out after his death. During his lifetime, a man or woman of full age owns his or her possessions outright and may give to whomever he wishes, spend freely, or let wealth go. Once death arrives, everything left behind becomes the estate. Of that estate, the deceased retains authority over no more than one third; the remaining two thirds belong to the heirs and lie beyond his power to direct. Should the heirs, being of sound mind, consent to what he gave during his lifetime — to whomever and in whatever manner he wished, or in accordance with the Sharia — that giving stands as permissible. And if the heirs choose to divide those two thirds among themselves by mutual agreement rather than by the division the Sharia prescribes, that choice is theirs to make.

Whatever he bequeathed — whether in health or during the illness that preceded his death, whether his faculties were intact or impaired, or the bequest was conveyed by sign — is to be carried out from the estate to the extent possible, even if he distributes the whole of it among his heirs or among others. If, during the illness of his death, he acknowledges a loan owed to someone, that acknowledgment is not acted upon until it is established; the person named is then assessed, and the heirs may choose to permit it.

A hadith relates from the Prophet: إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَزَّ وَجَلَّ رَضِيَ بِالْخُمُسِ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ — "God, mighty and majestic, was content with the fifth from His servants." He also said: الْخُمُسُ اقْتِصَادٌ وَالثُّلُثُ حَيْفٌ — "The fifth is moderation and the third is excess" — and that a bequest of a fourth was more beloved to him than a bequest of a third. Master al-Qadi al-Numan held that this reflects a matter of preference as reported from the Prophet, and that a bequest of a third is nonetheless permissible.

A bequest is voided only by:

- Loss of the testator's reason
- His apostasy
- Squandering
- Breach of trust

The executor of an executor is himself an executor. If the executor is permitted to trade with the property of a young child, that permission stands and he bears no liability for any loss; and if he stipulated a share of the profit for himself, that stipulation holds. If, however, he traded without having been authorized to do so in the bequest, he is liable for whatever is lost of the property, and the profit belongs to the orphan.

God Almighty said:

> يُوصِيكُمُ اللَّهُ فِي أَوْلَادِكُمْ لِلذَّكَرِ مِثْلُ حَظِّ الْأُنْثَيَيْنِ فَإِنْ كُنَّ نِسَاءً فَوْقَ اثْنَتَيْنِ فَلَهُنَّ ثُلُثَا مَا تَرَكَ وَإِنْ كَانَتْ وَاحِدَةً فَلَهَا النِّصْفُ وَلِأَبَوَيْهِ لِكُلِّ وَاحِدٍ مِنْهُمَا السُّدُسُ مِمَّا تَرَكَ إِنْ كَانَ لَهُ وَلَدٌ فَإِنْ لَمْ يَكُنْ لَهُ وَلَدٌ وَوَرِثَهُ أَبَوَاهُ فَلِأُمِّهِ الثُّلُثُ فَإِنْ كَانَ لَهُ إِخْوَةٌ فَلِأُمِّهِ السُّدُسُ مِنْ بَعْدِ وَصِيَّةٍ يُوصِي بِهَا أَوْ دَيْنٍ آبَاؤُكُمْ وَأَبْنَاؤُكُمْ لَا تَدْرُونَ أَيُّهُمْ أَقْرَبُ لَكُمْ نَفْعًا فَرِيضَةً مِنَ اللَّهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَلِيمًا حَكِيمًا.

> وَلَكُمْ نِصْفُ مَا تَرَكَ أَزْوَاجُكُمْ إِنْ لَمْ يَكُنْ لَهُنَّ وَلَدٌ فَإِنْ كَانَ لَهُنَّ وَلَدٌ فَلَكُمُ الرُّبُعُ مِمَّا تَرَكْنَ مِنْ بَعْدِ وَصِيَّةٍ يُوصِينَ بِهَا أَوْ دَيْنٍ وَلَهُنَّ الرُّبُعُ مِمَّا تَرَكْتُمْ إِنْ لَمْ يَكُنْ لَكُمْ وَلَدٌ فَإِنْ كَانَ لَكُمْ وَلَدٌ فَلَهُنَّ الثُّمُنُ مِمَّا تَرَكْتُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ وَصِيَّةٍ تُوصُونَ بِهَا أَوْ دَيْنٍ وَإِنْ كَانَ رَجُلٌ يُورَثُ كَلَالَةً أَوِ امْرَأَةٌ وَلَهُ أَخٌ أَوْ أُخْتٌ فَلِكُلِّ وَاحِدٍ مِنْهُمَا السُّدُسُ فَإِنْ كَانُوا أَكْثَرَ مِنْ ذَلِكَ فَهُمْ شُرَكَاءُ فِي الثُّلُثِ مِنْ بَعْدِ وَصِيَّةٍ يُوصَى بِهَا أَوْ دَيْنٍ غَيْرَ مُضَارٍّ وَصِيَّةً مِنَ اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَلِيمٌ
>
> "God enjoins you concerning your children: for the male the like of the portion of two females. If they are women, more than two, theirs is two thirds of what he left; and if she is one, hers is the half. And to his parents, to each one of them a sixth of what he left, if he had a child. If he had no child and his parents inherit from him, then to his mother a third; and if he had brothers, then to his mother a sixth, after a bequest he may have made or a debt. Your fathers and your sons, you do not know which of them is nearer to you in benefit. An ordinance from God; indeed God is Knowing, Wise. And yours is half of what your wives leave if they have no child; but if they have a child, then yours is a fourth of what they leave, after a bequest they may have made or a debt. And theirs is a fourth of what you leave if you have no child; but if you have a child, then theirs is an eighth of what you leave, after a bequest you may have made or a debt. And if a man or a woman is inherited from without direct heirs and he has a brother or a sister, then to each one of them a sixth; but if they are more than that, they are partners in the third, after a bequest made or a debt, without injury. A charge from God, and God is Knowing, Forbearing."

The order of settlement from the deceased's estate is therefore as follows:

1. The cost of the shroud and burial is taken out first.
2. Any debt the deceased owed to others is then discharged.
3. From what remains, the third — the deceased's own share — is examined: from it, the ransom of any outstanding fast or pilgrimage is paid.
4. The bequest, if any was made — whether for good works or otherwise — is then carried out from that third, provided the third is sufficient; if it is not, what the third covers is paid, and nothing beyond it is owed.
5. If the ransom and the obligation had already been discharged and no bequest was made, the third is not set aside at all, and the whole estate is divided among the heirs.

Whoever stands nearest to the deceased in the reckoning of the Sharia is his heir; the inheritance passes to that heir outright, with no levy deducted from it. The heirs are arranged in five classes according to their nearness or distance from the deceased. When relatives of the first class are present, no class beyond it need be consulted; when the second class is present, the third is passed over; and so on in order.

The classes are set out in the tables:

1. **The first class** comprises the children and the children of children, the father, the mother, and one of the two spouses. The son's son stands in the place of the son; the son's daughter and the daughter's children stand in the place of the daughter; and where the mother is absent, her mother stands in her place.

2. **The second class** comprises the father, the mother, and the brothers and sisters, with the spouse's share deducted first.

3. **The third class** comprises the brothers and sisters and their children — whether of both parents, of the father alone, or of the mother alone — the male taking the portion of two females, this division being made after the share of the husband or wife is deducted.

4. **The fourth class** comprises the grandfather and grandmother together with the brothers, sisters, and their children; the share of the husband or wife is likewise deducted first.

5. **The fifth class** comprises the paternal uncle, the paternal aunt, the maternal uncle, and the maternal aunt, together with the grandfather and grandmother. Those of the father's side and those of the mother's side each receive their prescribed portions, and here too the share of the husband or wife is taken out first.

In every class, the children of each group of brothers take their father's share, and the children of each group of sisters take their mother's share.

The tables also set out what is forbidden in marriage and in the combining of wives:

- **Forbidden forever:** the mother, the maternal aunt, the paternal aunt, the daughter of the brother, the daughter of the sister, and the sister.
- **Forbidden by suckling:** the milk-mother, the milk-sister, the milk-aunt, the daughter of the milk-brother, and the daughter of the milk-sister, and all of like standing however far the line descends — for what is forbidden by lineage is forbidden by suckling.
- **Also forbidden:** the mother of the wife, the daughters of the wife where the marriage has been consummated, and the wife of the father.

These prohibitions are set out in the tables alongside those that hold only under a condition, so that the lawful may be distinguished from the forbidden and no doubt remain.
