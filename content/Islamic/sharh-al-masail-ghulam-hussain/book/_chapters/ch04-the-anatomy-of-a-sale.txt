# The Anatomy of a Sale

**The Anatomy of a Sale**

God Most High said:

> ⟪ar:وَلَا تَأْكُلُوا أَمْوَالَكُم بَيْنَكُم بِالْبَاطِلِ إِلَّا أَن تَكُونَ تِجَارَةً عَن تَرَاضٍ⟫

"Do not consume one another's wealth wrongfully, unless it be through trade conducted with mutual consent."

There is likewise a hadith in which the Prophet (صلى الله عليه وسلم) forbade the sale of pebbles, and forbade deception (الخديعة), fraud (الغش), and sales involving uncertainty (الغرر).

A valid sale rests upon three pillars: the contracting parties, the object contracted over, and the wording or act by which the contract of sale is established.

The first pillar is the contracting party. The buyer must not be a minor, nor insane, nor blind, since none of these is regarded as a serious transactor.

The second pillar is the object of the contract, that is, the thing sold, which must be capable of being transferred from the possession of the one who releases it to the other. Five conditions govern it.

The first condition is that the thing sold must not be something whose consumption is forbidden. If it can be put to a use other than consumption, buying and selling it is permissible: a dog or a cheetah kept for guarding, hunting, or tracking; the dung of animals whose meat is not eaten, for use as fuel or for the treatment of wounds; elephants for riding and for bearing heavy loads; snakes and scorpions for use in medicine; and livestock and birds whose meat is consumed, whether they have come to their owner or been acquired. Creatures such as parrots, peacocks, and the like may be sold. The exception is a dog kept for no purpose whatsoever, whose sale is not permissible. Idols may be sold if they are reshaped into something of sound use, or kept as relics. Impurities and refuse may be sold for use in agriculture.

The second condition is that both the object sold and the one who disposes of it must belong to their rightful owners, or that the owner has granted permission.

The third condition concerns possession, and possession takes two forms, since property falls into two categories: movable and immovable. Movable property, such as a horse, a book, and similar things, can be transferred from place to place. Immovable property, such as land, cannot be moved from its location. For movable property, possession is transferred when the seller's right and his discretion pass to the buyer through inspection, testing, and receipt. For immovable property, possession is transferred when the buyer vacates it of whatever remained there at the time of sale. Accordingly, anything bought or sold before possession is taken is an invalid sale.

The fourth condition is that the object of sale must be capable of actual delivery, both in law and in fact. What cannot plainly be delivered may not be sold: air, or fish still in the water, or a fetus still in the womb, or the stud of a male animal, or something depicted on an animal's back, or milk still in the udder, and things of that kind. All of these are things that cannot be clearly separated from what is not being sold, and so they remain indeterminate. An exception is made where the impure or excluded portion is set aside and the remainder is sold, as when milk in an udder is sold together with milk already drawn out. Delivery may also be impossible in law, as with mortgaged or suspended property, since the period of the mortgage keeps the property in the creditor's hands, and an arrested asset remains in the hands of its holder, so that the seller is unable to deliver it.

The fifth condition is that the object of sale be specified in its essence, its quantity, and its description. If it is not identified, as when the seller says to the buyer, "I sold you one animal from this herd," or "ten cubits from this land," or "a garment from among these garments, take whichever you like," the transaction is corrupt. The seller must instead mark out and describe the object with sufficient detail. A sale is valid when its object is clearly specified by description, the seller characterizing and informing the buyer about the thing if it is not immediately present, so that the buyer's mind is put at ease regarding the described item. When the object of sale is clearly present at the time of the contract, the sale is valid and permissible.

The third pillar is the wording and the act by which the contract of sale is established and the object transferred from one party to the other. Such transfer may take many forms: mortgage, exchange, loan for use, and others. What must exist between the two persons is offer and acceptance, whether explicit or figurative. The seller says, "I sell you this," and the buyer says, "I have taken it," whether in explicit terms indicating sale, or in accepted figurative expressions and customary usages that convey the meaning of a contract of sale. Where the terms convey nothing intelligible, the sale is not valid. If the seller wishes to exercise the right of option in the sale and to add to the base price, that option is his.

There is no harm in selling dates, raisins, grapes, and other fruits while they are still on the vine or tree until they wither. There is no harm in buying something from a thief, an oppressor, or a betrayer, provided one knows with certainty that the item in question did not come from betrayal, theft, or oppression.

The Messenger of God (صلى الله عليه وسلم) forbade النجش, which is the artificial inflating of the price of a commodity by one who has no genuine intention to buy, but who hears of another's interest and drives him on to purchase. No one should outbid the offer of his brother.

If a man sells his house or other property on the condition that, should the buyer bring the price within a known period, the property be returned to him, then during that period any increase and any decrease belong to the buyer. It is incumbent upon the seller, if there is a defect in the sold item, to disclose it to the buyer and to make it known to him; otherwise the buyer may return the item, if he wishes, once he becomes aware of the defect.

If a dispute arises between seller and buyer, the buyer claiming to have paid the price and the seller denying it, and the dispute concerns property of a kind over which people do not ordinarily draw up documents, then the ruling is this: if the item is in the buyer's possession, the buyer's word is taken; if it has not left the seller's hands, the seller's word is taken, together with an oath that he did not receive the price. Neither party need take an oath if the sale is plain. If the sale concerns property of the kind that people ordinarily document, such as a horse, a house, or land, and the two differ over payment of the price, then the burden of proof falls upon the buyer to show that he paid what was due; otherwise the seller swears that he was not paid, as he denies it, and the price is handed over to him.
