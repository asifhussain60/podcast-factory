# The Anatomy of a Sale

The Anatomy of a Sale

God Most High says:

> وَلَا تَأْكُلُوا أَمْوَالَكُم بَيْنَكُم بِالْبَاطِلِ إِلَّا أَن تَكُونَ تِجَارَةً عَن تَرَاضٍ

"Do not consume one another's wealth wrongfully, unless it be through trade conducted with mutual consent."

There is also a hadith forbidding the sale of pebbles, deceptive transactions, and sales involving uncertainty.

A valid sale has three pillars. The first concerns the contracting parties, and from the contract of sale it is understood that the first condition is that the contracting party, the buyer, must not be a minor, nor insane, nor blind, for none of these is regarded as a serious transactor. The second pillar concerns the object of the contract, that is, the thing being sold, which must be capable of being transferred from the possession of the one who releases it to the other. There are five conditions for this.

The first condition is that the thing sold must not be something whose consumption is forbidden. If it can be put to a use other than consumption, then buying and selling it is permissible: for instance, a dog or a cheetah kept for guarding, hunting, or tracking; the dung of animals whose meat is not eaten, for use as fuel or for treating wounds; elephants for riding; snakes and scorpions for use in medicine; and livestock and birds whose meat is consumed, whether they have come to their owner or been acquired. Items such as parrots, peacocks, and similar creatures may be sold. The exception is a dog kept for no purpose whatsoever, and selling such a dog is not permissible. It is also permissible to sell idols if they are reshaped into something of sound use, or as relics. Impurities may be sold for use in agriculture.

The second condition is that both the object being sold and the one who disposes of it must belong to their rightful owners, or that the owner has granted permission.

The third pillar is that the sale be one of two kinds, since legal liability attaches to one of two categories: movable property and immovable property. Movable property, such as Persian goods, books, and similar items, can be transferred from place to place. Immovable property, such as land, cannot be moved from its location.

As for movable property, possession is transferred when the seller's right passes to the buyer through inspection, testing, and receipt. For immovable property, possession is transferred when the buyer vacates it of whatever remained there at the time of sale. Accordingly, anything bought or sold before possession is taken renders the sale invalid.

The fourth condition is that the object of sale must be capable of actual delivery; if it cannot be delivered, the sale is void. Thus it is not valid to sell air, or fish still in the water, or a fetus still in the womb, or grass still in a field, or something depicted on an animal's back, or a defective limb, and similar things, for all of these are things that cannot be clearly separated from what is not being sold, and so they remain indeterminate. An exception is made for the case where someone mortgages a thing together with something impure: the impure part is set aside and the pure part is sold, as in the case of milk in an udder sold together with milk already drawn out. In such a case, however, complete delivery may not be possible for the mortgagee or for a suspended transaction, since the period of the mortgage keeps the property in the creditor's hands, and an arrested asset remains in the hands of its holder, thus preventing the seller from delivering it.

The fifth condition is that the object of sale must be specifically identified and described. If it is not identified by inspection, then, for example, if the seller says to the buyer, "I sold you one animal from this herd," or "ten cubits from this land," or "a garment from among these garments, take whichever you like," this is a corrupt transaction. The seller must instead describe the object with sufficient detail. A sale is valid when its object is clearly specified by description, characterizing and teaching the buyer about the thing if it is not immediately apparent, so that the buyer's mind is put at ease regarding the described item. When the object of sale is clearly present at the time of the contract, it is valid and permissible to sell it.

The third pillar concerns the wording, the offer and acceptance by which the contract of sale is established and the object is transferred from one party to the other. This may take many forms: mortgage, exchange, outright sale, or other arrangements, all governed by the correspondence between offer and acceptance. For example, the seller says, "I sell you this," and the buyer agrees, whether in explicit terms indicating sale, or in accepted figurative expressions that convey the meaning. If the terms are ambiguous or the meaning obscure, the sale is invalid. If the seller wishes to exercise the right of option in the sale, he may increase the price accordingly; that option is his. It is permissible to sell dates, raisins, and grapes and other fruits while they are still on the vine or tree until they wither. It is also permissible to buy something from a thief, an oppressor, or a betrayer if one knows with certainty that the item in question did not come from theft, oppression, or betrayal.

The Messenger of God, (ع), forbade outbidding: that is, artificially inflating the price of a commodity by feigning interest without any genuine intention to buy, while someone else actually wishes to purchase it. No one should outbid a fellow Muslim's offer. As for an option: if a man's goods or property are involved, and a prospective buyer comes during an agreed period in order to make an offer, then during that period any increase in value belongs to the owner and any decrease belongs to the buyer.

It is incumbent upon the buyer, if there is a defect in the sold item, to accept it with the defect, but he may return it after becoming aware of the defect if he chooses. If a dispute arises between the seller and the buyer, for instance, the buyer claims to have paid the price and the seller denies it, people in such disputes rely upon documentation. In this situation, if the item is in the buyer's possession, the buyer's word is taken; if there is a means by which the seller can be absolved, then the seller's word is taken, and neither party need take an oath provided neither party has been hasty. In transactions of the kind that people ordinarily document, such as a horse, land, and the like, and where they differ over payment of the price, the ruling is well established in Islamic jurisprudence.
