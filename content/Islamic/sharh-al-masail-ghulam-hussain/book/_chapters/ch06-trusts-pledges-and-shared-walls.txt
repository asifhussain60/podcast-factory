# Trusts, Pledges, and Shared Walls

The rental of a house, a ship, or a riding animal is a permitted contract, whether what is hired is a mount ridden upon another conveyance or for some other purpose. In every case the amount and the term must be specified. If a tenant brings into a rented house what is harmful to the house, and does so, the choice belongs to the owner of the house: if he wishes he may let the lease stand, and if he wishes he may put the tenant out. The tenant may do within the house what he must necessarily do, provided it does not harm the building or weaken it. As for any work that harms the house and its structure, that is not his to do except with the consent of the owner of the house. If he carried out work in it without the permission of the owner, and loss followed, he bears the liability. If he claims that what he did was with the permission of the owner, the proof is upon him; if he establishes it, his word stands, and if not, the owner of the house takes the oath and the tenant is held responsible.

One who has rented a house that has fallen into ruin or collapsed has no claim to compel its owner to repair it, but he has the choice: if he wishes he may remain, and if he wishes he may depart. There is no objection to a man renting a dwelling, living in part of it, and letting the rest of it to another for more than he himself pays. Whoever rents a house in which there are trees, and stipulates their fruit, may lawfully eat of it. If the owner of the house stipulates to the one renting it that if he dwells there a day or two the month's rent falls due upon him, the stipulation is valid. What has been agreed must be written down. If it is not written and a dispute arises between the owner and the tenant, and there is no proof, the word is the word of the owner of the house. If a man rents a house in which there are goods belonging to the owner, and the owner promises to remove them and then neglects to do so, no rent is due except for the measure of what the tenant actually occupied of the house.

Whoever hires a particular animal or a particular ship, to travel by that ship or upon that animal to a known place, and the animal dies or the ship is disabled before the journey begins, the hire is void. If this occurs after he has mounted and covered some portion of the road, he owes according to the reckoning of what he has covered of the road. If the hire was contracted upon delivery and conveyance, without a particular animal or a particular ship being designated, the contract holds upon whatever animal or vessel is provided, and the full wage is due. Whoever hires an animal for a fixed period may use it for what he hired it for, to the measure that its like is used; and if he transgresses that measure, he is liable.

A further question. When a man hires an animal or a ship on the condition that it deliver him to such a place and such a station on a certain day or at a certain hour, and if it should reach him later than that hour or that day the hire shall be less than what was contracted, then hire upon this condition is void, and the hirer owes the wage of what he contracted.

A further question. Whoever hires an animal to a known place and then goes beyond it, and the animal perishes, is liable for its value to the one who let it. If the animal does not perish but the hirer has gone beyond the agreed limit, its owner has the choice: if he wishes he may hold him liable for whatever diminution occurred during the period in which he exceeded the limit, and if he wishes he may take from him the like of the hire for that.

As for suretyship, when a man stands guarantor for another, or for a person's safety, undertaking to guard him and his property and to deliver him safely to a known place, or guarantees the soundness of a person, then such guarantee and suretyship are permitted, and he may take a wage for it.

It is narrated from Mawlana Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع), from his father, and from his forefathers, that the Messenger of Allah (ع) permitted partnership in profit and in lands, when two men or three or more wish to enter into partnership in wealth: each of them brings out wealth equal to the wealth of his partners, or more, or less, and then they mix it together so that it becomes one property, no part of it distinguishable from another, and then they sell and buy in whatever kinds of trade they see fit. Whatever profit accrues belongs to them according to the reckoning of their shares, and whatever loss occurs falls upon each one according to the measure of his capital.

The partnership which is called مضاربة is likewise permitted, with the capital from one side and the labor from the other, the profit divided between them according to the reckoning and the appointment agreed upon. Upon the worker, that is to say the مضارب, nothing of the loss falls, because the loss returns to the owner of the capital. But if the worker departs from what he was instructed and transgresses, he is liable for what is lost. There is no valid partnership in which a man gives capital on the condition that he be given a fixed portion. Nor should the believer take as a partner one who is not a believer, for that is the field of the believer.

Concerning pre-emption, الشفعة. A hadith holds that the neighbor is waited for in his right of pre-emption if he is absent, when their road is one. And from Jabir (رض), he said:

«قضى النبي بالشفعة في كل ما لم يقسم، فإذا وقعت الحدود وصرف الطريق فلا شفعة حينئذ»

"The Prophet decreed pre-emption in everything that has not been divided; but when the boundaries have been set and the road turned aside, there is no pre-emption then."

He (ع) said:

«ليس للجار شفعة»

"The neighbor has no right of pre-emption."

And he said also:

«شفعة الشريك واجبة إذا كان بين المسلمين، وليس للذمي والكافر شفعة، لأن الشفعة حق المسلمين»

"The pre-emption of the partner is binding when it is among Muslims; the protected non-Muslim and the unbeliever have no pre-emption, for pre-emption is a right of the Muslims."

Pre-emption is reckoned and apportioned by shares. There is no pre-emption in a ship, in livestock, or in movable goods, except in what is held in common: a shared road, a shared wall, a shared portion of a building, a shared door, a tree, or houses within a single quarter. When division has fallen between these things, no pre-emption remains; and likewise after division there is no pre-emption between the owner of the upper storey and the owner of the lower, unless there remains something shared between them.

The right of pre-emption belongs to the absent and to the minor just as it belongs to the one present and to the adult. It is therefore permitted for a father to exercise pre-emption on behalf of his infant child, for the guardian on behalf of the orphan, and for the judge on behalf of one who has no guardian. The claimant of pre-emption, whether present or absent, whether he knew of the sale or did not know of it, retains his pre-emption so long as its time has not passed. The time of pre-emption in the Sharia is, for cultivators, one year; for others it is according to the custom of the locality in which he is. If its time has passed, there is no pre-emption. There is no claim for the seller if he did not obtain the written testimony and the mark of the one entitled to pre-emption at the time of the contract, so that after the sale the claimant may not rise up seeking the benefit. If the one entitled to pre-emption surrendered his right of pre-emption before the sale and then rose to claim it, he may still rise and claim, because pre-emption is not established except at the time of sale. If the claimant of pre-emption comes to the buyer, and the buyer says, "I bought it for such and such," and the pre-emption is surrendered to him on that basis, and then it becomes known that he bought it for less than that, the claimant may return upon him if he wishes. And if the seller reduced for the buyer, at the making of the contract of sale, the like of what is customarily reduced between contracting parties, that reduction is likewise granted to the one entitled to pre-emption.

Concerning العارية, the loan for use. God Almighty said:

«ولا تنسوا الفضل بينكم»

"And do not forget generosity between yourselves."

When a man wishes to do a kindness and a good deed, let him begin with those nearest to him: first his children, then the nearest of the believers, then those next to them, then the brethren in religion, then the needy, and the most needy of them is the most deserving that alms be given to him and kindness be done to him. Of the Sunnah of the Messenger of Allah (ع) is that he used to offer a loan, to grant the article on loan, and to receive the guest hospitably. The loaned article is the property of the one who lent it, and the borrower may use it only for what the lender permitted him.

A further question. If the borrower stood as guarantor for the loaned article, and then destroyed it, or destroyed some part of it, or damaged it, he is liable for what he damaged and destroyed. If he did not stand as guarantor, nothing falls upon him, unless the lender establishes proof that the loaned article was destroyed or damaged through the borrower's transgression and his poor care of it.

A further question. If the borrower claims the loss of the loaned article, and he has no proof of that, and he is of those who are suspected of dishonesty and treachery, he is not believed, and he is liable. And if the article had been given in pledge, the lender takes it back from the one holding it in pledge.

Concerning trusts. God Almighty said:

«إن الله يأمركم أن تؤدوا الأمانات إلى أهلها»

"Indeed God commands you to render back the trusts to those to whom they are due."

And He said also:

«فإن أمن بعضكم بعضا فليؤد الذي اؤتمن أمانته وليتق الله ربه»

"And if one of you entrusts another, let the one entrusted render back his trust, and let him fear God his Lord."

Mawlana Ja'far al-Sadiq (ع) said in his counsel to a group of his Shi'a:

«الله الله، وأدوا الأمانة إلى الأبيض والأسود، وإن كان حروريا أو شاميا، عدوا كان أو صديقا»

"God, God! Render the trust back to the white and the black, even if he be a Haruri or a Syrian, whether he be an enemy or a friend."

A further question. If a man is given a deposit for safekeeping, and before he has secured it, it is lost or perishes without any wrongdoing on his part against it and without his consuming it, then no liability falls upon him and no guarantee is owed by him, because the one who holds a deposit and the partner in trade are both entrusted persons. So if the deposit is gone, it is not for the one who left the deposit to accuse the one who held it unless he establishes proof.

A further question. If Zayd handed a deposit to 'Amr, and afterward 'Amr said, "You did indeed deposit it with me, but you instructed me to hand it over to Bakr, and I handed it to him," and Zayd denies that he gave such an instruction, then the proof of the instruction falls upon 'Amr, and it must be shown that Zayd commanded it. If there is no proof, then Zayd takes the oath. If 'Amr acknowledged that he handed it to Bakr but Bakr denies having received it from him, then the proof falls upon 'Amr that he handed it to Bakr; and if there is no proof, Bakr takes the oath and the deposit is taken from 'Amr.

It is related that two men came in the days of the caliphate of 'Umar to a wealthy woman among the women of Quraysh and deposited with her a hundred dinars. They said to her: "When we come to you together, hand it back to us." Then they departed. After a short while one of them returned alone and said that his companion had been prevented by some matter from returning with him, and that he had instructed him to come to her so that she would hand over the money, and he gave her a sign agreed between them. The woman, being of an unsuspecting nature, handed over the money to him, and he departed with it. Then the second came and said to her: "Hand over my money to me." She said: "Your companion came with your sign, and I handed the money to him." He said: "I told him no such thing." They brought the matter before 'Umar, who did not know how to judge between them, so he sent them both to 'Ali (ع). He said to the claimant: "If you both instructed her that when you come to her together she should hand it back to you, then go and bring your companion and take your right."

A further question. It is not proper for the one holding a trust to spend anything of the deposit. A child who has not reached the age of discernment does not bear liability for a deposit.

Concerning the pledge, الرهن. God Almighty said:

«يا أيها الذين آمنوا إذا تداينتم بدين إلى أجل مسمى فاكتبوه» ... «وإن كنتم على سفر ولم تجدوا كاتبا فرهان مقبوضة»

"O you who believe, when you contract a debt for a stated term, write it down," to His words, "And if you are upon a journey and find no scribe, then a pledge taken in hand."

A hadith runs:

«لأمين في السماء أمين في الأرض، ولو أسلفني لأديته، واذهب به عني وأرهنه عنده»

"One trusted in heaven is trusted on earth; had he lent to me I would have repaid him. Go on my behalf and pledge it with him."

The account behind it is that guests came one day to the Messenger of Allah, and he (ع) said to his servant: "Go and bring flour from such a Jew as a loan until a stated term." The Jew said: "I will give nothing until you pledge something with me." Then the Messenger (ع) spoke the words of the hadith mentioned.

Our master al-Qadi al-Nu'man, may God sanctify his soul, said in Kitab al-Da'a'im:

«إن الله سمى الرهان مقبوضة، فإذا لم يكن الرهن مقبوضا فليس هو برهان»

"God named pledges as taken in hand; so if the pledge is not taken in hand, it is no pledge at all."

The pledge and its rulings became mixed together in the minds of the jurists, so that they did not distinguish the legal pledge, that is the particular one, from the customary pledge, that is the general one. They did not perceive that the legal pledge is a particular category of the pledge taken absolutely, nor did they understand that the questions of pledge set down in the books of jurisprudence concern only the legal pledge. So they failed to distinguish between the two pledges and supposed that all pledges are only of the legal kind, whereas in truth the legal pledge is distinct from the customary pledge.

The legal pledge, from which the taking of benefit is forbidden in the Sharia, is that in which the pledger pledges something of his own when taking a loan from the one who holds the pledge, there being no writing at all. It must then be that the thing be in the holder's grip, for otherwise, when the debtor denies the debt, the lender has no evidence of his loan except that thing.

The customary pledge is that a document is written for the loan which the holder of the pledge lends to the pledger. If, along with the writing, something of property or other goods is pledged, whether he hands it over into the holder's possession or not, and its description is recorded in the document, this is nothing but an addition to the security. So when the pledge comes after the writing, it is lawful for the holder of the pledge to derive benefit from the pledged thing if he wishes, because the taking of benefit from a pledge accompanied by writing is not what is forbidden in the Sharia.

If someone pledges after the writing, and the document is destroyed, then what remains to him is only the thing placed in the grip of the holder of the pledge, as in the legal pledge, which exists where there is no writing and where the pledged thing is the proof of the lender's recovery of his loan from the pledger. But where there is writing, it is the writing that is relied upon between the pledger and the holder of the pledge. Benefit from the pledged thing after the writing is therefore permitted, whether by rent or by dwelling in it, as is current among people in the civilized lands: when a man wishes to transact by pledge, the pledger first writes a document for the loan he takes from the holder of the pledge, then acknowledges it in sound and unambiguous words, and may even designate witnesses and acknowledge before them that he took from so and so such a sum by way of loan, and pledged with him such a thing for such a term. The courts record it faithfully in the register of transactions, and when the debtor denies it, the judge holds him liable and compels him to repay the loan. And if we suppose that the pledged thing given along with the document is lost or destroyed, the holder of the pledge may still claim his loan from the debtor by that document.

Through the spread of writing in the customary pledge, the legal pledge, from which benefit was forbidden in the Sharia, has largely fallen away. By this practice both the pledger and the holder of the pledge find ease in payment and in profit; the means of trade have been widened and the avenues of gain multiplied, since the benefit lies on both sides. With the holder of the pledge there stands, in place of the sum advanced, a property or some other thing pledged to him, from which benefit accrues until the sum is repaid, just as the pledger gains profit from what he has taken. Below are set down the rulings of the legal pledge which are recorded from the earliest times.

It is narrated from Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq (ع) that he said: pledging is permitted in everything, of dwellings and lands, whether held purely or in partnership, and of ornaments, clothing, animals, grain and the like, by depositing it in the grip of the one from whom the dirhams are taken, so that it may remain with him as proof against the pledger should he deny the debt. The thing belongs to its owner, so it is not permitted for the holder of the pledge to derive benefit from it in any manner, nor to make away with it. If the pledge perishes, the word is the word of the pledger and the debt remains upon him for its value. If the holder claims that he returned it, his word is not accepted without proof. If they differ over the amount of the dirhams, the word is the word of the pledger, and upon the holder of the pledge is the proof of the excess he claims. When the pledge reaches its term, it may not be sold except in the presence of the pledger or his agent.

Concerning walls held in common. God Almighty said:

«ولا تعتدوا إن الله لا يحب المعتدين»

"And do not transgress; indeed God does not love the transgressors."

And the hadith:

«لا ضرر ولا ضرار»

"There shall be no harm and no reciprocating harm."

If the wall between two houses falls, and it stands in the property of one of the two owners, and the owner refuses to raise it, the neighbor has no right to compel him to build it; but if it was he who demolished it, and this brings harm upon the neighbor, the judge compels him and it becomes obligatory upon him to build it. If the wall is held in common, and one of the two calls his partner to build it and the other refuses, he is compelled to one of three things: either that he build it in partnership and pay half its cost; or that he permit the other to build it in the place of the wall and consent to his building it, in which case he may not place in it anything of timber or beams or other things except by mutual consent; or that he pay half its cost.

It is not permitted for anyone to open a window in a wall, whether shared or not shared, through which one may look into the interior of a neighbor's house, except by mutual consent. Nor is one prevented from building his own wall even though it should come between the sun and his neighbor's house. There is no objection to a man changing the road to his house or the door of his dwelling if the house opens upon a public thoroughfare; but if it lies within the quarter of a group of people, he may not change it without their permission.

It is narrated from Mawlana Ja'far al-Sadiq (ع) that he said: the division of land and dwellings is of three kinds. The first is the division made by the judge: when the partners are content, and are competent in the matter, and each of them knows what has been apportioned to him and is satisfied with it, the division is binding among them. The second is the division by trial, which is that what is divided is apportioned by planting when its parts are equal. The third is the division by estimation, which is that it be divided by value when the portions differ and are unequal. Those things which they all require, such as the passageway, the door, the public road among the partners, the well, the courtyard and the like, remain shared among the partners.

Imam al-Sadiq (ع) was asked about the division of the upper storey and the lower, and upon whom the repair of the roof falls. He said: upon the owner of the lower storey, and the roof is as ground for the owner of the upper storey, who takes benefit from it. If the owner of the upper storey causes damage, the repair falls upon him. This ruling holds where the matter has been left unspecified; but where the matter of the roof has been made plain and set down at the time of sale and purchase, then what is written upon the paper is what governs.
