# Trusts, Pledges, and Shared Walls

The rental of a house, a ship, or a riding animal is a recognized contract, whether what is hired is an animal to be ridden, another conveyance, or something else besides. The heading under which these rulings fall is أحكام الإجارة, the rulings of hire.

In every such contract the amount and the duration must be specified, together with the purpose for which the thing is taken. One who rents a house may not bring into it what damages the house or its structure. If he does so, the choice belongs to the owner of the house: he may let the lease stand, or he may put the tenant out. The tenant is entitled to do in the house whatever work he cannot do without, so long as it neither harms the building nor weakens it. Work that harms the house and its structure is not his to do except with the consent of the owner. If he carries out such work without the owner's permission, what is spoiled is charged against him and he is liable for it. Should he claim that he built with the owner's authorization, the proof rests upon him; if he produces it, his word stands, and if he does not, the owner of the house takes the oath and the tenant is held responsible.

One who has rented a house that has been broken open or has collapsed cannot compel its owner to repair it. He has instead the choice: he may remain, or he may depart. There is no objection to a man renting part of a dwelling for himself and letting the remainder to another for more than he took it for. If there are trees in the house whose fruit he has stipulated, it is lawful for him to eat of it. If the owner of the house stipulates against the one who rents it that should he dwell there a day or two the whole month's rent falls due, the stipulation is valid. What has been agreed must be written down. If it is not written, and a dispute arises between the owner and the tenant, and there is no evidence, then the word is the word of the owner of the house. If a man rents a house in which there is furniture belonging to the owner, and the owner promises to remove it and then neglects to do so, no rent is owed except for the portion of the house the tenant actually occupied.

As for one who hires a particular animal or a particular ship, to board that ship or to ride that animal to a known place: if the animal dies or the ship is disabled before the journey and the riding, the hire is void. If this occurs after he has mounted and covered part of the road, he owes according to the portion of the road covered. If the hire was contracted upon carriage and delivery, without a particular animal or a particular ship being named, the contract holds upon what was undertaken, and the full wage is due. One who hires an animal for a fixed term may use it for what he hired it for, to the extent that its like is used; and if he transgresses that, he is liable.

If a man hires an animal or a ship on the condition that he be brought to a certain place at a certain hour or on a certain day, and it is stipulated that if the arrival is delayed to another hour or another day the hire shall be less than what was contracted, then hire upon such a condition is defective, and the hirer owes the wage of what was contracted.

If a man hires an animal to a known place and takes it beyond that place, and the animal perishes, he is liable for it to the one who let it. If it does not perish but the hirer has taken it beyond the agreed limit, the owner has the choice: if he wishes, he may hold him liable for what its value has diminished during the period of the transgression; and if he wishes, he may take from him the like of the hire for that.

When a man stands surety for another, undertaking to guard his person and his property and to deliver him safely to a known place, or guaranteeing a man's soundness, then the guarantee and the surety are valid, and it is permissible for him to take a wage upon it.

It is related from Mawlana Ja'far ibn Muhammad (ع), from his father, from his forefathers, that the Messenger of Allah (صلع) permitted partnership in trade and in lands. Three men or more may agree to be partners in property, each putting forward capital like that of his partners, or more, or less, and then mingling it until it becomes a single fund whose parts are not distinguished one from another. Thereafter they may sell and buy in whatever kinds of trade they see fit. Whatever profit results is theirs according to their shares, and whatever loss occurs falls upon each one according to his capital. The partnership called المضاربة is likewise valid, the capital coming from one side and the labour from the other, the profit divided between them according to what was fixed and appointed. Nothing of the loss falls upon the worker, that is upon the one who trades with the capital, for the loss returns to the owner of the property. But if the worker acts against what he was ordered and transgresses, he is liable for what is diminished. There is no valid partnership in which a man gives another capital on condition of a fixed sum in return. Nor is it fitting for a believer to take as partner one who is not a believer, for that is the domain of the believer.

Concerning الشفعة, pre-emption:

> حديث الجار أحق بشفعته ينتظر به إن كان غائبا إذا كان طريقهما واحدا

"The neighbour has the better right to his pre-emption, and he is waited for if he is absent, when their road is one."

And it is related from Jabir that he said:

> قضى النبي صلع بالشفعة في كل ما لم يقسم فإذا وقعت الحدود وصرف الطريق فلا شفعة حينئذ

"The Prophet (صلع) decreed pre-emption in everything not yet divided; but when the boundaries have been set and the road turned aside, there is no pre-emption then."

He (ع) said:

> ليس للجار شفعة

"The neighbour has no pre-emption."

And he said also:

> شفعة الشريك واجبة إذا كان بين المسلمين وليس للذمي والكافر شفعة لأن الشفعة حق للمسلمين

"The pre-emption of the partner is obligatory when the matter is between Muslims; the protected non-Muslim and the unbeliever have no pre-emption, for pre-emption is a right of the Muslims."

Pre-emption is reckoned by shares. It does not apply in ships, in animals, or in goods, except in what is held in common: a shared road, a shared wall, something held in common of a building, a shared door, a tree, or houses in one quarter. Once division has fallen between these things, no pre-emption remains; and likewise none remains, after division, between the owner of the upper storey and the owner of the lower, unless something between them is still held in common. The right of pre-emption belongs to the absent and to the minor just as it belongs to the present and to the adult. A father may exercise pre-emption on behalf of his infant child, the executor on behalf of the orphan, and the judge on behalf of one who has no executor. Whether the one entitled is present or absent, whether he knew of the sale or did not know, he remains entitled to his pre-emption so long as its time has not passed. The time of pre-emption in the Shari'a is, for cultivators, one year; for others it is according to the custom of the locality in which he finds himself, so that when its time has passed, no pre-emption remains. The seller has no claim if he did not take down the testimony and the mark of the one entitled to pre-emption at the drawing of the deed, so that the benefit of the sale should not be undone. If the one entitled to pre-emption surrenders his right before the sale and then rises to claim it, he may still rise to claim it, for pre-emption is established only at the sale. If he presents himself to the buyer, and the buyer says that he bought for such and such a sum, and the pre-emption is surrendered to him on that footing, and then it is learned that he bought for less than that, the claimant may return upon him if he wishes. If the seller has remitted to the buyer, at the contract of purchase, what is customarily remitted between parties to a sale, that remission is granted to the one exercising pre-emption as well.

Concerning العارية, the loan for use, the verse:

> آية ولا تنسوا الفضل بينكم

"And do not forget generosity between yourselves."

When a man wishes to do a kindness and to give, let him begin with those nearest to him, then with his children, then with the nearest of the believers, then with those nearest to them, then with the brethren in religion, then with the needy, and the most destitute among them is the most deserving that alms be given to him and kindness done to him. It was of the practice of the Messenger of Allah (صلع) that he would offer a loan, grant the loan for use, and entertain the guest. The loan for use belongs to the one who lent it, and the borrower may use it only for what the lender permitted him.

If the borrower has guaranteed the loan for use and then destroys it, or destroys part of it, or spoils it, he is liable for what he spoiled and destroyed. If he did not guarantee it, nothing falls upon him, unless the lender establishes proof that the borrowed thing was destroyed or spoiled through his transgression and his poor care.

If the borrower claims the destruction of the borrowed thing and has no proof of that, and he is one who is suspected of the practice of betrayal, he is not believed and he is held liable. If the thing was pledged, the lender takes it back from the one who holds the pledge.

Concerning trusts, the verse:

> آية إن الله يأمركم أن تؤدوا الأمانات إلى أهلها

"Indeed Allah commands you to render back trusts to their people."

And likewise: if one of you trusts another, then let him who is trusted render his trust, and let him fear Allah his Lord. Mawlana Ja'far al-Sadiq (ع) said in his testament to a body of his followers:

> الله الله وأدوا الأمانة إلى الأبيض والأسود وإن كان حروريا أو شاميا عدوا كان أو صديقا

"Allah, Allah! Render the trust to the white and the black, though he be a Haruri or a Syrian, an enemy or a friend."

If a man is given a deposit, and before he has stored it away it is lost, or goes astray, or perishes without any offence of his against it and without his consuming it, then no liability falls upon him, for the holder of a deposit and the partner in trade are both entrusted persons. When the deposit is gone, it is not for the depositor to cast suspicion upon the one entrusted with it unless he establishes proof.

If Zayd handed a deposit to 'Amr, and 'Amr says that the deposit was indeed left with him but that he was instructed to hand it over to Bakr, and that he handed it over to him accordingly, then if there was proof of the instruction, and it is known that Zayd did give that command, his word stands. If there is no proof, Zayd is put to the oath. And if the acknowledgement is that he handed it to Bakr while Bakr denies receiving it from him, then the proof falls upon 'Amr that he handed it to Bakr; and if there is no proof, Bakr swears, and the deposit is taken from 'Amr.

It is related that two men came in the days of the caliphate of 'Umar to a wealthy woman of the women of Quraysh and deposited with her a hundred dinars. They said to her that when they should come to her together, she was to return the deposit to them. Then they went away. One of them returned after a short while and said that his companion had been overtaken by a matter that made it impossible for him to come back with him, and that he had instructed him to ask her to hand over the property, and had given him a token for that purpose. The woman was guileless, so she handed him the property, and he went off with it. Then the second came and said to her: hand over to me my property. She said that his companion had come with his token, and that she had handed him the property. He said that he had told him no such thing. They took the matter before 'Umar, and he did not know how to judge between them, so he sent them both to 'Ali (ع). He said to the claimant: since you two instructed her that when you should come to her together she was to return it to you, go and bring your companion and take what is your right.

It is not fitting for the one entrusted to spend anything of the deposit. A child who has not reached maturity is not held liable for a deposit.

Concerning ذكر الرهن, the mention of the pledge, the verse:

> آية يا أيها الذين آمنوا إذا تداينتم بدين إلى أجل مسمى فاكتبوه

and, to the end of the passage:

> وإن كنتم على سفر ولم تجدوا كاتبا فرهان مقبوضة

"O you who believe, when you contract a debt for a stated term, write it down," and: "And if you are upon a journey and find no scribe, then pledges taken in hand."

The hadith:

> إني لأمين في السماء أمين في الأرض ولو أسلفني لأديته اذهب به عني وارهنه عنده

"I am trustworthy in heaven, trustworthy on earth; had he lent to me I would have repaid him. Go on my behalf and pledge it with him."

The occasion of it was that guests came one day to the Messenger of Allah, and he (صلع) said to his servant to fetch flour from a certain Jew as a loan until a fixed time. The Jew said that he would give nothing until something was pledged with him, and the Messenger of Allah (صلع) spoke the words of the hadith mentioned.

Our master al-Qadi al-Nu'man, may Allah sanctify his soul, said in Kitab al-Da'a'im:

> إن الله سمى الرهان مقبوضة فإذا لم يكن الرهن مقبوضا فليس هو برهن

"Allah named pledges as taken in hand; so when the pledge is not taken in hand, it is no pledge."

It is not hidden that the pledge and its rulings became mixed together in the minds of the jurists, so that they did not distinguish, and did not separate, الرهن الشرعي, the pledge of the Shari'a, or the particular pledge, from الرهن العرفي, the customary or general pledge. They did not recognize that the pledge of the Shari'a is a particular category of pledge within the absolute pledge, and they did not understand that the questions of pledge set out in the books of jurisprudence concern only the pledge of the Shari'a. They did not distinguish the two pledges, and supposed that all pledges are of the legal kind, whereas the pledge of the Shari'a is distinct from the customary pledge.

The pledge of the Shari'a, from which the taking of benefit is forbidden in the Shari'a, is that in which the pledgor pledges something of his own when taking a loan from the pledgee, with no writing between them, so that the thing which comes into the pledgee's possession is the only evidence he has of his loan should the debtor deny it. The customary pledge is that a document is written for the loan which the pledgee lends to the pledgor; and if, together with the writing, something of property or other goods is pledged, whether he delivers it into his possession or leaves it aside while its description is recorded in the document, this is no more than an added security. When the pledge follows upon writing, it is permissible for the pledgee to take the benefit of the pledged thing if he wishes, for the taking of benefit from a pledge that is accompanied by writing is not what is forbidden in the Shari'a.

Should someone pledge after writing and then the document be destroyed, his position reverts to that of one who has handed over a thing into the possession of the pledgee, and the case becomes like that of the thing given under the pledge of the Shari'a, which is a pledge without writing, where the pledged thing is the proof that the pledgee's loan reached the pledgor. But where there is writing, it is the writing that is relied upon between pledgor and pledgee. Taking benefit from the pledged thing after writing is therefore permissible, whether by rent or by dwelling in it, as is the practice among people in the civilized lands: when a man wishes to enter into a transaction of pledge, the pledgor first writes the document for the loan he takes from the pledgee, and then acknowledges in sound and unambiguous words, and may even appoint witnesses and acknowledge before them, that he has taken from so and so such a sum by way of loan, and has pledged with him such a thing for such a term. The courts hold this in trust, the record reaches the register of transactions, and when the debtor denies it the judge holds him bound by that document and compels him to discharge the loan. And if we suppose that the pledged thing which was pledged along with the document is lost or destroyed, the pledgee may still, with that document, demand his loan from the debtor.

The spread of writing in the customary pledge has raised up that pledge whose benefit was forbidden in the Shari'a, so that under a pledge of this kind both the pledgor and the pledgee find ease in payment and in profit; the causes of trade have widened by this practice, and the means of earning have multiplied. The benefit lies on both sides: the pledgee, in place of the sum advanced, receives a dwelling or some other thing pledged to him, and takes benefit from it until the sum is discharged, just as the pledgor obtains profit from what he has borrowed. Set down below are the rulings of the pledge of the Shari'a as they are recorded.

It is related from the Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq (ع) that he said that a pledge is permissible in everything: in places and lands, whether held outright or in partnership, in ornaments, clothing, animals, grain and other goods, by depositing it in the possession of the one from whom the dirhams are taken, so that it may stand as proof with the pledgee should the pledgor deny the matter. The thing belongs to its owner, so it is not permissible for the pledgee to take benefit from it in any manner whatever, nor to make it his own. If the pledge perishes, the word is the word of the pledgor, and the debt remains upon him for its value. If the pledgee claims that he returned the pledge, his word is not accepted without proof. If they differ as to the amount of the dirhams, the word is the word of the pledgor, and upon the pledgee lies the proof of the excess he claims. When the pledge reaches its term, it may not be sold except in the presence of the pledgor or his agent.

Concerning harm, the verse:

> آية ولا تعتدوا إن الله لا يحب المعتدين

"And do not transgress; indeed Allah does not love the transgressors."

And the hadith:

> لا ضرر ولا ضرار

"No harm and no reciprocating harm."

If a wall between two houses falls, and it stands in the property of one of the two neighbours, and the owner refuses to raise it up again, the neighbour cannot compel him to build it. But if it was the owner himself who demolished it, and its demolition harms the neighbour, the judge obliges him and it becomes binding upon him to rebuild it. If the wall is shared, and one of the two partners calls his partner to rebuild it and the other refuses, he is compelled to one of three things:

First, that he build it in partnership and pay half its cost.

Second, that the other build it in the place of the wall, and that he permit his partner to build it, in which case he has no right to place in it any timber or beam or anything else except by mutual consent.

Third, that he pay half its cost.

No one may open a window in a wall, whether shared or not shared, from which he may look into the interior of his neighbour's house, except by mutual consent. One who builds his own wall is not prevented from doing so even if it comes between the sun and his neighbour's house. There is no objection to a man changing the doorway of his house or the door of his dwelling if the house stands upon a public thoroughfare; but if it stands in the quarter of a people, he may not change it without their permission.

It is related from Mawlana Ja'far al-Sadiq (ع) that he said that the division of land and of a house is of three kinds. The first is the division of the judge: when the partners are content, and are competent in the matter, and each of them takes what has been allotted to him and is satisfied with it, the division passes between them. The second is the division by trial, which is to divide what is to be divided by cultivation when its parts are equal. The third is the division by valuation, which is to divide according to value when the portions differ and one exceeds another. Those things which they all have need of, such as the passage or the door, the public road between the partners, the well, the courtyard and the like, remain shared between the partners.

The Imam al-Sadiq was asked about the division of the upper and the lower storey, and upon whom falls the repair of the ceiling below. He said that it falls upon the owner of the lower storey, and that the roof is as ground to the owner of the upper storey, who takes benefit from it; and if the owner of the upper storey causes damage, its repair is upon him. This ruling holds where the matter has been left unstated. But where the matter of the ceiling has been set down in writing at the time of sale and purchase, then what is written on the sheet is what governs.
