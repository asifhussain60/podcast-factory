# Lending Without Riba

God Most High, the Lord of charity, has granted that a loan made with the sincere intention of earning reward in the Hereafter is itself charity. He said:

⟪وَمَا آتَيْتُم مِّن رِّبًا لِّيَرْبُوَ فِي أَمْوَالِ النَّاسِ فَلَا يَرْبُو عِندَ اللَّهِ وَمَا آتَيْتُم مِّن زَكَاةٍ تُرِيدُونَ وَجْهَ اللَّهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُضْعِفُونَ⟫

"Whatever you give as riba so that it may increase through other people's wealth does not increase with God; but whatever you give in zakah, seeking God's face, it is they who will receive manifold reward."

The one who gives a loan seeking the benefit of the Hereafter will have it multiplied for him in the next life. It is also said: whoever wishes to cultivate the harvest of the Hereafter, We shall increase it for him; and whoever wishes to cultivate the harvest of this world, We give him of it, though he will have no share in the reward of the Hereafter. It is said as well that whatever God accepts with goodwill is doubled for the giver, meaning it is multiplied in the recompense of the next life.

Riba means increase. Not every increase, however, is forbidden, as with increases in sales transactions and similar dealings. Riba in the technical sense of Islamic law refers specifically to the increase that accrues on a loan when the lender's intention is to gain a worldly benefit, and when the principal is returned to him along with that increase. The blessings of God descend only upon the one whose intention in lending is sound.

A loan therefore falls into two categories: one in which the lender's purpose is worldly benefit, and one in which the lender's purpose is the reward of the Hereafter. Each category depends entirely on the intention of the one who lends, for actions are judged by intentions, and both categories fall within the domain of legally regulated transactions. A loan intended for worldly benefit, and extended on that basis, means that if the borrower is in a position to repay, the lender has the right to reduce or excuse the repayment, or to increase the time allowed and reduce the principal slightly or substantially, or to forgive the debt entirely, and there is reward in that.

As for a loan intended for the reward of the Hereafter, that reward is eighteen-fold, twenty-fold for maintaining the bond of brotherhood with a fellow believer, and twenty-four-fold for maintaining the bond of kinship, the latter extending one's lifespan, as is related in the chapter on the virtue of charity from the Messenger of God (ص). The owner of such a loan does not have the right to benefit from the principal in any way, whether small or great, or to impose any condition that burdens the principal. God Most High has designated every loan made purely for His sake as an act of devotion, and every worldly benefit derived from it is therefore forbidden. Transactions were conducted among the Muslims for God's sake, that is, as a legitimate loan. Sometimes, even when the time for repayment arrives and the borrower is in hardship, the lender should cancel the arrangement for that period and accept the principal as agreed; and if the borrower is genuinely and visibly in hardship, the lender should grant him additional time and even remit his principal.

Such was the case with 'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib and 'Uthman ibn 'Affan, who lent a Muslim man dirhams on the condition of repayment at the coming season. When the agreed time came, the Muslim man said: if you take your right in full, you bring ruin upon these wretched children; if you take half and leave the other half for my children, I shall give you at the following season twice the remaining half, which would be satisfactory. When the appointed time came and they demanded their right in full, the man had nothing to give them, and the matter reached the Messenger of God (ص). Then this was revealed:

⟪يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَذَرُوا مَا بَقِيَ مِنَ الرِّبَا إِن كُنتُم مُّؤْمِنِينَ فَإِن لَّمْ تَفْعَلُوا فَأْذَنُوا بِحَرْبٍ مِّنَ اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ وَإِن تُبْتُمْ فَلَكُمْ رُءُوسُ أَمْوَالِكُمْ لَا تَظْلِمُونَ وَلَا تُظْلَمُونَ وَإِن كَانَ ذُو عُسْرَةٍ فَنَظِرَةٌ إِلَىٰ مَيْسَرَةٍ وَأَن تَصَدَّقُوا خَيْرٌ لَّكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ⟫

"O you who believe, fear God and give up whatever riba remains outstanding, if you are truly believers. If you do not, then be warned of war from God and His Messenger. But if you repent, you shall have your principal: do no wrong and you will not be wronged. If the debtor is in hardship, grant him time until he is at ease; and if you remit it as charity, that is better for you, if only you knew."

The meaning is this: O people of faith, fear God and abandon the immediate benefit you have been taking until now from the borrower who is unable to render it, if you are people of faith. What is intended is the lender's becoming beloved to God and His Messenger, since the contrary is opposition to God and His Messenger. If the dispute is abandoned and only the principal of the loan is taken, the lender neither wrongs the borrower nor is wronged himself by forgoing the increase. The one pressed for repayment who cannot render the principal is to be granted respite until ease. And if the whole loan is remitted as charity, that is better, for those who know the secret of brotherhood. Most of the jurists have confused this question. May God grant us and them the strength to grasp the intended meaning.

Both categories of loan were in use among the Arabs, and among the Christians, the Jews and their associates, before the appearance of Islam and after it. Trade before the conquest of Mecca, along with its prices, lay in the grip of the People of the Book, and they wronged the Muslims, particularly in the matter of goods sold on credit or for cash, giving them little money and taking much in return, in buying and in selling, until the situation became straitened for the Muslims and they had no stratagem by which to escape it. The Messenger (ص) was waiting for the appointed time and for the ruling from God, and God gave him the conquest of Mecca, which he opened by God's leave in the eighth year of the Hijra. The strength of the Muslims and their standing increased by it.

Then in the ninth year this was revealed:

⟪إِنَّمَا الْمُشْرِكُونَ نَجَسٌ فَلَا يَقْرَبُوا الْمَسْجِدَ الْحَرَامَ بَعْدَ عَامِهِمْ هَٰذَا وَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ عَيْلَةً فَسَوْفَ يُغْنِيكُمُ اللَّهُ مِن فَضْلِهِ إِن شَاءَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ⟫

"The polytheists are indeed impure, so let them not approach the Sacred Mosque after this year of theirs. If you fear poverty, God will enrich you from His bounty if He wills. God is All-Knowing, All-Wise."

'Ali ibn Abi Talib conveyed it to them, as the Messenger (ص) had commanded him regarding the chapter of Bara'a, and he recited it to the polytheists. The Messenger (ص) then commanded the Muslims to take up the trade in grain and other kinds of goods themselves, and to bring in wealth from outside the community. The Muslims continued to bring it in through the port of Jidda, the nearest of the ports to Mecca, until the trade came into their hands and their affair grew strong by it. From that year they were able to pay the zakah on their wealth, and they dealt in both categories, the worldly and the other-worldly. With Muslims in straitened circumstances they dealt according to the benefit of the Hereafter, and in buying and selling they dealt according to worldly benefit. When they saw that one with whom they dealt for worldly benefit was in hardship, they were lenient with the increase or gave the principal of the loan as charity, as God Most High had commanded them. From such conduct, brotherhood and mutual generosity were established firmly among them, along with preferring others to themselves, and dealings and bonds of brotherhood increased in their hands.

They abandoned dealings with non-Muslims out of indignation, remembering how the polytheists had formerly dealt with them, and their aversion to the polytheists and others grew to the point that some would not permit dealing with them at all, nor drink water that a polytheist had touched, nor eat of their food. Yet in the Sharia there is nothing forbidden in that regard. Even gold received from them is not forbidden in general, except what they slaughter for idols or according to their own creed, for that is what the Sharia forbids, not what the polytheists merely handle. Where they are employed outside the government of Islam, taking a wage from that is permitted, for their creed has no entry into it, as the Shaykh said, and as with what is offered to other than God.

The narration transmitted with a general application from Mawlana Ja'far al-Sadiq (ع) is that he said: it is not fitting for a man among you to enter partnership with a dhimmi, nor to entrust him with goods, nor to leave a deposit with him, nor to give him pure affection. This rests upon the custom that ran among the Muslims in earlier times, and is not a declaration that such things are forbidden in the Sharia.

There is a further category of riba, as has come in the hadith: "الذهب بالذهب والفضة بالفضة والبر بالبر والشعير بالشعير والتمر بالتمر والملح بالملح مثلًا بمثل ويدًا بيد فمن زاد أو استزاد فقد أربى الآخذ والمعطي فيه سواء" — "Gold for gold, silver for silver, wheat for wheat, barley for barley, dates for dates, salt for salt, like for like, hand to hand; whoever gives more or asks for more has dealt in riba, the taker and the giver in it being alike." The reason for this form of barter is that at that time there was no coinage in circulation to serve people's needs, as is still the case in parts of Yemen, so people exchanged one commodity for another. Where coinage is current and people's needs are met by it, as in India and other lands, the practice of barter falls away, and with it this form of riba ceases.

As for the property of orphans, God said: test the orphans until they reach the age of marriage, and if you find in them sound judgment, hand over their property to them. It is related from Mawlana Ja'far (ع) that he said: the guardian of the orphan, when the orphan reads the Quran, has reached maturity, and sound judgment is discerned in him, hands over his property to him; but if he has reached maturity and has neither sound judgment nor a mind that can be trusted, his property is not handed over to him, and the guardian spends upon him from it with kindness.

The ruler withholds the power of disposal from one who squanders his wealth in the wrong place, and hands his property to the one who will administer it and meet his necessities. This withholding, whether from an orphan not yet of age or from an adult man who squanders, is called al-hajr.

As for a merchant whose debts consume his wealth or exceed it, when his creditors press their claims against him, the ruler must place him under restraint and take charge of his property. He is given from his property the rent of the house in which he lives, or the hire of a mount, or the wage of a hired man, if any of that remains due from him. What remains is then divided among the creditors in proportion to their loans. The wife's dowry is reckoned along with the creditors if it is still outstanding. If among the property something specific belonging to a particular claimant is found, and he seeks it, the matter rests with the ruler: if it is of small value it is given to him, and if not it is kept with the property. Thereafter the ruler and the lender make his insolvency publicly known, so that no one may lose his money by lending to him. This announcement is called taflis, the one placed under restraint and so announced being called muflis.

If the borrower continues to deal with people while his debts exceed his wealth, and no one has yet raised a complaint against him, so that he sells his estate or gives it to his children or to others, that is valid. But if it becomes clear to the judge that he acted so with corrupt intent, the judge may annul those dispositions.

Neither the judge nor the ruler may compel the insolvent debtor at the demand of the creditors; rather he is granted respite until ease.

A further question: a man incurred debt and was declared insolvent to his creditors, and afterward men lent to him and he did not prosper. Those who lent to him after the declaration of insolvency have a prior claim over the earlier creditors. The earlier creditors lent when the man was still dealing with people and benefit from their wealth was hoped for, since he was buying and selling among them. But those who lent to him after knowing that he was destitute gave only as support and assistance, so the one who gave a debt after the declaration of insolvency has the stronger claim.

A further question: the insolvent is not prevented from marrying, nor from other such matters involving dirhams, nor from riding a mount along with his wife; in these the choice is his, if he wishes.
