# The Discourse to Kumayl

The discourse the Imam delivered to Kumayl brings these aspects of the ethos into sharper focus. Its importance lies above all in how deeply esoteric it is. The Imam took his disciple Kumayl ibn Ziyad by the hand and led him out of Kufa to a graveyard; once the city lay behind them, he drew a deep breath and gave him the following private counsel.

> يَا كُمَيْل بْن زِيَادٍ، إِنَّ هذهِ الْقُلُوبَ أَوْعِيَةٌ فَخَيْرُهَا أَوْعَاهَا فَاحْفَظْ عَنِّي مَا أَقُولُ لَكَ۔ النَّاسُ ثَلاَثَةٌ- فَعَالِمٌ رَبَّانِيٌّ وَمُتَعَلِّمٌ عَلَى سَبِيلِ نَجَاةٍ، وَهَمَجٌ رَعَاعٌ أَتْبَاعُ كُلِّ نَاعِقٍ يَمِيلُونَ مَعَ كُلِّ رِيحٍ، لَمْ يَسْتَضِيئُوا بِنُورِ الْعِلْمِ، وَلَمْ يَلْجَؤُوا إِلَى رُكْنٍ وَثِيقٍ

O Kumayl ibn Ziyad, truly these hearts are vessels, containers for knowledge and wisdom, and the best of them are those which hold the most. So retain from me that which I say to you. People are divided into three types: a lordly knower (عَالِمٌ رَبَّانِيٌّ); one who seeks knowledge (مُتَعَلِّمٌ) for the sake of deliverance; and the common folk (هَمَجٌ رَعَاعٌ), the word naming the moth that hurls itself at any flame, following just anyone, swaying with every current, neither desiring to be illumined by the light of knowledge nor seeking refuge in any strong support.

O Kumayl, knowledge is better than wealth, for knowledge guards you, while you must guard your wealth; and wealth diminishes as it is spent, while knowledge only increases as it is disbursed, replenishing itself the more it is given away; the fruits of wealth vanish when the wealth itself is gone, the friends bought with money and the comforts it purchased all falling away with it, while no one can take a man's knowledge from him. O Kumayl ibn Ziyad, the wise apprehension of knowledge (مَعْرِفَةُ الْعِلْمِ) is a religion by which God is worshipped, for din, religion, is kin to dayn, a debt, and it is by acquiring knowledge that a man repays the debt his own intellect has incurred to its Maker. Through it man acquires obedience in his life and a good name after his death. And knowledge is a judge, while wealth is judged: knowledge is active and wealth passive, for the one who knows takes command of what he owns, his knowledge directing him how to handle it, whereas wealth is forever something ruled upon by its possessor.

O Kumayl ibn Ziyad, those who hoard wealth perish even while they live, but the knowers endure for as long as time subsists; their material forms are absent, but their images in the hearts of men are present. Ah, what abundant knowledge is here (and he pointed to his breast), would that I could find one to bear it. Yes, I found one who was quick to understand, but he could not be trusted with it, exploiting the tools of religion for the sake of worldly gain, empowering himself with the graces of God against His slaves and with His proofs against His friends; for when the vessel of the heart is corrupt, this knowledge, pure and precious as it is, becomes a weapon turned against the very friends of God. Then there was another, obedient to the bearers of truth but lacking heartfelt insight, in whom, at the first appearance of obscurity, doubt was kindled in the heart. So alas, neither this one nor that! Nor one who is greedy for pleasure and submissive to passion, nor one obsessed with acquisition and accumulation, neither of them a guardian of religion in any respect. They resemble nothing so much as grazing cattle. Thus does knowledge die with the death of its bearers, when its trust falls to the worthless and the self-serving.

But indeed, O my God, the earth will never be empty of one who establishes the proof of God, whether overtly and in the open or fearfully and in obscurity, lest God's proofs and elucidations come to naught. Yet how many are such as these, and where are they? By God, they may be the smallest in number, but with God they are the greatest in rank, for here authority is not the possession of the many, and truth is not settled by the count of those who hold it; the guardians of the proof are few, and it is they who stand highest in the sight of God. Through them God preserves His proofs and elucidations, so that they may entrust them to their compeers and sow them in the hearts of those who resemble them. Through them, knowledge penetrates the reality of insight; they take hold of the very spirit of certainty; they make easy what the extravagant find harsh, and they befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged; with their bodies they keep company with the world, while their spirits are bound to the transcendent realm. They are the vicegerents of God upon His earth, the summoners to His religion. Ah, how I long to see them! Go now, Kumayl, as you will.

The four sentences of the penultimate paragraph describe those who bear the title of lordly knower (عَالِمٌ رَبَّانِيٌّ): the fewest in number, yet the greatest in the sight of God, and the ones through whom His religion is sustained. By His religion is meant the divine reality of religion — the pure and unchanging essence of the faith, perfect and constant — as against religion in the ordinary sense, whose outward institutions are subject to the changes of human history. From Adam to the Seal of the prophets those outward institutions have shifted with the conditions of men, but the essence, the message, and the purpose have never altered: the salvation of the soul through willing submission to God, as the Quran declares: إِنَّ ٱلدِّينَ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلْإِسْلَمُ, "Truly, religion in the sight of God is submission" (Ali 'Imran: 19). It is through the knowledge of the true knowers that religion stays true to itself, and only through such sages that religion truly lives in the world.

"Through them, knowledge penetrates the reality of insight." The Imam does not say that their knowledge penetrates it, but that through them knowledge penetrates the reality of insight. Knowledge can remain merely abstract, far removed from that reality. There is the outward, surface knowledge of a thing — its zahir — and the inner, hidden knowledge of the same thing — its batin. Yet knowledge holds a potential that calls out to be realized. It needs to be brought to life so that it may reach something deeper than the words and concepts that give shape to its own outward form, the meaning that lies ready on the surface. A man may have a surface knowledge of a verse of the Quran, of a saying, of why he prays and fasts and how many cycles each prayer requires; but when he presses past that surface and asks what the bowing and the prostration truly represent, a world of knowledge opens that is wholly unlike the bare information he began with. Degrees of knowledge are plainly implied here, and with them the rejection of all literalism and superficiality, of the easy mistake of taking factual information for real knowledge, let alone for wisdom. The shift of consciousness this penetration requires is shown vividly by the Imam's gesture toward his breast: it is not the chest but the heart hidden behind it that holds knowledge. The chest is the outward form, the zahir that conceals the heart within its batin, and to reach the heart one must first penetrate the chest.

The word chosen to translate hajama is "penetrate," but the primary sense of the verb is to assault, to take by storm, to charge. In the warfare of that age the battle lines were drawn as a right wing, a left wing, and a center — the heart of the army (قَلْبُ الْعَسْكَرِ), where the king or commander stood. The aim of the assault was to break through the wings and reach that heart, for once the king was taken the battle was won, and the victor took command of everything the defeated had held: the kingdom, its treasury, its army, its people. The chessboard preserves the same rule. So the word helps to show that conceptual knowledge holds a latent force — one might almost call it an explosive power — deriving from the reality of knowledge, its haqiqa or spiritual essence. It is this that calls out to be made real through tahaqquq, realization in depth rather than mere verification on the surface; and the striving by which one storms the outer wall of words to reach the heart of their meaning is تَحْقِيق, investigation. This haqiqa can be pointed to, but never captured, by the words and concepts that make up its formal, communicable aspect — just as the chest can only be opened in order to reach the heart. By means of this inner force, which its outer conceptual form at once hides and evokes, knowledge can effect a shift of consciousness from one degree of knowledge to another: a man begins to see the very realities of this world differently from those who rest in the surface play of cause and effect, so that the same act which is a burden to one is a delight to another. An indirect, mental conception of reality thus prepares the way for a penetrating, existential perception of reality. This is the difference between mere sight and true insight: sight reaches only the outward, superficial reality, whereas the reality of insight opens the intelligence to reality as such — ordinary insight, like abstract knowledge, remaining only at the surface level of understanding. The same idea appears in a saying of Ali (ع) concerning the true understanding that comes to plumb, and so truly to know, the buried treasures of knowledge: "He who understands knows the depth of knowledge (مَنْ فَهِمَ عَلِمَ غَوْرَ اَلْعِلْمِ)." And the effort that true knowledge demands, as against the mere piling up of facts, is likewise indicated: "Through learning and assimilating knowledge, until it becomes deeply rooted in one's consciousness, is knowledge attained (بِالتَّعَلُّمِ يُنَالُ الْعِلْمُ)."

The urgency of realizing this immanent power within knowledge — of moving from the abstract to the concrete, the potential to the actual, the static to the dynamic, the formal to the essential — is powerfully evoked by hajama, a word bound up with battle. It warns against taking the first understanding of an idea for its full comprehension, and full intellectual comprehension for the idea's living interiorization in the spirit. For when knowledge at last storms the surface of religion and takes the heart, a transformation begins from within: the heart, now illumined by the realities it has seized, takes command of the limbs and the actions, which are the soul's own resources, exactly as the victor takes command of the kingdom he has won. This interiorization is a process of endless unfolding within, not the mere registering of some fact from without. It requires the effective integration of knowledge within being: knowledge from without must be transformed into being from within, woven into the deepest texture of one's existence. Knowledge thus demands not merely the attention of the mind but the total orientation of the soul.

In this ethos, then, the effective integration (تَوْحِيد) of knowledge within being cannot be reached without the participation of the whole soul; and this in turn requires the elimination of all the vices that flow from self-centeredness. Thus the الْعَاقِل, the true intellectual, is described by the Imam as one constantly engaged in struggle against his own soul — against the lower inclinations, the passionate whims, and the distracting caprices of the ego. These vices are not confined to the plane of morality; they encroach upon the domain of knowledge as well, and this becomes clear in the very measure that knowledge is grasped as a concrete vision of things rather than a merely notional or conceptual grasp of them. The vices are then seen as so many clouds obscuring the vision proper to the reality of insight. According to the Quran, evil actions become a rust that covers over the heart: "Nay, their hearts have become rusted by that which they have done" (Al-Mutaffifin: 14). The aqil, then, is engaged in a constant inner struggle against the tendencies that not only sully the soul but blind the heart. As a virtuous man he opposes vice on the moral plane, because it runs contrary to virtue; but vice is opposed for a further reason, one that lies on the higher plane of spiritual reality: it contradicts the nature of the Real, it obscures the reality of insight, and it blinds the eye of the heart which alone can see God.

This does not deny the validity of the moral perspective; the stress falls rather on the deeper motive that drives the الْعَاقِل toward virtue, a motive that rises above the moral plane while including it. Morality appears here as the fruit or effect, and spirituality as the seed or cause. By drawing attention to the intellectual side of the aqil's motive for shedding the vices, the Imam shows more clearly how immorality is rooted in ignorance; and to uproot that ignorance, for the true intellectual, is to dissolve the very substance of the vices. This uprooting demands great effort, a relentless battle within — indeed the most formidable of all battles, al-jihad al-akbar, the greatest struggle, as the Prophet defined it.

The following sayings of Ali bear on the importance of this inner struggle:

"Struggling against the soul through knowledge, such is the mark of the intellect."

"The strongest of people are those who are strongest against their own souls."

"Truly, one who fights his own soul in obedience to God, and does not sin against Him, has the rank of the righteous martyr in the sight of God."

"The ultimate battle is that of a man against his own soul."

"He who knows his soul fights it."

"No jihad is more excellent than the jihad of the soul."

In another saying the Imam clarifies what the Prophet meant by naming the greatest jihad the jihad of the soul (النَّفْس). It is not so much a matter of fighting against the soul as of the battle for the soul itself. This emerges from the metaphor by which the Imam defines the struggle: الْعَقْل, the intellect, leads the forces of al-Rahman, the Compassionate; al-hawa — whim, caprice, desire — commands the forces of al-shaytan, the devil; and النَّفْس, the soul, wavers between them, drawn by both (مُتَجَاذِبَةٌ بَيْنَهُمَا), falling under the sway of whichever of the two prevails.

These sayings make clear that for the true intellectual the soul in its entirety must be "conquered" and enlisted on the side of the intellect. The aim is not to destroy the lower soul but to free it from the destructive influence of its caprice, its egoistic whims, its subjectivism, its individualism — all of which are gathered up in the word هَوَى. The Prophet, the Quran tells us, does not speak from هَوَى; rather: "It is naught but a revelation revealed" (An-Najm: 3-4). The divine objectivity of revelation is here set against the human subjectivity of passionate whim. In the setting of the holy war within, the Imam's sayings bring a parallel opposition into focus: the objectivity of the intellect must prevail over the subjectivity of individual caprice and egotistic desire. It is the intellect that represents — in the sense of "makes present" — the divine power of al-Rahman, and that combats the forces of al-shaytan as embodied in al-hawa.

The word هَوَى is used further in the Quran to denote desires that spring from the ego, by which the ego becomes a kind of false god: "Hast thou seen him who maketh his desire his god?" (25: 43; almost identical at 45: 23). This is the subtle or hidden idolatry (الشِّرْكُ الْخَفِيُّ) that the Prophet described as more hidden than a black ant crawling on a dark stone in a moonless night. To root out this idolatry of the ego, a force higher than the ego itself must be brought to bear, since the ego can never be controlled by anything that belongs to it; and that higher force is the divine Reality, access to which is opened through the power of the intellect and the graces of faith. This complementarity between the effort to overcome the egoism of هَوَى and the inflow of divine grace is well expressed in the following Quranic verse: "As for he who fears the station of his Lord, and restraineth his soul from whim (هَوَى), verily, Paradise will be his abode" (An-Naziat: 40-41).

The soul's fundamental energy is not to be destroyed but converted and redirected — away from the fleeting objects of individualistic desire and the impulses of al-shaytan, and toward the one true object named by al-Rahman. This "conquest" of the soul is far more than the arrival at some mental conception. It is not only the mind, the will, and the character that must be transformed; the soul's very deepest disposition must be integrated into the truth perceived by the intellect, if a man is to perceive that truth in all its dimensions and ramifications across the different planes of being.

For the Imam the need to win the soul over in its totality follows naturally from his understanding of تَوْحِيد, the most fundamental principle of Islam. تَوْحِيد is a verbal noun, and to translate it as mere oneness or unity fails to capture its full force. Literally it means "affirming one" — the affirmation of and belief in God's oneness, which is the outward, theological meaning — or "realizing one," the realization that there is but one reality, which is the inner, spiritual meaning. In the Imam's view, then, the absolutely Real is perceived not only as being one but also as making one, as integrating. The Real is not only unique; it is also unifying. تَوْحِيد thus comes to mean not merely affirming the oneness of God, but being one oneself so as to reflect the One perfectly: to know God is to be "integrated." Here one may see a link with the etymology of 'aql, whose primary meaning is "to bind." Yet whereas the later mystical tradition tends to stress the limiting aspect of this word — the intellect as that which defines and encloses meaning, over against the heart, which intuits and discloses spiritual truth — here the intellect is that which "binds together" the scattered elements of the soul into an organic unity. This principle is expressed in the first of the following sayings of the Imam, each of which merits deep reflection:

"He who knows God integrates himself (مَنْ عَرَفَ اللهَ تَوَحَّدَ)."

"He who knows his soul disengages himself (مَنْ عَرَفَ نَفْسَهُ تَجَرَّدَ)."

"He who knows people isolates himself (مَنْ عَرَفَ النَّاسَ تَفَرَّدَ)."

"He who knows the world withholds himself (مَنْ عَرَفَ الدُّنْيَا تَزَهَّدَ)."

Elsewhere this total adherence to the Real is described by the Imam as "sincerity" — sidq or إِخْلَاص — terms that mean far more than simple truthfulness, as the root of إِخْلَاص already shows. The verb khalusa means to be utterly pure, true to one's essence, unmixed with any foreign element. This side of the meaning comes through clearly in the following saying of the Imam: "Make purely for God (أَخْلِصْ لِلَّهِ) your action and your knowledge, your love and your hatred, your taking and your leaving, your speech and your silence."

To be totally sincere, then, means to make oneself "purely" for God — to carry no "stain" of impurity, understood here as whatever is other than God, in one's devotion, action, intention, or even sentiment. One must love and hate only in relation to one's bond with God. Of all the tendencies that work their way into one's motives and intentions, the most destructive is egotism. إِخْلَاص, pure sincerity, is the active effort to be, think, and act purely for God — that is, with selflessness — which is why the Imam lays such stress on the holy war within the soul as the "ultimate battle." One who wins this battle attains the rank of the "righteous martyr," for the victor here is a martyr in the truest sense: he has sacrificed his lower life for the sake of God, with whom is the real life — the life witnessed by the intellect, the only element within human consciousness able to look upon the soul as "the other" with genuine objectivity.

As the saying holds, "struggling against the soul through knowledge, such is the mark of the intellect." The intellectual engaged in this battle is also one who bears "witness" (al-shahid, meaning both martyr and witness, as in the original Greek sense of the word). He bears witness to God through the intellect's sacrifice of whatever opposes the truth of God, and the chief opponent is his own egotism, his own "false life." To possess perfect إِخْلَاص, then, is to be rid of all hypocritical self-preoccupation and to put in its place a sincere theocentricity — a mode of being centered upon God rather than oneself. إِخْلَاص, therefore, means being for God, or belonging to God, in the sense pointed to by the Quranic verse: "Say: Truly my worship and my sacrifice, and my living and my dying, are for God, Lord of the universes" (Al-Anam: 162).

That worship is the first thing named in this verse is of particular significance for understanding Ali 's spiritual ethos, and the indispensable role of worship and devotion must be emphasized in setting that ethos out. The second chapter takes up this role in the context of the quest for the fundamental virtue of justice. The ultimate fruits of worship and devotion — of which the quintessence is the remembrance of God — are the subject of the third chapter. One of the defining features of the Imam's spiritual ethos is indeed the emphasis he places on prayer, taking that term in its widest sense to include all the means — from formal worship and supplication to meditation and reflection, remembrance and contemplation — by which the individual is oriented and attuned to the Real, اَلْحَقّ.

Worship is therefore an integral aspect of إِخْلَاص, precisely because it answers to and sustains a fundamental dimension of the human soul. That within the soul which yearns to worship, and is nourished by worship alone, must be given its due. In a spiritual context, if worship is pushed to the margins, one's sincerity is compromised: one is no longer being sincere with God, no longer being "just" to God, no longer rendering God His due. And the spiritual logic of the Imam holds that if one is not just with God, one cannot be just to His creatures. "Whoso establishes well-being between himself and God," he says, "God establishes well-being between him and mankind."

One might also cite here the Quranic verse: "Worship thy Lord until certainty cometh to thee" (Al-Hijr: 99). The quest for knowledge demands total dedication if it is to yield its inner riches, and that dedication includes the near-alchemical transformation of consciousness worked by total concentration on the divine Reality — prayer in one of its deepest modes. In this form of prayer, the intensity of dedication is such that nothing else can occupy one's consciousness alongside its object: the "remembrance" (ذِکْر) of God strictly implies the forgetting of everything else, and above all of oneself. The spiritual reality of the dhikr can only be reached when "you forget your soul in your remembrance." Concentration upon the divine Reality as the supreme object of knowledge, made present through the Name or Names of God, is thus a mode of prayer — one which, to be effective, requires both presence of mind and a genuine engagement of the will. To pray absent-mindedly or mechanically is far from enough. On this the Imam says, "He who prays without exertion is like one who shoots [arrows] without a bow." In the same way, there is nothing passive or easy about acquiring knowledge: "Knowledge calls out for action; if it is answered [it is of avail], otherwise it departs."

The same idea is expressed more forcefully in this saying of the Imam: "When you hear knowledge [being imparted] cleave to it, do not mix it up with jest, lest hearts spew it forth." Knowledge that is not taken seriously will be of no avail; it remains "undigested" and cannot be assimilated by the "heart" — that innermost faculty which has access to the "reality of insight" and which can thus "see" God. For it is the heart that sees Allah, not by eyesight, not by بصارۃ, but through the spiritual insight, the بصیرۃ, generated by the true principles of faith, the حقائق الایمان. Indeed, it is not the individual who "assimilates" knowledge to himself; it is knowledge — or rather the reality of being expressed through true knowledge — that assimilates the individual to itself: "Acquire knowledge, and [true] life will acquire you." The relationship between life and consciousness, so central to the intrinsic function of the spirit, is again at the heart of this maxim. At first the individual seeks knowledge and cultivates consciousness; then life "seeks" the individual, as being makes itself more fully real within him. Put differently, the individual first makes an active effort to integrate knowledge within his being; but at a certain point this activity turns into passive receptivity: being — "true life" — integrates the individual within itself. To return to the Imam's guidance to Kumayl: "Through them knowledge penetrates the reality of insight." It is not the knowers who penetrate; it is knowledge that penetrates to the reality of insight, doing so through them, and this in the very measure of their effacement within knowledge — an effacement that is the shadow cast by their effective integration within the life that "acquires" them.
