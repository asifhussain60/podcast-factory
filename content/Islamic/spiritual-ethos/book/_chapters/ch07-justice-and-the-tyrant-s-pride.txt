# Justice and the Tyrant's Pride

The letter is addressed to one of the Imam's closest and most trusted companions. For that reason, its warnings about injustice and oppression carry a very precise force, over and above the universal theme they express. They apply to everyone who lives under Islam and its laws, and who begins with every right intention, yet remains vulnerable to the temptations of political power. They reckon fully with an unfortunate but proven truth, caught in the maxim that power corrupts — or, in the Imam's own words, 'he who rules, appropriates (مَنْ مَلَكَ اسْتَأْثَرَ).'

The Imam's understanding of justice plainly reaches beyond both its own historical moment and the formal boundaries of the Islamic faith. The letter can be read as speaking not only to those who wish to govern by just principles, but to all who, though not corrupt at the outset, are corruptible — a class that takes in the overwhelming majority of humanity.

A note of somber realism sounds from the very opening of the letter. Man's potential for evil, once he is left to his own devices and cut off from divine grace, dominates the mood of the preamble. Hence its emphasis on abiding by God's wajibat, the religious obligations binding on every Muslim. Malik is instructed to set obedience to God above all else, 'to abide by what He has commanded in His Book ... for no one prospers except through abiding by them, and no one is wretched except through repudiating and neglecting them'. The preamble is introductory, but by no means perfunctory. Obedience to the divine commands is to be understood not merely as legally binding but as spiritually liberating. Such obedience plays a crucial part in freeing a person from the tyranny of the 'soul which incites to evil' (النَّفْسُ الْأَمَّارَةُ بِالسُّوءِ) — to which the Imam turns at once after calling Malik to obedience: 'For truly, the soul incites to evil, unless God has mercy.' This sentence is almost identical to the words of the Prophet Joseph in the Quran (Yusuf: 53), and it matters greatly in setting the scene for the ethical and socio-political instructions that follow. The relationship that determines the spiritual substance of the soul — and all the more its moral conduct — is the relationship between the human soul and divine grace. Without grace the soul inclines toward evil; with God's help it is freed from its base inclinations and guided back to its true, original nature, its fitra, to which the Quran refers in the verse that follows:

So set thy purpose for religion with unswerving devotion: the nature of God (firat Allah), according to which He hath created man. There is no altering God's creation. That is the right religion (ٱلدِّينُ ٱلْقَيِّمُ), but most men know not. (Ar-Rum: 30)

The present state of the fallen soul, then, is called to give way to the ideal state of original human nature. That nature is both the origin and the end of the human condition, and at once the true substance of every human soul — always accessible in principle, even if for most people it stays clouded by fallen nature in practice. The quest to revive this original nature turns on the complementary work of the human intellect and divine revelation. In the first sermon of the Nahj, the Imam states that God sent mankind 'His messengers, dispatching prophets in succession, in order to claim from them [His creatures] the fulfillment of the covenant of His creation; to remind them of His forgotten graces; to remonstrate with them through communication [of His revelation]; to unearth for them the buried treasures of the intellects (دَفْعُ الْعُقُولِ).'

These 'buried treasures' can be identified with the original, God-given knowledge that belongs to the fitra. Out of this whole human nature, in complete harmony with the divine nature, all the essential virtues flow freely and unhindered. 'Let your most beloved treasure be the treasure of virtuous acts', the Imam writes. Virtue will indeed be the 'most beloved treasure' of one who reverses the natural inclinations of the self-centered soul and sets a supernatural orientation toward its own deepest nature — and by that very act, toward the divine Reality that furnishes the foundations in being of all authentic virtue. For اَلْحَقّ, the Real, is not only the deepest foundation of virtue; it is the true substance of every positive quality, and, in the end, of every single thing that exists. As one of the most celebrated supplications attributed to the Imam puts it:

By Thy tremendousness (بِعَظَمَتِكَ), which has filled all things ... by Thy Names which have filled the foundations of all things, by Thy knowledge which encompasses all things, by the light of Thy face, through which all things are illumined ...

From this vantage point, every virtue can be seen as the reflection, on the human plane, of a divine attribute — what is called a divine 'Name'. Hence a theme running through the whole ihsani tradition, the path of spiritual excellence (احسان) that the Prophet defined as worshipping God as though one could see Him, knowing that even if one does not see Him, He sees him. That theme finds expression in the saying: 'Assume the character traits of God (تَخَلَّقُوا بِأَخْلَاقِ اللهِ).'

The link between human virtue and divine reality is wholly indispensable in the Imam's perspective. Subjectively, the practice of virtue draws down the matching divine quality; objectively, the divine quality is the source and — in accordance with true تَوْحِيد — the actual substance of all human virtue. Every positive quality, both in itself and in its manifestation, belongs to God, the One who 'has no partner' theologically and 'has no second' ontologically. This mystical grasp of the principle of تَوْحِيد is strongly implied in the Quranic verse, 'And thou threw not when thou didst throw [a handful of dust], but God it was who threw' (Al-Anfal: 17). These divine qualities are not to be seen as purely transcendent, as though they were wholly one with the divine essence and utterly beyond the soul's reach. They are also to be grasped as exemplars, as patterns of perfection for the soul to imitate as fully as it can. The goal is to become as much 'like' God as possible; yet this growth can unfold only within the bounds of one's servitude to Him. A person is generous for the sake of God, because God is الکریم, the Generous; he is compassionate and merciful because He is الرحمن الرحیم — never out of any claim that 'I am generous' or 'I am merciful', for the aim is to imitate God's qualities, not to claim to embody Him in person. The individual must therefore be effaced before God in order to be truly virtuous, and must at the same time share in and embody the very qualities he wishes God to show him. He stays lowly, humble, and effaced throughout, certain that no measure of virtue or intelligence can ever lessen or match the qualities of God — there being no proportion between the Absolute and the relative, the Creator and the created. His effort to embody these qualities is strengthened to the degree that he apprehends the divine attributes existentially, through intuition, rather than merely through reason, in theory. It is like gazing into a mirror: what one sees is a reflection, but an inverted one. The aim is to catch, on the human plane, the inverted reflection of divine transcendence. For man is at once خلیفۃ اللہ, God's vicegerent upon the earth, and عباد اللہ, His slave. The awareness of servitude keeps him, even while he represents God, from slipping into rivalry with Him in grandeur; the active commitment to representing God's attributes guards him from the spiritual paralysis of neglecting his duties toward the world. Such is the meaning of the verse, يَٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ أَنتُمُ ٱلْفُقَرَآءُ إِلَى ٱللَّهِۖ وَٱللَّهُ هُوَ ٱلْغَنِىُّ ٱلْحَمِيدُ, 'O mankind, you are the destitute before God, and God is the Self-sufficient, the Praiseworthy' (Fatir: 15). There must be a synthesis between the active human virtues and the spiritual awareness of one's nothingness before the divine Reality. The bond between intelligence and humility deserves emphasis here: intellectual perception of the divine attributes produces humility, and the effacement of the soul in turn draws attention away from the ego and toward the transcendent reality as the source of all virtue and truth.

The notion of the 'soul which incites to evil', and the Quranic story of Joseph (يُوسُفُ) in which it arises, deserves closer attention. The Imam could assume that his readers knew this context well, and he clearly meant it to stay before the mind of Malik, and of anyone else reading this letter on governance.

Joseph gives this description of man's base inclinations after his innocence of every charge has been established before the Egyptian king. Even so, he says, 'I do not exonerate myself. Truly, the soul incites to evil, unless my Lord has mercy.' On the two occasions when he faced the seductive temptations of Zulaykha, the Quran makes clear that he resisted not through his own moral resources but through the grace of God.

This theme of human helplessness before gratification that is alluring but immoral is strongly implied in the Imam's repetition of the Quranic phrase, 'Truly the soul incites to evil, unless God has mercy.' Here, then, are the two poles that draw the ordinary soul, the soul not yet 'at peace': its own base inclinations toward the negative possibilities of the fleeting world of appearances, and the higher pull toward the grace of God, which draws the soul back toward the pure positivity of ultimate reality. The sheer sharpness of the contrast within this powerful Quranic sentence quickens an awareness of the soul's absolute and unconditional need of God in every circumstance. It is a most fitting way to open a letter of instruction to a man about to be given authority over a vast and wealthy country, where temptations lie in wait on every side. It stands as a solemn reminder of the soul's openness to allurement on every plane, gross and subtle alike — an openness the soul cannot neutralize by itself, but only through the mercy and grace of God. And yet, to return to Joseph's words, the 'vision of the evidence' of God implies active discernment and, as its moral consequence, firm self-control. Human effort to avoid sin therefore does not contradict the need for grace in the attainment of virtue; rather, it expresses a grace already given, already present within the conscientious soul. The very capacity of the human will to strive for virtue is itself a grace already there, set within the spiritual constitution, or fitra — though a grace that must be made present, or actualized, through the sincere effort to draw upon this God-given power.

How this grace enters the soul as a shaping force of moral and spiritual life becomes clearer later in the Imam's letter. For now, the negative side of the question calls for more elaboration, since the ruler's ever-present temptation to abuse his power and commit injustice and tyranny deserves more than a passing mention. One might argue that how to refrain from injustice is a far harder and subtler question than its positive counterpart, how to act with justice. The Imam sets out the positive rules of political governance directly and plainly; what is far less plain is whether any holder of office can actually stay faithful to those rules when the temptations of power press upon him. Laws can be framed to punish corruption, but what no law can supply is the will, or the capacity, to stay incorruptible before the blandishments of political authority. This helps explain why the Imam's exhortations take on so markedly negative a cast in the early part of the letter — the command to 'break the soul in the face of passionate desires', to 'dominate your inclination', to 'withhold yourself from that which is not lawful for you'. It also explains why the moral demand of justice must be set firmly within a lived spirituality. Without that grounding, commands to be fair, just, and honest lack the existential quality that turns them from abstract propositions into unquestionable realities, and that turns formal obedience to rules imposed from outside into a spiritual kinship with the source of those rules.

Living by just precepts then stops being a matter of compliance with something imposed from outside and becomes instead the moral companion of one's inner being. In other words, a whole approach to justice leads to an identification with what is right, with اَلْحَقّ itself, rather than merely to the doing of what is right. There is a shift of consciousness from doing to being, from action to contemplation — yet without any weakening or undermining of action. On the contrary, action comes to reflect, on its own plane, the divine object of contemplation, and so becomes more effective, not less. An orientation toward the Real lends a deeper degree of reality both to what one does and to one's own mode of being. One does what is right insofar as one is real; the Islamic spiritual tradition names this process of realization تَحْقِيق, 'making real'. One's ethical conduct is 'made real' through spirituality, and spirituality, in turn, is expressed by, and made more concrete through, the practice of virtue.

The Imam then issues a stern warning: وَلاَ تَنْصِبَنَّ نَفْسَكَ لِحَرْبِ اللهِ, 'Do not set your soul up for war with God.' One may well ask what this means, and what kinds of acts or attitudes place the soul at war with God. The phrase seems to reach deeper than plain disobedience, deeper than the many breaches of religious obligation named at the letter's outset. That is the most obvious sense of 'fighting' against God, but it does not rule out subtler forms of opposition to divine reality — attitudes that may go along with outward acts of obedience yet contradict their very spirit. One example is the cardinal vice of pride (takabbur, or kibr), against which the Imam warns again and again: the inner state in which a person feels superior and treats others highhandedly, its signs so plain in his conduct that they cannot be hidden. A ruler's pride is a theft of a divine attribute, a seizing of a greatness that belongs to God alone, who declares: الْكِبْرِيَاءُ رِدَائِي، وَالْعَظَمَةُ إِزَارِي، فَمَنْ نَازَعَنِي وَاحِدًا مِنْهُمَا، قَذَفْتُهُ فِي النَّارِ, 'Pride is My cloak and greatness is My robe; whoever contends with Me over either of them, I shall cast him into the Fire.' God alone has the right to takabbur, and God alone may be called, without offense, al-Mutakabbir, 'the Proud'. To vaunt oneself before others is, in effect, to defy God's authority inwardly, and so to deny in an unspoken way His unique and incomparable majesty. This denial of something inseparable from divine sovereignty can itself be understood as a kind of 'war' with God, for a subject who openly defies his king is, in effect, waging war against him. In short, enmity toward transcendent reality is the invisible substance that pervades all pride, and every act of arrogance or oppression a ruler commits against those beneath him.

The paradox here can be grasped only through a sense of spiritual values: an attitude of superiority toward those 'below' is in fact an act of rebellion against that which is 'above'. An injustice toward creatures does two things at once — it draws down upon its perpetrator the justice of the Creator, and it is, in itself, outright opposition to the Creator. What looks like a horizontal vice — 'horizontal' in the sense that it belongs to this world — is in reality a spiritual disorder. The outward immorality of corruption is only the symptom of an inner spiritual malaise: an unspoken denial that the source of all power and authority is absolute, and hence unique, and a forgetting of the truth expressed in the Quranic verse, لِّمَنِ ٱلْمُلْكُ ٱلْيَوْم لِلَّهِ ٱلْوَحِدِ ٱلْقَهَّارِ, 'To whom belongeth the dominion this day? To God, the One, the All-Conquering' (Ghafir: 16). This truth should sound constantly in the ears of every ruler, 'this day' understood as every day, not only the Day of Judgment. In the Imam's sermon known as al-Qasia (القاسیۃ), 'the Abasement', so named because it humbles the proud, Iblis (ابلیس) is described as the first of those who 'competed with God' in grandeur, and thus as the very embodiment of pride and arrogance. When he was commanded to prostrate before Adam, Iblis refused, as the Quran recounts:

إِذْ قَالَ رَبُّكَ لِلْمَلَٓئِكَةِ إِنِّى خَلِقٌۢ بَشَرًۭا مِّن طِينٍۢ ۝ فَإِذَا سَوَّيْتُهُۥ وَنَفَخْتُ فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِى فَقَعُوا۟ لَهُ سَجِدِينَ ۝ فَسَجَدَ ٱلْمَلَٓئِكَةُ كُلُّهُمْ أَجْمَعُونَ ۝ إِلَّآ إِبْلِيسَ ٱسْتَكْبَرَ وَكَانَ مِنَ ٱلْكَفِرِينَ, 'When your Lord said to the angels: Truly I am going to create man from clay; and when I have fashioned him and breathed into him of My spirit, then fall down before him prostrate. So the angels prostrated themselves, all of them together; save Iblis: he was proud and was one of the disbelievers' (Sad: 71-74).

He refused because 'vehement passion' overcame him. He judged himself superior to Adam on the grounds that he was made of fire while Adam was made of clay, خلقتنی من نار و خلقتہ من طین. In taking pride in this presumed superiority, and thereby rebelling against God's command, Iblis becomes not only the first 'enemy of God' but also 'the leader of the bigots and the forerunner of the proud' (imam al-muta assibin wa salaf al-mustakbirin, امام المُتَعَصِّبِین و سلف المستکبرین). The Imam warns his listeners: 'Beware lest he infect you with his disease' through his insinuations, whisperings, and machinations. He urges them: 'Be resolute in placing self-abasement over your heads, casting self-glorification beneath your feet, and removing pride from your necks. And take up humility as the fortified watchtower between you and your enemy.'

Humility is the chief weapon in the war against Iblis, whose own aim is the reverse: to instil pride. For pride is not only the worst of vices but the poison that infects and destroys every virtue. A virtue is turned into a vice the moment pride accompanies it. 'The sin that grieves you is better, in the sight of God, than the virtue that makes you proud.' Bound up with this is the point made earlier about the dangers of excessive praise and the conceit it breeds, which are among 'Satan's most reliable opportunities to efface the virtue of one who is virtuous'.

Taken together, these statements form a commentary on the Quranic account of Iblis' disgrace. They grasp a simple act of disobedience at its spiritual root, and in doing so bring home the ever-present danger of falling prey to that 'hidden' form of شِرْك (polytheism) to which the Prophet refers in these words: الشِّرْكُ فِي هَذِهِ الْأُمَّةِ akhfa مِنْ دَبِيبِ النَّمْلِ علی حَجَرِ الأَسوَد فِی لِیلَۃِ الظُّلْما — 'The creeping of شِرْك in my community is more hidden (أَخْفَى) than the creeping of a black ant over a hard rock on a dark night.' شِرْك is commonly taken to mean the worship of material idols, شرک الجلی, its open and manifest form; but it must be understood more subtly as شرک الخفی, the hidden form — false gods concealed within one's intentions, attitudes, and orientations, all the elements of hidden pride, vanity, ostentation, and pretentiousness. As the Imam says, 'Know that the slightest pretension (رِيَاء — ریا) is polytheism.' To act for the sake of earning others' praise is to act for others rather than for God, and so to attribute partners to God without saying so: 'Whoso acts for other than God, God entrusts him to those for whose sake he acted.'

In this light the meaning of Iblis' disobedience can be understood more deeply. It is the outward moral consequence of an inner spiritual malaise, rooted in that pride which is tantamount to 'hidden polytheism' and which brings a disgraceful humiliation in its train. Iblis is disgraced because he disobeyed God, and he disobeyed God on account of pride. Pride is therefore, in and of itself, a disgrace — a state of 'dis-grace', a perversion of the human condition that carries within it the seed of its own inexorable inversion. 'Do you not see how God humiliated him through his pride?' the Imam asks. To be proud is already, in reality, a form of humiliation, for to make oneself great is tantamount to self-destruction.

Returning to the pride of those in power, the Imam sets out concisely the train of thought that starts up in the mind of a ruler as he steps onto the slippery slope toward oppression: وَلاَ تَقُولَنَّ: إِنِّي مُؤَمَّرٌ! آمُرُ! فَأُطَاعُ! إِنَّ ذلِكَ إِدْغَالٌ فِي الْقَلْبِ، وَمَنْهَكَةٌ لِلدِّينِ — 'Do not say, "I have been given authority, I order and am obeyed", for this leads to corruption in the heart and the erosion of religion.' This self-glorification is seen as producing first an inner, spiritual corruption, that of the 'heart', and only then the destruction of religion. The order of priority makes a point: the outward prescriptions of religion require the right inward attitude if they are to be applied with integrity and maintained with stability. The Imam's thought might be restated thus: when power goes to the head, faith departs from the heart; and conversely, for faith to be properly manifested in the world, it must flow from a heart suffused with humility. This is another aspect of 'putting each thing in its proper place', وضع کل شئی حق محلہ.

The Imam continues: وَإِذَا أَحْدَثَ لَكَ مَا أَنْتَ فِيهِ مِنْ سُلْطَانِكَ أُبَّهَةً أَوْ مَخِيلَةً، فَانْظُرْ إِلَى عِظَمِ مُلْكِ اللهِ فَوْقَكَ، فَإِنَّ ذلِكَ يُطَامِنُ إِلَيْكَ مِنْ طِمَاحِكَ، وَيَكُفُّ عَنْكَ مِنْ غَرْبِكَ،يَفِيءُ إِلَيْكَ بِمَا عَزَبَ عَنْكَ مِنْ عَقْلِكَ — 'If the authority of your position engenders vanity and arrogance, then look at the grandeur of God's dominion above you … this will calm your ambition, restrain you from your own vehemence, and restore to you what had strayed from your intellect.' The bond between intellect and humility deserves close attention here. The part of the intellect that 'strays' when vanity and arrogance take hold is precisely the part that is conscious of God's absolute reality and of the relative, derivative, and ultimately illusory character of everything else. It is telling that the word for 'arrogance', makhila (مَخِيلَةً), comes from the same root as the word for imagination, khayal (خیال), and is thus intimately connected with the realm of the imaginary, the fanciful, the illusory, and the unreal: when imagination fastens upon the unreal, it becomes infused with pride and turns into مخیلۃ, arrogance. Arrogant pride is therefore an intense form of self-delusion rooted in a defect of the intellect. It manifests as a vice, but its cause runs deeper than the moral level, for it involves not merely an overestimation of oneself but, more fundamentally, an underestimation of the Absolute. Seen this way, the overt egotism of the tyrant is an intensification of the congenital egocentricity that clings to anyone who has not realized the truth of God's sole reality and the ultimately illusory nature of all else. The sage alone fully assimilates the truth expressed in such Quranic verses as: كُلُّ شَىْءٍ هَالِكٌ إِلَّا وَجْهَهُ — 'Everything is dying away except His countenance' (Al-Qasas: 88); كُلُّ مَنْ عَلَيْهَا فَانٍۢ ۝ وَيَبْقَىٰ وَجْهُ رَبِّكَ ذُو ٱلْجَلَلِ وَٱلْإِكْرَامِ — 'Everything that is thereon is perishing, and there subsisteth but the Face of thy Lord, possessor of Glory and Bounty' (Ar-Rahman: 26-27); and وَقُلْ جَآءَ ٱلْحَقُّ وَزَهَقَ ٱلْبَطِلُ ۚ إِنَّ ٱلْبَطِلَ كَانَ زَهُوقًۭا — 'The Truth hath come and falsehood hath passed away; indeed, falsehood is ever passing away' (Al-Isra: 81). Only the sage draws from this truth the humility that dissolves all individualism and egocentricity — the roots of pride, vanity, and arrogance, which are in turn the lifeblood of tyranny and oppression.

The Imam's warning can now be better appreciated: إِيَّاكَ وَمُسَامَةَ اللهِ فِي عَظَمَتِهِ، وَالتَّشَبُّهَ بِهِ فِي جَبَرُوتِهِ، فَإِنَّ اللهَ يُذِلُّ كُلَّ جَبَّار، وَيُهِينُ كُلَّ مُخْتَال — 'Beware of comparing [yourself] with God in greatness and likening [yourself] to Him in might, for God abases every tyrant and disgraces every braggart.' A ruler who does not act with justice toward God and His creatures — for the Imam commands, أَنْصِفِ اللهَ وَأَنْصِفِ النَّاسَ مِنْ نَفْسِكَ، وَمِنْ خَاصَّةِ أَهْلِكَ، وَمَنْ لَكَ فِيهِ هَوىً مِنْ رَعِيَّتِكَ، فَإِنَّكَ إِلاَّ تَفْعَلْ تَظْلِمْ — will find that not only His creatures but God Himself becomes his adversary: وَمَنْ ظَلَمَ عِبَادَ اللهِ كَانَ اللهُ خَصْمَهُ دُونَ عِبَادِهِ، وَمَنْ خَاصَمَهُ اللهُ أَدْحَضَ حُجَّتَهُ، وَكَانَ لله حَرْباً حَتَّى يَنْزعَ وَيَتُوبَ — 'He remains at war with God until he desists and repents.' The tyrant will in the end be compelled to repent, even if only at the moment of death and imminent judgment. And even if he seems to have escaped the consequences of his tyranny in this life, he cannot escape them in the Hereafter.

As for the nature of the tyrant's war against God in this world — that is, before repentance or judgment overtakes him — it consists in his implicit opposition to ultimate reality, his violation of the deepest nature of things, of the true nature of his own being, his فطرۃ. This war takes various forms.

One form is the very persistence of tyranny itself. The longer it continues, the more wretched the tyrant's soul becomes, and the more firmly the seeds of misery are planted in his being. This is what is implied by the Quranic doctrine of istidraj (اِسْتِدْرَاج), the drawing out of punishment by degrees. The word derives from درج, meaning stages — as even in Urdu one says درجہ بدرجہ, step by step. God uses it in Surat al-Araf: ‏وَمِمَّنْ خَلَقْنَآ أُمَّةٌۭ يَهْدُونَ بِٱلْحَقِّ وَبِهِ يَعْدِلُونَ ‎۱۸۱‏وَٱلَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا۟ بِـَٔايَتِنَا سَنَسْتَدْرِجُهُم مِّنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ ‎۱۸۲ — 'Of those We have created are people who guide with truth and dispense justice therewith. But those who reject Our signs, We will lead them on by degrees [to punishment] from whence they know not' (Al-Araf: 181-2). This punishment meted out by degrees can take the form of apparent worldly success, as the verse makes clear: ‏وَلَا تُعْجِبْكَ أَمْوَلُهُمْ وَأَوْلَدُهُمْ ۚ إِنَّمَا يُرِيدُ ٱللَّهُ أَن يُعَذِّبَهُم بِهَا فِى ٱلدُّنْيَا وَتَزْهَقَ أَنفُسُهُمْ وَهُمْ كَفِرُونَ ‎۸۵ — 'Let not their wealth nor their children please thee. God desireth only to punish them thereby in the world, that their souls shall pass away while they are disbelievers' (At-Tawbah: 85). A tyrant or oppressor can be a Muslim in the formal sense and yet at the same time be guilty of a mode of disbelief — taking the word kafir in its strictly etymological sense, as one who 'covers over' the truth and its moral concomitants, both in his very being and in his actions. This connects directly with the contrast drawn in the verses just quoted (Al-Araf: 181-2): between those who dispense justice in accordance with the truth and 'those who reject Our signs' — the oppressors and tyrants, precisely.
