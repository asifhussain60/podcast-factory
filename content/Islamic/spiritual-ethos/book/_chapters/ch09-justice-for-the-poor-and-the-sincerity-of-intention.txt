# Justice for the Poor and the Sincerity of Intention

The Imam now turns Malik toward those who most need justice, opening the passage with the exclamation "Allah, Allah" to mark its urgency. The instruction concerns those with no resources whatsoever: the destitute, the crippled, orphans, the elderly, those most in need of justice from a governor, who must be treated in such a way that "God may excuse you on the day you meet Him." The governor is to provide for all of these, and for others who are in need but too proud to beg. He ought to appoint an officer charged specifically with uncovering the needs of the most destitute and attending to them. The Imam then adds words that reach the heart of the matter: "This is onerous for the governors, and [the fulfillment of] all rights is onerous, but God makes it light for those who aspire to the Hereafter, who restrain their souls in patience, and trust in the truth of that which is promised them by God."

It is the spiritual dimension that makes an otherwise crushing ideal workable. God's help is assured to those whose attention is not confined to this world alone, but whose aspirations reach beyond it to the Hereafter. Only when this world is seen through to the next does a fully just attitude toward this world become possible. Those whose talab, their aspiration, does not reach beyond this world are far more likely to take the easy path and neglect the destitute; for in the purely horizontal reckoning of politics, the cost to a ruler of a policy of charity toward the poor may well outweigh any benefit he can see for himself. Here an ethics bound to a purely this-worldly assessment — to pragmatic politics or to personal interest — shows its inherent flaws. But when the sense of what is owed to the poor is charged with firm faith in God's unfailing justice and boundless compassion, and filled with longing for the Hereafter, it generates an unflinching fidelity to the needs of the destitute and renews that fidelity again and again.

Such an attitude goes far beyond conventional notions of charity or generosity. A person who has realized it to perfection cannot be swayed by any contrary suggestion rooted in self-serving pragmatism or individualism, still less in indifference or wilfulness. With this attitude firmly in place, the next quality the Imam names — patience — follows naturally. Given a concrete rather than merely notional foretaste of the absolute values at stake, patience can be sustained without limit; it no longer rests on personal will alone, but is upheld by the empowering grace that flows from heartfelt faith. The Imam completes the thought by noting that those with the right aspirations come, in consequence, to trust in the truth of God's promise. If that promise — of His reward, His unfailing justice, His boundless compassion — is true, then the motive behind the governor's effort to show the poor mercy and justice is immeasurably deepened. The task the Imam calls onerous — the duty to help the weak and helpless, from whom no political benefit can be gained — is transformed into an inescapable duty that follows directly from spiritual conviction. That conviction rests on the certainty that such generosity and compassion are not merely good, right, and proper, but that these virtues accord with the true and inalienable substance of ultimate reality. The moral implications of spiritual reality, and equally the spiritual consequences of virtue, are hidden from worldly rulers — by their covert prejudices at best, and their overt vices at worst. Such rulers are blind, too, to the divine promise of ultimate felicity, and equally blind to the warnings of retribution for the oppressors, al-zalimun. The zalim may be understood as one who defies the divine commandments concerning justice and, more fundamentally, violates the compassion that lies at the very heart of the divine nature.

These considerations open onto the crucial question of intention. Even when the just person's effort to live according to justice is thwarted by the uncertainties of worldly life, two things remain: the right intentions that move him, and his awareness of the immutable divine qualities that serve as the models and principles guiding his endeavor. Together these produce a real, inward "taste" of the grace that furnishes the spiritual foundation of his moral life. If God's promise is true, and one is absolutely certain of that truth, then its effect on the soul of the sincere believer transforms a personal ethic into an unconditional imperative, and a desirable value into an undeniable reality. Morality is thus deepened by spirituality, and into it: a total way of being, one that embraces the whole of existence, within which the burden of individual duty is not only immeasurably lightened but becomes a companion to one's taste of spiritual reality. One then acts justly out of gratitude for what one has already received — the joy of spiritual certainty — and not only out of hope for something not yet attained.

The Quran speaks of the reward of Paradise as something already granted to the true believer. For one whose certainty of Paradise is absolute, the future has already arrived: "We have given thee [the paradisal fountain of] al-Kawthar; so pray to thy Lord and sacrifice ..." (Al-Kawthar: 1-2). To worship God out of certainty of the blessedness to come — a blessedness that has, in one respect, already come — is well expressed in the following saying of the Imam:

Indeed there is a group who worship God out of desire [for something not yet attained]; and this is the worship of the merchants. And there is a group who worship God out of fear, and this is the worship of the slaves. And there is a group who worship God out of gratitude, and this is the worship of the free.

The just person is further freed from the material consequences of his actions, in proportion to the uprightness and sincerity of his intentions, since in the Islamic view actions are judged not by their consequences but by their intentions. The intention is to be just — not for the sake of any earthly reward or tangible worldly result, but purely for the sake of justice itself. And this essence or principle of justice is in turn inseparable from the divine nature. For justice is one with God not simply because whatever God commands is just; rather, God commands just acts precisely because they are just, and because justice is one with His own nature: "I bear witness that He is Justice and He acts justly." Justice is not one attribute among others, something merely present in the divine nature; it is that nature. Were it only in His nature, it would stand alongside His other qualities; but because it is His very essence, all His other qualities essentially flow from it, so that His mercy no less than His wrath, His creation no less than His destruction, all proceed from His justice. If God acts justly by virtue of His very nature, which is pure justice, then the spiritual person will act justly out of a desire to conform to God's nature, and not simply out of obedience to God's commands, and never out of desire for any earthly reward.

This can be seen in any single virtue. If one speaks the truth without grasping the reason for it — that truth, الحق, is the very nature of the Creator, and that the goal in this world is to take on the qualities of that Creator — then truthfulness soon becomes situational and self-serving, open to compromise whenever circumstances seem to warrant it: I speak the truth when it benefits me and lie when lying benefits me. But once truth is seen as the nature of the divine, it becomes everlasting and eternal, and the consequences of speaking it cease to matter; one speaks it simply in emulation of الحق, the absolute truth. Virtue practiced on an ad hoc basis is no virtue at all, merely a series of pragmatic adaptations to shifting circumstances. This is why the life of Ali (ع), when studied, reflects an unflinching adherence to the divine virtues. His was a life governed not by his نفس but entirely by the divine nature of Allah, and his every celebrated action was the fruit of an irresistible attraction toward the nature of الحق. His gaze was never fixed on the action itself but on the source of the principles that generated it; by fixing his attention on the beauty of the divine nature and seeking to take it on, good actions emerged from him naturally and inevitably. As the Quran says:

The most pious will be far removed from it [the punishment of Hell], he who giveth of his wealth to purify himself. And nobody possesseth, in the sight of such a person, any favor that might be bestowed on him as reward [for his generosity]. [His action is performed] seeking only the Countenance of his transcendent Lord, and he will be content (Al-Layl: 17-21).

The same principle appears in the description of the righteous, the abrar. Commentators on the Quran agree that the occasion of revelation, sabab al-nuzul, for the following verses was a time when Ali and his family went three days running without food. At the end of each day, just as they were about to break their fast, someone in greater need came and asked for what they had. The Quran relates this as follows: "They feed, out of love for God, the needy, the orphan and the prisoner, saying, 'We feed you only for the sake of God; we desire from you neither reward nor thanks'" (Al-Insan: 8-9).

In the very measure that a good action is performed for the sake of that absolute goodness which God is, the one performing it rests wholly content, untouched by the blame or praise of others, neither deflated when outward adversity thwarts his good actions nor inflated when they succeed. This understanding of the relation between action and intention illuminates the meaning of the Imam's saying that "their hearts are in the Gardens [of Paradise] while their bodies are at work." Here again a difference emerges between a secular ethic of justice, one devoid of all spiritual or transcendental points of reference, and a spiritual conception of justice. A secular ethic stands or falls by how far it succeeds outwardly in realizing its ideal of justice; a spiritual conception already sees its ideal fulfilled in the sincere intention to be just. Even so, the fulfillment of a spiritual ideal of justice requires an unyielding struggle to achieve it, for without that struggle the intention proves to lack sincerity. Without matching action, aspirations and intentions remain shallow and purely theoretical. To hold an intention with total sincerity requires diligent, ceaseless striving toward its realization. Thus, however formidable the obstacles, however compelling the arguments of political pragmatism, however thoroughly his strenuous efforts are undone by the unavoidable pressures of outward life, the just person never despairs. He never abandons the effort to achieve justice, precisely because achieving justice in this world is not the whole of his aim. He knows that the intention is already, in an invisible but spiritually palpable way, its own fulfillment; and he knows, with sober realism, that the outward realization of any intention depends on circumstances beyond his control and, ultimately, on the grace of God.

On the surface this may seem to excuse the failure to achieve justice in society, and so to offer a subterfuge by which actual injustice is legitimated through an appeal to good but thwarted intentions. The very opposite is in fact the case. Far from betraying any lack of resolve in one moved by a sacred conception of justice, such a person's efforts are, on the contrary, as ceaseless and unyielding as the eternal and unfailing reward in which he fervently believes. Those efforts are sustained, too, by an awareness of the subtle continuity between just intentions and the divine quality of justice, and through it the divine nature itself. An inalienable sense of the sacred sustains the just person in his pursuit of justice. The argument is not that all secular or atheistic conceptions of justice are without value; only that, set beside a sacred conception, they lack those immeasurable and inexhaustible spiritual resources that sincere faith nourishes. The theories that dominate the present age bear this out. Relativism holds that knowledge, truth, and morality are not absolute but exist only in relation to culture, society, or historical context. Nihilism rejects all religious and moral principle and holds that life is essentially meaningless. Utilitarianism counts an action right only insofar as it is useful or benefits the majority, reducing good and bad to a reckoning of gain and loss with no fundamental principle beneath them. And opportunism takes whatever advantage presents itself regardless of principle, in the manner of the manipulator who flatters, performs, and pretends, saying or doing whatever it takes to get what he wants. These are human constructions, and the world arrived at them by severing practice from principle. Whatever value may emerge from them is not rooted in the divine, so that even where they coincide with the values of faith they do so only by coincidence and remain, for all their machinery, without spirit — dead corpses. "With men this is impossible, but with God all things are possible," as Jesus says in the Gospel. Applied to the present context, that principle might be stated thus: when justice is conceived in isolation from spiritual principles, personal idealism lies acutely exposed to outward contingencies and is all too likely to disintegrate under the pressure of difficulty and disappointment; in a sacred conception of justice, by contrast, divine grace holds the incorruptible ideal of justice high above the frailties and upheavals to which both the human soul and human society are subject. The more one's ideals are rooted in spiritual knowledge, the less one is prone to disappointment and despair in the face of difficulty.

The importance of this ideal of justice is clearly visible in the Imam's own experience as caliph. An unbroken succession of bloody conflicts against foes who professed Islam prevented him, outwardly, from enacting many of the principles and policies that flowed from his conception of truth and justice. Yet none of this produced dejection, despair, or cynicism, nor did it lessen in the slightest his efforts to achieve justice wherever and whenever possible. The quality of his own administration shows as much, as does the steady stream of letters and counsels to his officers and governors — the letter to Malik among them. Such despair is almost inconceivable for one whose inner life is shaped by the conviction that "No act is negligible if it is accompanied by piety." For such a person, despair is excluded by piety in the deepest sense of the word — the permanent awareness of divine Reality; for this degree of piety is the makhraj, the "way out," from the darkness of all earthly injustice. As the Imam says, "He who has pious awe toward God, for him God establishes a way out of tribulation, and a light out of darkness (اعْلَمُوا أَنَّهُ مَنْ يَتَّقِ اللَّهَ يَجْعَلْ لَهُ مَخْرَجًا مِنَ الْفِتَنِ، وَنُورًا مِنَ الظُّلَمِ)."
