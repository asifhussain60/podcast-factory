# Justice for the Poor and the Sincerity of Intention

The Imam now turns Malik toward those most in need of justice, and he opens the passage with an exclamation, "Allah, Allah," to mark its urgency. The instruction concerns those with no resources whatsoever: the destitute, the crippled, orphans, the elderly, those who are most in need of justice from a governor and who must be treated in such a way that "God may excuse you on the day you meet Him." All of these, along with others who are in need but too proud to beg, are to be provided for by the governor, who ought to appoint an officer charged specifically with uncovering the needs of the most destitute and attending to them. The Imam then adds words that reach the heart of the matter: "This is onerous for the governors, and [the fulfilment of] all rights is onerous, but God makes it light for those who aspire to the Hereafter, who restrain their souls in patience, and trust in the truth of that which is promised them by God."

The spiritual dimension is precisely what makes an otherwise crushing ideal practicable. Divine assistance is assured for those whose attention is not confined to this world alone but whose aspirations extend beyond it to the Hereafter. Only when this world is seen through to the next does a fully just attitude toward this world become possible. Those whose talab, their aspiration, does not reach beyond this world are far more likely to take the easy path of neglecting the destitute, for in the horizontal calculus of political calculation the costs to a ruler of pursuing a policy of charity toward the poor may well outweigh any discernible benefit to himself. Here an ethics tied to an exclusively this-worldly assessment, to pragmatic politics, or to personal interest exposes its inherent flaws. But when the notion of right in relation to the poor is charged with firm faith in God's inexorable justice and infinite compassion, and suffused with aspiration for the Hereafter, an attitude of unflinching fidelity to the needs of the destitute is generated and continually renewed.

Such an attitude goes far beyond conventional notions of charity or generosity. A person who has realized it to perfection will not be susceptible to any contrary suggestion rooted in self-serving pragmatism or individualism, let alone in indifference or wilfulness. With this attitude firmly in place, the next quality the Imam mentions, patience, follows naturally. In the light of a concrete rather than merely notional presentiment of the absolute values at stake, patience can be extended indefinitely, no longer dependent on personal will alone but sustained by the empowering grace that flows from heartfelt faith. The Imam completes his point by noting that those with the right aspirations consequently trust in the truth of God's promise. If that promise, of His reward, His unfailing justice, His infinite compassion, is true, then the motivation behind the governor's effort to extend mercy to the poor and deal with them justly is immeasurably deepened. The task the Imam describes as onerous, the obligation to help the weak and helpless, those from whose assistance no political benefit may be derived, is transformed into an inescapable duty that follows directly from spiritual conviction. That conviction rests on the certitude that such generosity and compassion are not merely good, right, and proper, but that these virtues accord with the true and inalienable substance of ultimate reality. The moral implications of spiritual reality, and equally the spiritual repercussions of virtue, are hidden from worldly rulers by their covert prejudices at best and their overt vices at worst. Such rulers are also blind to the truth of the divine promise of ultimate felicity, and equally blind to the warnings of retribution for the oppressors, al-zalimun. Here the zalim may be understood as one who defies the divine commandments on justice and, more fundamentally, violates the quality of compassion that lies at the very heart of the divine nature.

These considerations open onto the crucial question of intention. Even if the just person's effort to live according to justice is frustrated by the unpredictabilities of worldly life, the right intentions motivating him, together with his awareness of the immutable divine qualities as models and principles guiding his endeavour, produce a real, inward "taste" of the grace that furnishes the spiritual foundation of his moral life. If God's promise is true, and one is absolutely certain of that truth, then the impact of that truth on the soul of the sincere believer transforms a personal ethic into an unconditional imperative and a desirable value into an incontrovertible reality. Morality is thus deepened by, and into, spirituality: an existential, all-encompassing mode of being within which the burden of individual duty is not only immeasurably lightened but transformed into an accompaniment of one's taste of spiritual reality. One then acts justly out of gratitude for what one has already received, the joy of spiritual certainty, and not only out of hope for something not yet attained.

The Qur'an speaks of the paradisal reward as something already granted to the true believer; for one whose certainty of Paradise is absolute, the future has already arrived: "We have given thee [the paradisal fountain of] al-Kawthar; so pray to thy Lord and sacrifice ..." (108: 1–2). Worshipping God as a consequence of the certainty of the beatitude to come, and which has thus, in one respect, already come, is well expressed in the following saying of the Imam:

Indeed there is a group who worship God out of desire [for something not yet attained]; and this is the worship of the merchants. And there is a group who worship God out of fear, and this is the worship of the slaves. And there is a group who worship God out of gratitude, and this is the worship of the free.

The just person is furthermore liberated from the material consequences of his actions, in proportion to the rectitude and sincerity of his intentions, since in the Islamic perspective actions are evaluated not by their consequences but by their intentions. One's intention is to be just: not for the sake of any earthly reward or tangible consequence in this world, but purely for the sake of justice itself. And this essence or principle of justice is in turn inseparable from the divine nature. For justice is at one with God not simply because whatever God commands is just; rather, God commands just acts precisely because they are just, and because justice is one with His own nature: "I bear witness that He is Justice and He acts justly." The point is worth pressing. Justice is not one attribute among others, something merely present in the divine nature; it is that nature. Were it only in His nature it would stand alongside His other qualities; but because it is His very essence, all His other qualities essentially flow from it, so that His mercy no less than His wrath, His creation no less than His destruction, all proceed from His justice. If God acts justly by virtue of His very nature, which is pure justice, then the spiritual person will act justly out of a desire to conform to God's nature, and not simply out of obedience to God's commands, and never out of desire for any earthly reward.

This can be seen in any single virtue. If one speaks the truth without grasping the reason for it, that truth, الحق, is the very nature of the Creator, and that our goal in this world is to take on the qualities of that Creator, then truthfulness soon becomes situational and self-serving, open to compromise whenever circumstances seem to warrant it: I speak the truth when it benefits me and lie when that benefits me. But once truth is seen as the nature of the divine, it becomes everlasting and eternal, and the consequences of speaking it cease to matter; one speaks it simply in emulation of الحق, the absolute truth. Virtue practised on an ad hoc basis is no virtue at all, merely a series of pragmatic adaptations to shifting circumstances. This is why the life of Ali (ع), when studied, reflects an unflinching adherence to the divine virtues: his was a life governed not by his نفس but entirely by the divine nature of Allah, his every celebrated action the fruit of an irresistible attraction toward the nature of الحق. His gaze was never fixed on the action itself but on the source of the principles generating it; by fixing his attention on the beauty of the divine nature and seeking to take it on, good actions emerged from him naturally and inevitably. As the Qur'an says:

The most pious will be far removed from it [the punishment of Hell], he who giveth of his wealth to purify himself. And nobody possesseth, in the sight of such a person, any favour that might be bestowed on him as reward [for his generosity]. [His action is performed] seeking only the Countenance of his transcendent Lord, and he will be content (92: 17–21).

The same principle is expressed in the description of the righteous, the abrar. Commentators on the Qur'an agree that the occasion for the revelation, sabab al-nuzul, of the following verses was when Ali (ع) and his family went three consecutive days without food. At the end of each day, just as they were about to break their fast, someone in greater need came and asked for what they had. The Qur'an relates this as follows: "They feed, out of love for God, the needy, the orphan and the prisoner, saying, 'We feed you only for the sake of God; we desire from you neither reward nor thanks'" (76: 8–9).

In the very measure that good action is performed for the sake of that absolute goodness which God is, the one performing it is imperturbably content, untouched by the blame or praise of others, neither deflated by outward adversity that thwarts his good actions nor inflated by their successful outcome. This understanding of the relation between action and intention illuminates the meaning of the Imam's saying that "their hearts are in the Gardens [of Paradise] while their bodies are at work." Here again there is a difference between a secular ethic of justice, one devoid of all spiritual or transcendental points of reference, and a spiritual conception of justice. A secular ethic stands or falls according to the degree of outward success in realising its ideal of justice; a spiritual conception already sees the fulfilment of its ideal in the sincere intention to be just. That said, the fulfilment of a spiritual ideal of justice essentially requires an unyielding struggle to achieve it, for without that struggle the intention is shown to lack sincerity. Without concordant action, aspirations and intentions remain shallow and purely theoretical. To hold an intention with total sincerity strictly requires diligent, ceaseless striving toward its realization. Thus, however formidable the obstacles, however compelling the arguments of political pragmatism, however thoroughly one's strenuous efforts are undone by the unavoidable exigencies of outward life, the just person never despairs; he never abandons the effort to achieve justice, precisely because achieving justice in this world is not the whole of his aim. He knows that the intention is already, in an invisible but spiritually palpable way, its own fulfilment; and he knows, with sober realism, that the outward realization of any intention depends on circumstances beyond his control and, ultimately, on the grace of God.

While this may appear on the surface to excuse the failure to achieve justice in society, and thus to be exploitable as a subterfuge by which actual injustice is legitimated by appeal to good but thwarted intentions, the very opposite is in fact the case. Far from implying any lack of resolve on the part of one motivated by a sacred conception of justice, such a person's efforts will on the contrary be as ceaseless and unyielding as the eternal and unfailing reward in which he fervently believes. Those efforts are also sustained by an awareness of the subtle continuity between just intentions and the divine quality of justice, and thereby the divine nature itself. An inalienable sense of the sacred sustains the just person in his pursuit of justice. The argument is not that all secular or atheistic conceptions of justice are without value; only that, compared with a sacred conception, they lack those immeasurable and inexhaustible spiritual resources that are nourished by sincere faith. The theories that dominate the present age bear this out. Relativism holds that knowledge, truth, and morality are not absolute but exist only in relation to culture, society, or historical context. Nihilism rejects all religious and moral principle and holds that life is essentially meaningless. Utilitarianism counts an action right only insofar as it is useful or benefits the majority, reducing good and bad to a calculus of gain and loss with no fundamental principle beneath them. And opportunism takes whatever advantage presents itself regardless of principle, in the manner of the manipulator who flatters, performs, and pretends, saying or doing whatever it takes to get what he wants. These are human constructions, and the world arrived at them by severing practice from principle. Whatever value may emerge from them is not rooted in the divine, so that even where they coincide with the values of faith they do so by coincidence and remain, for all their machinery, without spirit, dead corpses. "With men this is impossible, but with God all things are possible," as Jesus says in the Gospel. Translated into the present context, that principle might be stated thus: when justice is conceived in isolation from spiritual principles, personal idealism is acutely vulnerable to outward contingencies and all too likely to disintegrate under the pressure of difficulty and disappointment; in a sacred conception of justice, by contrast, divine grace upholds the incorruptible ideal of justice high above the frailties and vicissitudes to which both the human soul and human society are subject. To the extent that one's ideals are rooted in spiritual knowledge, one's susceptibility to disappointment and despair in the face of difficulty is diminished.

The importance of this ideal of justice is clearly visible in the Imam's own experience as caliph. A continuous succession of bloody conflicts against foes who professed Islam prevented him outwardly from enacting many of the principles and policies that flowed from his conception of truth and justice, yet none of this produced dejection, despair, or cynicism, nor did it diminish in the slightest his efforts to achieve justice wherever and whenever possible, as is amply demonstrated by the quality of his own administration and by the steady stream of letters and counsels to his officers and governors, the letter to Malik among them. Such despair is almost inconceivable for one whose inner life is shaped by the conviction that "No act is negligible if it is accompanied by piety." For such a person, despair is excluded by piety in the deepest sense of the word, that is, the permanent awareness of divine Reality; for this degree of piety is the makhraj, the "way out," from the darkness of all earthly injustice. As the Imam says, "He who has pious awe towards God, for him God establishes a way out of tribulation, and a light out of darkness (man yattiqi'Llah yaj'al lahu makhrajan min al-fitan wa nuran min al-zulam)."
