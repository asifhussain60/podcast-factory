# In the Prophet's Shadow

In relation to the Prophet, Ali (عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ) was both a living presence and a promise. The epigraph names it: "I am the city of knowledge and Ali is its gate; so whoever desires knowledge, let him enter the gate" (أَنَا مَدِينَةُ الْعِلْمِ وَعَلِيٌّ بَابُهَا، فَمَنْ أَرَادَ الْعِلْمَ فَلْيَأْتِ الْبَابَ).

To speak of Ali ibn Abi Talib — cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad, fourth caliph of Islam, and first in the line of Shii Imams — is to speak of the very heart of Islamic spiritual life. In this central figure of Islam's earliest days, both of its two great sources come together: the revelation of the Quran and the inspired Sunna of the Prophet. Sunna here does not mean simply copying the Prophet's outward actions — a narrow view that is all too common today — but rather the inner spiritual substance of prophetic perfection, the perfection to which the Quran itself points:

> وَإِنَّكَ لَعَلَىٰ خُلُقٍ عَظِيمٍۢ
>
> "Verily, thou art of a tremendous nature" (Al-Qalam: 4).

The Prophet's words and deeds give voice to that spiritual substance, yet they do not exhaust it. To take this substance inwardly into oneself, rather than merely copy its outward forms, is the aim of every Muslim drawn to the spiritual life. Between the believer's soul and the prophetic nature there is a deep kinship, a mysterious closeness that reaches across time and space, as the following verse expresses:

> ٱلنَّبِىُّ أَوْلَىٰ بِٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ مِنْ أَنفُسِهِمْ ۖ وَأَزْوَجُهُۥٓ أُمَّهَتُهُمْ ۗ وَأُو۟لُوا۟ ٱلْأَرْحَامِ بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلَىٰ بِبَعْضٍۢ فِى كِتَبِ ٱللَّهِ مِنَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُهَجِرِينَ إِلَّآ أَن تَفْعَلُوٓا۟ إِلَىٰٓ أَوْلِيَآئِكُم مَّعْرُوفًۭا ۚ كَانَ ذَلِكَ فِى ٱلْكِتَبِ مَسْطُورًۭا
>
> "The Prophet is closer to the believers than they are to themselves, and his wives are their mothers; and by the ordinance of God, blood relatives are more entitled to one another than other believers and emigrants, unless you would show kindness to your close associates. This is decreed in the Record" (Al-Ahzab: 6).

The Prophet thus shows outwardly the very perfection that the spiritually sensitive believer senses, in some measure, at the core of his or her own soul.

It is no surprise, then, to find the Prophet saying that Ali "is as my own soul":

> عليٌّ مِنِّي بِمَنْزِلَةِ نَفْسِي

This confirms not only the extraordinarily close bond between Ali and the Prophet, but also the kinship — and in the end the oneness — between every sanctified soul and the prophetic nature. More mysterious still, the Prophet said to Ali, "You are from me, and I am from you":

> أنْتَ مِنِّی وَ أَنَا مِنْکَ

As for how fully Ali took into himself the substance (مَوَادّ) of the Quran, there is this saying of the Prophet:

> إِنَّ عَلِيًّا مَعَ الْقُرْآنِ وَالْقُرْآنُ مَعَ عَلِيٍّ، لَنْ يَفْتَرِقَا حَتَّى يَرِدَا عَلَيَّ الْحَوْضَ
>
> "Ali is with the Quran and the Quran is with Ali. They will not separate from each other until they return to me at the [paradisal] pool (al-hawd)."

Ali thus took fully within himself both the spirit of the Quran and the soul of the Prophet; and it is exactly this taking-within of the two sources of the Islamic revelation that forms the spiritual path of Islam.

The chief way to study and reflect upon the spirituality of Ali is to engage with the body of teachings handed down under his name. Not every one of the thousands of sayings attributed to him can be assigned to him with confidence; yet the sheer size of this body of work shows that he must have given voice to a great many profound teachings. No other companion of the Prophet left anything close to it. The same holds for the sciences. His founding role in a whole range of them may be open to debate — among them jurisprudence (فِقْهٌ), theology (كَلَامٌ), Quranic exegesis (تَفْسِيرٌ), rhetoric (بَلَاغَةٌ), grammar (نَحْوٌ), and calligraphy (خَطٌّ), the mystical knowledge of Sufism, and such hidden sciences as numerology (جَفْرٌ) and alchemy (اَلْكِيمِيَاءُ) — but the later masters in every one of these fields look back to him as the first impulse behind their discipline, which itself testifies to how far and how deep the influence of both his formal teaching and his personal radiance reached.

Because the Islamic intellectual sciences as a whole are so closely bound to Ali, the Prophet's description of him as the "gate" to prophetic wisdom, quoted in the epigraph, reads in two ways at once: as an account of what Ali was to the Prophet in their own lifetime, and as a prophecy of the part he would play as those sciences later unfolded. Each of these sciences is understood as one more outward, formal expression of the inward, essential spirit of the Islamic revelation — a spirit that Ali drew together within himself and faithfully passed on. The main historical outlines of his life come first.

Ali was a "follower" of the Prophet in the deepest sense of that word. He lived at the Prophet's side, in his very shadow, and took in spiritually all that shone out from him. The closeness between them is caught in the words of one of the Imam's own sermons:

> لَمَّا كُنْتُ وَلِيدًا ضَمَّنِي إِلَى صَدْرِهِ، فَكُنْتُ أتَّبِعُهُ اتّبَاعَ الفَصِيلِ أُمَّهُ
>
> "When I was but a child he took me under his wing. I would follow him as a baby camel follows its mother."

> يَرْفَعُ لِي فِي كُلِّ يَوْمٍ عَلَمًا مِنْ أَخْلاقِهِ الْكَرِيمَةِ وَيَأمُرُنِي بِالاِقْتِدَاءِ بِهِ
>
> "Every day he would hold up for me a sign of his noble character and command me to follow it."

> وَكَانَ يَتَعَاهَدُ غَارَ حِرَاءٍ فِي كُلِّ سَنَةٍ، فَأَرَاهُ وَلَا يَرَاهُ غَيْرِي
>
> "Each year he would go into seclusion at the mountain of Hira. I saw him there, and no one else did."

> وَلَمْ يَجْمَعْ بَيْتٌ وَاحِدٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ فِي الإِسْلامِ غَيْرَ رَسُولِ اللهِ صَلَّى اللهُ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلَّمَ وَخَدِيجَةَ وَأَنَا ثَالِثُهُمَا
>
> "At that time no single household was gathered together in the religion of Islam, save that of the Messenger of God, Khadija, and myself as the third among them."

> أَرَى نُورَ الْوَحْيِ وَالرِّسَالَةِ، وَأَشُمُّ رِيحَ النُّبُوَّةِ
>
> "I saw the light of the revelation and the message, and I smelt the fragrance of prophecy..."

This sketch concerns Ali as the faithful follower who carried on the prophetic pattern. Its focus is the early period of his life, from his birth (c. 599 CE) to the death of the Prophet (11/632). Two later periods fall outside it: the second, from the Prophet's death to Ali's own accession to the caliphate (35/656); and the third, his brief caliphate, torn by tragic civil wars and ended by his assassination in 40/661. These periods raise questions tangled in historical and source-critical difficulty, and to enter those disputes would pull away from the main aim of this book — to present the spiritual ethos of this extraordinary figure in a way that shows how widely it speaks, both within Islam and beyond it. That ethos rises above the boundaries of formal religion, just as it rises above the political quarrels underlying the outward Shii-Sunni divergence — quarrels that only draw attention away from the essential spiritual message of Ali.

What this book brings forward instead is the uniting power of his spiritual teachings — teachings whose great importance and depth Muslims of every school affirm alike. What follows is a brief survey of that earlier period of Ali's life spent with the Prophet, along with the ways the Prophet described him in well-attested sayings; this is the necessary background for understanding his role as the gate to prophetic wisdom. The second part of this opening chapter then takes up some key principles of his spiritual ethos, with special attention to the place of the intellect within it.

By the account of all Shii authorities and of several Sunni sources, Ali held the singular distinction of being born inside the Kaba in Mecca. His mother was Fatima bt. Asad; his father, Abu Talib, son of 'Abd al-Muttalib, was a leading member of the Hashimite clan. Abu Talib had raised the young orphan Muhammad, son of his brother Abd Allah, and would later become his chief protector once Islam was preached openly in Mecca. Some years before the mission began, in a time of drought, Muhammad lightened Abu Talib's burden by taking his young son Ali — then about five years old — into his own household. From that time on, Ali was like a son to him, and the two were seldom apart.

Ali is held to be the first male to embrace Islam, though he was only nine or ten at the time. Abu Bakr was the first grown man to enter the new religion, and Khadija, the Prophet's wife, was the first woman. That the young boy answered the prophetic message at once, and held to it without wavering ever after, carries great weight. About three years later, when revelation instructed the Prophet to "warn thy nearest kin":

> وَأَنذِرْ عَشِيرَتَكَ ٱلْأَقْرَبِينَ

(Ash-Shuara: 214), he invited the leading members of his clan to a feast and then addressed them: "O sons of 'Abd al-Muttalib, I know of no Arab who hath come to his people with a nobler message than mine. I bring you the best of this world and the next. God hath commanded me to call you unto Him. Which of you, then, will help me in this, and be my brother, mine executor and my successor among you?" All remained silent, except for the youthful Ali, who spoke up: "O Prophet of God, I will be thy helper in this." The Prophet then placed his hand on Alis neck and said, "This is my brother, mine executor and my successor among you. Hearken unto him and obey him." Though the clan's leaders met this with mockery at the time, the declaration was taken far more seriously as the years passed and the extraordinary character, nobility, and intelligence of the Prophet's young cousin grew ever more dazzlingly plain.

This early period also holds the part Ali played in the migration (هِجْرَة) from Mecca to Medina (622 CE), the event that marks the start of the Muslim calendar. The Prophet's enemies had plotted to kill him; knowing this, Ali risked his own life by taking the Prophet's place, sleeping in his bed on the night he left. Once in Medina, the Prophet took Ali as his "brother" in the pact of brotherhood he made between the "emigrants" (الْمُهَاجِرُونَ) — the Muslims who had come with him on the hijra from Mecca — and the "helpers" (الْأَنْصَارُ), the Medinan tribes who had embraced the new faith.

During the Medinan years, Ali made his mark on the battlefield, as a scribe of the Quranic revelation as it came down, and as a leading companion of the Prophet. In the battles against the overwhelming forces of the Meccan Quraysh and their allies, his courage and heroism were second to none. He stood in the front rank of nearly every major engagement fought under the Prophet's banner and was often made standard-bearer. He was never beaten in the single-combat duel with which battles customarily opened; indeed, he was never beaten in any battle he fought at all. Both his unbreakable courage and his matchless skill as a warrior became legendary. It is said that at the battle of Uhud a heavenly voice was heard to proclaim, "There is no chivalric knight but Ali, no sword but Dhul-Faqar (لَا فَتَى إِلَّا عَلِيّ، وَلَا سَيْفَ إِلَّا ذُو الْفَقَارِ)," the last being the name of the sword the Prophet had given him. The battle that did most to fix Alis name as the foremost — indeed the unconquerable — warrior of Islam was Khaybar, in 7/629. The Muslims had been able to make no headway against the heavily fortified defenses of their foes, which seemed impossible to breach. The Prophet then declared that he would hand the army's banner to one who "loves God and His Messenger and is loved by God and His Messenger," and that through him victory would be granted. He sent for Ali, who was away on account of an ailment of the eyes. The Prophet healed him by placing saliva on his eyes, and Ali went on to lead the Muslims to victory. In the thick of the fighting, having lost his own shield, he is said to have torn a gate from its hinges and used it as a shield — a gate that, after the battle, eight men could barely lift.
