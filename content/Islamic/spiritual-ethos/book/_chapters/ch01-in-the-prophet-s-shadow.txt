# In the Prophet's Shadow

I am the city of knowledge and Ali (ع) is its gate; so whoever desires knowledge, let him enter the gate. (Ana madinatu'l-ilm wa 'Ali babuha; Fa-man arada'l-ilm fal-ya'ti'l-bab).

To speak of Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع), cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad, fourth caliph of Islam and first in the line of Shi'i Imams, is to speak of the quintessential spirituality of the Islamic tradition. In this seminal figure of nascent Islam one finds an integral expression of the two fundamental sources of Islamic spirituality: the Qur'anic revelation and the inspired Sunna of the Prophet. By Sunna is meant not simply the outward imitation of the Prophet's actions, a reductionism all too prevalent in our times, but rather the spiritual substance of prophetic perfection to which the Qur'an itself points:

وَإِنَّكَ لَعَلَىٰ خُلُقٍ عَظِيمٍۢ

'Verily, thou art of a tremendous nature' (68: 4).

The Prophet's words and deeds express that spiritual substance but do not exhaust it. To inwardly assimilate this substance, rather than merely imitate its outward forms, is the goal of every spiritually inclined Muslim. There is a profound affinity between the believer's soul and the prophetic nature, a mysterious proximity that transcends time and space, as expressed in the verse:

ٱلنَّبِىُّ أَوْلَىٰ بِٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ مِنْ أَنفُسِهِمْ ۖ وَأَزْوَجُهُۥٓ أُمَّهَتُهُمْ ۗ وَأُو۟لُوا۟ ٱلْأَرْحَامِ بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلَىٰ بِبَعْضٍۢ فِى كِتَبِ ٱللَّهِ مِنَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُهَجِرِينَ إِلَّآ أَن تَفْعَلُوٓا۟ إِلَىٰٓ أَوْلِيَآئِكُم مَّعْرُوفًۭا ۚ كَانَ ذَلِكَ فِى ٱلْكِتَبِ مَسْطُورًۭا

'The Prophet is closer to the believers than they are to themselves, and his wives are their mothers; and by the ordinance of God, blood relatives are more entitled to one another than other believers and emigrants, unless you would show kindness to your close associates; this is decreed in the Record' (33: 6).

The Prophet thus outwardly manifests the perfection that the spiritually sensitive believer intuits, to some degree or another, as defining the very essence of his or her own soul.

It is not surprising, therefore, to find the Prophet saying that Ali (ع) 'is as my own soul (ka-nafsi)', عليٌّ مِنِّي بِمَنْزِلَةِ نَفْسِي, affirming not only the extraordinarily close bond between Ali (ع) and himself, but also the affinity, and ultimately the identity, between every sanctified soul and the prophetic nature. Even more mysteriously, he said to Ali (ع), 'You are from me, and I am from you (anta minnī wa ana minka)', أنْتَ مِنِّی وَ أَنَا مِنْکَ. As for Ali (ع)'s assimilation of the substance (مواد) of the Qur'an, there is this prophetic saying:

إِنَّ عَلِيًّا مَعَ الْقُرْآنِ وَالْقُرْآنُ مَعَ عَلِيٍّ، لَنْ يَفْتَرِقَا حَتَّى يَرِدَا عَلَيَّ الْحَوْضَ

'Ali is with the Qur'an and the Qur'an is with Ali (ع). They will not separate from each other until they return to me at the [paradisal] pool (al-hawd).'

The spirit of the Qur'an and the soul of the Prophet were thus fully interiorized by Ali (ع), and it is precisely this interiorization of the twin sources of the Islamic revelation that constitutes the spiritual path of Islam.

The principal way of investigating and meditating upon the spirituality of Ali (ع) is to engage with the corpus of teachings attributed to him. Even if not every one of the thousands of sayings bearing his name can be confidently ascribed to him, the sheer magnitude of the corpus testifies that he must indeed have articulated a very large number of profound teachings. No other companion of the Prophet possesses anything approaching the body of work attributed to Ali (ع). Similarly, even if his foundational role in the development of a whole range of sciences remains debatable, sciences such as jurisprudence (fiqh), theology (kalam), Qur'anic exegesis (tafsir), rhetoric (balagha), grammar (nahw), and calligraphy (khatt), the mystical knowledge associated with Sufism, and such arcane sciences as numerology (jafr) and alchemy (al-kimiya), the fact that later authorities in all these fields regard him as the original impulse behind their disciplines speaks to the far-reaching and penetrating influence of both his formal teachings and his personal radiance.

What follows sketches the main historical outlines of his life. Ali (ع) was a 'follower' of the Prophet in the deepest sense of the word. He lived physically in the Prophet's shadow and absorbed spiritually everything that radiated from him. The intimacy of their relationship is captured in the words of one of the Imam's own sermons:

لَمَّا كُنْتُ وَلِيدًا ضَمَّنِي إِلَى صَدْرِهِ، فَكُنْتُ أتَّبِعُهُ اتّبَاعَ الفَصِيلِ أُمَّهُ

When I was but a child he took me under his wing. I would follow him [the Prophet] as a baby camel follows its mother.

يَرْفَعُ لِي فِي كُلِّ يَوْمٍ عَلَمًا مِنْ أَخْلاقِهِ الْكَرِيمَةِ وَيَأمُرُنِي بِالاِقْتِدَاءِ بِهِ

Every day he would hold up for me a sign of his noble character and command me to follow it.

وَكَانَ يَتَعَاهَدُ غَارَ حِرَاءٍ فِي كُلِّ سَنَةٍ، فَأَرَاهُ وَلَا يَرَاهُ غَيْرِي

Each year he would go into seclusion at [the mountain of] Hira'. I saw him there, and no one else did.

وَلَمْ يَجْمَعْ بَيْتٌ وَاحِدٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ فِي الإِسْلامِ غَيْرَ رَسُولِ اللهِ صَلَّى اللهُ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلَّمَ وَخَدِيجَةَ وَأَنَا ثَالِثُهُمَا

At that time no single household was gathered together in the religion of Islam, save that of the Messenger of God, Khadija, and myself as the third among them.

أَرَى نُورَ الْوَحْيِ وَالرِّسَالَةِ، وَأَشُمُّ رِيحَ النُّبُوَّةِ

I saw the light of the revelation and the message, and I smelt the fragrance of prophecy ...

It is Ali (ع) as the faithful follower and transmitter of the prophetic norm that this sketch addresses. The focus therefore falls on the early period of his life, from his birth (c. 599 CE) to the death of the Prophet (11/632). The second period, from the Prophet's death to Ali (ع)'s own assumption of the caliphate (35/656), and the third, consisting of his brief caliphate, dominated by tragic civil wars and ending with his assassination in 40/661, are not treated here. The issues those periods raise are fraught with historical and historiographical complexity, and engaging with such controversies would deflect from the primary aim of this book: to introduce the spiritual ethos of this extraordinary figure in a way that demonstrates its universal relevance, both within Islam and beyond it. For this ethos transcends formal religious boundaries just as it rises above the political disputes on which the outward Shi'i-Sunni divergence rests, disputes that serve only to draw attention away from the essential spiritual message of Ali (ع).

What is to be highlighted, by contrast, is the unifying force of his spiritual teachings, teachings whose immense importance and profundity all Muslims, whatever their school of thought, affirm. What follows briefly surveys the earlier period of Ali (ع)'s life spent with the Prophet, together with the ways in which the Prophet described Ali (ع) in well-attested sayings, since this constitutes the indispensable background against which his role as gate to prophetic wisdom must be understood. The second part of this introductory chapter then addresses some key principles of his spiritual ethos, with particular emphasis on the role of the intellect within it.

According to all Shi'i authorities and several Sunni sources, Ali (ع) had the unique distinction of being born inside the Ka'ba in Mecca. His mother was Fatima bt. Asad; his father, Abu Talib, son of 'Abd al-Muttalib, was a leading member of the Hashimite clan. Abu Talib had raised the young orphan Muhammad, son of his brother 'Abd Allah, and would later become his chief protector when Islam was openly preached in Mecca. Some years before the mission began, during a time of drought, Muhammad eased the burden on Abu Talib by taking his young son Ali (ع), then about five years old, into his own household. From that point on, Ali (ع) was like a son to him, and the two were rarely apart.

Ali (ع) is regarded as the first male to embrace Islam, though he was only nine or ten years old at the time. Abu Bakr was the first adult male to enter the new religion, and Khadija, the Prophet's wife, was the first woman. The immediacy of the young boy's response to the prophetic message and his unflinching loyalty thereafter are of great significance. About three years later, when the Prophet was instructed by revelation to 'warn thy nearest kin' (26: 214), he invited the leading members of his clan to a feast and then addressed them: 'O sons of 'Abd al-Muttalib, I know of no Arab who hath come to his people with a nobler message than mine. I bring you the best of this world and the next. God hath commanded me to call you unto Him. Which of you, then, will help me in this, and be my brother, mine executor and my successor amongst you?' All remained silent, except for the youthful Ali (ع), who spoke up: 'O Prophet of God, I will be thy helper in this.' The Prophet then placed his hand on Ali (ع)'s neck and said, 'This is my brother, mine executor and my successor amongst you. Hearken unto him and obey him.' Though the clan's leaders greeted this with mockery at the time, the declaration was taken far more seriously as the years passed and the extraordinary character, nobility, and intelligence of the Prophet's young cousin became ever more dazzlingly evident.

Also of importance in this early period is the role Ali (ع) played in the migration (hijra) from Mecca to Medina (622 CE), the event that marks the beginning of the Muslim calendar. The Prophet's enemies had plotted to assassinate him; knowing this, Ali (ع) risked his own life by pretending to be the Prophet, sleeping in his bed on the night of his departure. Once in Medina, the Prophet adopted Ali (ع) as his 'brother' in the pact of brotherhood he established between the 'emigrants' (al-muhajirun), the Muslims who had accompanied him on the hijra from Mecca, and the 'helpers' (al-ansar), the Medinan tribes who had embraced the new faith.

During the Medinan period, Ali (ع) distinguished himself on the battlefield, as a scribe of the unfolding Qur'anic revelation, and as a leading companion of the Prophet. His courage and heroism in battles against the overwhelmingly superior forces of the Meccan Quraysh and their allies were second to none. He stood at the forefront of nearly all the major engagements fought under the Prophet's banner and was frequently appointed standard-bearer. He was never defeated in any single-combat duel with which battles customarily began; indeed, he was never defeated in any battle he fought. Both his indomitable courage and his incomparable skill as a warrior became legendary. It is said that during the battle of Uhud, a heavenly voice was heard proclaiming, 'There is no chivalric knight but Ali (ع), no sword but Dhu'l-Faqar (la fata illa 'Alī, la sayf illa dhu'l-faqar)', the latter being the name of the sword the Prophet had given him. The most celebrated of all battles in establishing Ali (ع)'s reputation as the pre-eminent, indeed invincible, warrior of Islam was that of Khaybar in 7/629. The Muslims had been unable to make any headway against the heavily fortified, apparently impregnable defences of their opponents. The Prophet then declared that he would give the army's banner to one who 'loves God and His Messenger and is loved by God and His Messenger', through whom victory would be granted. He sent for Ali (ع), who was absent because of an affliction of the eyes. The Prophet healed him by placing saliva upon his eyes, and Ali (ع) went on to lead the Muslims to victory. In the thick of the battle, having lost his own shield, he is said to have wrenched a gate from its hinges and used it as a shield, a gate which, after the battle, eight men could barely lift.
