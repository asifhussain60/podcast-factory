# Dhikr: The Polish of the Heart

in the very substance of the intellect there stand principles by which all else is understood, principles rooted ultimately in the divine nature of the Creator. It is from this ground that the present chapter opens, turning from the foundation of remembrance to the spiritual realisation it is meant to bring about.

The subject here is the meaning of dhikru'Llah, 'the remembrance of God', one of the most fundamental principles in Islamic spirituality. It is approached through the sayings of the Imam, read essentially as commentaries on the Qur'anic notion of dhikru'Llah, and through the doctrinal formulations that have emerged from within the mystical traditions of Islam. To speak of Ali (ع) is to speak of the quintessential spirituality of the Islamic faith. His influence is felt wherever spirituality has flourished in Islam, whether in the more clearly defined disciplines of Sufism and Shi'i 'Irfan, or in the realms of futuwwa (spiritual chivalry), the craft guilds, music, poetry and the arts. As the 'gate' to the city of prophetic wisdom, his presence leaves its mark on all the noblest manifestations of the spirit of Islam.

Within the Sufi tradition in particular there is a uniquely organic connection with the Imam. Whether they be Shi'i or Sunni, Arab or Persian, Turk or Malay, Mauritanian or Bosnian, the Sufi orders are united in the figure of Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع), who stands at the summit of all the chains of affiliation (salasila, sing. silsila) by which the masters trace their spiritual lineage back to the Prophet. This unifying role is equally apparent in relation to the most significant internal division within Islam, that between Sunnism and Shi'ism. Beyond the vast common ground the two traditions share in essential beliefs and practices, they are brought together in a particularly direct and existential way by their shared orientation towards sanctity (walaya) and gnosis (ma'rifa) as the goal of the religious life. Seyyed Hossein Nasr writes: 'Islamic esotericism or gnosis crystallized into the form of Sufism in the Sunni world, while it poured into the whole structure of Shi'ism, especially during its early period.' The esoteric core of Islam thus expresses itself in Sunni Sufism and in Shi'ite 'Irfan with different 'accents', taking on different forms and expressions without this attunement to the essence of tawhid being fundamentally altered. Indeed, according to Sayyid Haydar Amuli, one of the great figures in whom Shi'ism and Sufism converge, the true Shi'i cannot but be a 'Sufi' if he plumbs the mystical depths of the Imams' teachings; conversely, the Sufi is a Shi'i if he comes to recognise that the most important roots of Sufism lie in the Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt, the first poles (aqtab, sing. qutb) of the spiritual tradition through whom the mystical blessings of the Prophet were transmitted in an initiatic context. For Amuli the differences of outward form between the two traditions are dissolved in the fire of walaya, sanctity, the true substance and goal of both alike. And for both, Ali (ع) is the wali Allah, the 'friend and saint of God', par excellence.

The manner in which Ali (ع) is regarded within the Sufi tradition is captured representatively by Ali Hujwiri, author of the first manual of Sufism written in Persian, the Kashf al-mahjub: 'Among them [the Sufis] is the son of Muhammad's uncle, he who drowned in the ocean of trying affliction, and was consumed in the fire of loving passion (ghariq-i bahr-i bala' wa hariq-i nar-i wala), the leader of the saints and the pure ones (muqtada-yi awliya' wa'l-asfiya'). In this Path he holds a place of tremendous honour and elevated degree. He had complete mastery over the precise expression of the principles of the supernal realities, such that Junayd said of him, "Ali is our master as regards principles and affliction (shaykhuna fi'l-usul wa'l-bala)." ... That is, Ali (ع) is the Imam of this Path in respect of both theoretical knowledge and practical application (andar 'ilm wa mu'amalat imam-i in tariqat), for the adepts of Sufism call the knowledge of this Path its "principles", while its practice is summed up in the endurance of affliction.'

The mention of the 'endurance of affliction' recalls the Imam's reference to the sages as those who 'befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged'. One reading of that elliptical phrase is that among the things the sages befriend and the ignorant flee is prayer, and more specifically the act of pure concentration on the Absolute, excluding all other thoughts; for this is a kind of death, the ego and all its preoccupations being as it were 'sacrificed' before the divine Reality on which concentration is exclusively fixed. The remembrance of God can be understood both as the consummation of that concentration and as the path leading to the summit of spiritual realisation: self-effacement before the sole reality of God.

The corpus of Ali (ع)'s teachings elucidates in a remarkable manner the notion, the principle and the practice of dhikru'Llah, a theme of central importance both in the Qur'an and in the Sunna of the Prophet. The Imam's sayings constitute a valuable commentary on those Qur'anic verses that speak of dhikr; and, given the centrality of the dhikr within Sufi practice and doctrine, they help explain why the Sufis regard the Imam as the first spiritual 'Pole' (qutb) of Sufism after the Prophet himself. The close relationship between remembrance and spiritual realisation is illumined by both the Imam's sayings and the expositions of the Sufi tradition, particularly those belonging to the school of Ibn al-'Arabi, known in the tradition as al-Shaykh al-akbar, 'the greatest Master', on account of his towering influence on subsequent Sufi thought. These expositions can be seen as doctrinal reverberations of the dense, often elliptical pronouncements of the Imam. Taken together, the Imam's sayings and the Sufis' elaborations bring key aspects of Islamic spirituality into sharp focus.

The term dhikr must first be considered, and with it the reason for its prominence in Islamic devotional practice. The term is extremely rich, and no single English word can do justice to its range of meanings. It encompasses remembrance, recollection, invocation, repetition, incantation, recitation; and also memory, mention, telling, relating a story, praise, glorification, celebration, fame, eminence, honour, exhortation and warning; as well as denoting the Qur'an itself, or any book of revelation or religion. The definition that comes closest to the spiritual principle expressed by the term is that given by al-Raghib al-Isfahani in his Qur'anic dictionary. He writes that dhikr is that by means of which 'man preserves that knowledge (ma'rifa) which he has attained, so it is akin to memory (hifz), except that memory is spoken of in respect of its function of acquisition (ihraz), whereas dhikr is spoken of in respect of its function of rendering present (istihdar). Dhikr is also used to refer to the presence of a thing in one's consciousness (lit. "heart") or in one's speech. Thus, it is said that there are two types of dhikr: dhikr of the heart and dhikr of the tongue, and each comprises two further kinds: a dhikr which follows forgetfulness, and a dhikr which does not follow forgetfulness but which, on the contrary, expresses a continuous remembering.'

Much in this definition resonates with the Imam's perspective. What deserves emphasis is that when the thing remembered, or 'rendered present' to consciousness, is God, the importance of dhikru'Llah as a principle becomes self-evident. Dhikru'Llah is to be understood, first and foremost, as a principle of awareness, an in-depth assimilation of the divine presence; but it must also be understood as an action, a practice or series of practices, and thus as a spiritual discipline. Dhikru'Llah can therefore be grasped both as the end and as a means to that end, both as spiritual awareness and as the methodic practice aimed at cultivating that awareness. It is within the Sufi tradition that this relationship between the practice of the dhikr and the principle of consciousness it intends is most fully elaborated. There the dhikr is presented both as a contemplative discipline centring on the methodic invocation or repetition of a Name of God, and as the cognitive consequence and mystical fruit of that discipline.

The dhikr is thus the spiritual method par excellence of the Sufis, a discipline that contributes to the realisation, within the heart, of that which is present to the mind, thereby transforming an abstract, notional grasp of certain ideas into a concrete perception of spiritual realities. Insofar as dhikru'Llah is conceived as the permanent awareness of the divine Reality, it can be presented as the very quintessence of religion as such, being that which 'once again binds' (re-ligare) the human being to God, and thus as the raison d'être of all religious rites and practices. If prayer constitutes the core of religious practice, the dhikru'Llah is, as the Qur'an states simply, akbar, that is, 'greater' or 'greatest':

ٱتْلُ مَآ أُوحِىَ إِلَيْكَ مِنَ ٱلْكِتَبِ وَأَقِمِ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ ۖ إِنَّ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ تَنْهَىٰ عَنِ ٱلْفَحْشَآءِ وَٱلْمُنكَرِ ۗ وَلَذِكْرُ ٱللَّهِ أَكْبَرُ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ مَا تَصْنَعُونَ

'Truly, prayer keepeth [one] away from lewdness and iniquity, and the remembrance of God is greater' (29: 45).

Numerous sayings of the Prophet attest to the pre-eminence of the remembrance of God. It is related that the Prophet asked his companions: 'Shall I not tell you about the best and purest of your works for your Lord, and the most exalted of them in your ranks, and the work that is better for you than giving silver and gold, and better for you than encountering your enemy, with you striking their necks and them striking your necks?' Those he addressed replied: 'What is that, O emissary of God?' He said, 'The perpetual invocation of God, exalted and glorious (dhikru'Llah 'azza wa jalla da'iman).'

Ali (ع) affirms likewise: 'Perpetuate the dhikr, for truly it illumines the heart, and it is the most excellent form of worship (huwa afdal al-'ibada).' The performance of dhikr receives much emphasis in the Shi'i tradition, which records numerous sayings of the early Imams on the virtues of invoking the Names of God and various sacred phrases. Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq described his followers specifically in terms of dhikr: 'Our Shi'a are those who, when alone (idha khalau), invoke and remember God much.' The khalwa, the spiritual retreat in which one seeks to be 'alone' with God, is described by 'Allama Tabataba'i, one of the most important masters of Shi'i gnosis in the twentieth century: 'As to the particular spiritual retreat (khalwat-i khass) it is distancing oneself from people ... it is especially required during certain types of verbal invocations (adhkar-i kalamiyya) ... and is considered essential by the masters of the path.'

Within the Sufi tradition, the following statement by Shaykh Ahmad al-'Alawi on the dhikr may be taken as entirely representative:

Remembrance is the mightiest rule of the religion ... The law was not enjoined upon us, neither were the rites of worship ordained but for the sake of establishing the remembrance of God ... In a word, our performance of the rites of worship is considered strong or weak according to the degree of our remembrance while performing them.

Al-Ghazali raises the question of why the dhikr should be the most meritorious practice when it is so simple to perform compared to all the other acts of worship, which demand so much effort. His answer is that a full understanding of this superiority can only be reached through the 'science of unveiling' ('ilm al-mukashafa). What can be said from the standpoint of the practical religious sciences ('ilm al-mu'amala) is that the most efficacious dhikr is that which is performed continuously with the presence of the heart (hudur al-qalb). This is the aim of the dhikr, and it is this which 'takes precedence over all acts of worship; nay, it is through this that the other acts of worship are dignified; this is the ultimate fruit of the practical acts of worship.' The principle of dhikru'Llah thus relates to the highest levels of religious practice, contemplative discipline and spiritual realisation, and so to the very heart of Islamic spirituality. Despite all their variety across time and place, their divergent perspectives and emphases, the Sufis are unanimous on the centrality of dhikru'Llah to their way. And despite all their differences of name, orientation, school of thought or mode of practice, the Sufi orders are united in the pivotal figure of Ali (ع) as the one who connects all the later masters of the spiritual path to the Prophet himself. This convergence of the Imam and the dhikru'Llah is far from coincidental. The Imam's elucidation of the meaning of the dhikr, together with his actual initiation of disciples into its methodic practice, constitutes a key aspect of his role as spiritual guide, the wali Allah, or the Pole (qutb) of his age, and is therefore central to his esoteric function within the spiritual tradition of Islam, in both its Sunni and Shi'i manifestations.

As regards this esoteric function, very little historically firm information survives about the Imam's initiatic role in this domain, that is, his actual initiation of disciples into the practice of invocation (known in the tradition as talqin), just as little survives about his own initiation into the esoteric practice by the Prophet. This is hardly surprising, given that the practice is, precisely, esoteric; it is not enjoined upon all as an obligatory rite. Rather, within both Sufism and Shi'i 'Irfan, the invocation is bestowed upon those with a particular vocation, those who have received a 'calling' to practise something beyond the obligatory rites binding upon all. Nonetheless, there are historical accounts that provide prima facie evidence of the Imam's role in transmitting the dhikr. The individuals described in the earliest sources as being devoted to the dhikr as a particular practice were known as close associates, if not disciples, of the Imam. His role in this initiatic domain is stressed in the orally transmitted sources of the Sufi way, and expressed in several written sources also. These refer to reports of the transmission of the dhikr from the Prophet to the Imam and from him to several other disciples who went on to initiate others into what later came to be called the way of tasawwuf.

Despite the scarcity of historically sound material on the earliest usage and transmission of dhikru'Llah, there remains the extremely important literary data provided by the sayings recorded in the Nahj al-balagha and the Ghurar al-hikam, as well as in the supplications attributed to the Imam. Taken together, these sayings constitute a homogeneous corpus, a distinct body of doctrine that furnishes a rich source of philosophical reflection, spiritual meditation and metaphysical insight into the nature and meaning of this practice, which occupies so central a place in the mystical tradition of Islam.

These sayings shed light not only on the meaning of the practice of dhikr but also on the philosophy and epistemology underlying it; and they offer precious allusions to the fruits of its practice, its consummation in spiritual realisation. The Imam's sayings, on the one hand, constitute an important source of commentary on the Qur'anic verses relating to this theme; and, on the other, present a clear foreshadowing of the more elaborate doctrines of the dhikr found in the later Sufi and 'Irfani traditions. They thus serve as a bridge connecting what came before the Imam, prophetic revelation, with what came after him, the form of mystical elaboration. The texts and manuals of the mystical tradition seem to make explicit what was more often than not implicit in the Imam's sayings. The mystics elaborated on esoteric truths that the Imam had conveyed in sayings that were concise, often paradoxical and elliptical, but always intellectually challenging and spiritually fruitful. Reflection on these sayings, read in the context of the Qur'an and the Sunna they illuminate, and in light of the later Sufi doctrine they anticipate, can bring one closer to a proper understanding of the role of dhikru'Llah in the practical, operative and visionary dimensions of Islamic spirituality.

Reflection on those sayings finds its most fitting starting point in the Imam's comment on the dhikr in sermon 213 of the Nahj al-balagha. There the Imam first quotes words from verse 37 of Sura 24 of the Qur'an, entitled al-Nur ('The Light'), in which the invocation of God's Name is mentioned, and then comments on them. The words in question are these: 'men whom neither trade nor merchandise diverts from the remembrance of God'. This fragment of the verse is organically connected with the preceding 'verse of light', the ayat al-nur so rich in mystical symbolism, and so the Imam's comment takes on a considerably deeper resonance. The Imam clearly knew that his recitation of the words from verse 37 would call to mind in his listeners the verses that preceded them; his choosing not to quote the verse of light in full only heightens the rhetorical effect of the ellipsis, such hints, implications, evocations and allusions being characteristic of Arabic balagha. The context of the Imam's words is therefore best held alongside the verse of light in full:

God is the light of the heavens and the earth. A similitude of His light is a niche wherein is a lamp; the lamp is enclosed in a glass; the glass is as it were a shining star. [The lamp] is lit [by the oil of] a blessed olive tree, neither of the East nor of the West. The oil well-nigh shineth forth, though fire touch it not. Light upon light! God guideth to His light whom He will; and God striketh similitudes for mankind; and God knoweth all things (24: 35).

After this verse come the following two:

In houses which God has allowed to be elevated, and that His Name be remembered therein, He is glorified in the mornings and in the evenings by men whom neither trade nor merchandise diverteth from the remembrance of God, and from establishing the prayer, and from the bestowing of alms ... (24: 36-7).

After reciting the words 'men whom neither trade nor merchandise diverteth from the remembrance of God', the Imam begins his sermon:

Truly, God has made the remembrance (al-dhikr) a polish for the hearts, by which they hear after being deaf, and see after being blind and yield after being resistant ... There have always been slaves of God ... with whom He held intimate discourse through their thoughts and spoke with them through the essence of their intellects. They diffused illumination through the awakened light in their hearing and their seeing and their hearts, calling unto the remembrance of the days of God ... Indeed, there is a special group (ahl) who belong to the dhikr; they have adopted it in place of the world, such that 'neither trade nor merchandise' distracts them from it. They spend the days of their life in it ... It is as though they had left this world for the Hereafter, and they are there, witnessing what is beyond this world ...

The weight of this passage lies in the Imam's description of the dhikr as a 'polish for hearts' (jila 'an li'l-qulub), set beside the reference to the 'awakened light' in the hearing, sight and hearts of those who have applied this polish to their hearts. The connection between the illumination of the heart and the verse of light is clear. The latent light of the heart is disclosed by the remembrance of God; the light within shines together with the light from on high: 'light upon light'. Once the heart is illumined, all the other faculties share in the luminosity generated by the invocation, and so there is, again, further 'light upon light'. The very word given for 'polish', jila', can itself be read as an implicit commentary on the light verse, for it derives from the verb jala, whose primary meaning is 'to become clear', exposed to view, disclosed, brought to light. The Qur'an refers to 'the night when it enshroudeth in darkness, and the day when it shineth forth in brightness (idha tajalla)' (92: 1-2). Likewise: 'By the day, as it bringeth to light [the sun] (idha jallahā)' (91: 3). The consciousness lying hidden within the heart is 'brought to light' through the effect of the polishing wrought by the remembrance of God.

The concern here is the clarifying of the organ of consciousness, the spiritual 'heart', both enabling it to function as a mirror that reflects truths from above and revealing its own hidden depths, thereby disclosing truths from within: those concealed contents of consciousness the Imam calls the 'buried treasures of the intellect' (dafa'in al-uqul). The dhikr is presented as a means of purifying this spiritual mode of perception, the 'heart', which cannot be simply identified with the rational faculty, and still less with the organs of sensory perception, as discussed in Chapter 1.

The Qur'an points to the deeper significance of the heart and its distinctive mode of 'vision': 'Have they not travelled in the land, that they might have hearts with which to comprehend, and ears with which to hear? Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but blind are the hearts that are in the breasts' (22: 46). The outward sight of the eye reaches only the surface of things, whereas the vision proper to the heart is an insight that penetrates beneath that surface to the reality concealed there. And again: 'They have hearts with which they understand not, eyes with which they see not, ears with which they hear not. They are like cattle, nay, even further astray. They are the heedless (al-ghafilun)' (7: 179). The reference at the end of verse 179 to the 'heedless', the ghafilun, returns to the dhikr, for the ghafil is the very opposite of the dhakir, the 'rememberer'; and the verse that immediately follows constitutes one of the most important 'methodic' foundations of the mystical practice of dhikr: 'Unto God belong the most beautiful Names, so call Him by them. And leave behind those who desecrate His Names; they will be requited for what they do' (7: 180). The relationship between remembrance and the heart is affirmed plainly in these verses: '... Those who believe and whose hearts are at peace in the remembrance of God, is it not in the remembrance of God that hearts are at peace?' (13: 28); 'Truly, therein is a reminder (dhikra) for him who hath a heart' (50: 37); 'Those are true believers whose hearts quake with awe when God is invoked' (8: 2); 'Is he whose breast God hath opened up to submission (islam), such that he followeth a light from his Lord [like he who remains closed to submission and in darkness]? So woe be to those whose hearts are hardened against the remembrance of God, they are in plain error' (39: 22).

By contrast, the verse that follows refers to hearts that 'soften' to dhikru'Llah: 'God hath revealed the most beautiful saying, a Book [containing] similitudes and paired [threats and promises] at which the skins of those who fear their Lord quiver; then their skins and their hearts soften to the remembrance of God' (39: 23).

The Imam's comment on verse 37 of the 'Light' chapter should therefore be understood as shedding light on all those Qur'anic verses that refer to the 'understanding' or spiritual vision to which the heart has access in principle, and that allude to dhikru'Llah as the means of translating this potentiality into actuality. The heart needs to be 'polished' by the invocation of God. The Imam's comment echoes and reinforces a saying of the Prophet frequently quoted in Sufi circles: 'For everything there is a polish (siqala), and the polish of the hearts is the dhikru'Llah.'

What is clearly being referred to here is an actual discipline, a practice, an action, or a regular series of actions, without this in any way excluding the meaning of dhikr as awareness or as a state of recollectedness in the divine presence, a disposition of soul shaped by the consciousness that 'if you cannot see Him, He sees you'. The 'polishing' in question can be understood both as the work of purifying thought through reflection and meditation, and as a regular methodic practice, whether this takes the form of glorification (tasbih), magnification (tamjid), praise (tahmid), sanctification (taqdis), the declaration of divine unity (tahlil), or the dhikr in the strict sense, the invocation of God's Names, by means of which the 'rust' of forgetfulness is removed. Ali (ع) also refers in his comment to a particular group who 'belong' to the dhikr, calling them an ahl. The Prophet likewise spoke of the ahl al-dhikr: 'God has angels who rove the pathways, seeking out the ahl al-dhikr, and when they find a group of people (qawm) invoking God, they call out to one another, "Come to that which you desire!"'

Later in time, one of the greatest members of the qawm, one of the names by which the Sufis refer to themselves, gives poetic expression to the image of the heart polished by the dhikr. It is the custom of kings, Rumi tells in the Mathnawi, to place the Sufis right before them: for they are a mirror for the soul, and better than a mirror, breasts polished by invocation and meditation, such that the mirror of the heart receives the pure, virginal image.

Another aspect of the heart's polishing deserves reflection, this time centred on the nature of the 'vision' experienced by the heart that is purified by the dhikr and vivified by faith. In the Imam's perspective, this vision is divinely inspired; it is nothing less than the spiritual vision of God already referred to: 'Eyes see Him not through sight's observation, but hearts see Him through the verities of faith.'

In another saying, the Imam refers to 'pure hearts' as the 'places' from which God can be seen, 'so whoever purifies his heart sees God'. The connection between the purification of the heart and the remembrance of God is confirmed in this saying: 'Where are they whose actions are accomplished purely for God, and who purify their hearts [so that they become] places for the remembrance of God?'

Taken together, these sayings of the Imam on the impact of the dhikr upon the heart can be read as a commentary on the important hadith qudsi cited in Chapter 2: 'My slave never ceases to draw near to Me through supererogatory acts (nawafil) until I love him. And when I love him, I am his hearing by which he hears, his sight by which he sees, his hand by which he grasps, and his foot by which he walks.' The Sufis regard this 'divine utterance' as defining a foundational principle of their metaphysical doctrine while simultaneously pointing to the goal of their mystical practice.

A mutual confirmation stands between the Imam's saying and the hadith qudsi. Through the dhikr, the supererogatory act par excellence, the individual's perceptions and actions are mysteriously assimilated to the divine Reality, and the result is that the heart attains spiritual vision. But then the heart, as the centre of consciousness, can no longer be regarded in its exclusively relative, human dimension. Its inmost essence, or secret (sirr), pertains in truth to the divine, and it is this alone that can see as God sees and, ultimately, 'see' God Himself. As the Sufi master Abu Talib al-Makki wrote, 'None sees Him to whom nothing is similar but Him to whom nothing is similar.' If it is true that God enters into the human acts of perception proper to the outward senses, then a fortiori the supreme act of spiritual awareness, the vision of God, is in reality not a vision of God by man but a vision of God by God, effected through rather than by the individual's 'heart'. The Imam says, 'Know God through God (i'rafu'Llah bi'Llah).' In the final analysis, then, it is God who both 'sees' and is 'seen'. According to a typical Sufi reading of the Qur'anic verse 'by a witnesser and that which is witnessed' (85: 3), it is God Himself who is both the 'witnesser' (shahid) and the 'witnessed' (mashhud). Al-Kashani, for example, writes in his esoteric exegesis, until quite recently wrongly attributed to Ibn al-'Arabi, that the human witness is effaced in his witnessing, so that it is God who witnesses Himself through the individual whose separative consciousness is extinguished, such that 'He is the identity of the witness, there being no difference between the two except in terms of notional expression.'

This anticipates the final section, where the ultimate fruits of invocation will be explored in terms of spiritual realization through self-effacement. For now, the focus remains on a more relative aspect of the heart's vision of God, that whereby the reality of God is rendered clear and present to the heart by means of the 'verities of faith'. The agent here is the inner dimension of human consciousness, and the object 'perceived' is the divine Reality. In other words, profound faith reveals the objective presence of God, a presence that is ubiquitous and inalienable, constituting the ontological reality of all existing things. As the Imam's famous Du'a' Kumayl expresses it, noted earlier: 'By Thy tremendousness ('azmatika) which has filled all things ... by Thy Names which have filled the foundations of all things, by Thy knowledge which encompasses all things, by the light of Thy face, through which all things are illumined ...' (emphasis added).

Nothing can exist without the 'foundation', the ontological infrastructure, as it were, that the Name, or combination of Names, of God furnishes for it. What constitutes the true being and the positive qualities of each manifested thing is therefore, ultimately, a divine Name or combination of Names. To see 'things' correctly means seeing the divine attributes manifested by and through them, and thus perceiving them as existential reflections of principial realities, as so many tajalliyyat, or theophanies, self-disclosures of the divine Reality. The 'heart' purified by invocation comes to see God everywhere.

This vision of the divine in all things derives from two principles affirmed in the Qur'an: the first concerns the divine 'object', that is, the objective, metaphysical nature of reality, and the second concerns the human subject and the mystical assimilation of that reality. On the one hand, nothing that exists, even the most outward aspects of material existence, can be excluded from the divine Reality, for 'He is the First and the Last, and the Outward and the Inward' (57: 3), and 'Wherever ye turn there is the Face of God' (2: 115); and on the other hand, 'We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and in their own souls, until it be clear to them that He is the Real' (41: 53). Everything around us thus provides a sign pointing back to its Creator.

The heart polished and purified by the dhikr can decipher these signs and thereby 'see' God everywhere and in everything; yet the possibility of such a vision in no way implies a reduction of the divine Reality to the level of sensible phenomena. As noted earlier, the first sermon of the Nahj al-balagha proclaims that 'God is with every thing, but not through association; and other than every thing, but not through separation.' This 'withness' (ma'iyya) of God does not imply any relativization of God, any association or conjoining (muqarana) of the divine Reality with created things, or any diminution of divine transcendence. The interplay of ideas here can be taken as a commentary both on the Qur'anic verses that proclaim divine immanence, the presence of God to and within all things, and on those that stress divine transcendence, surpassing and incomparable to all things. God may be 'absolutely' close to us, yet we are infinitely far from Him. On the one hand, 'We are closer to man than the neck-artery' (50: 16), He is as near to us as our own jugular vein, and on the other, '... there is none comparable unto Him' (112: 4).

Nothing that exists can be altogether separate from the all-encompassing reality of God, and yet that reality has no common measure with anything that exists. His oneness both includes and excludes all things; hence the affirmation of God's immanence within the world, His being 'with every thing', does not imply any diminution of His transcendence, and conversely, the affirmation of God's transcendence above the world, His being 'other than every thing', does not imply His absence from the world. The oneness of God implies all of this, just as it negates any merely numerical oneness, as was briefly discussed in the first chapter: in the Imam's perspective, God is One not in the sense of a countable 'one', a unit or entity among other units, but in the sense that His oneness admits no 'other'. In his words, 'That which has no second does not enter into the category of numbers.'

This can be read as an esoteric commentary on the Qur'anic verse "Say: He, God is One" (112:1). The opposite of this divine oneness, the multiplicity of gods implied in the error of shirk, is refined by this conception and transformed into a statement about being rather than merely theology. The act of shirk, of associating "partners" with God, is thereby carried to a higher register and shown to imply not simply the affirmation of false deities but the attribution to God of a merely numerical oneness. To reduce His oneness to number is to confine it within the wholly relative category of determinate entities. Each such entity is a unit, and from this standpoint God would be no more than one among other similar "beings." But the Imam insists that God "has no second"; there is no other "being" in being but Him. To call Him "one" in the ordinary sense is itself imprecise, for it implies that there could be a second in sequence. He is better described as unique. No entity or unit that apparently exists can, in reality, be compared with His being, which is one in an infinitely all-encompassing sense and unique in an absolutely exclusive sense: a oneness that is inclusive of all reality and exclusive of all alterity. This radical notion of a oneness that absolutely excludes the possibility of any "otherness" in Being implies that created entities are so many false gods, to the precise extent that their apparent "oneness" in existence is compared with God's true oneness, or rather with God's "one-and-onliness."

In another sermon, Ali (ع) addresses the aspect of divine immanence, focusing this time on the kind of awareness the human intellect can have of the divine Reality, which returns to the question of what vision of God is possible for the human being:

"Praise be to God who is present in the hidden inwardness of things, and whose being is indicated by the signs of manifest things. The eye of the onlooker cannot behold Him, so the eye of one who does not see Him cannot deny His reality, nor can the heart of one who affirms His reality see Him. He is utmost in elevation, so no thing is more elevated than He; but He is also close in His nearness, so no thing is closer than He ... God has not made the intellects capable of defining His qualities, but He has also not veiled the intellects from essential knowledge of Him."

This passage tells us, on the one hand, that human intellects cannot define and still less can know the divine attributes; and on the other, that "essential knowledge" of Him is attainable. The apparent contradiction resolves in another key dimension of dhikr, that of recollection, remembrance, recognition. One is reconstituting, re-collecting, fragments of a knowledge that already exists but has been forgotten. One recognizes what one has always known, and it is precisely for this reason that one is able to know it once again. This does not mean that the knowledge in question is composed of parts to be reassembled, which would be empirical knowledge. The remembrance here is more like an awakening, an illumination that dawns as the clouds of congenital forgetfulness are dispelled.

This approach to knowledge through remembrance recurs throughout the Imam's sayings. In Sermon 1 of the Nahj, for instance, appears the phrase, "Foremost in religion is knowledge of Him (awwal al-din ma'rifatuhu)." The word awwal literally means "the first" and can be understood as the starting-point, the foundation, and hence the foremost element of religion. The word ma'rifa means not merely knowledge but also carries the sense of "recognition." The implication is clearly that the basis of all religious orientation is man's pre-existing knowledge of the divine Reality. One re-cognizes what one has always known at the deepest level of consciousness; one ac-knowledges what one already knows but which had apparently been forgotten.

Further on in the same sermon, God sent to mankind "His messengers, dispatching prophets in succession, in order to claim from them [His creatures] the fulfilment of the covenant of His creation; to remind them of His forgotten graces; to remonstrate with them through communication [of His revelation]; to unearth for them the buried treasures of the intellects ..." (dafa'in al-'uqul).

The "buried treasures" can be identified with the knowledge of divine realities ingrained in man's deepest nature, those realities to which humanity was made to bear witness at the dawn of creation: "And when thy Lord brought forth from the children of Adam, from their loins, their seed, and made them testify of themselves, 'Am I not your Lord?' They said, 'Yea, we testify.' [This was] lest ye say on the Day of Resurrection, 'Truly, of this we were unaware'" (7:172).

These divine realities can also be understood in reference to the divine spirit breathed into man at the creation of the human soul: "Then He fashioned [man] and breathed into him of His Spirit" (32:9); to the "names" taught by God to Adam: "And He taught Adam the names, all of them ..." (2:31); and to the fitra, the primordial nature of man, patterned upon the divine nature: "... the nature of God (fitrat Allah), that according to which He created man" (30:30). The primordial nature of the human soul can thus be seen to comprise the very element, knowledge of God, to which all of humanity is made to bear witness in the pact (mithaq) mentioned in the verse above. This identification between the fitra and the knowledge of God is made explicit by Imam Muhammad al-Baqir, in a saying quoted by al-Khu'i as a commentary on that sentence of the first sermon. Al-Baqir was asked about the meaning of the words in the Qur'an, "the nature of God, that according to which He created man." He answered, "He created them [such that their primordial nature was] in conformity with tawhid, at the covenant regarding knowledge of Him, [when they testified] that He is indeed their Lord."

Knowledge of God, then, essentially defines human nature; it is for this reason that the human intellect cannot be veiled, as the Imam said, from "essential knowledge of God." Yet even though this knowledge is ingrained in human nature, forgetfulness (ghafla) has become man's second nature. In Qur'anic terms, the very passage of time smothers and thereby hardens the heart: "Hath not the moment come for the believers, that their hearts be humbled to the remembrance of God, and to what hath been revealed of the truth; and that they not become as those who were given the Book aforetime. Then the term was prolonged for them, so their hearts were hardened; and many of them are evil-doers" (57:16).

The verse speaks both of this hardening of the heart over time, which can be identified with the ghafla of second nature, and of its opposite, the heart's being present, here and now, to the dhikru'Llah. To remedy forgetfulness, more is needed than a simple factual reminder, some item of information, some element of formal, conceptual, discursive knowledge. No amount of empirical data, coming exclusively from without, can bring man to knowledge of God. Rather, it is integral remembrance, spiritual awareness welling up from within and crystallizing upon contact with revelation from without, that renders the innate knowledge of God present once again to heart and mind, that knowledge which is "buried" deep in the intellect. There is no common measure between this consciousness of God and ordinary empirical knowledge of phenomena. The incommensurability between the two kinds of knowledge is expressed by the Imam in sayings such as, "Praise be to God who is well-known (ma'ruf) without being seen." The paradox is sharpened by a further formulation of the same principle: "By things their Creator manifests Himself to the intellects; and by things He is guarded from the sight of the eyes." This opens onto a complex theme in the Imam's perspective, the nature of the "veil" constituted by created phenomena, and how it is to be understood in the light of the infinite and inalienable reality of God. On the one hand, all created phenomena are a veil, in that they prevent the divine Essence from being seen by outward vision; on the other hand, all phenomena cannot but manifest the divine, bearing witness to its creativity and revealing something of its reality, this being clear to the intellect in its normative state and dazzlingly evident to the heart illumined and purified by the remembrance of God.

The following aphorisms of one of the great masters of Sufi gnosis or ma'rifa (معرفۃ), Ibn 'Ata'illah, are altogether pertinent here. They read as a series of reflections on the paradox expressed so succinctly by the Imam. Ibn 'Ata'illah writes: "That which shows you the existence of His omnipotence is that He veiled you from Himself by that which has no existence alongside of Him." In one respect, God is "guarded from the eyes," in the Imam's words, and thus veiled by "things"; but insofar as God alone truly "is," these "things" do not really exist, so God is veiled by "nothing." Through their very "appearance," both in the sense of their coming into existence and in the sense of their "apparent" existence, it is "by things" that "their Creator manifests Himself." The mystery of this self-manifestation of God by and through all things is pressed with full force by the following rhetorical questions of Ibn 'Ata'illah:

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the one who manifests everything?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the one who is manifest through everything?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the one who is manifest in everything?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the Manifest to everything?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He was the Manifest before the existence of everything?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is more manifest than anything?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the One alongside of whom there is nothing?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is nearer to you than anything else?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since were it not for Him the existence of everything would not be manifest?

It is a marvel how Being has been manifested in non-being, and how the contingent has been established alongside of Him who possesses the attribute of Eternity!

The import of all these sayings can be grasped within a paradox tersely expressed by the Imam: "The veils do not veil Him (la tahjubuhu al-hujub)." The veils cannot help revealing what they are supposed to conceal; they are not merely transparent to the reality they cannot hide, but in and of themselves they reveal rather than conceal it. This recalls the Imam's famous saying, so often quoted by the Sufis: "Were the veil to be removed, I would not increase in certitude" (لو کشف الغطاء ما ازدت یقینا). The veils of creation ultimately reveal what they initially conceal, yet this revelation is perceived only by the eye of the heart purified through the dhikr, the heart that now "sees after being blind." The Imam continues, deepening the paradox still further:

"The veil between Him and His creatures is His very creation of them. [This veil is] for the sake of His prevention of that which is possible in their essences, and for the sake of making possible that which is prevented in regard to Him; and to distinguish the Creator from the created, and the limit from the limited, and the Lord from the vassal."

All of creation, every "veil" it constitutes, is thus nothing other than the divine dimension of al-Zahir: the Apparent, the Clear, the Evident, the Manifest. Yet creation is al-Zahir not on its own account, but by virtue of the divine reality of which it is a projection, a form of tajalli. God, as al-Zahir, projects something of His "hidden treasure" within and as the creation. The world manifests the "apparent" aspect of God, that which "appears" from Him, that which He can manifest or bring into being, even while concealing the mystery of God: that which remains "hidden," al-Batin, the supra-manifest source of being, the uncreated essence. The veil of creation is therefore nothing other than the self-revelation of God. It must be stressed, however, that it is so only by virtue of what pierces the veil of its own apparent otherness, that divine creativity which the creation cannot in fact conceal, even though in principle it has the character of a veil.

The veil constituted by creation prevents what the essences of creatures make possible. What the essences make possible is the realization of inmost identity with the divine Reality, tawhid at its highest level, a possibility that derives from the fact that this same divine Reality is the ontological foundation of "their essences." It is the divine Names, qualities, and attributes that "fill the foundations of all things." At the foundations, or within the "essences," there is but the one and only divine Reality. If the realization of this truth of identity is thus "possible," what the veil "prevents" is the affirmation of this identity at the wrong level of being, that of empirical existence, rather than at the deepest level, where the "essences" of things are nothing other than the divine qualities and thus the divine Reality itself. To equate created phenomena with God is tantamount to pure shirk, the cardinal sin of "association," and it is against this that a vision of divine oneness must guard. One must always "distinguish the Creator from the created, and the limit from the limited, and the Lord from the vassal."

The veil of creation, in other words, acts as a barrier against pantheism, the reduction of the Creator's infinite transcendence to the finite forms of creatures. Yet insofar as the veil has no existence apart from the self-manifestation of the Real, it paradoxically also makes possible "that which is prevented in regard to Him": rendered transparent to the divine Reality of which it is one expression, the veil cannot prevent the immanent reality of God from shining through and within all things. On one side stands essential identity, without any diminution of divine transcendence; on the other stands existential distinction, without any attenuation of divine immanence. This returns to the foundational sentence of the first sermon of the Nahj: "He is with every thing, but not through association; and other than every thing, but not through separation." It recalls too a central theme in Sufi metaphysics: the dynamic relationship between tanzih, the declaration of divine incomparability and transcendence, and tashbih, the affirmation of similarity and, at the deepest level, of immanence. In the words of Ibn al-'Arabi:

"He is not declared incomparable in any manner that will remove Him from similarity, nor is He declared similar in any manner that would remove Him from incomparability. So do not declare Him nondelimited and thus delimited by being distinguished from delimitation! For if He is distinguished then He is delimited by His nondelimitation. And if He is delimited by His nondelimitation, then He is not He."

To return to the Imam's powerfully evocative statement, "The veils do not veil Him." Both tanzih and tashbih can become veils unless each is seen in the light of the other; both transcendence and immanence can become veils unless the two perspectives are held together in spiritual vision; both the creation, as divine manifestation, and the uncreated Essence, above and beyond all manifestation, can become veils unless they are integrated in the spiritual vision proper to tawhid, and thus rendered transparent, each "revealing" the other. Equally, a one-sided emphasis on God as "the Apparent" becomes a veil by blinding one to God as "the Hidden," and conversely: for even the hidden aspect of God, considered in isolation, will, despite its non-manifest nature, constitute a conceptual veil that prevents the seeing of God's "evidence," His "evident-ness," in the very heart of the created world. The "veils" are thus not only created phenomena but also the limitations inherent in every human conception of God, including the divine qualities and attributes. Hence the need for a radical negation, on the plane of human conception, of those divine attributes. Only on the basis of such an apophasis can consciousness begin to fathom the true nature of divine transcendence and see through the veils that "do not veil Him."

These perspectives on the nature of the veil are better understood as the evocation of a spiritual vision than as the logical expression of empirical observation. They are the fruits of contemplative synthesis, not the conclusions of rational analysis. Such a vision resolves apparent contradiction through intuitive awareness of the unitive simplicity of reality, and it is not the product of some tortuous mental effort to reconcile the complexities of existence. It is at once a vision reflected by the heart burnished by the remembrance of God and itself a form of that very remembrance. What the rational faculty will always perceive as irreducible contradiction, the spirit of the intellect can apprehend as a fruitful paradox.

This aspect of the Imam's teaching finds vivid expression in the words of the famous Sufi Abu Sa'id al-Kharraz, who, when asked how he came to know God, replied: 'through the fact that He brings opposites together (jam'uhu al-diddayn).' He then recited the Qur'anic verse, 'He is the First and the Last, and the Outward and the Inward.'

It is spiritual intuition that imparts the capacity to see as one, in the unifying vision of tawhid, that which appears on the surface as many. In sharpening the paradox on the conceptual plane, Ali (ع) is, to use modern parlance, 'deconstructing' the artifices of mental conception, and in doing so he paves the way for receptivity to intuitive apprehension and spiritual contemplation. The very work of the intellect, عقل, a word whose root means to bind or restrain, is one of binding together. The realities the heart discloses through intuition and inspiration are drawn by it into the organic unity of tawhid through an exercise in logic, a movement of negation and affirmation, of constriction and expansion.

It is only at these deeper levels of consciousness that the complementary process of reconstruction can take place, a re-cognition of the Real, al-Haqq, arising from the heart's vision of God through 'the verities of faith' (haqa'iq al-iman). Only these haqa'iq are truly commensurate with al-Haqq. Only these realities hidden within the depths of faith, and brought to light in a heart polished by the remembrance of God, can do justice to the reality of God.
