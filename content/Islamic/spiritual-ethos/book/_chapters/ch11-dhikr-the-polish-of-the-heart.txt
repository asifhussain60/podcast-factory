# Dhikr: The Polish of the Heart

The subject here is the meaning of dhikruLlah, 'the remembrance of God', one of the most fundamental principles in Islamic spirituality. Two sources open it up. The first is the sayings of the Imam, read essentially as commentaries on the Quranic idea of dhikruLlah. The second is the doctrinal formulations that arose within the mystical traditions of Islam. To speak of Ali (ع) is to speak of Islamic spirituality in its purest, most essential form. His influence is felt wherever spirituality has flourished in Islam — in the more clearly defined disciplines of Sufism and Shii 'Irfan, and equally in the realms of futuwwa (spiritual chivalry), the craft guilds, music, poetry and the arts. As the gate to the city of prophetic wisdom, he leaves his mark on all the noblest expressions of the spirit of Islam.

The Sufi tradition in particular has a uniquely organic connection with the Imam. Sufi orders differ in every outward way — Shii or Sunni, Arab or Persian, Turk or Malay, Mauritanian or Bosnian — yet they are united in the figure of Ali ibn Abi Talib. He stands at the summit of all the chains of affiliation (salasila, sing. silsila) through which the masters trace their spiritual lineage back to the Prophet. This same unifying role appears in relation to the most significant internal division within Islam, that between Sunnism and Shiism. The two traditions already share a vast common ground in essential beliefs and practices. Beyond that, they are drawn together in a direct and living way by a shared orientation: both look toward sanctity (وَلَايَة) and gnosis (معرفۃ) as the goal of the religious life. Seyyed Hossein Nasr writes: 'Islamic esotericism or gnosis crystallized into the form of Sufism in the Sunni world, while it poured into the whole structure of Shiism, especially during its early period.' The esoteric core of Islam thus expresses itself in Sunni Sufism and in Shii 'Irfan with different accents. It takes on different forms and expressions, yet its attunement to the essence of تَوْحِيد is never fundamentally altered. According to Sayyid Haydar Amuli — one of the great figures in whom Shiism and Sufism converge — the true Shii cannot help being a Sufi', if he plumbs the mystical depths of the Imams' teachings. The reverse holds too: the Sufi is a Shii, once he recognizes that the most important roots of Sufism lie in the Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt. These Imams were the first poles (aqtab, sing. قُطْبٌ) of the spiritual tradition, and through them the mystical blessings of the Prophet were transmitted within an initiatic setting. For Amuli, the differences of outward form between the two traditions dissolve in the fire of وَلَايَة, sanctity — the true substance and goal of both alike. And for both, Ali is the wali Allah, the 'friend and saint of God', beyond all others.

The way Ali is regarded within the Sufi tradition is captured representatively by Ali Hujwiri, author of the first manual of Sufism written in Persian, the Kashf al-mahjub: 'Among them [the Sufis] is the son of Muhammad's uncle, he who drowned in the ocean of trying affliction, and was consumed in the fire of loving passion (غَرِيقِ بَحْرِ بَلَاءٍ وَحَرِيقِ نَارِ وَلَاءٍ), the leader of the saints and the pure ones (مُقْتَدَایِ أَوْلِيَاءِ وَالْأَصْفِيَاءِ). In this Path he holds a place of tremendous honor and elevated degree. He had complete mastery over the precise expression of the principles of the supernal realities, such that Junayd said of him, "Ali is our master as regards principles and affliction (شَيْخُنَا فِي الْأُصُولِ وَالْبَلَاءِ)."... That is, Ali is the Imam of this Path in respect of both theoretical knowledge and practical application (اندر علم و معاملات امام این طریقت), for the adepts of Sufism call the knowledge of this Path its "principles", while its practice is summed up in the endurance of affliction.'

The phrase 'endurance of affliction' recalls the Imam's description of the sages as those who 'befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged'. One reading of that elliptical phrase is this: among the things the sages befriend and the ignorant flee is prayer — and more precisely, the act of pure concentration on the Absolute, which shuts out every other thought. Such concentration is a kind of death, for the ego and all its preoccupations are, as it were, 'sacrificed' before the divine Reality on which the mind is fixed alone. The remembrance of God can be understood in two ways at once: as the fulfillment of that concentration, and as the path leading to the summit of spiritual realization — self-effacement before the sole reality of God.

The body of Ali 's teachings sheds remarkable light on the meaning, the principle and the practice of dhikruLlah — a theme of central importance in both the Quran and the Sunna of the Prophet. The Imam's sayings form a valuable commentary on those Quranic verses that speak of dhikr. And because the dhikr stands at the center of Sufi practice and doctrine, these sayings help explain why the Sufis regard the Imam as the first spiritual Pole (قُطْبٌ) of Sufism after the Prophet himself. The close bond between remembrance and spiritual realization is illumined from two directions: by the Imam's own sayings, and by the expositions of the Sufi tradition — above all those of the school of Ibn al-Arabi, known in the tradition as al-Shaykh al-akbar, the greatest Master', for his towering influence on later Sufi thought. These expositions can be read as doctrinal echoes of the Imam's dense, often elliptical pronouncements. Taken together, the Imam's sayings and the Sufis' elaborations bring key aspects of Islamic spirituality into sharp focus.

The term dhikr deserves first consideration, along with the reason for its prominence in Islamic devotional practice. It is an extremely rich word, and no single English term can capture its full range. It carries the senses of remembrance, recollection, invocation, repetition, incantation and recitation; and also of memory, mention, telling, relating a story, praise, glorification, celebration, fame, eminence, honor, exhortation and warning; and it can denote the Quran itself, or any book of revelation or religion. The definition that comes closest to the spiritual principle the word expresses is the one given by al-Raghib al-Isfahani in his Quranic dictionary. He writes that dhikr is that by means of which 'man preserves that knowledge (معرفۃ) which he has attained, so it is akin to memory (حِفْظ), except that memory is spoken of in respect of its function of acquisition (إِحْرَاز), whereas dhikr is spoken of in respect of its function of rendering present (اِسْتِحْضَار). Dhikr is also used to refer to the presence of a thing in one's consciousness (lit. "heart") or in one's speech. Thus, it is said that there are two types of dhikr: dhikr of the heart and dhikr of the tongue, and each comprises two further kinds: a dhikr which follows forgetfulness, and a dhikr which does not follow forgetfulness but which, on the contrary, expresses a continuous remembering.'

Much in this definition resonates with the Imam's own perspective. The key point is this: when the thing remembered, or 'rendered present' to consciousness, is God, the importance of dhikruLlah as a principle becomes self-evident. DhikruLlah is to be understood, first and foremost, as a principle of awareness — a deep taking-in of the divine presence. But it must also be understood as an action, a practice or set of practices, and so as a spiritual discipline. DhikruLlah can therefore be grasped both as the end and as a means to that end: both as spiritual awareness, and as the methodic practice that cultivates that awareness. It is the Sufi tradition that works out most fully this relationship between the practice of the dhikr and the state of consciousness it intends. There the dhikr is presented in two lights at once: as a contemplative discipline centered on the methodic invocation or repetition of a Name of God, and as the cognitive consequence and mystical fruit of that discipline.

The dhikr is thus the supreme spiritual method of the Sufis. It is a discipline that helps to realize within the heart what is already present to the mind, turning an abstract, notional grasp of certain ideas into a concrete perception of spiritual realities. Insofar as dhikruLlah means the permanent awareness of the divine Reality, it can be presented as the very essence of religion itself — that which 'once again binds' (re-ligare) the human being to God, and so the reason for being of all religious rites and practices. If prayer is the core of religious practice, dhikruLlah is, as the Quran states simply, akbar — 'greater' or 'greatest':

> ٱتْلُ مَآ أُوحِىَ إِلَيْكَ مِنَ ٱلْكِتَبِ وَأَقِمِ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ ۖ إِنَّ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ تَنْهَىٰ عَنِ ٱلْفَحْشَآءِ وَٱلْمُنكَرِ ۗ وَلَذِكْرُ ٱللَّهِ أَكْبَرُ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ مَا تَصْنَعُونَ
>
> 'Truly, prayer keepeth [one] away from lewdness and iniquity, and the remembrance of God is greater' (Al-Ankabut: 45).

Many sayings of the Prophet attest to the pre-eminence of the remembrance of God. It is related that the Prophet asked his companions: 'Shall I not tell you about the best and purest of your works for your Lord, and the most exalted of them in your ranks, and the work that is better for you than giving silver and gold, and better for you than encountering your enemy, with you striking their necks and them striking your necks?' Those he addressed replied: 'What is that, O emissary of God?' He said, 'The perpetual invocation of God, exalted and glorious (ذِكْرُ اللَّهِ عَزَّ وَجَلَّ دَائِمًا).'

Ali affirms the same: 'Perpetuate the dhikr, for truly it illumines the heart, and it is the most excellent form of worship (هُوَ أَفْضَلُ الْعِبَادَةِ).' The Shii tradition places much emphasis on the practice of dhikr, and records many sayings of the early Imams on the virtues of invoking the Names of God and various sacred phrases. Imam Jafar al-Sadiq described his followers specifically in these terms: 'Our Shia are those who, when alone (إِذَا خَلَوْا), invoke and remember God much.' The khalwa — the spiritual retreat in which one seeks to be 'alone' with God — is described by 'Allama Tabatabai, one of the most important masters of Shii gnosis in the twentieth century: As to the particular spiritual retreat (خَلْوَتِ خَاصٍّ) it is distancing oneself from people... it is especially required during certain types of verbal invocations (أَذْكَارٌ كَلَامِيَّةٌ)... and is considered essential by the masters of the path.'

Within the Sufi tradition, the following statement on the dhikr by Shaykh Ahmad al-Alawi may be taken as entirely representative:

Remembrance is the mightiest rule of the religion ... The law was not enjoined upon us, neither were the rites of worship ordained but for the sake of establishing the remembrance of God ... In a word, our performance of the rites of worship is considered strong or weak according to the degree of our remembrance while performing them.

Al-Ghazali raises the question of why the dhikr should be the most meritorious practice, when it is so simple to perform compared with all the other acts of worship, which demand so much effort. His answer is that this superiority can be fully understood only through the 'science of unveiling' (عِلْمُ الْمُكَاشَفَةِ). What can be said from the standpoint of the practical religious sciences (عِلْمُ الْمُعَامَلَةِ) is that the most efficacious dhikr is the one performed continuously with the presence of the heart (حُضُورُ الْقَلْبِ): This is the aim of the dhikr, and it is this which takes precedence over all acts of worship; nay, it is through this that the other acts of worship are dignified; this is the ultimate fruit of the practical acts of worship. The principle of dhikruLlah thus reaches to the highest levels of religious practice, contemplative discipline and spiritual realization, and so to the very heart of Islamic spirituality. For all their variety across time and place, and all their differing perspectives and emphases, the Sufis are unanimous that dhikruLlah is central to their way. And for all their differences of name, orientation, school of thought or mode of practice, the Sufi orders are united in the pivotal figure of Ali, the one who connects every later master of the spiritual path to the Prophet himself. This meeting of the Imam and the dhikruLlah is far from accidental. The Imam explained the meaning of the dhikr and himself initiated disciples into its methodic practice; both belong to his role as spiritual guide — the wali Allah, or the Pole (قُطْبٌ) of his age — and so lie at the center of his esoteric function within the spiritual tradition of Islam, in both its Sunni and Shii forms.

As for this esoteric function, very little firm historical information survives about the Imam's initiatic role — that is, his actual initiation of disciples into the practice of invocation, known in the tradition as talqin. Just as little survives about his own initiation into the esoteric practice by the Prophet. This is hardly surprising, since the practice is, precisely, esoteric: it is not imposed on all as an obligatory rite. Rather, within both Sufism and Shii 'Irfan, the invocation is bestowed on those with a particular vocation, those who have received a calling to practice something beyond the obligatory rites binding on everyone. Even so, some historical accounts offer prima facie evidence of the Imam's role in transmitting the dhikr. The people described in the earliest sources as devoted to the dhikr as a particular practice were known as close associates, if not disciples, of the Imam. His role in this initiatic domain is stressed in the orally transmitted sources of the Sufi way, and set down in several written sources too. These record reports of the dhikr passing from the Prophet to the Imam, and from him to several other disciples, who went on to initiate others into what later came to be called the way of tasawwuf.

Even though little historically sound material survives on the earliest use and transmission of dhikruLlah, there remains an extremely important body of literary evidence: the sayings recorded in the Nahj al-balagha and the Ghurar al-hikam, together with the supplications attributed to the Imam. Taken together, these sayings form a coherent corpus, a distinct body of doctrine. It offers a rich source of philosophical reflection, spiritual meditation and metaphysical insight into the nature and meaning of this practice, which holds so central a place in the mystical tradition of Islam.

These sayings shed light not only on the meaning of the practice of dhikr, but also on the philosophy and the theory of knowledge that underlie it; and they give precious hints of the fruits of its practice, its fulfillment in spiritual realization. On one side, the Imam's sayings are an important source of commentary on the Quranic verses that treat this theme. On the other, they clearly foreshadow the more elaborate doctrines of the dhikr found in the later Sufi and 'Irfani traditions. They thus serve as a bridge, joining what came before the Imam — prophetic revelation — to what came after him, the work of mystical elaboration. The texts and manuals of the mystical tradition seem to make explicit what was, more often than not, implicit in the Imam's sayings. The mystics drew out esoteric truths that the Imam had conveyed in sayings that were concise, often paradoxical and elliptical, yet always intellectually challenging and spiritually fruitful. Reflection on these sayings — read in the light of the Quran and the Sunna they illuminate, and of the later Sufi doctrine they anticipate — can bring one closer to a proper understanding of the role of dhikruLlah in the practical, operative and visionary dimensions of Islamic spirituality.

Reflection on those sayings finds its most fitting starting point in the Imam's comment on the dhikr in sermon 213 of the Nahj al-balagha. There the Imam first quotes words from verse 37 of Sura 24 of the Quran, entitled al-Nur ('The Light'), where the invocation of God's Name is mentioned, and then comments on them. The words are these: 'men whom neither trade nor merchandise diverts from the remembrance of God'. This fragment belongs to the preceding 'verse of light', the ayat al-nur so rich in mystical symbolism, and that bond gives the Imam's comment a considerably deeper resonance. The Imam plainly knew that reciting the words from verse 37 would call the earlier verses to his listeners' minds; by choosing not to quote the verse of light in full, he only sharpened the effect of the ellipsis — for such hints, implications, evocations and allusions are characteristic of Arabic balagha. His words are therefore best read alongside the verse of light in full:

God is the light of the heavens and the earth. A similitude of His light is a niche wherein is a lamp; the lamp is enclosed in a glass; the glass is as it were a shining star. [The lamp] is lit [by the oil of] a blessed olive tree, neither of the East nor of the West. The oil well-nigh shineth forth, though fire touch it not. Light upon light! God guideth to His light whom He will; and God striketh similitudes for mankind; and God knoweth all things (An-Nur: 35).

After this verse come the following two:

In houses which God has allowed to be elevated, and that His Name be remembered therein, He is glorified in the mornings and in the evenings by men whom neither trade nor merchandise diverteth from the remembrance of God, and from establishing the prayer, and from the bestowing of alms ... (An-Nur: 36-7).

After reciting the words 'men whom neither trade nor merchandise diverteth from the remembrance of God', the Imam begins his sermon:

Truly, God has made the remembrance (الذِّكْر) a polish for the hearts, by which they hear after being deaf, and see after being blind and yield after being resistant ... There have always been slaves of God ... with whom He held intimate discourse through their thoughts and spoke with them through the essence of their intellects. They diffused illumination through the awakened light in their hearing and their seeing and their hearts, calling unto the remembrance of the days of God ... Indeed, there is a special group (أَهْلٌ) who belong to the dhikr; they have adopted it in place of the world, such that 'neither trade nor merchandise' distracts them from it. They spend the days of their life in it ... It is as though they had left this world for the Hereafter, and they are there, witnessing what is beyond this world ...

The weight of this passage lies in the Imam's description of the dhikr as a 'polish for hearts' (جِلَاءً لِلْقُلُوبِ), set beside his reference to the awakened light' in the hearing, sight and hearts of those who have applied that polish. The link between the illumination of the heart and the verse of light is clear. The remembrance of God discloses the latent light of the heart, and the light within then shines together with the light from on high: 'light upon light'. Once the heart is illumined, every other faculty shares in the luminosity the invocation generates, and so there is, again, further 'light upon light'. The very word given for 'polish', jila, can itself be read as an implicit commentary on the light verse, for it derives from the verb jala, whose primary meaning is 'to become clear' — exposed to view, disclosed, brought to light. The Quran speaks of 'the night when it enshroudeth in darkness, and the day when it shineth forth in brightness (إِذَا تَجَلَّى) (Al-Layl: 1-2), and likewise: By the day, as it bringeth to light [the sun] (إِذَا جَلَّىٰهَا) (Ash-Shams: 3). The consciousness that lies hidden within the heart is brought to light through the polishing worked by the remembrance of God.

The concern here is to clarify the organ of consciousness, the spiritual 'heart' — both so that it may function as a mirror reflecting truths from above, and so that its own hidden depths are revealed, disclosing truths from within: those concealed contents of consciousness the Imam calls the 'buried treasures of the intellect' (دَفَائِنُ الْعُقُولِ). The dhikr is presented as a means of purifying this spiritual mode of perception, the 'heart', which cannot simply be identified with the rational faculty, still less with the organs of sensory perception, as discussed in Chapter 1.

The Quran points to the deeper significance of the heart and its distinctive mode of 'vision': 'Have they not traveled in the land, that they might have hearts with which to comprehend, and ears with which to hear? Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but blind are the hearts that are in the breasts' (Al-Hajj: 46). The outward sight of the eye reaches only the surface of things, whereas the heart's proper vision is an insight that pierces beneath that surface to the reality concealed there. And again: 'They have hearts with which they understand not, eyes with which they see not, ears with which they hear not. They are like cattle, nay, even further astray. They are the heedless (الْغَافِلُونَ) (Al-Araf: 179). That closing reference to the heedless', the ghafilun, returns to the dhikr, for the ghafil is the very opposite of the dhakir, the 'rememberer'; and the verse immediately following is one of the most important 'methodic' foundations of the mystical practice of dhikr: 'Unto God belong the most beautiful Names, so call Him by them. And leave behind those who desecrate His Names; they will be requited for what they do' (Al-Araf: 180). The bond between remembrance and the heart is affirmed plainly in these verses: ... Those who believe and whose hearts are at peace in the remembrance of God, is it not in the remembrance of God that hearts are at peace? (Ar-Ra'd: 28); 'Truly, therein is a reminder (ذِكْرَى) for him who hath a heart' (Qaf: 37); 'Those are true believers whose hearts quake with awe when God is invoked' (Al-Anfal: 2); 'Is he whose breast God hath opened up to submission (اسلام), such that he followeth a light from his Lord [like he who remains closed to submission and in darkness]? So woe be to those whose hearts are hardened against the remembrance of God, they are in plain error' (Az-Zumar: 22).

By contrast, the verse that follows speaks of hearts that 'soften' to dhikruLlah: 'God hath revealed the most beautiful saying, a Book [containing] similitudes and paired [threats and promises] at which the skins of those who fear their Lord quiver; then their skins and their hearts soften to the remembrance of God' (Az-Zumar: 23).

The Imam's comment on verse 37 of the 'Light' chapter should therefore be understood as shedding light on every Quranic verse that refers to the 'understanding', or spiritual vision, to which the heart has access in principle, and that names dhikruLlah as the means of turning this potentiality into actuality. The heart needs to be 'polished' by the invocation of God. The Imam's comment echoes and reinforces a saying of the Prophet often quoted in Sufi circles: 'For everything there is a polish (صِقَالَةٌ), and the polish of the hearts is the dhikruLlah.'

What is being referred to here is an actual discipline — a practice, an action, or a regular series of actions — though this in no way excludes the meaning of dhikr as awareness, as a state of recollectedness in the divine presence, a disposition of soul shaped by the consciousness that 'if you cannot see Him, He sees you'. The 'polishing' in question can be understood in two ways at once: as the work of purifying thought through reflection and meditation, and as a regular methodic practice — whether that takes the form of glorification (تَسْبِيح), magnification (تَمْجِيدٌ), praise (تَحْمِيدٌ), sanctification (تَقْدِيس), the declaration of divine unity (تَهْلِيلٌ), or the dhikr in the strict sense, the invocation of God's Names, by which the 'rust' of forgetfulness is removed. Ali also refers in his comment to a particular group who 'belong' to the dhikr, calling them an ahl. The Prophet likewise spoke of the ahl الذِّكْر: 'God has angels who rove the pathways, seeking out the ahl الذِّكْر, and when they find a group of people (قَوْمٌ) invoking God, they call out to one another, "Come to that which you desire!"

Later in time, one of the greatest members of the qawm — one of the names by which the Sufis call themselves — gave poetic expression to the image of the heart polished by the dhikr. It is the custom of kings, Rumi tells in the Mathnawi, to place the Sufis right before them: for they are a mirror for the soul, and better than a mirror — breasts polished by invocation and meditation, such that the mirror of the heart receives the pure, virginal image.

Another aspect of the heart's polishing deserves reflection: the nature of the 'vision' experienced by the heart that the dhikr purifies and faith vivifies. In the Imam's perspective this vision is divinely inspired; it is nothing less than the spiritual vision of God already referred to: 'Eyes see Him not through sight's observation, but hearts see Him through the verities of faith.'

In another saying, the Imam calls 'pure hearts' the 'places' from which God can be seen: 'so whoever purifies his heart sees God'. The connection between the purification of the heart and the remembrance of God is confirmed in this saying: 'Where are they whose actions are accomplished purely for God, and who purify their hearts [so that they become] places for the remembrance of God?'

Taken together, these sayings of the Imam on the impact of the dhikr upon the heart can be read as a commentary on the important hadith qudsi cited in Chapter 2: 'My slave never ceases to draw near to Me through supererogatory acts (نَوَافِلُ) until I love him. And when I love him, I am his hearing by which he hears, his sight by which he sees, his hand by which he grasps, and his foot by which he walks.' The Sufis regard this 'divine utterance' as defining a foundational principle of their metaphysical doctrine, while at the same time pointing to the goal of their mystical practice.

A mutual confirmation stands between the Imam's saying and the hadith qudsi. Through the dhikr — the supererogatory act par excellence — the individual's perceptions and actions are mysteriously assimilated to the divine Reality, and the heart thereby attains spiritual vision. But the heart, as the center of consciousness, can then no longer be regarded in its purely relative, human dimension. Its inmost essence, or secret (سِرٌّ), belongs in truth to the divine, and this alone can see as God sees and, ultimately, 'see' God Himself. As the Sufi master Abu Talib al-Makki wrote, 'None sees Him to whom nothing is similar but Him to whom nothing is similar.' If God does indeed enter into the human acts of perception proper to the outward senses, then all the more must the supreme act of spiritual awareness, the vision of God, be in reality not a vision of God by man but a vision of God by God, effected through the individual's 'heart' rather than by it. The Imam says, 'Know God through God (اِعْرِفُوا اللهَ بِاللهِ).' In the final analysis, then, it is God who both 'sees' and is 'seen'. On a typical Sufi reading of the Quranic verse 'by a witnesser and that which is witnessed' (Al-Buruj: 3), it is God Himself who is both the 'witnesser' (شَاهِدٌ) and the 'witnessed' (مَشْهُود). Al-Kashani, for example, writes in his esoteric exegesis — until quite recently wrongly attributed to Ibn al-Arabi — that the human witness is effaced in his witnessing, so that it is God who witnesses Himself through the individual whose separative consciousness is extinguished, such that 'He is the identity of the witness, there being no difference between the two except in terms of notional expression.'

This anticipates the final section, where the ultimate fruits of invocation will be explored in terms of spiritual realization through self-effacement. For now the focus remains on a more relative aspect of the heart's vision of God: that by which the reality of God is made clear and present to the heart through the 'verities of faith'. The agent here is the inner dimension of human consciousness, and the object 'perceived' is the divine Reality. In other words, profound faith reveals the objective presence of God — a presence that is everywhere and cannot be taken away, and that constitutes the ontological reality of all existing things. As the Imam's famous Dua Kumayl puts it, noted earlier: 'By Thy tremendousness (عَظَمَتِكَ) which has filled all things ... by Thy Names which have filled the foundations of all things, by Thy knowledge which encompasses all things, by the light of Thy face, through which all things are illumined ...' (emphasis added).

Nothing can exist without the 'foundation' — the ontological infrastructure, as it were — that the Name, or combination of Names, of God supplies for it. What constitutes the true being and the positive qualities of each manifested thing is therefore, in the end, a divine Name or combination of Names. To see 'things' correctly means seeing the divine attributes manifested by and through them, and so perceiving them as existential reflections of principial realities — as so many tajalliyyat, or theophanies, self-disclosures of the divine Reality. The 'heart' purified by invocation comes to see God everywhere.

This vision of the divine in all things rests on two principles the Quran affirms: the first concerns the divine 'object', that is, the objective, metaphysical nature of reality; the second concerns the human subject and the mystical assimilation of that reality. On the one hand, nothing that exists — not even the most outward aspects of material existence — can be excluded from the divine Reality, for 'He is the First and the Last, and the Outward and the Inward' (Al-Hadid: 3), and 'Wherever ye turn there is the Face of God' (Al-Baqarah: 115). On the other hand, 'We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and in their own souls, until it be clear to them that He is the Real' (Fussilat: 53). Everything around us thus provides a sign pointing back to its Creator.

The heart polished and purified by the dhikr can decipher these signs and so 'see' God everywhere and in everything; yet the possibility of such a vision in no way reduces the divine Reality to the level of sensible phenomena. As noted earlier, the first sermon of the Nahj al-balagha proclaims that 'God is with every thing, but not through association; and other than every thing, but not through separation.' This 'withness' (مَعِيَّة) of God implies no relativization of God, no association or conjoining (مُقَارَنَةٌ) of the divine Reality with created things, and no diminution of divine transcendence. The interplay of ideas here can be read as a commentary both on the Quranic verses that proclaim divine immanence — the presence of God to and within all things — and on those that stress divine transcendence, surpassing and incomparable to all things. God may be 'absolutely' close to us, yet we are infinitely far from Him. On the one hand, 'We are closer to man than the neck-artery' (Qaf: 16) — He is as near to us as our own jugular vein — and on the other, ... there is none comparable unto Him (Al-Ikhlas: 4).

Nothing that exists can be wholly separate from the all-encompassing reality of God, and yet that reality has no common measure with anything that exists. His oneness both includes and excludes all things. To affirm God's immanence within the world — His being 'with every thing' — therefore takes nothing away from His transcendence; and to affirm His transcendence above the world — His being 'other than every thing' — does not make Him absent from it. The oneness of God carries all of this, and at the same time it denies any merely numerical oneness, as the first chapter briefly discussed. In the Imam's view, God is One not in the sense of a countable 'one', a single unit among other units, but in the sense that His oneness admits no 'other'. In his own words, 'That which has no second does not enter into the category of numbers.'

This can be read as an esoteric commentary on the Quranic verse, 'Say: He, God is One' (Al-Ikhlas: 1). The opposite of this divine oneness — the multiplicity of gods implied in the error of شِرْك — is refined by this conception and turned into a statement about being rather than about theology alone. The act of شِرْك, of associating 'partners' with God, is thereby raised to a higher register, and shown to imply not simply the affirmation of false deities but the attribution to God of a merely numerical oneness. To reduce His oneness to number is to confine it within the wholly relative category of determinate entities. Each such entity is a unit, and on this reckoning God would be no more than one among other similar 'beings'. But the Imam insists that God 'has no second': there is no other 'being' in being but Him. To call Him 'one' in the ordinary sense is itself imprecise, for it implies that a second could follow in sequence. He is better described as unique. No entity or unit that appears to exist can, in reality, be compared with His being, which is one in an infinitely all-encompassing sense and unique in an absolutely exclusive sense — a oneness that includes all reality and excludes all otherness. This radical idea — of a oneness that absolutely excludes any 'otherness' in Being — means that created entities are so many false gods, precisely to the extent that their apparent 'oneness' in existence is set beside God's true oneness, or rather beside God's 'one-and-onliness'.

In another sermon, Ali turns to divine immanence, focusing this time on the kind of awareness the human intellect can have of the divine Reality — which returns to the question of what vision of God is possible for a human being:

"Praise be to God who is present in the hidden inwardness of things, and whose being is indicated by the signs of manifest things. The eye of the onlooker cannot behold Him, so the eye of one who does not see Him cannot deny His reality, nor can the heart of one who affirms His reality see Him. He is utmost in elevation, so no thing is more elevated than He; but He is also close in His nearness, so no thing is closer than He ... God has not made the intellects capable of defining His qualities, but He has also not veiled the intellects from essential knowledge of Him."

This passage says two things. On the one hand, human intellects cannot define, still less know, the divine attributes; on the other, 'essential knowledge' of Him is attainable. The apparent contradiction resolves in another key dimension of dhikr — that of recollection, remembrance, recognition. What takes place is a re-collecting of fragments of a knowledge that already exists but has been forgotten. One recognizes what one has always known, and it is precisely for this reason that one can know it again. This does not mean the knowledge is made of parts to be reassembled, which would be empirical knowledge. The remembrance here is more like an awakening, an illumination that dawns as the clouds of inborn forgetfulness are dispelled.

This path to knowledge through remembrance recurs throughout the Imam's sayings. In Sermon 1 of the Nahj, for instance, comes the phrase, "Foremost in religion is knowledge of Him (أَوَّلُ الدِّينِ مَعْرِفَتُهُ)." The word awwal literally means 'the first', and can be understood as the starting-point, the foundation, and so the foremost element of religion. The word marifa means not merely knowledge but also carries the sense of 'recognition'. The implication is plain: the basis of all religious orientation is man's pre-existing knowledge of the divine Reality. One re-cognizes what one has always known at the deepest level of consciousness; one ac-knowledges what one already knows but had seemingly forgotten.

Further on in the same sermon, God sent to mankind "His messengers, dispatching prophets in succession, in order to claim from them [His creatures] the fulfillment of the covenant of His creation; to remind them of His forgotten graces; to remonstrate with them through communication [of His revelation]; to unearth for them the buried treasures of the intellects ..." (دَفَائِنُ الْعُقُولِ).

The 'buried treasures' can be identified with the knowledge of divine realities ingrained in man's deepest nature — the realities to which humanity was made to bear witness at the dawn of creation: "And when thy Lord brought forth from the children of Adam, from their loins, their seed, and made them testify of themselves, 'Am I not your Lord?' They said, 'Yea, we testify.' [This was] lest ye say on the Day of Resurrection, 'Truly, of this we were unaware'" (Al-Araf: 172).

These divine realities can also be understood by reference to the divine spirit breathed into man at the creation of the human soul: "Then He fashioned [man] and breathed into him of His Spirit" (As-Sajdah: 9); to the "names" taught by God to Adam: "And He taught Adam the names, all of them ..." (Al-Baqarah: 31); and to the fitra, the primordial nature of man, patterned upon the divine nature: "... the nature of God (fitrat Allah), that according to which He created man" (Ar-Rum: 30). The primordial nature of the human soul can thus be seen to hold the very element — knowledge of God — to which all of humanity is made to bear witness in the pact (ٱلْمِيثَقَ) named in the verse above. Imam Muhammad al-Baqir makes this identity between the fitra and the knowledge of God explicit, in a saying quoted by al-Khui as a commentary on that sentence of the first sermon. Al-Baqir was asked about the meaning of the Quranic words, "the nature of God, that according to which He created man." He answered, "He created them [such that their primordial nature was] in conformity with تَوْحِيد, at the covenant regarding knowledge of Him, [when they testified] that He is indeed their Lord."

Knowledge of God, then, essentially defines human nature; and for this reason the human intellect cannot be veiled, as the Imam said, from "essential knowledge of God." Yet although this knowledge is ingrained in human nature, forgetfulness (غَفْلَةٌ) has become man's second nature. In Quranic terms, the mere passage of time smothers the heart and so hardens it: "Hath not the moment come for the believers, that their hearts be humbled to the remembrance of God, and to what hath been revealed of the truth; and that they not become as those who were given the Book aforetime. Then the term was prolonged for them, so their hearts were hardened; and many of them are evil-doers" (Al-Hadid: 16).

The verse speaks both of this hardening of the heart over time — which can be identified with the غَفْلَةٌ of second nature — and of its opposite: the heart's being present, here and now, to the dhikruLlah. To cure forgetfulness, more is needed than a simple factual reminder, some item of information, some piece of formal, conceptual, reasoned knowledge. No amount of empirical data, arriving only from without, can bring man to knowledge of God. Rather it is integral remembrance — a spiritual awareness that wells up from within and crystallizes on contact with revelation from without — that makes the innate knowledge of God present once again to heart and mind, the knowledge 'buried' deep in the intellect. There is no common measure between this consciousness of God and ordinary empirical knowledge of things. The Imam expresses the gulf between the two kinds of knowledge in sayings such as, "Praise be to God who is well-known (مَعْرُوف) without being seen." A further wording of the same principle sharpens the paradox: "By things their Creator manifests Himself to the intellects; and by things He is guarded from the sight of the eyes." This opens onto a difficult theme in the Imam's teaching: the nature of the 'veil' formed by created things, and how it is to be understood in the light of the infinite and inalienable reality of God. On one hand, all created things are a veil, in that they keep the divine Essence from being seen by outward vision. On the other, all things cannot but manifest the divine, bearing witness to its creativity and revealing something of its reality — plain to the intellect in its normative state, and dazzlingly evident to the heart illumined and purified by the remembrance of God.

The following aphorisms of Ibn 'Ataillah — one of the great masters of Sufi gnosis, or marifa — are wholly to the point here. They read as a series of reflections on the paradox the Imam expressed so tersely. Ibn Ataillah writes: "That which shows you the existence of His omnipotence is that He veiled you from Himself by that which has no existence alongside of Him." In one respect God is "guarded from the eyes," in the Imam's words, and so veiled by "things"; but since God alone truly "is," these "things" do not really exist, and so God is veiled by "nothing." Through their very "appearance" — both their coming into existence and their "apparent" existence — it is "by things" that "their Creator manifests Himself." The mystery of this self-manifestation of God by and through all things is pressed to the full by the following rhetorical questions of Ibn Ataillah:

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the one who manifests everything?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since were it not for Him the existence of everything would not be manifest?

It is a marvel how Being has been manifested in non-being, and how the contingent has been established alongside of Him who possesses the attribute of Eternity!

The import of all these sayings can be grasped within a paradox the Imam expressed tersely: "The veils do not veil Him (لَا تَحْجُبُهُ الْحُجُبُ)." The veils cannot help revealing what they are meant to conceal. They are not merely transparent to the reality they cannot hide; in and of themselves they reveal it rather than conceal it. This recalls the Imam's famous saying, so often quoted by the Sufis: "Were the veil to be removed, I would not increase in certitude" (لو کشف الغطاء ما ازدت یقینا). The veils of creation reveal in the end what they at first conceal, yet only the eye of the heart, purified through the dhikr, perceives this revelation — the heart that now "sees after being blind." The Imam continues, deepening the paradox further:

"The veil between Him and His creatures is His very creation of them. [This veil is] for the sake of His prevention of that which is possible in their essences, and for the sake of making possible that which is prevented in regard to Him; and to distinguish the Creator from the created, and the limit from the limited, and the Lord from the vassal."

All of creation, every "veil" it forms, is thus nothing other than the divine dimension of al-Zahir: the Apparent, the Clear, the Evident, the Manifest. Yet creation is al-Zahir not in its own right, but by virtue of the divine reality of which it is a projection, a form of tajalli. God, as al-Zahir, projects something of His "hidden treasure" within the creation and as the creation. The world manifests the "apparent" aspect of God — that which "appears" from Him, that which He can manifest or bring into being — while still concealing the mystery of God: that which remains "hidden," al-Batin, the supra-manifest source of being, the uncreated essence. The veil of creation is therefore nothing other than the self-revelation of God. Yet this holds only by virtue of what pierces the veil of its own apparent otherness — that divine creativity which creation cannot in fact conceal, even though in principle it has the character of a veil.

The veil formed by creation prevents what the essences of creatures make possible. What the essences make possible is the realization of inmost identity with the divine Reality — تَوْحِيد at its highest level — a possibility that follows from the fact that this same divine Reality is the ground of being of "their essences." It is the divine Names, qualities, and attributes that "fill the foundations of all things." At the foundations, or within the "essences," there is but the one and only divine Reality. If the realization of this truth of identity is thus "possible," what the veil "prevents" is the affirmation of that identity at the wrong level of being — the level of empirical existence — rather than at the deepest level, where the "essences" of things are nothing other than the divine qualities, and thus the divine Reality itself. To equate created things with God amounts to pure شِرْك, the cardinal sin of "association," and it is against this that a vision of divine oneness must guard. One must always "distinguish the Creator from the created, and the limit from the limited, and the Lord from the vassal."

The veil of creation, in other words, acts as a barrier against pantheism — the reduction of the Creator's infinite transcendence to the finite forms of creatures. Yet since the veil has no existence apart from the self-manifestation of the Real, it also, paradoxically, makes possible "that which is prevented in regard to Him": made transparent to the divine Reality of which it is one expression, the veil cannot prevent the immanent reality of God from shining through and within all things. On one side stands essential identity, with no diminution of divine transcendence; on the other stands existential distinction, with no lessening of divine immanence. This returns to the founding sentence of the first sermon of the Nahj: "He is with every thing, but not through association; and other than every thing, but not through separation." It recalls too a central theme in Sufi metaphysics: the dynamic relationship between tanzih — the declaration of divine incomparability and transcendence — and tashbih — the affirmation of similarity and, at the deepest level, of immanence. In the words of Ibn al-Arabi:

"He is not declared incomparable in any manner that will remove Him from similarity, nor is He declared similar in any manner that would remove Him from incomparability. So do not declare Him nondelimited and thus delimited by being distinguished from delimitation! For if He is distinguished then He is delimited by His nondelimitation. And if He is delimited by His nondelimitation, then He is not He."

To return to the Imam's powerfully evocative statement, "The veils do not veil Him." Both tanzih and tashbih can become veils unless each is seen in the light of the other; both transcendence and immanence can become veils unless the two are held together in spiritual vision; and both the creation, as divine manifestation, and the uncreated Essence, above and beyond all manifestation, can become veils unless they are integrated in the spiritual vision proper to تَوْحِيد, and so made transparent, each "revealing" the other. In the same way, a one-sided stress on God as "the Apparent" becomes a veil by blinding one to God as "the Hidden" — and the reverse holds too. For even the hidden aspect of God, taken in isolation, will, despite its non-manifest nature, form a conceptual veil that prevents the seeing of God's "evidence," His "evident-ness," in the very heart of the created world. The "veils" are thus not only created things but also the limits built into every human conception of God, including the divine qualities and attributes. Hence the need for a radical negation, on the plane of human conception, of those divine attributes. Only on the basis of such an apophasis can consciousness begin to fathom the true nature of divine transcendence, and see through the veils that "do not veil Him."

These perspectives on the nature of the veil are better understood as the evocation of a spiritual vision than as the logical statement of empirical observation. They are the fruits of contemplative synthesis, not the conclusions of rational analysis. Such a vision resolves apparent contradiction through an intuitive awareness of the unitive simplicity of reality; it is not the product of some tortuous mental effort to reconcile the complexities of existence. It is at once a vision reflected by the heart burnished by the remembrance of God, and itself a form of that very remembrance. What the rational faculty will always perceive as irreducible contradiction, the spirit of the intellect can grasp as a fruitful paradox.

This aspect of the Imam's teaching finds vivid expression in the words of the famous Sufi Abu Said al-Kharraz. Asked how he came to know God, he replied: 'through the fact that He brings opposites together (جَمْعُهُ الضِّدَّيْنِ).' He then recited the Quranic verse, 'He is the First and the Last, and the Outward and the Inward.'

It is spiritual intuition that gives the capacity to see as one, in the unifying vision of تَوْحِيد, what appears on the surface as many. In sharpening the paradox on the conceptual plane, Ali is, in modern parlance, 'deconstructing' the artifices of mental conception, and in doing so he opens the way to intuitive apprehension and spiritual contemplation. The very work of the intellect, عقل — a word whose root means to bind or restrain — is one of binding together. The realities the heart discloses through intuition and inspiration are drawn by it into the organic unity of تَوْحِيد through an exercise in logic, a movement of negation and affirmation, of constriction and expansion.

It is only at these deeper levels of consciousness that the complementary process of reconstruction can take place: a re-cognition of the Real, اَلْحَقّ, arising from the heart's vision of God through 'the verities of faith' (حَقَائِقُ الْإِيمَان). Only these haqaiq are truly on a level with اَلْحَقّ. Only these realities, hidden within the depths of faith and brought to light in a heart polished by the remembrance of God, can do justice to the reality of God.
