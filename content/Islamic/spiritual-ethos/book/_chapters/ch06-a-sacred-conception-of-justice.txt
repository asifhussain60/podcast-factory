# A Sacred Conception of Justice

The discussion of justice that follows is not an exercise in theoretical speculation about what justice means in Ali (ع)'s letter to Malik al-Ashtar, written fourteen centuries ago. It is not the kind of definition one finds in a dictionary, where justice amounts to little more than fairness, equality, and fair play. The aim is, first and foremost, to bring the discussion of justice into alignment with the concept and reality of the sacred, the reality of the sacred One, القدس. On one hand, the principle of justice carries a sacred and spiritual dimension that contemporary discourse so often overlooks. On the other, a sensitive appreciation of the meaning of the sacred opens a deeper understanding of justice's roots within the divine nature itself, and a more profound awareness of why justice is necessary, in the world and in the soul.

The basic argument is this: in Ali (ع)'s worldview, the ethical orientation toward justice is immeasurably deepened when it is consciously linked to the spiritual precepts of the Islamic faith. The spirit underlying moral rules and ethical values is strengthened by a commitment to transcendent principles, even though, or precisely because, those principles surpass the realm of action in which the rules and precepts themselves operate. The شریعۃ can be pictured as having a body and a spirit, as a person does. Its body consists of various actions, prayer, fasting, the paying of زکوۃ, but the spirit animating those actions rests on the nature of the divine, which is justice. Adherence to the شریعۃ is therefore a training in becoming like the divine through the principles of justice, and so action must be complemented by contemplation of the divine. Contemplation and action are complementary here, not contradictory, for this complementarity goes to the very heart of the Islamic message of tawhid, توحید, an integrating oneness that makes theory and practice one, so that a person becomes a true موحد. This unity is embodied with dazzling clarity in the life of the Imam, as it is in the life of the Prophet. The harmony between the two principles, contemplation and action, being and doing, theory and practice, ideal and reality, pervades the remarkable document that is the subject of these reflections.

These reflections stand against the background of contemporary ethical discourse and may be seen as a modest contribution, from an Islamic perspective, to the scholarly debates on virtue and ethics. In particular, they should be understood in light of one of the most serious and far-reaching critiques of modern and postmodern conceptions of virtue in general, and of justice in particular, that of Alasdair MacIntyre. Put simply, his critique holds that for several centuries ethical discourse in Western society has drifted far from its moorings in absolute values. He argues compellingly that debates about morality and ethics, together with their philosophical foundations, are no longer grounded in conceptions of 'the good life' or of 'the Good' as such. As a consequence, MacIntyre holds, various shades of relativism, nihilism, utilitarianism, and opportunism have emerged de facto as the shifting bases on which ethical values are formulated, if they are formulated at all. Relativism holds that knowledge, truth, and morality are not absolute but relative, existing only in relation to a culture, a society, or a historical context. Nihilism rejects all religious and moral principles and takes life to be essentially meaningless. Utilitarianism counts an action right only if it is useful or benefits a majority, so that good and bad rest on nothing more fundamental than a calculation of greater benefit or loss. And opportunism seizes whatever benefit circumstances offer, regardless of any plan or principle, the way of the manipulator, who flatters, performs, or deceives in order to get what he wants.

Whatever value these human-made theories occasionally yield, none of it is rooted in the divine, and so even where they happen to mirror the values of faith they are baseless, without spirit, dead corpses. The practice of virtue on an ad hoc basis is not virtue properly speaking; it is a series of pragmatic adaptations to changing circumstances in the absence of any genuine principle of virtue. The virtue of truthfulness illustrates this. If one resolves always to speak the truth without grasping the underlying reason for it, that truth is the very nature of our Creator, الحق, the absolute truth, and that our goal in this world is to take on the virtues of our Creator, then truthfulness will in the end become situational and self-serving, something to be compromised whenever circumstances seem to warrant it, spoken when it profits and abandoned when it costs. But once truth is seen as the nature of the divine, it becomes everlasting and eternal, indifferent to consequence, because one speaks it in emulation of the immortal nature of God. The virtue itself becomes immortal, and the need to practise it on an ad hoc basis falls away. 'We are looking,' writes MacIntyre, 'for a conception of the good which will enable us to order other goods, for a conception of the good which will enable us to extend our understanding of the purpose and content of the virtues, for a conception of the good which will enable us to understand the place of integrity and consistency in life ...'

It is at this level, in particular, that the insights of Ali (ع) and other sages of the Islamic tradition are especially relevant. Not only does MacIntyre's proposed solution to the crisis, the reformulation of an Aristotelian conception of ethics grounded in a Thomistic worldview, share a great deal with the Islamic tradition of ethical discourse (akhlaq); one can also argue that a careful engagement with the Imam's legacy yields far more than a merely theoretical approach to the question of justice, and this in two respects. First, the Imam was himself a paragon of justice and thus a living embodiment of this and other fundamental virtues. In his life one observes a perfect, indeed unflinching, adherence to this cardinal virtue, a life not governed by his نفس but governed entirely by the divine nature of God. The remarkable actions we read about and marvel over emerge as the fruit of his irresistible attraction toward the nature of الحق, for his existence was perfectly oriented toward God. His focus was never on the action itself but on the source of the principles generating it, so that in fixing upon the beauty of the divine nature and striving to take it on, good actions arose from him naturally and inevitably. Although this work does not enter into many details of his life and its lessons, certain incidents and events that help illustrate this point will be recalled. Secondly, and more importantly for the present purpose, the discourses, letters, and sayings attributed to Ali (ع) offer a rich source of reflection on the dynamic spirituality that lies at the root of a life of virtue. One is afforded a glimpse into a way of life totally governed by the sacred, one in which the desire for the 'good life' emerges as the fruit of an irresistible attraction toward the Good as such, which is identical to the absolutely Real, al-Haqq.

In other words, one is ethically inspired by 'the Good' in direct proportion to one's existential orientation toward the Real. If moral rules are grounded in ethical perspectives, and if those perspectives are rooted in a conception of the 'good life', then that conception of the Good is itself derived from the moral agent's fundamental understanding of what life means. Amyn B. Sajoo, in a recent thoughtful work on Muslim ethics, draws a careful distinction between ethics and morals. He argues that ethical discourse is concerned more with conceptions of 'the good', while morals pertain more specifically to rules about right and wrong. Ethics deals with the soul and the spirit of the good, the باطن, and it is out of ethics that morals are born. 'Certainly,' he continues, 'the concepts overlap substantially in theory and practice; but it is well to bear in mind the more encompassing sense of reality that typifies ethics.' Situating ethics within the wider framework of spiritual virtue, in the sense of ihsan, احسان, expands even further the compass of both ethics and morality, lending both types of discourse a dimension of existential depth and grounding them in a more organic and all-encompassing framework. For there are three components to the spiritual life: اسلام, which supplies the outer aspects of morality; ایمان, which is the framework of ethics; and the union of the two, serving as their underlying principle, which issues in احسان, the good life, the beautiful life. Abstract questions of right and wrong come to be rooted in the more definitive principle that emerges in response to the question: what is reality?

The previous chapter's discussion of the deeper meanings of divine unity bears on this. In the Imam's spiritual vision of tawhid, one perceives, according to one's capacity, an ultimate reality that not only 'has no second' but also has no moral 'opposite'. People tend to picture the world in terms of good against evil, God against شیطان, always thinking in opposites; but the Imam teaches that God has no opposite at all. ابلیس does not stand against God, for nothing has the capacity to stand against God. He stands against God's creation, and his entire effort is to drive people away from their inherent divine nature by beautifying its opposite, beautifying arrogance where God's nature is modest, lewdness where it is decent, boastfulness where it is humble. For the goodness that is proper to God is not the moral opposite of evil; rather, it occupies an entirely different plane, that of al-Haqq, الحق, the absolutely Real, the only 'opposite' to which is unreality or nothingness. While goodness is at one with the pure positivity of the Absolute, evil derives solely from a negative capacity to negate the Real, the Good. In other words, the essence of all goodness and beauty is identified with the Creator, while evil is a modality of the created, the corrupted state of a creation that has deviated from the divine nature. This is strongly implied in the Qur'anic verse of سورۃ الفلق which calls the believer to seek refuge in God from 'the evil of that which He created' (113: 2):

قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ ٱلْفَلَقِ ۱ مِن شَرِّ مَا خَلَقَ ۲

'Say: I seek refuge with the Lord of the daybreak, from the evil of what He has created.'

This verse is often mistranslated as 'from the evil which He created', which is wrong both theologically and lexically. The expression 'min sharri ma khalaqa', مِن شَرِّ مَا خَلَقَ, clearly refers to the evil that derives from creation, not to creation as such. In theological terms, God creates morally free agents and it is they who are the authors of evil in creation; when creation chooses, through its own free will, to negate the divine nature of its Creator, evil is born. God may be said to 'create' all actions only in the metaphysical sense that He is the source of all being, and thus of all modalities within being, and actions constitute one such modality; God cannot, however, be said to have created evil as such, only the conditions within which evil emerges. Evil is thus the negation of the Good, which is at one with the Real; as such, evil has no ultimate ontological principle. It is but a temporary state of deviation from that pure and divine nature, an illusion witnessed by the 'eye of the heart'.

The verse, then, can be understood as a call to seek refuge in the Real from the unreal, there being no common measure in terms of being between goodness and evil. Goodness is opposable only by the appearance of evil, an appearance that the 'eye of the heart' grasps as illusion, that eye which truly 'sees' God through 'the verities of faith'. This vision of the relative reality of evil, far from engendering indifference toward it, greatly empowers the spiritual resolve to overcome it, for the moral effort to be good intensifies in proportion to one's conviction of accountability to that goodness. This conviction, in turn, is carried inward from the mind to the heart by the certainty that goodness is 'more real' than evil; and this certainty itself is nourished by the spiritual vision of the good as the Real. The perception of the radical incommensurability between good and evil therefore does not lead to indifference in the face of evil, but to a more existentially compelling orientation toward the good. In the Imam's perspective, the notions of 'right' and 'rightful due' that are comprised within the meaning of haqq, حق, are infused with an aura of absoluteness: understanding what is right leads to the commitment to fulfil one's obligations, while what is rightfully due is the restoration of reality through the elimination of illusory evil, and both are equally infused with reality, which is justice. The moral notions of rectitude and propriety are expressions of a principle that transcends morality, a principle pertaining to ultimate reality. One's commitment to fulfilling one's obligations, to giving every being its rightful due, becomes infused, likewise, with a powerful charge of reality; the resolution to be just is rendered more absolute by the very quality of absoluteness proper to the Real.

This duty to give what is due to each and every being relates to the most explicit definition of justice given by the Imam, وَضَعَ الشَّئُی حَقَّ مَحَلِّہِ, 'Justice puts everything in its right place.' One is 'just' insofar as one gives everything its proper due, renders the haqq owed to each person, indeed to each and every thing in existence; and the ability to be 'just' in this demanding and universal sense is enhanced in proportion to one's attunement to al-Haqq, the Real. As the previous chapter makes clear, this spiritual conception of virtue not only encompasses one's duty to all, but engages all that one is. One has to be good before one can do good; and the only way 'to be' is to be at one with the Real, that which alone truly 'is'.

The Imam's comment on justice evokes the more famous definition of Plato: '...we have laid down, as a universal principle, that everyone ought to perform the one function in the community for which his nature best suited him... that principle, or some form of it, is justice.' There are indeed many remarkable parallels between Plato's *Republic* and the Imam's letter to Malik al-Ashtar. Some of the more obvious correspondences deserve attention, together with the way in which the Imam's explicit spirituality illuminates what remains implicit in Plato's philosophy, namely, how one can actually attain the vision of the Real, which Plato calls 'gazing upon the Good'. It is this contemplation of the Good as such that functions as the source of one's capacity to act ethically at every level, for it allows one to take the Good 'as a pattern for the right ordering of the state and of the individual'.

The influence of Platonic and Neoplatonic conceptions of justice on the formulation of ethical discourse in Islam has been amply attested in the sources. Within this discourse, justice is not seen as one virtue among several, but as the perfection or sum of all the other virtues, especially the three principal Platonic virtues of temperance, courage, and wisdom, which must characterize, respectively, the concupiscent, the irascible, and the rational dimensions of the soul. When all the dimensions of the soul, each 'filled out' by its proper virtue, are in their right place, the soul may be called 'just'.

What has not been given sufficient attention, however, is the degree to which there exists a spiritual correspondence between Plato's chief means of arriving at the vision of the Good, anamnesis, and the cardinal role of dhikr (ذکر) in Islam, both terms being translatable by the English word "remembrance." And how is this justice achieved? What unlocks its power is precisely this remembrance, this recollection of ultimate reality and of immutable principles at their different levels of application. It is not a matter of learning things anew, but of recollecting principles that are inherent in the very substance of the intellect, principles by which all things can be understood, and which are ultimately rooted in the Sovereign Good itself. Such remembrance is the cognitive and philosophical foundation of ethical thought. That its importance has been largely ignored is symptomatic of the severing of ethics from spirituality in modern discourse, which treats each discipline as though it were hermetically sealed from the other. Whereas ethics is still taken seriously as a branch of philosophy and as a discipline in its own right, spirituality is all too often dismissed as irrelevant to serious scholarly inquiry and relegated to the realm of the imaginary, the vague, and the mythological. This marginalization of spirituality from putatively serious discourse is closely related to the reductive view of knowledge that has increasingly characterized Western thought since the Renaissance, a reduction that goes hand in hand with the progressive emptying out of the sacred dimension of life.

In the Islamic tradition, the realms of ethics and spirituality are intimately connected. Nowhere is this more apparent than in the Sufi tradition, which is increasingly and most aptly called the ihsani tradition, understanding the word ihsan (احسان: excellence, virtue, goodness) in the sense the Prophet gave it in a tradition of central importance concerning the three fundamental aspects of the Islamic way. It is known as "the hadith of Gabriel," for the questioner in this exchange, unbeknown to the Companions who were present, was the angel Gabriel (جبرئیل) in human form. He approached the Prophet as he sat among his Companions and said:

'O Muhammad, tell me about submission (al-islam),' يَا مُحَمَّدُ، أخْبِرْنِي عَنْ الإسْلاَمِ. The Prophet replied: الإِسْلاَمُ أَنْ تَشْهَدَ أَنْ لاَ إِلهَ إِلاَ اللهُ وَأَنَّ مُحَمَّداً رَسُولُ اللهِ، وَتُقِيمَ الصَّلاَةَ، وَتُؤْتِيَ الزَّكَاةَ، وَتَصُومَ رَمَضَانَ، وَتَحُجَّ الْبَيتَ إِن اسْتَطَعْتَ إِلَيْهِ سَبِيلاً, 'Al-islam is to testify that there is no god but God and Muhammad is the messenger of God, to perform the prayers, to pay the poor-due (al-zakat), to fast in Ramadan, and to make the pilgrimage to the House if you are able to do so.' 'You have spoken truly (صَدَقْتَ),' Gabriel said. The Companions, who saw only a man and did not know he was an angel, were astonished that he should question the Prophet and then declare that he had spoken truly. 'Then tell me about faith (al-iman),' he said, فَأَخْبِرْنِي عَنِ الإِيْمَانِ. The Prophet said: أَنْ تُؤْمِنَ بِاللهِ، وَمَلاَئِكَتِهِ، وَكُتُبِهِ، وَرُسُلِهِ، وَالْيَوْمِ الآخِرِ، وَتُؤْمِنَ بِالْقَدَرِ خَيْرِهِ وَشَرِّهِ, 'It is to believe in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, and the Last Day, and to believe in divine destiny, both the good and the evil thereof.' 'You have spoken truly (صَدَقْتَ),' he said. 'Then tell me about virtue (al-ihsan),' he said, فَأَخْبِرْنِي عَنِ الإِحْسَانِ. He said: أَنْ تَعْبُدَ اللهَ كَأَنَّكَ تَرَاهُ، فَإِنْ لَمْ تَكُنْ تَرَاهُ فَإِنَّهُ يَرَاكَ, 'It is to worship God as though you see Him, and if you see Him not, yet truly He sees you.'

The word ihsan can also be literally translated as "doing what is beautiful," its root (حَسَنَ) being related primarily to beauty, husn; and virtue and vice are often referred to in Islamic ethical discourse as husn and qubh (قبح), literally beauty and ugliness. Ultimately it is the vision of divine beauty that inspires virtue, which is beauty of soul, just as it is departure from the divine qualities that leaves the soul ugly (قبیح). It is therefore unsurprising that, broadly speaking, while the specialists in the domain of outward action according to Islam have been the jurists, the fuqaha', and the specialists in the domain of formal belief have been the theologians, it fell to the mystics to be the custodians of spiritual virtue, of "making beautiful." Indeed, it is no coincidence that the overwhelming majority of artists in the Islamic world, calligraphers, painters, poets, musicians, and the rest, have been practising Sufis.

From the Prophet's definition of ihsan as "worshipping God as if one could see Him," the crucial relationship between worship and virtue stands clear, between spirituality and ethics, between devotion to the Creator and goodness toward creatures: one cannot have the one without the other. This relationship lies at the very heart of the spiritual tradition in Islam. Conversely, it is the denial and subversion of this tradition by some modern Muslims that, more than any other single factor, is ultimately responsible for the atrocities currently being committed by Muslims in the name of Islam.

Both the theory and the practice of ethics have always been integral to Sufism. Even some of those who wrote treatises in more philosophical and less explicitly spiritual terms, such as al-Farabi, were known also to be practising Sufis. The most influential figure in whom the domains of spirituality and ethics meet is, of course, al-Ghazali, whose magisterial *Ihya' 'ulum al-din* (Revival of the Sciences of Religion) stands as a monument to the impact of Islam's deepest spirit upon the ethical disposition of the believing soul. He has aptly been called the greatest thinker on ethics in Islam; and given how thoroughly his thinking is shaped by Sufism, this alone bears testimony to the close relationship between Sufism and ethics. It would not be an exaggeration to say that the majority of treatises written by Sufis are, in fact, treatises on "ethics," if ethics is understood not merely as akhlaq in the narrow sense of the term, but also as the fruit of ihsan, or spiritual virtue. The various spiritual stations (maqamat) described by the Sufis frequently bear the names of particular virtues, such as patience (sabr), gratitude (shukr), contentment (rida'), generosity (karam), and so on.

The thread running through all these stations is a transformative awareness of God, the principle of being and acting "as if you could see God," that is, conducting and composing oneself in the consciousness of being constantly in the divine presence, for even if one cannot see Him, "He sees you." The imperative of ihsan thus lies at the very root of a life of virtue in the spiritual universe of Islam. In his sermons in the نھج البلاغۃ, Ali (ع) repeatedly draws the reader back to the "vision" of God, at whatever degree, as the true basis of a life of virtue, and repeatedly warns of the dangers of its opposite, the engrossing vision of oneself, as the source of all vice. On one hand, the Imam says, "Know that your every action is dependent upon your prayer," that is, upon one's way of "gazing upon the Good," to use Platonic terms; on the other, he warns against vanity, conceit, and the lavish praise of oneself by others, for these are Satan's (شیطان) most reliable opportunities to efface the virtue, the ihsan, of the virtuous (المُحسنین). The true, objective vision of al-Haqq (الحق), the Real, realized through the effacement of oneself in prayer, for in the prostration (سجدۃ) of prayer one effaces one's own ego, one's nafs (نفس), is here juxtaposed with the distorted, subjective vision of the ego produced by the inflation of pride, which is precisely what Iblis (ابلیس) desires. The first leads to justice, putting all things in their proper place, including, most fundamentally, the ego itself; the second leads to all the injustices that flow from self-aggrandizement, including fanaticism and bigotry, for Satan, the epitome of pride and disobedience, is described as "the leader of the fanatics and the forerunner of the proud" (امام المتعصبین و سلف المستکبرین, imam al-muta'assibin wa salaf al-mustakbirin). Bigotry is an intolerance toward those who hold different opinions; and fanatical bigotry is a particularly intense form of injustice, for it entails that pernicious prejudice which subordinates the objective requirements of justice to the putative prerogatives of one's self, whether that self is defined in terms of the individual, the family, the clan, the tribe, the nation, or the religion.

The Imam's epistle appointing Malik al-Ashtar as his governor of Egypt appears as letter 52 in most editions of the نھج البلاغۃ. The letter has served as a source of inspiration across the centuries, read as an ideal constitution for Islamic governance. Through its relatively detailed description of the duties and rights of the ruler, the various functionaries of the state, and the main classes of society, it complements the more general framework of principles enshrined in the famous "Constitution of Medina" dictated by the Prophet. The advice contained in the letter, however, far exceeds the parameters conventionally set by political or legal texts on governance. Although addressed in the first instance to the ruler or governor of the polity, much of the advice in fact pertains to universally applicable ethical principles and is therefore as relevant to the governed as to those who govern. The letter is as much a statement of ethics as of politics, not a discursive "system" or theory of ethics, to be sure, but an inspiring expression of the spiritual ethos that the Imam embodied and radiated, an ethos flowing directly from the sources of the Islamic revelation, and one that therefore discloses the roots of virtue and ethics within a climate governed by the all-embracing principle of revelation. It is possible, accordingly, to read this document also as a commentary on that revelation, one that in turn encourages the reader to delve more deeply into the meanings enshrined in the revealed texts. Malik al-Ashtar was a long-standing and dedicated follower of the Imam and was referred to as one of his "right-hand" men. Mu'awiya, the Imam's main antagonist in the civil war that dominated his caliphate, was among those who used this term, notably after hearing of the success of his mission to have Malik poisoned before he could take up his post as governor of Egypt. Mu'awiya is reported by Tabari as remarking: "Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع) had two right hands. One of them was cut off at Siffin, and the other today." For his part, the Imam's response to the news of Malik's assassination was as follows: "Malik, and what was Malik! By God, were he a mountain, he would be the solitary one soaring above all others, and were he a rock, he would have been impenetrably solid. No horse could ascend such a mountain, no bird could fly up to it."

The Imam's definition of justice stands at the center of what follows: "Justice puts everything in its right place." In everyday speech, justice means little more than fairness, equality, and fair play, a serviceable but essentially formal account of the word. The conception at work here is of an altogether different order: it aligns justice with the sacred, with القدس, the All-Holy, and locates the very roots of justice within the divine nature itself. What connects this conception of justice to the sacred is the imperative of first putting one's relationship with God in its right place; everything else derives from this spiritual imperative. The same order of precedence is clearly established in an important statement in the Imam's letter to Malik: "Be just with God and be just with people [giving them what is their due] from yourself." Being just with God means, among other things, conforming as best one can to His own nature, which is not merely the source of justice but is justice. And the emphasis falls on that word is: justice is not simply one attribute among His many attributes, standing alongside His mercy, His wrath, and His love; it is the very essence of His nature, so that all His other qualities flow from it. His mercy is an expression of His justice, and so too are His anger, His love, His creating, and His destroying. "I bear witness," says the Imam in one of his sermons, "that He is Justice ('adl) and He acts justly." It might be objected that between the divine reality of justice and its human expression there is a radical disjuncture. In God, justice means that everything is in its right place; for man, justice entails the effort to put everything in its right place, an immutable metaphysical quality on the divine side, and a dynamic, volitional effort on the human side. One response to this objection is as follows: man's moral effort to act justly in a world where things are not in their right place is rooted in an innate spiritual predisposition toward justice. Just action thus expresses the principle of justice, which, in man as in God, is that immutable principle by which everything is in its right place. Just as divine action in creation, judgement, and other domains is an expression of absolute justice, so in man just action expresses not only a moral, volitional, and intellectual effort to realize justice, but also a spiritual affinity with the ultimate nature of reality, with things as they truly are in God, and therefore as they ought to be here on earth. Applied to state and society, one of the most obvious consequences of this conception of justice is the necessity of piety and rectitude on the part of rulers. As the Imam says in another sermon: "The most momentous of the reciprocal rights that God has made obligatory is the right of the ruler over the subjects, and the right of the subjects over the ruler, a reciprocal obligation which God has ordained for them." He then makes a point altogether indispensable to any discourse on good governance in Islam: "The subjects will not be righteous except through the righteousness of the rulers, and the rulers will not be righteous except through the uprightness of the subjects. So if the subjects render to the ruler his rightful due and he renders them theirs, then will right be honoured among them, the ways of religion will be upheld, and the banners of justice will be erected." This insistence on the piety of the ruler is, as one would expect, a central aspect of the Imam's message to his governor; but it involves far more than a simple exhortation to abide by the formal rules of Islamic law.

The whole of the Imam's life can be seen as a quest to conform as perfectly as possible to the divine nature, and to this quality of justice in particular. There are far too many dimensions of this principle, social, economic, political, juridical, as they appear in the Imam's life and teachings to address within a brief compass. One defining moment in his caliphate illustrates the rigour with which he adhered to justice at whatever cost. The revolt of Mu'awiya, and the civil war into which the fledgling Muslim empire was thereby plunged, can be seen as having begun not at Siffin but in Medina. Upon assuming the caliphate, Ali (ع) was advised by his cousin Ibn al-'Abbas to temporarily confirm all of 'Uthman's governors in their posts, and then, once he had consolidated the new order, to remove them. The Imam's reply to this proposed strategy was reported as follows: "I do not doubt that this would be best for the sake of reconciliation in this world. But there is my obligation to the Truth (alladhī yalzamanī min al-haqq) and my knowledge of 'Uthman's governors, so, by God, I shall never appoint one of them."

Thus was the die cast: the Imam dismissed Mu'awiya along with all of his predecessor's governors. It was clear to the Imam that Ibn al-'Abbas' proposed solution had every chance of success; he knew that all Mu'awiya wanted was worldly power and that Mu'awiya would have been content to govern Syria had the Imam allowed it. While commentators have cited this incident, which made the revolt of the governors all but inevitable, as evidence of Ali (ع)'s lack of political acumen and his intransigence, it ought instead to be understood as an expression of his disdain for worldly ease when that ease must be purchased at the price of essential principles. As he says in one of his aphorisms: "People renounce no part of their religion for the sake of rectifying their world without God opening them up to something worse than it," that is, worse than the very defect they were seeking to rectify.

It is clear that confirming in power those governors whose corruption was beyond both doubt and redemption would have amounted, for the Imam, to "renouncing" a part of his religion, for religion without justice is not religion. Supporting unjust governors, even as a tactical manoeuvre, constituted a compromise of religious principle that was simply inconceivable to him. This attitude was embodied in countless episodes of the Imam's life, all of which demonstrate that the principles enshrined in the letter to Malik al-Ashtar were far from merely theoretical.

What follows examines a particular dimension of justice, one that is political on the surface, ethical in its outward expression, but spiritual in its depth: the justice of the political ruler, understood in relation to its most clearly definable opposite, which is injustice, tyranny, oppression, and corruption. A deep assimilation of the Imam's spirituality can bestow an existential power upon the moral effort to live justly, a power that is absent when justice is conceived exclusively in terms of individual or horizontal values, that is, in terms of a secular moral philosophy divorced from religious or spiritual principles. The crucial role of devotion and worship, both formal and supra-formal, belongs to this same inquiry. Contemplation of the divine Reality is the fountainhead from which integral virtue flows spontaneously and ceaselessly, a point sharpened by comparison with Plato's "gazing" on the Sovereign Good as the source of all virtue.

The inquiry that follows takes its bearings from a single document above all others: the letter of Ali (ع) to Malik al-Ashtar. It is there that a sacred conception of justice is set out with the greatest fullness, and it is from its key passages that the present analysis draws.

Around that letter stand the Imam's other writings. His further letters, his sermons, and his sayings are gathered here as they bear upon the same theme, extending and deepening what the letter to Malik lays down. Justice, treated in this way, is not read out of one text in isolation but heard across the whole body of his counsel.

Beyond the words of Ali (ع) himself, the analysis turns to certain traditions of the Prophet and to verses of the Qur'an. These are brought forward not as ornament but as illumination, for they cast light on the very themes the letter raises and disclose the ground on which its conception of justice rests.
