# A Sacred Conception of Justice

A Sacred Conception of Justice

The reflections on justice that follow are not an exercise in theoretical speculation about what justice means in Ali (ع)'s letter to Malik al-Ashtar, written fourteen centuries ago. Nor are they the kind of definition found in a dictionary, where justice amounts to little more than fairness, equality, and fair play. The aim is, first of all, to bring the discussion of justice into alignment with the concept and the reality of the sacred — the reality of the sacred One, القدس. On one side, the principle of justice carries a sacred and spiritual dimension that contemporary discussion so often overlooks. On the other, a careful appreciation of what the sacred means opens a deeper understanding of the roots of justice within the divine nature itself, and a fuller awareness of why justice is necessary, both in the world and in the soul.

The central argument is this. In Ali 's worldview, the ethical orientation toward justice is immeasurably deepened once it is consciously tied to the spiritual precepts of the Islamic faith. The spirit that underlies moral rules and ethical values is strengthened by a commitment to transcendent principles — even though, or precisely because, those principles rise above the realm of action in which the rules themselves operate. The شریعۃ may be pictured as having a body and a spirit, much as a person does. Its body is made up of actions: prayer, fasting, the paying of زکوۃ. But the spirit that animates those actions rests on the nature of the divine, which is justice. To adhere to the شریعۃ is therefore to train in becoming like the divine through the principles of justice, and so action must be joined by contemplation of the divine. Here contemplation and action complete each other rather than conflict, for their union lies at the very heart of the Islamic message of تَوْحِيد, توحید — an integrating oneness that makes theory and practice one, so that a person becomes a true موحد. This unity shines with dazzling clarity in the life of the Imam, as it does in the life of the Prophet. The harmony between the two principles — contemplation and action, being and doing, theory and practice, ideal and reality — runs throughout the remarkable document that is the subject of these reflections.

These reflections stand against the background of contemporary ethical discourse and may be seen as a modest contribution, from an Islamic perspective, to the scholarly debates on virtue and ethics. In particular, they should be understood in light of one of the most serious and far-reaching critiques of modern and postmodern conceptions of virtue in general, and of justice in particular — that of Alasdair MacIntyre. Put simply, his critique holds that for several centuries ethical discourse in Western society has drifted far from its moorings in absolute values. He argues, compellingly, that debates about morality and ethics, together with their philosophical foundations, are no longer grounded in any conception of 'the good life' or of 'the Good' as such. As a result, MacIntyre holds, various shades of relativism, nihilism, utilitarianism, and opportunism have emerged in practice as the shifting bases on which ethical values are formed, if they are formed at all. Relativism holds that knowledge, truth, and morality are not absolute but relative, existing only in relation to a culture, a society, or a historical moment. Nihilism rejects all religious and moral principles and takes life to be essentially meaningless. Utilitarianism counts an action right only if it is useful or benefits a majority, so that good and bad rest on nothing more than a calculation of greater benefit or loss. And opportunism seizes whatever advantage circumstances offer, heedless of any plan or principle — the way of the manipulator, who flatters, performs, or deceives to get what he wants.

Whatever value these human-made theories occasionally yield, none of it is rooted in the divine; and so, even where they happen to mirror the values of faith, they remain baseless, without spirit — dead corpses. Virtue practiced on an ad hoc basis is not virtue properly speaking. It is a series of pragmatic adjustments to changing circumstances, made in the absence of any genuine principle of virtue. Truthfulness illustrates this. The underlying reason for always speaking the truth is twofold: that truth is the very nature of our Creator — الحق, the absolute truth — and that our purpose in this world is to take on the virtues of our Creator. If one resolves to speak the truth without grasping that reason, truthfulness will in the end become situational and self-serving, something to be compromised whenever circumstances seem to warrant it, spoken when it profits and abandoned when it costs. But once truth is seen as the nature of the divine, it becomes everlasting and eternal, indifferent to consequence, because one speaks it in emulation of the immortal nature of God. The virtue itself becomes immortal, and the need to practice it on an ad hoc basis falls away. 'We are looking,' writes MacIntyre, 'for a conception of the good which will enable us to order other goods, for a conception of the good which will enable us to extend our understanding of the purpose and content of the virtues, for a conception of the good which will enable us to understand the place of integrity and consistency in life ...'

It is at this level, in particular, that the insights of Ali and other sages of the Islamic tradition are especially relevant. MacIntyre's proposed solution to the crisis — a reformulation of an Aristotelian conception of ethics grounded in a Thomistic worldview — shares a great deal with the Islamic tradition of ethical discourse (أَخْلَاق). But one can argue further: a careful engagement with the Imam's legacy yields far more than a merely theoretical approach to the question of justice, and this in two respects. First, the Imam was himself a paragon of justice, and thus a living embodiment of this and other fundamental virtues. In his life one sees a perfect, indeed unflinching, adherence to this cardinal virtue — a life governed not by his نفس but entirely by the divine nature of God. The remarkable deeds recorded of him, at which one marvels, are the fruit of his irresistible attraction toward the nature of الحق, for his whole existence was oriented toward God. His attention rested never on the action itself but on the source of the principles that generated it; fixing upon the beauty of the divine nature and striving to take it on, he found good actions arising from him naturally and inevitably. Although this work does not enter into many details of his life and its lessons, certain incidents and events that help illustrate the point will be recalled. Secondly — and more important for the present purpose — the discourses, letters, and sayings attributed to Ali offer a rich source of reflection on the dynamic spirituality that lies at the root of a life of virtue. In them one is granted a glimpse of a way of life wholly governed by the sacred, one in which the desire for the 'good life' emerges as the fruit of an irresistible attraction toward the Good as such, which is identical to the absolutely Real, اَلْحَقّ.

In other words, one is ethically inspired by 'the Good' in direct proportion to one's existential orientation toward the Real. If moral rules rest on ethical perspectives, and those perspectives rest in turn on a conception of the 'good life', then that conception of the Good is itself drawn from the moral agent's fundamental understanding of what life means. Amyn B. Sajoo, in a recent thoughtful work on Muslim ethics, draws a careful distinction between ethics and morals. Ethical discourse, he argues, is concerned more with conceptions of 'the good', while morals pertain more specifically to rules about right and wrong. Ethics deals with the soul and the spirit of the good, the باطن, and out of ethics morals are born. 'Certainly,' he continues, 'the concepts overlap substantially in theory and practice; but it is well to bear in mind the more encompassing sense of reality that typifies ethics.' To set ethics within the wider framework of spiritual virtue — in the sense of احسان, احسان — widens the compass of both ethics and morality still further, lending each a dimension of existential depth and grounding both in a more organic and all-encompassing whole. For the spiritual life has three components: Islam, which supplies the outer aspects of morality; ایمان, which is the framework of ethics; and the union of the two, serving as their underlying principle, which issues in احسان — the good life, the beautiful life. Abstract questions of right and wrong thus come to rest on the more definitive principle that emerges in answer to the question: what is reality?

The previous chapter's discussion of the deeper meanings of divine unity bears on this. In the Imam's spiritual vision of تَوْحِيد, one perceives, according to one's capacity, an ultimate reality that not only 'has no second' but also has no moral 'opposite'. People tend to picture the world as good against evil, God against شیطان, forever thinking in opposites; yet the Imam teaches that God has no opposite at all. Satan does not stand against God, for nothing has the power to stand against God. He stands against God's creation, and his whole effort is to drive people away from their inherent divine nature by making its opposite beautiful — beautifying arrogance where God's nature is modest, lewdness where it is decent, boastfulness where it is humble. For the goodness proper to God is not the moral opposite of evil. It occupies an altogether different plane, that of اَلْحَقّ, الحق, the absolutely Real, whose only 'opposite' is unreality, or nothingness. Goodness is at one with the pure positivity of the Absolute, while evil arises solely from a negative capacity to negate the Real, the Good. The essence of all goodness and beauty, in other words, is identified with the Creator, whereas evil is a mode of the created — the corrupted state of a creation that has deviated from the divine nature. This is strongly implied in the Quranic verse of سورۃ الفلق, which calls the believer to seek refuge in God from 'the evil of that which He created' (Al-Falaq: 2):

> قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ ٱلْفَلَقِ ۱ مِن شَرِّ مَا خَلَقَ ۲
>
> 'Say: I seek refuge with the Lord of the daybreak, from the evil of what He has created.'

This verse is often mistranslated as 'from the evil which He created', which is wrong both theologically and lexically. The expression 'min sharri ma khalaqa', مِن شَرِّ مَا خَلَقَ, plainly refers to the evil that derives from creation, not to creation as such. In theological terms, God creates morally free agents, and it is they who are the authors of evil in creation; when creation, by its own free will, chooses to negate the divine nature of its Creator, evil is born. God may be said to 'create' all actions only in the metaphysical sense that He is the source of all being, and so of all modes within being, of which action is one; He cannot be said to have created evil as such, but only the conditions within which evil arises. Evil is thus the negation of the Good, which is at one with the Real, and as such it has no ultimate ontological principle. It is only a temporary state of deviation from that pure and divine nature — an illusion witnessed by the 'eye of the heart'.

The verse, then, may be understood as a call to seek refuge in the Real from the unreal, since between goodness and evil there is no common measure in being. Goodness can be opposed only by the appearance of evil — an appearance the 'eye of the heart' grasps as illusion, that eye which truly 'sees' God through 'the verities of faith'. This vision of the merely relative reality of evil, far from breeding indifference toward it, greatly strengthens the spiritual resolve to overcome it, for the moral effort to be good intensifies in proportion to one's conviction of accountability to that goodness. That conviction is in turn carried inward, from the mind to the heart, by the certainty that goodness is 'more real' than evil — a certainty itself nourished by the spiritual vision of the good as the Real. To perceive the radical incommensurability between good and evil, therefore, leads not to indifference in the face of evil but to a more existentially compelling orientation toward the good. In the Imam's perspective, the notions of 'right' and 'rightful due' gathered within the meaning of haqq, حق, are charged with an aura of absoluteness. To understand what is right leads to the commitment to fulfill one's obligations; what is rightfully due is the restoration of reality through the removal of illusory evil; and both are equally infused with reality, which is justice. The moral notions of rectitude and propriety are expressions of a principle that transcends morality, a principle belonging to ultimate reality. One's commitment to fulfilling one's obligations — to giving every being its rightful due — becomes charged, likewise, with a powerful sense of reality, and the resolve to be just is made more absolute by the very absoluteness proper to the Real.

This duty to give each and every being its due connects to the most explicit definition of justice the Imam gives: وَضَعَ الشَّئُی حَقَّ مَحَلِّہِ, 'Justice puts everything in its right place.' One is 'just' in so far as one gives everything its proper due, rendering the haqq owed to each person — indeed to each and every thing in existence; and the capacity to be 'just' in this demanding, universal sense grows in proportion to one's attunement to اَلْحَقّ, the Real. As the previous chapter makes clear, this spiritual conception of virtue not only takes in one's duty to all but engages all that one is. One must be good before one can do good, and the only way 'to be' is to be at one with the Real — that which alone truly 'is'.

The Imam's remark on justice calls to mind the more famous definition of Plato: ...we have laid down, as a universal principle, that everyone ought to perform the one function in the community for which his nature best suited him... that principle, or some form of it, is justice. There are indeed many remarkable parallels between Plato's *Republic* and the Imam's letter to Malik al-Ashtar. Some of the more obvious correspondences deserve attention, along with the way the Imam's explicit spirituality illuminates what stays implicit in Plato's philosophy: namely, how one may actually attain the vision of the Real, which Plato calls 'gazing upon the Good'. It is this contemplation of the Good as such that serves as the source of one's capacity to act ethically at every level, for it lets one take the Good 'as a pattern for the right ordering of the state and of the individual'.

Platonic and Neoplatonic ideas of justice have left a well-documented mark on the way ethics came to be discussed in Islam. In that discussion, justice is not one virtue standing beside others; it is the perfection, the sum, of all the rest — above all of the three chief Platonic virtues: temperance, courage, and wisdom. These belong, in turn, to the appetitive, the spirited, and the rational parts of the soul. When every part of the soul is filled out by its proper virtue and stands in its right place, the soul may be called "just."

What has not received enough attention is how closely Plato's chief means of reaching the vision of the Good — anamnesis — corresponds, at the spiritual level, to the central place of dhikr in Islam. Both words can be rendered in English as "remembrance." And how is this justice achieved? What releases its power is precisely this remembrance: the recollection of ultimate reality and of unchanging principles as they apply at their several levels. It is not a matter of learning something new, but of calling back to mind principles already present in the very substance of the intellect — principles by which all things can be understood, and which are rooted, finally, in the Sovereign Good itself. Such remembrance is the ground on which ethical thought rests, both as knowledge and as reasoning. That its importance has been largely overlooked is a symptom of the modern habit of cutting ethics off from spirituality, treating each as though it were sealed away from the other. Ethics is still taken seriously, as a branch of philosophy and a discipline in its own right; spirituality, by contrast, is too often waved aside as beside the point in serious scholarship and consigned to the realm of the imaginary, the vague, and the mythological. This pushing of spirituality to the margins of supposedly serious discourse is closely bound up with the shrunken view of knowledge that has increasingly marked Western thought since the Renaissance — a narrowing that goes hand in hand with the steady draining away of the sacred dimension of life.

In the Islamic tradition, ethics and spirituality are closely bound together. This shows nowhere more clearly than in the Sufi tradition, which is now often, and rightly, called the ihsani tradition. The word احسان is taken here in the sense the Prophet gave it in a saying of central importance about the three foundations of the Islamic way. That saying is known as "the hadith of Gabriel," because the man who put the questions — though the Companions present did not know it — was the angel Gabriel (جبرئیل) in human form. He came up to the Prophet as he sat among his Companions and said:

'O Muhammad, tell me about submission (الْإِسْلَام),' يَا Muhammad، أخْبِرْنِي عَنْ الإسْلاَمِ.

The Prophet replied: الإِسْلاَمُ أَنْ تَشْهَدَ أَنْ لاَ إِلهَ إِلاَ اللهُ وَأَنَّ Muhammadاً رَسُولُ اللهِ، وَتُقِيمَ الصَّلاَةَ، وَتُؤْتِيَ al-zakat، وَتَصُومَ رَمَضَانَ، وَتَحُجَّ الْبَيتَ إِن اسْتَطَعْتَ إِلَيْهِ سَبِيلاً, 'الْإِسْلَام is to testify that there is no god but God and Muhammad is the messenger of God, to perform the prayers, to pay the poor-due (الزَّكَاةَ), to fast in Ramadan, and to make the pilgrimage to the House if you are able to do so.'

'You have spoken truly (صَدَقْتَ),' Gabriel said. The Companions, who saw only a man and did not know he was an angel, were astonished that he should question the Prophet and then declare that he had spoken truly.

'Then tell me about faith (الإِيْمَانِ),' he said, فَأَخْبِرْنِي عَنِ الإِيْمَانِ.

The Prophet said: أَنْ تُؤْمِنَ بِاللهِ، وَمَلاَئِكَتِهِ، وَكُتُبِهِ، وَرُسُلِهِ، وَالْيَوْمِ الآخِرِ، وَتُؤْمِنَ بِالْقَدَرِ خَيْرِهِ وَشَرِّهِ, 'It is to believe in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, and the Last Day, and to believe in divine destiny, both the good and the evil thereof.'

'You have spoken truly (صَدَقْتَ),' he said. 'Then tell me about virtue (الإِحْسَانِ),' he said, فَأَخْبِرْنِي عَنِ الإِحْسَانِ.

He said: أَنْ تَعْبُدَ اللهَ كَأَنَّكَ تَرَاهُ، فَإِنْ لَمْ تَكُنْ تَرَاهُ فَإِنَّهُ يَرَاكَ, 'It is to worship God as though you see Him, and if you see Him not, yet truly He sees you.'

The word احسان can also be translated literally as "doing what is beautiful," since its root (حَسَنَ) is bound up first of all with beauty, حَسَنَ. In Islamic ethical discourse, virtue and vice are often called حَسَنَ and qubh (قُبْح) — beauty and ugliness. In the end it is the vision of divine beauty that awakens virtue, which is beauty of soul, just as turning away from the divine qualities is what leaves the soul ugly (قُبْح). It is no surprise, then, that — broadly speaking — while the jurists, the فُقَهَاءُ, have been the specialists in outward action, and the theologians the specialists in formal belief, it fell to the mystics to be the guardians of spiritual virtue, of "making beautiful." And it is no accident that the great majority of artists in the Islamic world — calligraphers, painters, poets, musicians, and the rest — have been practicing Sufis.

The Prophet's definition of احسان as "worshipping God as if one could see Him" makes plain the crucial bond between worship and virtue — between spirituality and ethics, between devotion to the Creator and goodness toward His creatures. One cannot be had without the other. This bond lies at the very heart of the spiritual tradition in Islam. And it is the denial and betrayal of that tradition by some modern Muslims that, more than any other single cause, lies behind the atrocities now being committed by Muslims in the name of Islam.

Both the theory and the practice of ethics have always been part of the very fabric of Sufism. Even some who wrote in more philosophical and less openly spiritual terms — al-Farabi among them — were known to be practicing Sufis as well. The figure in whom spirituality and ethics meet most powerfully is, of course, al-Ghazali, whose great Revival of the Sciences of Religion stands as a monument to what Islam's deepest spirit does to the ethical temper of the believing soul. He has rightly been called the greatest thinker on ethics in Islam; and since his thought is shaped through and through by Sufism, this by itself testifies to how closely Sufism and ethics are joined. It would be no exaggeration to say that most treatises written by Sufis are in fact treatises on "ethics" — provided ethics is understood not only as أَخْلَاق in the narrow sense, but also as the fruit of احسان, of spiritual virtue. The various spiritual stations (مَقَامَات) the Sufis describe often bear the names of particular virtues: patience (صَبْرٌ), gratitude (شُكْرٌ), contentment (رِضَاءٌ), generosity (كَرَم), and so on.

The thread running through all these stations is a transforming awareness of God — the principle of being and acting "as if you could see God," of carrying and composing oneself in the constant awareness of standing in the divine presence, for even if one cannot see Him, "He sees you." The call of احسان thus lies at the very root of a life of virtue in the spiritual world of Islam. In his sermons in the نَھْجُ الْبَلَاغَۃِ, Ali turns his hearers again and again to the "vision" of God, in whatever measure, as the true ground of a virtuous life, and warns again and again against its opposite — the all-absorbing vision of oneself — as the source of every vice. On the one hand the Imam says, "Know that your every action is dependent upon your prayer," that is, upon one's way of "gazing upon the Good," to put it in Platonic terms. On the other, he warns against vanity, conceit, and the lavish praise of oneself by others, for these are Satan's (شَیْطَان) surest openings for effacing the virtue, the احسان, of the virtuous (المُحسنین). Set against each other here are two visions. The true, objective vision of اَلْحَقّ, the Real, is reached by effacing oneself in prayer, for in the prostration (سجدۃ) of prayer one effaces one's own ego, one's nafs (نَفْس). Opposed to it stands the distorted, self-centered vision of the ego, swollen by pride — which is exactly what Iblis (اِبْلِیس) desires. The first leads to justice, putting all things in their proper place, and, most fundamentally, the ego itself; the second leads to every injustice that flows from self-aggrandizement, fanaticism and bigotry among them. For Satan, the very embodiment of pride and disobedience, is described as "the leader of the fanatics and the forerunner of the proud" (امام المتعصبین و سلف المستکبرین, imam al-mutaassibin wa salaf al-mustakbirin). Bigotry is intolerance toward those who hold different opinions; and fanatical bigotry is an especially fierce form of injustice, for it carries that poisonous prejudice which bends the objective demands of justice to the supposed privileges of one's own self — whether that self is defined as the individual, the family, the clan, the tribe, the nation, or the religion.

The Imam's letter appointing Malik al-Ashtar as his governor of Egypt appears as letter 52 in most editions of the نَھْجُ الْبَلَاغَۃِ. Across the centuries it has served as a source of inspiration, read as an ideal constitution for Islamic government. In its fairly detailed account of the duties and rights of the ruler, of the various officers of the state, and of the main classes of society, it fills out the more general framework of principles set down in the famous "Constitution of Medina" that the Prophet dictated. Yet the counsel in the letter goes far beyond the bounds usually set by political or legal texts on government. Though it is addressed in the first place to the ruler or governor, much of its counsel in fact concerns ethical principles that hold everywhere, and so it speaks as much to the governed as to those who govern. The letter is as much a statement of ethics as of politics — not a worked-out "system" or theory of ethics, to be sure, but a moving expression of the spiritual ethos the Imam embodied and radiated. That ethos flows straight from the sources of the Islamic revelation, and so it lays bare the roots of virtue and ethics within a world governed by the all-embracing principle of revelation. The document can therefore be read as a commentary on that revelation as well — one that, in turn, draws its readers to look more deeply into the meanings held within the revealed texts. Malik al-Ashtar was a long-standing and devoted follower of the Imam, spoken of as one of his "right-hand" men. Muawiya, the Imam's chief opponent in the civil war that dominated his caliphate, was among those who used the phrase — notably on hearing that his plot to have Malik poisoned had succeeded before Malik could take up his post as governor of Egypt. Tabari reports Muawiya as saying: "Ali ibn Abi Talib had two right hands. One of them was cut off at Siffin, and the other today." The Imam, for his part, met the news of Malik's assassination with these words: "Malik, and what was Malik! By God, were he a mountain, he would be the solitary one soaring above all others, and were he a rock, he would have been impenetrably solid. No horse could ascend such a mountain, no bird could fly up to it."

The Imam's definition of justice stands at the center of what follows: "Justice puts everything in its right place." In everyday speech, justice means little more than fairness, equality, and fair play — a workable account, but a merely formal one. The idea at work here is of an altogether different order. It joins justice to the sacred, to القدس, the All-Holy, and finds the very roots of justice within the divine nature itself. What ties this conception of justice to the sacred is the demand that one first put one's relationship with God in its right place; everything else follows from that spiritual demand. The same order of priority is set out plainly in an important line of the Imam's letter to Malik: "Be just with God and be just with people [giving them what is their due] from yourself."

Being just with God means, among other things, conforming as far as one can to His own nature — which is not merely the source of justice but is justice. The weight falls on that word is. Justice is not one attribute among His many, ranged beside His mercy, His wrath, and His love; it is the very essence of His nature, and all His other qualities flow from it. His mercy is an expression of His justice, and so are His anger, His love, His creating, and His destroying. "I bear witness," says the Imam in one of his sermons, "that He is Justice (عَدْل) and He acts justly."

It might be objected that there is a deep gulf between justice as a divine reality and justice as a human act. In God, justice means that everything is in its right place; in man, justice means the effort to put everything in its right place — an unchanging metaphysical quality on the divine side, a living act of will on the human side. To this the reply is that man's moral effort to act justly, in a world where things are not in their right place, springs from an inborn spiritual leaning toward justice. Just action thus expresses the principle of justice, which, in man as in God, is that unchanging principle by which everything stands in its right place. As divine action in creation, judgment, and elsewhere expresses absolute justice, so in man just action expresses not only a moral, willed, and intellectual effort to bring justice about, but also a spiritual kinship with the ultimate nature of reality — with things as they truly are in God, and therefore as they ought to be here on earth.

Applied to state and society, one of the plainest consequences of this conception of justice is that rulers must be pious and upright. As the Imam says in another sermon: "The most momentous of the reciprocal rights that God has made obligatory is the right of the ruler over the subjects, and the right of the subjects over the ruler, a reciprocal obligation which God has ordained for them." He then makes a point wholly indispensable to any account of good governance in Islam: "The subjects will not be righteous except through the righteousness of the rulers, and the rulers will not be righteous except through the uprightness of the subjects. So if the subjects render to the ruler his rightful due and he renders them theirs, then will right be honored among them, the ways of religion will be upheld, and the banners of justice will be erected." This insistence on the ruler's piety is, as one would expect, central to the Imam's message to his governor; but it involves far more than a simple call to keep the formal rules of Islamic law.

The whole of the Imam's life can be seen as a quest to conform as perfectly as he could to the divine nature, and to this quality of justice above all. The principle has far too many sides — social, economic, political, legal — as they appear in his life and teachings to be treated in a short space. One defining moment in his caliphate shows the rigor with which he held to justice whatever it cost. The revolt of Muawiya, and the civil war into which the young Muslim empire was thereby plunged, can be said to have begun not at Siffin but in Medina. When Ali took up the caliphate, his cousin Ibn al-Abbas advised him to confirm all of 'Uthman's governors in their posts for the time being, and then, once the new order was secure, to remove them. The Imam's reply to this proposal was reported thus: "I do not doubt that this would be best for the sake of reconciliation in this world. But there is my obligation to the Truth (الَّذِي يَلْزَمُنِي مِنَ الْحَقِّ) and my knowledge of Uthman's governors, so, by God, I shall never appoint one of them."

And so the die was cast: the Imam dismissed Muawiya along with all of his predecessor's governors. The Imam saw clearly that Ibn al-Abbas' proposal had every chance of working; he knew that all Muawiya wanted was worldly power, and that Muawiya would have been content to govern Syria had the Imam allowed it. Commentators have taken this episode — which made the governors' revolt all but inevitable — as evidence of Ali 's want of political shrewdness and his stubbornness. It should rather be understood as an expression of his disdain for worldly ease when that ease can be bought only at the price of essential principles. As he says in one of his sayings: "People renounce no part of their religion for the sake of rectifying their world without God opening them up to something worse than it" — that is, worse than the very defect they were trying to set right.

Clearly, to keep in power governors whose corruption was beyond both doubt and remedy would have amounted, for the Imam, to "renouncing" a part of his religion — for religion without justice is not religion. To support unjust governors, even as a tactical move, was a compromise of religious principle simply unthinkable to him. This attitude ran through countless episodes of his life, all of which show that the principles set down in the letter to Malik al-Ashtar were far from merely theoretical.

What follows examines one particular dimension of justice — political on the surface, ethical in its outward expression, but spiritual in its depth: the justice of the political ruler, understood against its most clearly definable opposite, which is injustice, tyranny, oppression, and corruption. A deep taking-in of the Imam's spirituality can lend a real, existential power to the moral effort to live justly — a power that is missing when justice is conceived only in individual or horizontal terms, that is, as a secular moral philosophy cut off from religious or spiritual principles. The crucial place of devotion and worship, both formal and beyond-formal, belongs to this same inquiry. Contemplation of the divine Reality is the wellspring from which whole and undivided virtue flows freely and without end — a point brought into sharper relief by comparison with Plato's "gazing" upon the Sovereign Good as the source of all virtue.

The inquiry that follows takes its bearings from a single document above all others: the letter of Ali to Malik al-Ashtar. It is there that a sacred conception of justice is set out most fully, and it is from its key passages that the present analysis draws.

Around that letter stand the Imam's other writings. His further letters, his sermons, and his sayings are gathered here as they bear on the same theme, extending and deepening what the letter to Malik sets down. Treated in this way, justice is not read out of one text in isolation but heard across the whole body of his counsel.

Beyond the words of Ali himself, the analysis turns to certain traditions of the Prophet and to verses of the Quran. These are brought forward not as ornament but as light, for they illuminate the very themes the letter raises and lay bare the ground on which its conception of justice rests.

> **Editorial note (tradition-grounded).** 
> The chapter turns on *tawhid* — the "integrating oneness" that binds contemplation to action and
> makes one a *muwahhid*. A related teaching preserved in this tradition clarifies the word
> itself: it is built from a root meaning "to be one," and as a form-II noun it names not the mere
> counting of God as a single item but the deliberate *act* of declaring Him one — a oneness that
> admits no partner, division, or second. That grammatical shape is worth holding onto here,
> because it mirrors exactly what the chapter argues: unity is not something passively observed
> but something a person actively enacts, joining theory to practice until being and doing become
> one. Read this way, becoming a *muwahhid* is itself the work of justice the chapter describes —
> the willed alignment of the soul with the undivided nature of the divine.
