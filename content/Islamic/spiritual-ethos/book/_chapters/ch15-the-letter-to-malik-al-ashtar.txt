# The Letter to Malik al-Ashtar

The Imam wrote this letter to Malik al-Ashtar when he appointed him governor of Egypt and its regions, at a time when the rule of the previous governor, Muhammad b. Abi Bakr, had grown unstable. It is the longest of his letters, and the richest of them in both beauty of form and excellence of meaning.

In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful.

This is what the servant of God, Ali (ع), Commander of the Faithful, commands Malik b. al-Harith al-Ashtar in his mandate (عہد) of appointment, upon making him governor of Egypt: to collect its revenues, to fight its enemies, to seek the welfare of its people, and to bring prosperity to its lands.

He commands him to fear God, to place obedience to God before all else, and to hold fast to what He has commanded in His Book, both the obligatory acts and the recommended ones — for no one attains virtue except by following them, and no one becomes wretched except by denying and neglecting them. He commands him to assist God with his heart, his hand, and his tongue, for God, majestic is His name, has undertaken to grant victory to whoever assists Him and to exalt whoever glorifies Him. And he commands him to curb the passions of his soul and to hold it back when whim and caprice run high, for the soul incites to evil unless God shows mercy.

Know, Malik, that I am sending you to a land that has known governments before yours, some just and some unjust. The people will now weigh your conduct just as you once weighed the conduct of the rulers before you, and they will speak of you just as you spoke of them. The righteous are known only through what God causes to be said of them on the tongues of His servants. So let the treasure dearest to you be the treasure of good deeds. Master your desires, and hold yourself back from whatever is not lawful to you, for such restraint is the soul dealing fairly with itself, keeping it balanced between what it likes and what it dislikes.

Habituate your heart to mercy, love, and kindness toward your subjects. Do not stand over them like a ravening beast that counts them fair prey, for they are of two kinds: either your brother in religion or your equal in creation. Mistakes slip from them and faults befall them, whether by intention or by accident. So extend to them your forgiveness and your pardon, just as you would wish God to extend to you His forgiveness and pardon; for you stand above them, the one set in authority over you stands above you, and God stands above the one who appointed you. He has asked you to manage their affairs, and He is testing you through them.

Do not set yourself on a course of war with God, for you have no strength against His retribution and cannot do without His forgiveness and mercy. Never regret a pardon, and never rejoice in a punishment. Do not act in haste on any impulse when a way around it can still be found. Do not say, "I have been given authority, so I command and am obeyed," for that breeds corruption in the heart, weakens religion, and draws misfortune near. If the authority you hold breeds in you pride or vanity, look to the greatness of God's dominion above you and to His power over you — a power the like of which you do not hold even over yourself. This will settle your ambition, restrain your vehemence, and restore to you the good sense that had strayed. Beware of matching yourself with God in greatness or likening yourself to Him in might, for God humbles every tyrant and disgraces every braggart.

Be just with God and be just with people, granting them their due from yourself, from your close kin, and from those of your subjects for whom you feel fondness; for if you do not, you are an oppressor. Whoever oppresses the servants of God has God for his adversary, and not merely His servants; and whomever God opposes, He refutes his plea, and that man remains at war with God until he desists and repents. Nothing so surely hastens the withdrawal of God's grace or the coming of His retribution as persistence in oppression, for God hears the cry of the oppressed and lies in wait for the oppressors.

Let the dearest of your concerns be those most balanced in truth, most complete in justice, and most inclusive of your subjects' contentment; for the discontent of the common folk (الْعَامَّةُ) cancels the contentment of the elite (الْخَاصَّةُ), while the discontent of the elite is absorbed within the contentment of the common folk. In times of ease no one is a heavier burden to a ruler than the elite, and in hardship none less helpful; none is more averse to fair treatment, more insistent in asking for favors, less grateful when given them, slower to accept an excuse when refused, or weaker in patience under the blows of fortune. It is the common folk of the community who are the pillar of religion, the mass of the Muslims, and the defense against enemies; so let your inclination and your leaning be toward them.

Let those among your subjects who most eagerly hunt out the faults of others be the furthest from you and the most despised in your eyes, for people do have faults, and the ruler is the one most bound to conceal them. Do not expose what is hidden from you; your duty is only to correct what has become plain, and God will judge whatever remains hidden. Veil people's failings as much as you can, and God will veil from your subjects those failings of your own that you would wish kept concealed. Loosen from the people every knot of resentment, and cut away from your own heart the cord of every enmity. Turn a blind eye to whatever is unclear to you, and do not be quick to believe a slanderer, for a slanderer is a deceiver, however sincerely he may seem to give counsel.

Do not admit a miser into your counsel, for he will turn you away from generosity and frighten you with poverty; nor a coward, for he will make you feel too weak for your affairs; nor a greedy man, for he will dress up avidity as though it were justice. Miserliness, cowardice, and greed are separate traits, yet a single bad opinion of God unites them. The worst of your ministers is the one who served evil rulers before you and shared in their sins. Do not let such men into your inner circle, for they are the helpers of sinners and the brothers of tyrants. You can find good replacements for them among men of equal intelligence and capability who carry none of that burden of sin, who never helped a tyrant in his tyranny or a sinner in his sin. Such men will cost you the least, support you the best, incline most warmly toward you, and attach themselves least to anyone but you; so choose them as your intimates, in private and in public.

Among these, give the highest place to the one who most readily speaks the bitter truth to you and gives you the least encouragement in whatever you do that God dislikes for His friends, however sharply that strikes at your own desires. Attach yourself to people of piety and truthfulness, and train them not to flatter you or to gladden you with praise for something you never did, for lavish praise breeds conceit and carries a person toward vanity. Never set the good and the wicked on the same footing before you, for that would make the good think little of their goodness and let the wicked settle deeper into their wickedness; hold each of them to what he has bound himself to. Know that nothing so builds a ruler's confidence in his subjects as his kindness toward them, his easing of their burdens, and his refusal to press them beyond what they can bear. So conduct yourself in a way that earns their trust, for that trust will spare you long trouble; the one most deserving of your good opinion is the one you have treated well, and the one most deserving of your distrust is the one you have treated badly.

Do not break any good tradition set by the leaders of this community, by which harmony was knit together and the subjects were set right; and do not bring in any new practice that harms the traditions already in place, for then the reward for the old traditions will go to the one who founded them, while the burden of breaking them will fall on you. Study much with the scholars (الَعُلَمَاءِ) and hold much discourse with the sages (الْحُكَمَاءِ), so as to strengthen what keeps your lands sound and to uphold what those before you had already set right.

Know that the subjects are made up of several classes, no one of which can be sound without the others, and no one of which can do without the rest. Among them are the soldiers of God (junud Allah); the scribes (kuttab, sing. katib) who serve the common people and the elite; the judges who administer justice (قُضَاةُ الْعَدْلِ); the officials who uphold fairness and secure the right of redress (رِفْقٌ); those who pay the poll-tax (jizya, جزیۃ) and the land-tax (خَرَج) from among the protected people (أَهْلُ الذِّمَّةِ) and the Muslims; the merchants and artisans; and the lowest class, the needy and the destitute. To each of these God has assigned its share, and He has fixed its bounds and its duties in His Book or in the Sunna (سنۃ) of His Prophet, God bless him and his family, as a binding covenant (عہد) from Him, kept safe with us.

As for the soldiers, they are, by the grace of God, the fortresses of the subjects, the adornment of the governors, the strength of religion, and the pathways to security (سُبُلُ الْأَمْنِ). The subjects cannot sustain themselves except through the soldiers, and the soldiers in their turn cannot be sustained except through the خَرَج, the revenue God brings forth for them; by it they gain the strength to wage war on their enemies, on it they depend for their own welfare, and through it they meet their needs. Neither of these two classes can be maintained without the third: the judges, the administrators, and the scribes, who uphold every contract, gather and order all interests and benefits, and are entrusted with the affairs of state, particular and general alike. None of these can function without the merchants and artisans, who gather the goods that are needed, set up the markets, and by their own labor supply what the work of others could not procure. Last comes the lowest class, the needy and the destitute, whose relief and support is an obligation owed to them. For every one of these classes there is abundance with God, and every one has upon the governor a right measured by what its welfare requires. The governor cannot fully discharge the duties God has laid upon him except through earnest striving and seeking God's help (الِاهْتِمَامُ وَالِاسْتِعَانَةُ), training himself to hold fast to what is right, and bearing patiently whatever comes his way, be it easy or hard.

Appoint as commander of your soldiers the man you feel most deeply to be the most sincere toward God, the Prophet, and your Imam, the purest of heart and the most excellent in forbearance (حِلْم): one who is slow to anger, glad to pardon, kind to the weak, and firm with the strong; neither driven by violence nor held back by weakness. Hold fast to men of noble descent, from pious families of established name and repute, and to those known for their bravery, their courage, their generosity (السَّخَاءُ), and their tolerance (السَّمَاحَةُ), for they are a group formed by nobility and a party made of honor. Then oversee their affairs as parents oversee their child. Let no act by which you strengthen them seem too great in your eyes, and do not belittle any kindness, however slight, that you have promised them, for such kindness is a summoner (دَاعِيَةٌ) calling them to give you good counsel and to place their trust in you. Do not neglect the close inspection of their affairs for the sake of matters that seem weightier, for there are small acts of kindness that benefit them greatly, and there are weightier matters that cannot be set aside.

Let the most favored of your commanders be the one most generous to the people and most open-handed with his bounty, so that they and their dependants have ample means. Let their concern be gathered into a single resolve: to fight the enemy. Your kindness toward them will incline their hearts toward you.

The greatest source of joy for governors is the establishment of justice in the land and the flow of love from their subjects. That love will not appear unless the subjects' hearts are secure, and their counsel will not be sound unless the governor watches over those charged with their welfare and makes the burden of his rule light and easy to bear, so that they no longer anxiously hope for it to end. Raise their hopes, praise them continually, and recount the brave deeds of those who have been brave, for abundant praise of noble acts rouses the courageous and spurs on the sluggish, if God wills. Acknowledge the courageous deed of every man; do not credit one man's deed to another, and do not fall short in granting anyone his due. Do not let a man's nobility lead you to reckon a small accomplishment great, nor a man's low station lead you to reckon a great one small. Refer to God and His Messenger any matter that weighs heavily upon you or is unclear to you, for God the Exalted has said to a people whom He loved to guide: 'O ye who believe, obey God and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. And if ye dispute with one another over anything, then refer it to God and the Messenger' (An-Nisa: 59). To 'refer to God' is to follow what is clear and unequivocal in His Book; to 'refer to the Messenger' is to follow that part of his Sunna which unites rather than that which divides.

For the settlement of disputes among the people, choose the one you hold to be the most excellent of your subjects: one whom complex matters do not confound and whom quarrelling litigants do not enrage; who does not persist in error, and does not shrink from returning to the truth once he perceives it; whose soul is not drawn toward greed; who is not content with a shallow understanding but probes to the depths; who is most cautious before ambiguities, most firm in holding to the proofs, least wearied by litigants returning again and again, most patient in uncovering the true state of affairs, and most resolute once the right judgment is plain; whom praise does not make vain and whom enticement does not sway. Such people are indeed rare.

Then examine carefully and often the carrying-out of the judge's verdicts, and be generous in what you pay him, so that want is removed and his need to seek help from others is lessened. Give him a rank of nearness to yourself that none of your other close companions might hope to attain, so that he is safe in your presence and shielded from the attacks of other influential men. Scrutinize all of this with a piercing scrutiny, for this religion had been held captive in the hands of evil people, abused by them for their own caprice and exploited by them for the sake of this world.

Then look into the affairs of your administrators. Employ them only after testing them, and do not appoint anyone out of partiality or favouritism, for these two are a wellspring of every kind of injustice and treachery. Seek out men of proven experience and modesty, from righteous households and families of precedence in Islam, for they are the most noble in character, the most rooted in dignity, the least inclined toward greed, and the most mindful of how all things end. Then provide them with a generous livelihood, for this gives them the strength to keep themselves in good order, frees them from reaching into the funds in their charge, and stands as an argument against them should they disobey your orders or betray your trust. Examine their actions carefully by appointing over them supervisors known for their sincerity and reliability, for your quiet watching of their affairs will move them to keep faith with their trust and to be gentle with the subjects. Keep watch over your assistants as well: if any one of them stretches out his hand toward a treacherous deed, and the reports of your watchers agree upon it so that you hold it sufficient evidence, then lay corporal punishment upon him, take him to account for what his act has brought about, set him in a place of disgrace, brand him with the charge of treachery, and gird him with the shame of the accusation.

Look also into the matter of the خَرَج land-tax, in such a way that those subject to it are kept in a proper state of well-being, for it is from their welfare, and from the sound collection of the tax itself, that the good of all others is drawn. There can be no welfare of the rest except through them, since all without exception depend upon the revenues of the خَرَج and upon those who pay it. Let your concern for the cultivation of the land outweigh your concern for collecting the tax, for no tax will be gathered where there is no cultivation. Whoever exacts the tax without cultivating the land ruins the land and destroys the people, and his rule will not endure for long. So if the people complain of a heavy burden, or of some blight, or of the cutting off of irrigation, or of a lack of rain, or of a change in the condition of the soil from flooding or drought, then lighten their burden in the measure that you hope will set their situation right. Do not let any relief you grant them weigh heavily upon you, for it is an investment that will return to you in the prosperity of your land, the adornment of your rule, the finest praise you reap from them, and the pride you take in dispensing justice among them. You may then rely upon an increase in their capacity as a result of what you invested in them when you gave them respite, and you may trust them, having accustomed them to your justice and your kindness.

It is possible that a situation might arise in which you depend upon them, and they would then bear this responsibility gladly, for a prosperous land can sustain whatever burden you place upon it. The devastation of the land comes about only through the destitution of its inhabitants, and the destitution of its inhabitants comes about only when the desire to amass wealth rules the souls of the governors, when they have doubts about what endures, and when they profit little from exemplary teachings.

Now consider with care the situation of your scribes. Appoint the best of them to manage your affairs. Assign those letters containing the most sensitive strategies and secrets to the scribe in whom you find the most comprehensive moral qualities: one who will not be emboldened by his elevated status to contradict you in public; one who is not negligent in delivering to you the correspondence of your officials and properly despatching your replies; one who takes and gives on your behalf with care. He must not weaken any agreement contracted for you, nor be incapable of repudiating what has been contracted to your detriment. He must not be ignorant of the value of his own capabilities, for one who is unaware of his own worth will be even more ignorant of the worth of others. Do not make your selection solely on the basis of your own instinct, confidence, and trust, for men know how to present themselves to rulers through affectation and good service that conceals neither sincerity nor fidelity. Rather, test them by what they did in the service of good people who came before you, and appoint the one whose virtue left the deepest impression upon the common people and whose reputation for integrity is most widely known. This will be proof of your sincerity toward God and toward the one on whose behalf you have been appointed to this office, for you will be putting the right man in the right place, and in so doing enacting justice.

Appoint for each of your affairs a head officer among your officials, one who will not be daunted by the magnitude of affairs nor scattered by their multiplicity. Any fault of your scribes which you have overlooked will be ascribed to you.

Then attend to the merchants and artisans, treating them well and urging the same on others, those of fixed abode, those who travel with their goods, and those who earn their livelihood with their hands. They are sources of benefit and the means by which conveniences are obtained. Through them, goods are procured from distant and remote places, brought by land and sea, from plains and mountains, and from places where men neither settle nor even dare to venture. They live in a state of reconciliation (سِلْمٌ), so no calamity need be feared from them, and in a state of peace (صُلْحٌ), so no disturbance need be expected. Examine their affairs attentively, both those close to you and those in every corner of your land. Yet be aware that, despite what has been said, many of them harbor a despicable stinginess, a repugnant greed, a propensity to hoard goods, and a tendency to fix prices arbitrarily. All of this brings loss to the people and is a source of shame for governors. So prohibit hoarding, for the Messenger of God ﷺ prohibited it. Let trading be carried out with propriety and fairness, at prices that harm neither party, buyer nor seller. If any one of them succumbs to the temptation to hoard after you have prohibited it, inflict upon him an exemplary but not excessive punishment.

Then, O God, O God, pay particular attention to the lowest class: those who have no wherewithal, the destitute, the needy, the afflicted, the disabled. Within this class are those who beg and those whose wretchedness calls out to be alleviated but who do not beg. Be mindful of God in regard to their rights, for He has entrusted those rights to your care. Assign to them a portion from your public treasury (بَيْتُ الْمَالِ) and a portion of the produce of what is taken as booty by the Muslims in every region, for those who are furthest away have the same rights as those nearest. Upholding the right of each of them is incumbent upon you. Do not let any haughtiness on your part cause you to neglect them, for you will not be pardoned even the slightest shortcoming toward them on account of attending to some seemingly more important matter. Do not turn your concern away from them or assume a contemptuous attitude toward them. Keep a watchful eye over the affairs of those who cannot gain access to you and who are disdained by men of high standing. Appoint from among those you trust a God-fearing and humble person to bring their affairs to your attention. Treat these people in a manner such that God may excuse you on the day you meet Him, for they are more in need of justice from you than any others among your subjects. Offer your excuse to God in respect of fulfilling each one's right. Assume responsibility for the orphans and the elderly who have no resources and yet cannot bring themselves to beg. This is onerous for governors, and the fulfillment of all rights is onerous, but God makes it light for those who aspire to the Hereafter, who restrain their souls in patience, and trust in the truth of what God has promised them.

Apportion a part of your time to those who have special needs, making yourself free to attend to them personally, sitting with them in a public assembly in all due humility before God, your Creator. Keep your soldiers, guards, and officers away from them so that they may speak to you without inhibition, for I heard the Messenger of God ﷺ say, on more than one occasion: 'A nation in which the rights of the weak are not wrested in an uninhibited manner from the strong will never be blessed.' Bear patiently any coarseness or inarticulate expression they might show; do not display irritation or disdain toward them, and God will thereby extend His compassion to you in its fullest measure and make the reward for your obedience to Him binding. Give whatever you give with beneficence, and withhold, if this be unavoidable, with grace and apology.

There are certain affairs which you must handle personally, among them replying to your officers when your scribes are incapable of doing so, and issuing responses to the requests of people when they are brought to you in person because your aides find themselves at a loss on their account.

Perform each day the task proper to it, for to each day belongs a particular task. Set apart the finest portion of your available time, and the greatest share thereof, for your soul and for what is between you and God, even though all times and all actions within them are for God, provided the intention underlying them is good and your subjects derive security as a consequence. Let your observance of those duties (faraid, sing. farida) relating exclusively to God be the special means by which you purify your religion for Him. Give unto God of your vital energy in your nights and your days, and perform fully that by which you draw near to God, doing so perfectly, without becoming dull or deficient, taking your body to its limits.

When you lead the people in prayer, do so without repelling them or squandering it, for among them are people with infirmities or special needs. Indeed, I asked the Messenger of God ﷺ, when he sent me as his representative to Yemen, 'How should I lead them in prayer?' He replied: 'Lead them according to the prayer performed by the weakest among them, and be merciful to the believers.'

In addition to all of this, do not prolong any period of absence from your subjects, for the isolation of governors from those they govern creates a kind of constriction and breeds deficiency in awareness of their affairs. Such isolation cuts rulers off from acquiring knowledge of things hidden from them, so that what is great comes to appear small and what is small, great; the beautiful appears ugly and the ugly, beautiful; the true becomes mixed with the false. The governor is but a human being: he cannot know what people conceal from him, and truth bears no visible mark by which sincere expressions may be distinguished from false ones.

You can only be one of two kinds of men: either a man who gives himself generously for the sake of the truth, in which case, why seclude yourself, thereby preventing yourself from bestowing a necessary right or performing an honorable deed? Or else you are a man afflicted with an unwillingness to give of himself, in which case, how quickly people will refrain from making requests of you, despairing of your generosity! And this, despite the fact that fulfilling most of the needs people bring to you is not burdensome, whether they be complaints against injustice or demands for fairness in transactions.

The governor has favorites and intimate companions, and among them some are prone to presumptuousness, arrogance, and unfairness in their dealings. Cut this out at the root by eliminating the causes of these vices, and by granting no landed estates to anyone in your entourage or circle of friends. Give them no reason to hope for such a grant; should they receive one, it would harm those living nearby in their access to water and in any shared undertaking, the burden of which would inevitably fall on the neighbors while the profit went to those holding the estate, not to you. What would fall to you is only the blame (عَيْبٌ) for it, in this world and the next. Impose what is right upon all to whom it applies, whether they are close to you or distant. Be steadfast and vigilant in this, regardless of how it weighs upon your intimates and favorites. Always seek the end that this principle leads to, however heavily the path may lie upon you, for its outcome is praiseworthy.

If any of your subjects suspect you of an injustice, present your case openly so that the suspicion is cleared away from you by plain explanation. Such conduct is a discipline for yourself, a kindness to your subjects, and a means of pleading your cause that will help you keep them upright in adherence to the truth.

Never turn away from a call to peace with your enemy when God's pleasure lies in it, for in peace there is rest for your soldiers, relief from your cares, and security for your lands. Yet once you have made peace, remain fully on your guard against your enemy, for he may have drawn close only in order to catch you off guard; so take every precaution, and hold even your own favorable opinion of him under suspicion. Yet if you and your enemy enter into a solemn agreement, or he receives from you a covenant of protection (ذِمَّةٌ), then keep your agreement faithfully and honor your obligation of protection with complete trustworthiness. Make your very life a shield for what you have promised, for among all the obligations God has laid down there is none on which people stand more firmly united, despite all the differences of their inclinations and the variety of their opinions, than the honoring of pledges. Even the polytheists observed it among themselves, quite apart from their dealings with the Muslims, so greatly did they fear the consequences of treachery. Do not betray your covenant of protection, do not break your promise, and do not deal treacherously with your enemy, for none dares defy God but the ignorant and wretched (جَاهِلٌ شَقِيٌّ). God has made His pledge and His protection a sanctuary of security, which by His mercy He has spread over His servants, a refuge in whose inviolable strength they find rest, and toward whose sheltering power they hasten. Let there be no corruption within it, no treachery, and no deception. Do not enter into any agreement that leaves room for evasion, and do not resort to twisting words once an agreement has been confirmed and made binding. Do not let any hardship that befalls you drive you to break, without right, an agreement God has made binding upon you; for patience under a difficulty whose relief and good outcome you await is far better than a treachery whose consequence you dread, that a reckoning from God (tilba) should close in upon you from which you will find no release in this world or the next.

Beware of shedding blood unlawfully, for nothing so swiftly invites retribution, weighs more heavily in its consequence, or is more apt to end a blessing and cut short a span of life, than bloodshed without right. On the Day of Resurrection, God, glorified be He, will begin His judgment among His servants with the blood they have shed. Do not seek to strengthen your authority through unlawful bloodshed, for that in truth weakens and undermines it; indeed, it brings it down and takes it away entirely.

You have no excuse before God, nor before me, if you kill anyone deliberately, for that demands capital punishment. And if you are overtaken by an error, and your whip, your sword, or your hand goes too far in inflicting a penalty, for even a single blow, and whatever exceeds it, can bring about death, do not let the pride of your authority hold you back from paying the relatives of the slain what is rightfully theirs.

Beware of self-admiration, of leaning on whatever pleases you in yourself, and of loving to be flattered, for these are among the surest openings Satan finds to efface the good works (احسان) of the virtuous (المُحسنین). Beware of making your subjects feel indebted to you for the good you do them, of inflating your own deeds, and of promising what you then fail to fulfill; for laying people under obligation destroys the good itself, self-praise extinguishes the light of truth, and breaking a promise earns you the hatred of God and of men. God the Exalted has said:

> كَبُرَ مَقْتًا عِندَ ٱللَّهِ أَن تَقُولُوا۟ مَا لَا تَفْعَلُونَ
>
> 'It is indeed hateful to God that you say that which you do not do' (As-Saff: 3).

Beware of rushing into matters before their time has come, and of letting them slip when the moment to act has arrived; of stubbornness when they grow tangled, and of slackness once they have become clear. Put every matter in its proper place, and attend to every task at its proper time. Beware of taking for yourself what belongs equally to all, and of feigning blindness to what is urgent and plain to every eye, for it will be taken from you for the benefit of others. Before long the coverings will be lifted from all affairs, and justice will be sought from you on behalf of the wronged. So master the heat of your pride, the sharpness of your temper, the force of your hand, and the edge of your tongue; guard against all of this by holding back sudden impulse and delaying the blow until your anger has subsided and you have regained your self-command. You will never achieve this mastery over yourself, however, unless you increase your remembrance of your return to your Lord.

It is incumbent upon you to remember the just governance and the noble custom that have come before you, the practice of the Prophet, and the obligations set forth in the Book of God. Follow faithfully what you have seen us do in these matters, and strive with all your soul to act upon what has been enjoined upon you in this mandate, by which the plea has been made complete against you, so that you will have no excuse when your soul races toward its caprice.

I ask God, by the vastness of His mercy and the greatness of His power to grant every desire, that He bestow upon me and upon you the success that wins His pleasure, that we may present a clear justification before Him and before His creatures, earn the fairest praise among His servants, and leave behind the most beautiful traces in the land, with blessing made complete and honor ever increased; and that He seal my life and yours with felicity (اَلسَّعَادَةُ) and martyrdom (اَلشَّهَادَةُ). Truly:

> ٱلَّذِينَ إِذَآ أَصَبَتْهُم مُّصِيبَةٌۭ قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّآ إِلَيْهِ رَجِعُونَ

Those who, when a misfortune befalls them, say: Truly unto God we belong, and unto Him we are returning (Al-Baqarah: 156).

And peace be upon the Messenger of God and upon his good and pure progeny.

> **Editorial note (tradition-grounded).** 
> When the letter tells Malik to hold himself back from "whatever is not lawful to you," the word
> behind *lawful* is *halal*, whose root (h-l-l) means to untie or loosen a knot: the permitted is
> what has been unknotted — loosed from prohibition. The wider tradition preserves this sense, and
