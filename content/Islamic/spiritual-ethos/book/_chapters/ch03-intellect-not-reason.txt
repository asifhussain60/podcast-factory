# Intellect, Not Reason

The spiritual ethos of Ali (ع) has an intellectual heart. The intellect (الْعَقْل) plays a central part in how his spiritual teachings are worked out and taken in. Sketching the key features of that ethos, with special attention to its intellectual side, gives firmer ground to the explorations that follow. One aim is to bring out something of the 'spirit of the intellect', الرُّوحُ الْعَقْلُ — the phrase coined for the bond between the intellect and the spirit as it appears in the Imam's worldview. This spirit surpasses the workings of the rational mind even as it contains them, and it reaches into areas not usually tied to the intellect today, such as moral conduct and a sense of beauty. This spiritual understanding of the intellect is directly connected to the Quranic idea of the spirit (الرُّوح), and much of what follows examines what comes of engaging deeply with this unifying spirit that belongs to the intellect.

It helps at the outset to explain why the English word 'intellect' is preferred over 'reason' to render 'aql. The word is meant to call up the original sense of intellectus in Latin Christendom, a sense almost identical to that of nous in the Greek of the Church Fathers. Intellectus means understanding', and it holds within it the idea of standing under a thing: to understand something is to stand beneath a perception of reality and let that perception overshadow and govern one's actions, for a person always acts in the shadow of how things are perceived. Intellectus, or nous, is therefore the faculty able to see transcendent realities directly, in contemplation — the very realities of which the Quran speaks but which are hidden from the senses. Reason, which renders the Latin ratio and the Greek dianoia, is by contrast indirect and step by step; it works through logic and reaches only mental concepts of those realities. The root of ratio means a relation or comparison between two numbers, and this catches how reason proceeds: by comparison, by a binary logic of affirming and denying, weighing one thing against another until it has defined what it examines. Reason so understood is the instrument the intellect uses, but it can carry a person only as far as the outward form of a thing, only as far as the mental concept or image of a reality. With the intellect one can contemplate, or 'see', the Absolute; with reason one can only think about it. To know about a reality is one thing; to contemplate and experience it directly is quite another, and it is the intellect, not reason, that is capable of that experience — even though it is through reason that the intellect is brought to it.

These opening reflections on the spirit of the intellect in the Imam's ethos turn chiefly on a short discourse of the greatest importance in the Nahj al-balagha: the one the Imam gave to Kumayl b. Ziyad, among his closest disciples. The method here is that of an interpretive commentary on the Imam's words, drawing not only on his other sayings on the same theme but also on Quranic verses that agree with them and, at times, on traditions of the Prophet that either help clarify the Imam's saying at hand or are themselves clarified by it. Three factors belonging to the spirit of this intellectual outlook must be stressed first: the centrality of revelation; the harmony between revelation and the intellect; and the distinction between the intellect, understood as the principle by which consciousness is articulated, and reason, understood as one expression of that principle.

To begin with the first: everything in the Imam's life and teachings is centered on the Quranic revelation and on its embodiment in the Prophet's conduct. The Quran is the center around which the intellect must turn, for the intellect needs a center, principles, and definitions to hold fast to if it is to be rightly guided. Both the thought and the life of the Imam can be read as a kind of spiritual commentary, a تَأْوِيل, on the sacred text. On the doctrinal plane, it can be said with confidence that Ali had an inborn gift for the work of تَأْوِيل. On the plane of his own life as well, his path — from his birth in the most holy shrine of the Kaba to his martyrdom in the mosque of Kufa — reads as a dramatic unfolding of the inner meaning of the revealed text. Ali 's refusal to stop at the surface of things, whether religious, ethical, or political, showed an unflinching fidelity to the sacred substance of the religion, a fidelity that called for inward intellectual penetration and outward armed resistance alike. The saying of the Prophet cited earlier bears closely on this: Ali would fight for the تَأْوِيل of the Quran just as he, the Prophet, had fought for its tanzil, عَلِیُّ یُقَاتِلُ عَلیٰ تَاوِیلِہ کَما قَتَلُ عَلیَ تَنزِیلِہِ. Ali s short caliphate was dominated by bloody civil wars, and a strong case can be made that all three — the wars of the Camel (اَلْجَمَلُ), of Nahrawan, and of Siffin — were fought for the sake of interpreting and applying the Quran rightly, for the clarification of its central message of تَوْحِيد, and for the realization of that oneness on every level, spiritual and social alike. These were not political battles or mere struggles for power; they were waged to clarify and uphold the oneness of God according to the principles of the Quran — in thought, in speech, and in action. The Imam's response to a bedouin brings this vividly home. Just as the battle of the Camel (اَلْجَمَلُ) was about to begin, the man approached him and said, O Commander of the Faithful (یا أمیر المومنین)! Do you say that God is one?' Those present rebuked the bedouin, telling him this was neither the time nor the place for such questions. The Imam, however, intervened: 'Leave him, for surely what the bedouin wishes is what we wish for the people.'

This response bears directly on the principle that right intention comes before outward action, whatever that action may be. Had the Imam been guided by reason alone, he would have sided with those who rebuked the bedouin and told him to find a fitter time and place; but he saw with the intellect that stands above reason. For the Imam, the deepest intention behind every action, whether religious in the ordinary sense or military as here, is knowledge of the true nature of ultimate reality. What the Imam 'wishes for the people', then, is precisely this knowledge, and it must lie at the very heart of all concerns, motives, and actions, whether on the battlefield or the prayer-rug. There is therefore no time or place where the question 'What do you mean by saying God is one?' is beside the point. It was for the sake of true knowledge of the divine, and of all that flows from it in society, that the Imam was fighting, in fulfillment of the prophetic saying just mentioned. On the very field of battle, then, the Imam gives a discourse on the meaning of divine oneness. The first part of his reply holds a key to his whole spiritual ethos. He rejects any notion that God's oneness is numerical, a matter of counting. He tells the bedouin that one may not say 'one' while holding any numerical idea in mind, for 'that which has no second does not enter into the category of numbers'. The moment a thing can be counted as 'one', it at once admits the possibility of 'two', 'three', 'four'; a oneness understood in this numerical sense is thus undone by the very number that would affirm it.

'That which has no second' is not merely a divinity to which no partner can be ascribed, as a theologian might put it, but that reality which has no 'otherness' — the Real, اَلْحَقّ, at once transcendent and immanent, beyond all 'things' and yet made manifest by all 'things', at once out of reach and inseparable from all that is. To say that God has no otherness is not to say that things exist which might have been His partners but which believers simply choose not to associate with Him; it is to affirm that nothing in all of existence bears His likeness, nothing that could be set beside Him. In these few words the Imam works, for those who have 'ears to hear', a shift of awareness: from the doctrinal notion of one God against many gods to the mystical grasp of the one and only Reality, before which everything else is mere appearance. All things come to be seen as outward appearances waiting to be read, as signs (آیات) pointing beyond themselves to That of which they are the manifestations, for an aya is precisely a meaningful token that points beyond itself to another. Tracing these appearances back to their source and origin is the literal meaning of تَأْوِيل: a return to the awwal, 'the First' (عَودَۃ الی الاول). On the one hand, the Real is described as the beginning and end of all things, the apparent and the hidden; there is nothing in being but this one Reality: 'He is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward' (Al-Hadid: 3).

> هُوَ ٱلْأَوَّلُ وَٱلْآخِرُ وَٱلظَّهِرُ وَٱلْبَاطِنُ ۖ وَهُوَ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

On the other hand, apparent multiplicity is to be 'brought back' to real oneness: 'We shall show them Our signs (سَنُرِيهِمْ آيَتِنَا) on the horizons and in their own souls, so that it be clear to them that He is the Real' (Fussilat: 53).

> سَنُرِيهِمْ آيَتِنَا فِى ٱلأٓفَاقِ وَفِىٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَهُمْ أَنَّهُ ٱلْحَقُّ

In one saying attributed to Ali, the Prophet is described as the first whom God taught the science of تَأْوِيل, and who in turn taught it to Ali. According to the Prophet, 'Truly, Ali is with the Quran, and the Quran is with Ali, أنَّ علیٌ مع القرآن، و قرآن مع العلی. In another famous saying, often quoted by Sufi commentators, Ali claimed that he could load seventy camels with the pages of commentary he could give on the Fatiha alone, the opening chapter of the Quran. Ali s cousin Ibn Abbas, often called the first commentator', likewise claimed to have learned the science of interpretation from Ali.

Closely related is a saying of the Imam of the first importance, which helps explain why he is held in the highest esteem within the discipline of Quranic exegesis, tafsir: 'The Quran consists of a book inscribed, between two covers; it speaks not with a tongue, it cannot do without an interpreter (لَا بُدَّ لَهُ مِنْ تُرْجُمَانٍ).' The mutual dependence of revelation and the intellect is underlined again in the saying, 'The prophet of a man is the interpreter of his intellect (رَسُولُ الرَّجُلِ تَرْجُمَانُ عَقْلِهِ).' Elsewhere the intellect is set in plain correspondence with the 'inner' messenger: the intellect is 'the messenger of the Real'.

The Imam's teachings, which give voice to his spiritual ethos, should therefore be read above all as creative interpretations of the Quranic revelation. They set in motion a movement from the letter of the revelation to its spirit, and from the spirit of the revelation to the spirit of the Revealer — a work of 'retracing', of returning to the source, the origin, the beginning, al-awwal, achieved to the degree that the spirit of the intellect is brought to life within the interpreter. Yet the Revealer stays of necessity hidden even as He is revealed through the revelation, hidden because the essence of what is revealed, the divine Reality in itself, cannot be uttered or put into words; only the forms of its manifestations can be revealed. This play between form and the formless, the evident and the hidden, the revealed and the concealed — such pairs of opposites lie at the root of the paradoxical manner of expression for which the Imam is renowned, and which recurs throughout this book. The relationship that especially concerns the present discussion is the second of the three factors named above: that between revelation and 'intellection', by which is meant the creative use of the intellect. This relationship matters all the more in a setting so largely defined by fidelity to revelation. Where strict rationalists hold intellect and revelation to be mutually exclusive, here they are seen as fully harmonious, standing in creative reciprocity, each needing the other for its completeness, its wholeness, for the 'making one' that is the very essence of تَوْحِيد.

Indeed, one of the most striking features of the Imam's outlook is the way it lays bare the emptiness of many false or overdrawn oppositions that later came to mark, or were forced upon, Islamic thought. This owes much to the deeply synthetic quality of the Imam's discourses, seen as 'pre-philosophical' by some scholars and as 'supra-philosophical' — inspired and evocative — by those within the Islamic spiritual tradition. The seemingly unbridgeable split between intellect and revelation shows up in several opposed forms: reason versus faith, convention (سنۃ) versus nature (طَبِيعَة), imitation (تَقْلِيد) versus verification (تَحْقِيق), piety versus inquiry, and so on. In Ali 's ethos, however, intellect and revelation are not mutually exclusive but complementary, and at the highest level inseparable, since truth is one. The human intellect plainly cannot be equated with divine revelation, yet the spirit that animates the intellect is not, in the end, bound by human limits. It is this spirit that perceives and grasps the divine character of revelation.

The divine spirit that shapes the form of revelation and fills its content must be fathomable, to some degree, by human awareness; without this, revelation would amount to no more than a set of rules and regulations for the will, rather than a source of creative inspiration for the intelligence. It cannot be stressed too strongly that divine revelation, far from curbing the workings of reason and imposing some unintelligible command, on the contrary asks for a creative engagement with all the resources of the intelligence — above all those acts of reflection and meditation for which the Imam himself is renowned, and which the Quran calls for again and again. It is the very opposite of a decree imposed from outside: the Quran constantly demands fikr, deep and sustained reflection, and such reflection does not shut the meaning of the text within its literal surface but opens it out, unfolding the inner horizons of meaning folded within the letter.

For the revealed text is itself silent; it speaks only through its interpreter, the intellect. This understanding of the bond between intellect and revelation shuts out all literalism and shallowness, every attempt to pen meaning within a flat, one-sided reading. How deeply the text's meaning is sounded depends on the depth and penetration of the interpreter's own intellect, for the text carries many levels of meaning at once. At its most profound, this 'prophetic' intellect rejoins the very source of revelation itself; for it is this 'inner prophet', the innermost degree of consciousness, that is the ultimate ground on which a person can confirm the verses of the outer revelation as true — to validate them through lived experience, and not merely to affirm them as dogma. It is in this sense that the Imam says, in the first sermon of the Nahj, that one of the purposes for which God sent forth prophets to mankind was 'to unearth for them the buried treasures of the intellects' (دَفَائِنُ الْعُقُولِ).

This saying is one of the keys to the spiritual ethos under consideration, and it returns again and again throughout these essays. The intellect is not stifled by revelation; it is brought to life by it. Revelation is a clarion call to the spiritual depths of the intellect, cultivating the seeds of consciousness — the seeds of توحید — that God has planted there and that lie buried within us, and bringing them to fruition. For the spirit of one's intellect already carries the initial imprint of its Creator: in the world of the spirit it witnessed its Lord and testified to His lordship, and revelation, the ذکر, does no more than remind it of that primordial testimony. A reminder, after all, is never a new piece of information; it is knowledge one already possesses but has forgotten, for one can only be reminded of what one already knows. We were created in this world in a kind of amnesia. Just as a physician tells the family of a man who has lost his memory to surround him with familiar things, with signs (آیات) out of his past, to jog his memory, so God has set His reminders in the horizons and the souls (آفَاق وَ أَنْفُس). The spirit of one's intellect, then, cannot be alien to what descends as revelation from on high; on the contrary, it must be fundamentally akin to it, and it is precisely for this reason that one can be absolutely certain that the revelation truly comes from the Absolute. The point is not to set up a dichotomy between intellect and revelation, but to see the intellect as revelation, or at least as one mode of revelation: the God-given means by which the individual comes to discern, affirm, and plumb the divine 'revelations' outside himself, both those of scripture and those constituted by the 'signs' (آیات) of the natural world. If the human intellect can perceive and recognize revelation, it is because both are rooted in one and the same ultimate source; we came from a spiritual realm, and so did revelation, sent to remind us of what we once witnessed and have since forgotten. Prophetic revelation awakens and quickens the depths of the human intellect; it does not replace or displace it. The messengers do for these buried seeds what the farmer does when he waters what lies hidden beneath the soil, what the miner does when he digs into the mountain for its gold and silver, and what the diver does when he descends to the sea-bed to bring up its buried treasures. From this perspective the revealed text is not some extraneous source set against the intelligence, but the objective, outward expression of the very principles that the intellect itself yields up to the 'inner prophet' — the intellect serving as the inner messenger whom the outer messenger awakens and nourishes. By 'intellect' is meant not simply the faculty of reasoning but the source of consciousness that articulates the human spirit.

The intellect holds unsuspected depths, buried treasures, and is far from being exhausted by its surface operations — the cognitions and reasonings proper to its purely mental dimension. A third matter now follows: the relationship between intellect and reason. Just as there can be no contradiction whatsoever between revelation and intellection, so too a false dichotomy between reason and intellect must be avoided. Reason should rather be seen as one of the modes of the intellect; reason no more contradicts intellect than intellect contradicts revelation. Reason is a mode of the intellect, but it is not to be equated with it wholesale, just as one cannot reduce the universal objectivity of revelation to the subjective specificities of any one individual's intellection, to his or her own 'private' revelation. To equate reason with intellect would amount to denying the very existence of the 'buried treasures' of the intellect — those supra-rational dimensions that are, at first, buried beneath, and thus hidden from, the rational surface of the intellect. The revelation constituted by the intellect is embedded within the creation of the human being, the intellect being that which most critically defines the human being as such. Far from pitting the intellect against revelation, each is to be seen in the light of the other: the substance of revelation cannot be engaged except through the intellect, on the one hand, and the 'buried treasures' of the intellect cannot be disclosed except through revelation, on the other — a genuine co-dependency of the two.

This understanding of the intellect stands close to what the Imam refers to as the 'heart' (قَلْب), that innermost mode of perception capable of 'seeing' God. It is the 'heart' that can 'see' God — not with eyesight, not with بصارۃ, but with insight, with بصیرۃ, that spiritual insight generated by the 'verities of faith' (حَقَائِقُ الْإِيمَان / حقائق الایمان). As the Imam puts it, in response to the questioner asking whether he had 'seen' his Lord: 'Eyes see Him not through sight's observation, but hearts see Him through the verities of faith.'

Between the spiritual depths of the intellect and its rational surface there is both continuity and discontinuity. The continuity shows in an evident fact: all thinking begins with concepts that convey intelligible meaning. Thought sets out from ideas that are rationally discernible, and these must bear some relation to the realities they express, or they collapse into ideas that lead nowhere. But there is discontinuity too between the spiritual and rational aspects of the intellect, in that reason stops short at the ideas it has 'de-fined'. The Arabic word for definition, hadd (حَدّ), expresses — like its English equivalent — both senses at once: that of a conceptual definition and that of a limitation, a boundary, the very boundary that reason draws around an idea by weighing its pros and cons until the idea is fixed and 'defined'. To go beyond the limits imposed by this rational act of definition, something more than reason is needed; and it is this 'something more' that makes the ethos under consideration a spiritual one, and not a rationalistic one. It is certainly 'rational', in the sense that the form of the ethos is indeed open to rational scrutiny; but it is not 'rationalistic', inasmuch as its essence is not predicated on reason alone.

Now Ali frequently hints, in highly allusive terms, at the mysteries and marvels — the unearthed treasures of the intellect — that he has directly witnessed by the eye of his purified heart. He also alludes to this supra-rational mode of awareness indirectly, through a bewildering use of apophatic discourse and paradoxical utterance, of which the corpus attributed to him is full. In this ethos it seems that the higher the truth, the deeper the paradox: the sheer lack of any common measure between reality as it is in itself and the human conceptions formed of it is effectively evoked by this creative use of paradox. In modern parlance, each human conception of reality needs to be 'deconstructed'. But then, unlike a modern deconstructionism so radically destructive that it has nothing left to 'reconstruct', the Imam's apophasis feeds directly into modes of spiritual intuition, contemplation, and realization of that which reason, on its own, cannot attain. It is this intuition and contemplation which 're-members' — or 'reconstructs', if one likes — the spiritual realities that transcend the categories of reason. The value of the paradoxes, the antinomies, the apophasis, and the negation is entirely predicated on the eminently positive realities which the intellect can plumb, thereby becoming aware of its own hidden depths. The resulting consciousness does not so much negate reason as situate it, revealing the limits of reason's proper sphere of operation: it reveals that reason alone cannot 'comprehend' the higher realities, nor express them fully, however indispensable reason may be in initiating the cognitive movement toward those realities.

Thus, when the Imam negates, as he does so often, the possibility of human conceptions encompassing the divine Reality, what is being negated is the limitation that is the inescapable consequence of all such conceptions — not the positive content, the mana or spiritual meaning, of the conception itself. For this conception is a necessary starting point for a process of assimilation that engages a deeper level of awareness than that 'de-fined' by the mind, and which therefore extends far beyond the domain of speculative thought. In this light, the negation of the limitations of human conception should be seen as the prelude to an absolute affirmation: the affirmation of the Absolute, which has no limitation. The spiritual negation of conceptual limitation is of an upward, 'constructive' order, not a nihilistic, deconstructive one. Nowhere is this style of discourse more apparent than in the famous opening lines of the first sermon of the Nahj al-balagha:

Praise be to God, whose laudation those who speak cannot deliver, whose graces those who count cannot number, whose rightful due those who strive cannot render; He who cannot be grasped by far-reaching aspirations, nor fathomed by profound intuitions; He whose attribute has no binding limitation, no existing description, no time appointed, no term extended.

The divine attribute (الصِّفَة) has no limit in terms of which it can be defined — laysa li-sifatih hadd mahdud — and yet it is affirmed as real. It has no 'existing description', and yet in some sense it is described (مَوْصُوف), by the very words of the Quranic revelation; for in the Quran God is described as having such attributes as mercy, compassion, and power. He is the Merciful, the Compassionate, the Almighty, and yet the Imam affirms that these very attributes cannot be defined, bounded, or described. The paradox sharpens still further when the Imam speaks of the perfection of إِخْلَاص — the 'rendering sincere', the purification of one's devotion to God and of one's conception of Him: this perfection, he says, is to divest (نفی) God of all attributes. On the one hand the attributes are affirmed; on the other they are to be negated. The rational mind can only think of God in terms of the attributes given verbal expression in the Quran; and yet here the Imam appears to negate these same attributes, thereby depriving the mind of any convenient conceptual handle by which to grasp the nature of the divine Reality. The simultaneous affirmation and negation of the attributes raises an apparently irresolvable contradiction, and in doing so enhances receptivity to a deeper mode of knowing. This style of discourse appears designed to awaken in the listener some latent awareness inscribed in the heart or the depths of the intellect, and to invite the seeker to dive into those depths, into the knowledge 'buried' in the intellect. The very shock of the apparent contradiction in this apophasis seems intended to precipitate a leap of consciousness, from the formal activity of reason to the supra-rational contemplation of the spirit.

The words spoken by the Imam immediately after affirming that his heart had indeed witnessed God — not by means of outward vision but through 'the verities of faith' — run:

My Lord is not described in terms of dimension (بُعْدٌ), nor motion (حَرَكَة), nor rest (سُكُونٌ) ... nor through coming or going: He is subtle in subtlety but not described by subtlety, magnificent in magnificence but not described by magnificence, great in greatness but not described by greatness ...

The attributes are again affirmed and denied. God possesses these attributes but is not reducible to them. The mind can begin to think of God in terms of these categories, but He is not reducible to such conceptions. The attributes afford the mind something by which the divine nature can be understood, but this understanding is fragmentary, never exhaustive, and it needs to be negated as the prelude to a fuller apprehension. The initial conceptual orientation to the divine Reality needs to be both negated and sublimated: negated as a mental conception, while the positive content of the conception needs to be sublimated through spiritual intuition. Only then can the two-dimensional 'portrait' defined by reason be given a third dimension — that of depth. But this third dimension emerges only after the insufficiency of the rational depiction is understood; whence the need for that cognitive shock which paradox produces, a shock intended to reveal the pretension of reason in its effort to disclose the nature of the Absolute, the transcendent source of reason's own activities.

The Imam's discourses show a repeated combination of affirmation and denial, which mirrors the negation and affirmation found in the first testimony of Islam: la ilaha, 'no god', being the negation (نفی); then illaLlah, 'except God', coming as the affirmation (اِثْبَات). This helps to show that the negation of the attributes which the Imam names as the perfection of إِخْلَاص is in fact an affirmation of the infinitude of the divine Reality. Each of the attributes is to be 'negated', but only insofar as it is conceived by the mind. Such negation is necessary because, at the mental level, each attribute excludes other attributes, and also excludes the object of attribution itself — that is, the divine Essence — such exclusion following inescapably from the very possibility of conceptually distinguishing between the attribute and the object of attribution, between the divine quality and the divine Essence. For any conception to take place in the mind, defined ideas are necessary; and where there are definitions, there are limits and thus exclusions. The reality of the attribute, however, is unlimited, by virtue of its absolute identity with the Essence. The attribute that has been 'negated' is paradoxically affirmed — not in itself, but inasmuch as it is nothing but God: illaLlah. Thus one can place each of the negated attributes in the position of the nafy of the first testimony and arrive at such formulations as la rahmana illaLlah, 'There is no All-Compassionate except God'; la rahima illaLlah, 'No All-Merciful except God', and so on. The apparent negation of the attributes becomes, in fact, an affirmation of their true identity, shorn of any restrictive association with the poverty of mental conceptions. The attributes are not superadded to the Essence but are absolutely one with it, differentiable from it only conceptually, not ontologically. The Essence does indeed comprise all the attributes mentioned — greatness, subtlety, and so on — but it is also infinitely more than these attributes; and, for their part, the attributes are infinite only within and as the Essence. The Imam's apophasis here can be seen as a commentary on the Quranic refrain, 'Glorified be God above what they describe' (Al-Muminun: 91). The infinitude of God strictly requires, on the human plane, the negation of all that the mind can conceive through reason — but not of all that the heart can 'see' through spiritual vision. This very negation on the plane of reason calls out to something deeper, and this in turn can only be understood in the light of spiritual praxis, which is always assumed as the reflective, devotional, or contemplative context for the hearers of the Imam's sermons and discourses. The heart, symbolic seat of the contemplative intelligence, calls out to be 'polished', and this is precisely what the 'remembrance of God' does — this dhikr (ذِکْر) Allah being, according to the Imam, the 'most excellent form of worship'. Even at this level of praxis there is a further paradox: the 'reality' of the remembrance is not attained until 'you forget yourself in your remembrance'.

It is not just the rational mind and the determinate conceptions proportioned to it that are to be negated or 'deconstructed', but the individual soul itself, as the locus of all cognition, rational or supra-rational. This final denouement, or فَنَاء, is the ultimate form of apophasis.

These considerations show the extent to which the spirit of the intellect, in Ali 's perspective, transcends the rational faculty. The intellect must be seen, first and foremost, as a spiritual faculty; and the spirit, in turn, must be appreciated as the source of the intellect, as well as the source of life. Without the intellect the spirit is reduced to unconscious existence; conversely, without the spirit, the intellect tends toward lifeless thought. These aspects of the spirit are clearly expressed in the first sermon of the Nahj, which can be read as a commentary upon the Quranic verses describing the creation of man in terms of God's breathing' of His spirit into man: 'He began the creation of man with clay... He fashioned him and breathed into him of His spirit' (ثُمَّ سَوَّىٰهُ وَنَفَخَ فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِهِ).

The following lines, from the first sermon, form one of the many passages in which this infusion of the divine spirit into man is commented upon by Ali:

> ثُمَّ جَمَعَ سُبْحَانَهُ مِنْ حَزْنِ الاْرْضِ وَسَهْلِهَا، وَعَذْبِهَا وَسَبَخِهَا۔ تُرْبَةً سَنَّهَا بالمَاءِ حَتَّى خَلَصَتْ۔ وَلاَطَهَا بِالبَلَّةِ حَتَّى لَزَبَتْ۔ فَجَبَلَ مِنْها صُورَةً ذَاتَ أَحْنَاءَ وَوُصُولَ، وَأَعْضَاءَ وَفُصُول۔ أَجْمَدَهَا حَتَّى اسْتَمْسَكَتْ، وَأَصْلَدَهَا حَتَّى صَلْصَلَتْ۔ لِوَقْت مَعْدُود، وَأجَل مَعْلُوم۔ ثُمَّ نَفَخَ فِيها مِنْ رُوحِهِ فَمَثُلَتْ إِنْساناً ذَا أَذْهَانُ يُجيلُهَا، وَفِكَر يَتَصَرَّفُ بِهَا، وَجَوَارِحَ يَخْتَدِمُهَا، وَأَدَوَات يُقَلِّبُهَا، وَمَعْرِفَة يَفْرُقُ بِهَا بَيْنَ الحَقِّ وَالبَاطِلِ۔

Then He, glorified be He, gathered up a quantity of dust, from some hard earth and some soft, some sweet earth and some salty, and poured water onto it until it was pure, then kneaded into it some moisture until it stuck together; then He fashioned from this a form with curves and joints, limbs and parts. He compacted it until it held tightly together, solidified it until it became as sounding clay, assigning to it a measured duration and a known consummation. Then He breathed into it of His spirit, and it stood up as a human being, endowed with intellectual powers with which to reflect, thoughts by which to conduct himself, limbs to put to service, instruments at his disposal, and knowledge with which to discern between the true and the false ...

The body had been fashioned entire — its eyes, its ears, the organ of thought all in place — and yet it could neither hear nor see nor reflect until God breathed His spirit into it; only then did it stand up as a human being. It is this 'in-spiration' — literally, the in-breathing and the insertion of the spirit, of God's breath — that produces consciousness and life. Death, by contrast, is the 'ex-piration', the departure of the spirit. Life in the absence of 'aspiration' — the intention to be true to one's spirit — is a kind of death. It is through the spirit that inanimate, unconscious matter is brought to life and to awareness of itself: it generates not only knowledge but self-knowledge as well. This intimate intertwining between the gift of life and the capacity to know — existence that is conscious, and knowledge that is existential — is one of the foundations of any truly spiritual perspective. The question is not one of setting the spirit against the body, but of recognizing that the infusion of the spirit into the body is precisely what brings the body to life; and it is for this reason that knowledge vivifies, in the very measure that it is spiritual: 'O ye who believe, respond to God and His Messenger when they call you to that which vivifies you' (Al-Anfal: 24). According to the Imam, one of the most important aspects of this call from God to human beings is to 'remind them of the buried treasures of the intellect.'

Existentiation is the bestowal of the spirit, which is consciousness. The 'aql, or intellect, is the principle of articulation for the spirit, and so it is not merely a mental faculty but is itself constitutive of our true life. The intellect is that of which the mental faculty is one formal expression, one mode of its operation — but by no means the only one. The aql is many-faceted, comprising aspects that in our times are rarely associated with intelligence. To be true to one's intellect — to the treasures buried deep within it, and not merely to the rational functions operative on its surface — amounts to being spiritual.

For Ali, the true intellectual — al-aqil — is one who not only thinks correctly but also acts ethically, and, at the deepest level, one who seeks to realize the ultimate Reality. The intellectual is defined as one who 'puts all things in their proper place' (العاقل من وضع شئی حق محلہ), one who aligns his acts with the intentions of his Creator. This same alignment is the very definition of justice (عَدْل); only the true intellectual can therefore be fully just (عَادِل), for only one who sees things as they truly are is able to put them in their right place.

Thought, action, and realization are all the concern of the true intellectual, the الْعَاقِل. Correct thinking, impeccable virtue, and authentic being are inextricably bound up with the intellect in the spiritual ethos considered here. These aspects of the ethos come into sharper focus in the light of the Imam's discourse to Kumayl.
