# Intellect, Not Reason

What follows sketches some of the key dimensions of the spiritual ethos of Ali (ع), with particular emphasis on its intellectual aspects. Beyond demonstrating the pivotal role played by the intellect (al-'aql) in the elaboration and assimilation of the Imam's spiritual teachings, this exercise helps to ground more firmly the explorations of the ethos offered in the chapters that follow. One of the key aims is to illuminate something of the 'spirit of the intellect', the term الروح العقل that the author coins for the relationship between the intellect and the spirit, as it appears in the Imam's worldview: a spirit that surpasses, while containing, the activities of the rational mind, and that encompasses domains not nowadays associated with the intellect, such as moral comportment and aesthetic sensibility. This spiritual understanding of the intellect is directly related to the Qur'anic conception of the spirit (al-ruh), and it bears fruits that come from engaging deeply with this unifying spirit that is proper to the intellect.

It is useful at the outset to explain why 'intellect' is preferred over 'reason' as a translation of 'aql. What the word must evoke is the original meaning of intellectus in Latin Christendom, a meaning virtually identical to that of nous in the Patristic Greek tradition. The Latin intellectus means 'understanding', and it carries within it the sense of standing under a thing: to understand something is to stand beneath a perception of reality and allow that perception to overshadow and govern one's actions, since our actions are always performed in the shadow of how we perceive things. Intellectus and nous are thus the faculty capable of a direct, contemplative vision of transcendent realities, the very realities of which the Qur'an speaks but which are hidden from the senses, whereas reason, translating the Latin ratio and the Greek dianoia, is indirect and discursive in nature; it works through logic and arrives only at mental concepts of those realities. The root of ratio denotes a relation or comparison between two numbers, and this captures how reason proceeds: by comparison, by a binary logic of affirmation and negation, weighing one thing against another until it has defined what it is considering. Reason, so understood, is the instrument the intellect employs; but it can carry us only as far as the outward form of a thing, only as far as the mental concept or image of a reality. With the intellect one is able to contemplate or 'see' the Absolute; with reason one can only think about it. To know about a reality is one thing; to contemplate and experience it directly is quite another, and it is the intellect, not reason, that is capable of that experience, even if it is through reason that the intellect is brought to it.

This preliminary exploration of the spirit of the intellect in the Imam's ethos is sketched chiefly through reflections on a short discourse of the utmost importance in the Nahj al-balagha: the discourse the Imam gave to one of his closest disciples, Kumayl b. Ziyad. These reflections employ the same approach used in the two chapters that follow, an interpretive commentary on the Imam's words that draws not only on his other sayings on the theme in question, but also on concordant Qur'anic verses and, occasionally, traditions of the Prophet that either help illuminate the meaning of the Imam's saying under consideration, or are themselves elucidated by it. Before that dialogue, three factors inherent to the spirit of the intellectual perspective must be stressed: the centrality of revelation, the harmony between revelation and the intellect, and the distinction between the intellect, conceived as the principle of the articulation of consciousness, and reason, understood as one expression of that principle.

The first is the centrality of revelation. Everything about the Imam's life and teachings is centred on the Qur'anic revelation and its embodiment in the Prophet's conduct. The Qur'an is the centre around which the intellect must revolve, for the intellect needs a centre, principles and definitions to which it can hold fast for proper guidance. Both the thought and the life of the Imam can indeed be read as a kind of spiritual commentary, a ta'wil, on the sacred text. On the doctrinal plane, it can be stated with confidence that Ali (ع) possessed an innate aptitude for the work of ta'wil. On the existential plane as well, his life, from his birth in the most holy shrine of the Ka'ba to his martyrdom in the mosque of Kufa, reads as a dramatic unfolding of the inner meaning of the revealed text. Ali (ع)'s refusal to stop short at the superficial surface of things, whether religious, ethical, or political, manifested an unflinching fidelity to the sacred substance of the religion, a fidelity that entailed not only inward intellectual penetration but also outward combative resistance. The saying of the Prophet cited earlier is highly significant in this connection: Ali (ع) would fight for the ta'wil of the Qur'an just as he, the Prophet, had fought for its tanzil, عَلِیُّ یُقَاتِلُ عَلیٰ تَاوِیلِہ کَما قَتَلُ عَلیَ تَنزِیلِہِ. Ali (ع)'s short caliphate was dominated by bloody civil wars. A strong case can be made that all three of these wars, those of the Camel (al-Jamal), of Nahrawan, and of Siffin, were fought for the sake of the correct interpretation and application of the Qur'an, for the clarification of its fundamental message of tawhid, and for the realization of that oneness on every level, spiritual and social alike. These were not political battles or mere power struggles; they were battles waged to clarify and enforce the oneness of God according to the principles of the Qur'an, in thought, in speech, and in action. This claim is vividly substantiated by the Imam's response to a bedouin who, just as the battle of the Camel (al-Jamal) was about to begin, approached him and said, 'O Commander of the Faithful (یا أمیر المومنین)! Do you say that God is one?' Those present rebuked the bedouin, telling him this was neither the time nor the place for such questions. The Imam, however, intervened: 'Leave him, for surely what the bedouin wishes is what we wish for the people.'

Reflection on the spirituality of Ali (ع) can begin with this response, which bears directly on the principle that right intention takes priority over outward action, whatever that action may be. Had the Imam been guided by reason alone, he would have agreed with those who rebuked the bedouin and told him to find a more fitting time and place; but he saw with the intellect that stands above reason. For the Imam, the most fundamental intention motivating every action, whether religious in the ordinary sense or military as here, is knowledge of the true nature of ultimate reality. What the Imam 'wishes for the people', then, is precisely this knowledge, which must lie at the very heart of all concerns, motivations, and actions, whether on the battlefield or the prayer-rug. There is therefore no time or place where the question 'What do you mean by saying God is one?' is irrelevant or out of place. It was for the sake of correct knowledge of the divine, and all its ramifications in society, that the Imam was fighting, in fulfilment of the prophetic saying just mentioned. On the very field of battle, then, the Imam delivers a discourse on the meaning of divine oneness. The very first part of his reply contains a key to understanding his entire spiritual ethos. He refutes any notion that God's oneness is numerical or countable. He tells the bedouin that it is not permissible to say 'one' while holding any numerical conception in mind, for 'that which has no second does not enter into the category of numbers'. The moment a thing can be counted as 'one', it immediately admits the possibility of 'two', 'three', 'four', and so a oneness understood in this numerical sense is undone by the very number that would affirm it.

'That which has no second' is not merely a divinity to which no partner can be ascribed, as a theologian might put it, but that reality which has no 'otherness', the Real, al-Haqq, which is at once transcendent and immanent, beyond all 'things' and manifested by all 'things', simultaneously inaccessible and inalienable. To say that God has no otherness is not to say that things exist which might have been His partners but which believers simply decline to associate with Him; it is to affirm that nothing whatever in existence bears His likeness, nothing that could conceivably be set beside Him. In these few words the Imam effects, for those who have 'ears to hear', a shift of consciousness from the dogmatic notion of one God versus many gods to the mystical apprehension of the one and only Reality, in the face of which everything else is mere appearance. All things come to be apprehended as outward phenomena calling out to be deciphered, as signs (ayat) pointing beyond themselves to That of which they are manifestations, for an aya is precisely a meaningful token that points beyond itself to another. Retracing these appearances back to their source and origin is the literal meaning of ta'wil: going back to the awwal, 'the First' (عَودَۃ الی الاول). On one hand, the Real is described as the beginning and end of all things, the apparent and the hidden; there is nothing in being but this one Reality: 'He is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward' (57: 3). On the other hand, apparent multiplicity is to be 'brought back' to real oneness: 'We shall show them Our signs (sa-nurihim ayatina) on the horizons and in their own souls, so that it be clear to them that He is the Real', سَنُرِيهِمْ آيَتِنَا فِى ٱلأٓفَاقِ وَفِىٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَهُمْ أَنَّهُ ٱلْحَقُّ (41: 53).

In one saying attributed to Ali (ع), the Prophet is described as the first of those whom God taught the science of ta'wil, and who in turn taught it to Ali (ع). According to the Prophet, 'Truly, Ali (ع) is with the Qur'an, and the Qur'an is with Ali (ع)', أنَّ علیٌ مع القرآن، و قرآن مع العلی. In another famous saying, often quoted by Sufi commentators, Ali (ع) claimed he could load seventy camels with the pages of commentary he could offer on the Fatiha alone, the opening chapter of the Qur'an. Ali (ع)'s cousin, Ibn 'Abbas, often referred to as the 'first commentator', claimed to have learnt the science of interpretation from Ali (ع).

In this connection, the following saying of the Imam is of fundamental importance, and helps explain why he is held in the highest esteem within the discipline of Qur'anic exegesis, tafsir: 'The Qur'an consists of a book inscribed, between two covers; it speaks not with a tongue, it cannot do without an interpreter (la budda lahu min tarjuman).' The reciprocity between revelation and the intellect is further underlined in the saying, 'The prophet of a man is the interpreter of his intellect (rasul al-rajul tarjuman 'aqlihi).' Elsewhere there is an explicit correspondence between the intellect and the 'inner' messenger: the intellect is 'the messenger of the Real'.

The Imam's teachings, which express his spiritual ethos, should therefore be read essentially as creative interpretations of the Qur'anic revelation. They initiate a movement from the letter of the revelation to its spirit, and from the spirit of the revelation to the spirit of the Revealer, a process of 'retracing', of returning to the source, the origin, the beginning, al-awwal, accomplished in proportion to how fully the spirit of the intellect is enlivened within the interpreter. Yet the Revealer remains necessarily concealed even as He is revealed through the revelation, concealed because the essence of what is revealed, the divine Reality in itself, is ineffable and inexpressible; only the forms of its manifestations are susceptible to revelation. The dialectical interplay between form and the formless, the evident and the hidden, the revealed and the concealed, such antinomies lie at the root of the paradoxical mode of expression for which the Imam is renowned, and which recurs throughout this book. The relationship that especially concerns the present discussion is the second of the three factors mentioned above: that between revelation and 'intellection', by which is meant the creative use of the intellect. This relationship takes on particular importance in a context substantially defined by fidelity to revelation. Whereas rationalists in the strict sense regard intellect and revelation as mutually exclusive, in this perspective they are seen as perfectly harmonious, subsisting in a state of creative reciprocity, each requiring the other for completeness, for integrity, for the 'making one' that is the very essence of tawhid.

Indeed, one of the most striking aspects of the Imam's perspective is the way it exposes the hollowness of many false or excessively polarized dichotomies that later came to characterize, or were imposed upon, Islamic thought. This is due to the highly synthetic quality of the Imam's discourses, viewed as 'pre-philosophical' by some academics, and as 'supra-philosophical', inspired and evocative, by those within the Islamic spiritual tradition. The apparently insuperable dichotomy between intellect and revelation appears in various polarized forms: reason versus faith, convention (sunna) versus nature (tabi'a), imitation (taqlīd) versus verification (tahqīq), piety versus inquiry, and so on. In Ali (ع)'s ethos, however, intellect and revelation are not mutually exclusive but complementary, and at the highest level inseparable, truth being one. While the human intellect clearly cannot be equated with divine revelation, the spirit animating the intellect is not, in the final analysis, confined by human limitations. It is this spirit that perceives and grasps the divine quality of revelation.

The divine spirit articulating the form and infusing the content of revelation must be fathomable to some degree by human consciousness; without this, revelation would amount to nothing more than a set of rules and regulations for the will, rather than a source of creative inspiration for the intelligence. It cannot be stressed too strongly that divine revelation, far from curtailing the operations of reason and imposing some unintelligible diktat, on the contrary presupposes a creative engagement with all the resources of the intelligence, in particular those processes of reflection and meditation for which the Imam himself is renowned, and which the Qur'an repeatedly calls for. It is precisely the opposite of a decree imposed from without: the Qur'an constantly demands fikr, deep and sustained reflection, and such reflection does not enclose the meaning of the text within its literal surface but discloses it, opening up the inner horizons of meaning folded within the letter.

For the revealed text is itself silent; it speaks only through its interpreter, the intellect. This understanding of the bond between intellect and revelation rigorously excludes all literalism and superficiality, every attempt to confine meaning within a flat, one-sided reading. How deeply the text's signification is plumbed depends on the depth and penetration of the interpreter's own intellect, since the text carries multiple levels of meaning simultaneously. At its most profound, this 'prophetic' intellect rejoins the very source of revelation itself; for it is this 'inner prophet', the inmost degree of consciousness, that is the ultimate ground on which a person is able to confirm the verses of the outer revelation as true: to validate them through lived experience, and not merely to affirm them by dogma. It is in this sense that the Imam says, in the first sermon of the Nahj, that one of the purposes of God's sending forth of prophets to mankind was 'to unearth for them the buried treasures of the intellects' (dafa'in al-'uqul).

This saying is one of the keys to the spiritual ethos under consideration, and it recurs again and again throughout these essays. The intellect is not stifled by revelation; it is brought to life by it. Revelation is a clarion call to the spiritual depths of the intellect, cultivating and bringing to fruition the seeds of consciousness, the seeds of توحید, that God has planted there and that lie buried within us. For the spirit of one's intellect already carries the initial imprint of its Creator: in the world of the spirit it witnessed its Lord and testified to His lordship, and revelation, the ذکر, does no more than remind it of that primordial testimony. A reminder, after all, is never a new piece of information; it is knowledge one already possesses but has forgotten, for one can only be reminded of what one already knows. We were created in this world in a kind of amnesia, and just as a physician tells the family of a man who has lost his memory to surround him with familiar things, with signs (آیات) out of his past, to jog his memory, so God has set His reminders in the horizons and the souls (آفاق و أنفس). The spirit of one's intellect, then, cannot be alien to what descends as revelation from on high; on the contrary, it must be fundamentally akin to it, and it is precisely for this reason that one can be absolutely certain that the revelation truly comes from the Absolute. The point is not to set up a dichotomy between intellect and revelation, but to see the intellect as revelation, or at least as one mode of revelation: the God-given means by which the individual comes to discern, affirm, and plumb the divine 'revelations' outside himself, both those of scripture and those constituted by the 'signs' (ayat) of the natural world. If the human intellect can perceive and recognize revelation, it is because both are rooted in one and the same ultimate source; we came from a spiritual realm, and so did revelation, sent to remind us of what we once witnessed and have since forgotten. Prophetic revelation awakens and quickens the depths of the human intellect; it does not replace or displace it. The messengers do for these buried seeds what the farmer does when he waters what lies hidden beneath the soil, what the miner does when he digs into the mountain for its gold and silver, what the diver does when he descends to the sea-bed to bring up its buried treasures. From this perspective the revealed text is not some extraneous source set against the intelligence, but the objective, outward expression of the very principles that the intellect itself yields up to the 'inner prophet', the intellect serving as the inner messenger that the outer messenger awakens and nourishes, where by 'intellect' is meant not simply the faculty of reasoning but the source of consciousness that articulates the human spirit.

The intellect holds unsuspected depths, its buried treasures, and is far from being exhausted by its surface operations, the cognitions and reasonings proper to its purely mental dimension. These considerations lead to the third of the factors named above: the relationship between intellect and reason. Just as there can be no contradiction whatsoever between revelation and intellection, so too a false dichotomy between reason and intellect must be avoided. Reason should rather be seen as one of the modes of the intellect; reason no more contradicts intellect than intellect contradicts revelation. Reason is a mode of the intellect, but it is not to be equated with it grosso modo, just as the universal objectivity of revelation cannot be reduced to the subjective specificities of any one individual's intellection, to his or her own 'private' revelation. To equate reason with intellect would be tantamount to denying the very existence of the 'buried treasures' of the intellect, those supra-rational dimensions that are, initially, buried beneath and thus hidden from the rational surface of the intellect.

The revelation constituted by the intellect is embedded within the creation of the human being, the intellect being that which most critically defines the human being as such. Far from pitting the intellect against revelation, each is to be seen in the light of the other: the substance of revelation cannot be engaged except through the intellect, and the 'buried treasures' of the intellect cannot be disclosed except through revelation, a genuine co-dependency of the two. This understanding of the intellect brings us close to what the Imam refers to as the 'heart' (قلب), that innermost mode of perception capable of 'seeing' God. It is the 'heart' that can 'see' God, not with eyesight, not with بصارۃ, but with insight, with بصیرۃ, that spiritual insight generated by the 'verities of faith' (haqa'iq al-iman / حقائق الایمان). As the Imam puts it, in response to the questioner asking whether he had 'seen' his Lord: 'Eyes see Him not through sight's observation, but hearts see Him through the verities of faith.'

Concerning the relationship between intellect and reason, one can say that between the spiritual depths of the intellect and its rational surface there is both continuity and discontinuity. The aspect of continuity is manifested in the evident fact that all thinking begins with concepts that convey intelligible meaning; thought sets out from ideas that are rationally discernible, and these must bear some relation to the realities they express, or they collapse into ideas that lead nowhere. But there is also discontinuity between the spiritual and the rational aspects of the intellect, in that reason stops short at the ideas it has 'de-fined'. The Arabic word for definition, hadd (حد), expresses, like its English equivalent, both senses at once: that of a conceptual definition and that of a limitation, a boundary, the very boundary that reason draws around an idea by weighing its pros and cons until it is fixed and 'defined'. To go beyond the limits imposed by this rational act of definition, something more than reason is necessary; and it is this 'something more' that makes the ethos under consideration a spiritual one, and not a rationalistic one. It is certainly 'rational', in the sense that the form of the ethos is indeed susceptible to rational scrutiny; but it is not 'rationalistic', inasmuch as its essence is not predicated on reason alone.

Ali (ع) frequently hints, in highly allusive terms, at the mysteries and marvels, the unearthed treasures of the intellect, that he has directly witnessed by the eye of his purified heart. He also alludes to this supra-rational mode of awareness indirectly, through a bewildering use of apophatic discourse and paradoxical utterance, of which the corpus attributed to him is full. In this ethos it seems that the higher the truth, the deeper the paradox: the sheer incommensurability between reality as it is in itself and the human conceptions formed of it is effectively evoked by this creative use of paradox. In modern parlance, each human conception of reality needs to be 'deconstructed'; but then, unlike a modern deconstructionism so radically destructive that it has nothing left to 'reconstruct', the Imam's apophasis feeds directly into modes of spiritual intuition, contemplation, and realization of that which reason, on its own, is incapable of attaining. It is this intuition and contemplation which 're-members', or 'reconstructs', if one likes, the spiritual realities that transcend the categories of reason. The value of the paradoxes, the antinomies, the apophasis, and the negation is entirely predicated on the eminently positive realities which the intellect can plumb, thereby becoming aware of its own hidden depths. The resulting consciousness does not so much negate reason as situate it, revealing the limits of reason's proper sphere of operation: it reveals that reason alone cannot 'comprehend' the higher realities, nor express them fully, however indispensable it may be in initiating the cognitive movement towards those realities.

Thus, when the Imam negates, as he does so often, the possibility of human conceptions encompassing the divine Reality, what is being negated is the limitation that is the inescapable corollary of all such conceptions, and not the positive content, the ma'na or spiritual meaning, of the conception itself; for this conception is a necessary starting point for a process of assimilation that engages a deeper level of awareness than that 'de-fined' by the mind, and which therefore extends far beyond the domain of speculative thought. In this light, the negation of the limitations of human conception should be seen as the prelude to an absolute affirmation: the affirmation of the Absolute, which has no limitation. The spiritual negation of conceptual limitation is of an upward, 'constructive' order, not a nihilistic, deconstructive one. Nowhere is this style of discourse more apparent than in the famous opening lines of the first sermon of the Nahj al-balagha:

> Praise be to God, whose laudation those who speak cannot deliver, whose graces those who count cannot number, whose rightful due those who strive cannot render; He who cannot be grasped by far-reaching aspirations, nor fathomed by profound intuitions; He whose attribute has no binding limitation, no existing description, no time appointed, no term extended.

The divine attribute (sifa) has no limit in terms of which it can be defined, laysa li-sifatih hadd mahdud, and yet it is affirmed as real. It has no 'existing description', and yet in some sense it is described (mawsuf), by the very words of the Qur'anic revelation; for in the Qur'an God is described as having such attributes as mercy, compassion, and power. He is the Merciful, the Compassionate, the Almighty, and yet the Imam affirms that these very attributes cannot be defined, bounded, or described. The paradox sharpens still further when the Imam speaks of the perfection of ikhlas, the 'rendering sincere', the purification of one's devotion to God and of one's conception of Him: this perfection, he says, is to divest (nafy) God of all attributes. On the one hand the attributes are affirmed, and on the other they are to be negated. The rational mind can only think of God in terms of the attributes given verbal expression in the Qur'an; and yet here the Imam appears to negate these same attributes, thereby depriving the mind of any convenient conceptual handle by which to grasp the nature of the divine Reality. The simultaneous affirmation and negation of the attributes raises an apparently irresolvable contradiction, and in so doing enhances receptivity to a deeper mode of knowing. This style of discourse appears designed to awaken in the listener some latent awareness inscribed in the heart or the depths of the intellect, and to invite the seeker to dive into those depths, into the knowledge 'buried' in the intellect. The very shock of the apparent contradiction in this apophasis seems intended to precipitate a leap of consciousness, from the formal activity of reason to the supra-rational contemplation of the spirit.

The words spoken by the Imam immediately after affirming that his heart had indeed witnessed God, not by means of outward vision but through 'the verities of faith', run as follows:

> My Lord is not described in terms of dimension (bu'd), nor motion (haraka), nor rest (sukun) ... nor through coming or going: He is subtle in subtlety but not described by subtlety, magnificent in magnificence but not described by magnificence, great in greatness but not described by greatness ...

The attributes are again affirmed and denied. God possesses these attributes but is not reducible to them. One can begin to think of God in terms of these categories, but He is not reducible to human conceptions. The attributes afford the mind something by which the divine nature can be understood, but this understanding is fragmentary, never exhaustive, and it needs to be negated as the prelude to a fuller apprehension. The initial conceptual orientation to the divine Reality needs to be both negated and sublimated: negated as a mental conception, while the positive content of the conception needs to be sublimated through spiritual intuition; only then can the two-dimensional 'portrait' defined by reason be given a third dimension, that of depth. But this third dimension emerges only after the insufficiency of the rational depiction is understood, whence the need for that cognitive shock which paradox produces, a shock intended to reveal the pretension of reason in its effort to disclose the nature of the Absolute, the transcendent source of its own activities.

One sees in the Imam's discourses a repeated combination of affirmation and denial, which mirrors the negation and affirmation found in the first testimony of Islam: la ilaha, 'no god', being the negation (nafy); then illa'Llah, 'except God', coming as the affirmation (ithbat). This helps one to see that the negation of the attributes mentioned by the Imam as being the perfection of ikhlas is in fact an affirmation of the infinitude of the divine Reality. Each of the attributes is to be 'negated', but only insofar as it is conceived by the mind. Such negation is necessary because, at the mental level, each attribute excludes other attributes, and also excludes the object of attribution itself, that is, the divine Essence, such exclusion following inescapably from the very possibility of conceptually distinguishing between the attribute and the object of attribution, between the divine quality and the divine Essence. For any conception to take place in the mind, defined ideas are necessary, and where there are definitions, there are limits and thus exclusions. The reality of the attribute, however, is unlimited, by virtue of its absolute identity with the Essence. The attribute that has been 'negated' is paradoxically affirmed, not in itself but inasmuch as it is nothing but God: illa'Llah. Thus, one can place each of the negated attributes in the position of the nafy of the first testimony and arrive at such formulations as la rahmana illa'Llah, 'There is no All-Compassionate except God'; la rahima illa'Llah, 'No All-Merciful except God', and so on. The apparent negation of the attributes becomes, in fact, an affirmation of their true identity, shorn of any restrictive association with the poverty of mental conceptions. The attributes are not superadded to the Essence but are absolutely one with it, differentiable from it only conceptually, not ontologically. The Essence does indeed comprise all the attributes mentioned, such as greatness, subtlety, and so on, but it is also infinitely more than these attributes; and, for their part, the attributes are infinite only within and as the Essence. The Imam's apophasis here can be seen as a commentary on the Qur'anic refrain, 'Glorified be God above what they describe' (23: 91). The infinitude of God strictly requires, on the human plane, the negation of all that the mind can conceive through reason, but not all that the heart can 'see' through spiritual vision. This very negation on the plane of reason calls out to something deeper, and this in turn can only be understood in the light of spiritual praxis, which is always assumed as the reflective, devotional, or contemplative context for the hearers of the Imam's sermons and discourses. The heart, symbolic seat of the contemplative intelligence, calls out to be 'polished', and this is precisely what the 'remembrance of God' does, this dhikr Allah being, according to the Imam, the 'most excellent form of worship', discussed further in Chapter 3. However, even at this level of praxis there is a further paradox: the 'reality' of the remembrance is not attained until 'you forget yourself in your remembrance'.

It is not just the rational mind and the determinate conceptions proportioned thereto that are to be negated or 'deconstructed', but the individual soul itself as the locus of all cognition, rational or supra-rational. This final denouement, or fana', the ultimate form of apophasis, is discussed further in Chapter 3.

These considerations show the extent to which the spirit of the intellect, in Ali (ع)'s perspective, transcends the rational faculty. The intellect must be seen, first and foremost, as a spiritual faculty, and the spirit, in turn, must be appreciated as the source of the intellect, as well as the source of life. Without the intellect the spirit is reduced to unconscious existence; conversely, without the spirit, the intellect tends towards lifeless thought. These aspects of the spirit are clearly expressed in the first sermon of the Nahj, which can be read as a commentary upon the Qur'anic verses describing the creation of man in terms of God's 'breathing' of His spirit into man: 'He began the creation of man with clay ... He fashioned him and breathed into him of His spirit' (ثُمَّ سَوَّىٰهُ وَنَفَخَ فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِهِ, 32: 7–9).

The following lines, from the first sermon, form one of the many passages in which this infusion of the divine spirit into man is commented upon by Ali (ع):

> ثُمَّ جَمَعَ سُبْحَانَهُ مِنْ حَزْنِ الاْرْضِ وَسَهْلِهَا، وَعَذْبِهَا وَسَبَخِهَا۔ تُرْبَةً سَنَّهَا بالمَاءِ حَتَّى خَلَصَتْ۔ وَلاَطَهَا بِالبَلَّةِ حَتَّى لَزَبَتْ۔ فَجَبَلَ مِنْها صُورَةً ذَاتَ أَحْنَاءَ وَوُصُولَ، وَأَعْضَاءَ وَفُصُول۔ أَجْمَدَهَا حَتَّى اسْتَمْسَكَتْ، وَأَصْلَدَهَا حَتَّى صَلْصَلَتْ۔ لِوَقْت مَعْدُود، وَأجَل مَعْلُوم۔ ثُمَّ نَفَخَ فِيها مِنْ رُوحِهِ فَمَثُلَتْ إِنْساناً ذَا أَذْهَانُ يُجيلُهَا، وَفِكَر يَتَصَرَّفُ بِهَا، وَجَوَارِحَ يَخْتَدِمُهَا، وَأَدَوَات يُقَلِّبُهَا، وَمَعْرِفَة يَفْرُقُ بِهَا بَيْنَ الحَقِّ وَالبَاطِلِ۔

> Then He, glorified be He, gathered up a quantity of dust, from some hard earth and some soft, some sweet earth and some salty, and poured water onto it until it was pure, then kneaded into it some moisture until it stuck together; then He fashioned from this a form with curves and joints, limbs and parts. He compacted it until it held tightly together, solidified it until it became as sounding clay, assigning to it a measured duration and a known consummation. Then He breathed into it of His spirit, and it stood up as a human being, endowed with intellectual powers with which to reflect, thoughts by which to conduct himself, limbs to put to service, instruments at his disposal, and knowledge with which to discern between the true and the false ...

The body had been fashioned entire, its eyes, its ears, the organ of thought all in place, and yet it could neither hear nor see nor reflect until God breathed His spirit into it; only then did it stand up as a human being. It is this 'in-spiration', literally, the in-breathing and the insertion of the spirit, of God's breath that produces consciousness and life. Death, by contrast, is the 'ex-piration', the departure of the spirit. Life in the absence of 'aspiration', the intention to be true to one's spirit, is a kind of death. It is through the spirit that inanimate, unconscious matter is brought to life and to awareness of itself: not only does it generate knowledge, but self-knowledge as well. This intimate intertwining between the gift of life and the capacity to know, existence that is conscious, and knowledge that is existential, is one of the foundations of any truly spiritual perspective. The question is not one of setting the spirit against the body, but of recognizing that the infusion of the spirit into the body is precisely what brings the body to life; and it is for this reason that knowledge vivifies, in the very measure that it is spiritual: 'O ye who believe, respond to God and His Messenger when they call you to that which vivifies you' (8: 24). According to the Imam, one of the most important aspects of this call from God to human beings is to 'remind them of the buried treasures of the intellect'.

Existentiation is the bestowal of the spirit, which is consciousness. The 'aql, or intellect, is the principle of articulation for the spirit, and so it is not merely a mental faculty but is itself constitutive of true life. The intellect is that of which the mental faculty is one formal expression, one mode of its operation, but by no means the only one. The sayings of the Imam gathered here show the 'aql to be multi-faceted, comprising aspects that are rarely associated with intelligence today. To be true to one's intellect, to the treasures buried deep within it and not merely to the rational functions operative on its surface, is tantamount to being spiritual.

For Ali (ع), the true intellectual, al-'aqil (العاقل), is one who not only thinks correctly but also acts ethically, and, at the deepest level, one who seeks to realize the ultimate Reality. The intellectual is defined as one who puts all things in their proper place, العاقل من وضع شئی حق محلہ, one who aligns his acts with the intentions of his Creator. This is also the very definition of justice, عدل, as Chapter 2 shows. Only the true intellectual can therefore be fully just, عادل, for only one who sees things as they truly are is able to put them in their right place.

Thought, action, and realization are all the concern of the true intellectual, the 'aqil. Correct thinking, impeccable virtue, and authentic being are inextricably bound up with the intellect in the spiritual ethos considered here. These aspects of the ethos come into sharper focus in the light of the Imam's discourse to Kumayl.
