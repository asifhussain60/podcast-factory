# The Fruits of Certainty

"They rejoice in their intimacy with the spirit of certainty."

The sentence that follows in the discourse to Kumayl bears directly on the sheer happiness that flows from the life of the spirit. In the Arabic expression wa basharu ruh al-yaqin, the primary meaning of the word bashara is to have intimate experience of something, to come into direct physical contact with it. This form of the verb also carries connotations of joy, so that "rejoice in intimate contact" better conveys what the single word expresses. To touch the spirit of certainty is not to arrive at some cold logical fact, the conclusion of a purely mental process. This contact means entering deeply into the very object of one's certainty, ultimately nothing less than the divine itself. Since this reality is infinitely more beatific than any conceivable paradisal bliss, for the Qur'an affirms that the divine ridwan, beatitude or contentment, is greater than Paradise, it cannot but bestow joy upon the soul that opens itself effectively to it:

وَعَدَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَتِ جَنَّتٍۢ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَرُ خَلِدِينَ فِيهَا وَمَسَكِنَ طَيِّبَةًۭ فِى جَنَّتِ عَدْنٍۢ ۚ وَرِضْوَنٌۭ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ أَكْبَرُ ۚ ذَلِكَ هُوَ ٱلْفَوْزُ ٱلْعَظِيمُ

"God has promised the believing men and the believing women gardens beneath which rivers flow, to abide therein forever, and goodly dwellings in gardens of everlasting bliss; and contentment from God is greater. That is the supreme triumph." (9:72)

In the words of Ali (ع), "Learning and reflecting upon knowledge is the delight of the knowers."

This is what one might call an epistemology of joy, for "the most joyous of mankind is the intellectual (al-aqil)." This joyous epistemology stands in sharp contrast to modern scientistic conceptions of knowledge as dry, cold, and abstract, and it deserves careful consideration, for it reveals a crucial dimension of the spiritual knowledge proper to the Imam's perspective, and indeed to all spiritual traditions worthy of the name. Engaging with truths ought to impart happiness, and it does so in the measure that the truths in question are fundamental to one's own being or to Being as such. These two modes of being are ultimately identical, given the uniqueness, the one-and-onliness, of Being. That which has no second, no other, cannot enter the category of number; being is indivisible. Hence the being of the individual cannot ultimately differ from Being as such, however great the existential disparity, or rather incommensurability, between the individual and God. True knowledge is knowledge of Being; it is therefore inevitably a form of self-discovery, even though knowledge of the Absolute is impossible without absolute self-abandonment. The highest discovery calls for the deepest effacement.

In the initial stages of this process, true knowledge as acquired by the intellectual is a kind of self-discovery that brings the happiness of a homecoming, a return from the exile of ignorance. This follows because "the most intellectual of people" is "the one who is closest to God," and "the one who is most alive." To be close to God is to be fully alive, and this true life gives profound joy, the joie de vivre, the sheer joy of living. It is a joy proper to existence as such, and independent of particular experiences that may or may not be pleasurable.

Here the crucial relationship between life and consciousness comes into view: between the fruits of the in-spiration of God's spirit on one hand, and this life of spiritual consciousness and joy on the other. Those who are true to the spirit of God within them genuinely possess joy. It is also by reason of their intimate and unshakeable certainty of the divine goodness and compassion that the true faqih, not so much "the jurist" as "one who truly understands," is defined by the Imam as "he who does not make people despair of the mercy of God, and does not make them lose hope in the gracious spirit of God (rawh Allah)." Such is the sage's immersion in the joyous certainty of God's mercy that there is no room for despair. To despair of this mercy is itself a mark of unbelief:

قَالَ وَمَن يَقْنَطُ مِن رَّحْمَةِ رَبِّهِۦٓ إِلَّا ٱلضَّآلُّونَ

"He said: And who despairs of the mercy of his Lord except those who stray in error?" (15:56)

The divine assurances of mercy in the Qur'an come fully alive in the souls that possess a profound and not merely notional knowledge of the beatific nature of the divine Reality:

وَٱكْتُبْ لَنَا فِى هَذِهِ ٱلدُّنْيَا حَسَنَةًۭ وَفِى ٱلْآخِرَةِ إِنَّا هُدْنَآ إِلَيْكَ ۚ قَالَ عَذَابِىٓ أُصِيبُ بِهِۦ مَنْ أَشَآءُ ۖ وَرَحْمَتِى وَسِعَتْ كُلَّ شَىْءٍۢ ۚ فَسَأَكْتُبُهَا لِلَّذِينَ يَتَّقُونَ وَيُؤْتُونَ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ وَٱلَّذِينَ هُم بِـَٔايَتِنَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

"And ordain for us good in this world and in the Hereafter; truly we have turned to Thee. He said: My chastisement, I smite with it whom I will, and My mercy encompasses all things; and I shall ordain it for those who are God-fearing and give the alms, and those who believe in Our signs." (7:156)

وَإِذَا جَآءَكَ ٱلَّذِينَ يُؤْمِنُونَ بِـَٔايَتِنَا فَقُلْ سَلَمٌ عَلَيْكُمْ ۖ كَتَبَ رَبُّكُمْ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِ ٱلرَّحْمَةَ ۖ أَنَّهُۥ مَنْ عَمِلَ مِنكُمْ سُوٓءًۢا بِجَهَلَةٍۢ ثُمَّ تَابَ مِنۢ بَعْدِهِۦ وَأَصْلَحَ فَأَنَّهُۥ غَفُورٌۭ رَّحِيمٌۭ

"And when those who believe in Our signs come to thee, say: Peace be upon you. Your Lord has prescribed for Himself mercy, so that whoever of you does evil in ignorance, then repents thereafter and makes amends, then truly He is Forgiving, Merciful." (6:54)

These are the ones who truly understand, the true fuqaha'.

This opening to joy through intimate knowledge of God's mercy is inseparable from beauty, the perception of which is another unexpected concomitant of the intellect in the Imam's perspective. The appreciation of beauty is not merely a matter of subjective aesthetics; it is an objective dimension of the intellect's own function. Beauty also leads directly to the heart of ethics. The Arabic ihsan is often translated simply as "virtue" or "excellence," but one of its key literal meanings is "making beautiful," for husn means "beauty." Virtue, ihsan, can thus be understood as that which makes the soul beautiful. In this light, the relationship among intellect, ethics, and aesthetics becomes far clearer. "The excellence of the intellect," says Ali (ع), "is in the beauty of things outward and inward (jamal al-zawahir wa'l-bawatin)." The outward beauty of forms and actions must be complemented by inward beauty, beauty of soul, or virtue in its most integral and penetrating sense. All of this follows naturally from two sayings attributed to the Prophet: "Truly God is beautiful and He loves beauty," and "Assume the character traits of God."

This cultivation of beautiful traits within oneself makes one lovable to God, since He loves beauty. The process of making oneself lovable to God is inseparable from emulating the virtues brought to perfection in the soul of the Prophet:

قُلْ إِن كُنتُمْ تُحِبُّونَ ٱللَّهَ فَٱتَّبِعُونِى يُحْبِبْكُمُ ٱللَّهُ وَيَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ ذُنُوبَكُمْ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌۭ رَّحِيمٌۭ

"Say: If you love God, follow me; God will love you and forgive you your sins. And God is Forgiving, Merciful." (3:31)

This drawing of God's love through making oneself beautiful in the fullest sense is alluded to in another saying of the Imam: "The foundation of the intellect is the assimilation of love (awwal al-'aql al-tawaddud)." Rather than referring simply to love as such (wudd/mawadda), the Imam draws attention to the need to make oneself lovable to God by using the form tawaddud, alluding thereby to the process of integrating and realizing the principle of love within oneself, as well as expressing outwardly the love that is experienced inwardly, love being appreciated at once as outward radiance and inner attraction.

To follow the Prophet, and thus make oneself receptive to the divine influx of love, means, among other things, cultivating the prophetic virtues. These virtues are prophetic not in the sense that they belong exclusively to the Prophet, but because he is held to have realized virtue in all its plenitude and is therefore the exemplar, par excellence, of virtue as such. Beauty and love are thus not the only qualities that articulate the intellect's receptivity to the divine Reality; kindness and contentment, courtesy, generosity and modesty, and wise forbearance (hilm) are equally essential. None of these moral qualities is absent from the soul of the true intellectual. There can be no wisdom or intellect in the fullest sense if the soul lacks them. It is precisely the presence of these virtues that enables the intellect to develop the dimensions of depth and breadth without which it remains confined to the flat, horizontal plane of its own operations, confined to the activity of reason alone, which, until touched by faith and deepened by grace, remains unaware of what lies beneath the surface of its own field of activity, oblivious of the hidden treasures of the intellect, and blind to the need to integrate the whole soul within the mould of that integral, or unifying, virtue which bestows integrity upon intelligence. Such integration, this making one, is absolutely fundamental to tawhid, now grasped as a dynamic process of spiritual unification demanded by the very idea of God's oneness. Truth must clearly inspire the soul; but it is equally necessary that, conversely, virtue should aerate the intelligence, providing the leaven that allows it to expand to its full dimensions.

Sinful, immoral qualities are understood in this perspective not only as vices but as so many blind spots and intellectual dysfunctions. The heart is diseased by vice; its eye is blinded by them and thus rendered unable to see the Real:

فِى قُلُوبِهِم مَّرَضٌۭ فَزَادَهُمُ ٱللَّهُ مَرَضًۭا ۖ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌۢ بِمَا كَانُوا۟ يَكْذِبُونَ

"In their hearts is a disease, and God has increased their disease; and for them is a painful chastisement because they used to lie." (2:10)

أَفَلَمْ يَسِيرُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَتَكُونَ لَهُمْ قُلُوبٌۭ يَعْقِلُونَ بِهَآ أَوْ آذَانٌۭ يَسْمَعُونَ بِهَا ۖ فَإِنَّهَا لَا تَعْمَى ٱلْأَبْصَرُ وَلَكِن تَعْمَى ٱلْقُلُوبُ ٱلَّتِى فِى ٱلصُّدُورِ

"Have they not travelled in the land, so that they might have hearts to understand with, or ears to hear with? For truly it is not the eyes that are blind, but blind are the hearts within the breasts." (22:46)

The vices are all comprised within egotism, which is in turn often described simply as caprice (hawa). In one saying, the Imam refers to the conquest of caprice as tantamount to salvation: "He attains deliverance whose intellect dominates his caprice." Deliverance, falah, which carries the sense both of salvation and beatitude, is thus to some degree accessible here and now; it is not a grace granted only posthumously. This deliverance involves tasting the joy that emanates from certainty, from intimate contact with the spirit of that certainty. As for the means of cultivating receptivity to this spirit of certainty, what is required, over and above obedience to the rules and regulations of the revealed Law, is that combination of purity of intention and sincerity of devotion expressed by the word ikhlas. Without this sincerity or purity, compliance with formal rules is nothing but hypocrisy (nifaq) and pretension (riya), which the Imam in turn defines as the gravest of all sins, shirk, the attribution of partners to God: "Know that the slightest pretension (riya) is polytheism." This sentence can be read as a commentary on one of the Qur'an's most important condemnations of hypocrisy. The verses in question refer to those who deny the essence of faith while outwardly conforming to it:

"Hast thou seen the one who belieth religion? He is the one who repelleth the orphan and urgeth not the feeding of the poor. So woe be to those who pray, those who are heedless of their prayer, who want only to be seen, and withhold small kindnesses." (107: 1-7)

This short sura makes clear that formal acts of devotion have no value whatsoever unless they are integrated into a life of virtue, and unless they are performed with the right intention, that is, with ikhlas. It also helps explain why the Imam's ethos is described here not merely as religious but as spiritual: it takes the ideals of religion to their most profound application. To be spiritual in this sense is precisely to be true to one's intellect, true to the treasures buried deep within it, and not merely to the rational functions operating on its surface. Without this dimension of depth, religion is meaningless. "There is no religion for one who has no intellect," says the Imam. Likewise: "Action without knowledge is error." What counts is the intention motivating action, for "action, all action, is dust, except what is purified within it." This detachment from action as such, and the orientation instead toward an inner state of being from which good actions cannot but emerge, clearly reflects the Qur'anic teaching:

لَن يَنَالَ ٱللَّهَ لُحُومُهَا وَلَا دِمَآؤُهَا وَلَكِن يَنَالُهُ ٱلتَّقْوَىٰ مِنكُمْ ۚ كَذَلِكَ سَخَّرَهَا لَكُمْ لِتُكَبِّرُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ مَا هَدَىٰكُمْ ۗ وَبَشِّرِ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ

"Their flesh will not reach God, nor their blood, but piety from you reaches Him. Thus has He subjected them to you, that you may magnify God for having guided you. And give glad tidings to those who do good." (22:37)

No group perhaps better exemplified the principle in the saying "There is no religion for one who has no intellect" than the Kharijites. Among those who rebelled against the Imam were numerous readers (qurra', sing. qari') of the Qur'an, people who enjoyed considerable religious prestige on account of their Qur'anic knowledge and intense worship. Their piety, however, was more apparent than real; it was, in fact, a zeal of bitterness, a caricature of true piety. When his supporters mentioned the long night vigils performed by members of this group, the Imam gave a sharp reply: "Sleeping with certainty is better than praying with doubt." Certainty imparts peace and joy; thus sleep, for one immersed in what the Imam calls the spirit of certainty, is a bodily repose that only deepens the happiness flowing from a good conscience and spiritual certitude, the latter understood as a kind of permanent wakefulness, in the sense of being awake to higher realities. Bodily sleep does not, therefore, contradict spiritual wakefulness. As the hadith of the Prophet has it: "My eyes sleep, but my heart sleeps not."

In relation to formal worship, the true intellectual, the 'aqil, whom the Imam defines as the one who puts each thing in its proper place (العاقل من وضع شئی حق محلہ), is one who obeys the divine injunction to pray. But rather than praying in mechanical fashion and supposing that the action alone suffices, he knows that such obedience is a necessary but not sufficient condition for achieving receptivity to grace. There must be consciousness of what one is doing and why one is doing it; it is this awareness, together with virtue, inward beauty, that attracts divine acceptance. "The most worthy knowledge for you," says the Imam, "is that by virtue of whose presence action is accepted by God."

"They make easy what the extravagant find harsh; they befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged."

This sentence bears, among other things, on the impact upon the soul of true sages made by recollection of the inevitability of death and the consequent necessity of renunciation within this world. In the first place, what the extravagant, the avid, worldly types, find harsh is outward adversity, difficulty, and deprivation. These are all circumstances which the Qur'an views as the trials that inevitably accompany life in this world:

"And We shall try you with something of fear and hunger and loss of property and life and fruits; but give glad tidings to the patient, those who, when struck by affliction say: Verily, we belong to God and verily unto Him we are ever-returning. These are the ones upon whom there are blessings from their Lord, and mercy; and these are the ones who are rightly guided." (2: 155-157)

The wise, the rightly-guided (al-muhtadun), perceive the blessings showered upon those who are patient in the face of affliction, a patience that is itself a concomitant of their knowledge of the truth: "By the Age, truly man is in a state of loss, except those who believe and do virtuous deeds and exhort one another to truth and exhort one another to patience" (103: 1-3). These are the believers who remain steadfast in the face of trials, firmly rooted in their awareness of their origin and end, and in the knowledge that unto Him we are ever-returning. For this reason, they can make easy the trials and difficulties of life, whereas the extravagant experience them solely in their aspect of harshness and bitterness. The ease that inheres in every difficulty is hidden from the ignorant but discerned by the wise: "Verily, with hardship cometh ease; verily with hardship cometh ease" (94: 5-6). The wise go even further than merely anticipating ease as the inevitable fruit of hardship; they taste this fruit already as the seed hidden within hardship itself, for they actually befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged.

One level of meaning here is illuminated by this saying of Ali (ع): "Death is more intimate to me than the breast of the mother is to the suckling babe." In the verse cited above, the mention of trials and difficulties is preceded by a reminder that those slain in the path of God are not truly dead, "nay, they are alive but ye perceive not." This is not simply an affirmation of the life hereafter; it is also an allusion to the esoteric meaning of being slain for God: al-fana' fi'Llah, extinction in God. The Imam's intimacy with death can be understood in relation to this death while still alive, which he also alludes to in another saying: "I am the death of the dead." Because life in the world is itself a kind of death, a limitation, and hence a privation, in contrast to the infinite plenitude of the divine realms, dying to this death is tantamount to true life:

وَمَا هَذِهِ ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَآ إِلَّا لَهْوٌۭ وَلَعِبٌۭ ۚ وَإِنَّ ٱلدَّارَ ٱلْآخِرَةَ لَهِىَ ٱلْحَيَوَانُ ۚ لَوْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْلَمُونَ

"And this life of the world is nothing but sport and play; and truly the abode of the Hereafter, that indeed is life, did they but know." (29:64)

It is not the physical world as such that is to be negated; rather, it is worldliness, egotistic attachment to the world, that must be overcome. One of the key principles of the Imam's perspective, stressed repeatedly throughout the Nahj al-balagha, bears on this: the principle of zuhd, which no single English word can render adequately. It comprises several meanings: asceticism, austerity, abstention, detachment, doing without, and making little of. It is in this attitude of the zahid toward the world that the fruits of certainty ripen into a settled freedom, the soul holding the world lightly because it has already tasted the life that lies beyond it.

The Imam describes the world as an abode for which annihilation is ordained, and whose people are decreed to depart from it. It is sweet and green; it hastens to the one who seeks it and confounds the heart that gazes upon it. "So set out from it with the best of the provision available to you, and ask not from it above what suffices, nor seek from it more than what can be attained."

What is this "best of provision" (zad)? In many places in the Nahj it is described as taqwa, another word that resists simple translation, its meaning encompassing piety, fear of God, awe, and God-consciousness. "I exhort you, O slaves of God, to taqwa, which is the provision," says the Imam. This provision for the next world is attained only in the measure of one's detachment from the perishable aspects of the life of this world, for "whoso desireth that life of the world which hasteneth away, in that life We hasten for him what We will ..." (17:18).

The Imam describes the despicable aspect of the world in vivid terms. "By God, this world of yours is more contemptible in my eyes than a chewed-up bone of a pig in the hand of a leper." "An image of the world is the snake: soft to touch, but virulent poison within." The world, he says, is "as worthless to me as that which drips from the nose of a goat." It is the grandeur and beauty of God that render the world insignificant and distasteful; only in the light of the Absolute do the pleasures and attractions of the world appear fleeting and illusory. "The greatness of the Creator for you belittles creatures in your view." In a similar vein the Imam speaks of a "brother in God": "What made him great in my eyes was the smallness of the world in his eyes" (kana yu'azzimuhu fī 'aynī sighar al-dunya fi 'aynihi).

This, according to the Qur'anic usage of zahid, is the real meaning of zuhd. In the sura named after Joseph (Yusuf), the brothers of Yusuf "deemed him of little value" in selling him for a small price (12:20); they are described in relation to him as zahidin. In the Imam's perspective the world is likewise deemed of little value, not altogether valueless, but rendered all but insignificant in relation to the Hereafter, to eternity, to ultimate reality. All created things are reduced to infinitesimal, fleeting shadows destined for annihilation: "Everything perishes except His Face" (28:88). Yet the world is not deprived of all value; its significance lies precisely in what it cannot help but signify: "And there is nothing that does not hymn His glory with praise" (17:44). Everything in the world, every creature in existence, proclaims the infinite creativity of the Creator. To fathom the mystery of this universal praise, the Qur'an offers an image: "And the birds in flight, each one knoweth its prayer and glorification" (24:41).

It is therefore not at all contradictory for the Imam to despise the world and yet wax lyrical in his descriptions of the beauties of virgin nature, including that unsurpassable marvel, the peacock. This comes in a sermon that also describes birds in general, as well as ants, serpents, and elephants. The sermon concludes with a galvanizing description of Paradise, juxtaposing the marvels of this world with the delights of the next; in the light of the latter, all terrestrial phenomena pale into insignificance. For once the "eye of the heart" is directed toward Paradise, "your soul would become averse to the marvels of this world, its passions, its pleasures and its embellished scenery."

It might seem contradictory to bring together such apparently opposed qualities as joy in certainty and the befriending of hardship. But the latter is in fact the fruit of the former. All difficulties without exception are endured through the spiritual resources made available by faith combined with intellectual assimilation, or rather, in the measure that faith is intellectually assimilated and thus becomes ma'rifa or gnosis, comprising a certainty so deep and so complete that even if the veil be lifted it cannot increase, according to the famous saying. It is possible to believe, at one level of the mind, that there is no god but God. The Imam, however, draws attention to what this apparently simple belief should imply when it is sincere or pure: "I bear witness that there is no god but God alone Who has no partner, a testimony whose sincerity has been tested and whose essence has been assimilated with conviction (mu'taqadan musaffaha)."

The word musaffa refers to the choicest and purest part of any substance, its very essence. The root massa means "to suck," the idea being that the liquid or juice of a substance or fruit holds its essence, that which is extracted from it. In the present context the Imam is stressing the spiritual substance of the testimony, as distinct from mere verbal affirmation or mental comprehension of the words. He affirms a heartfelt connection with the reality to which the testimony bears witness; the words that define it are an echo of the reality they convey, and are not to be confused with that reality itself. He continues: "We cling to it always, for as long as we endure, storing it up as protection against the terrors that will befall us. For truly, it renders faith resolute, opens up spiritual virtue, pleases al-Rahman (the All-Compassionate), repels al-shaytan (the devil)."

The befriending of that by which the ignorant are estranged points not only to the trials of life, which are a source of strength for the wise and a source of repulsion for the ignorant, but also to the ineluctability of death. For the wise, death means release, the encounter with God, blissful eternity, in short a mode of tawhid, whereby the soul enjoys ever deeper degrees of integration within higher principles. For the ignorant, by contrast, death is perceived as a frightful disintegration, the end of their world rather than the beginning of endless life. Here too belongs the prefiguration of death in life: fana', "extinction," "effacement," the perishing of egoistic consciousness before the Absolute. The ignorant recoil in horror at the prospect of losing what they falsely take to be their life, not realizing that this sacrifice of egoistic and limited self-consciousness is the very ground on which infinite consciousness is glimpsed or tasted. One can only taste the ocean of the Real by being drowned in it.

The means to this ultimate attainment is likewise embraced by the wise and feared by the ignorant, namely the act of pure prayer, total concentration on the Real. This concentration strictly precludes all worldly or egotistic preoccupations; the sheer intensity of focus upon the Absolute burns away thoughts of the relative, and for those who ignorantly identify with relativities, both inward and outward, this amounts to a kind of death. For the wise, by contrast, this concentration on the Absolute, the unsullied remembrance of God, is a joyous contemplation of reality before which the ego is displaced and all illusions are dispelled. This is expressed in the words of the passage: "With their bodies, they keep company with the world, while their spirits are tied to the transcendent realm."

This sentence becomes more concrete when read alongside another well-known expression of the Imam, who describes himself as belonging to a group whose spiritual degree is such that "their hearts are in the Gardens of Paradise while their bodies are at work." To be tied or attached to that realm, encompassing the paradisal abodes and extending up to the divine Presence itself, means that they are already within that transcendent realm more fully than they are within this earthly one. This helps explain the saying that God keeps man perpetually in that which his soul desires. Those whose hearts or spirits are in the transcendent domain are those whose consciousness is substantially, not merely conceptually, determined by transcendent realities. Those whose consciousness, by contrast, is shaped by worldly concerns are in this world in the sense of being enclosed by its formal limitations; their orientation and imagination are trapped within a worldly framework. Their actions bear the mark of this worldliness, whereas the actions of the wise are the fruits of their immersion in a higher reality. Their outward actions in the world belie their inward transcendence of all action; they act, yet are not bound by the fruits of their action. They know that "action, all action, is dust, except what is purified within it."

The notion of the Hereafter plays a distinct role within the Imam's ethical framework, for the Hereafter is repeatedly invoked in his discourses. Is this simply a matter of holding out a heavenly reward and threatening a hellish punishment in order to "enjoin the good and forbid the evil"? Even if the answer is partly affirmative, there is a deeper dimension to this apparently straightforward use of divine warnings and promises as levers for moral action.

The ethical orientation here is defined not merely by belief in God but by the spiritual reality of the Hereafter as well, both objectively, in itself, and subjectively, insofar as it is assimilated by the individual. It thereby becomes a dynamic dimension, a process, an orientation, a reflux, within the believer's own soul, rather than a bare belief or hope. Anticipation of the Hereafter characterizes every believer in principle; its realization characterizes the soul of the wali Allah, the friend of God, the saint and the sage. The Imam speaks in a famous sentence that bears witness to his total immersion in the reality of which he is certain: "Were the veil to be removed, I would not increase in certitude (law kushifa'l-ghita', ma azdadtu yaqinan)." The foretaste of the Hereafter deepens, rather than diminishes, in proportion to one's virtue, piety, and sanctity; it does not become a merely heuristic device whose value is exhausted once its moral impact is achieved. A moving passage from the Nahj bears reflection in this light, a description of a blissful vision of the reality of Paradise, not merely a rhetorical flourish in the service of moral exhortation:

"If you were to cast the eye of your heart towards what is described for you of the Garden, your soul would become averse to the marvels of this world, its passions, its pleasures and its embellished scenery. And your soul would be rapt in contemplation of the swaying trees whose roots lie hidden in dunes of musk on the banks of the rivers of the Garden, and the clusters of dazzling pearls hanging down from the branches of its trees ..."

The highest degree of spirituality transcends the desire for Paradise and the fear of Hell, along with the moral conduct proportioned to them. But this means that when such higher degrees of realization are attained, the soul's state can properly be described as paradisal, that is, as being already so utterly content in the beatific presence of God that it desires nothing more. This state of soul is called in the Qur'an al-nafs al-mutma'inna (89:27), "the soul at peace in absolute certainty."

The Imam frequently echoes, implicitly if not explicitly, the Qur'anic principle that "the Hereafter is better and more lasting" (87:17). The Hereafter is not only the time at which divine warnings and promises reach their consummation; it is also the space in which all positive qualities, human, angelic, and divine, are manifested in their fullness, those qualities that appear on earth as reflections of higher realities and that encompass and subtly penetrate the entire sphere of terrestrial existence. The Qur'an, perhaps more than any other scripture, furnishes the imagination with numerous and complex descriptions of paradisal bliss. One of the key functions of these descriptions is alluded to in the Qur'an itself: "Assuredly, We purify them with a pure quality, remembrance of the Abode of the Hereafter" (38:46). The mere thought of Paradise is itself a purification of the mind and heart, a means of guarding the soul against the ever-present temptation to seek its ultimate happiness and well-being in this world alone. Conversely, one's well-being in the herebelow, in regard to action, lies in the performance of good deeds, and such well-being is proportioned to the depth of one's belief that "Whatever good ye send before for your souls, ye will find it with God, better and more tremendous as reward" (73:20). A sincere orientation toward the Hereafter is thus transformed into a perception of heavenly beatitude already in the herebelow, in the form of those very acts and attitudes which, being good, share the same nature as the felicity to which they lead. In this light, morally good actions and noble intentions are grasped by one who sees beyond the veil of this world as vivid prefigurations of heavenly realities, not merely as ethical prerequisites for salvation. Goodness does not simply lead to a heavenly reward; it already participates in that reward: "Is the reward of goodness (ihsan) aught but goodness?" (55:60). The Imam's saying, "The fruit of good action is like its root," can be read as an echo of the idea that the end is already present in the means that lead to it. One verse of the Qur'an fittingly closes this theme and opens toward what follows:

"And give good tidings to those who believe and perform virtuous deeds, that for them are Gardens underneath which rivers flow. Every time they are given to eat from the fruits thereof, they say: 'This is what we were given to eat before.' And they were given the like thereof" (2:25).

The fruits of the paradisal gardens are thus experienced already in the herebelow, in the form of all goodness, beauty, and truth, and the diverse modes of happiness that flow from them. All such experiences are so many foretastes of the ultimate beatitude in Paradise. On the ethical plane, the performance of good deeds, and even more directly the realization of intrinsic virtue, ihsan, is therefore not simply a prerequisite for posthumous salvation, but is already a kind of deliverance, here and now: a deliverance from the imprisonment of sin on the outward plane, and from the bondage of egocentricity on the inward plane. This theme of salvation here and now, grounded in unshakeable certitude, is fundamental to the spirit of the Imam's teachings. What follows turns to the cardinal virtue of justice within Ali (ع)'s perspective, rooted as it is in ihsan, a virtue defined by the Prophet as worshipping God as if one saw Him, and concretely affirmed by the Imam as real and not merely hypothetical, for God is indeed "seen" by the pure of heart "through the verities of faith."
