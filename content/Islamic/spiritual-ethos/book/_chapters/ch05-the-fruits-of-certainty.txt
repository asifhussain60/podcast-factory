# The Fruits of Certainty

The discourse to Kumayl continues: "They rejoice in their intimacy with the spirit of certainty." This sentence speaks directly to the sheer happiness that flows from the life of the spirit. In the Arabic phrase *wa basharu ruh al-yaqin*, the word *bashara* means first of all to have intimate experience of something, to come into direct physical contact with it. In this form the verb also carries a sense of joy, so that "to rejoice in intimate contact" conveys better what the single word holds. To touch the spirit of certainty is not to reach some cold logical fact at the end of a purely mental process. It means entering deeply into the very object of one's certainty — and that object, in the end, is nothing less than the divine itself. This reality is infinitely more blessed than any bliss of Paradise one could imagine, for the Quran affirms that the divine رِضْوَان, its beatitude and contentment, is "greater" than Paradise. It cannot, then, but bestow joy upon the soul that truly opens itself to it:

> وَعَدَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَتِ جَنَّتٍۢ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَرُ خَلِدِينَ فِيهَا وَمَسَكِنَ طَيِّبَةًۭ فِى جَنَّتِ عَدْنٍۢ ۚ وَرِضْوَنٌۭ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ أَكْبَرُ ۚ ذَلِكَ هُوَ ٱلْفَوْزُ ٱلْعَظِيمُ
>
> "God has promised the believers, men and women, gardens beneath which rivers flow, wherein they abide, and goodly dwellings in gardens of Eden. And beatitude from God is greater. That is the supreme triumph" (At-Tawbah: 72).

In the words of Ali (ع), "Learning and reflecting upon knowledge is the delight of the knowers."

This might be called a way of knowing steeped in joy, for "the most joyous of mankind is the intellectual (اَلْعَاقِل)." Such a view stands in sharp contrast to the modern conception, modeled on science, that treats knowledge as dry, cold, and abstract. It deserves careful thought, for it opens up a crucial dimension of the spiritual knowledge proper to the Imam's perspective, and indeed to every spiritual tradition worthy of the name. To engage with truths ought to bring happiness, and it does so to the degree that those truths are fundamental to one's own being, or to Being as such. In the end these two are one and the same, given the uniqueness — the "one-and-onliness" — of Being. What has no second, no other, cannot be counted; Being is indivisible. So the being of the individual cannot finally differ from Being as such, however vast the difference in existence between the individual and God — a difference, indeed, past all common measure. True knowledge is knowledge of Being, and so it cannot help but be a form of self-discovery, even though knowledge of the Absolute is impossible without absolute self-abandonment. The highest discovery calls for the deepest self-effacement.

In the early stages, though, the true knowledge the intellectual gains is a self-discovery that brings the happiness of a homecoming — a return from the exile of ignorance. This is so because "the most intellectual of people" is "the one who is closest to God" and "the one who is most alive." To be close to God is to be fully alive, and this true life gives a deep joy, the *joie de vivre*, the sheer joy of living. It is a joy that belongs to existence itself, independent of any particular experience, pleasant or not.

Here the crucial bond between life and consciousness comes into view — between the fruits of the in-spiration of God's spirit on one side, and this life of spiritual awareness and joy on the other. Those who are true to the spirit of God within them truly possess joy. It is likewise from their intimate and unshakeable certainty of God's goodness and compassion that the Imam defines the true فَقِيهٌ — not so much "the jurist" as "one who truly understands" — as "he who does not make people despair of the mercy of God, and does not make them lose hope in the gracious spirit of God (*rawh Allah*)." So deeply is the sage immersed in the joyous certainty of God's mercy that no room is left for despair. To despair of this mercy is itself a mark of unbelief:

> قَالَ وَمَن يَقْنَطُ مِن رَّحْمَةِ رَبِّهِۦٓ إِلَّا ٱلضَّآلُّونَ
>
> "And who despaireth of the mercy of his Lord except those who stray in error?" (Al-Hijr: 56).

The Quran's assurances of mercy come fully alive in souls that possess a deep knowledge of the blessed nature of the divine Reality — a knowledge lived, not merely thought:

> وَٱكْتُبْ لَنَا فِى هَذِهِ ٱلدُّنْيَا حَسَنَةًۭ وَفِى ٱلْآخِرَةِ إِنَّا هُدْنَآ إِلَيْكَ ۚ قَالَ عَذَابِىٓ أُصِيبُ بِهِۦ مَنْ أَشَآءُ ۖ وَرَحْمَتِى وَسِعَتْ كُلَّ شَىْءٍۢ ۚ فَسَأَكْتُبُهَا لِلَّذِينَ يَتَّقُونَ وَيُؤْتُونَ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ وَٱلَّذِينَ هُم بِـَٔايَتِنَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

"My mercy encompasseth all things" (Al-Araf: 156), and

> وَإِذَا جَآءَكَ ٱلَّذِينَ يُؤْمِنُونَ بِـَٔايَتِنَا فَقُلْ سَلَمٌ عَلَيْكُمْ ۖ كَتَبَ رَبُّكُمْ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِ ٱلرَّحْمَةَ ۖ أَنَّهُۥ مَنْ عَمِلَ مِنكُمْ سُوٓءًۢا بِجَهَلَةٍۢ ثُمَّ تَابَ مِنۢ بَعْدِهِۦ وَأَصْلَحَ فَأَنَّهُۥ غَفُورٌۭ رَّحِيمٌۭ
>
> "thy Lord has prescribed for Himself mercy" (Al-Anam: 54).

These are the ones who truly understand, the true فُقَهَاءُ.

This opening to joy through intimate knowledge of God's mercy cannot be separated from beauty, and in the Imam's view the perception of beauty is another unexpected companion of the intellect. The love of beauty is not merely a matter of private taste; it belongs objectively to the intellect's own working. Beauty also leads straight to the heart of morality. The Arabic احسان is often translated simply as "virtue" or "excellence," yet one of its plain, literal meanings is "making beautiful," since حَسَنَ means "beauty." Virtue, احسان, can thus be understood as what makes the soul beautiful. Seen this way, the bond among intellect, morality, and beauty grows far clearer. "The excellence of the intellect," says Ali, "is in the beauty of things outward and inward (جَمَالُ الظَّوَاهِرِ وَالْبَوَاطِنِ)." The outward beauty of forms and actions must be matched by inward beauty — beauty of soul, or virtue in its fullest and deepest sense. All of this follows naturally from two sayings attributed to the Prophet: "Truly God is beautiful and He loves beauty," and "Assume the character traits of God."

To cultivate beautiful traits within oneself makes one lovable to God, since He loves beauty. And making oneself lovable to God cannot be separated from imitating the virtues brought to perfection in the soul of the Prophet:

> قُلْ إِن كُنتُمْ تُحِبُّونَ ٱللَّهَ فَٱتَّبِعُونِى يُحْبِبْكُمُ ٱللَّهُ وَيَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ ذُنُوبَكُمْ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌۭ رَّحِيمٌۭ
>
> "Say: O ye who believe, if ye love God, follow me, the Prophet; God will love you" (Ali 'Imran: 31).

Another saying of the Imam points to this drawing-down of God's love through making oneself beautiful in the fullest sense: "The foundation of the intellect is the assimilation of love (أَوَّلُ الْعَقْلِ التَّوَدُّدُ)." Instead of speaking of love as such (*wudd/mawadda*), the Imam chooses the form تَوَدُّد to draw attention to the need to make oneself lovable to God. The word points to a twofold work: taking the principle of love into oneself and making it real there, and at the same time letting the love felt within shine outward — for love is at once an outward radiance and an inward attraction.

To follow the Prophet, and so make oneself open to the inflowing of divine love, means among other things cultivating the prophetic virtues. These virtues are called prophetic not because they belong to the Prophet alone, but because he is held to have realized virtue in all its fullness and is therefore the exemplar, par excellence, of virtue itself. Beauty and love are not the only qualities that give shape to the intellect's openness to the divine Reality. Kindness and contentment, courtesy, generosity and modesty, and wise forbearance (حِلْم) matter just as much. Not one of these moral qualities is missing from the soul of the true intellectual. There can be no wisdom or intellect in the fullest sense in a soul that lacks them. It is exactly the presence of these virtues that lets the intellect grow in depth and in breadth. Without them it stays trapped on the flat, horizontal plane of its own operations, held to the work of reason alone. Such reason, until faith touches it and grace deepens it, does not know what lies beneath the surface of its own field. It stays blind to the hidden treasures of the intellect, and blind to the need to gather the whole soul within the mold of that integral, or unifying, virtue which gives intelligence its integrity. This making-one, this integration, lies at the very root of تَوْحِيد, now understood as a living process of spiritual unification demanded by the very idea of God's oneness. Truth must plainly inspire the soul; but just as necessary, in the other direction, is that virtue should aerate the intelligence — the leaven that lets it rise to its full dimensions.

In this perspective, sinful and immoral qualities are seen not only as vices but as so many blind spots, so many failures of the intellect. Vice sickens the heart; it blinds the heart's eye and so leaves it unable to see the Real. As the Quran says:

> فِى قُلُوبِهِم مَّرَضٌۭ فَزَادَهُمُ ٱللَّهُ مَرَضًۭا ۖ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌۢ بِمَا كَانُوا۟ يَكْذِبُونَ

"In their hearts is a disease and God increased them in disease" (Al-Baqarah: 10), and

> أَفَلَمْ يَسِيرُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَتَكُونَ لَهُمْ قُلُوبٌۭ يَعْقِلُونَ بِهَآ أَوْ آذَانٌۭ يَسْمَعُونَ بِهَا ۖ فَإِنَّهَا لَا تَعْمَى ٱلْأَبْصَرُ وَلَكِن تَعْمَى ٱلْقُلُوبُ ٱلَّتِى فِى ٱلصُّدُورِ
>
> "It is not the sights that are blind, but blind are the hearts" (Al-Hajj: 46).

All the vices are contained within egotism, which in turn is often called simply caprice (هَوَى). In one saying the Imam treats the conquest of caprice as the same as salvation: "He attains deliverance whose intellect dominates his caprice." Deliverance, فَلَاحٌ, which carries the sense both of salvation and of blessedness, is thus within reach to some degree here and now; it is not a grace granted only after death. Such deliverance means tasting the joy that flows from certainty, from intimate contact with the spirit of that certainty. As for how to cultivate an openness to this spirit of certainty: beyond obedience to the rules and regulations of the revealed Law, what is required is that union of pure intention and sincere devotion that the word إِخْلَاص expresses. Without this sincerity, this purity, obedience to formal rules is nothing but hypocrisy (نِفَاقٌ) and pretension (رِيَاء) — which the Imam in turn calls the gravest of all sins, شِرْك, the setting-up of partners to God: "Know that the slightest pretension (رِيَاء) is polytheism." This saying can be read as a commentary on one of the Quran's weightiest condemnations of hypocrisy. The verses speak of those who deny the essence of faith while outwardly keeping to it:

"Hast thou seen the one who belieth religion? He is the one who repelleth the orphan and urgeth not the feeding of the poor. So woe be to those who pray, those who are heedless of their prayer, who want only to be seen, and withhold small kindnesses" (Al-Maun: 1-7).

This short sura makes plain that formal acts of devotion have no value at all unless they are woven into a life of virtue and performed with the right intention — that is, with إِخْلَاص. It also helps explain why the Imam's ethos is called here not merely religious but spiritual: it carries the ideals of religion to their deepest application. To be spiritual in this sense is precisely to be true to one's intellect, true to the treasures buried deep within it, and not merely to the reasoning that works on its surface. Without this depth, religion is empty. "There is no religion for one who has no intellect," says the Imam. And likewise: "Action without knowledge is error." What counts is the intention behind the action, for "action, all action, is dust, except what is purified within it." This turning away from the action in itself, toward an inner state of being from which good actions must flow, clearly reflects the Quranic teaching:

> لَن يَنَالَ ٱللَّهَ لُحُومُهَا وَلَا دِمَآؤُهَا وَلَكِن يَنَالُهُ ٱلتَّقْوَىٰ مِنكُمْ ۚ كَذَلِكَ سَخَّرَهَا لَكُمْ لِتُكَبِّرُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ مَا هَدَىٰكُمْ ۗ وَبَشِّرِ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ
>
> "It is not their sacrificed flesh nor their blood that reacheth God, but it is piety from you that reacheth Him" (Al-Hajj: 37).

"There is no religion for one who has no intellect." Perhaps no group shows the truth of this saying better than the Kharijites. Among those who rebelled against the Imam were many readers (قُرَّاء, sing. *qari*) of the Quran — men who enjoyed considerable religious prestige for their Quranic knowledge and their intense worship. Their piety, though, was more apparent than real; it was in fact a zeal born of bitterness, a caricature of true piety. When his supporters spoke of the long night vigils kept by members of this group, the Imam answered sharply: "Sleeping with certainty is better than praying with doubt." Certainty brings peace and joy. So for one immersed in what the Imam calls the spirit of certainty, sleep is a rest of the body that only deepens the happiness flowing from a good conscience and from spiritual certitude — a certitude that is itself a kind of unbroken wakefulness, an awakeness to higher realities. Sleep of the body, then, does not contradict wakefulness of the spirit. As the hadith of the Prophet has it: "My eyes sleep, but my heart sleeps not."

When it comes to formal worship, the true intellectual, the الْعَاقِل — whom the Imam defines as the one who puts each thing in its proper place (العاقل من وضع شئی حق محلہ) — is one who obeys the divine command to pray. Yet he does not pray by rote, supposing that the act alone is enough. He knows that such obedience is necessary but not sufficient for opening himself to grace. There must be awareness of what one is doing and why; and it is this awareness, together with virtue, inward beauty, that draws divine acceptance. "The most worthy knowledge for you," says the Imam, "is that by virtue of whose presence action is accepted by God."

"They make easy what the extravagant find harsh; they befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged." This sentence bears, among other things, on how the remembrance of death — its certainty, and the renunciation of this world that certainty demands — works upon the souls of the true sages. To begin with, what the extravagant, the greedy, worldly sort find harsh is outward hardship, difficulty, and loss. The Quran views all such circumstances as the trials that must accompany life in this world:

"And We shall try you with something of fear and hunger and loss of property and life and fruits; but give glad tidings to the patient, those who, when struck by affliction say: 'Verily, we belong to God and verily unto Him we are ever-returning.' These are the ones upon whom there are blessings from their Lord, and mercy; and these are the ones who are rightly guided" (Al-Baqarah: 155-157).

The passage continues cleanly. Here is the articulated chapter prose.

The rightly-guided perceive the blessings poured out upon those who stay patient under affliction—a patience that grows from their knowledge of the truth: "By the Age, truly man is in a state of loss, except those who believe and do virtuous deeds and exhort one another to truth and exhort one another to patience" (Al-Asr: 1-3). These believers stand firm through trials, rooted in their awareness of where they came from and where they return, and in the knowledge that unto Him we are ever-returning. So they can make the trials and hardships of life easy to bear, while the extravagant meet those same trials only as harshness and bitterness. The ease folded into every difficulty stays hidden from the ignorant but is plain to the wise: "Verily, with hardship cometh ease; verily with hardship cometh ease" (Ash-Sharh: 5-6). The wise do more than await ease as the certain fruit of hardship; they taste that fruit already as the seed hidden inside hardship itself, for they befriend the very thing that the ignorant flee.

One layer of this meaning is lit by a saying of Ali: "Death is more intimate to me than the breast of the mother is to the suckling babe." In the verse cited above, the mention of trials and hardships follows a reminder that those slain in the path of God are not truly dead, "nay, they are alive but ye perceive not." This affirms the life hereafter, but it also points to the inner meaning of being slain for God: اَلْفَنَاءُ فِي اللَّهِ, extinction in God. The Imam's intimacy with death can be understood in relation to this death while still alive, which he touches on in another saying: "I am the death of the dead." Because life in the world is itself a kind of death—a limitation, and so a lack—set against the boundless fullness of the divine realms, dying to this death amounts to true life:

> وَمَا هَذِهِ ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَآ إِلَّا لَهْوٌۭ وَلَعِبٌۭ ۚ وَإِنَّ ٱلدَّارَ ٱلْآخِرَةَ لَهِىَ ٱلْحَيَوَانُ ۚ لَوْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْلَمُونَ

"And the life of this world is nothing but sport and play," says the Quran, "and verily, the abode of the Hereafter, that is true life, did they but know" (Al-Ankabut: 64).

What must be negated is not the physical world as such, but worldliness—the self-centered attachment to the world. One key principle of the Imam's outlook, stressed again and again throughout the *Nahj al-balagha*, bears directly here: the principle of زُهْدٌ, which no single English word can render adequately. It gathers several meanings: asceticism, austerity, abstention, detachment, doing without, and making little of. The following passage from the *Nahj* conveys well the attitude of the *zahid* toward this world:

The world is an abode for which annihilation is ordained, and for its people departure from it is decreed. It is sweet and green, and it hastens to one who seeks it, and confounds the heart that gazes upon it. So set out from it with the best of the provision available to you, and ask not from it above what suffices and seek not from it more than what can be attained.

What is this best of provision, this زَادٌ? In many places the *Nahj* describes it as تَقْوَى, another word that resists simple translation, its meaning spanning piety, fear of God, awe, and God-consciousness. "I exhort you, O slaves of God, to تَقْوَى, which is the provision," says the Imam. This provision for the next world is won only in the measure of one's detachment from the perishable side of the life of this world, for "whoso desireth that which hasteneth away, in that We hasten for him what We will":

> مَّن كَانَ يُرِيدُ ٱلْعَاجِلَةَ عَجَّلْنَا لَهُۥ فِيهَا مَا نَشَآءُ لِمَن نُّرِيدُ ثُمَّ جَعَلْنَا لَهُۥ جَهَنَّمَ يَصْلَىٰهَا مَذْمُومًۭا مَّدْحُورًۭا
>
> "Whoever desires the fleeting life, We hasten for him therein what We will, for whom We intend; then We appoint for him Hell, wherein he burns, disgraced and rejected." (Al-Isra: 18)

The Imam describes the contemptible side of the world in vivid terms. "By God, this world of yours is more contemptible in my eyes than a chewed-up bone of a pig in the hand of a leper." "An image of the world is the snake, soft to touch, but virulent poison within." The world is "as worthless to me as that which drips from the nose of a goat." It is the grandeur and beauty of God that renders the world small and distasteful; only in the light of the Absolute do the world's pleasures and attractions show themselves as fleeting and illusory: "The greatness of the Creator for you belittles creatures in your view." In the same spirit, the Imam speaks of a brother in God: "What made him great in my eyes was the smallness of the world in his eyes (*وَكَانَ يُعَظِّمُهُ فِي عَيْنِي صِغَرُ الدُّنْيَا فِي عَيْنِهِ*)."

This, in the Quranic usage of *zahid*, is the real meaning of زُهْدٌ. In the sura named after Joseph (يُوسُفُ), the brothers of Yusuf held him of little value in selling him for a small price, and in relation to him they are described as زَاهِدِينَ:

> وَشَرَوْهُ بِثَمَنٍۭ بَخْسٍۢ دَرَهِمَ مَعْدُودَةٍۢ وَكَانُوا۟ فِيهِ مِنَ ٱلزَّهِدِينَ
>
> "And they sold him for a paltry price, a few dirhams counted out, and they were of those who deemed him of little worth." (Yusuf: 20)

In the Imam's outlook the world is likewise held of little value—not valueless outright, but rendered all but insignificant beside the Hereafter, beside eternity, beside ultimate reality. All created things shrink to tiny, fleeting shadows bound for annihilation: "Everything perishes except His Face":

> وَلَا تَدْعُ مَعَ ٱللَّهِ إِلَهًا آخَرَ ۘ لَآ إِلَهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ۚ كُلُّ شَىْءٍ هَالِكٌ إِلَّا وَجْهَهُۥ ۚ لَهُ ٱلْحُكْمُ وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ
>
> "And do not invoke with God another deity. There is no deity except Him. Everything will perish except His Face. His is the judgment, and to Him you will be returned." (Al-Qasas: 88)

Yet the world is not stripped of all value; indeed, its significance lies precisely in what it cannot help but signify: "And there is nothing that does not hymn His glory with praise":

> تُسَبِّحُ لَهُ ٱلسَّمَوَتُ ٱلسَّبْعُ وَٱلْأَرْضُ وَمَن فِيهِنَّ ۚ وَإِن مِّن شَىْءٍ إِلَّا يُسَبِّحُ بِحَمْدِهِۦ وَلَكِن لَّا تَفْقَهُونَ تَسْبِيحَهُمْ ۗ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ حَلِيمًا غَفُورًۭا
>
> "The seven heavens and the earth and whatever is in them exalt Him. And there is not a thing except that it exalts Him by His praise, but you do not understand their way of exalting. Indeed, He is ever Forbearing and Forgiving." (Al-Isra: 44)

Everything in the world, every creature in existence, proclaims the boundless creativity of the Creator. So that the imagination might grasp the mystery of this universal praise, the Quran says: "And the birds in flight, each one knoweth its prayer and glorification":

> أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُسَبِّحُ لَهُۥ مَن فِى ٱلسَّمَوَتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ وَٱلطَّيْرُ صَٓفَّتٍۢ ۖ كُلٌّۭ قَدْ عَلِمَ صَلَاتَهُۥ وَتَسْبِيحَهُۥ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ عَلِيمٌۢ بِمَا يَفْعَلُونَ
>
> "Do you not see that God is exalted by whoever is within the heavens and the earth and by the birds with wings spread in flight? Each one has known its prayer and its exalting, and God is Knowing of what they do." (An-Nur: 41)

There is thus no contradiction in the Imam's despising the world and yet growing lyrical over the beauties of untouched nature, including that unsurpassable marvel, the peacock. This comes in a sermon that also describes birds in general, along with ants, serpents, and elephants. The sermon ends with a stirring description of Paradise, setting the marvels of this world beside the delights of the next; in the light of the latter, all earthly things fade into insignificance. For once the eye of the heart turns toward Paradise, "your soul would become averse to the marvels of this world, its passions, its pleasures and its embellished scenery."

It might seem contradictory to hold together such apparently opposed qualities as joy in certainty, discussed above, and the befriending of hardship. Yet the latter is in fact the fruit of the former. Every difficulty without exception is borne through the spiritual resources that faith makes available when it is joined to intellectual assimilation—or rather, in the measure that faith is intellectually assimilated and so becomes *marifa* (معرفۃ) or gnosis, holding a certainty so deep and so complete that "even if the veil be lifted" it cannot increase, according to the famous saying noted earlier. It is possible to believe, at one level of the mind, that there is no god but God. The Imam, however, draws attention to what this apparently simple belief should imply when it is sincere or pure: "I bear witness that there is no god but God alone Who has no partner, a testimony whose sincerity has been tested and whose essence has been assimilated with conviction (*mutaqadan musaffaha*)."

The word مُصَفَّى names the choicest and purest part of any substance, its very essence. The root *massa* means "to suck," the idea being that the liquid or juice of a substance or fruit holds its essence, the part drawn out from it. Here the Imam is stressing the spiritual substance of the testimony, as distinct from mere spoken affirmation or mental grasp of the words. He affirms a heartfelt bond with the reality to which the testimony bears witness; the words that define it are an echo of the reality they carry, and are not to be confused with that reality itself. He continues: "We cling to it always, for as long as we endure, storing it up as protection against the terrors that will befall us. For truly, it renders faith resolute, opens up spiritual virtue, pleases *al-Rahman* (the All-Compassionate), repels *al-shaytan* (the devil)."

Returning to "befriending that by which the ignorant are estranged," this points not only to the trials of life—a source of strength for the wise and of repulsion for the ignorant—but also to the inescapability of death. For the wise, death means release, the meeting with God, blissful eternity—in short, a mode of تَوْحِيد, whereby the soul enjoys ever deeper degrees of integration within higher principles. For the ignorant, by contrast, death appears as a terrifying disintegration, the end of their world rather than the beginning of endless life. There is also the prefiguration of death in life already noted above: فَنَاء, "extinction," "effacement," the "perishing" of egoistic consciousness before the Absolute. The ignorant recoil in horror at the prospect of losing what they falsely take to be their life, not seeing that this sacrifice of egoistic and limited self-consciousness is the very ground on which infinite consciousness is glimpsed or tasted. The ocean of the Real can be tasted only by being drowned in it.

The means to this ultimate attainment is likewise embraced by the wise and feared by the ignorant: the act of pure prayer, total concentration on the Real. Such concentration shuts out every worldly or egotistic preoccupation; the sheer intensity of focus upon the Absolute burns away thoughts of the relative, and for those who ignorantly identify with relativities, inward and outward alike, this amounts to a kind of death. For the wise, by contrast, this concentration on the Absolute, the unsullied remembrance of God, is a joyous contemplation of reality before which the ego is displaced and all illusions dissolve. This leads straight to the next sentence of the passage: "With their bodies, they keep company with the world, while their spirits are tied to the transcendent realm."

This sentence grows more concrete when read alongside another well-known expression of the Imam. He describes himself as belonging to a group whose spiritual degree is such that "their hearts are in the Gardens of Paradise while their bodies are at work." To be tied or attached to that realm—which takes in the paradisal abodes and reaches up to the divine Presence itself—means that they already dwell within that transcendent realm more fully than within this earthly one. This helps explain the saying quoted earlier: God keeps man perpetually in that which his soul desires. Those whose hearts or spirits are in the transcendent domain are those whose consciousness is shaped, substantially and not merely conceptually, by transcendent realities. Those whose consciousness, by contrast, is shaped by worldly concerns are in this world in the sense of being enclosed by its formal limits; their orientation and imagination are trapped within a worldly frame. Their actions bear the mark of this worldliness, whereas the actions of the wise are the fruits of their immersion in a higher reality. Their outward actions in the world belie their inward transcendence of all action; they act, yet are not bound by the fruits of their action. As noted earlier, they know that action, all action, is dust, except what is purified, done sincerely, within it.

At this point the notion of the Hereafter deserves consideration within the Imam's ethical framework. The Hereafter is invoked again and again in the Imam's discourses. Is this simply a matter of holding out a heavenly reward and threatening a hellish punishment to enjoin the good and forbid the evil? Even if the answer is partly yes, there is a deeper dimension to this apparently straightforward use of divine warnings and promises as levers for moral action.

The ethical orientation here is defined not merely by belief in God but by the spiritual reality of the Hereafter as well, both objectively, in itself, and subjectively, insofar as the individual assimilates it. It thereby becomes a living dimension, a process, an orientation, a reflux, within the believer's own soul, rather than a bare belief or hope. Anticipation of the Hereafter marks every believer in principle; its realization marks the soul of the *wali Allah*, the friend of God, the saint and the sage. As noted earlier, the Imam speaks in a famous sentence that bears witness to his total immersion in the reality of which he is certain: "Were the veil to be removed, I would not increase in certitude (*law kushifal-ghita, ma azdadtu yaqinan*)." The anticipation—or better, the foretaste—of the Hereafter deepens rather than fades in proportion to one's virtue, piety, and sanctity; it does not dwindle into a mere heuristic device whose value is spent once its moral impact is achieved. The following moving passage from the *Nahj* deserves reflection in this light. What appears here is the description of a blissful vision of the reality of Paradise, not merely a rhetorical flourish in the service of moral exhortation:

If you were to cast the eye of your heart toward what is described for you of the Garden, your soul would become averse to the marvels of this world, its passions, its pleasures and its embellished scenery. And your soul would be rapt in contemplation of the swaying trees whose roots lie hidden in dunes of musk on the banks of the rivers of the Garden, and the clusters of dazzling pearls hanging down from the branches of its trees …

The highest degree of spirituality does, of course, transcend the desire for Paradise and the fear of Hell, along with the moral conduct proportioned to them. But this means that when such higher degrees of realization are attained, the soul's state can properly be called paradisal—that is, already so utterly content in the beatific presence of God that it desires nothing more. This state of soul is called in the Quran اَلنَّفْسُ الْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ, "the soul at peace in absolute certainty":

> يَٓأَيَّتُهَا ٱلنَّفْسُ ٱلْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ
>
> "O reassured soul." (Al-Fajr: 27)

The Imam frequently echoes, implicitly if not explicitly, the Quranic principle that "the Hereafter is better and more lasting":

> وَٱلْآخِرَةُ خَيْرٌۭ وَأَبْقَىٰٓ
>
> "But the Hereafter is better and more enduring." (Al-Ala: 17)

The Hereafter is not only the time at which divine warnings and promises reach their fulfillment; it is also the space in which all positive qualities—human, angelic, divine—are manifested in their fullness, those qualities that appear on earth as reflections of higher realities and that encompass and subtly penetrate the whole sphere of earthly existence. The Quran, perhaps more than any other scripture, furnishes the imagination with numerous and complex descriptions of paradisal bliss. One of the key functions of these descriptions is alluded to in the Quran itself: "Assuredly, We purify them with a pure quality, remembrance of the Abode of the Hereafter":

> إِنَّآ أَخْلَصْنَهُم بِخَالِصَةٍۢ ذِكْرَى ٱلدَّارِ
>
> "Indeed, We chose them for an exclusive quality: remembrance of the Home of the Hereafter." (Sad: 46)

The mere thought of Paradise is itself a purification of mind and heart, a way of guarding the soul against the ever-present temptation to seek its ultimate happiness and well-being in this world alone. Conversely, one's well-being here below, where action is concerned, lies in the doing of good deeds, and such well-being is proportioned to the depth of one's belief that "Whatever good ye send before for your souls, ye will find it with God, better and more tremendous as reward":

> إِنَّ رَبَّكَ يَعْلَمُ أَنَّكَ تَقُومُ أَدْنَىٰ مِن ثُلُثَىِ ٱلَّيْلِ وَنِصْفَهُۥ وَثُلُثَهُۥ وَطَآئِفَةٌۭ مِّنَ ٱلَّذِينَ مَعَكَ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ يُقَدِّرُ ٱلَّيْلَ وَٱلنَّهَارَ ۚ عَلِمَ أَن لَّن تُحْصُوهُ فَتَابَ عَلَيْكُمْ ۖ فَٱقْرَءُوا۟ مَا تَيَسَّرَ مِنَ ٱلْقُرْآنِ ۚ عَلِمَ أَن سَيَكُونُ مِنكُم مَّرْضَىٰ ۙ وَآخَرُونَ يَضْرِبُونَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ يَبْتَغُونَ مِن فَضْلِ ٱللَّهِ ۙ وَآخَرُونَ يُقَتِلُونَ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ ۖ فَٱقْرَءُوا۟ مَا تَيَسَّرَ مِنْهُ ۚ وَأَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَآتُوا۟ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ وَأَقْرِضُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًۭا ۚ وَمَا تُقَدِّمُوا۟ لِأَنفُسِكُم مِّنْ خَيْرٍۢ تَجِدُوهُ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ هُوَ خَيْرًۭا وَأَعْظَمَ أَجْرًۭا ۚ وَٱسْتَغْفِرُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ ۖ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌۭ رَّحِيمٌۢ
>
> "And whatever good you put forward for yourselves, you will find it with God. It is better and greater in reward." (Al-Muzzammil: 20)

In other words, a sincere orientation toward the Hereafter is transformed into a perception of heavenly beatitude already here below, in the form of those very acts and attitudes which, being good, share the same nature as the felicity to which they lead. In this light, morally good actions and noble intentions are grasped by one who sees beyond the veil of this world as vivid prefigurations of heavenly realities, not merely as ethical prerequisites for salvation. Goodness, in other words, does not simply lead to a heavenly reward; it already participates in that reward: "Is the reward of goodness (احسان) aught but goodness?":

> هَلْ جَزَآءُ ٱلْإِحْسَنِ إِلَّا ٱلْإِحْسَنُ
>
> "Is the reward for good anything but good?" (Ar-Rahman: 60)

The Imam's saying, "The fruit of good action is like its root," can be read as an echo of the idea that the end is already present in the means that lead to it. Fitting to close is the following verse of the Quran, which also serves as an introduction to what follows:

> وَبَشِّرِ ٱلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّلِحَتِ أَنَّ لَهُمْ جَنَّتٍۢ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَرُ ۖ كُلَّمَا رُزِقُوا۟ مِنْهَا مِن ثَمَرَةٍۢ رِّزْقًۭا ۙ قَالُوا۟ هَذَا ٱلَّذِى رُزِقْنَا مِن قَبْلُ ۖ وَأُتُوا۟ بِهِۦ مُتَشَبِهًۭا ۖ وَلَهُمْ فِيهَآ أَزْوَجٌۭ مُّطَهَّرَةٌۭ ۖ وَهُمْ فِيهَا خَلِدُونَ
>
> "And give good tidings to those who believe and perform virtuous deeds, that for them are Gardens underneath which rivers flow. Every time they are given to eat from the fruits thereof, they say: This is what we were given to eat before. And they were given the like thereof." (Al-Baqarah: 25)

The fruits of the paradisal gardens are thus tasted already here below, in the form of all goodness, beauty, and truth, and the diverse modes of happiness that flow from them. All such experiences are so many foretastes of the ultimate beatitude in Paradise. On the ethical plane, the doing of good deeds, and, even more directly, the realization of intrinsic virtue, احسان, is therefore not simply a prerequisite for posthumous salvation, but is already a kind of deliverance, here and now: a deliverance from the imprisonment of sin on the outward plane, and from the bondage of egocentricity on the inward plane. This theme of salvation here and now, grounded in unshakeable certitude, is fundamental to the spirit of the Imam's teachings and will be explored further throughout the rest of this book. In the following chapter, the cardinal virtue of justice will be considered within Ali 's perspective, rooted as it is in احسان, a virtue defined by the Prophet as worshipping God as if one saw Him, and concretely affirmed by the Imam as real and not merely hypothetical, for God is indeed seen by the pure of heart through the verities of faith.
