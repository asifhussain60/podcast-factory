# Prayer, Remembrance, and Gazing on the Good

The lynchpin of the entire epistle now comes into view: the most important means by which the sacred substance of all the virtues is assimilated by the ruler, namely, prayer. At the opening of the letter the Imam began with a counsel to observe the obligatory prayers. Here, after stressing the need to perform every administrative task at its proper time, he instructs Malik to set apart the most excellent portion of his available time for what is between him and God, that is, for acts of worship and devotion. Malik is urged to give special attention to the obligatory prayers as the means by which he purifies his religion for God, rendering sincere his devotion to Him. This is not merely an exhortation to perform the formal prayers. It must be understood above all in the light of the deeper meaning of purifying one's religion for God, the purifying of the very substance of the relationship between the soul and God, for it is the depth of that relationship which determines the quality of all human virtue. As the Imam put it in a saying already noted, "Know that your every act is dependent upon your prayer," in other words, every action depends upon the way one gazes upon God, who is the source of all good. What matters is not only the formal, physical, and verbal performance of the prayer, but the inward quality that accompanies it and enlivens it from within. Sincerity, intensity, and frequency are essential to its efficacy. The Imam continues, "Give unto God of your vital energy in your nights and your days, and perform fully that by which you draw near to God, doing so perfectly, without becoming dull or deficient, taking your body to its limits (balighan min badanika mā balagha)."

The whole emphasis here falls on the word taqarrub, drawing near. One attains divine nearness most directly through deep devotion, offered up alone, in the dead of night, with all one's heart. Once that nearness is realized, all one's other actions in the world are imbued with something of the spiritual quality gained through divine proximity. In this manner all action can be assimilated to devotion in the wider sense, all outward actions being truly governed by the sincere intention to give oneself to God. If the inner life is dominated by prayer, prayer comes to penetrate the outer life as well. Hence the Imam, immediately after telling Malik to set apart the best of his time for God, adds, "all times [and the actions performed therein] are for God if the intention underlying them is good and if your subjects derive security as a consequence." In other words, all the actions of a ruler will partake of the sacred to the degree that his devotion truly reaches into the source of the sacred. One may then envisage an overflow, as it were, from the fountain of obligatory and voluntary prayer into the outward domain of action. This subtle truth is expressed in the famous hadith qudsi of the Prophet, often referred to as the hadith of taqarrub:

> My slave draws near to Me through nothing I love more than that which I have made obligatory for him. My slave never ceases to draw near to Me through supererogatory acts until I love him. And when I love him, I am his hearing by which he hears, his sight by which he sees, his hand by which he grasps, and his foot by which he walks.

To see and to act through God can be understood, at one level, as seeing things as they really are and then acting upon that vision, which is to say perfect justice, the placing of each thing in its proper order. This is why the Imam repeatedly draws the reader of his sermons back to the vision of God, setting that vision as the true foundation of a life of virtue. In prayer the ego is effaced through prostration, and this submission to God through self-effacement is itself what puts all things in their right place, including, and especially, the ego. The hadith qudsi marks a distinction between the obligatory and the supererogatory acts of devotion. Both are means of drawing near, but it is primarily through the latter that the slave comes to perceive and act through the divine. The Imam's phrase, taking your body to its limits, carries the same weight. Strenuous effort is called for, and it is far from sufficient to perform the prayers and devotions in a mechanical or perfunctory manner. "He who prays without making an effort," says the Imam, "is like one who shoots arrows without a bow." As noted earlier, the sincerity (ikhlas) demanded by the Imam's conception of the oneness of God calls for the integration of the soul, and it is in prayer that the deepest dimensions of the soul are expressed, cultivated, and integrated. The prayer must therefore be accomplished not merely with the tongue or the body, but with all that one is.

Elsewhere, Ali (ع) refers to the key supererogatory practice by which the spiritual station of nearness is attained. He does so in a commentary on the words of a Qur'anic verse, "… men whom neither commerce nor trade diverts from the remembrance of God" (24:36).

> Truly God has made the remembrance (al-dhikr) a polish for the hearts, by which they hear after suffering from deafness, and see after being blind … There have always been slaves of God … with whom He held intimate discourse through their thoughts and spoke with them through the essence of their intellects. They diffused illumination through the awakened light in their hearing and their seeing and their hearts, calling unto the remembrance of the days of God.

The importance of the dhikr, both as a principle of awareness of ultimate reality and as a concrete means of attaining that awareness, is paramount in the Imam's perspective. By the word dhikr the Imam implies both a methodic practice of invoking a divine Name and a principle of permanent awareness of God embracing all modes of worship, meditation, and reflection. It is to be understood not only as being aware of God or remembering God, but also as an intense mode of prolonged, prayerful meditation on ultimate reality, represented both symbolically and sacramentally by one or more of the divine Names. This remembrance is not the learning of new things from scratch. It is the recollection of ultimate reality, of the immutable, unchanging principles and divine attributes that are inherent in the very substance of our intellects, by which everything can be understood, and which are ultimately rooted in the divine nature of our Creator. This aspect of remembrance receives fuller treatment in the chapter that follows. The relationship between the remembrance of God and the cultivation of justice is made clear in this sermon, for the Imam describes those illuminated by the dhikr:

> Truly there are people who belong to the dhikr; they have adopted it in place of the world, such that "neither commerce nor trade" distracts them from it; they spend the days of their life in it … they instruct people about justice and themselves are steadfast therein.

It is those souls who truly belong to the dhikr who are not only steadfast in justice but also able to instruct people about justice. Putting first things first, they are able to show how to place all things in their proper order. Fervent, lived spirituality is therefore not opposed to dedicated activity in the world. It infuses that activity with an ethical depth that springs from direct contact with the divine, a contact articulated by prayer in general and by the dhikr in particular. Just as the sincerity of a good intention is proven by the effort to realize it, so the sincerity of prayer is likewise proven, and rendered efficacious, through the sustained effort to prolong and intensify it, taking your body to its limits. What this has to do with justice is plain. If justice is grasped as a quality rooted in the Real (al-Haqq), then nearness to the Real cannot but draw one nearer to justice and to all positive qualities, spiritual, intellectual, and moral. The relationship between worship and justice must therefore be firmly established if the conception of justice is to be qualified as sacred. From this perspective, justice is both cause and consequence of worship. On one hand, justice requires that everything be given its due, and God must accordingly be given His due, which entails, sacramentally, worship and prayer. On the other hand, justice emerges ethically as a consequence of that worship, as do all the virtues, in proportion to the nearness granted by God to His worshipper and in accordance with the radiant repercussions of that nearness.

What the Imam offers in his stress on prayer, together with its essence, the remembrance of God, is a devotional principle that illuminates and, in a sense, sublimates Plato's philosophical position. It might be seen as rendering explicit in spiritual terms what remains implicit in Plato's pedagogy. After discussing the question of justice at great length, Socrates comes to address the crux of the matter, the nature of the Sovereign Good, which is of paramount concern to the ideal ruler, the guardian, whose moral and intellectual formation lies at the heart of the dialogue's practical dimension:

> … the highest object of knowledge is the essential nature of the Good, from which everything that is good and right derives its value for us … I need not tell you that, without that knowledge, to know everything else, however well, would be of no value to us …

Socrates declines to respond to Glaucon's entreaties to describe the nature of this Good, offering instead to speak of its offspring. This leads him to draw an analogy between the Good and the Sun: what the Sun is to visibility, the Good is to intelligibility and being. He proceeds with the famous allegory of the cave and then describes the educational programme that would turn the attention of his prospective rulers from the world of shadows to the world of real objects, a programme comprising mathematics, arithmetic, geometry, solid geometry, astronomy, harmonics, and dialectic. Having successfully passed through all stages of this education, "when they are fifty, those who have come safely through and proved the best at all points in action and in study must be brought at last to the goal. They must lift up the eye of the soul to gaze on that which sheds light on all things; and when they have seen the Good itself, take it as a pattern for the right ordering of the state and of the individual, themselves included." Yet Plato nowhere states what this gazing actually means, nor how it is to be achieved, by what means, intellective, contemplative, or methodic.

At an important point in the Republic, Glaucon presses Socrates on the definition of the Sovereign Good: "But, Socrates, what is your account of the Good? Is it knowledge, or pleasure, or something else?" Socrates refuses to give a direct answer, saying after some further discussion, "I am afraid it is beyond my powers; with the best will in the world I should only disgrace myself and be laughed at." Very much in the manner of Glaucon, Dhi'lib al-Yamanī provocatively asked Ali (ع) whether he had seen the object of his devotion. To this the Imam replied, "I would not be worshipping a lord whom I have not seen." Dhi'lib then asked, "O Commander of the Faithful! How did you see Him?" The Imam replied with the sentence that serves as one of the leitmotifs of this book: "O Dhi'lib! Eyes see Him not through sight's observation, but hearts see Him through the verities of faith."

The polishing of the heart effected by remembrance, by ذکر, comes to mind here, and this very word evokes the essence of Plato's approach to knowledge as recollection, anamnesis. For remembrance in this sense is not the learning of something new from scratch, but the recollection of ultimate reality, of the immutable divine principles and attributes inherent in the very substance of our intellects, by which everything else can be understood. Yet Plato in none of his dialogues makes a direct reference to the devotional, methodic, or contemplative counterpart of his educational, ethical, and intellective endeavour. There is no explicitly postulated link between remembrance of the forms or ideas that engender true knowledge and the gazing upon the Good, the contemplation of that which sheds light on all things. It is not merely knowing and remembering the forms, but coming to know the Bestower of Forms, which in Islam is referred to by the divine Name al-Musawwir. Whether there was any spiritual method in Plato's teaching, and if there was, why he did not divulge it, remains a matter of speculation. One answer may be gathered from a statement in Plato's famous Seventh Letter, where he denies that any exposition can be given of the true reality of philosophy, and points instead to the disciplined way of life and rigorous daily routine that the subject demands if philosophy in the deepest sense is to be realized. "No treatise by me concerning it exists or ever will exist. It is not something that can be put into words like other branches of learning; only after long partnership in a common life devoted to this very thing does truth flash upon the soul, like a flame kindled by a leaping spark, and once it is born there, it nourishes itself thereafter." This returns to the importance of active spirituality for the practice of authentic virtue. If the Real, indefinable in its essence, is the foundation of all good, the means of assimilating the Real existentially, and not merely comprehending it mentally, cannot be set aside. Plato's philosophical approach to the Good can be augmented and deepened by an appreciation of Ali (ع)'s means of drawing near to the Real, for in the Imam's perspective intellectual comprehension and spiritual devotion are inseparable and complementary. Integral, deep-rooted justice emerges as one of the fruits of this drawing near, just as, for Plato, gazing on that which sheds light on all things, the Good itself, results in the capacity to take the Good as a pattern for the right ordering of the state and of the individual.

These pages have sketched the outlines of a spiritual approach to the theme of justice as it presents itself principally in the Imam's letter to Malik al-Ashtar. The surface of this important subject has only been skimmed, yet the Imam's illumination of the principle of justice, addressed in the first instance to a prospective governor of Egypt in the first century of the Islamic era, is by no means outmoded. Whether one operates in an avowedly Islamic or an overtly secular environment, whether the rule be autocratic or democratic, whether political institutions be strong or weak, wherever individuals are vested with power and authority, and by that very token exposed to the temptations that power offers, the approach to justice articulated in the Imam's ethos will not be irrelevant. This ethos does not presuppose any particular form of government. It engages the universal theme of moral responsibility, but does so in terms of that which transcends the level of morality, offering insights derived from a direct vision of ultimate spiritual realities. The principles contemplated through this vision surpass the domain of politics, and for that very reason are capable of penetrating deeply and refashioning, from above, the moral substance of political consciousness. Although the Imam's perspective is particularly pertinent to the interface between spirituality, morality, and governance within Islamic discourse and praxis, its relevance extends to all contexts in which the sense of the sacred plays a part in the articulation of moral sensibility and the cultivation of political responsibility.

The Imam's letters and discourses are a vast and largely untapped source of inspired ideas, capable of illuminating the role of spiritual principles in formulating ethical values and shaping the quality of political practice. In particular, any integral Islamic approach to politics and society must take into account the requirements and fruits of worship, understanding that word in its widest sense. Sincere worship transforms the notion of the Absolute into a concrete reality, imparting to the soul an existential impetus and a spiritual resource that buttresses, stabilises, and deepens one's moral life. What is derived from consciousness of the divine Reality is not so much a philosophy of ethics as a spiritual morality, an existential orientation that is at once the consequence of a lived spirituality and the cause of its continuous, dynamic, and ever-deepening realisation of spiritual truth, the Real as such, al-Haqq. This means that the haqq, the right due to each person or group, will be given in accordance with the principle of justice, that of putting each thing in its proper place, وَضَعَ الشَّئُی حَقَّ مَحَلِّہِ. Virtue, from such a perspective, is a form of ethical realism, where realism denotes an assimilation of spiritual reality, and morally correct behaviour translates that realism into action across every plane, individual, social, and political.

Should it be objected that this perspective is hopelessly utopian, the harsh exigencies of politics and the sublime ideals of spirituality being poles apart, the reply is this. If no effort is made to comprehend the deeper dimensions of Islamic piety and spirituality, then any ostensibly Islamic discourse, whether in politics, sociology, ethics, or any other field, will be reduced to an outer shell, stripped of the inner life that gives it all its meaning and, more importantly, its transformative power. For Islamic concepts to be grasped aright, they must be assimilated not merely by the mind but by the heart, failing which they remain mere abstractions. As the Imam insists, it is the heart, the inmost point of consciousness, that sees the true nature of things. This is why the vision of God as if one could see Him lays bare the crucial relationship between worship and virtue, between spirituality and ethics, and equally between devotion to the Creator and kindness towards His creation. One cannot have the one without the other, for to conduct oneself as though one could see God is to live in the consciousness of standing constantly in the divine presence, since even if we cannot see Him, He sees us. Without this vision there is no ihsan in the ethico-spiritual sense discussed earlier, and without ihsan the very integrity of the Islamic way of life is fatally undermined. It is precisely the denial and subversion of this tradition that bears ultimate responsibility for the atrocities committed by a tiny minority of Muslims in our times, people who betray the highest ideals of the Islamic ethical tradition by resorting to unbridled violence in its name, as though one might hurt creation in order to please the Creator. Without ihsan, religion is left acutely vulnerable, transformed into an ideology, driven by individual passion and subject to political manipulation. It is no surprise that those most prone to terrorism in the name of Islam have emerged from the currents most hostile to the ihsanī or mystical tradition.

This returns to the devotional practices that are all too often regarded as the preserve of a minority, viewed as either mystical or misguided depending on one's standpoint, rather than being understood as a God-given means of narrowing the gap between what one aspires to and what one is, of moving from the actual towards the ideal, of making concrete what would otherwise remain abstract, of living what is only thought about, and of rendering existentially galvanising what might otherwise be merely theoretically compelling. While the principles established here are universal in their import and applicability, it would be wrong to attempt to de-contextualise the Imam's perspective by dismissing, as so much unnecessary superstructure, his repeated, insistent, and uncompromising emphasis on the imperative of prayer. The principle of justice requires that all the elements of one's life, devotion, worship, ethics, action, be bound together in a harmonious, indissoluble, and interconnected whole, in accordance with the ideal of tawhid. And tawhid means precisely this integration, the making of many into one, so that when a person's theory and practice have themselves become one, that person has become a true bearer of that oneness. There can be no question of detaching the domain of the virtues from that of spirituality, for in the Imam's perspective the only true virtue is one that is rooted in, sustained by, and oriented towards the divine Reality. A virtue that is not so rooted, even one that happens by coincidence to mirror an Islamic value, is baseless and without spirit. Cut off from its source, it becomes situational and self-serving, open to compromise the moment circumstances warrant it, and ceases to be a virtue at all, dwindling into a mere series of pragmatic adaptations to shifting conditions. Justice, together with all the other virtues, can only be brought to fruition in a soul, and in a society, permeated and penetrated by spirituality, the essence of which is the principle and practice of the remembrance of God. And this remembrance, this dhikr, is not the learning of something entirely new. It is a recollection of the ultimate Reality, a recollection of the immutable principles and unchanging qualities inherent in the very substance of our intellects, principles by which everything else can be understood, and which are ultimately rooted in the divine nature of our Creator. Remembrance of this kind is nothing less than the cognitive and philosophical foundation of ethical thought in Islam. In the last analysis, it is the remembrance of God that determines the quality of human virtue. It is to the spiritual realisation intended and cultivated by this remembrance that the following chapter is addressed.
