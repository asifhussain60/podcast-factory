# Conscience, Compassion, and the Ordering of Society

One form this enmity with God takes is the inner war against one's own conscience — what Urdu calls the ⟪ar:ضمیر⟫. The voice of conscience is the whole substance of secular ethics, and it can be understood here within its fuller context, not merely as the overgrown foundation of individual morality that secular ethical thought has made of it. That fuller context is the source — beyond the individual, beyond the physical — from which individual conscience springs: the divine infusion of spiritual discernment into the soul, the divine inspiration that lies beneath every distinction between right and wrong. At the level of the morally responsible soul, then, the divine quality of justice becomes the voice of inner conscience:

> وَنَفْسٍۢ وَمَا سَوَّىٰهَا ۷ فَأَلْهَمَهَا فُجُورَهَا وَتَقْوَىٰهَا ۸
>
> 'And the soul and that which perfected it, inspiring it with [consciousness of] its wickedness and its righteousness' (Ash-Shams: 7-8).

The war against one's own conscience, even when the oppressor scarcely acknowledges it, is in truth a war against God, and all the more so.

At a deeper level, then, this war against God is revealed as a war against the very reality that makes the soul a soul. The soul contains not only a moral conscience — a built-in, inseparable awareness of the difference between good and evil, placed in it by God — but also an inborn consciousness of God's lordship and of its own answering servitude as a creature. Spiritual consciousness is therefore the source of moral conscience. The Quran describes how this spiritual consciousness dwells within each soul in terms that stress its nature before and beyond existence, calling up a 'moment' at the very dawn of creation — a moment, or degree of being, that comes before the human condition itself yet enters into the very shaping of the human soul:

> وَإِذْ أَخَذَ رَبُّكَ مِنۢ بَنِىٓ آدَمَ مِن ظُهُورِهِمْ ذُرِّيَّتَهُمْ وَأَشْهَدَهُمْ عَلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ أَلَسْتُ بِرَبِّكُمْ ۖ قَالُوا۟ بَلَىٰ ۛ شَهِدْنَآ ۛ أَن تَقُولُوا۟ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَمَةِ إِنَّا كُنَّا عَنْ هَذَا غَفِلِينَ ۱۷۲

'And when thy Lord brought forth from the children of Adam, from their loins, their seed, and made them testify against their souls [saying], "Am I not your Lord?" They said, "Yes, verily, we testify." [This was] lest ye say on the Day of Resurrection, "Truly, of this we were unaware" (Al-Araf: 172). The moment He created them He made them witness His glory and testify to it, so that they could plead no ignorance on the Day of Judgment.

What comes to light here is that the source of morality rises above the human level even while forming part of the very definition of what it is to be human. Seen this way, what makes the human soul fully human is precisely that element of transcendence which infinitely surpasses the soul and yet, mysteriously, supplies its own deepest being. In this light, tyranny and oppression take on a subtler meaning: these vices are drawn much closer to the lived reality of every soul, rather than confined to those who hold and abuse political power. The tyrant is, before anything else, one who violates his own soul, one who lets it be ruled by 'the soul which commands evil', النَّفْس al-ammara (⟪ar:النفس الامارۃ بالسوء⟫). And so the saying the Imam cites as summing up the attitude of one on the way to corrupting his own soul and becoming an oppressor — وَلاَ تَقُولَنَّ: إِنِّي مُؤَمَّرٌ آمُرُ فَأُطَاعُ, 'I order and am obeyed' — is to be understood not only in social terms but also within the small world of the soul. It becomes the utterance of the evil element within the soul: the nafs al-ammara, which literally 'orders' (أَمَرَ) and is obeyed by every other faculty of the soul — intelligence, imagination, will, sentiment, sensibility, and the rest.

The struggle against one's own soul (mujahadat النَّفْس, ⟪ar:مجاھدۃ النفس⟫) is a theme the Imam returns to again and again. The need to wrestle with and master one's own faults is an essential part of justice in the sense defined earlier: to put each thing in its proper place begins with setting oneself right before presuming to reform or govern others; it means restoring the divine nature within oneself. As the Imam says, 'If your aspiration is to reform the people, begin with yourself, for your pursuit of the reform of others while your own soul is corrupt is the greatest of faults.' In his letter appointing Muhammad b. Abi Bakr (⟪ar:محمد بن ابی بکر⟫), the governor of Egypt before Malik (⟪ar:مالک اشتر⟫), the Imam draws a firm link between the granting of power and the duty to resist one's own soul: 'And be aware, O Muhammad b. Abi Bakr, that I have appointed you as governor over the most immense of the forces at my disposal, the people of Egypt, so you are obliged to oppose your soul and protect your religion.' The tie between the size of the power placed in one's hands and the spiritual responsibility that comes with it is worth close attention. A ruler must be on guard, first against his own soul and its desires, and second in regard to the religion. The greater the power at hand, the greater the duty to control and restrain oneself; for if the soul gives way to its desires and satisfies them through that power, the outcome is the corruption not only of the ruler himself but of the very religion he is charged to uphold. Here again priorities are set by a principle that is essentially spiritual: the outward form and practice of religion depends for its integrity on the inward state of the Muslim's soul. This is one more expression of the principle of putting each thing in its proper place. The commanding soul that claims for itself the right to independence and mastery is described with terse precision in the Quranic verse:

> كَلَّآ إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَنَ لَيَطْغَىٰٓ ۶ أَن رَّآهُ ٱسْتَغْنَىٰٓ ۷
>
> 'Truly man is rebellious, in that he deemeth himself independent' (Al-Alaq: 6-7).

Insofar as the soul pretends to be self-sufficient and tries to cut itself off from its total dependence on God, it sets itself up as a god in its own right — the person coming to believe that he himself is God. Herein lies the true portrait of the tyrant, the one who, as the Quran puts it, 'taketh his own desire as his god' (Al-Jathiyah: 23): أَفَرَءَيْتَ مَنِ ٱتَّخَذَ إِلَهَهُۥ هَوَىٰهُ. To know one's own nothingness before God, and therefore one's absolute need of Him, is the chief means of loosening the grip of the soul that commands to evil. For only the soul that already commands itself to evil will command others to evil; and only the soul that is lowly before God will be humble, compassionate, and just in its dealings with people.

This principle emerges clearly from these words of the Imam: 'Let the most beloved of affairs to you be those most centered upon the right, the most comprehensive in justice, and the most inclusive of popular approval, for the disapproval of the common folk undermines the approval of the elite ...' This stress on the importance of the common people is not only sound practical counsel for fair and effective government; it can also be read as an echo of the spiritual duty to recognize man's real lowliness before God, and so as a way of pressing the ruler to identify with the poor rather than the rich, in keeping with the principle:

> يَٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ أَنتُمُ ٱلْفُقَرَآءُ إِلَى ٱللَّهِۖ وَٱللَّهُ هُوَ ٱلْغَنِىُّ ٱلْحَمِيدُ
>
> 'O mankind, ye are the poor before God, and God, He is the Rich, the absolutely Praiseworthy' (Fatir: 15).

The Imam's own poverty and severe austerity were proverbial. Yet this was far from a simple turning away from the world for its own sake, as his exchange with an ascetic, 'Asim b. Ziyad, makes clear. Asim's family had complained to the Imam that he denied himself too much, and the Imam told him to think of his family and not to cut himself off from the good things (الطَّيِّبَاتُ) that God has permitted. Asim retorted, O Commander of the Faithful, and yet here you are, in your rough clothes and your coarse food!' The Imam replied, 'Woe to you, I am not like you. God, the Exalted, has made it incumbent on true leaders (أَئِمَّةُ الْحَقِّ) that they proportion themselves to the weakest among the people (يُقَدِّرُ أَنْفُسَهُمْ بِضَعَفَةِ النَّاسِ), so that the poverty of the poor will not engender covetousness.' Here is a rigorous statement of the duty of the most powerful to identify themselves, in material terms, with the most powerless. It is hard to imagine a more effective way of lessening worldliness and strengthening spirituality in a society than this deliberate self-lowering, in purely material terms, to the level of the weakest people one rules. He who could have the most chooses to have the least. The Imam implies that if a leader is to stand among the أَئِمَّةُ الْحَقِّ, he must show his attachment to اَلْحَقّ — the ultimate spiritual reality, in whose light material wealth counts for nothing. So even though the most powerful and the most powerless may both be poor in material terms, both alike have access to the treasures of the spiritual realm, in proportion to how far they grasp the truth expressed in the verse already cited: 'O mankind, ye are the poor before God, and God, He is the Rich, the absolutely Praiseworthy'. On the level of social psychology, too, the Imam's insistence that rulers live like the poor, borne out in his own life, is an effective way of ensuring that the unavoidable inequalities of a society will not breed envy among the poor.

The compassion of justice

Identification with the poor also deepens a ruler's awareness of his own unceasing need of God, helping him to live more fully in reliance on divine mercy, in hope and trust; and the natural companion of such a life is the giving of mercy and compassion to one's fellow human beings. The capacity to act with compassion in no way conflicts with the demands of justice; it is, rather, an intrinsic part of justice understood ontologically — that is, in accordance with the fundamental nature of reality. Acting with compassion agrees with justice not only in the sense of putting things in their right place, since compassion is to be extended wherever and whenever it is called for; it also agrees with justice by being at one with the true nature of things, with the intrinsic character of the Real. This is why, at the head of every chapter of the Quran but one, the name 'Allah' is followed by 'the infinitely Compassionate, the ever-Merciful' (al-Rahman, al-Rahim). To put it another way, the capacity for compassion springs not so much from personal feeling as from an inborn kinship with the very nature of divine reality — that higher reality in which, as the Quran tells us, 'My mercy encompasseth all things' (Al-Araf: 156); a reality in which 'thy Lord has prescribed for Himself mercy' (Al-Anam: 54); a reality whose essential character is fixed by the principle, 'My mercy takes precedence over My wrath'. Among the many sayings of Ali (ع) on the need to show mercy are these: 'The dispensing of mercy brings down [divine] mercy.' 'As you grant mercy, so will you be granted mercy.' 'I am astounded by the person who hopes for mercy from one above him, while he is not merciful to those beneath him.'

Neither the severe aspect of the nature of things nor the 'wrathful' side of God is denied here, but both are clearly placed beneath the higher ontological principle of mercy. There is, in other words, no common measure between the merely relative things that are subject to wrath and punishment and the realities — blessed in their very nature — to which divine mercy and compassion open the way. A soul is therefore more 'real' the more it reflects this predominance of mercy, both in its spiritual orientation and in its moral conduct. And within human society, it lies in the very nature of justice, understood in this sacred way, to lean toward compassion wherever possible, even though there must also be a place for the strict application of corrective penalty where that cannot be avoided. Indeed, in his letter the Imam goes on to instruct Malik to inflict corporal punishment on any executive officer found guilty of misappropriating public funds. The Imam's counsels and letters are full of stern warnings of swift and severe retribution to his governors, the severity of these warnings matching his unflinching fidelity to the demands of fairness and honesty in handling public revenues. For example, he writes to Ziyad b. Abihi, deputy to the governor of Basra — Ali 's cousin Abd Allah b. al-Abbas:

I swear by God an oath in all sincerity: if news reaches me that you have misappropriated the revenue of the Muslims, whether a small or large amount, I shall inflict a severe punishment upon you, one which will lighten your wealth, burden your back, and degrade your affair.

The Imam was just as severe toward his own relatives at the least hint of a request for favors that would require the abuse of public funds. In one sermon he recalls an incident in which his blind brother, الْعَاقِل b. Abi Talib, asked for funds from the treasury beyond what was his due. He thought I would sell my religion for him, the Imam said. His response was to bring a red-hot piece of iron close to his brother's body, telling him that by making such a request he was urging Ali himself to enter a far fiercer fire. In the same way, when told of the misappropriation of funds by his cousin and long-standing ally 'Abd Allah b. al-Abbas, the Imam answered with a thundering rebuke. He ordered his cousin: Fear God and return their property to these people. If you do not, and God gives me authority over you, I excuse myself before God in regard to you [and your wrongdoing], and I shall indeed strike you with my sword, with which whomever I have struck enters the fire [of Hell]. By God, even if Hasan and Husayn had done the like of what you have done, they would not have been granted any leniency by me.'

The mention of the Prophet's grandsons is telling. However close the tie to a wrongdoer, however exalted his standing, the principle of justice demands complete impartiality: there are to be no favors for one's favorites. This stance is not only the exact opposite of the nepotism that had undermined the rule of the Imam's predecessor; it is also precisely what the Quran commands: 'O ye who believe, be staunch in justice, witnesses of God, even though it be against yourselves, your parents or your near of kin ...' (An-Nisa: 135).

On the theme of mercy, the passage that follows places the necessity of compassion in a universal context. It stands as one of the Imam's most important statements on the unity of the human race and the equality of all human beings, and as a corrective to every form of prejudice, particularism, and sectarianism that would confine justice or compassion to members of one's own group, however it is defined:

Infuse your heart with mercy for the subjects, love for them and kindness toward them. Be not like a ravenous beast of prey above them, seeking to devour them. For they are of two types: either your brother in religion or your like in creation. Mistakes slip from them, defects emerge from them, deliberately or accidentally. So bestow upon them your forgiveness and your pardon, just as you would have God bestow upon you His forgiveness and pardon; for you are above them, and the one who has authority over you is above you, and God is above him who appointed you ... and through them He tests you.

Here the universal reach of compassion is joined to a reminder of God's absolute sovereignty. No man — neither a governor nor the ruler who appointed him — is anything more than a slave of God, wholly dependent upon His mercy. Anyone who stands in some position of superiority over others must therefore keep his own lowness before the Absolute always in mind. That awareness works in two directions at once: it moves him to compassion toward those below him, and it draws down upon him the compassion of God above him.

One incident shows well how the Imam put justice into practice with compassion, and gives vivid form to the principle already noted, that all people are 'your like in creation'. The Imam came upon an old, blind beggar and asked about him. He was told that the man was a Christian. He said to those around him, 'You have employed him until he is old and infirm, and now you refuse to help him. Give him maintenance from the public funds (بَيْتُ الْمَالِ).' The words state in brief the Islamic principle of social welfare (مَصْلَحَة), which rests on redistributive justice and on a strict refusal to discriminate between Muslims and others. Social justice and religious equality flow from a compassion understood not as mere sentiment but as an intrinsic dimension of the Real; and so they become a fundamental religious duty binding on every Muslim, ruler and citizen alike.

The last sentence of the passage quoted earlier — which warns the governor to regard his power over his subjects not as a privilege but as a trial from God — expresses the Imam's own attitude to rulership. In the famous sermon al-Shiqshiqiyya he says that he accepted power only as an unavoidable duty:

Had God not taken from the learned [a promise] that they would not acquiesce in the rapacity of the tyrant nor in the hunger of the oppressed ... I would truly have flung its reins [that of the caliphate] back upon its withers ... and you would indeed have discovered that this world of yours is as insignificant to me as that which drips from the nose of a goat.

This is a striking embodiment of Plato's ideal attitude toward power — the attitude of the true 'philosopher', who rules solely for the sake of the people, 'not regarding it as a distinction, but as an unavoidable task ... caring only for the right and the honors to be gained from that, and above all, for justice as the one indispensable thing in whose service and maintenance they will reorganize their own state.'

The principle behind this approach to authority and governance is plainly that of noblesse oblige. To be granted the grace, the blessing, or the privilege of knowledge, together with a clear sense of justice, is to be placed under a correspondingly greater obligation: one's duty to God grows in proportion to the graces bestowed. The Imam gives the principle memorable form in one of his sermons, spoken after one of his supporters had praised him: 'The blessings of God upon a person do not become immense without the right of God over him becoming immense.'

'A good opinion of God'

A subtle point in the letter is easily overlooked, both because it is put so tersely and because its positive counterpart is never stated outright. It comes as the Imam instructs Malik about the kind of advisers he should keep near him, telling him to avoid misers and cowards:

> وَلاَ تُدْخِلَنَّ فِي مَشُورَتِكَ بَخِيلاً يَعْدِلُ بِكَ عَنِ الْفَضْلِ، وَيَعِدُكَ الْفَقْرَ، وَلاَ جَبَاناً يُضعِّفُكَ عَنِ الاْمُورِ، وَلاَ حَرِيصاً يُزَيِّنُ لَكَ الشَّرَهَ بِالْجَوْرِ، فَإِنَّ الْبُخْلَ وَالْجُبْنَ وَالْحِرْصَ غَرَائِزُ شَتَّى يَجْمَعُهَا سُوءُ الظَّنِّ بِاللهِ.

Do not include among those you consult a miser who would hold you back from generosity and warn you of destitution; nor a coward who would make you feel too weak for your affairs; nor a greedy person who would make the amassing of wealth by evil means seem attractive to you. For miserliness, cowardice and avarice are so many diverse inclinations, all united by a bad opinion of God.

The phrase 'a bad opinion' (سُوءُ الظَّنِّ بِاللهِ, su al-zann) points to a basic misunderstanding of the divine nature, and from that misunderstanding a whole series of false ideas about the qualities of God follows. Each vice grows from it. The miser holds the bad opinion that God may fail to provide in time to come; the coward, that God may fail to protect him; the greedy person, that God has given him less than he needs. Seen this way, every human vice traces back to a failure of the intellect, which is itself the fruit of weak faith, or even of outright atheism — for to disbelieve in God is to deny His reality, and so to hold the worst 'opinion' of Him. The converse follows just as plainly. A strong and deep faith produces a 'good opinion' (حُسْنُ الظَّنّ, حَسَنَ al-zann) of God: a true perception of the divine Reality, both intellectual and contemplative. And this good opinion in turn lets the soul be genuinely, and not merely superficially, marked by the divine qualities that Reality contains. Those qualities are to be understood as transcendent — absolutely one with the divine Essence, and utterly beyond the reach of the human soul — but also as models, exemplars, patterns, and perfections that the soul is to imitate so far as it can.

This is the very opposite of 'comparing oneself with God in greatness'. One must become as 'like' God as possible, yet this growth unfolds entirely within the unchanging fact of one's servitude to Him: it is the imitation of God's qualities, never the personification of God. One must be generous because God is al-Karim (اَلْکَرِیم), the Generous, and compassionate and merciful because He is al-Rahman (الرحمن الرحیم), the Compassionate — not because one is generous or merciful in oneself. Yet one must be equally lowly, humble, and self-effacing, for no measure of virtue or intelligence can lessen the gulf between the Absolute and the relative, between the Creator and the created. Humility and self-effacement, and virtues like them, are so many ways of expressing the human being's utter dependence upon God, or of manifesting the transcendence of the Absolute. Between the human and the divine, then, there is analogy of two kinds, positive and inverse. By the first, 'He who shows not mercy will not have mercy shown to him'; by the second, as quoted above:

> يَٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ أَنتُمُ ٱلْفُقَرَآءُ إِلَى ٱللَّهِۖ وَٱللَّهُ هُوَ ٱلْغَنِىُّ ٱلْحَمِيدُ
>
> 'O mankind, ye are the poor before God, and God, He is the Rich, the absolutely Praiseworthy' (Fatir: 15).

The two must therefore be brought together: the active human virtues, which are positive reflections of the divine qualities, and the spiritual awareness of one's own nothingness before the divine Reality, which is, on the human plane, the inverted reflection of divine transcendence. The image is that of a mirror. What one sees in a mirror is oneself, but reversed; and to look into the divine mirror is to see, on the human plane, that same inverted reflection of God's transcendence. A human being is at once the slave and the representative of God — 'abduLlah (عباد اللہ) and khalifatuLlah (خلیفۃ اللہ). Awareness of one's servitude keeps one from sliding, even without knowing it, from representing God to rivalling Him in grandeur. A living commitment to representing God keeps that awareness of servitude from sinking into forgetfulness of the dignity and the responsibilities of the human state — a forgetfulness that ends in spiritual paralysis and the neglect of one's duty toward the world. The balance between these two sides of human existence is itself a further aspect of sacred justice, of setting things in their right order, and it tunes the quality of moral action to the harmony of pure being.

State and society

The Imam then describes in the letter the different classes of people within state and society — the army, the scribes, the judiciary, executive officers, tax-collectors, peasants, merchants, artisans, and last of all the destitute — each with its own rights and duties. The instructions in this part of the letter may be regarded as justice at its sharpest edge in practice, taking full account of the demands of social and political life. The governor is to treat every class under his authority as holding both rights and obligations; yet the essential character of his relationship to society as a whole is once again fixed by his primary duty to God. For it is God who has assigned each class its share, and who has laid down, as a binding covenant from Him, the limits and duties of each, according to His Book or the Sunna of the Prophet:

> وَكُلٌّ قَدْ سَمَّى اللهُ سَهْمَهُ، وَوَضَعَ عَلَى حَدِّهِ وَفَرِيضَتِهِ فِي كِتَابِهِ أَوْ سُنَّةِ نَبِيِّهِ (صَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَآلِهِ) عَهْداً مِنْهُ عِنْدَنَا مَحْفُوظاً

God has ordained the share of every one of them and laid down His covenant about the limits of each in His Book and the Sunna of His Prophet by way of a settlement which is preserved with us.

The governor's duty to society is therefore part of the universal covenant between man and God — the 'ahd (عہد) that recurs so often in the Quran. By calling on this Quranic term to reinforce the duty of acting justly toward every class of society, the Imam knew that his words would gain immeasurably in force from their resonance with verses such as these:

> أَفَمَن يَعْلَمُ أَنَّمَآ أُنزِلَ إِلَيْكَ مِن رَّبِّكَ ٱلْحَقُّ كَمَنْ هُوَ أَعْمَىٰٓ ۚ إِنَّمَا يَتَذَكَّرُ أُو۟لُوا۟ ٱلْأَلْبَبِ ۱۹ ٱلَّذِينَ يُوفُونَ بِعَهْدِ ٱللَّهِ وَلَا يَنقُضُونَ ٱلْمِيثَقَ ۲۰

'Is one who knoweth that what is revealed to thee from thy Lord is the truth like one who is blind? But only men of substance take heed, those who abide by the covenant (عہد) of God, and break not the tryst (ٱلْمِيثَقَ) (Ar-Ra'd: 19-20); and:

> وَلَا تَقْرَبُوا۟ مَالَ ٱلْيَتِيمِ إِلَّا بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ حَتَّىٰ يَبْلُغَ أَشُدَّهُۥ ۚ وَأَوْفُوا۟ بِٱلْعَهْدِ ۖ إِنَّ ٱلْعَهْدَ كَانَ مَسْـُٔولًۭا ۳۴
>
> 'Approach not the wealth of the orphan except with that which is more right, until he attains maturity; and abide by the covenant, truly, the covenant will be asked about' (Al-Isra: 34).

Significantly, just before the instructions quoted above, the Imam advises Malik to study long with the 'scholars' (الَعُلَمَاءِ) and to converse long with the 'sages' (الْحُكَمَاءِ):

> وَأَكْثِرْ مُدَارَسَةَ الَعُلَمَاءِ، وَمُنَافَثَةَ الْحُكَمَاءِ، فِي تَثْبِيتِ مَا صَلَحَ عَلَيْهِ أَمْرُ بِلاَدِكَ، وَإِقَامَةِ مَا اسْتَقَامَ بِهِ النَّاسُ قَبْلَكَ

The purpose is to 'consolidate that which brings well-being to your lands, and to further entrench that which has already been established by your predecessors.' The distinction between scholars and sages matters here. The scholars (عُلَمَاء) know the formal rules as set down in the Quran and the Sunna — the letter of the law, the Sharia (شریعۃ). The sages (حکما) know how to apply that knowledge; theirs is the spirit of wisdom, for wisdom is knowledge put into practice. The distinction is between the letter of the law and the spirit behind it — the spirit without which the rules cannot be applied with care and sensitivity.

There follows a passage that describes, with admirable concision, how each class or segment of society depends upon the others. It begins with the army as the foundation of the state and ends with the governor himself as the center around which the different elements turn. The image is that of a wheel. The governor is the hub, working on the horizontal plane, among created things; the various classes — the army, the scribes, the executive officers, the dispensers of justice, the tax-collectors, the merchants, the artisans, and, lowest of all, the needy and destitute — are the spokes that radiate from him to the rim. Yet the hub is nothing without the axle that turns it. This horizontal center is nothing without the vertical axis that joins it, and through it the whole of society, to the Real. As a hub is useless without its link to the axle, so all the governor's efforts are useless without his link to the Absolute, on which he must wholly depend if society is truly to flourish. The soldiers are called the 'fortresses' of the subjects; and at the close of the passage the governor is told that he cannot accomplish what God requires of him without resolute determination and recourse to Him (al-ihtimam and al-istiana) — a point driven home later in the letter, where the needs of the poor are addressed. Of the instructions concerning the several classes of state and society, a few salient points are enough. For the commander of the troops, Malik is told to choose the man who is 'the most sincere in relation to God, the Prophet and your Imam, the purest of heart, the one most excellent in forbearance (حِلْم), who is slow to anger, happy to pardon, kind to the weak, severe with the strong; one who is neither stirred by violence nor held back by weakness.'

This portrait of the ideal commander reflects the Imam's own character. What stands out is its emphasis on kindness, gentleness, and forbearance — qualities not usually associated with fighting men. Here is a spiritually refined approach to an activity that, left to itself, easily tramples the norms of decency and propriety. Once again the Imam's description of the perfect warrior resembles Plato's description of the 'guardian', the protector of the ideal state. Plato states the central difficulty in raising a force to protect and defend the city as this: how are men whose nature is 'spirited' and courageous 'to be kept from behaving pugnaciously to one another and to the rest of their countrymen'? For 'they must be gentle to their own people and dangerous only to their enemies.' This echoes the Quranic description of those who are with the Prophet as 'severe against the disbelievers, compassionate among themselves' (Al-Fath: 29) — hence the Imam's insistence on the gentle qualities a warrior needs as the essential complement to the martial ones.

For the next class, the judges, the Imam names the virtues that must be present in those who dispense legal justice: they must not be confused by complexities or provoked by litigants; they must be unflinching before the truth, free from greed, unsatisfied by shallow solutions, rigorous in argument, steadfast in seeking out the truth of every matter, and proof against praise and temptation. 'Such people,' the Imam adds, 'are indeed rare.' He also recommends that judges be paid generously, 'so that any deficiency will be removed, thus diminishing the need for help from people' — a condition for keeping any judiciary incorruptible.

For the administrators, Malik is told to test their honesty before employing them and to take scrupulous care that no partiality or favouritism sways his choices. He is to keep watching and investigating their conduct; and, as noted earlier, severe punishments are to be imposed wherever there is evidence of abuse of privilege or misuse of public funds.

For those who till the soil, from whom the land tax is collected, the Imam offers this wise maxim: 'Let your concern with the cultivation of the land outweigh your concern with the collection of the tax, for no tax will be collected if there be no cultivation. And whoever exacts the tax without cultivating the land ruins the land and destroys the people.' His further urgings to attend to the needs of the tillers and to deal fairly with all who seek redress of wrongs close with this warning:

The devastation of the land only comes about through the destitution of its inhabitants; and the destitution of its inhabitants only comes about when the desire to amass wealth rules the souls of the governors, when they have doubts about what endures, and when they profit little from exemplary teachings.

As for the phrase 'having doubts about what endures' (سُوءُ ظَنِّهِمْ بِالْبَقَاءِ), the commentators suggest two meanings. It may be that the governors think they will last forever, forgetting that they will die and be judged in the Hereafter; or it may be that they doubt their position will last long, and so seize whatever opportunities they can while they can. The first reading seems the more likely, so closely does it echo the following Quranic verses — which may themselves be counted among the 'exemplary teachings' (عِبْرَة) the Imam names at the very end of the sentence: 'Woe be to every slandering backbiter, who amasseth wealth and counteth it. He thinketh that his wealth will render him immortal. Nay, but he will be flung to the all-consuming Fire' (Al-Humazah: 1-4).

The Imam uses for the amassing of wealth the same word, jamaa, that appears in this verse. The lesson is plain: greed is a symptom of spiritual short-sightedness. Forgetfulness of what truly endures breeds greed in one degree or another — greed being, here, the craving for a false material richness in place of a true spiritual plenitude — and greed in turn breeds exploitation and oppression. Once more the Imam's vision shows that the vice of injustice does not merely produce corruption; it is itself the product of a chain of causes rooted in a fundamental inversion of spiritual values, a failure to see the true nature of things.

Conscience, Compassion, and the Ordering of Society

The same vision that traces injustice to a disorder within the soul governs the more practical counsel that follows, where the ordering of society is shown to rest upon the conscience of those who hold authority. Even the advice on appointing scribes is sealed with a spiritual message. Malik is told to resist the temptation to appoint only those who have shown him their best side. The governor is to examine each candidate's past record along with the impression he has left upon ordinary people, and then to appoint only those well known for trustworthiness. To do all this is called 'proof of your sincerity toward God.' The choice of officials is thus no mere matter of administration; it is a measure of the ruler's own truthfulness before his Lord, and the care he takes in it answers to God, not to the men who court his favor.

Merchants and craftsmen the Imam then describes as those who bring many benefits to society and must therefore be treated well. On the whole they are a peaceable class, and should be left in peace to go about their work. Yet the vices they are prone to must be held in check: hoarding, miserliness, and cheating. Here again the outward regulation of trade rests upon an inward discernment — one that tells apart the honest labor that enriches a community from the greed that preys upon it.

> *ahd* is the language of solemn promise — to bind oneself by a pledge and be charged with
> keeping it. In this tradition it reaches in two directions at once: the primordial compact
> between God and humanity ("Am I not your Lord?") and the charge of office a ruler receives — the
> very pledge Ali lays on his governor Malik al-Ashtar. The chapter draws on both, and the single
> word shows why they are one duty: the pledge made and the trust to be kept.
