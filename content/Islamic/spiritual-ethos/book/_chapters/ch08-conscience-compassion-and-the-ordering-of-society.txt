# Conscience, Compassion, and the Ordering of Society

One form this enmity with God takes is the inner war against one's own conscience, what is called in Urdu the ⟪ar:ضمیر⟫. The voice of conscience, the very essence and sum total of secular ethics, can here be grasped in its fuller context, rather than merely as the somewhat hypertrophied foundation of individual morality to which secular ethical discourse has reduced it. That fuller context is the supra-individual, metaphysical source from which individual conscience springs: the divine infusion of spiritual discernment within the soul, the divine inspiration that underlies all ethical discrimination. The divine quality of justice is thus translated, at the level of the morally responsible soul, into the voice of inner conscience:

وَنَفْسٍۢ وَمَا سَوَّىٰهَا ۷ فَأَلْهَمَهَا فُجُورَهَا وَتَقْوَىٰهَا ۸

'And the soul and that which perfected it, inspiring it with [consciousness of] its wickedness and its righteousness' (91:7–8). The war against one's conscience, even if the oppressor barely acknowledges it, is in fact, and a fortiori, a war against God.

At a deeper level, then, this war against God stands revealed as a war against the very reality that defines the soul as such. For the soul not only contains a moral conscience, an intrinsic and inalienable awareness of the difference between good and evil, instilled within it by God; it also contains an innate consciousness of the reality of divine lordship, together with the corresponding creaturely servitude. Spiritual consciousness is thus the source of moral conscience. The Qur'an describes the immanence of this spiritual consciousness within each soul in terms that emphasize its supra- or pre-existential nature, evoking a 'moment' at the very dawn of creation, a moment, or ontological degree, that precedes the human condition as such yet enters into the creative articulation of the human soul:

وَإِذْ أَخَذَ رَبُّكَ مِنۢ بَنِىٓ آدَمَ مِن ظُهُورِهِمْ ذُرِّيَّتَهُمْ وَأَشْهَدَهُمْ عَلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ أَلَسْتُ بِرَبِّكُمْ ۖ قَالُوا۟ بَلَىٰ ۛ شَهِدْنَآ ۛ أَن تَقُولُوا۟ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَمَةِ إِنَّا كُنَّا عَنْ هَذَا غَفِلِينَ ۱۷۲

'And when thy Lord brought forth from the children of Adam, from their loins, their seed, and made them testify against their souls [saying], "Am I not your Lord?" They said, "Yes, verily, we testify." [This was] lest ye say on the Day of Resurrection, "Truly, of this we were unaware"' (7:172). The moment He created them He made them witness His glory and testify to it, so that they could plead no ignorance on the Day of Judgement.

What emerges here is that the source of morality transcends the human level even while forming part of the very definition of humanity. From this perspective, what renders the human soul fully human is precisely that element of transcendence which infinitely surpasses the soul and yet, mysteriously, furnishes its own deepest being. In this light, tyranny and oppression take on a far more nuanced meaning, these vices being brought much closer to the lived reality of every soul rather than being confined to those who possess and abuse political power. The tyrant is, first and foremost, one who violates his own soul, one who allows it to be dominated by 'the soul which commands evil', al-nafs al-ammara (⟪ar:النفس الامارۃ بالسوء⟫). Thus the statement the Imam cites as epitomizing the attitude of one who is on the way to corrupting his own soul and becoming an oppressor, وَلاَ تَقُولَنَّ: إِنِّي مُؤَمَّرٌ آمُرُ فَأُطَاعُ, 'I order and am obeyed', is not only to be understood in social terms but must also be interpreted microcosmically. It thereby becomes the statement of the evil element within the soul: the nafs al-ammara, which literally 'orders' (amara) and is obeyed by every other faculty of the soul: intelligence, imagination, will, sentiment, sensibility, and the rest.

The combat against one's own soul (mujahadat al-nafs, ⟪ar:مجاھدۃ النفس⟫) is a theme the Imam returns to repeatedly. This necessity of struggling with and overcoming one's own faults is an essential dimension of justice in the sense defined earlier: placing each thing in its proper place begins with rectifying oneself before presuming to reform or govern others; it means restoring the divine nature within oneself. As the Imam says, 'If your aspiration is to reform the people, begin with yourself, for your pursuit of the reform of others while your own soul is corrupt is the greatest of faults.' In his letter appointing Muhammad b. Abī Bakr (⟪ar:محمد بن ابی بکر⟫), Malik's (⟪ar:مالک اشتر⟫) predecessor as governor of Egypt, the Imam draws a firm connection between the bestowal of power and the obligation to resist one's own soul: 'And be aware, O Muhammad b. Abi Bakr, that I have appointed you as governor over the most immense of the forces at my disposal, the people of Egypt, so you are obliged to oppose your soul and protect your religion.' The relationship between the magnitude of the power placed at one's disposal and the spiritual responsibility that follows from it deserves careful attention. One must be on guard, first in relation to one's own soul and its desires, and secondly in regard to the religion. The greater the power available, the greater the obligation to control and restrain oneself; for if the soul yields to its desires and gratifies them through the power at its disposal, the result is the corruption not only of oneself but of the very religion one is charged to uphold. Here again priorities are ordered by an essentially spiritual principle: the outward form and application of religion depends for its integrity upon the inward disposition of the Muslim's soul. This is another expression of the principle of putting each thing in its proper place. The commanding soul that arrogates to itself the right of autonomy and dominion is described with lapidary precision by the Qur'anic verse:

كَلَّآ إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَنَ لَيَطْغَىٰٓ ۶ أَن رَّآهُ ٱسْتَغْنَىٰٓ ۷

'Truly man is rebellious, in that he deemeth himself independent' (96:6–7). Insofar as the soul pretends to self-sufficiency and attempts to sever itself from its total dependence upon God, it sets itself up as a god in its own right, the person actually coming to believe that he himself is God, and herein lies the true portrait of the tyrant, the one who, as the Qur'an puts it, 'taketh his own desire as his god' (45:23): أَفَرَءَيْتَ مَنِ ٱتَّخَذَ إِلَهَهُۥ هَوَىٰهُ. Knowledge of one's nothingness before God, and consequently of one's unconditional need of Him, may be taken here as the principal means of loosening the grip of the soul that commands to evil. For only the soul that already commands itself to evil will command others to evil; and only the soul that is lowly before God will be humble, compassionate, and just in its dealings with people.

This principle emerges clearly from the following words of the Imam: 'Let the most beloved of affairs to you be those most centred upon the right, the most comprehensive in justice, and the most inclusive of popular approval, for the disapproval of the common folk undermines the approval of the elite ...' This emphasis on the importance of the common people is not only sound practical counsel for fair and effective governance; it can also be read as a reflection of the spiritual imperative to recognize man's actual lowliness before God, and thus as a way of urging the ruler to identify with the poor rather than the rich, in accordance with the principle:

يَٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ أَنتُمُ ٱلْفُقَرَآءُ إِلَى ٱللَّهِۖ وَٱللَّهُ هُوَ ٱلْغَنِىُّ ٱلْحَمِيدُ

'O mankind, ye are the poor before God, and God, He is the Rich, the absolutely Praiseworthy' (35:15). The Imam's own poverty and rigorous austerity were proverbial. That this was far from a simple shunning of the world for its own sake, however, is made clear in his exchange with an ascetic, 'Asim b. Ziyad, whose family complained to the Imam that he was excessively abstemious. The Imam told him to think of his family and not to cut himself off from the good things (al-tayyibat) that God has permitted. 'Asim retorted, 'O Commander of the Faithful, and yet here you are, in your rough clothes and your coarse food!' The Imam replied, 'Woe to you, I am not like you. God, the Exalted, has made it incumbent on true leaders (a'immat al-haqq) that they proportion themselves to the weakest among the people (yaqaddiru anfusahum bi-da'fati'l-nas), so that the poverty of the poor will not engender covetousness.' Here is a rigorous expression of the duty of the most powerful to identify themselves with the most powerless in material terms. It is difficult to conceive of a more effective way of diminishing worldliness and enhancing spirituality in society than this deliberate self-lowering, in purely material terms, to the level of the weakest people over whom one rules. He who could have the most chooses to have the least. The Imam is implying that if a leader is to stand among the a'immat al-haqq, he must manifest his attachment to al-Haqq, the ultimate spiritual reality in whose light material wealth is of no consequence. Thus, even though the most powerful and the most powerless may both be poor in material terms, they alike have access to the treasures of the spiritual domain, in proportion to their realization of the truth expressed in the verse already cited: 'O mankind, ye are the poor before God, and God, He is the Rich, the absolutely Praiseworthy'. On the plane of social psychology, too, the Imam's insistence that rulers live like the poor, as exemplified in his own life, is an effective means of ensuring that the inevitable inequalities of society will not breed envy among the poor.

The compassion of justice

Identification with the poor also deepens the ruler's awareness of his own perpetual need of God, helping him to live more fully in reliance upon divine mercy, in hope and trust; and the natural concomitant of such a life is the bestowal of mercy and compassion upon one's fellow human beings. The capacity to act with compassion in no way conflicts with the demands of justice; rather, it is an intrinsic dimension of justice conceived ontologically, that is, in accordance with the fundamental nature of reality. To act with compassion accords with justice not only in the sense of putting things in their right place, one must extend compassion wherever and whenever it is called for; it also conforms to justice by being at one with the true nature of things, with the intrinsic character of the Real. It is for this reason that at the head of every chapter of the Qur'an (except one) the name 'Allah' is followed by 'the infinitely Compassionate, the ever-Merciful' (al-Rahman, al-Rahim). Put differently, the capacity to act with compassion stems not so much from personal sentiment as from an innate affinity with the very nature of divine reality, that higher reality in which, as the Qur'an tells us, 'My mercy encompasseth all things' (7:156); a reality in which 'thy Lord has prescribed for Himself mercy' (6:54); a reality whose essential character is determined by the principle, 'My mercy takes precedence over My wrath'. Among the many sayings of Ali (ع) on the necessity of showing mercy one finds: 'The dispensing of mercy brings down [divine] mercy.' 'As you grant mercy, so will you be granted mercy.' 'I am astounded by the person who hopes for mercy from one above him, while he is not merciful to those beneath him.'

Neither the rigorous aspect of the nature of things nor the 'wrathful' side of God is being denied here, but both are clearly subordinated to the higher ontological principle of mercy. There is, in other words, no common measure between the relativities that are subject to wrath and punishment and the realities, intrinsically beatific, to which divine mercy and compassion give access. One is therefore more 'real' insofar as one's soul reflects this predominance of mercy, both in spiritual orientation and moral conduct. And in the realm of human society, it lies in the very nature of justice, conceived in this sacred manner, to tend towards compassion wherever possible, even though there must also be a place for the rigorous application of corrective penalty where that is unavoidable. Indeed, in his letter the Imam goes on to instruct Malik to inflict corporal punishment on any executive officer found guilty of misappropriating public funds. The Imam's counsels and letters are replete with stern warnings of swift and severe retribution to his governors, the severity of these warnings being proportionate to his unflinching fidelity to the requirements of fairness and honesty in the administration of public revenues. For example, he writes to Ziyad b. Abihi, deputy to the governor of Basra, Ali (ع)'s cousin Abd Allah b. al-'Abbas:

I swear by God an oath in all sincerity: if news reaches me that you have misappropriated the revenue of the Muslims, whether a small or large amount, I shall inflict a severe punishment upon you, one which will lighten your wealth, burden your back, and degrade your affair.

The Imam was equally severe toward his own relatives when there was even a hint of a request for favours requiring the abuse of public funds. In one sermon he refers to an incident in which his blind brother, 'Aqil b. Abi Talib, made a request for funds from the treasury beyond what was his due. 'He thought I would sell my religion for him,' the Imam said. His response was to bring a red-hot piece of iron close to his brother's body, telling him that by making such a request he was urging Ali (ع) himself to enter into a far more intense fire. Similarly, when informed of the misappropriation of funds by his cousin and long-standing ally 'Abd Allah b. al-'Abbas, the Imam's response was a thundering remonstrance. He ordered his cousin: Fear God and return their property to these people. If you do not, and God gives me authority over you, I excuse myself before God in regard to you [and your wrongdoing], and I shall indeed strike you with my sword, with which whomever I have struck enters the fire [of Hell]. By God, even if Hasan and Husayn had done the like of what you have done, they would not have been granted any leniency by me.

The mention of the Prophet's grandsons is significant. However close the relationship with a wrongdoer, however exalted their status, the principle of justice demands absolute impartiality: there are to be no favours for one's favourites. This attitude is not only the precise opposite of the nepotism that had undermined the administration of the Imam's predecessor, it is also exactly what the Qur'an instructs: 'O ye who believe, be staunch in justice, witnesses of God, even though it be against yourselves, your parents or your near of kin ...' (4:135).

Returning to the theme of mercy, the following passage is remarkable for placing the necessity of compassion in a universal context. It stands as one of the Imam's most important statements on the unity of the human race and the equality of all human beings, and as a corrective to every form of prejudice, particularism, and sectarianism that would restrict the application of justice or compassion to members of one's own group, however defined:

Infuse your heart with mercy for the subjects, love for them and kindness towards them. Be not like a ravenous beast of prey above them, seeking to devour them. For they are of two types: either your brother in religion or your like in creation. Mistakes slip from them, defects emerge from them, deliberately or accidentally. So bestow upon them your forgiveness and your pardon, just as you would have God bestow upon you His forgiveness and pardon; for you are above them, and the one who has authority over you is above you, and God is above him who appointed you ... and through them He tests you.

The universal scope of compassion is here allied to a reminder of God's absolute sovereignty. No man, whether a governor or the ruler who appointed him, is anything other than a slave of God, utterly dependent upon His mercy. Thus every person who finds himself in a position of relative superiority over others must constantly remember his own inferiority before the Absolute; and this awareness both moves him to compassion toward those beneath him and draws to himself the compassion of God above him.

An incident may also be cited that demonstrates well the Imam's compassionate implementation of justice and serves as a vivid expression of the principle noted earlier: all people are 'your like in creation'. The Imam came across an old, blind beggar and inquired about him. He was told that the beggar was a Christian. He said to those around him, 'You have employed him until he is old and infirm, and now you refuse to help him. Give him maintenance from the public funds (bayt al-mal).' This sentence also expresses succinctly the Islamic principle of social welfare (maslaha), rooted in redistributive justice and a policy of strict non-discrimination between Muslims and others. Social justice and religious equality flow from compassion conceived not merely as a sentiment but as an intrinsic dimension of the Real, and therefore translate into a fundamental religious duty incumbent upon all Muslims, rulers and citizens alike.

It should be noted that the final sentence of the passage cited earlier, warning the governor that he should regard his power over his subjects not simply as a privilege but as a trial from God, clearly expresses the Imam's own attitude to rulership. In the famous sermon al-Shiqshiqiyya, he says that he accepted power only as an unavoidable duty:

Had God not taken from the learned [a promise] that they would not acquiesce in the rapacity of the tyrant nor in the hunger of the oppressed ... I would truly have flung its reins [that of the caliphate] back upon its withers ... and you would indeed have discovered that this world of yours is as insignificant to me as that which drips from the nose of a goat.

Here is a striking embodiment of Plato's ideal attitude toward power, the attitude of the true 'philosopher', who acts as ruler solely for the sake of the people, 'not regarding it as a distinction, but as an unavoidable task ... caring only for the right and the honours to be gained from that, and above all, for justice as the one indispensable thing in whose service and maintenance they will reorganize their own state.'

The principle underlying this approach to authority and governance is plainly that of noblesse oblige. When one has been granted the grace, the blessing, or the privilege of knowledge together with a clear sense of justice, a correspondingly greater obligation is created. One's duty to God grows in proportion to the graces bestowed. The Imam gives this principle memorable expression in one of his sermons, delivered after he had been praised by one of his supporters: 'The blessings of God upon a person do not become immense without the right of God over him becoming immense.'

'A good opinion of God'

A subtle point in the Imam's letter can all too easily be overlooked, given the extreme conciseness with which it is expressed and the fact that its positive counterpart is not explicitly stated. It arises when the Imam is instructing Malik on the kinds of advisers he should keep close to him. He tells him to avoid misers and cowards:

وَلاَ تُدْخِلَنَّ فِي مَشُورَتِكَ بَخِيلاً يَعْدِلُ بِكَ عَنِ الْفَضْلِ، وَيَعِدُكَ الْفَقْرَ، وَلاَ جَبَاناً يُضعِّفُكَ عَنِ الاْمُورِ، وَلاَ حَرِيصاً يُزَيِّنُ لَكَ الشَّرَهَ بِالْجَوْرِ، فَإِنَّ الْبُخْلَ وَالْجُبْنَ وَالْحِرْصَ غَرَائِزُ شَتَّى يَجْمَعُهَا سُوءُ الظَّنِّ بِاللهِ.

Do not include among those you consult a miser who would hold you back from generosity and warn you of destitution; nor a coward who would make you feel too weak for your affairs; nor a greedy person who would make the amassing of wealth by evil means seem attractive to you. For miserliness, cowardice and avarice are so many diverse inclinations, all united by a bad opinion of God.

The phrase 'a bad opinion' (سُوءُ الظَّنِّ بِاللهِ, sū' al-zann) points to a fundamental misunderstanding of the divine nature, one that gives rise to a series of misconceptions about the qualities of God. When the divine nature is misunderstood, these very vices follow: the miser holds the bad opinion that God may not provide in the future; the coward believes that God may not protect him; and the greedy person feels that God has given him less than he needs. From this perspective, all human vices trace back to an intellectual dysfunction, which is itself the consequence of weak faith, or even outright atheism, since disbelief in God is a denial of His reality and therefore a form of 'bad opinion' regarding Him. The converse follows logically: a strong and deep faith generates a 'good opinion' (حسن الظن, husn al-zann) of God, an accurate intellectual and contemplative perception of the divine Reality, and this good opinion in turn causes the soul to be truly impressed, and not merely superficially so, by the divine qualities that Reality contains. These qualities must be understood not only as transcendent, being absolutely one with the divine Essence and utterly beyond the reach of the human soul; they must also be grasped as models, exemplars, patterns, and perfections for the soul to emulate within the measure of its possibilities.

This is the very opposite of 'comparing oneself with God in greatness'. One must be as much 'like' God as possible, yet this spiritual development unfolds entirely within the unalterable context of one's servitude to Him; it is the emulation of God's qualities, not the personification of Him. In other words, one must be generous because God is al-Karim (الکریم), the Generous, and compassionate and merciful because He is al-Rahman (الرحمن الرحیم), the Compassionate, not because I am generous or merciful of myself. But one must equally be lowly, humble, and self-effacing, for no degree of virtue or intelligence can reduce the incommensurability between the Absolute and the relative, between the Creator and the created. All such virtues as humility and self-effacement are ways of expressing the absolute dependence of the human being upon God, or of manifesting the transcendence of the Absolute. Thus, between the human and the divine there is both positive and inverse analogy: in terms of the first, 'He who shows not mercy will not have mercy shown to him'; and in terms of the second, as cited above:

يَٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ أَنتُمُ ٱلْفُقَرَآءُ إِلَى ٱللَّهِۖ وَٱللَّهُ هُوَ ٱلْغَنِىُّ ٱلْحَمِيدُ

'O mankind, ye are the poor before God, and God, He is the Rich, the absolutely Praiseworthy' (35: 15). There must therefore be a synthesis between the active human virtues, as positive reflections of the divine qualities, and the spiritual consciousness of one's nothingness before the divine Reality, that inverted reflection, on the human plane, of divine transcendence. It is like looking into a mirror: what one sees is oneself, yet seen in reverse; and when one looks into the divine mirror, the goal is to see, on the human plane, an inverted reflection of divine transcendence. One is simultaneously slave and representative of God, 'abdu'Llah (عباد اللہ) and khalifatu'Llah (خلیفۃ اللہ). A consciousness of one's servitude ensures that one does not slide, even unconsciously, from representing God to rivalling Him in grandeur; while an active commitment to representing God ensures that this awareness of servitude does not collapse into a forgetfulness of the dignity and responsibilities that belong to the human state, giving rise to spiritual paralysis and the neglect of one's duty toward the world. The equilibrium between these two dimensions of human existence is itself another aspect of sacred justice, of placing things in their right order, and it attunes the quality of moral action to the harmony of pure being.

State and society

The Imam then sets out in his letter a description of the different classes of people within state and society: the army, the scribes, the judiciary, executive officers, tax-collectors, peasants, merchants, artisans, and finally the destitute, each class with its particular rights and duties. The instructions in this section of the letter can be regarded as justice at its sharpest edge in actual practice, taking full account of the demands of social and political life. The governor is to regard each class under his authority as possessing both rights and obligations, yet the essential character of the governor's relationship to society as a whole is, once again, determined by his primary duty to God, for it is God who has prescribed to each class its share and has ordained, as a binding covenant with us from Him, the limits and duties of each, according to His Book or the Sunna of the Prophet:

وَكُلٌّ قَدْ سَمَّى اللهُ سَهْمَهُ، وَوَضَعَ عَلَى حَدِّهِ وَفَرِيضَتِهِ فِي كِتَابِهِ أَوْ سُنَّةِ نَبِيِّهِ (صلى الله عليه وآله) عَهْداً مِنْهُ عِنْدَنَا مَحْفُوظاً

God has ordained the share of every one of them and laid down His covenant about the limits of each in His Book and the Sunna of His Prophet by way of a settlement which is preserved with us.

The governor's duty to society is therefore part of the universal covenant between man and God, the 'ahd (عہد) encountered so frequently in the Qur'an. Here again, by invoking this Qur'anic term to reinforce the obligation of acting justly toward all classes of society, the Imam knew that the force of his words would be deepened immeasurably through their resonance with verses such as:

أَفَمَن يَعْلَمُ أَنَّمَآ أُنزِلَ إِلَيْكَ مِن رَّبِّكَ ٱلْحَقُّ كَمَنْ هُوَ أَعْمَىٰٓ ۚ إِنَّمَا يَتَذَكَّرُ أُو۟لُوا۟ ٱلْأَلْبَبِ ۱۹ ٱلَّذِينَ يُوفُونَ بِعَهْدِ ٱللَّهِ وَلَا يَنقُضُونَ ٱلْمِيثَقَ ۲۰

'Is one who knoweth that what is revealed to thee from thy Lord is the truth like one who is blind? But only men of substance take heed, those who abide by the covenant ('ahd) of God, and break not the tryst (mithaq)' (13: 19–20); and:

وَلَا تَقْرَبُوا۟ مَالَ ٱلْيَتِيمِ إِلَّا بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ حَتَّىٰ يَبْلُغَ أَشُدَّهُۥ ۚ وَأَوْفُوا۟ بِٱلْعَهْدِ ۖ إِنَّ ٱلْعَهْدَ كَانَ مَسْـُٔولًۭا ۳۴

'Approach not the wealth of the orphan except with that which is more right, until he attains maturity; and abide by the covenant, truly, the covenant will be asked about' (17: 34).

It is also significant that, immediately before the instructions cited above, the Imam advises Malik to study at length with the 'scholars' (al-ulama) and to converse at length with the 'sages' (al-hukama):

وَأَكْثِرْ مُدَارَسَةَ الَعُلَمَاءِ، وَمُنَافَثَةَ الْحُكَمَاءِ، فِي تَثْبِيتِ مَا صَلَحَ عَلَيْهِ أَمْرُ بِلاَدِكَ، وَإِقَامَةِ مَا اسْتَقَامَ بِهِ النَّاسُ قَبْلَكَ

This should be done so as to 'consolidate that which brings well-being to your lands, and to further entrench that which has already been established by your predecessors.' The distinction between scholars and sages matters here. The scholars (علماء) are those who possess knowledge of the formal rules as established in the Qur'an and the Sunna, the letter of the law, the Sharia (شریعۃ); the sages (حکما) are those who know how to apply that knowledge, which is the spirit of wisdom, for wisdom is knowledge put into practice. It is the distinction between the letter of the law and the spirit behind it, the spirit without which the rules cannot be applied with care and sensitivity.

There follows a passage in which the interdependence of each class or segment of society is described with admirable conciseness. It begins with the army as the foundation of the state and ends with the governor himself as the centre around which the different elements revolve. The image is that of a wheel: the governor is the hub, operating on the horizontal plane with creation; the various classes, the army, the scribes, the executive officers, the dispensers of justice, the tax-collectors, the merchants, the artisans, and the lowest class of the needy and destitute, are the spokes radiating from him and connecting to the rim. Yet the hub itself is nothing without the axle that turns it. This horizontal centre is nothing without the vertical axis that connects it, and through it the whole of society, with the Real. Just as a hub is useless without its connection to the axle, all the governor's efforts are useless without his connection to the Absolute, upon which he must be wholly dependent if society is truly to flourish. The soldiers are described as the 'fortresses' of the subjects, and at the close of this passage the governor is told that he cannot accomplish what God requires of him without resolute determination and recourse to Him (al-ihtimam and al-isti'ana). This crucial point is reinforced later in the letter when the needs of the poor are addressed. As regards the instructions pertaining to the different categories of state and society, it suffices to draw attention to a few salient points. Concerning the commander of the troops, Malik is told to select for this position the person who is 'the most sincere in relation to God, the Prophet and your Imam, the purest of heart, the one most excellent in forbearance (hilm), who is slow to anger, happy to pardon, kind to the weak, severe with the strong; one who is neither stirred by violence nor held back by weakness.'

This portrait of the ideal commander reflects the Imam's own character. What deserves notice is the emphasis placed on kindness, gentleness, and forbearance, qualities not ordinarily associated with fighting men. There is here a spiritually refined approach to an activity which, left to its own devices, easily runs roughshod over the norms of decency and propriety. Once again there is a resemblance between the Imam's description of the perfect warrior and Plato's description of the 'guardian', the protector of the ideal state. Plato identifies the central difficulty in forming forces for the protection and defence of the city as follows: how to ensure that men whose natural disposition is 'spirited' and courageous are 'to be kept from behaving pugnaciously to one another and to the rest of their countrymen'? For 'they must be gentle to their own people and dangerous only to their enemies.' This echoes the Qur'anic description of those who are with the Prophet as being 'severe against the disbelievers, compassionate amongst themselves' (48: 29), whence the Imam's insistence on the gentle qualities required in a warrior as the essential complement to the martial virtues.

Turning to the next category, the judges, the Imam identifies the following as key virtues that must be present in those who dispense legal justice: they must not be confused by complexities or provoked by litigants; they must be unflinching in the face of truth, free from greed, dissatisfied with superficial solutions, rigorous in argumentation, steadfast in the search for the truth of every matter, and immune to praise and temptation. 'Such people', the Imam adds, 'are indeed rare.' He also recommends that judges be paid generously, 'so that any deficiency will be removed, thus diminishing the need for help from people', a prerequisite for maintaining the incorruptibility of any judiciary.

As regards the administrators, Malik is told to test their honesty before employing them and to be scrupulous in ensuring that no partiality or favouritism influences his choices. He must continue to monitor and investigate their conduct. As noted earlier, stringent punishments are to be administered whenever there is evidence of abuse of privilege or misappropriation of public funds.

Concerning those who till the soil and from whom the land tax is collected, the Imam offers this wise maxim: 'Let your concern with the cultivation of the land outweigh your concern with the collection of the tax, for no tax will be collected if there be no cultivation. And whoever exacts the tax without cultivating the land ruins the land and destroys the people.' The Imam's further exhortations to attend to the needs of the tillers of the soil and to be fair to all who seek redress of wrongs are concluded with the following warning:

The devastation of the land only comes about through the destitution of its inhabitants; and the destitution of its inhabitants only comes about when the desire to amass wealth rules the souls of the governors, when they have doubts about what endures, and when they profit little from exemplary teachings.

As regards the phrase 'having doubts about what endures (su' zannihim bi'l-baqa)', the commentators suggest it may mean either that the governors think they will endure forever, forgetting that they will die and be judged in the Hereafter, or that they doubt their position will last for long and therefore exploit whatever opportunities are available to them while they can. The first explanation seems more likely, given how closely it echoes the following Qur'anic verses, which may themselves be counted among the 'exemplary teachings' (ibra) the Imam mentions at the very end of the sentence: 'Woe be to every slandering backbiter, who amasseth wealth and counteth it. He thinketh that his wealth will render him immortal. Nay, but he will be flung to the all-consuming Fire' (104: 1–4).

The Imam uses the same word jama'a for the amassing of wealth that appears in this verse. The lesson is plain: greed is a symptom of spiritual myopia. Forgetfulness of what truly endures gives rise to varying degrees of greed, understood here as desire for a false material richness in place of a true spiritual plenitude, and this in turn breeds exploitation and oppression. Once more the Imam's vision shows how the vice of injustice not only produces corruption but is itself the product of a chain of causes rooted in a fundamental inversion of spiritual values, a failure to perceive the true nature of things.

Conscience, Compassion, and the Ordering of Society

The same vision that traces injustice to a disorder within the soul governs the more practical counsel that follows, where the ordering of society is shown to rest upon the conscience of those who hold authority. The advice given regarding the appointment of scribes is itself sealed with a spiritual message. Malik is told that he must resist the temptation to appoint only those who have shown him their best side. The governor must examine each candidate's prior record together with the impression he has left upon ordinary people, and then appoint only those well known for their trustworthiness. To do all of this is described as 'proof of your sincerity towards God.' The choice of officials is thus not a matter of administration alone; it is a measure of the ruler's own truthfulness before his Lord, and the care he takes in it answers to God rather than to the men who seek his favour.

Merchants and craftsmen are then described by the Imam as those who bring diverse benefits to society and must therefore be treated well. On the whole they are a peaceable class, and should be left in peace to pursue their work. Yet the vices to which they are prone must be kept in check: hoarding, miserliness, and cheating. Here again the outward regulation of trade rests upon an inward discernment, distinguishing the honest labour that enriches a community from the greed that preys upon it.
