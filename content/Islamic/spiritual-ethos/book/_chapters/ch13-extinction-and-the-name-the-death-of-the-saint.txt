# Extinction and the Name: The Death of the Saint

The first response to that question begins with a limit. A person cannot, by his own effort, cross from remembrance into forgetfulness. Someone who is conscious, and who is concentrating on the act of invocation, cannot make himself unconscious of himself in the course of that invocation. The Imam's phrasing is exact: the soul is to be forgotten 'in' the remembrance. Any effort to will oneself into unconsciousness comes from a conscious will, and so it deepens self-consciousness rather than erasing it. The point, then, is not to force oneself to forget oneself while invoking God. It is rather to concentrate on the Name so completely that no room is left in one's awareness — no 'space,' whether mental or existential — for anything but the Named, the reality being invoked. A first degree of self-effacement can therefore be understood as a natural by-product of that total concentration.

The full completion of self-effacement in فَنَاء, however, comes as a divine grace. It exceeds all individual effort, even though the act of invocation is understood to draw this grace down, or to clear from the heart the 'rust' that hides from a person his own nothingness before the sole reality of God, 'that which has no second'. This part of the response can be gathered up in an esoteric reading of the Quranic words, ... thou wilt not forget, unless God willeth it so (Al-Ala: 6-7). Al-Kalabadhi, who wrote one of the most important early manuals of Sufism, opens his chapter on dhikr with words that echo the Imam's: 'The haqiqa of the dhikr is this: that you forget everything other than the madhkur (the Invoked) in the dhikr. For He says: "So remember thy Lord when thou hast forgotten" (Al-Kahf: 24). That is, when you have forgotten what is not God, then you have remembered God.'

The second response can be framed through the principle of inverse analogy. Human invocation is analogous to divine invocation: it is a kind of reflection of it. And like any reflected image, human invocation both shares in what it reflects and turns it upside down. In the first respect, the act of invocation brings the invoker a fuller consciousness and a fuller share in being. In the second respect, human invocation reaches its completion in what looks like the very opposite of this: the effacement of the individual in the object he invokes. Yet the opposition is only apparent. In reality this effacement is the fulfillment of that same growth in consciousness and being, though its final stage is a sudden leap — the negation of the very limits that make a person a separate individual. The individual is limited, and a limit is itself a kind of negation; so to negate this negation amounts to pure affirmation: the affirmation of unconditional being that lies beyond the individual. On the plane of the self, this negation of a negation can be seen as the reflection of the negation of nothingness that Ibn al-Arabi expresses in his line, 'Praise be to Him who engendered things from nothing, then negated it.'

Here the Imam's enigmatic saying returns: 'I am the death of the dead.' It recalls the words Rumi places in the Imam's mouth in these lines of his Mathnawi:

He said, 'I am wielding the sword for God's sake, I am the servant of God, I am not under the command of the body.

I am the Lion of God, I am not the lion of my passion: my deed bears witness to my religion.

In war I am (manifesting the truth of) thou didst not throw when thou threwest: I am (but) as the sword, and the wielder is the (divine) Sun.

I have removed the baggage of self out of the way, I have deemed (what is) other than God to be non-existence.

I am a shadow, the Sun is my lord; I am the chamberlain, I am not the curtain (which prevents approach) to Him.

I am filled with the pearls of union, like a (jewelled) sword: in battle I make (men) living, not slain.

The cosmic unfolding of creation moves from God to man, and it requires the negation of a relative nothingness that is pregnant with the possibilities of being. Man's realization of God moves the other way, and it requires the negation of that same 'nothingness' once breathed into being by God. Ibn al-Arabi's treatise Extinction in Contemplation names what is at stake: 'the extinction of that which never was ... and the subsistence of that which never ceased to be.' The following Quranic verses are clearly implied: 'Everything thereon perisheth (فَانٍۢ), and there subsisteth (يَبْقَىٰ) only the Face of thy Lord, possessor of glory and bounty' (Ar-Rahman: 26-27).

Yet everything that exists has two aspects, or two 'faces': a divine face, which is the thing's true being, and a created face, which is 'nothing'. The 'Face' of the Lord that alone subsists is therefore not only the divine Reality above and beyond all things, but that same reality within all things. This is the meaning of al-Ghazali's esoteric commentary on the Quranic verse, 'Everything perisheth except His Face' (Al-Qasas: 88). Al-Ghazali comments:

[it is] not that it perisheth at some particular moment, but rather it is forever perishing, since it cannot be conceived except as perishing. For each thing other than God is, when considered in and by itself, pure nothingness; and if considered from the face (وَجْهَهُ) to which existence flows to it from the Prime Reality, it is viewed as existing, but not in itself, solely from the face which accompanies Him Who gives it existence. Therefore, the divine face is the sole thing in reality.

He then ties the mystical realization of this oneness to the invocation of God and to the forgetting of the self within that invocation, and so he gives a valuable commentary on what this total dissolution of self-consciousness means. The Sufis who verify this oneness through mystical 'taste' (ذَوْقٌ), he writes, are 'drowned in oneness', so that they become 'dumbfounded':

No capacity remained within them save to recall Allah; yea, not so much as the capacity to recall their own selves ... when this state prevails, it is called in relation to him who experiences it Extinction, nay Extinction of Extinction, for the soul has become extinct to itself, extinct to its own extinction; for it becomes unconscious of itself and unconscious of its own unconsciousness, since were it conscious of its own unconsciousness, it would be conscious of itself.

Relevant here too is a saying that al-Qushayri attributes to Abu 'Uthman, which throws light on another side of the unconsciousness' reached through invocation: 'Whoever has not tasted the loneliness of unconsciousness will not find the food of the intimacy of remembrance.' Here losing consciousness of oneself is called 'loneliness', while becoming fully conscious of God through invocation yields 'intimacy'. One cannot find true intimacy with the divine Reality except by stripping the self of its false 'intimacy' — that self-satisfied, habitual self-centeredness which is the shadow cast by self-awareness. It is this awareness of self that, in the Imam's statement, must be forgotten. Such higher forgetfulness can arise only on the ground of the remembrance of God; for only the source of Being, unconditional Consciousness, and eternal Life can extinguish, eclipse, or efface the relative existence, partial consciousness, and apparent life of the individual. This, the haqiqa of the dhikr, is put with perfect economy in the Imam's own words: ... forget your own soul in your remembrance.

The state of فَنَاء, the highest degree of this 'forgetting' of oneself, is therefore a grace. The individual cannot attain it, however hard he strives. Yet this does not mean the invoker gives up effort. On the contrary, as the Sufi tradition attests again and again, فَنَاء is closely bound to the practice of dhikr, and is in fact its very climax. After this state, or hal, of extinction comes the station, or maqam, of subsistence, baqa: a return of consciousness to the self — a self now transfigured by the knowledge of reality that was impressed upon the soul in the state of فَنَاء. For it is only in the state of فَنَاء that the final 'vision' of absolute oneness is reached, a vision that leaves no room for the duality of being which clings inescapably to individuality. To assert the 'individual' is to assert its correlate, the Lord, and so to affirm duality.

Al-Kashani makes this point in his commentary on the Quranic verse, 'Remember Him as He hath guided you' (Al-Baqarah: 198), where he traces the guided ascent through the degrees of the dhikr. The first is the dhikr of the tongue, which corresponds to the soul (النَّفْس). Then comes the dhikr of the heart (الْقَلْب), which corresponds to the divine acts, those that make His graces manifest. Then the dhikr of the 'secret' (اَلسِّرُّ), which corresponds to the vision of the divine acts together with 'the unveiling of the sciences of the manifestation of the divine qualities'. Then the dhikr of the spirit (الرُّوح), corresponding to 'the contemplation of the lights of the manifestations of the divine qualities, with an apprehension of the light of the Essence'. Then comes the dhikr of 'the hidden' (الْخَفِيُّ), which is 'the contemplation of the beauty of the Essence along with the subsistence of duality'. And finally the dhikr of the Essence, 'which is essential vision, attained through the removal of duality.'

The same point emerges from the counsel of Ibn 'Ataillah, which shows how the dhikr opens a person to the divine presence, and to the grace by which alone the individual can truly forget' himself — that is, become 'unconscious of his own unconsciousness', as al-Ghazali put it:

Do not abandon the invocation because you do not feel the Presence of God therein. For your forgetfulness of the invocation of Him is worse than your forgetfulness in the invocation of Him. Perhaps He will take you from an invocation with forgetfulness (غَفْلَةٌ) to one with vigilance (يَقَظَة), and from one with vigilance to one with the Presence of God (حُضُور), and from one with the Presence of God to one wherein everything but the Invoked (الْمَذْكُورُ) is absent. 'And that is not difficult for God.'

In his commentary on the Hikam, Ibn 'Ajiba adds: So, in the measure that one is extinguished in the Name, one is extinguished in the Essence.' This follows from the principle that the Name is the Named. In another work, a commentary on a poem by Shushtari on the divine Name Allah, he sets out this central tenet of the invocatory discipline in Sufism more plainly. The first line of the poem reads, speaking of the letters that make up the word Allah: 'The alif before two lams, then a ha': coolness of the eyes. Ibn 'Ajiba comments: The "coolness of the eyes", this is joy, for tears of joy, as one knows by experience, are cool. The author means this: this Name is the joy of my heart, its happiness, its beatitude.' He then quotes some lines from al-Hallaj on the letters of the Name Allah, adds a verse of his own, and follows with this comment: 'The Name is not other than the Named, for joy cannot come from anything but the essence of a thing, not from the name as such.'

This sums up the doctrine that the Name and the Named are identical — the rationale for the Sufi practice of dhikruLlah. Yet a problem arises here. However closely the Imam's statements on the dhikr may agree with these later Sufi expressions of the same underlying principles, this particular emphasis on the identity of the Name and the Named appears, on the surface, to conflict with many sayings of the Shii Imams that deny the Name is the Named. Imam Jafar al-Sadiq, for instance, is reported to have said:

The Name of Allah is other than Him. For everything to which the name of a thing is applicable is a creation, hence other than God. For whatever the tongue expresses, or is worked on by the hand, is a creation ... everything described is created, and the Creator of things cannot be described within the limits of a named object.

Allah has ninety-nine Names. If the Name were the Named, then every one of the Names would be a god. But Allah is the one meaning, or spiritual reality, mana, indicated by the Names, and all of them are other than Him.

This apparent contradiction can be resolved in two ways. First, one must weigh carefully what the word 'Name' means in each of the two contexts: the context of doctrinal distinction, where the Absolute is held strictly apart from the relative, and the Essence from all forms; and the context of methodic concentration, where the Absolute is understood to be the reality of the very form through which it reveals itself. One can then turn to one of the most esoteric aspects of how the Shii Imams understood themselves: their references to their own selves as the 'most beautiful Names' (ٱلْأَسْمَآءُ ٱلْحُسْنَىٰ).

As for the meaning of 'Name', Ibn al-Arabi offers a precise definition: [T]he Names have two connotations; the first connotation is God Himself who is what is named, the second that by which one Name is distinguished from another ... As being essentially the other, the Name is the Reality, while as being not the other, it is the imagined Reality. On the one hand, each Name is identical with every other Name, because each is essentially identical with the Named — the one Essence that is the source of all the Names. On the other hand, each Name has its own particular meaning and quality: it is the relationship through which the one Reality engages the created cosmos. In this second respect each Name is distinct from the others, and distinction implies limitation, and so relativity, and in the end a kind of illusion before the one and only Reality. It is in this sense that the Name takes on the character of 'imagined Reality'. For Ibn al-Arabi, the Names have no separate existence of their own; they all return to the one and only Reality, that of the divine Essence. This dissolves the problem raised in the saying of Imam Jafar — the problem that different Names would imply different 'gods'.

A lexicon of Sufi terms, the Lataif al-ilam fi isharat ahl al-ilham (Subtle Disclosures in the Allusions of the Folk of Inspiration), attributed to al-Kashani, gives a definition of the divine Names that also helps to distinguish these two connotations. Under the term 'Names of the Divinity', the text reads:

These are, in the terminology of the Sufis, expressive of the manifest aspect of being, in respect of its determination within a specific meaning. For every divine Name is the manifest aspect of being, which, in turn, is identical to the Essence, but not as the Essence is in itself; rather, in respect of its specification and determination within a particular meaning, or what one might call a 'quality'. For example, the Name al-Hayy ('The Living') is a Name of manifest, determined being, but in respect of its specification and determination as a particular meaning, it is Life. So in regard to being itself, al-Hayy is identical to the Essence, while in regard to its determination as a particular meaning, it is other than the Essence. If you have understood what we have said, you will know what is meant by the saying: the Name is not identical to the Essence, the Named, neither is it other than It.

The Name and the Named differ in name and in thought, and in this respect the Name is not the Named. Yet the two are also spiritually assimilated, and in this respect the Name is the Named. A further distinction must then be drawn. The spoken form of the Name is one thing; the Name as an attribute of the Essence is another. Taken as an attribute, the Name is nothing but the divine quality it designates, and that quality is in turn identical to the Essence. Taken as the 'name' of this Name, it is merely a spoken form. Qaysari, another leading commentator on Ibn al-Arabi, brings this distinction to light in the introduction to his commentary on the Fusus al-hikam.

The Essence receives a Name when it is considered together with some determined quality and in relation to one of Its theophanies. 'The Compassionate' has an essence, which is compassion; 'The All-Conquering' has an essence, which is all-conquering power. Such spoken Names are Names of Names, and this is how the saying is to be understood: the Name is identical to the Named.

In his monumental commentary on Qaysari's introduction, Jalal al-Din Ashtiyani, a contemporary authority on Shii 'Irfan, adds an important observation after quoting this passage. It returns the discussion to two earlier threads: extinction through the invocation of the Name, and the point that the Imams themselves are the most beautiful Names'. The saints, Ashtiyani writes, often speak of the Names as one with the Named because they have in mind the referent (مِصْدَاقٌ), which is the الصِّفَة, the divine attribute or quality: 'The الصِّفَات are in one respect the specifications of the Essence, while in the station of unicity they are annihilated in the Essence, and are thus identical to the Essence.'

This metaphysical account of how the Name, the الصِّفَة, and the Essence stand to one another mirrors, on the objective and universal plane, the path the soul travels inwardly and mystically when it is extinguished in and by the invocation of the Name. This extinction is what فَنَاء means: the perishing of egoistic consciousness before the Absolute. The الصِّفَات are 'annihilated' in the Essence and are thereby absolutely and unconditionally one with it; in the same way, the soul extinguished by the Name reaches at a single stroke both its total nullification and its ultimate realization — no longer as an individual soul. This 'extinction' on the plane of the individual soul amounts to 'realization' on the plane of transcendence only by virtue of the divine theophany, the particular divine Name, the 'heart' that forms the soul's true being, its mustanad ilahi. Here the deepest reality of تَوْحِيد comes to light. The individual no longer affirms that 'there is no god but God', for the individual has been extinguished by the reality that 'there is no reality but the one and only Reality'. God is 'that which has no second'. Just as 'No one has truly said Allah but Allah', so too no one realizes God but God. God alone comes to 'know' or 'be' Himself, and this is one aspect of the purpose of creation, as the hadith qudsi indicates: 'I was a hidden treasure and I loved to be known.'

Seen in this light, the saints (اَلْأَوْلِيَاءُ) are perfectly self-realized theophanies, wholly effaced in that of which they are the theophanic manifestations; and their very effacement in the divine Reality is the ultimate source of their knowledge of it. This is all the more true of the Prophet himself, the prototype of all the saints and the 'perfect man', al-insan al-kamil. On this point Sabziwari, in his commentary on the divine Names, cites the saying of Ali (ع), 'We are the most beautiful Names': 'There is no doubt that they are His most exalted indicators and His greatest signs (عَلَائِمِهِ الْعُظْمَى وَآيَاتِهِ الْكُبْرَى), as the Prophet said, God bless him and his family, "Whoso sees me has seen the Truth (اَلْحَقّ)." He goes on to equate the spiritual station, or inner reality, of the Imams with that of the divine Names and attributes, while stressing that the divine Essence transcends all Names and attributes and remains forever ineffable, known only to itself. The Prophet, the Imam, the saint, the perfect man', thus manifest those aspects of the divine nature that can be perceived on the human plane. The more the divine Essence is exalted in transcendence above all Names and attributes, the more brilliantly those Names and attributes are reflected in the mirror that the 'perfect man' constitutes. The unknowable transcendence of the hidden 'treasure' is, in some measure, offset by the faithful reflection of its communicable aspects in the 'friend of God'.

Identifying the Imam with the divine Name or attribute has implications worth weighing, for in one respect such an identification would appear to be شِرْك — the 'association' of a creature, however exalted, with the transcendent Reality. The same charge appears to apply to the ecstatic utterances (شَطْحِيَّاتٌ) of many of the most celebrated Sufis, such as the anal-Haqq ('I am the Real') of al-Hallaj and the subhani ('Glory to me') of Bayazid. Yet from a viewpoint shaped by the Imam's own conception of the divine attributes, a very different picture emerges — one that, far from being tainted by شِرْك, in fact reaches the most profound application of تَوْحِيد. The first sermon of the Nahj al-balagha contains these remarkable and highly important statements about the attributes of God:

The perfection of purification (أَخْلَاص) is to divest Him of all attributes (الصِّفَات) because of the testimony of every attribute that it is other than the object of attribution, and because of the testimony of every such object that it is other than the attribute. So whoever ascribes an attribute to God, glorified be He, has conjoined Him with something else; and whoever so conjoins Him has made Him twofold; and whoever makes Him twofold has fragmented Him; and whoever thus fragments Him is ignorant of Him.

When the Imam says that pure devotion to God requires stripping God of all attributes, what is negated is not the divine attributes themselves. What is negated is any notion of them as separate, self-standing, or added-on realities — as things substantially 'other than' the Essence of God, or set apart from it in their very being. The reality of God's attributes is plainly affirmed in an earlier phrase of the same sermon: 'He whose attribute has no binding limitation (الَّذِي لَيْسَ لِصِفَتِهِ حَدٌّ مَحْدُودٌ).' What the sermon denies is that the reality of any divine attribute can be confined within the bounds of human thought and language. No divine attribute can stand apart from the divine nature; that nature is infinite; and so every attribute is of infinite scope as well, beyond all the limits that formal thought and verbal definition presuppose.

The attribute or Name, then, is to be affirmed in one respect and negated in another: affirmed as not other than the Essence, negated insofar as it is regarded in itself, on its own account. This same metaphysical view of how the attribute or Name stands to the Essence can be applied, mystically, to how the individual soul stands to the divine Reality. On its own account the individual is nothing; but in respect of the divine ground beneath it — the divine Name, the mustanad ilahi that forms its essential reality — it is not other than the unique Real. As Ibn al-Arabi puts it, 'The final end and ultimate return of the gnostics is that the Real is identical with them, while they do not exist.' This captures perfectly the twofold aspect of the realized sage's soul: insofar as he is aware that 'he does not exist', he knows that the Real is his true identity. However paradoxical it seems, the mystic's realization of absolute Reality goes hand in hand with his realization of his own non-existence. Ibn al-Arabi expresses these two aspects of one and the same realization in compressed form. He describes the summit of his spiritual ascent, his miraj, recounting that God removed from him his 'contingent dimension (إِمْكَانٌ), resulting in the attainment of the inner realities of all the Names'. He continues:

Thus I attained in this nocturnal journey the inner realities (مَعَانِي) of all the Names, and I saw them returning to One Subject (مُسَمَّى) and One Entity (عَيْنٌ): that Subject was what I witnessed and that Entity was my Being. For my voyage was only in myself and pointed to myself, and through this I came to know that I was a pure servant' without a trace of lordship in me at all.

This passage helps to explain so many of the ecstatic utterances of the Sufis and the seemingly self-deifying statements of the Imams. Their personal servitude to God is not denied. On the contrary, it is fully realized through the knowledge of their true identity, for that identity as the Named is reached only when their individuality is effaced. Like the divine attributes, they are mustahlak, annihilated in the divine Essence, so that no individual appropriation of the glory of God is possible. When the Imam appears to speak of his own greatness, this is nothing but the greatness of God expressed through him as a mazhar, a locus for the theophanic manifestation of the divine — and expressed precisely in the measure of his own effacement in that greatness.

The same point is reinforced by what 'Attar recounts about Imam Jafar al-Sadiq in his Tadhkiratul-awliya'. Someone accused him of being proud, and the Imam replied: 'I am not proud, for pride pertains to the divine greatness. Since I have gone beyond the pride of self, His pride has come and taken the place of my own pride. With pride of self present, there can be no divine greatness, but through the divine greatness, pride will be present.' The divine qualities shine with greater brilliance, not less, through the prism of a soul made transparent to the divine Reality — a transparency that comes from the dissolving of all the clouds of egoism and passion.

A holy utterance bears on this: 'My slave draws near to Me through nothing I love more than that which I have made obligatory for him. My slave never ceases to draw near to Me through supererogatory acts (نَوَافِلُ) until I love him. And when I love him, I am his hearing by which he hears, his sight by which he sees, his hand by which he grasps, and his foot by which he walks.'

In the light of all this, the paradox this divine utterance contains can now be resolved. On the one hand, the thing most beloved to God appears to be the obligatory rites. On the other, it is the supererogatory rites that carry the slave near to God until He loves him — and that love seems the greater, since it issues in the divine penetration of the human faculties, the unitive realization proper to تَوْحِيد. The paradox dissolves once it is seen that God's 'love' for the obligatory rites is a love directed toward something apart from Himself, whereas His love for the 'slave' — the pure slave effaced in his Lord — is infinitely greater, since it shares in God's love for His own Self-manifestation, and so for Himself. In the beginning God 'loved to be known', and so He created the world. In 'knowing' Himself through the effaced slave, the seeming 'other', His love to be known is realized through the saint whose greatest glory is precisely his own effacement, poverty, and emptiness of self.

It is in this sense that the following statement of the Imam is to be understood: 'Down from me torrents do pour; up to me no bird can soar.' Torrents pour down from him because — and in the measure that — he is extinguished in the source of the torrent. The image recalls the Quranic description of the 'slaves of God' who, unlike the 'righteous', drink directly from the celestial spring of Kafur, identified in the end with the divine Essence: 'Truly, the righteous shall drink from a cup flavoured with Kafur, a spring wherefrom the slaves of God drink, making it gush forth abundantly' (Al-Insan: 5-6). These slaves make the spring flow more abundantly because, in al-Kashani's words, 'they are themselves the sources of this spring; there is no duality or otherness. Were it otherwise, it would not be the fountain of Kafur, because of the darkness of the veil of egoity (ana iyya) and duality.'

The final degree of the dhikr, as al-Kashani defined it, is the dhikr of the Essence, 'which is essential vision, attained through the removal of duality'. The dhikr 'polishes' away not only the impurities of the heart and mind but, in the end, through the grace of the madhkur, every taint of duality — and above all that root supposition of duality, the individual as such. It removes all subtle or hidden شِرْك, the 'partners' in being that are non-existent before the One 'that has no second', and it establishes the spiritual principle of تَوْحِيد, so that the heart, transfigured by the knowledge of the sole Reality, comes to see the light of that Reality reflected in all the mirrors of creation. To realize this one and only Reality — to 'make it real' in the fullest sense — is, by the same stroke, to realize the unreality, or merely apparent reality, of everything else.

At this level of realization a measure of perplexity inevitably attends the revelation of the divine Reality on every side. The more the divine Essence is exalted beyond all comprehension, the more unavoidable it becomes that creaturely consciousness must be utterly annihilated if that Essence is to be 'realized', if gnosis of it is to be reached. On the one hand, one 'knows' God by knowing that His Essence is unknowable, utterly other, utterly transcendent — the essence of the Creator lying beyond the veil that creation itself forms. On the other hand, the very distinction between creature and Creator is effaced once the creature has 'forgotten' itself, not merely as a notion but existentially, in a state of genuine contemplative extinction. It is then no longer a matter of one form of self-consciousness replacing another, but of the 'self' being taken out of the reckoning altogether, leaving only pure consciousness as such — which cannot 'belong' to any entity without being reduced to the relativity proper to that entity. Consciousness of the Absolute, that is, 'being conscious of the Absolute', is thus transmuted into absolute consciousness. The Real is 'realized' not by another but through another, which is the source of the title al-arif biLlah, literally 'the knower through God' rather than simply 'the knower of God'. The Imam says, 'Know God through God.' Knowledge of God at this deepest level is the Real knowing itself through the creature; and to say 'through' implies an unobstructed passage, the creature being marked by perfect faqr — poverty, self-effacement, and, in the end, extinction.

Realization through remembrance, however, means not only "forgetting" everything in existence except God, but also discovering that in reality there is nothing in being but God. In the Imam's image of the "polish" worked by remembrance, the heart — once blind and deaf — is made able to "see" and to "hear." It sees the Face of God wherever it turns, and it hears the cosmic hymn of remembrance and praise that the whole of creation sings, for every creature proclaims the boundless creativity of its Creator. As the Quran says:

>
> "Do you not see that God is the one who is glorified by everything in the heavens and the earth, and the birds with wings outstretched; each one indeed knows its prayer and its glorification, and God is All-Aware of what they do" (An-Nur: 41).

A final reflection turns to a passage from the Imam's famous and much-disputed Khutbat al-bayan, "The Sermon of Clarifying Expression":

I am the Sign of the All-Powerful. I am the gnosis of the mysteries. I am the Threshold of Thresholds. I am the companion of the radiance of the divine Majesty. I am the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden. I am the Face of God. I am the mirror of God, the supreme Pen, the Tabula secreta. I am he who in the Gospel is called Elijah. I am he who is in possession of the secret of God's Messenger.

This remarkable affirmation of the Imam's inmost reality may seem shocking to the exoteric mentality, and is all too easily misread as a blasphemous self-deification of the kind associated with Hallaj. One key to understanding these declarations of "his" true nature correctly is the statement: "I am the gnosis of the mysteries." Once this is grasped, every other statement falls naturally into place. For here the Imam is not identifying himself with God, but with the knowledge of God — and not with his own knowledge of God, since that would still be a relative knowledge, belonging to him as a relative being. Only knowledge itself — the knowledge God has of Himself, not the knowledge a human being has of God — can be fully identified with its object. The distinctions between knowledge, knower, and known dissolve only at this transcendent level, just as the distinction between knowledge and being ceases to operate there. Thus the knower is the known, when the knowledge in question is that consciousness which the divine Reality has of itself. Individual consciousness reaches this knowledge only on one condition: the utter extinction of every trace of individual, creaturely selfhood. To "remember" God, one must have "forgotten" oneself. To "be" the knowledge of God is, by a kind of metaphysical reverberation, to "be" everything that God is; for the consciousness that is wholly at one with the Absolute is nothing other than "the radiance of the divine Majesty." Insofar as this consciousness is one with "the gnosis of the mysteries" — and these mysteries (أَسْرَارٌ) are all rooted in the ultimate mystery (اَلسِّرُّ), the one and only Essence of God — it is necessarily one with all the divine attributes. It is in this sense that the Imam can proclaim: "I am the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden."

The same reasoning applies to the claim to be "the Face of God" (wajh Allah). It becomes intelligible only once one grasps that the Imam describes himself as the "mirror" of God. A mirror loses its identity as a mirror when attention rests entirely on the object reflected in it: the mirror's own surface becomes, as it were, invisible, and only the object is seen. Conversely, when the mirror itself becomes the object of attention, the face reflected in it can no longer be seen. The mirror is thus identified with what it reflects — which in a certain sense is "in" the mirror — yet the mirror does not cease to be a mirror, and the Face does not cease to be wholly independent of it. The "veil" here is the mirror itself. To attend to the mirror as a mirror prevents one from seeing the object it reflects; but once attention rests wholly on the image the mirror holds, the mirror ceases to be a veil and instead reveals the object itself. Within this symbolism, forgetting the mirror answers to the effacement of the self, and fixing on the image within it answers to the remembrance of God.

Yet this leads to another paradox: the deepest effacement of individuality goes hand in hand with the most perfect expression of humanity. The "friends of God," who know themselves to be nothing before the Absolute, are precisely those who most faithfully reflect the divine Names and qualities — qualities that belong not to them but to God. This is why the great saints and sages are also models of virtue, virtue whose true foundations are the "most beautiful Names" and attributes of God. Those divine attributes shine through them most clearly because the mirrors of their hearts have been polished by the remembrance of God, and the most stubborn stains — those left by egotism — have been removed. The saint's creativity is raised to its highest pitch precisely because the divine qualities reflected by the heart are no longer blocked — on the inner side, by the individual coagulations that egotism generates, nor, on the outer side, by the cosmic coagulations of this lower world. For such saints are those whose "hearts are in the Gardens [of Paradise] while their bodies are at work." Their hearts, bound and attached to that divine realm — the abodes and stations of paradise reaching all the way to the divine presence — are already "within" that transcendent realm; they already live more in that spiritual world than in this earthly one. And so, though they labor in this world with their bodies, their outward actions veil their inward transcendence of higher realities: they act, but they are not bound by the fruits of their action.

Such souls have not merely understood this principle; they have been seized by it, in their very existence, at the root of their being. It is the principle voiced in the Quranic verse:

> وَمَا هَذِهِ ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَآ إِلَّا لَهْوٌۭ وَلَعِبٌۭ ۚ وَإِنَّ ٱلدَّارَ ٱلْآخِرَةَ لَهِىَ ٱلْحَيَوَانُ ۚ لَوْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْلَمُونَ
>
> "And the life of this world is naught but sport and play; and truly, the abode of the Hereafter, that is true life" (Al-Ankabut: 64).

Their instincts are therefore always attuned to that higher reality. When Ali was struck by the poisoned sword of Ibn Muljam, the cry that broke from him was not one of pain but of triumph: "I have triumphed, by the Lord of the Kaba!" At the very moment of death there was in him nothing but the remembrance of God, and the Imam passed away, it is said, with his tongue still repeating the words la ilaha illaLlah. His death was thus completed exactly as his life had been lived, in accord with the wisdom of the Prophet, who, when asked which of all acts is the most meritorious, replied: "That you die while your tongue is moistened with the invocation of God."

All the rites and practices bound up with the chivalric orders of Islam were traced, without exception, back to the Prophet's son-in-law, Ali ibn Abi Talib, who was thereby established as the supreme source of the virtues of futuwwa, as Muhammad Jafar Mahjub observes in his "Chivalry and Early Persian Sufism." S. H. Nasr writes to similar effect that Ali, having received the "truth and power" of futuwwa from the Prophet, "became the supreme source of futuwwa in Islam for both Sunnis and Shiites... The personality of Ali, at once sage and knight, contemplative and protector of laborers and craftsmen, has continued to dominate through the centuries over the horizon of futuwwa as it has over much of Sufism." Nasr also draws attention to the organic link between futuwwa and the guilds, the asnaf, singular sinf, of craftsmen and artists established throughout the length and breadth of the Muslim world.

It is often said that the Naqshabandi tariqa is the one exception to this rule, tracing its line of descent from the Prophet through the first caliph, Abu Bakr, rather than through Ali. But this affiliation with Abu Bakr belongs to only one of its three principal silsilas. 'Abd al-Majid b. Muhammad al-Khani, himself a Naqshabandi-Khalidi shaykh, calls the first chain (السِّلْسِلَةُ الْأُولَى) the "golden chain," which begins with Ali and runs through the Shii Imams down to Ali b. Musa, then to Maruf al-Karkhi and the other masters. The second chain (السِّلْسِلَةُ الثَّانِيَةُ) likewise begins with Ali, running through Hasan al-Basri. Only the third chain, called al-silsilat al-siddiqiyya, runs from the Prophet to Abu Bakr (الصِّدِّيقُ).
