This is a work of scholarship, and at its close a work of translation. Reza Shah-Kazemi published it in 2006, reading the Shii and Sufi traditions of Islam together and drawing on sources that reach back to the seventh century. Its subject is the spirituality of Ali ibn Abi Talib — cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad, fourth caliph, and first of the Shii Imams — and its argument is that in this one figure the inward substance of the Islamic tradition finds an integral expression.

The book moves inward. It opens with Ali's nearness to the Prophet and the household around him, then turns to what he understood by intellect as distinct from mere reason, and to the certainty that intellect yields. From there it follows his conception of justice — sacred rather than merely legal — through the ordering of society, the claims of the poor, and the sincerity that must underlie every act of rule. Its final movement is devotional: prayer, the remembrance of God, and the extinction of the self in the divine Name.

The closing chapters set commentary aside and let Ali speak for himself. The first sermon of the Nahj al-Balagha and his letter to the governor Malik al-Ashtar are given in full translation, so that the reader may weigh the man against his own words.
