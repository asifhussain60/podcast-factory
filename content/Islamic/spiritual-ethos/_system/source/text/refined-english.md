# Spiritual Ethos

*Justice and Remembrance — Reza Shah-Kazemi. Chapters one to three, with both primary texts from the appendices.*

## Introducing Ali (ع) and his Spiritual Ethos

I am the city of knowledge and Ali (ع) is its gate; so whoever desires knowledge, let him enter the gate. (Ana madinatu'l-ilm wa 'Ali babuha; Fa-man arada'l-ilm fal-ya'ti'l-bab).

To speak of Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع) — cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad, fourth caliph of Islam and first in the line of Shi'i Imams — is to speak of the quintessential spirituality of the Islamic tradition. In this seminal figure of nascent Islam we find an integral expression of the two fundamental sources of Islamic spirituality: the Qur'anic revelation and the inspired Sunna of the Prophet. By Sunna we do not mean simply the outward imitation of the Prophet's actions — a reductionism all too prevalent in our times — but rather the spiritual substance of prophetic perfection to which the Qur'an itself refers: وَإِنَّكَ لَعَلَىٰ خُلُقٍ عَظِيمٍۢ — 'Verily, thou art of a tremendous nature' (68: 4). The Prophet's words and deeds express that substance without exhausting it. The inner assimilation of this substance, rather than the merely formal imitation of words and deeds, is the goal of every spiritually inclined Muslim. There is a profound affinity between the believer's soul and the prophetic nature, a mysterious proximity that transcends time and space, as the following verse makes clear: ٱلنَّبِىُّ أَوْلَىٰ بِٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ مِنْ أَنفُسِهِمْ ۖ وَأَزْوَٰجُهُۥٓ أُمَّهَٰتُهُمْ ۗ وَأُو۟لُوا۟ ٱلْأَرْحَامِ بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلَىٰ بِبَعْضٍۢ فِى كِتَٰبِ ٱللَّهِ مِنَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُهَٰجِرِينَ إِلَّآ أَن تَفْعَلُوٓا۟ إِلَىٰٓ أَوْلِيَآئِكُم مَّعْرُوفًۭا ۚ كَانَ ذَٰلِكَ فِى ٱلْكِتَٰبِ مَسْطُورًۭا: 'The Prophet is closer to the believers than they are to themselves, and his wives are their mothers; and by the ordinance of God, blood relatives are more entitled to one another than other believers and emigrants, unless you would show kindness to your close associates — this is decreed in the Record' (33: 6). The Prophet thus outwardly manifests the perfection which the spiritually sensitive believer intuits, to some degree or another, as determining the very essence of his or her own soul.

It is not surprising, therefore, to find the Prophet saying that Ali (ع) 'is as my own soul (ka-nafsi)' — عليٌّ مِنِّي بِمَنْزِلَةِ نَفْسِي — affirming not only the extraordinarily close bond between Ali (ع) and himself, but also the affinity — and ultimately the identity — between every sanctified soul and the prophetic nature. More mysteriously still, he said to Ali (ع), 'You are from me, and I am from you (anta minnī wa ana minka)' — أنْتَ مِنِّی وَ أَنَا مِنْکَ. As for Ali (ع)'s assimilation of the substance (⟪ar:مواد⟫) of the Qur'an, we have this prophetic saying: إِنَّ عَلِيًّا مَعَ الْقُرْآنِ وَالْقُرْآنُ مَعَ عَلِيٍّ، لَنْ يَفْتَرِقَا حَتَّى يَرِدَا عَلَيَّ الْحَوْضَ — "Ali is with the Qur'an and the Qur'an is with Ali (ع). They will not separate from each other until they return to me at the [paradisal] pool (al-hawd)." The spirit of the Qur'an and the soul of the Prophet were thus fully interiorized by Ali (ع), and it is precisely this interiorization of the twin sources of Islamic revelation that constitutes the spiritual path of Islam.

Our principal way of investigating and meditating upon the spirituality of Ali (ع) is to examine the corpus of teachings attributed to him. Even if not every one of the thousands of sayings ascribed to him can be confidently traced back to him, the sheer magnitude of the corpus testifies to the fact that he must indeed have articulated a very large number of profound teachings. No other companion of the Prophet has anything approaching this body of work. Similarly, even if his foundational role in the development of a whole range of sciences remains debatable — sciences such as jurisprudence (fiqh), theology (kalam), Qur'anic exegesis (tafsir), rhetoric (balagha), grammar (nahw), and calligraphy (khatt), along with the mystical knowledge associated with Sufism and such arcane disciplines as numerology (jafr) and alchemy (al-kimiya) — the fact that later authorities in all these fields regard him as having provided their initial impetus speaks to the far-reaching influence of both his formal teachings and his personal radiance.

Given how closely the Islamic intellectual sciences as a whole are identified with Ali (ع), the Prophet's description of him as the 'gate' to prophetic wisdom — cited as our epigraph — appears at once as a description of what Ali (ع) was in relation to the Prophet during their own age, and as a prophecy of the role he would play in the subsequent unfolding of the tradition's sciences. All such sciences may be understood as outward, formal manifestations of the essential inward spirit of the Islamic revelation — the spirit that Ali (ع) synthetically assimilated and faithfully transmitted. Before going any further, however, the main historical outlines of his life deserve to be sketched.

### Biographical Sketch

Ali (ع) was a 'follower' of the Prophet in the deepest sense of the word. He lived physically in the Prophet's shadow and absorbed spiritually all that radiated from him. The intimacy of their relationship is captured in the words of one of the Imam's own sermons:

⟪ar:لَمَّا كُنْتُ وَلِيدًا ضَمَّنِي إِلَى صَدْرِهِ، فَكُنْتُ أتَّبِعُهُ اتّبَاعَ الفَصِيلِ أُمَّهُ⟫ — When I was but a child he took me under his wing. I would follow him [the Prophet] as a baby camel follows its mother. ⟪ar:يَرْفَعُ لِي فِي كُلِّ يَوْمٍ عَلَمًا مِنْ أَخْلاقِهِ الْكَرِيمَةِ وَيَأمُرُنِي بِالاِقْتِدَاءِ بِهِ⟫ — Every day he would hold up for me a sign of his noble character and bid me follow it. ⟪ar:وَكَانَ يَتَعَاهَدُ غَارَ حِرَاءٍ فِي كُلِّ سَنَةٍ، فَأَرَاهُ وَلَا يَرَاهُ غَيْرِي⟫ — Each year he would go into seclusion at [the mountain of] Hira'. I saw him there, and no one else did. ⟪ar:وَلَمْ يَجْمَعْ بَيْتٌ وَاحِدٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ فِي الإِسْلامِ غَيْرَ رَسُولِ اللهِ صَلَّى اللهُ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلَّمَ وَخَدِيجَةَ وَأَنَا ثَالِثُهُمَا⟫ — At that time no household had gathered for the religion of Islam except that of the Messenger of God and Khadija, with myself as the third. ⟪ar:أَرَى نُورَ الْوَحْيِ وَالرِّسَالَةِ، وَأَشُمُّ رِيحَ النُّبُوَّةِ⟫ — I saw the light of revelation and the message, and I breathed the fragrance of prophecy ...

It is Ali (ع) as the faithful follower and transmitter of the prophetic norm that we wish to address in this biographical sketch. We shall therefore look at the early period of his life, from his birth (c. 599 CE) to the death of the Prophet (11/632). The second period — from the Prophet's death to Ali (ع)'s assumption of the caliphate (35/656) — and the third, consisting of his brief caliphate, dominated by tragic civil wars and ending with his assassination in 40/661, will not be treated here. The issues that period raises are fraught with historical and historiographical complexities, and engaging them would draw us away from the primary aim of this book: to introduce the spiritual ethos of this extraordinary figure and demonstrate its universal relevance, both within Islam and beyond it. That ethos transcends formal religious boundaries just as it rises above the political questions on which the outward Shi'i–Sunni divergence rests — questions that serve only to distract from Ali (ع)'s essential spiritual message.

What we wish to highlight instead is the unifying force of his spiritual teachings, whose immense importance and profundity all Muslims, whatever their school of thought, affirm. In what follows we shall briefly survey the earlier period of Ali (ع)'s life — the years spent with the Prophet — together with the way the Prophet described Ali (ع) in well-attested sayings, since this constitutes the indispensable background against which his role as gate to prophetic wisdom must be understood. The second part of this introductory chapter will then address some key principles of his spiritual ethos, with particular emphasis on the role of the intellect within it.

According to all Shi'i authorities and several Sunni sources, Ali (ع) had the unique distinction of being born inside the Ka'ba in Mecca. His mother was Fatima bt. Asad; his father, Abu Talib, son of 'Abd al-Muttalib, was a leading member of the Hashimite clan. Abu Talib had raised the young orphan Muhammad — son of his brother 'Abd Allah — and later became his chief protector when the message of Islam was openly proclaimed in Mecca. Several years before the mission began, during a period of drought, Muhammad relieved the burden on Abu Talib by taking his young son Ali (ع), then about five years old, into his own household. From that time on Ali (ع) was like a son to him, and the two were rarely apart.

Ali (ع) is regarded as the first male to enter the religion of Islam, though he was only nine or ten years old at the time. Abu Bakr was the first adult male to embrace the new faith, and Khadija, the Prophet's wife, was the first woman. The immediacy of the young boy's response to the prophetic message and his unwavering loyalty thereafter are of great significance. About three years later, when the Prophet was instructed by revelation to 'warn thy nearest kin' (26: 214), he invited the leading members of his clan to a feast and then addressed them: 'O sons of 'Abd al-Muttalib, I know of no Arab who has come to his people with a nobler message than mine. I bring you the best of this world and the next. God has commanded me to call you unto Him. Which of you, then, will help me in this, and be my brother, mine executor and my successor amongst you?' All were silent — except for the young Ali (ع), who spoke up: 'O Prophet of God, I will be thy helper in this.' The Prophet placed his hand on Ali (ع)'s neck and said, 'This is my brother, mine executor and my successor amongst you. Hearken unto him and obey him.' Though the clan's leaders met this with mockery at the time, it was taken far more seriously as the years passed and the extraordinary character, nobility, and intelligence of the Prophet's young cousin became ever more brilliantly evident.

Also significant in this early period is the role Ali (ع) played in the migration (hijra) from Mecca to Medina (622 CE), the event that marks the beginning of the Muslim calendar. The Prophet's enemies had plotted to assassinate him, and knowing this, Ali (ع) risked his own life by taking the Prophet's place — sleeping in his bed on the very night he departed. In Medina, the Prophet adopted Ali (ع) as his 'brother' in the pact of brotherhood he established between the 'emigrants' (al-muhajirun) — the Muslims who had accompanied him from Mecca — and the 'helpers' (al-ansar), the tribes of Medina who had embraced the new faith.

In the Medinan period, Ali (ع) distinguished himself on the battlefield, as a scribe of the unfolding Qur'anic revelation, and as a leading companion of the Prophet. His courage and heroism in battles fought against the overwhelmingly superior forces of the Meccan Quraysh and their allies were unrivalled. Ali (ع) stood at the forefront of nearly every major engagement fought under the Prophet's banner and was frequently appointed standard-bearer. He was never defeated in single combat — the duels with which battles customarily began — nor, indeed, in any battle in which he fought. His indomitable courage and incomparable skill as a warrior became legendary. It is said that during the battle of Uhud a heavenly voice was heard proclaiming, 'There is no chivalric knight but Ali (ع), no sword but dhu'l-faqar (la fata illa 'Alī, la sayf illa dhu'l-faqar)' — the latter being the name of the sword the Prophet had given him. But the battle that did most to establish Ali (ع)'s reputation as the pre-eminent — indeed invincible — warrior of Islam was that of Khaybar in 7/629. The Muslims had been unable to make any headway against the heavily fortified and apparently impregnable defences of their opponents. The Prophet then declared that he would give his army's banner to one who 'loves God and His Messenger and is loved by God and His Messenger,' through whom victory would be granted. He sent for Ali (ع), who was absent owing to an affliction of the eyes. The Prophet healed him by placing saliva on his eyes, and Ali (ع) went on to lead the Muslims to victory. In the thick of battle, having lost his own shield, he is said to have seized a gate and used it in its place — a gate that, after the battle, could only be lifted by eight men.

Ali (ع)'s role as a warrior is but one of many factors that account for his singular place in the Islamic tradition. Of far greater significance is his closeness, in every respect, to the Prophet, and the wisdom and sanctity that flowed from that closeness. Ali (ع) was accorded the immense honour of marrying the Prophet's daughter Fatima, regarded as the most saintly woman in Islam alongside her mother, the Prophet's first wife Khadija. One of the epithets the tradition bestows on Fatima is Majma' al-Nurayn, 'Confluence of the Two Lights' — the two lights of nubuwwa (prophecy) and walaya (sanctity). Ali (ع) was thus, after the Prophet, the head of what was known as the Ahl al-Bayt, 'the People of the House,' that is, the Prophet's family.

The Ahl al-Bayt are mentioned in an important Qur'anic verse as having been purified of all defilement: 'God only wisheth to remove from you all impurity, O People of the House, and to purify you with a complete purification' (33: 33). According to several of the most important early sources, the Prophet called for Fatima, Ali (ع), and their two sons Hasan and Husayn and declared, 'This is my Ahl al-Bayt.' On one occasion, as most sources report, the Prophet gathered these members of his family beneath his cloak and recited the words of this verse. The occasion arose when a Christian delegation from Najran was engaged in religious debate with the Prophet and a verse was revealed directing that a challenge of mutual imprecation (mubahila) be put to the Christians. The Prophet brought the four persons named above for this mubahila, but the Christians declined the challenge.

The importance of the Ahl al-Bayt is expressed in another verse in which the Prophet is instructed, 'Say: I ask you for no reward, save love of the near of kin' (42: 23). According to several of the most important Qur'anic commentators, the 'near of kin' in this verse refers to the Prophet's Ahl al-Bayt. Love of the Prophet's family is thus explicitly enjoined by revelation — without thereby excluding the verse's broader import, namely love of all those bound to one by close kinship. How deeply the Prophet's family is to be revered by all Muslims is further indicated by the fact that blessings are to be invoked upon his al, or progeny, as part of the blessings invoked upon him. The Qur'an instructs the believers: 'Truly, God and His angels bless the Prophet. O ye who believe, bless him and greet him with peace' (33: 56). When this verse was revealed, the Prophet was asked how the blessing should be performed, and he replied that it was to be offered upon him and his al as follows: 'O God, bless Muhammad and the progeny of Muhammad, as Thou hast blessed Abraham and the progeny of Abraham. Truly, Thou art the Praised, the Glorious ...' The importance of this blessing — recited during the canonical prayer — together with the obligation to love the Prophet's family, is beautifully expressed in the following lines by Imam al-Shafi'i, founder of one of the four schools of Sunni jurisprudence:

Loving you, O family of the household of the Prophet of God, is an obligation (fard) from God in the Qur'an which He revealed; Sufficient to show the grandeur of your dignity is that one who blesses you not — no prayer has he.

Also worthy of note is the following prophetic saying on the spiritual significance of the Ahl al-Bayt:

Truly, I am leaving behind amongst you the two weighty things (al-thaqalayn): the Book of God and my Ahl al-Bayt. They will not be parted from each other until they return to me at the [paradisal pool] al-hawd.


In addition to these sayings concerning Ali (ع) as the most important member of the Prophet's Ahl al-Bayt, there are sayings of the Prophet specifically about Ali (ع) that deserve to be cited in full, for it is only on the basis of these sayings that one can begin to appreciate the immensely important role Ali (ع) plays in the spiritual dimension of the Islamic faith. These sayings or traditions (ahadith, sing. hadith) are presented here as a list with minimal commentary, which is how they appear in most standard hadith collections. Anyone who takes prophetic guidance seriously cannot help but take Ali (ع) seriously, and it is largely for this reason that countless Muslims across the centuries have meditated on these sayings, drawing from them diverse interpretations as well as incalculable inspiration. In a certain sense, these sayings form the prism through which the tradition views Ali (ع), and they serve to deepen one's spiritual receptivity to his teachings. The crucial importance of Ali (ع) within the spiritual firmament of nascent Islam emerges with greater clarity in the light of these prophetic descriptions; they should therefore be regarded as a significant dimension of the 'prophetic background' to Ali (ع)'s seminal contribution to Islamic spiritual and intellectual life, whether in a Shi'i, Sunni, or Sufi context.

The traditions given below can be found in any standard Shi'i hadith collection, but in order to show that they — together with their implications and the devotion they have generated — are by no means confined to Shi'i Islam, we cite them here from two of the most authoritative Sunni traditionists: Ahmad al-Nasa'i (d. 303/915), author of the Sunan, one of the six collections of prophetic traditions in the Sunni corpus regarded as 'canonical' (al-kutub al-sitta); and al-Hakim al-Nīsabūrī (d. 405/1014), author of al-Mustadrak 'alā'l-ṣaḥīḥayn (Supplement to the Two Sound Collections). This latter work gathers prophetic traditions that meet the exacting criteria of authenticity established by Bukhari and Muslim — the authors of the ṣaḥīḥayn — but which those scholars did not include in their own collections. The sayings pertaining to Ali (ع)'s distinctions, virtues, and merits — all of which the Arabic word fada'il (sing. fadila) encompasses — explain why Ahmad b. Hanbal (d. 241/855), another leading Sunni traditionist and founder of one of the four Sunni schools of law, made the following statement: 'No companion of the Prophet has had such fada'il ascribed to him as those which have been ascribed to Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع).'

'Truly, Ali (ع) is from me and I am from him (inna 'Ali minnī wa ana minhu), and he is the wali (patron/spiritual master) of every believer after me.'

'Ali (ع) is with the Qur'an and the Qur'an is with Ali (ع), إِنَّ عَلِيًّا مَعَ الْقُرْآنِ وَالْقُرْآنُ مَعَ عَلِيٍّ، لَنْ يَفْتَرِقَا حَتَّى يَرِدَا عَلَيَّ الْحَوْضَ. They will not separate from each other until they return to me at the [paradisal pool] (al-hawd).'

'Three things were revealed to me regarding Ali (ع): he is the leader of the Muslims, the guide of the pious, and chief of the radiantly devout (sayyidu'l-muslimin, imamu'l-muttaqin, wa qa'idu'l-ghurra'l-muhajjalin).'

'Gazing upon Ali (ع) is an act of worship (al-nazar ilā 'Alī ibada).'

'May God have mercy on Ali (ع). O God, make the truth revolve around Ali (ع) wherever he turns (adiri'l-haqq ma'ahu haythu dara).'

'I am the city of knowledge and Ali (ع) is its gate; so whoever desires knowledge, let him enter the gate.'

'O Ali (ع), you are a leader (sayyid) in this world and the Hereafter. Your beloved is my beloved, and my beloved is the beloved of God; your enemy is my enemy, and my enemy is the enemy of God. Woe to those who hate you after me [after I have passed away].'

'Whoever desires to live my life and to die my death and to dwell in the eternal Garden my Lord has promised me, let him orient himself towards Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع), for truly he will never lead you away from right guidance, nor lead you into error.'

The Prophet said that Ali (ع) was 'as my own soul' (ka-nafsi).

He said to Ali (ع), 'You are from me and I am from you (anta minnī wa anā minka), أنْتَ مِنِّی وَ أِنَا مِنْکَ.'

'... whoever obeys Ali (ع) obeys me, and whoever disobeys him disobeys me.'

'You will clarify for my community that over which they will differ after me (anta tubayyinu li-ummatī ma'khtalafū fihi ba'di).'

'There is one amongst you who will fight for the ta'wil [spiritual interpretation] of the Qur'an as I have fought for its tanzil [literal revelation], عَلِیٌّ یُقَاتِلُ عَلیٰ تَاوِیلِہِ کَما قَاتَلْتُ عَلیٰ تَنزِیلِہِ.' Abu Bakr asked, 'Is it I?' The Prophet said, 'No.' 'Umar asked, 'Is it I?' The Prophet said, 'No, it is the one who is mending the sandal.' The Prophet had given Ali (ع) his sandal to mend.

'O Ali (ع), there is in you something akin to Jesus, on whom be peace and blessings. The Jews hated him to such an extent that they slandered his mother; and the Christians loved him to such an extent that they ascribed to him a rank he did not possess.' 'O Ali (ع), whoever separates himself from me separates himself from God, and whoever separates himself from you, O Ali (ع), separates himself from me.'

'Whoever curses Ali (ع) curses me, and whoever curses me curses God.'

The Prophet said to his wife 'A'isha, 'Call unto me the leader (sayyid) of the Arabs.' She asked, 'O Prophet of God, are you not the leader of the Arabs?' He said, 'I am the leader of the children of Adam, and Ali (ع) is the leader of the Arabs.' When Fatima was asked by some women why the Prophet had given her in marriage to so poor a man as Ali (ع), she brought this to the Prophet. He said: 'Are you not pleased that God — exalted and glorified be He — looked upon all the people of the world and chose two men from among them, one of whom is your father and the other your husband?'

'The first of you to reach the [paradisal] pool (al-hawd) is the first of you who entered Islam: Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع).'

'Ali (ع) is from me and I am from him ('Alī minnī wa ana minhu), and no one can fulfil my duty but myself and Ali (ع).'

Ali (ع) himself relates that the Prophet told him that none but a believer will love him, and none but a hypocrite will hate him.

When the Prophet was about to depart on the expedition to Tabuk, he left Ali (ع) as his deputy in Medina. Ali (ع) was saddened at not being able to join him. The Prophet said, 'Are you not glad that your rank in relation to me should be as Aaron's rank in relation to Moses — except that there is no prophet after me?'

The Prophet prayed to God to bring 'the most beloved of Thy creatures' to share with him a meal of fowl. Only when Ali (ع) arrived did the Prophet invite him to join him.

Among the several Qur'anic verses the Prophet commented upon with reference to Ali (ع) is 13:7: 'Verily thou art a warner, and for every people there is a guide.' The Prophet said, 'I am the warner ... you are the guide, O Ali (ع). After me, the rightly-guided shall be guided by you.'

Regarding the revelation of verse 55 of Sura 5 (al-Ma'ida) — 'Verily your wali is only God and His Messenger and those who believe, establish the prayer and give alms while bowing in prayer' — the commentators relate this to the incident in which Ali (ع), while bowing in prayer, extended his ring to a beggar who had asked for alms. The Prophet recited this verse upon hearing of the incident and added, in what are perhaps the most celebrated words of all concerning Ali (ع): 'For whomever I am the mawla [guardian, master, close friend], Ali (ع) is his mawla (man kuntu mawlahu fa-'Alī mawlahu).'

This final saying was expressed on several occasions, the most famous being after the Prophet's last pilgrimage to Mecca in the year 10/632, at a pool midway between Mecca and Medina known as Ghadir Khumm. The Prophet gathered all the pilgrims, had a pulpit erected, and delivered an address to the thousands assembled. The address culminated in the declaration: 'For whomever I am the mawla, Ali (ع) is his mawla.' For Shi'is, this constitutes a clear designation (nass) of Ali (ع) as the Prophet's successor; for Sunnis, it signals the special closeness of Ali (ع) to the Prophet, but not a nomination to political succession. That the reference to Ali (ع) as mawla — in some versions, as wali — carries the highest spiritual significance is not seriously disputed. 'Umar is reported to have approached Ali (ع) and said, 'Bakh bakh' — an expression of congratulation — 'you have become my mawla and the mawla of every Muslim.' The polemical debate that has occupied countless minds over the centuries, and that continues to this day, turns on the political implications of Ali (ع)'s spiritual authority, his walaya.

Although it is not our purpose to examine Ali (ع)'s role within Shi'i Islam in detail, one should note that his charisma was such that even during the Prophet's own lifetime a group was known as the shi'a, or 'partisans,' of Ali (ع). Al-Țabarī (d. 310/923), author of the first voluminous commentary on the Qur'an, refers to the report that the Prophet interpreted the verse 'Those who have faith and do righteous deeds — they are the best of created beings' (98:7) as referring to Ali (ع) and his 'shi'a.' Four individuals in particular were renowned for their devotion to Ali (ع) during the Prophet's lifetime and are regarded as the prototypes of early Shi'ism: Salman al-Farisi, Abu Dharr al-Ghifarī, 'Ammar b. Yasir, and Miqdad b. 'Amr. While this early expression of love for Ali (ع) should not be identified with the theological doctrines later elaborated in what came to be known as the Shi'i branch of Islam, it is worth noting that at the core of Shi'ism — in all its varied expressions — lies fidelity to Ali (ع). This fidelity, loyalty, or love constitutes one dimension of walaya; the other, complementary dimension being the spiritual authority and sanctity of Ali (ع) himself. It is equally important to stress that neither devotion to Ali (ع) nor a spiritual orientation towards the walaya he embodies is the exclusive preserve of Shi'ism. Rather, it extends across the whole of the Islamic faith: devotion to Ali (ع) as head of the Prophet's Ahl al-Bayt is found throughout the length and breadth of the Muslim world, and is especially profound wherever Sufism flourishes. As will be discussed in Chapter 3, Ali (ع) is regarded as the first spiritual 'pole' (qutb) of Sufism after the Prophet himself, and stands at the summit of all the spiritual chains (salasil, sing. silsila) through which the Sufi orders trace their lineage back to the Prophet. To speak of Ali (ع) is not to speak of exclusivist sectarianism but of universal spirituality.

### The Spirit Of The Intellect

What we propose to do in this section is sketch some of the key dimensions of the spiritual ethos of Ali (ع), with particular emphasis on its intellectual aspects. Beyond demonstrating the pivotal role played by the intellect (al-'aql) in the elaboration and assimilation of the Imam's spiritual teachings, this exercise should help to ground more firmly the explorations of the ethos undertaken in the following chapters. One of the central aims here is to illuminate what may be called the 'spirit of the intellect'—the term ⟪ar:الروح العقل⟫ coined by the author to describe the relationship between intellect and spirit—as it appears in the Imam's worldview: a spirit that surpasses the activities of the rational mind while encompassing them, and that extends into domains not nowadays associated with the intellect, such as moral comportment and aesthetic sensibility. We also hope to show how this spiritual understanding of the intellect relates directly to the Qur'anic conception of the spirit (al-ruh), and to explore some of the fruits that come from engaging deeply with this unifying spirit intrinsic to the intellect.

It may be useful at the outset to explain why we prefer the English word 'intellect' over 'reason' as a translation of 'aql. What we wish to evoke is the original meaning of intellectus in Latin Christendom, a meaning virtually identical to that of nous in the Patristic Greek tradition. The Latin intellectus means 'understanding', and it carries within it the sense of standing under something: to understand a thing is to stand beneath a perception of reality and allow that perception to overshadow and govern one's actions, since our actions are always performed under the shadow of how we perceive things. Intellectus/nous is therefore the faculty capable of direct, contemplative vision of transcendent realities—the very realities the Qur'an speaks of but which are hidden from the senses—whereas reason, translating the Latin ratio and the Greek dianoia, is indirect and discursive in character; it works through logic and arrives only at mental concepts of those realities. The root meaning of ratio is a relation or comparison between two numbers, and this captures precisely how reason proceeds: by comparison and by a binary logic of affirmation and negation, weighing one thing against another until it has defined what it is considering. Reason, so understood, is the instrument the intellect employs; but it can carry us only as far as the outward form of a thing, only as far as the mental concept or image of a reality. With the intellect one is able to contemplate or 'see' the Absolute; with reason one can only think about it. Knowing about a reality is one thing; contemplating and experiencing it directly is quite another—and it is the intellect, not reason, that is capable of that experience, even as it is through reason that the intellect is brought to it.

This preliminary exploration of the spirit of the intellect in the Imam's ethos will be sketched chiefly through reflections on a short but supremely important discourse in the Nahj al-balagha: the one the Imam delivered to one of his closest disciples, Kumayl b. Ziyad. In these reflections the same approach will be used as in the two following chapters—an interpretive commentary on the Imam's words that draws not only on his other sayings bearing on the theme at hand, but also on concordant Qur'anic verses and, occasionally, on prophetic traditions that either shed light on the Imam's saying or are themselves illuminated by it. Before turning to that dialogue, however, it is important to stress three factors inherent to the intellectual perspective under consideration: the centrality of revelation, the harmony between revelation and the intellect, and the distinction between the intellect as the governing principle of consciousness and reason as one of its expressions.

Beginning with the first: everything in the Imam's life and teachings is centred on the Qur'anic revelation and its embodiment in the Prophet's conduct. The Qur'an is the centre around which the intellect must revolve, for the intellect needs a centre—principles and definitions to hold fast to for its proper guidance. Indeed, both the thought and the life of the Imam can be read as a kind of spiritual commentary, a ta'wil, on the sacred text. On the doctrinal level, it can be said with confidence that Ali (ع) possessed an innate aptitude for performing the work of ta'wil. On the existential level too, his life—from his birth within the most holy sanctuary of the Ka'ba to his martyrdom in the mosque of Kufa—reads as a dramatic unfolding of the inner meaning of the revealed text. Ali (ع)'s refusal to rest at the superficial surface of things, whether religious, ethical, or political, expressed an unflinching fidelity to the sacred substance of the religion: a fidelity that demanded not only inward intellectual penetration but also outward combative resistance. The Prophet's saying cited earlier is of considerable importance here: Ali (ع) would fight for the ta'wil of the Qur'an as the Prophet himself had fought for its tanzil—عَلِیُّ یُقَاتِلُ عَلیٰ تَاوِیلِہ کَما قَتَلُ عَلیَ تَنزِیلِہِ. As noted earlier, Ali (ع)'s brief caliphate was dominated by bloody civil wars. A strong case can be made that all three of those wars—the Battle of the Camel (al-Jamal), the Battle of Nahrawan, and the Battle of Siffin—were fought precisely for the sake of the correct interpretation and application of the Qur'an, the clarification of its fundamental message of tawhid, and the realization of that oneness on every level, spiritual and social alike. These were not political contests or mere power struggles; they were battles waged to clarify and enforce the oneness of God in accordance with the Qur'an's principles—in thought, in speech, and in action. This claim is illustrated vividly by the Imam's response to a bedouin who, just as the Battle of the Camel (al-Jamal) was about to begin, came to him and said: 'O Commander of the Faithful (یا أمیر المومنین)! Do you say that God is one?' Those present rebuked the bedouin, telling him this was neither the time nor the place for such questions. The Imam, however, intervened: 'Leave him, for surely what the bedouin wishes is what we wish for the people.'

<!-- page 24 -->

We can begin our reflections on the spirituality of Ali (ع) by considering his response to the bedouin—a response that speaks to the principle that right intention must take precedence over outward action, whatever that action may be. Had the Imam been guided by reason alone, he would have agreed with those who rebuked the bedouin and sent him away to find a more suitable time and place; but he saw with an intellect that stands above reason. For the Imam, the most fundamental intention behind every action—whether religious in the ordinary sense or military as in this case—is knowledge of the true nature of ultimate reality. What the Imam 'wishes for the people' is precisely this knowledge, which must lie at the very heart of all concerns, motivations, and actions, whether on the battlefield or the prayer-rug. There is therefore no time or place where the question 'What do you mean by saying God is one?' is irrelevant or out of place. It was for the sake of correct knowledge of the divine—and all its concomitants and repercussions in society—that the Imam was fighting, in fulfilment of the prophetic saying just mentioned. On the very field of battle, then, the Imam delivers a discourse on the meaning of divine oneness.

The opening of the Imam's reply contains a key to understanding his entire spiritual ethos. He refutes the idea that God's oneness is in any sense numerical or countable. He tells the bedouin that it is not permissible to say 'one' while holding any numerical conception in mind, because 'that which has no second does not enter into the category of numbers.' The moment a thing can be counted as 'one', it immediately admits the possibility of a 'two', a 'three', a 'four'—and so a oneness understood in this numerical sense is undone by the very number that would affirm it.

'That which has no second' is not merely a divinity to whom no partner can be ascribed, as the theologian would have it, but that reality which has no otherness whatsoever: the Real, al-Haqq, at once transcendent and immanent, beyond all 'things' and manifested through all 'things', simultaneously inaccessible and inalienable. To say that God has no otherness is not to say that things exist which might have been His partners and which believers simply decline to associate with Him; it is to affirm that nothing in existence bears His likeness, nothing could conceivably be set beside Him. In these few words the Imam effects—for those who have 'ears to hear'—a shift of consciousness from the dogmatic notion of one God over against many gods to the mystical apprehension of the one and only Reality, in the face of which everything else is mere appearance. All things become so many outward phenomena calling out to be deciphered, so many signs (ayat) pointing beyond themselves to that of which they are manifestations—for an aya is precisely a meaningful token that points beyond itself to another. Retracing these appearances back to their source and origin is the literal meaning of ta'wil: going back to the awwal, 'the First' (عَودَۃ الی الاول). On one hand, the Real is described as the beginning and end of all things, the apparent and the hidden; there is nothing in being but this one Reality: 'He is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward' (57:3). On the other hand, apparent multiplicity is to be 'brought back' to real oneness: 'We shall show them Our signs (sa-nurihim ayatina) on the horizons and in their own souls, so that it be clear to them that He is the Real'—سَنُرِيهِمْ آيَتِنَا فِى ٱلأٓفَاقِ وَفِىٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَهُمْ أَنَّهُ ٱلْحَقُّ (41:53).

One saying attributed to Ali (ع) refers to the Prophet as the first person taught by God the science of ta'wil, and records that the Prophet in turn taught it to Ali (ع). According to the Prophet, as noted earlier, 'Truly, Ali (ع) is with the Qur'an, and the Qur'an is with Ali (ع)'—أنَّ علیٌ مع القرآن، و قرآن مع العلی. In another celebrated saying, frequently quoted by Sufi commentators, Ali (ع) claimed that he could load seventy camels with the pages of commentary he might offer on the opening chapter of the Qur'an, the Fatiha. It is worth noting also that Ali (ع)'s cousin Ibn 'Abbas, often called the 'first commentator', acknowledged that he had learned the science of interpretation from Ali (ع).

In this context the following saying of the Imam is of fundamental importance among the many that explain why he is held in the highest esteem within the discipline of Qur'anic exegesis, tafsir: 'The Qur'an consists of a book inscribed between two covers; it speaks not with a tongue, it cannot do without an interpreter (la budda lahu min tarjuman).' The reciprocity between revelation and the intellect is further underlined in the saying, 'The prophet of a man is the interpreter of his intellect (rasul al-rajul tarjuman 'aqlihi).' Elsewhere we find an explicit identification between the intellect and the inner messenger: the intellect is 'the messenger of the Real.'

The Imam's teachings, which give expression to his spiritual ethos, should therefore be read essentially as creative interpretations of the Qur'anic revelation. They initiate a movement from the letter of the revelation to its spirit, and from the spirit of the revelation to the spirit of the Revealer—a process of retracing, of returning to the source, the origin, the beginning, al-awwal, accomplished in the measure that the spirit of the intellect is enlivened within the interpreter. Yet the Revealer remains perforce concealed even as He is revealed through the revelation, concealed because the essence of what is revealed—the divine Reality in itself—is ineffable and inexpressible; only the forms of its manifestations are susceptible to revelation. The dialectical interplay between form and the formless, the evident and the hidden, the revealed and the concealed—these antinomies lie at the root of the paradoxical mode of expression for which the Imam is renowned, and which we will encounter throughout this book. The relationship that concerns us most immediately is the second of the three factors mentioned above: the relationship between revelation and intellection, by which we mean the creative exercise of the intellect. This relationship takes on particular significance in a context substantially defined by fidelity to revelation. Where rationalists in the strict sense regard intellect and revelation as mutually exclusive, this perspective sees them as perfectly harmonious—subsisting in a state of creative reciprocity, each requiring the other for completeness and integrity, for that 'making one' which is the very essence of tawhid.

<!-- page 26 -->

Indeed, one of the most striking features of the Imam's perspective is the way it exposes the hollowness of many of the false or excessively polarized dichotomies that would later come to characterize—or be imposed upon—Islamic thought. This is a consequence of the highly synthetic quality of the Imam's discourses, which some academics regard as 'pre-philosophical' while those within the Islamic spiritual tradition view them as 'supra-philosophical', inspired and evocative. The apparently insurmountable dichotomy between intellect and revelation ramifies into various polarized oppositions: reason versus faith, convention (sunna) versus nature (tabi'a), imitation (taqlīd) versus verification (tahqīq), piety versus inquiry, and so on. In Ali (ع)'s ethos, however, intellect and revelation are not mutually exclusive but complementary and, at the highest level, inseparable, since truth is one. While the human intellect clearly cannot be equated with divine revelation, the spirit animating the intellect is not, in the final analysis, confined by human limitations, as we shall see. It is this spirit that perceives and grasps the divine quality of revelation.

The divine spirit that articulates the form and infuses the content of revelation must be fathomable to some degree by human consciousness; without this, revelation would amount to nothing more than a set of rules and regulations for the will, rather than a source of creative inspiration for the intelligence. It cannot be stressed too strongly that divine revelation, far from curtailing the operations of reason or imposing an unintelligible decree, on the contrary presupposes a creative engagement with all the resources of intelligence—above all those processes of reflection and meditation for which the Imam himself is renowned, and which the Qur'an repeatedly calls for. Revelation is precisely the opposite of an edict imposed from without: the Qur'an constantly demands fikr, deep and sustained reflection, and such reflection does not confine the meaning of the text within its literal surface but opens it up, disclosing the inner horizons of meaning folded within the letter.

For the revealed text is itself silent; it speaks only through its interpreter, the intellect. This understanding of the bond between intellect and revelation rigorously excludes all literalism and superficiality—every attempt to imprison meaning within a flat, one-dimensional reading. How deeply the text's significance is plumbed depends on the depth and penetration of the interpreter's own intellect, for the text carries multiple levels of meaning simultaneously. At its most profound, this 'prophetic' intellect rejoins the very source of revelation; for it is this 'inner prophet'—the inmost degree of consciousness—that ultimately enables a person to confirm the verses of the outer revelation as true: to validate them through lived experience, rather than merely affirming them as dogma. It is in this sense that the Imam says, in the first sermon of the Nahj, that one of the purposes of God's sending forth of prophets to humanity was 'to unearth for them the buried treasures of the intellects' (dafa'in al-'uqul).


This saying is one of the keys to the spiritual ethos we are considering, and we shall return to it again and again throughout these essays. The intellect is not stifled by revelation; it is brought to life by it. Revelation is a clarion call to the spiritual depths of the intellect, cultivating and bringing to fruition the seeds of consciousness — the seeds of توحید — that God has planted there and that lie buried within us. For the spirit of one's intellect already carries the initial imprint of its Creator: in the world of the spirit it witnessed its Lord and testified to His lordship, and revelation, the ذکر, does no more than remind it of that primordial testimony. A reminder, after all, is never new information; it is knowledge one already possesses but has forgotten, for one can only be reminded of what one already knows. We were created in this world in a kind of amnesia — and just as a physician advises the family of a man who has lost his memory to surround him with familiar things, with signs (آیات) from his past, to jog his recollection, so God has set His reminders in the horizons and in souls (آفاق و أنفس). The spirit of one's intellect cannot therefore be alien to what descends as revelation from on high; on the contrary, it must be fundamentally akin to it, and it is precisely for this reason that one can be absolutely certain that revelation truly comes from the Absolute. The point is not to set up a dichotomy between intellect and revelation, but to see the intellect as itself a mode of revelation: the God-given means by which the individual comes to discern, affirm, and plumb the divine 'revelations' outside himself — both those of scripture and those constituted by the 'signs' (ayat) of the natural world. If the human intellect can perceive and recognize revelation, it is because both are rooted in one and the same ultimate source; we came from a spiritual realm, and so did revelation, sent to remind us of what we once witnessed and have since forgotten. Prophetic revelation awakens and quickens the depths of the human intellect; it does not replace or displace it. The messengers do for these buried seeds what the farmer does when he waters what lies hidden beneath the soil, what the miner does when he digs into the mountain for its gold and silver, what the diver does when he descends to the sea-bed to bring up its buried treasures. From this perspective, the revealed text is not some extraneous source set against human intelligence; it is the objective, outward expression of the very principles that the intellect itself yields up to the 'inner prophet' — the intellect serving as the inner messenger whom the outer messenger awakens and nourishes. Here 'intellect' means not simply the faculty of reasoning but the source of consciousness that articulates the human spirit.

<!-- page 22 -->

The intellect, then, holds unsuspected depths — 'buried treasures' — and is far from exhausted by its surface operations, the cognitions and reasonings proper to its purely mental dimension. These considerations bring us to the third of the factors mentioned above: the relationship between intellect and reason. Just as there can be no contradiction whatsoever between revelation and intellection, so too one must avoid setting up a false dichotomy between reason and intellect. Reason should be seen as one of the modes of the intellect; it no more contradicts the intellect than the intellect contradicts revelation. Reason is a mode of the intellect but must not be equated with it wholesale — just as one cannot reduce the universal objectivity of revelation to the subjective particularities of any one individual's intellection, to his or her own 'private' revelation. To equate reason with intellect would be tantamount to denying the very existence of the 'buried treasures' of the intellect — those supra-rational dimensions that are, initially, buried beneath, and thus hidden from, the rational surface of the intellect. The revelation constituted by the intellect is embedded within the very creation of the human being, the intellect being what most critically defines the human being as such. Far from pitting the intellect against revelation, then, each must be seen in the light of the other: the substance of revelation cannot be engaged except through the intellect, and the 'buried treasures' of the intellect cannot be disclosed except through revelation — a genuine co-dependence of the two. As will be seen below, this understanding of the intellect brings us close to what the Imam refers to as the 'heart' (قلب), that innermost mode of perception capable of 'seeing' God. It is the 'heart' that sees God — not with eyesight, not with بصارۃ, but with insight, with بصیرۃ, that spiritual vision generated by the 'verities of faith' (haqa'iq al-iman / حقائق الایمان). As the Imam puts it, in response to the questioner who asked whether he had 'seen' his Lord: 'Eyes see Him not through sight's observation, but hearts see Him through the verities of faith.' We shall return to this 'inner vision' of God through the 'verities of faith'.

To return to the relationship between intellect and reason: one can say that between the spiritual depths of the intellect and its own rational surface there is both continuity and discontinuity. The aspect of continuity is evident in the fact that all thinking begins with concepts that carry intelligible meaning; thought sets out from ideas that are rationally discernible, and these must bear some relation to the realities they express, or they become ideas that lead nowhere. But there is also discontinuity between the spiritual and rational aspects of the intellect, in that reason stops short at the ideas it has 'de-fined'. The Arabic word for definition, hadd (حد), expresses — like its English equivalent — both senses at once: that of a conceptual definition and that of a limitation, a boundary, the very boundary that reason draws around an idea by weighing its pros and cons until it is fixed and 'defined'. To go beyond the limits imposed by this rational act of definition, something more than reason is required; and it is this 'something more' that makes the ethos we are considering a spiritual one rather than a rationalistic one. It is certainly 'rational', in the sense that its form is indeed open to rational scrutiny; but it is not 'rationalistic', inasmuch as its essence is not predicated on reason alone.

<!-- page 23 -->

Ali (ع) frequently hints, in highly allusive terms, at the mysteries and marvels — the unearthed treasures of the intellect — that he has directly witnessed by the eye of his purified heart. He also alludes to this supra-rational mode of awareness indirectly, through a bewildering use of apophatic discourse and paradoxical utterance, of which the corpus attributed to him is full. In this ethos it seems that the higher the truth, the deeper the paradox: the sheer incommensurability between reality as it is in itself and our human conceptions of it is effectively evoked by this creative use of paradox. In modern terms, every human conception of reality needs to be 'deconstructed'; but unlike a modern deconstructionism so radically destructive that nothing remains to 'reconstruct', the Imam's apophasis feeds directly into modes of spiritual intuition, contemplation, and realization of that which reason, on its own, is incapable of attaining. It is this intuition and contemplation that 're-members' — or 'reconstructs', if one prefers — the spiritual realities that transcend the categories of reason. The value of the paradoxes, the antinomies, the apophasis, and the negation is entirely predicated on the eminently positive realities that the intellect can plumb as it becomes aware of its own hidden depths. The resulting consciousness does not so much negate reason as situate it, thereby revealing the limits of reason's proper sphere of operation: it reveals that reason alone cannot 'comprehend' the higher realities, nor fully express them, however indispensable it may be in initiating the cognitive movement toward those realities.

Thus, when the Imam negates — as he does so often — the possibility of human conceptions encompassing the divine Reality, what is being negated is the limitation that is the inescapable corollary of all such conceptions, not the positive content, the ma'na or spiritual meaning, of the conception itself; for this conception is a necessary starting point for a process of assimilation that engages a deeper level of awareness than that 'de-fined' by the mind, and which therefore extends far beyond the domain of speculative thought. In this light, the negation of the limitations of human conception should be seen as the prelude to an absolute affirmation: the affirmation of the Absolute, which has no limitation. The spiritual negation of conceptual limitation is of an upward, constructive order, not a nihilistic, deconstructive one. Nowhere is this style of discourse more apparent than in the famous opening lines of the first sermon of the Nahj al-balagha:

Praise be to God, whose laudation those who speak cannot deliver, whose graces those who count cannot number, whose rightful due those who strive cannot render; He who cannot be grasped by far-reaching aspirations, nor fathomed by profound intuitions; He whose attribute has no binding limitation, no existing description, no time appointed, no term extended.

The divine attribute (sifa) has no limit in terms of which it can be defined — laysa li-sifatih hadd mahdud — and yet it is affirmed as real. It has no 'existing description', and yet in some sense it is described (mawsuf), by the very words of Qur'anic revelation; for in the Qur'an God is described as possessing such attributes as mercy, compassion, and power. He is the Merciful, the Compassionate, the Almighty — and yet the Imam affirms that these very attributes cannot be defined, bounded, or described. The paradox sharpens still further when the Imam speaks of the perfection of ikhlas, the 'rendering sincere' — the purification of one's devotion to God and of one's conception of Him: this perfection, he says, is to divest (nafy) God of all attributes. On one hand the attributes are affirmed; on the other they are to be negated. The rational mind can only think of God in terms of the attributes given verbal expression in the Qur'an; and yet here the Imam appears to negate these same attributes, thereby depriving the mind of any convenient conceptual handle by which to grasp the divine Reality. The simultaneous affirmation and negation of the attributes raises an apparently irresolvable contradiction, and in doing so enhances receptivity to a deeper mode of knowing. This style of discourse appears designed to awaken in the listener some latent awareness inscribed in the heart or the depths of the intellect, and to invite the seeker to dive into those depths, into the knowledge 'buried' in the intellect. The very shock of the apparent contradiction in this apophasis seems intended to precipitate a leap of consciousness — from the formal activity of reason to the supra-rational contemplation of the spirit.

<!-- page 24 -->

Consider the words spoken by the Imam immediately after affirming that his heart had indeed witnessed God, not by means of outward vision but through 'the verities of faith':

My Lord is not described in terms of dimension (bu'd), nor motion (haraka), nor rest (sukun) ... nor through coming or going: He is subtle in subtlety but not described by subtlety, magnificent in magnificence but not described by magnificence, great in greatness but not described by greatness ...

The attributes are again affirmed and denied. God possesses these attributes but is not reducible to them. We can begin to think of God in terms of these categories, but He is not reducible to our conceptions of them. The attributes offer the mind something by which the divine nature can be understood, but this understanding is fragmentary, never exhaustive, and it must be negated as the prelude to a fuller apprehension. The initial conceptual orientation toward the divine Reality needs to be both negated and sublimated: negated as a mental conception, while the positive content of the conception is sublimated through spiritual intuition. Only then can the two-dimensional 'portrait' defined by reason be given its third dimension — that of depth. But this third dimension emerges only once the insufficiency of the rational depiction is understood, hence the need for that cognitive shock which paradox produces: a shock intended to expose the pretension of reason in its effort to disclose the nature of the Absolute, the transcendent source of reason's own activity.

One sees in the Imam's discourses a repeated combination of affirmation and denial that mirrors the negation and affirmation found in the first testimony of Islam: la ilaha, 'no god', as the negation (nafy); then illa'Llah, 'except God', as the affirmation (ithbat). This helps one to see that the negation of attributes which the Imam identifies as the perfection of ikhlas is in fact an affirmation of the infinitude of the divine Reality. Each attribute is to be 'negated', but only insofar as it is conceived by the mind. This negation is necessary because, at the mental level, each attribute excludes other attributes, and also excludes the very object of attribution — the divine Essence — such exclusion following inescapably from the conceptual possibility of distinguishing between the attribute and the object of attribution, that is, between the divine quality and the divine Essence. For any conception to take place in the mind, defined ideas are necessary, and where there are definitions there are limits, and thus exclusions. The reality of the attribute, however, is unlimited, by virtue of its absolute identity with the Essence. The attribute that has been 'negated' is paradoxically affirmed — not in itself, but inasmuch as it is nothing but God: illa'Llah. One can therefore place each negated attribute in the position of the nafy of the first testimony and arrive at such formulations as la rahmana illa'Llah, 'There is no All-Compassionate except God'; la rahima illa'Llah, 'No All-Merciful except God', and so on. The apparent negation of the attributes becomes, in fact, an affirmation of their true identity, stripped of any restrictive association with the poverty of mental conceptions. The attributes are not superadded to the Essence but are absolutely one with it, differentiable from it only conceptually, not ontologically. The Essence indeed comprises all the attributes mentioned — greatness, subtlety, and the rest — but it is also infinitely more than these attributes; and, for their part, the attributes are infinite only within and as the Essence. The Imam's apophasis here can be read as a commentary on the Qur'anic refrain, 'Glorified be God above what they describe' (23: 91). The infinitude of God strictly requires, on the human plane, the negation of all that the mind can conceive through reason — though not of all that the heart can 'see' through spiritual vision. This very negation at the plane of reason calls out to something deeper, and that deeper dimension can only be understood in the light of spiritual praxis, which is always assumed as the reflective, devotional, or contemplative context for the hearers of the Imam's sermons and discourses. The heart — symbolic seat of the contemplative intelligence — calls out to be 'polished', and this is precisely what the 'remembrance of God' accomplishes; this dhikr Allah is, according to the Imam, the 'most excellent form of worship', as we shall see in Chapter 3 of this book. Yet even at this level of praxis there is a further paradox: the 'reality' of the remembrance is not attained until 'you forget yourself in your remembrance'.

It is not just the rational mind and the determinate conceptions proportioned to it that are to be negated or 'deconstructed', but the individual soul itself as the locus of all cognition, rational or supra-rational. This final denouement, or fana', the ultimate form of apophasis, will be discussed further in Chapter 3.

These considerations have been offered in order to show the extent to which the spirit of the intellect, in Ali (ع)'s perspective, transcends the rational faculty. The intellect must be seen, first and foremost, as a spiritual faculty; and the spirit, in turn, must be appreciated as the source of the intellect, as well as the source of life. Without the intellect, the spirit is reduced to unconscious existence; conversely, without the spirit, the intellect tends toward lifeless thought. These aspects of the spirit are clearly expressed in the first sermon of the Nahj, which can be read as a commentary on the Qur'anic verses describing the creation of man in terms of God's 'breathing' of His spirit into him: 'He began the creation of man with clay ... He fashioned him and breathed into him of His spirit' (⟪ar:ثُمَّ سَوَّىٰهُ وَنَفَخَ فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِهِ⟫, 32: 7–9).

The following lines from the first sermon constitute one of the many passages in which Ali (ع) comments on this infusion of the divine spirit into man:

ثُمَّ جَمَعَ سُبْحَانَهُ مِنْ حَزْنِ الاْرْضِ وَسَهْلِهَا، وَعَذْبِهَا وَسَبَخِهَا۔ تُرْبَةً سَنَّهَا بالمَاءِ حَتَّى خَلَصَتْ۔ وَلاَطَهَا بِالبَلَّةِ حَتَّى لَزَبَتْ۔ فَجَبَلَ مِنْها صُورَةً ذَاتَ أَحْنَاءَ وَوُصُولَ، وَأَعْضَاءَ وَفُصُول۔ أَجْمَدَهَا حَتَّى اسْتَمْسَكَتْ، وَأَصْلَدَهَا حَتَّى صَلْصَلَتْ۔ لِوَقْت مَعْدُود، وَأجَل مَعْلُوم۔ ثُمَّ نَفَخَ فِيها مِنْ رُوحِهِ فَمَثُلَتْ إِنْساناً ذَا أَذْهَانُ يُجيلُهَا، وَفِكَر يَتَصَرَّفُ بِهَا، وَجَوَارِحَ يَخْتَدِمُهَا، وَأَدَوَات يُقَلِّبُهَا، وَمَعْرِفَة يَفْرُقُ بِهَا بَيْنَ الحَقِّ وَالبَاطِلِ۔


Then He — glorified be He! — gathered up a quantity of dust, from some hard earth and some soft, some sweet earth and some salty, and poured water onto it until it was pure, then kneaded moisture into it until it stuck together; then He fashioned from this a form with curves and joints, limbs and parts. He compacted it until it held firmly together, solidified it until it rang like sounding clay, assigning to it a measured duration and a known end. Then He breathed into it of His spirit, and it rose up as a human being, endowed with intellectual powers for reflection, thoughts by which to conduct himself, limbs to put to service, instruments at his disposal, and knowledge with which to discern the true from the false ...

<!-- page 46 -->

The body had been fashioned entire — its eyes, its ears, the organ of thought all in place — and yet it could neither hear nor see nor reflect until God breathed His spirit into it; only then did it stand up as a human being. It is this in-spiration — literally, the in-breathing of God's spirit — that produces consciousness and life. Death, by contrast, is ex-piration: the departure of the spirit. Life without aspiration — without the intention to be true to one's spirit — is a kind of death. Through the spirit, inanimate and unconscious matter is brought to life and to awareness of itself: it not only generates knowledge but also self-knowledge. This intimate intertwining between the gift of life and the capacity to know — existence that is conscious, and knowledge that is existential — is one of the foundations of any truly spiritual perspective. The question is not one of setting spirit against body, but of recognizing that the infusion of the spirit into the body is precisely what brings the body to life. It is for this reason that knowledge vivifies, in the very measure that it is spiritual: "O ye who believe, respond to God and His Messenger when they call you to that which vivifies you" (8:24). As we have seen, the Imam counts one of the most important aspects of this divine call as being to "remind them of the buried treasures of the intellect."

Existentiation is the bestowal of the spirit, which is consciousness; hence the 'aql, or intellect — the principle of articulation for the spirit — is not merely a mental faculty but is itself constitutive of our true life. The intellect is that of which the mental faculty is one formal expression, one mode of its operation, but by no means the only one. As the Imam's sayings below will amply demonstrate, the 'aql is multi-faceted, comprising aspects that in our own time are rarely associated with intelligence. Being true to one's intellect — to the treasures buried deep within it, and not merely to the rational functions operative on its surface — is tantamount to being spiritual. For Ali (ع), the true intellectual (al-'aqil, ⟪ar:العاقل⟫) is one who not only thinks correctly but also acts ethically, and, at the deepest level, one who seeks to realize the ultimate Reality. The intellectual is defined as one who "puts all things in their proper place" (⟪ar:العاقل من وضع شئی حق محلہ⟫) — one who aligns his acts with the intentions of his Creator. As will be seen in Chapter 2, this is also the very definition of justice (⟪ar:عدل⟫); only the true intellectual can therefore be fully just (⟪ar:عادل⟫), for only one who sees things as they truly are is able to put them in their right place. Thought, action, and realization are all the concern of the true intellectual, the 'aqil. Correct thinking, impeccable virtue, and authentic being are inextricably bound up with the intellect in the spiritual ethos considered here. These aspects of the ethos come into sharper focus in the light of the Imam's discourse to Kumayl, to which we now turn.

### The Discourse To Kumayl

This discourse has been the subject of numerous commentaries over the centuries. Its importance lies, among other things, in its deeply esoteric character. The Imam mysteriously takes his disciple Kumayl by the hand, leads him outside Kufa to a graveyard, and when the city lies behind them draws a deep breath and imparts the following private counsel.

يَا كُمَيْل بْن زِيَادٍ، إِنَّ هذهِ الْقُلُوبَ أَوْعِيَةٌ فَخَيْرُهَا أَوْعَاهَا فَاحْفَظْ عَنِّي مَا أَقُولُ لَكَ۔ النَّاسُ ثَلاَثَةٌ- فَعَالِمٌ رَبَّانِيٌّ وَمُتَعَلِّمٌ عَلَى سَبِيلِ نَجَاةٍ، وَهَمَجٌ رَعَاعٌ أَتْبَاعُ كُلِّ نَاعِقٍ يَمِيلُونَ مَعَ كُلِّ رِيحٍ، لَمْ يَسْتَضِيئُوا بِنُورِ الْعِلْمِ، وَلَمْ يَلْجَؤُوا إِلَى رُكْنٍ وَثِيقٍ

O Kumayl ibn Ziyad, truly these hearts are vessels — containers for knowledge and wisdom — and the best of them are those that hold the most. So retain from me what I say to you. People are of three kinds: a lordly knower ('alim rabbani); one who seeks knowledge (muta'allim) for the sake of deliverance; and the common folk (hamaj ra'a') — a word that names the moth hurling itself at any flame — who follow just anyone, swaying with every wind, neither desiring to be illumined by the light of knowledge nor seeking refuge in any firm support.

<!-- page 47 -->

O Kumayl, knowledge is better than wealth: knowledge guards you, while you must guard your wealth. Wealth diminishes as it is spent, while knowledge only grows as it is shared, replenishing itself the more it is given away. The fruits of wealth vanish when the wealth itself is gone — the friends bought with money, the comforts it purchased, all fall away with it — while no one can take a man's knowledge from him. O Kumayl ibn Ziyad, the wise apprehension of knowledge (ma'rifat al-'ilm) is a religion by which God is worshipped — for din, religion, is kin to dayn, a debt, and it is by acquiring knowledge that a man repays the debt his own intellect has incurred to its Maker. Through it he acquires obedience in his life and a good name after his death. Knowledge is a judge, while wealth is judged: knowledge is active and wealth passive, for the one who knows takes command of what he owns, his knowledge directing him in how to handle it, whereas wealth is forever something ruled upon by its possessor.

O Kumayl ibn Ziyad, those who hoard wealth perish even while they live, but the knowers endure for as long as time subsists; their material forms are absent, but their images remain present in the hearts of men. Ah, what abundant knowledge is here — and he pointed to his breast — would that I could find someone fit to bear it. Yes, I found one who was quick to understand, but he could not be trusted with it; he exploited the tools of religion for worldly gain, empowering himself with the graces of God against His servants and with His proofs against His friends. When the vessel of the heart is corrupt, knowledge — pure and precious as it is — becomes a weapon turned against the very friends of God. Then there was another, obedient to the bearers of truth but lacking heartfelt insight, in whom, at the first appearance of obscurity, doubt was kindled in the heart. So alas, neither this one nor that — nor one who is greedy for pleasure and submissive to passion, nor one obsessed with acquisition and accumulation. None of them is a guardian of religion in any respect. They are no better than grazing cattle. Thus does knowledge die with the death of its bearers, when its trust falls to the worthless and the self-serving.

But indeed, O my God, the earth will never be empty of one who establishes the proof of God, whether openly and in public or fearfully and in obscurity, lest God's proofs and elucidations come to naught. How many are such as these, and where are they? By God, they may be fewest in number, but with God they are greatest in rank — for authority here is not the possession of the many, and truth is not settled by a count of those who hold it. The guardians of the proof are few, and it is they who stand highest in the sight of God. Through them God preserves His proofs and elucidations, so that they may entrust them to their peers and sow them in the hearts of those who resemble them. Through them, knowledge penetrates the reality of insight; they lay hold of the very spirit of certainty; they make easy what the extravagant find harsh, and they befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged; with their bodies they keep company with the world, while their spirits are bound to the transcendent realm. They are the vicegerents of God upon His earth, the summoners to His religion. Ah, how I long to see them! Go now, Kumayl, as you will.

<!-- page 48 -->

For our purposes here it suffices to reflect upon the four sentences of the penultimate paragraph, in which we are given the description of those who bear the title of lordly knower ('alim rabbani): fewest in number yet greatest in the sight of God, through whom His religion is sustained. His religion — that is, the divine reality of religion, its pure and immutable essence, perfect and unchanging — as distinct from religion in the ordinary sense, whose formal institutions are subject to the vicissitudes of human history. From Adam to the Seal of the Prophets those outward institutions have shifted with the conditions of humanity, but the essence, the message, and the purpose have never changed: the salvation of the soul through willing submission to God, as the Quran declares, ⟪ar:إِنَّ ٱلدِّينَ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلْإِسْلَمُ⟫ — "Truly, religion in the sight of God is submission" (3:19). It is by means of the knowledge borne by the true knowers that religion remains true to itself, and only through such sages that religion truly lives in the world.

"Through them, knowledge penetrates the reality of insight." The Imam does not say that their knowledge penetrates it, but that through them knowledge penetrates the reality of insight. Knowledge, as we have noted, can remain merely abstract, far removed from the reality of insight; there is the outward, surface knowledge of a thing — its zahir — and the inner, hidden knowledge of the same thing — its batin. Yet knowledge contains a potential that calls out to be realized; it needs to be enlivened so as to penetrate to something deeper than the words and concepts that articulate its outward form, deeper than the meaning lying ready on the surface. A man may have surface knowledge of a Quranic verse, of a saying, of why he prays and fasts and how many cycles each prayer requires; but when he presses past that surface — asking what the bowing and the prostration truly represent — a world of knowledge opens that is wholly unlike the bare information he began with. Degrees of knowledge are plainly implied here, along with a warning against all literalism and superficiality, against the facile equation of factual information with actual knowledge, let alone with wisdom. The shift of consciousness required for this penetration is conveyed graphically by the Imam's gesture toward his breast: for it is not the chest but the heart hidden behind it that holds knowledge. The chest is the outward form, the zahir that conceals the batin within, and to reach the heart one must first penetrate the chest.

The word chosen to translate hajama is "penetrate," but the verb's primary sense is to assault, to take by storm, to charge. In the warfare of that age, battle lines were drawn as a right wing, a left wing, and a center — the heart of the army (qalb al-'askar) — where the king or commander stood. The aim of the assault was to break through the wings and reach that heart, for once the king was taken the battle was won and the victor took command of all the resources of the defeated: the kingdom, its treasury, its army, its people. The chessboard preserves the same principle. The word thus reveals that there is within conceptual knowledge a latent force — one might almost call it an explosive power — deriving from the reality of knowledge, its haqiqa or spiritual essence. It is this that calls out to be made real through tahaqquq, realization in depth rather than mere surface verification. The striving by which one storms the outer wall of words to reach the heart of their meaning is tahqiq, investigation. This haqiqa can be indicated, but never captured, by the words and concepts that make up its formal, communicable aspect, just as the chest can only be opened to reach the heart. By means of this inner force — which its outer conceptual form at once conceals and evokes — knowledge is able to effect a shift of consciousness from one degree of understanding to another. A man begins to see the very realities of this world differently from those who rest in the surface play of cause and effect, so that the same act that is a burden to one is a delight to another. An indirect, mental conception of reality thus paves the way for a penetrating, existential perception of it. This is the difference between mere sight and true insight: sight reaches only the outward and superficial, whereas the reality of insight opens the intelligence to reality as such. The same idea is expressed in a saying of Ali (ع) on the true understanding that plumbs and thereby genuinely knows the buried treasures of knowledge: "He who understands knows the depth of knowledge (man fahima 'alima ghawr al-'ilm)." And the effort that true knowledge demands, as against the mere accumulation of facts, is likewise indicated: "Through learning and assimilating knowledge, until it becomes deeply rooted in one's consciousness, is knowledge attained (bi'l-ta'allum yunalu'l-'ilm)."

<!-- page 49 -->

The urgency of realizing this immanent power within knowledge — of moving from the abstract to the concrete, the potential to the actual, the static to the dynamic, the formal to the essential — is powerfully evoked by hajama, a word inseparable from battle. It warns us against mistaking a first understanding of an idea for its full comprehension, and against mistaking full intellectual comprehension for dynamic interiorization in the spirit. For when knowledge at last storms the surface of religion and takes the heart, a transformation begins from within: the heart, now illumined by the realities it has seized, takes command of the limbs and actions — the soul's own resources — exactly as the victor takes command of the kingdom he has won. This interiorization is a process of infinite unfolding within, not the mere registering of some fact from without. It requires the effective integration of knowledge within being; knowledge from without must be transformed into being from within, woven into the deepest texture of one's existence. Knowledge therefore demands not merely the attention of the mind but the total orientation of the soul.

In this ethos, then, the effective integration (tawhid) of knowledge within being is unattainable without the participation of the whole soul; and this in turn requires the elimination of all the vices that flow from egocentricity. Thus the 'aqil, the true intellectual, is described by the Imam as one constantly engaged in struggle against his own soul — against the lower inclinations, the passionate whims, and the distracting caprices of the ego. All these vices, far from being confined to the moral plane, encroach upon the domain of knowledge, and this is understood in the very measure that knowledge is grasped as a concrete vision of things rather than a merely notional or conceptual apprehension. The vices are then seen as so many clouds obscuring the vision proper to the reality of insight. According to the Quran, evil actions become a rust that covers over the heart: "Nay, their hearts have become rusted by that which they have done" (83:14). The 'aqil is therefore engaged in a constant inner struggle against the tendencies that not only sully the soul but blind the heart. As a virtuous man he opposes vice on the moral plane because it is contrary to virtue; but vice is opposed for a further reason, situated on the higher plane of spiritual reality — it contradicts the nature of the Real, it obscures the reality of insight, blinding the eye of the heart, which alone can see God.

This is not a question of denying the validity of the moral perspective; the stress falls rather upon the deeper motivation that propels the 'aqil toward virtue, a motivation that transcends the moral plane while encompassing it. Morality emerges here as fruit or effect, and spirituality as seed or cause. By drawing attention to the intellectual dimension of the 'aqil's motive for shedding the vices, the Imam shows more clearly how immorality is rooted in ignorance; and the uprooting of that ignorance, for the true intellectual, is tantamount to dissolving the very substance of the vices. This uprooting demands great effort — a relentless battle within, indeed the most formidable of all battles: al-jihad al-akbar, the greatest struggle, as the Prophet defined it.

One should take careful note of the following sayings of Ali (ع) on the importance of this inner struggle:

"Struggling against the soul through knowledge — such is the mark of the intellect."


"The strongest of people are those who are strongest against their own souls."

"Truly, one who fights his own soul in obedience to God, and does not sin against Him, has the rank of the righteous martyr in the sight of God."

"The ultimate battle is that of a man against his own soul."

"He who knows his soul fights it."

"No jihad is more excellent than the jihad of the soul."

In another saying the Imam clarifies what the Prophet meant by defining the greatest jihad as that of the soul (al-nafs). It is not so much a matter of fighting against the soul as of fighting for the soul itself. This emerges from the metaphor the Imam uses to describe the struggle: al-'aql, the intellect, leads the forces of al-Rahman, the Compassionate; al-hawa — whim, caprice, desire — commands the forces of al-shaytan, the devil; and al-nafs, the soul, wavers between them, drawn by both (mutajadhiba baynahuma), falling into the domain of whichever of the two prevails.

These sayings make clear that, for the true intellectual, the soul in its entirety must be "conquered" and brought over to the side of the intellect. The goal is not to destroy the lower soul but to free it from the destructive grip of its own caprice — its egoistic whims, its subjectivism, its individualism — all of which are gathered up in the word hawa. The Prophet, we are told in the Qur'an, does not speak from hawa; rather: "It is naught but a revelation revealed" (53: 3–4). The divine objectivity of revelation is here set against the human subjectivity of passional whim. The Imam's sayings bring an analogous polarity into focus in the context of the inner holy war. The objectivity of the intellect must prevail over the subjectivity of individual caprice and egotistic desire. It is the intellect that represents — in the sense of "makes present" — the divine power of al-Rahman, and that wages war against the forces of al-shaytan, which are represented by al-hawa.

The word hawa appears again in the Qur'an in the sense of the desires that arise from the ego, through which the ego sets itself up as a kind of false god: "Hast thou seen him who maketh his desire his god?" (25: 43; almost identical at 45: 23). This is the subtle or hidden idolatry (al-shirk al-khafi) that the Prophet described as being more concealed than a black ant crawling on a dark stone in a moonless night. To uproot this idolatry of the ego, something higher than the ego must be brought to bear, since it is impossible for the ego to be mastered by anything that belongs to the ego itself. That higher power is the divine Reality, access to which is opened through the intellect and the graces of faith. The following Qur'anic verse captures this complementarity between the effort to overcome the egoism of hawa and the inflow of divine grace: "As for he who fears the station of his Lord, and restraineth his soul from whim (hawa), verily, Paradise will be his abode" (79: 40–41).

The soul's fundamental energy is not to be destroyed but converted and redirected — away from the transient objects of individualistic desire, away from the impulses of al-shaytan, and towards the one true object expressed by al-Rahman. This "conquest" of the soul involves far more than arriving at some mental conception. It is not only the mind, the will and the character that must be aligned with the truth the intellect perceives — the soul's very bedrock orientation must be integrated into that truth, if one is to perceive it in all its dimensions and ramifications across the different planes of being.

It is important to stress here that, for the Imam, the need to win over the soul in its totality follows naturally from his understanding of tawhid, the most fundamental principle of Islam. One must not lose sight of the fact that tawhid is a verbal noun. It is often translated simply as "oneness" or "unity," but it literally means "affirming one" — affirming and believing in the oneness of God, which is its outward, theological sense — or "realizing one" — realizing that there is but one reality, which is its inner, spiritual sense. In the Imam's perspective, therefore, the absolutely Real is perceived not only as being one but as making one, as integrating. The Real is not only unique; it is also unifying. Tawhid then comes to mean not merely affirming the oneness of God, but being one in order to reflect the One perfectly — to know God is to be "integrated." One notices here a connection with the etymology of the word 'aql, whose primary meaning is "to bind." Yet whereas the later mystical tradition tends to stress the limitative aspect of that word — the intellect being that which defines and encloses meaning, as opposed to the heart which intuits and discloses spiritual truth — here the intellect is understood as that which "binds together" the disparate elements of the soul into an organic unity. This principle is expressed in the first of the following series of the Imam's sayings, each of which merits deep reflection:

"He who knows God integrates himself (man 'arafa'Llah tawahhad)."

"He who knows his soul disengages himself (man 'arafa nafsahu tajarrad)."

"He who knows people isolates himself (man 'arafa'l-nas tafarrad)."

"He who knows the world withholds himself (man 'arafa'l-dunya tazahhad)."

Elsewhere, the Imam refers to this total adherence to the Real as "sincerity," sidq or ikhlas — terms that carry far more weight than simple truthfulness, as the very root of the word ikhlas already suggests. The verb khalusa means to be utterly pure, true to one's essence, unadulterated by any extraneous substance. This dimension of the meaning is well conveyed in the following saying of the Imam: "Make purely for God (akhlis li'Llah) your action and your knowledge, your love and your hatred, your taking and your leaving, your speech and your silence."

To be totally sincere, then, means to make oneself "purely" for God — to have no "stain" of impurity, understood here as anything other than God, in one's devotion, action, intention, or even sentiment. One must love and hate only in relation to one's bond with God. Of all the tendencies that insinuate themselves into one's motivations and intentions, the most pernicious is egotism. Ikhlas, "pure sincerity," involves the active effort to be, to think, and to act purely for God — that is, with selflessness — which is why the Imam places such emphasis on the holy war within the soul as the "ultimate battle." One who prevails in this battle attains the rank of the "righteous martyr." The victor here is the martyr because he has sacrificed his lower life for the sake of God, with whom true life resides — the life that is witnessed by the intellect, the only element within human consciousness capable of looking upon the soul objectively, as "the other."

To repeat: "struggling against the soul through knowledge — such is the mark of the intellect." The intellectual engaged in this battle is also one who bears "witness" (al-shahid, meaning both martyr and witness, as in the original Greek sense of "martyr"). He bears witness to God through the intellect's sacrifice of whatever opposes the truth of God, and the chief opponent is his own egotism, his own "false life." To possess perfect ikhlas, then, is to be rid of all hypocritical self-preoccupation and to replace it with sincere theocentricity — a mode of being centred upon God rather than upon oneself. Thus, ikhlas means being for God, or belonging to God, in the sense indicated by the Qur'anic verse: "Say: Truly my worship and my sacrifice, and my living and my dying, are for God, Lord of the universes" (6: 162).

The fact that worship is the first thing mentioned in this verse carries particular significance for anyone seeking to understand the nature of Ali (ع)'s spiritual ethos. What must be underlined in presenting this ethos is the indispensable role of worship and devotion. In our second chapter, this role will become evident within the context of the quest for the fundamental virtue of justice. The ultimate fruits of worship and devotion — of which the quintessence is the remembrance of God — will be the subject of Chapter 3. One of the defining features of the Imam's spiritual ethos is precisely the emphasis he places on prayer, understood in its widest sense: all those means, from formal worship and supplication to meditation and reflection, remembrance and contemplation, by which the individual is oriented and attuned to the Real, al-Haqq.

Worship is therefore an integral dimension of ikhlas, precisely because it addresses and sustains a fundamental need of the human soul. That within the soul which yearns to worship, and is nourished only by worship, must be given its due. In a spiritual context, when worship is marginalized, sincerity is compromised — one is no longer being sincere with God, no longer being "just" to God, no longer rendering God His due. The Imam's spiritual logic then dictates that if one is not just with God, one cannot be just to His creatures. "Whoso establishes well-being between himself and God," he says, "God establishes well-being between him and mankind."

One might also cite the Qur'anic verse: "Worship thy Lord until certainty cometh to thee" (15: 99). The quest for knowledge, then, demands total dedication if it is to yield its inner riches, and this dedication includes the quasi-alchemical transformation of consciousness wrought by total concentration on the divine Reality — prayer in one of its deepest modes. In this form of prayer, the intensity of "dedication" is such that nothing else finds room in one's consciousness beside the object of that dedication — the "remembrance" (dhikr) of God strictly implying the forgetting of everything else, most of all oneself. As mentioned above, the spiritual reality of the dhikr can only be reached when "you forget your soul in your remembrance." Concentration upon the divine Reality as the supreme object of knowledge — rendered present through the Name or Names of God — is thus a mode of prayer, and one that requires, to be efficacious, both presence of mind and a genuine engagement of the will. Absent-minded, mechanical prayer is far from sufficient. In this connection, the Imam says: "He who prays without exertion is like one who shoots [arrows] without a bow." Equally, there is nothing passive or effortless about the process of acquiring knowledge: "Knowledge calls out for action; if it is answered [it is of avail], otherwise it departs."

The same idea is expressed more forcefully in this saying of the Imam: "When you hear knowledge [being imparted] cleave to it, do not mix it up with jest, lest hearts spew it forth." Knowledge that is not taken seriously is of no avail; it remains "undigested" and resists assimilation by the "heart" — that innermost faculty which has access to the "reality of insight," and which alone can "see" God. For it is the heart that "sees" Allah — not by eyesight, not by ⟪ar:بصارۃ⟫, but through the spiritual insight, the ⟪ar:بصیرۃ⟫, generated by the true principles of faith, the ⟪ar:حقائق الایمان⟫. Indeed, it is not the individual who "assimilates" knowledge to himself; it is knowledge — or the reality of being expressed by true knowledge — that assimilates the individual to itself: "Acquire knowledge, and [true] life will acquire you." The relationship between life and consciousness, so central to the intrinsic function of the spirit as discussed earlier, is again at the heart of this maxim. At first, the individual seeks knowledge and cultivates consciousness; then, life "seeks" the individual as being realizes itself more fully within him. In other words, the individual first makes an active effort to "integrate" knowledge within his being; but at a certain point, that activity is transformed into passive receptivity — being, "true life," integrates the individual within itself. To return to the Imam's guidance to Kumayl: "Through them knowledge penetrates the reality of insight." It is not the knowers who penetrate; it is knowledge that penetrates to the reality of insight, doing so through them, and precisely in the measure of their effacement within knowledge — an effacement that is the shadow cast by their genuine integration within the life that "acquires" them.

"They rejoice in their intimacy with the spirit of certainty."

The next sentence in the discourse to Kumayl is of particular importance in helping us grasp the sheer happiness that flows from the "life of the spirit." In the Arabic expression wa basharu ruh al-yaqin, the primary meaning of the word bashara is to have intimate experience of a thing, to come into direct physical contact with it. This form of the verb also carries a connotation of joy; "rejoice in intimate contact" therefore better captures something of what this single word conveys. To touch the "spirit of certainty" is to come into contact not with some cold logical fact, the conclusion of a purely mental process; rather, this contact with the spirit of certainty means a deep engagement with the object of certainty, with that of which one is certain — and ultimately that object is nothing less than the divine itself. Since this reality is infinitely more beatific than any conceivable paradisal bliss — according to the Qur'an, the divine ridwan (beatitude, contentment) is "greater" than Paradise (9: 72) — it cannot but bestow joy upon the soul that genuinely opens itself to it. In the words of Ali (ع): "Learning and reflecting upon knowledge is the delight of the knowers."

This is what one might call an "epistemology of joy," for "the most joyous of mankind is the intellectual (al-aqil)." This joyous epistemology — which stands in direct contradiction to modern scientistic conceptions of knowledge as dry, cold, and abstract — deserves careful attention, as it reveals a crucial dimension of the spiritual knowledge proper to the Imam's perspective and, indeed, to all spiritual traditions worthy of the name. Coming into contact with truths should impart happiness, and it does so in proportion to how fundamental those truths are to one's own being or to Being as such. The two modes of being are ultimately identical, given the uniqueness — the "one-and-onliness" — of Being. That which has no second, no other, cannot enter the category of number; being is indivisible. The being of the individual therefore cannot ultimately differ from Being as such, however great the existential disparity — or rather, incommensurability — between the individual and God. True knowledge is knowledge of Being, and so it is necessarily a kind of self-discovery, even though knowledge of the Absolute is impossible without absolute self-abandonment. The highest discovery calls for the deepest effacement.

In the initial stages of this process, however, true knowledge acquired by the intellectual is a kind of self-discovery that carries the happiness of a homecoming — a return from the exile of ignorance. This follows because "the most intellectual of people" is "the one who is closest to God,"112 and "the one who is most alive." To be close to God is to be fully alive, and this true life gives profound joy — the joie de vivre, the sheer happiness of living. This is a joy proper to existence as such and, as will be seen below, it is independent of particular experiences that may or may not be pleasurable.

Here again we see the vital relationship between life and consciousness — between the fruits of the "in-spiration" of the spirit of God on the one hand, and the bond between this life of spiritual consciousness and joy on the other. Those who are faithful to the spirit of God within them truly possess joy. It is also by reason of this intimate and unshakeable certainty of the divine goodness and compassion that the true faqih — understood not so much as "the jurist" but as "one who truly understands" — is defined by the Imam as "he who does not make people despair of the mercy of God, and does not make them lose hope in the gracious spirit of God (rawh Allah)." The sage is so immersed in the joyous certainty of God's mercy that there is simply no room for despair. To despair of this mercy is a sign of disbelief: "...and who despaireth of the mercy of his Lord except those who stray in error?" (15: 56). The divine assurances of mercy in the Qur'an — such as "My mercy encompasseth all things" (7: 156) and "thy Lord has prescribed for Himself mercy" (6: 54) — come fully alive in souls that possess profound rather than merely notional knowledge of the beatific nature of the divine Reality. These are the ones who truly "understand," the true fuqaha'.


<!-- page 55 -->
This opening to joy through intimate knowledge of God's mercy is inseparable from beauty, the perception of which is another unexpected companion of the intellect in the Imam's perspective. The appreciation of beauty is not merely a matter of subjective aesthetics; it is an objective dimension of the intellect's proper function. Beauty also takes us to the heart of ethics. The Arabic ihsan is often translated simply as 'virtue' or 'excellence', but one of its core literal meanings is 'making beautiful'—husn means 'beauty'. Virtue, ihsan, can therefore be understood as that which 'makes the soul beautiful'. Seen in this light, the relationship between intellect, ethics, and aesthetics comes into clearer focus: 'The excellence of the intellect,' says Ali (ع), 'is in the beauty of things outward and inward (jamal al-zawahir wa'l-bawatin).' The outward beauty of forms and actions must be matched by inward beauty—beauty of soul, or virtue in its most integral and penetrating sense. All of this follows naturally from two sayings attributed to the Prophet: 'Truly God is beautiful and He loves beauty,' and: 'Assume the character traits of God.'

Cultivating these 'beautiful traits' within oneself makes one lovable to God, since He 'loves beauty'. This process of making oneself lovable to God is inseparable from emulating the virtues brought to perfection in the soul of the Prophet. 'Say: O ye who believe, if ye love God, follow me [the Prophet]; God will love you' (3: 31). This drawing of God's love through making oneself beautiful in the fullest sense is touched upon in another saying of the Imam: 'The foundation of the intellect is the assimilation of love (awwal al-'aql al-tawaddud).' Rather than referring simply to love as such (wudd/mawadda), the Imam deliberately uses the form tawaddud, pointing to the need to make oneself lovable to God—to integrate the principle of love within oneself and to express outwardly what is experienced inwardly. Love is thus understood as both outward radiance and inner attraction.

To 'follow' the Prophet and thereby open oneself to the divine influx of love means, among other things, cultivating the prophetic virtues. These virtues are prophetic not in the sense that they belong exclusively to the Prophet, but because he is understood to have realized virtue in its fullest plenitude and is therefore the exemplar, par excellence, of virtue as such. It is no surprise, then, to find that beauty and love are not the only qualities that articulate the intellect's receptivity to the divine Reality; kindness and contentment,120 courtesy,121 generosity and modesty,122 and wise forbearance (hilm) all belong to this constellation as well. None of these moral qualities can be absent from the soul of the true intellectual. There can be no wisdom or intellect in the fullest sense when the soul lacks them. It is precisely the presence of these virtues that allows the intellect to develop the dimensions of depth and breadth without which it remains confined to the flat, horizontal plane of its own operations—confined, that is, to the activity of reason alone, which, until touched by faith and deepened by grace, remains unaware of what lies beneath the surface of its own field, oblivious of the 'hidden treasures' of the intellect, blind to the need to 'integrate' the whole soul within the mould of that 'integral', or 'unifying', virtue which bestows integrity upon intelligence. Such integration—such 'making one'—is absolutely fundamental to tawhid, now grasped as a dynamic process of spiritual unification demanded by the very idea of God's oneness. Truth must inspire the soul; but equally, virtue must 'aerate' the intelligence, providing the leaven that allows it to expand to its full dimensions.

Sinful and immoral qualities are understood in this perspective not only as vices but as so many blind spots and intellectual dysfunctions. The heart is 'diseased' by vice; its inner eye is blinded and rendered unable to 'see' the Real. As the Qur'an says, 'In their hearts is a disease and God increased them in disease' (2: 10), and 'It is not the sights that are blind, but blind are the hearts' (22: 46).

As noted above, the vices are all rooted in egotism, which is in turn often described simply as 'caprice' (hawa). In one saying, the Imam presents the conquest of caprice as tantamount to salvation: 'He attains deliverance whose intellect dominates his caprice.' Deliverance—falah, a word that encompasses both salvation and beatitude—is thus to some degree accessible here and now; it is not a grace reserved exclusively for the afterlife. This deliverance involves tasting the joy that emanates from certainty, from intimate contact with the spirit of that certainty. As for the means of cultivating receptivity to this spirit of certainty, what is required—over and above obedience to the rules and regulations of the revealed Law—is that combination of purity of intention and sincerity of devotion expressed by the word ikhlas. Without this sincerity or purity, compliance with formal rules is nothing but hypocrisy (nifaq) and pretension (riya), which the Imam defines as the gravest of all sins: shirk, the attribution of partners to God. 'Know that the slightest pretension (riya) is polytheism.' This sentence can be read as a commentary on one of the Qur'an's most important condemnations of hypocrisy—verses that describe those who deny the essence of the faith even while outwardly conforming to it:

Hast thou seen the one who belieth religion? He is the one who repelleth the orphan and urgeth not the feeding of the poor. So woe be to those who pray, those who are heedless of their prayer, who want only to be seen, and withhold small kindnesses (107: 1-7).

This short sura makes clear that formal acts of devotion are worthless unless they are integrated into a life of virtue and performed with the right intention—that is, with ikhlas. It also helps explain why the Imam's ethos is better described as 'spiritual' rather than simply 'religious': it takes the ideals of religion to their most profound application. To be spiritual in this sense is precisely to be true to one's intellect—true to the treasures buried deep within it, not merely to the rational functions operating on its surface. Without this dimension of depth, religion is empty. 'There is no religion for one who has no intellect,' says the Imam. Likewise, 'Action without knowledge is error.' What counts is the intention behind action, for 'action—all action—is dust, except what is purified [accomplished sincerely] within it.' This detachment from action as such, and the orientation instead toward an inner state of being from which good actions naturally arise, clearly reflects the Qur'anic teaching: 'It is not their [sacrificed] flesh nor their blood that reacheth God, but it is piety from you that reacheth Him' (22: 37).

'There is no religion for one who has no intellect.' One might argue that no group illustrated this principle more vividly than the Kharijites. Among those who rebelled against the Imam were numerous 'reciters' (qurra', sing. qari) of the Qur'an—people who commanded considerable religious prestige on account of their scriptural knowledge and intense worship. Their piety, however, was more apparent than real; it was, in truth, a zeal of bitterness, a caricature of genuine devotion. When his supporters spoke admiringly of the long night vigils kept by members of this group, the Imam gave a sharp reply: 'Sleeping with certainty is better than praying with doubt.' Certainty, as we have seen, brings peace and joy; thus, for one who is immersed in what the Imam calls 'the spirit of certainty', sleep is a bodily repose that deepens the happiness flowing from a good conscience and spiritual certitude—a certitude understood as a kind of permanent 'wakefulness', in the sense of being awake to higher realities. Bodily sleep, therefore, does not contradict spiritual wakefulness. As the Prophet's hadith has it, 'My eyes sleep, but my heart sleeps not.'

In relation to formal worship, the true intellectual—the 'aqil, whom the Imam defines as the one who puts each thing in its proper place (العاقل من وضع شئی حق محلہ)—obeys the divine injunction to pray. But rather than praying mechanically and assuming that the outward act alone suffices, he knows that such obedience is a necessary but not sufficient condition for becoming receptive to grace. There must be consciousness of what one is doing and why; and it is this awareness, together with virtue and inward beauty, that draws divine acceptance. 'The most worthy knowledge for you,' says the Imam, 'is that by virtue of whose presence action is accepted by God.'

'They make easy what the extravagant find harsh; they befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged.'

This sentence leads us, among other things, to the impact that recollection of death's inevitability has on the souls of the true sages, and the consequent necessity of renunciation within this world. What the worldly and extravagant find 'harsh' is, in the first place, outward adversity—difficulty, deprivation, suffering. These are circumstances that the Qur'an views as the trials that inevitably attend life in this world:

And We shall try you with something of fear and hunger and loss of property and life and fruits; but give glad tidings to the patient—those who, when struck by affliction say: 'Verily, we belong to God and verily unto Him we are ever-returning.' These are the ones upon whom there are blessings from their Lord, and mercy; and these are the ones who are rightly guided. (2: 155-157)

The wise, the rightly-guided (al-muhtadun), perceive the blessings showered upon those who are patient in the face of affliction—a patience that is itself a fruit of their knowledge of the truth: 'By the Age, truly man is in a state of loss—except those who believe and do virtuous deeds and exhort one another to truth and exhort one another to patience' (103: 1-3). These believers remain steadfast under trial, firmly rooted in their awareness of their origin and their end, and in the knowledge that 'unto Him we are ever-returning.' For this reason, they can 'make easy' life's trials and difficulties, whereas the extravagant experience them only as harsh and bitter. The ease that inheres within every difficulty is hidden from the ignorant but discerned by the wise: 'Verily, with hardship cometh ease; verily with hardship cometh ease' (94: 5-6). The wise go even further than anticipating ease as the inevitable fruit of hardship; they taste that fruit already as a seed hidden within the hardship itself, for they actually 'befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged.'

One level of meaning here can be illuminated by this saying of Ali (ع): 'Death is more intimate to me than the breast of the mother is to the suckling babe.' In the verse cited above, the mention of trials and difficulties is preceded by a reminder that those slain 'in the path of God' are not really dead—'nay, they are alive but ye perceive not.' This is not simply an affirmation of the afterlife; it also alludes to the esoteric meaning of being 'slain' for God: al-fana' fi'Llah, 'extinction in God'. The intimacy with death that the Imam speaks of can be understood in relation to this 'death' while still alive, which he also alludes to elsewhere: 'I am the death of the dead.' Because life in this world is itself a kind of death—a limitation, and therefore a privation in contrast to the infinite plenitude of the divine realms—dying to this death is tantamount to true life. 'And the life of this world is nothing but sport and play,' says the Qur'an, 'and verily, the abode of the Hereafter—that is [true] life, did they but know' (29: 64).

It is important to stress, however, that it is not the physical world as such that is to be negated. What must be overcome is worldliness—egotistic attachment to the world. Here we need to dwell a little on one of the key principles running throughout the Nahj al-balagha: the principle of zuhd, which no single English word can capture. It encompasses asceticism, austerity, abstention, detachment, 'doing without', and 'making little of'. The following passage from the Nahj articulates well the zahid's attitude toward this world:

The world is an abode for which annihilation is ordained, and for its people departure from it is decreed. It is sweet and green, and it hastens to one who seeks [it], and confounds the heart that gazes upon it. So set out from it with the best of the provision available to you, and ask not from it above what suffices and seek not from it more than what can be attained.136

What is this 'best of provision' (zad)? In many places in the Nahj it is described as taqwa—another word that resists easy translation, encompassing piety, fear of God, awe, and God-consciousness: 'I exhort you, O slaves of God, to taqwa, which is the provision,' says the Imam. This provision for the next world is attained only in proportion to one's detachment from the perishable aspects of 'the life of this world', for 'whoso desireth that [life of the world] which hasteneth away, in that [life] We hasten for him what We will...' (17: 18).

The contemptible aspect of the world is described in vivid terms by the Imam: 'By God, this world of yours is more contemptible in my eyes than a [chewed-up] bone of a pig in the hand of a leper'; 'an image of the world is the snake—soft to touch, but virulent poison within';139 the world is 'as worthless to me as that which drips from the nose of a goat.' It is the grandeur and beauty of God that renders the world insignificant and distasteful; it is only in the light of the Absolute that the pleasures and attractions of the world appear fleeting and illusory: 'The greatness of the Creator for you belittles creatures in your view.' In a similar vein, the Imam speaks of a 'brother in God': 'What made him great in my eyes was the smallness of the world in his (kana yu'azzimuhu fī 'aynī sighar al-dunya fi 'aynihi).'142

This, according to the Qur'anic use of zahid, is the real meaning of zuhd. In the sura named after Joseph (Yusuf), his brothers 'deemed him of little value' when they sold him for a small price (12: 20); they are described, in relation to him, as 'zahidin'. In the Imam's perspective, the world is likewise deemed of little value—not that it is altogether without worth, but that in relation to the Hereafter, to eternity, to ultimate reality, it is rendered all but insignificant. All created things are reduced to infinitesimal, fleeting shadows destined for annihilation: 'Everything perishes except His Face' (28: 88). Yet the world is not stripped of all value; its significance lies precisely in what it points to and cannot help but point to: 'And there is nothing that does not hymn His glory with praise' (17: 44). Every creature in existence proclaims the infinite creativity of the Creator; and to help us imaginatively grasp the mystery of this universal praise, the Qur'an tells us: 'And the birds in flight—each one knoweth its prayer and glorification' (24: 41).

It is therefore not at all contradictory for the Imam both to despise the world and to wax lyrical in his descriptions of the beauties of virgin nature—including that unsurpassable marvel, the peacock. This appears in a sermon that also describes birds in general, and touches on ants, serpents, and elephants. Notably, the sermon concludes with a galvanizing description of Paradise, setting the marvels of this world beside the delights of the next; in the light of the latter, all earthly phenomena pale into insignificance. Once the 'eye of the heart' is turned toward Paradise, 'your soul would become averse to the marvels of this world—its passions, its pleasures and its embellished scenery.'

It might seem contradictory to bring together such apparently opposing qualities as joy in certainty, discussed above, and the 'befriending' of hardship. But the latter is in fact the fruit of the former. All difficulties without exception are endured through the spiritual resources made available by faith combined with intellectual assimilation—or rather, in the measure that faith is intellectually assimilated and thereby becomes ma'rifa, or gnosis, comprising a certainty so deep and so complete that 'even if the veil be lifted' it cannot increase, according to the famous saying noted earlier. It is possible to believe, at one level of the mind, that 'there is no god but God.' The Imam, however, draws attention to what this apparently simple belief implies when it is genuinely 'sincere' or 'pure': 'I bear witness that there is no god but God alone Who has no partner—a testimony whose sincerity has been tested and whose essence has been assimilated with conviction (mu'taqadan musaffaha).'


The word *musaffa* refers to the choicest and purest part of any substance—that which constitutes its essence. The root *massa* means "to suck," the idea being that the liquid or juice of a substance or fruit contains its essence, what is "sucked" out of it. Here the Imam is pressing home the spiritual substance of the testimony, as opposed to its merely verbal affirmation or mental comprehension. He is affirming a heartfelt connection with the reality to which the testimony bears witness; the words that define the testimony are, as it were, an echo of the reality they convey, and must not be confused with that reality itself. He continues: "We cling to it always, for as long as we endure, storing it up [as protection] against the terrors that will befall us. For truly, it renders faith resolute, opens up spiritual virtue, pleases al-Rahman (the All-Compassionate), repels al-shaytan (the devil)."

Returning to "befriending that by which the ignorant are estranged," one might say that this implies not only the trials of life—which are a source of strength for the wise and of repulsion for the ignorant—but also the ineluctability of death. For the wise, death means release, the encounter with God, blissful eternity: in other words, a mode of *tawhid* whereby the soul enjoys ever greater degrees of integration within higher principles. For the ignorant, by contrast, death is perceived as a frightful disintegration, the end of their world rather than the beginning of endless life. One should also recall in this connection the prefiguration of death within life already mentioned—that is, *fana'*, "extinction," "effacement," the "perishing" of egoistic consciousness before the Absolute. The ignorant recoil in horror at the prospect of losing what they falsely take to be "their" life, not realizing that this sacrifice of limited, ego-bound self-consciousness is precisely the ground on which infinite consciousness is glimpsed or "tasted." One can only "taste" the ocean of the Real by being drowned in it.

As we shall see in Chapter 3, one could say further that the very means to this ultimate attainment is likewise "embraced" by the wise and feared by the ignorant—namely, the act of pure prayer, total concentration on the Real. Such concentration strictly precludes all worldly or egotistic preoccupations; the sheer intensity of focus on the Absolute burns away every thought of the relative, and for those who ignorantly identify with relativities, both inward and outward, this amounts to a kind of death. For the wise, on the contrary, this concentration on the Absolute—the unsullied "remembrance of God"—is a joyous contemplation of reality before which the ego is displaced and all illusions are dispelled. This leads directly to the next sentence in the passage: "With their bodies, they keep company with the world, while their spirits are tied to the transcendent realm."

This sentence becomes more concrete when read alongside another well-known expression of the Imam. He describes himself as belonging to a group whose degree of spirituality is such that "their hearts are in the Gardens [of Paradise] while their bodies are at work." Being "tied" or "attached" to that realm—encompassing the paradisal abodes right up to the divine Presence itself—means they are already "within" the transcendent realm more fully than they are within this earthly one. This helps clarify the saying quoted earlier: God keeps a person perpetually in that which his soul desires. Those whose hearts or spirits are "in" the transcendent domain are those whose consciousness is substantially, not merely conceptually, shaped by transcendent realities. Those whose consciousness is governed by worldly concerns are "in" this world in the sense of being enclosed by its formal limitations; their orientation and imagination remain trapped within a worldly framework. Their actions bear the imprint of this worldliness, whereas the actions of the wise are the fruits of their immersion in a higher reality. One might say that their outward actions in the world belie their inward transcendence of all action; they act, but are not bound by the fruits of their action. As noted earlier, they know that "action—all action—is dust, except what is purified (accomplished sincerely) within it."

At this point it is worth pausing briefly over the role played by the notion of the Hereafter within the Imam's ethical framework. As we saw above, and will see again in what follows, the Hereafter is repeatedly invoked or evoked in his discourses. Is this simply a matter of offering a heavenly carrot and threatening a hellish stick in order to "enjoin the good and forbid the evil"? Even if that answer holds at one level, there is a deeper dimension to this apparently simple use of divine warnings and promises as ethical motivation.

The point of view on ethical orientation adopted here is defined essentially by the spiritual reality not only of God but also of the Hereafter—both objectively, in itself, and subjectively, insofar as it is assimilated by the individual. It thus becomes a dynamic dimension—a process, an orientation, a reflux—within the very soul of the believer, rather than a mere belief or hope. Anticipation of the Hereafter characterizes every believer in principle; its realization is another matter entirely, and it is this that marks the soul of the *wali Allah*, the friend of God, the saint and sage. As noted earlier, the Imam declares in a famous sentence that bears witness to his total immersion in the reality of which he is certain: "Were the veil to be removed, I would not increase in certitude (*law kushifa'l-ghita', ma azdadtu yaqinan*)." The anticipation—or better, the foretaste—of the Hereafter deepens in proportion to one's virtue, piety and sanctity; it does not dwindle into a merely heuristic tool whose value is exhausted once its impact on moral action has been achieved. In this connection, the following moving passage from the *Nahj* deserves reflection. What we have here is the description of a blissful vision of the reality of Paradise, not merely a rhetorical flourish in the service of moral exhortation:

If you were to cast the eye of your heart towards what is described for you of the Garden, your soul would become averse to the marvels of this world—its passions, its pleasures and its embellished scenery. And your soul would be rapt in contemplation of the swaying trees whose roots lie hidden in dunes of musk on the banks of the rivers of the Garden, and the clusters of dazzling pearls hanging down from the branches of its trees … 149

It is of course true that the highest degree of spirituality transcends both the desire for Paradise and the fear of Hell, together with the moral conduct proportioned to them. But this means that when such higher degrees of realization are attained, the state of the soul is properly characterized as "paradisal"—as already so utterly content in the beatific presence of God that it can desire nothing more. This state of soul is called in the Qur'an *al-nafs al-mutma'inna* (89: 27), "the soul at peace in absolute certainty."

The Imam frequently echoes, implicitly if not explicitly, the Qur'anic principle that "the Hereafter is better and more lasting" (87: 17). The Hereafter is not only the "time" when divine warnings and promises reach their consummation; it is also the "space" within which all positive qualities—human, angelic, divine—are manifested in their fullness: those qualities that appear on earth as reflections of higher realities, quietly encompassing and penetrating the entire sphere of terrestrial existence. The Qur'an, perhaps more than any other scripture, offers the imagination numerous and complex descriptions of paradisal bliss. One of the key functions of this is alluded to in the Qur'an itself: "Assuredly, We purify them with a pure quality—remembrance of the Abode (of the Hereafter)" (38: 46). The very thought of Paradise is itself a purification of mind and heart, a means of turning the soul away from the ever-present temptation to seek its ultimate happiness in this world alone. Conversely, one's well-being in the here-below, where action is concerned, lies in the performance of good deeds, and such well-being is proportioned to the depth of one's belief that "Whatever good ye send before for your souls, ye will find it with God, better and more tremendous as reward" (73: 20). In other words, a sincere orientation toward the Hereafter is transformed into a perception of heavenly beatitude already in the here-below, taking the precise form of those acts and attitudes which, being good, share the very nature of the felicity to which they lead. In this light, morally good actions and noble intentions are grasped by one who sees beyond the veil of this world as vivid prefigurations of heavenly realities, not merely as ethical prerequisites for salvation. Goodness does not simply lead to a heavenly reward; it already partakes of that reward: "Is the reward of goodness (*ihsan*) aught but goodness?" (55: 60). The Imam's saying, "The fruit of good action is like its root," can be read as an echo of this idea that the end is already present in the means leading to it. Let us close with the following verse of the Qur'an, which both concludes this chapter and opens the way into the next:

And give good tidings to those who believe and perform virtuous deeds, that for them are Gardens underneath which rivers flow. Every time they are given to eat from the fruits thereof, they say: "This is what we were given to eat before." And they were given the like thereof (2: 25).

The "fruits" of the paradisal gardens are thus experienced already in the here-below, in the form of all goodness, beauty and truth, and the diverse modes of happiness flowing from them. Every such experience is a foretaste of the ultimate beatitude in Paradise. On the ethical plane, the performance of good deeds—and still more directly, the realization of intrinsic virtue, *ihsan*—is therefore not simply a prerequisite for posthumous salvation, but is already a kind of deliverance, here and now: deliverance from the imprisonment of sin on the outward plane, and from the bondage of egocentricity on the inward. This theme of salvation here and now, grounded in unshakeable certitude, is fundamental to the spirit of the Imam's teachings and will be explored further throughout the remainder of this book. In the following chapter, the cardinal virtue of justice will be considered within Ali (ع)'s perspective, rooted as it is in *ihsan*—that virtue defined by the Prophet as worshipping God as if one saw Him, a vision the Imam affirms as real and not merely hypothetical, for God is indeed "seen" by the pure of heart "through the verities of faith."


## A Sacred Conception of Justice in the Letter of Ali (ع) to Malik al-Ashtar

### Contemporary Ethics And The Ihsanī Tradition

The discussion of justice in this chapter is not simply a theoretical exercise in unpacking what Ali (ع) meant by justice in his letter to Malik al-Ashtar fourteen centuries ago. The primary aim is to situate justice in relation to the concept and the reality of the sacred. On one hand, the principle of justice possesses a sacred and spiritual dimension that contemporary discourse too often overlooks. On the other, a genuine appreciation of the sacred leads to a deeper understanding of justice's roots within the divine nature itself, and to a more acute awareness of why justice is necessary — in the world and in the soul. The basic argument of this essay is as follows: in Ali (ع)'s worldview, the ethical orientation toward justice is immeasurably deepened when it is consciously linked to the spiritual precepts of the Islamic faith. The spirit animating moral rules and ethical values is strengthened by commitment to transcendent principles — even though, or precisely because, those principles exceed the realm of action in which the rules themselves operate. In this perspective, contemplation and action are complementary rather than contradictory, and this complementarity goes to the very heart of the Islamic message of tawhid — the integrating oneness that is embodied with dazzling clarity in the life of the Imam, as it is in the life of the Prophet. This harmony between contemplation and action, being and doing, theory and practice, ideal and reality, pervades the remarkable document that is the subject of this essay.

The reflections offered here are also meant to be read against the backdrop of contemporary ethical discourse, and may be seen as a modest contribution, from an Islamic perspective, to ongoing scholarly debates about virtue and ethics. In particular, they should be read in light of one of the most serious and far-reaching critiques of modern and postmodern notions of virtue — and of justice in particular — namely that of Alasdair MacIntyre. Put simply, his critique holds that for several centuries ethical discourse in Western society has drifted far from its moorings in absolute values. He argues compellingly that debates about morality and ethics, together with their philosophical foundations, are no longer grounded in conceptions of "the good life" or of "the Good" as such. MacIntyre contends that, as a consequence, various forms of relativism, nihilism, utilitarianism, and opportunism have emerged, in practice, as the shifting bases upon which ethical values are formulated — when they are formulated at all. Practising virtue on an ad hoc basis is not virtue properly speaking; it is a series of pragmatic adjustments to changing circumstances in the absence of any principle of virtue as such. "We are looking," writes MacIntyre, "for a conception of the good which will enable us to order other goods, for a conception of the good which will enable us to extend our understanding of the purpose and content of the virtues, for a conception of the good which will enable us to understand the place of integrity and consistency in life ..."

It is precisely at this level that the insights of Ali (ع) and other sages of the Islamic tradition are especially relevant. MacIntyre's own proposed solution to the crisis — the reformulation of an Aristotelian ethics grounded in a Thomistic worldview — in fact shares a great deal with the Islamic tradition of ethical discourse (akhlaq). Beyond that point of convergence, however, a careful engagement with the Imam's legacy yields something more than a merely theoretical approach to justice, and this in two respects. First, the Imam was himself a paragon of justice — a living embodiment of this and other fundamental virtues. His life exhibits a perfect, indeed unflinching, adherence to this cardinal virtue. Although this work does not dwell extensively on the details of his biography, certain incidents and events will be mentioned where they help to illustrate the point. Second, and more important for our purposes, the discourses, letters, and sayings attributed to Ali (ع) offer a rich source of reflection on the dynamic spirituality that lies at the root of a life of virtue. They afford a glimpse into a way of life wholly governed by the sacred — one in which the desire for "the good life" emerges as the fruit of an irresistible attraction toward the Good as such, which is identical to the absolutely Real, al-Haqq.

In other words, one is ethically inspired by "the Good" in proportion to one's existential orientation toward the Real. If moral rules are grounded in ethical perspectives, and those perspectives are rooted in a conception of "the good life," then that conception of the Good is itself derived from the moral agent's basic understanding of what life means. Amyn B. Sajoo, in a recent and thoughtful work on Muslim ethics, draws a careful distinction between ethics and morals. Ethical discourse, he argues, is concerned more with conceptions of "the good," while morality pertains more specifically to rules about right and wrong. "Certainly," he continues, "the concepts overlap substantially in theory and practice; but it is well to bear in mind the more encompassing sense of reality that typifies ethics." Placing ethics within the wider framework of spiritual virtue — in the sense of ihsan, as we shall see shortly — expands that compass still further, lending both ethics and morality a dimension of existential depth and grounding them in a more organic and all-encompassing framework. Abstract questions of right and wrong come to be rooted in the more foundational principle that emerges in response to the question: what is reality?

One should keep in mind here the discussion in the previous chapter on the deeper meanings of divine unity. In the Imam's spiritual vision of tawhid, one perceives — according to one's capacity — an ultimate reality that not only "has no second" but also has no moral "opposite." The goodness proper to God is not the moral opposite of evil; it belongs to an entirely different plane — that of al-Haqq, the absolutely Real, whose only "opposite" is unreality or nothingness. Goodness is thus at one with the pure positivity of the Absolute, while evil derives solely from a negative capacity to negate the Real, the Good. In other words, goodness is identified in its essence with the Creator, while evil is a modality of the created. This is strongly implied in the Qur'anic verse that bids the believer seek refuge in God from "the evil of that which He created" (113:2). This verse is often mistranslated as "from the evil which He created," which is wrong both theologically and lexically. The expression "min sharri ma khalaqa" refers plainly to the evil that derives from creation, not to creation as such. Theologically, God creates morally free agents, and it is they who are the authors of evil in the world. God may be said to "create" all actions only in the metaphysical sense that He is the source of all being, and therefore of all modalities within being — of which actions are one. But God cannot be said to have created evil as such, only the conditions within which evil can emerge. Evil is thus the negation of the Good, which is at one with the Real; as such, evil has no ultimate ontological principle.

The verse may therefore be understood as an appeal to seek refuge in the Real from the unreal — there being no common measure, in terms of being, between goodness and evil. Goodness is opposable only by the appearance of evil, an appearance that the "eye of the heart" grasps as illusion — that eye which truly "sees" God through "the verities of faith." This vision of the relative reality of evil, far from breeding indifference toward it, powerfully strengthens the spiritual resolve to overcome it. The moral effort to be good is intensified in proportion to one's conviction of accountability to goodness itself. That conviction is in turn carried inward, from mind to heart, by the certainty that goodness is "more real" than evil — a certainty nourished by the spiritual vision of the good as the Real. The perception of the radical incommensurability between good and evil thus leads not to indifference in the face of evil, but to a more existentially compelling orientation toward the good. In the Imam's perspective, the notions of "right" and "rightful due" contained within the meaning of haqq are suffused with an aura of absoluteness. The moral notions of rectitude and propriety are expressions of a principle that transcends morality — a principle that touches ultimate reality. One's commitment to fulfilling one's obligations, to giving every being its rightful due, is likewise infused with a powerful sense of reality; the resolution to be just is made more absolute by the very quality of absoluteness that belongs to the Real.

This duty to give what is due to each and every being connects directly to the most explicit definition of justice the Imam offers: "Justice puts everything in its right place." One is "just" insofar as one gives everything its proper due, renders the haqq owed to each person — indeed to each and every thing in existence — and the ability to be "just" in this demanding and universal sense is heightened in proportion to one's attunement to al-Haqq, the Real. As the previous chapter made clear, this spiritual conception of virtue not only encompasses one's duty to all but engages everything that one is. One has to be good before one can do good; and the only way "to be" is to be at one with the Real — that which alone truly "is."

The Imam's reflection on justice calls to mind Plato's more famous definition: "... we have laid down, as a universal principle, that everyone ought to perform the one function in the community for which his nature best suited him ... that principle, or some form of it, is justice." There are indeed many remarkable parallels between Plato's Republic and the Imam's letter to Malik al-Ashtar. This chapter will occasionally note some of the more obvious correspondences, and at its close will consider the way in which the Imam's explicit spirituality illuminates what Plato leaves implicit — namely, how one actually attains the vision of the Real that Plato calls "gazing upon the Good." It is this contemplation of the Good as such that serves as the source of one's capacity to act ethically at every level, for it allows one to take the Good "as a pattern for the right ordering of the state and of the individual."

The influence of Platonic and Neoplatonic conceptions of justice on Islamic ethical discourse has been amply documented in the sources. Within that discourse, justice is not so much one virtue among several as it is the perfection or sum of the other virtues — especially the three principal Platonic virtues of temperance, courage, and wisdom. These must characterize, respectively, the concupiscent, the irascible, and the rational dimensions of the soul. When all dimensions of the soul, each "filled out" by its corresponding virtue, are in their proper place, the soul may be called "just." What has not received sufficient attention in the sources, however, is the degree to which there is a spiritual correspondence between Plato's primary means of arriving at the vision of the Good — anamnesis — and the cardinal role of dhikr in Islam, both terms being translatable into English as "remembrance." This correspondence underscores the importance of remembrance, of a recollection of ultimate reality and immutable principles, at every level of application. It is not a matter of learning things anew, but of recollecting principles that are inherent in the very substance of the intellect — principles by which all things can be understood, and which are ultimately rooted in the Sovereign Good itself. Such remembrance should be regarded as the cognitive and philosophical foundation of ethical thought. That its importance has been ignored is symptomatic of the severance of ethics from spirituality in modern discourse, which treats each discipline as if it were hermetically sealed from the other. While ethics is still taken seriously as a branch of philosophy and a discipline in its own right, spirituality is all too often dismissed as irrelevant to serious scholarly inquiry and relegated to the realm of the imaginary, the vague, and the mythological. This marginalization of spirituality from ostensibly serious discourse is closely related to the reductive view of knowledge that has increasingly characterized Western thought from the Renaissance onward — a reduction that goes hand in hand with an evisceration of the sacred dimension of life.

In the Islamic tradition, the realms of ethics and spirituality are intimately connected. Nowhere is this more apparent than in the Sufi tradition, which is increasingly and most aptly referred to as the ihsani tradition — taking the word ihsan (excellence, virtue, goodness) in the sense given to it by the Prophet in a tradition of central importance regarding the three fundamental dimensions of the Islamic way. It is known as "the hadith of Gabriel," because the stranger who questioned the Prophet — unbeknown to the Companions present — was the angel Gabriel in human form.

"O Muhammad, tell me about submission (al-islam)," the stranger asked. The Prophet replied, "Al-islam is to testify that there is no god but God and Muhammad is the messenger of God, to perform the prayers, to pay the poor-due (al-zakat), to fast in Ramadan, and to make the pilgrimage to the House if you are able to do so."

The narration continues:

"He [Gabriel] said, 'You have spoken truly' — and we [the Companions] were amazed at his asking the question and then confirming the answer. He said, 'Then tell me about faith (al-iman).' He [the Prophet] said, 'It is to believe in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, and the Last Day, and to believe in divine destiny, both the good and the evil thereof.' He said, 'You have spoken truly.' He said, 'Then tell me about virtue (al-ihsan).' He said, 'It is to worship God as though you see Him, and if you see Him not, yet truly He sees you.'"

The word ihsan can also be translated literally as "doing what is beautiful," since its root is primarily related to beauty, husn. Virtue and vice are often described in Islamic ethical discourse as husn and qubh — literally beauty and ugliness. Ultimately, it is the vision of divine beauty that inspires virtue, which is beauty of soul. It is therefore unsurprising that, broadly speaking, while the specialists in outward action according to Islam have been the jurists, the fuqaha', and the specialists in formal belief have been the theologians, it fell to the mystics to be the custodians of spiritual virtue — of "making beautiful." It is no coincidence, either, that the overwhelming majority of artists in the Islamic world — calligraphers, painters, poets, musicians — have been practising Sufis.

From the Prophet's definition of ihsan as "worshipping God as if one could see Him," one can see clearly the vital relationship between worship and virtue, spirituality and ethics, devotion to the Creator and goodness toward creatures. Understanding this relationship takes us to the very heart of the spiritual tradition in Islam. Conversely, it is the denial and subversion of this tradition by some Muslims today that — more than any other single factor, one might venture to say — ultimately underlies the atrocities being committed by Muslims in the name of Islam.

It should be noted that both the theory and the practice of ethics have always been integral to Sufism. Even some of those who wrote in more philosophical and less explicitly spiritual terms — such as al-Farabi (d. 872/950) — were known to be practising Sufis. The most influential figure in whom the domains of spirituality and ethics converge is, of course, al-Ghazali (d. 505/1111), whose magisterial Ihya' 'ulum al-din (Revival of the Sciences of Religion) stands as a monument to the impact of Islam's deepest spirit upon the ethical disposition of the believing soul. He has aptly been called the greatest ethical thinker in Islam, and given that his thought is so thoroughly shaped by Sufism, this alone testifies to the close relationship between Sufism and ethics. Indeed, it would not be an exaggeration to say that the majority of treatises written by Sufis are, in effect, treatises on ethics — if ethics is understood not merely as akhlaq in the narrow sense, but also as the fruit of ihsan, of spiritual virtue. The various spiritual stations (maqamat) described by the Sufis frequently bear the names of virtues: patience (sabr), gratitude (shukr), contentment (rida'), generosity (karam), and so on.


The thread running through all of these stations is the transformative awareness of God — the principle of being and acting "as if you could see God." This means conducting and composing oneself in the constant consciousness of the divine presence, for even if you cannot see Him, "He sees you." The imperative of ihsan thus lies at the very root of a virtuous life in Islam's spiritual universe. As we shall see in this and the following chapter, Ali (ع) repeatedly draws the reader back to the "vision" of God — at whatever degree — as the true basis of virtue, and repeatedly warns of its opposite: the engrossing vision of the self, which is the source of all vice. On one hand, the Imam tells us, "Know that your every action is dependent upon your prayer" — that is, upon your way of "gazing upon the Good," to use Platonic terms. On the other, he warns against vanity, conceit, and lavish self-praise, for these are "Satan's most reliable opportunities to efface the virtue (ihsan) of the virtuous (al-muhsinin)."²³ The true, objective vision of al-Haqq, the Real, realized through self-effacement in prayer, is here set against the distorted, subjective vision of the ego, inflated through pride. The first leads to justice — placing all things, the ego above all, in their proper position. The second leads to every injustice that flows from self-aggrandizement, including fanaticism and bigotry, for Satan, the epitome of pride and disobedience, is described as "the leader of the fanatics and the forerunner of the proud (imam al-muta'assibin wa salaf al-mustakbirin)." Fanatical bigotry is a particularly intense form of injustice: it is that pernicious prejudice which subordinates the objective demands of justice to the supposed prerogatives of the self — whether that self is defined in terms of the individual, the family, the clan, the tribe, the nation, or religion.

**The Letter to Malik al-Ashtar**

The Imam's epistle appointing Malik al-Ashtar as his governor of Egypt appears as number 52 in most editions of the Nahj al-balagha.²⁵ In broad historical terms, the letter has served as a source of inspiration across the centuries, read as an ideal constitution for Islamic governance. Through its relatively detailed account of the duties and rights of the ruler, the various functionaries of the state, and the main classes of society, it complements the more general framework of principles enshrined in the Prophet's famous "Constitution of Medina." As will become clear, however, the advice the letter contains far exceeds the parameters conventionally set by political or legal texts on governance. Although addressed in the first instance to a ruler, much of its counsel applies to universally valid ethical principles and is therefore as relevant to the governed as to those who govern. It is as much a statement of ethics as of politics — not a discursive system or theory of ethics, to be sure, but an inspiring expression of the spiritual ethos that the Imam embodied and radiated, an ethos flowing directly from the sources of Islamic revelation, and one that therefore discloses the roots of virtue and ethics within a framework governed by the all-embracing principle of revelation. The document may thus be read as a commentary on that revelation, encouraging the reader to delve more deeply into the meanings enshrined in the revealed texts.

Malik al-Ashtar was a long-standing and devoted follower of the Imam, widely regarded as one of his "right-hand" men. Even Mu'awiya — the Imam's principal antagonist in the civil war (37–41/657–661) that dominated his caliphate — used that description of Malik, reportedly after learning that his mission to poison Malik had succeeded before Malik could take up his post as governor of Egypt. Al-Tabari records Mu'awiya as remarking: "Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع) had two right hands. One of them was cut at Siffin, and the other today." For his part, the Imam's response to news of Malik's assassination was: "Malik — and what was Malik! By God, were he a mountain, he would be the solitary one soaring above the rest; and were he a rock, he would have been impenetrably solid. No horse could ascend such a mountain, no bird could fly up to it."

<!-- page 97 -->

Let us return to the Imam's definition of justice noted earlier: "Justice puts everything in its right place." What connects this conception of justice to the sacred is the prior imperative of placing one's relationship with God in its right place; everything else derives from that spiritual imperative. The same order of precedence is clearly established in the following important passage from the Imam's letter to Malik: "Be just with God and be just with people — giving them what is their due — from yourself." Being just with God means, among other things, conforming as fully as one can to His own nature, which is not merely the source of justice but is justice itself. "I bear witness," the Imam declares in one of his sermons, "that He is Justice ('adl) and He acts justly." One might object that between the divine reality of justice and its human expression there is a radical disjunction: in God, justice means that everything is already in its right place, whereas for human beings justice entails the effort to put everything in its right place — an immutable metaphysical quality on the divine side, a dynamic, volitional effort on the human side. One response is this: the human moral effort to act justly in a world where things are not in their right place is rooted in an innate spiritual predisposition toward justice. Just action thereby expresses the principle of justice, which — in the human being as in God — is that immutable principle by which everything is in its right place. Just as divine action in creation, judgement, and other domains is an expression of absolute justice, so in the human being just action expresses not only a moral, volitional, and intellectual effort to realize justice, but also a spiritual affinity with the ultimate nature of reality — with things as they truly are in God, and therefore as they ought to be here on earth.

Applied to state and society, one of the most obvious consequences of this conception of justice is the necessity of piety and rectitude on the part of rulers. As the Imam states in another sermon: "The most momentous of the reciprocal rights that God has made obligatory is the right of the ruler over the subjects and the right of the subjects over the ruler — a reciprocal obligation that God has ordained for them." He then makes a point indispensable to any serious discourse on good governance in Islam: "The subjects will not be righteous except through the righteousness of the rulers, and the rulers will not be righteous except through the uprightness of the subjects. So if the subjects render to the ruler his rightful due and he renders them theirs, then right will be honoured among them, the ways of religion will be upheld, and the banners of justice will be raised." This insistence on the piety of the ruler is, as one would expect, central to the Imam's message to his governor; but as what follows will make clear, it involves far more than a simple exhortation to observe the formal rules of Islamic law.

<!-- page 98 -->

The whole of the Imam's life can be seen as a quest to conform as perfectly as possible to the divine nature — and to this quality of justice in particular. The social, economic, political, and juridical dimensions of this principle as they appear in his life and teachings are too numerous to address within the brief compass of this essay. Let us simply recall one defining moment of his caliphate that illustrates the rigour with which he adhered to justice at all costs. The revolt of Mu'awiya, and the civil war into which the young Muslim empire was thereby plunged, can be seen as having begun not at Siffin but in Medina. Upon assuming the caliphate, Ali (ع) was advised by his cousin Ibn al-'Abbas to temporarily confirm all of 'Uthman's governors in their posts and, once the new order had consolidated, to remove them. The Imam's reply was reported as follows: "I do not doubt that this would be best for the sake of reconciliation in this world. But there is my obligation to the Truth (alladhī yalzamanī min al-haqq) and my knowledge of 'Uthman's governors — so, by God, I shall never appoint one of them."³⁵

The die was thus cast: the Imam dismissed Mu'awiya along with all of his predecessor's governors. He was under no illusion that Ibn al-'Abbas' proposal had every chance of working — he knew full well that Mu'awiya sought nothing more than worldly power and would have been content to govern Syria had the Imam allowed it. Commentators have read this episode, which all but guaranteed the governors' revolt, as evidence of Ali (ع)'s political naivety and inflexibility. It ought instead to be understood as an expression of his disdain for worldly ease purchased at the price of essential principles. As he says in one of his aphorisms: "People renounce no part of their religion for the sake of rectifying their worldly affairs without God opening them up to something worse than the very defect they were seeking to remedy."³⁷

It is clear that confirming in power governors whose corruption was beyond both doubt and remedy would have amounted, for the Imam, to renouncing a part of his religion — for religion without justice is not religion. Supporting unjust governors, even as a tactical expedient, represented a compromise of religious principle that was simply inconceivable to him. This attitude was expressed in countless acts throughout his life and demonstrates that the principles enshrined in the letter to Malik al-Ashtar were far from merely theoretical.

In the sections that follow, we turn first to an analysis of a particular dimension of justice — one that is political on the surface, ethical in its outward expression, and spiritual at its depth. This is the justice of the political ruler: justice conceived in relation to its most clearly defined diametrical opposite — injustice, tyranny, oppression, and corruption. The argument advanced here is that deep assimilation of the spirituality embodied by the Imam can confer an existential power upon the moral effort to live by just principles, a power that is absent when justice is conceived exclusively in terms of individual or horizontal values — that is, in terms of a secular moral philosophy divorced from religious or spiritual grounding. The crucial role of devotion and worship — both formal and supra-formal — is then examined. Contemplation of the divine Reality, it is proposed, is the fountainhead from which integral virtue flows spontaneously and without cease. This point will be sharpened by a brief comparison with Plato's "gazing" upon the Sovereign Good as the source of all virtue.

The analysis draws on key extracts from Ali (ع)'s letter to Malik al-Ashtar, taking into account relevant passages from his other letters, sermons, and sayings, as well as prophetic traditions and Quranic verses that illuminate the themes the letter raises. A full translation of the Imam's letter is provided in Appendix II of this work.


### Justice Versus Tyranny

The letter is addressed to one of the Imam's closest and most trusted companions, so his warnings about injustice and oppression, while expressing a universal theme, must also be understood in a very precise sense. They apply to all those who, while formally subject to Islam and its laws and possessing, initially, the right intentions, remain vulnerable to the temptations of political power — with full acknowledgment of the unfortunate but proven truth captured in the maxim "power corrupts," or, in the Imam's own words, "he who rules, appropriates (man malaka ista thara)."

The Imam's approach to justice clearly transcends the historical boundaries of its context, just as it goes beyond the formal boundaries of the Islamic faith. The letter addresses not only those who wish to rule according to just principles, but also all those who, though without corrupt intentions at the outset, are nonetheless corruptible — a category that embraces the overwhelming majority of mankind.

Human virtue and ultimate reality

A note of sombre realism sounds at the very outset of the letter. The potential for evil in man, when left to his own resources and deprived of divine grace, dominates the tone of the preamble — hence the stress on abiding by God's wajibat, the religious obligations binding on every Muslim. Malik is told to prefer obedience to God above all things, "to abide by what He has commanded in His Book ... for no one prospers except through abiding by them, and no one is wretched except through repudiating and neglecting them." The preamble is introductory but by no means perfunctory: obedience to the divine commands is to be understood not only as legally binding but as spiritually liberating. This obedience plays a crucial part in freeing one from the tyranny of the "soul which incites to evil" (al-nafs al-ammara bi'l-su'), to which the Imam refers immediately after calling Malik to obedience: "For truly, the soul incites to evil, unless God has mercy."40 This sentence is almost identical to the words of the Prophet Joseph in the Qur'an (12: 53), and it is of the utmost importance in setting the scene for the ethical injunctions and socio-political instructions that follow. The essential relationship that determines the spiritual substance of the soul — and, a fortiori, its moral comportment — is the relationship between the human soul and divine grace. Without grace, the soul tends toward evil; but with God's assistance, it is liberated from its own base inclinations and guided back to its true, primordial nature, its fitra, to which the Qur'an refers in the following verse: So set thy purpose for religion with unswerving devotion: the nature of God (fitra Allah), according to which He hath created man. There is no altering God's creation. That is the right religion (al-din al-qayyim), but most men know not. (30: 30) The fallen soul's actual condition must yield, then, to the ideal state of original human nature — a nature that is both the origin and the end of the human condition, and also the true substance of every human soul, and thus always accessible in principle, even if for most people it remains clouded in practice by fallen human nature. In the quest to revive this primordial nature, one returns to the complementary functions of the human intellect and divine revelation. In the first sermon of the Nahj, cited earlier, the Imam tells us that God sent His messengers to mankind, "dispatching prophets in succession, in order to claim from them [His creatures] the fulfilment of the covenant of His creation; to remind them of His forgotten graces; to remonstrate with them through communication [of His revelation]; to unearth for them the buried treasures of the intellects (dafa'in al-'uqul)."

These "buried treasures" can be identified with the original, God-given knowledge proper to the fitra. From this integral human nature, in complete harmony with the divine nature, all the essential virtues flow spontaneously and without impediment. "Let your most beloved treasure be the treasure of virtuous acts," the Imam writes. Virtue will indeed be the "most beloved treasure" for one who reverses the natural inclinations of the egoistic soul and establishes a supernatural orientation toward its own deepest nature — and, by that very token, toward the divine Reality that furnishes the ontological foundations of all authentic virtue. For al-Haqq, the Real, is not only the deepest foundation of virtue; it is the true substance of every positive quality and, in the final analysis, of every single existent entity. As the words of one of the most famous supplications attributed to the Imam put it:

By Thy tremendousness (bi-'a matika), which has filled all things ... by Thy Names which have filled the foundations of all things, by Thy knowledge which encompasses all things, by the light of Thy face, through which all things are illumined ...43 From this perspective, every virtue can be considered the reflection on the human plane of a divine attribute — what is denoted as a divine "Name." Hence the fundamental theme of the ihsani tradition finds expression in the saying: "Assume the character traits of God (takhallaqu bi-akhlāqi'Llah)."

The relationship between human virtue and divine reality is altogether indispensable in the Imam's perspective. On one hand — in subjective terms — the practice of virtue attracts the corresponding divine quality; and on the other — in objective terms — the divine quality is the source and, in accordance with true tawhid, the actual substance of all human virtue. That is to say, every positive quality, both in itself and in its manifestation, pertains to God, the One who "has no partner" theologically speaking, and who "has no second" in ontological terms. This mystical assimilation of the principle of tawhid is powerfully implied in the Qur'anic verse: "And thou threw not when thou didst throw [a handful of dust], but God it was who threw" (8: 17). Not only must the individual be effaced before God in order to be truly virtuous; he must also participate in and embody the very qualities he wishes God to manifest toward him — and his effort to do so is empowered in proportion to his comprehension of those same divine attributes, grasped through intuition and existentially rather than merely through reason and theory. The relationship between intelligence and humility deserves to be underlined here: the intellectual perception of divine attributes results in humility, and the effacement of the soul in turn shifts attention away from the ego and directs it toward the transcendent reality as the source of all virtue and truth.

It is worth dwelling a little on the notion of the "soul which incites to evil" and on the Qur'anic story of Joseph (Yusuf) within which this notion arises, for knowledge of this context would have been assumed by the Imam when he composed the letter, and he clearly intended it to be borne in mind by Malik — and, by extension, by any other reader of this letter on governance.

Joseph offers this description of man's base inclinations after his innocence of all charges has been established in the presence of the Egyptian king. Even then he says, "I do not exonerate myself. Truly, the soul incites to evil, unless my Lord has mercy." Indeed, on both occasions when he was subjected to the seductive temptations of Zulaykha, the Qur'an makes clear that his successful resistance to her wiles was owed not to his own moral resources but to the grace of God.

This theme of human helplessness in the face of attractive but immoral gratification is heavily implied in the Imam's repetition of the Qur'anic phrase, "Truly the soul incites to evil, unless God has mercy." These, then, are the two poles of attraction for the ordinary soul — the soul not yet "at peace": its own base inclinations toward the negative possibilities within the fleeting world of appearances, and the higher orientation toward the grace of God, drawing the soul back toward the pure positivity of ultimate reality. The sheer acuteness of the contrast contained in this powerful Qur'anic sentence galvanizes an awareness of the soul's absolute and unconditional need of God in all circumstances. It is a most fitting way to begin a letter of instruction to one being vested with authority over a vast and wealthy country where temptations of every kind lie in wait. It stands as a solemn reminder of the soul's susceptibility to allurement on every plane — gross and subtle alike — a susceptibility that can be neutralized and overcome not by the soul itself, but only by the mercy and grace of God. Yet, returning to Joseph's words, the "vision of the evidence" of God implies active discernment and, as its moral consequence, resolute self-control. Thus the human effort to avoid sin does not contradict the necessity of grace for the attainment of virtue; rather, it expresses a grace already bestowed, already present within the conscientious soul. The very capacity of the human will to strive for virtue is itself a pre-existent "grace" embedded within the spiritual constitution, the fitra — yet it is a grace that must be rendered present, or actualized, through the sincere effort to avail oneself of this God-given power to strive for virtue.

The manner in which this grace becomes a determinative element of moral and spiritual life will be made clearer later in the Imam's letter; but for now, the negative side of the question requires further elaboration. The ever-present temptation for a ruler to abuse power and perpetrate injustice and tyranny deserves more than a passing reference. One might argue that the question of how to refrain from injustice is a far more difficult and subtle problem than its positive counterpart — how to act with justice. The positive rules of political governance are laid down by the Imam in a direct and straightforward manner,47 but what is far less straightforward is the actual ability of any holder of political office to maintain fidelity to those rules when confronted with the temptations that power offers. One may frame laws to punish corruption; what cannot be legislated is the will or the capacity to remain incorruptible in the face of the blandishments that political authority brings. This helps explain why the Imam's exhortations take on such a negative quality in the early part of the letter — as in the commands to "break the soul in the face of passionate desires," to "dominate your inclination," to "withhold yourself from that which is not lawful for you." It also explains why the ethical imperative of justice must be placed firmly within the context of a lived spirituality: without that context, injunctions to be fair, just, and honest will lack the existential quality that transforms them from abstract postulates into unquestionable realities, and that transmutes formal obedience to externally imposed rules into spiritual affinity with the source of those rules.

Living according to just precepts then ceases to be compliance with something imposed from without and becomes the moral concomitant of one's inner being. In other words, an integral approach to justice leads to an identification with what is right — with al-Haqq as such — and not merely to the doing of right. There is a shift of consciousness from doing to being, from action to contemplation, without this implying in any way that action is weakened or undermined. On the contrary, action comes to reflect, on its own plane, the divine object of contemplation, and thereby becomes more, not less, effective. An orientation toward the Real imparts a deeper degree of reality to what one does in the realm of action and to one's own mode of being. One does what is right insofar as one is real; this process of realization is expressed in the Islamic spiritual tradition as tahqiq, "making real": one's ethical comportment is "made real" through spirituality, and spirituality is in turn expressed by — and rendered more concrete through — the practice of virtue.

Overcoming subtle polytheism

The Imam then issues the stern warning: "Do not set your soul up for war with God." One might reasonably ask what kinds of actions or attitudes put the soul at war with God. There seems to be an allusion to something rather deeper than simple disobedience to God manifested as violations of the religious obligations mentioned at the outset of the letter. While that is the most obvious meaning of "fighting" against God, it does not exclude more subtle modes of opposition to divine reality — attitudes that may accompany outward acts of obedience yet contradict the spirit of those acts. Consider, for example, the cardinal vice of pride (takabbur), against which the Imam repeatedly warns. The ruler's pride constitutes a misappropriation of a divine attribute, an arrogation to oneself of a greatness that belongs exclusively to God: He alone has the right to takabbur, and He alone can be called, without insult, al-Mutakabbir, "the Proud." To vaunt oneself before others is tantamount to inwardly opposing the authority of God and thus amounts to an implicit denial of His unique and incomparable majesty. This denial of a concomitant of God's sovereignty can itself be seen as a kind of "war" with God — for enmity with the transcendent reality is the invisible substance that pervades all pride and every act of arrogance or oppression by a ruler toward those beneath him.

The paradox here — one that only a sense of spiritual values can reveal — is that an attitude of superiority toward those who are "below" constitutes, in fact, an attitude of rebellion toward that which is "above." Hence, an injustice toward creatures not only draws down upon its perpetrator the justice of the Creator; it also manifests, in and of itself, outright opposition to the Creator. What appears to be a horizontal vice — "horizontal" in the sense of relating to this world — is in reality a spiritual disorder. The outward immorality of corruption is merely the symptom of an inner spiritual malaise: an implicit denial of the absoluteness — and hence uniqueness — of the source of all power and authority, a forgetfulness of the reality expressed in the Qur'anic verse, "To whom belongeth the dominion this day? To God, the One, the All-Conquering" (40: 16). The truth expressed by this verse should resound constantly in the ears of all rulers, "this day" being understood as "every day" and not only the Day of Judgement. In the Imam's sermon known as al-Qasi'a, "the Abasement" — so named on account of its humiliation of the proud — Iblis (Satan) is described as the first of those who "competed with God" in respect of grandeur, and is thus the very embodiment of the principle of pride and arrogance. When ordered to prostrate before Adam (38: 71–74), Iblis refused because "vehement passion" overcame him; he deemed himself superior to Adam because he was created from fire, whereas Adam was created from clay. Taking pride in this presumed superiority and thereby rebelling against God's command, Iblis is not only the first "enemy of God" but also, as noted earlier, "the leader of the bigots and the forerunner of the proud (imam al-muta'assibin wa salaf al-mustakbirin)." The Imam warns his listeners: "Beware lest he infect you with his disease" through his insinuations, whisperings, and machinations. He urges them: "Be resolute in placing self-abasement over your heads, casting self-glorification beneath your feet, and removing pride from your necks. And take up humility as the fortified watchtower between you and your enemy."

Humility, then, is the principal weapon in the war against Iblis, whose key aim, conversely, is to inculcate pride — for pride is not only the worst of vices but also the poison that infects and destroys all virtue. Virtue itself is converted into vice the moment it is accompanied by pride. "The sin that grieves you is better, in the sight of God, than the virtue that makes you proud." One should also recall what was noted above about the dangers of excessive praise and the conceit it generates, these being among "Satan's most reliable opportunities to efface the virtue of one who is virtuous."

Taken together, these statements constitute a commentary on the Qur'anic account of Iblis' disgrace, whereby a simple act of disobedience is grasped at its spiritual root in a way that brings home the ever-present danger of falling prey to that "hidden" form of shirk (polytheism), to which the Prophet refers in the following words: "The creeping of shirk in my community is more hidden (akhfa) than the creeping of a black ant over a hard rock on a dark night." The superficial notion of shirk as the worship of material idols must be understood more subtly as false gods hidden within one's intentions, attitudes, and orientations — in every element of concealed pride, vanity, ostentation, and pretentiousness. As the Imam says, "Know that the slightest pretension (riya') is polytheism." To act for the sake of earning the praise of others is to act for others and not for God; it is therefore an implicit attribution of partners to God: "Whoso acts for other than God, God entrusts him to those for whose sake he acted."


In this light, the meaning of Iblīs' act of disobedience can be more deeply plumbed. It is the outward moral consequence of an inner spiritual malaise rooted in a pride tantamount to 'hidden polytheism,' which brings in its wake disgraceful humiliation. Iblīs is disgraced because he disobeyed God, and he disobeyed God on account of pride. Pride is therefore, in and of itself, a disgrace — a state of 'dis-grace,' a perversion of the human condition that carries within itself the seed of its own inexorable inversion. 'Do you not see how God humiliated him through his pride?' the Imam asks. To be proud is already, in reality, a form of humiliation, for self-aggrandizement is tantamount to self-destruction.

Returning to the pride of those in power, the Imam captures succinctly the train of thought set in motion within the mind of a ruler who is stepping onto the slippery slope toward oppression: 'Do not say, "I have been given authority, I order and am obeyed", for this leads to corruption in the heart and the erosion of religion.' It is worth noting that this self-glorification is seen as producing first an inner, spiritual corruption — that of the 'heart' — and only then the destruction of religion. This order of priority underscores the fact that the outward prescriptions of religion require the right inward disposition if they are to be applied with integrity and maintained with stability. One might restate the Imam's idea thus: when power goes to the head, faith departs from the heart; and conversely, for faith to be properly manifested in the world, it must flow from a heart suffused with humility. This is another expression of 'putting each thing in its proper place.'

The Imam continues: 'If the authority of your position engenders vanity and arrogance, then look at the grandeur of God's dominion above you … this will calm your ambition, restrain you from your own vehemence and restore to you what had strayed from your intellect.' The connection between intellect and humility deserves careful attention here. The part of the intellect that 'strays' when vanity and arrogance take hold is precisely the part that remains conscious of the absolute reality of God and the relative, derivative, and ultimately illusory character of everything else. It is significant that the Arabic word for 'arrogance,' makhila, shares a root with the word for imagination, khayal, tying it intimately to the realm of the imaginary, the fanciful, the illusory, and the unreal. Arrogant pride is therefore an intense form of self-delusion, rooted in an intellectual defect. It manifests as a vice, but its cause runs deeper than the moral level, for it involves not merely an over-estimation of oneself, but, more fundamentally, an under-estimation of the Absolute. Seen this way, the overt egotism of the tyrant is an intensification of the congenital egocentricity that accompanies every person who has not realized the truth of God's sole reality and the ultimately illusory nature of all else. The sage alone fully assimilates the truth expressed in such Qur'anic verses as: 'Everything is dying away except His countenance' (28:88); 'Everything that is thereon is perishing, and there subsisteth but the Face of thy Lord, possessor of Glory and Bounty' (55:26–27); and 'The Truth hath come and falsehood hath passed away; indeed, falsehood is ever passing away' (17:81). Only the sage draws from this truth the humility that dissolves all individualism and egocentricity — the roots of pride, vanity, and arrogance that are, in turn, the lifeblood of tyranny and oppression.

One can now better appreciate the Imam's warning: 'Beware of comparing [yourself] with God in greatness and likening [yourself] to Him in might, for God abases every tyrant and disgraces every braggart.' One who fails to act with justice toward God and His creatures, and instead tyrannizes them, will find that not only those creatures but God Himself becomes his adversary: 'He remains at war with God until he desists and repents.' The tyrant will eventually be compelled to repent, even if only at the point of death and imminent judgment. Even if he appears to have successfully evaded the consequences of his tyranny in this life, he cannot evade them in the Hereafter.

As for the nature of the tyrant's 'war' against God in this world — his implicit opposition to ultimate reality, his violation of the deepest nature of things — prior to repentance or judgment, this war takes various forms. One form is constituted by the very persistence of tyranny itself, for the longer it continues, the more wretched the soul of the tyrant becomes, and the more firmly the seeds of misery are planted in his being. This is implied in the Qur'anic doctrine of istidraj — the drawing out of punishment by degrees: 'Of those We have created are people who guide with truth and dispense justice therewith. But those who reject Our signs, We will lead them on by degrees [to punishment] from whence they know not' (7:181–2). The punishment meted out 'by degrees' can take the form of apparent worldly success, as is clear from the verse: 'Let not their wealth nor their children please thee. God desireth only to punish them thereby in the world, that their souls shall pass away while they are disbelievers' (9:85). The tyrant or oppressor can be a Muslim in the formal sense and yet be guilty of a mode of 'disbelief,' taking the word kafir in its strictly etymological sense as one who 'covers over' the truth — and its moral concomitants — through his being and his actions. This connects directly to the contrast drawn in the verses cited above (7:181–2) between those who dispense justice in accordance with the truth and 'those who reject Our signs' — the oppressors and tyrants.

### Moral Conscience And Spiritual Consciousness

Another form taken by 'enmity with God' is the inner war with one's own conscience. The voice of conscience — the very essence and sum total of secular ethics — can here be grasped in its fuller context, rather than simply as the somewhat hypertrophied foundation of individual morality to which conscience has been reduced in secular ethical discourse. That fuller context is the supra-individual, or metaphysical, source of individual conscience: the divine infusion of spiritual discernment within the soul. This infusion or inspiration serves as the basis of ethical discrimination. The divine quality of justice is thus translated, at the level of the morally responsible soul, into the voice of inner conscience: 'And the soul and that which perfected it, inspiring it with [consciousness of] its wickedness and its righteousness' (91:7–8). The war against one's conscience — even if the oppressor barely acknowledges it — is in fact, and a fortiori, a war against God.

At a deeper level, then, this 'war against God' emerges clearly as a war against the very reality that defines the soul as such. For the soul not only contains a moral conscience — an intrinsic and inalienable awareness of the difference between good and evil instilled within it by God — it also contains an innate consciousness of the reality of divine lordship, together with the corresponding servitude of the creature. Spiritual consciousness is thus to be understood as the source of moral conscience. The Qur'an describes the immanence of this spiritual consciousness within each soul in terms that emphasize its supra- or pre-existential nature, portraying it as a 'moment' at the very dawn of creation — a 'moment,' or ontological degree, that precedes the human condition as such yet enters into the creative articulation of the human soul:

And when thy Lord brought forth from the children of Adam, from their loins, their seed, and made them testify against their souls [saying], 'Am I not your Lord?' They said, 'Yes, verily, we testify.' [This was] lest ye say on the Day of Resurrection, 'Truly, of this we were unaware' (7:172).

One observes here that the source of morality transcends the human level even while forming part of the very definition of humanity. From this perspective, what renders the human soul fully human is precisely the element of transcendence that goes infinitely beyond it and yet mysteriously furnishes its own deepest being. In this light, tyranny and oppression take on a far more nuanced meaning, these vices being brought much closer to the lived reality of every soul rather than being confined to those who possess and abuse political power. The tyrant is, first and foremost, one who violates his own soul, allowing it to be dominated by 'the soul which commands [evil],' al-nafs al-ammara. Thus, the statement the Imam offers as epitomizing the attitude of one on his way to corrupting his soul and becoming an oppressor — 'I order and am obeyed' — is not to be understood only in social terms but also interpreted microcosmically. It becomes the statement of the evil part of the soul, the nafs al-ammara, which literally 'orders' (amara) and is obeyed by the soul's other faculties: the intelligence, imagination, will, sentiment, sensibility, and so on.

The combat against one's own soul (mujahadat al-nafs) is a theme the Imam returns to repeatedly. This necessity of struggling with and overcoming one's own faults is an essential dimension of justice in the sense defined earlier: placing each thing in its proper place means rectifying oneself before presuming to reform or rule over others. As the Imam says, 'If your aspiration ascends to the reforming of the people, begin with yourself, for your pursuit of the reform of others, when your own soul is corrupt, is the greatest of faults.' In his letter appointing Muhammad b. Abī Bakr — Malik's predecessor as governor of Egypt — the Imam draws a strong connection between the bestowal of power and the need to oppose one's own soul: 'And be aware, O Muhammad b. Abi Bakr, that I have appointed you as governor over the most immense of the forces at my disposal, the people of Egypt, so you are obliged to oppose your soul and protect your religion.'51 The relationship between the magnitude of the power entrusted and the spiritual responsibility it entails deserves careful attention. The first obligation is toward one's own soul and its desires; the second is toward the religion one must guard. The greater the power available, the greater the obligation to control and restrain oneself — for if the soul yields to its desires and gratifies them with the power at its disposal, the result is corruption not only of oneself but also of the religion one is charged to uphold. Once again, priorities are ordered by an essentially spiritual principle: the outward form and application of religion depends for its integrity on the inward disposition of the Muslim soul. This is yet another expression of the principle of putting each thing in its proper place. The commanding soul that arrogates to itself autonomy and dominion is described with precision by the Qur'anic verse: 'Truly man is rebellious, in that he deemeth himself independent' (96:6–7). Insofar as the soul pretends to be self-sufficient and attempts to sever itself from its total dependence upon God, it sets itself up as a god in its own right — and here lies the true description of the tyrant, the one who, as the Qur'an puts it, 'taketh his own desire as his god' (45:23). Knowledge of one's nothingness before God, and consequently of one's unconditional need of God, may be taken as the principal means of loosening the grip of 'the soul which commands to evil.' For only the soul that already commands itself to evil commands others to evil, and only the soul that is lowly before God will be humble, compassionate, and just in its dealings with people.

This principle emerges clearly from the following words of the Imam: 'Let the most beloved of affairs to you be those most centred upon the right, the most comprehensive in justice, and the most inclusive of popular approval, for the disapproval of the common folk undermines the approval of the elite …' This emphasis on the importance of the common people is not only sound practical advice for fair and effective governance; it also reflects the spiritual imperative of recognizing the actual lowliness of man before God, and thus encourages the ruler to identify with the poor rather than the rich, in keeping with the principle: 'O mankind, ye are the poor before God, and God, He is the Rich, the absolutely Praiseworthy' (35:15). The Imam's own poverty and rigorous austerity were indeed proverbial. That this was far from a mere shunning of the world for its own sake, however, is made clear in the exchange between the Imam and an ascetic, 'Aşim b. Ziyad, whose family complained to the Imam that he was excessively abstemious. The Imam tells him to think of his family and not to cut himself off from the good things (al-tayyibat) that God has permitted. 'Aşim retorts: 'O Commander of the Faithful, and here you are, in your rough clothes and your coarse food!' The Imam replies: 'Woe to you, I am not like you. God, the exalted, has made it incumbent on true leaders (a'immat al-haqq) that they proportion themselves to the weakest among the people (yaqaddiru anfusahum bi-da'fati'l-nas), so that the poverty of the poor will not engender covetousness.' Here is a rigorous expression of the obligation on the most powerful to identify themselves with the most powerless in material terms, so that the poor are not driven to envy. It is difficult to conceive of a more effective way of diminishing worldliness and enhancing spirituality in society than this deliberate lowering of oneself, in purely material terms, to the level of the weakest people one governs. He who could have the most chooses to have the least. The Imam implies that if a leader is to be among the a'immat al-haqq, he must manifest his attachment to al-Haqq — the ultimate spiritual reality in whose light material wealth is of no consequence. Even if the most powerful and the most powerless are equally poor in material terms, both have access to the treasures of the spiritual domain, in proportion to their realization of the truth expressed in the verse cited: 'O mankind, ye are the poor before God, and God, He is the Rich, the absolutely Praiseworthy.' It should also be noted that, on the plane of social psychology, the Imam's insistence that rulers live like the poor — exemplified in his own life — is an effective means of ensuring that the inevitable inequalities of society do not breed envy among the poor.

**The compassion of justice**

Identification with the poor also deepens the ruler's awareness of his own perpetual need of God, helping him to live more fully on divine mercy, in hope and trust; and the natural concomitant of such a life is the extension of mercy and compassion to one's fellow human beings. The capacity to act with compassion in no way conflicts with the demands of justice; rather, it is an intrinsic aspect of justice conceived ontologically — that is, in accordance with the fundamental nature of reality. To act with compassion accords with justice not only in the sense of placing things in their right position — extending compassion wherever and whenever it is called for — but also in the sense of conforming to the true nature of things, to the intrinsic character of the Real. It is for this reason that at the head of every chapter of the Qur'an (except one), the name 'Allah' is followed by 'the infinitely Compassionate, the ever-Merciful' (al-Rahman, al-Rahim). Put differently, the capacity to act with compassion stems not so much from personal sentiment as from an innate affinity with the very nature of divine reality — that higher reality in which, as the Qur'an declares, 'My mercy encompasseth all things' (7:156); in which 'thy Lord has prescribed for Himself mercy' (6:54); and whose essential character is determined by the principle, 'My mercy takes precedence over My wrath.' One also finds many sayings of Ali (ع) on the necessity of showing mercy: 'The dispensing of mercy brings down [divine] mercy.' 'As you grant mercy, so will you be granted mercy.' 'I am astounded by the person who hopes for mercy from one above him, while he is not merciful to those beneath him.'


Neither the rigorous side of the nature of things nor the 'wrathful' side of God is being denied here, but they are clearly subordinated to the higher ontological principle of mercy. There is, in other words, no common measure between the relativities subject to wrath and punishment and the realities — intrinsically beatific — to which divine mercy and compassion give access. One is therefore more 'real' insofar as one's soul reflects this predominance of mercy, both in spiritual orientation and in moral conduct. And in the realm of human society, it is in the very nature of justice, conceived in this sacred sense, to tend toward compassion wherever possible — even though there must also be room for the rigorous application of corrective penalty where that is unavoidable. Indeed, in his letter the Imam goes on to instruct Malik to inflict corporal punishment upon any executive officer found guilty of misappropriating public funds. The Imam's counsels and letters are replete with stern warnings of swift and severe retribution directed at his governors, the severity of those warnings commensurate with his unflinching fidelity to the demands of fairness and honesty in administering public revenues. He writes, for example, to Ziyad b. Abihi, deputy to the governor of Basra and cousin to Ali (ع), Abd Allah b. al-'Abbas:

I swear by God an oath in all sincerity: if news reaches me that you have misappropriated the revenue of the Muslims, whether a small or large amount, I shall inflict a severe punishment upon you, one which will lighten your wealth, burden your back, and degrade your affair.

The Imam was equally severe toward his own relatives, even at the faintest hint of a request for favours that would require the abuse of public funds. In one sermon he recounts an incident in which his blind brother, 'Aqil b. Abi Talib, requested funds from the treasury beyond what he was entitled to. 'He thought I would sell my religion for him,' the Imam said. His response was to bring a red-hot piece of iron close to his brother's body, telling him that by making such a request he was urging Ali (ع) himself to enter a far more intense fire. Returning to the theme of mercy, the following passage is remarkable for situating the necessity of compassion in a universal context. It stands as one of the Imam's most important statements on the unity of the human race and the equality of all human beings, and serves as a direct corrective to every form of prejudice, particularism, and sectarianism that would extend justice or compassion only to members of one's own 'group', however defined:

Infuse your heart with mercy for the subjects, love for them and kindness towards them. Be not like a ravenous beast of prey above them, seeking to devour them. For they are of two types: either your brother in religion or your like in creation. Mistakes slip from them, defects emerge from them, deliberately or accidentally. So bestow upon them your forgiveness and your pardon, just as you would have God bestow upon you His forgiveness and pardon; for you are above them, and the one who has authority over you is above you, and God is above him who appointed you … and through them He tests you.

The universal reach of compassion here is coupled with a reminder of God's absolute sovereignty. No man — whether a governor or the ruler who appointed him — is anything other than a slave of God, utterly dependent upon His mercy. Every person who finds himself in a position of relative superiority over others must therefore constantly remember his own inferiority before the Absolute. That awareness both draws out compassion toward those beneath him and draws down upon himself the compassion of God above him.

One might also cite an incident that illustrates well the Imam's compassionate implementation of justice, and that gives concrete expression to the principle stated above — that all people are 'your like in creation'. The Imam came across an old, blind beggar and inquired about him. He was told the beggar was a Christian. He said to those around him: 'You have employed him until he is old and infirm, and now you refuse to help him. Give him maintenance from the public funds (bayt al-mal).' This response succinctly embodies the Islamic principle of social welfare (maslaha), grounded in redistributive justice and a policy of strict non-discrimination between Muslims and others. Social justice and religious equality flow from compassion understood not merely as a sentiment but as an intrinsic dimension of the Real — and therefore as a fundamental religious duty incumbent upon all Muslims, rulers and citizens alike.

It should be noted that the closing sentence of the passage cited above — warning the governor to regard his power over his subjects not simply as a privilege but as a trial from God — gives clear expression to the Imam's own attitude toward rulership. In the famous sermon al-Shiqshiqiyya, he says that he accepted power only as an unavoidable duty:

Had God not taken from the learned a promise that they would not acquiesce in the rapacity of the tyrant nor in the hunger of the oppressed … I would truly have flung its reins — those of the caliphate — back upon its withers … and you would indeed have discovered that this world of yours is as insignificant to me as that which drips from the nose of a goat.

In this we find a striking embodiment of Plato's ideal attitude toward power — that of the true 'philosopher', who acts as ruler only for the sake of the people, 'not regarding it as a distinction, but as an unavoidable task … caring only for the right and the honours to be gained from that, and above all, for justice as the one indispensable thing in whose service and maintenance they will reorganize their own state.'

The principle underlying this approach to authority and governance is clearly that of noblesse oblige. When one has been granted the grace, blessing, or privilege of knowledge together with a clear sense of justice, a correspondingly greater obligation arises. One's duty to God increases in proportion to the graces one has received. The Imam expresses this in one of his sermons, delivered after a supporter had praised him: 'The blessings of God upon a person do not become immense without the right of God over him becoming immense.'

**'A good opinion of God'**

We now come to a subtle point in the Imam's letter, one that can all too easily be overlooked given the extreme conciseness with which it is expressed, and given that its positive counterpart is left unstated. It arises when the Imam is instructing Malik on the kinds of advisers he should keep close to him. He tells Malik to avoid misers and cowards, and adds: 'Truly, miserliness, cowardice and avarice are so many diverse inclinations comprised within a bad opinion of God.' The phrase 'a bad opinion' (sū' al-zann) evokes a fundamental misunderstanding of the divine nature, arising from a series of misconceptions about God's qualities. Seen from this angle, all human vices stem from an intellectual dysfunction that is itself the consequence of weak faith — or even outright atheism, since disbelief in God is a denial of His reality and thus a kind of 'bad opinion' regarding Him. The converse follows logically: a strong and deep faith generates a 'good opinion' (husn al-zann) of God — an accurate intellectual and contemplative perception of divine Reality — and this good opinion in turn causes the soul to be genuinely impressed, and not merely superficially so, by the divine qualities inherent in that Reality. These qualities must be understood not only as transcendent, being absolutely one with the divine Essence and entirely beyond the reach of the human soul; they must also be grasped as models, exemplars, and perfections for the soul to emulate within the measure of its possibilities. This is, it should be noted, the very opposite of 'comparing oneself with God in greatness'. One must become as 'like' God as possible, but this spiritual development unfolds entirely within the unalterable context of one's servitude to Him. One must be generous because God is al-Karim, the Generous; compassionate because He is al-Rahman, the Compassionate; and so on. Yet one must also remain lowly, humble, and self-effaced, for no degree of virtue or intelligence can diminish the incommensurability between the Absolute and the relative. Virtues such as humility and self-effacement are modes of expressing man's absolute dependence upon God, or of manifesting divine transcendence. Thus between the human and the divine there is both positive and inverse analogy: in terms of the first, 'He who shows not mercy will not have mercy shown to him'; and in terms of the second, as cited above: 'O mankind, ye are the poor before God, and God, He is the Rich, the absolutely Praiseworthy' (35: 15). There must therefore be a synthesis between

the active human virtues — as positive reflections of the divine qualities — and the spiritual awareness of one's own nothingness before the divine Reality, which is the inverted reflection, on the human plane, of divine transcendence. One is at once slave and representative of God, 'abdu'Llah and khalifatu'Llah. Consciousness of servitude ensures that one does not slide — even unconsciously — from representing God into competing with Him in grandeur; while an active commitment to represent God ensures that this awareness of servitude does not slide into forgetfulness of the dignity and responsibilities proper to the human state, thereby producing spiritual paralysis and neglect of one's duty toward the world. The equilibrium between these two dimensions of human existence is itself another aspect of sacred justice — of placing things in their right order — and it attunes the quality of moral action to the harmony of pure being.

**State and society**

The Imam then sets out in his letter a description of the different classes of people within state and society: the army, the scribes, the judiciary, executive officers, tax-collectors, peasants, merchants, artisans, and finally the destitute — each class having its particular rights and duties. The instructions in this part of the letter can be regarded as the cutting edge of justice in actual practice, taking full account of the demands of social and political life. The governor is to regard each class under his authority as bearing both rights and obligations, but the essential character of his relationship to society as a whole is, once again, determined by his primary duty to God — for it is God who has 'prescribed to each class its share and has ordained — as a binding covenant ('ahdan) with us from Him — for each its limits and its duties, according to His Book or the Sunna of the Prophet.'

The governor's duty to society is therefore part of the universal covenant between man and God, the 'ahd so frequently encountered in the Qur'an. By using this Qur'anic term to reinforce the obligation of acting justly toward all classes of society, the Imam ensures that the impact of his words is deepened immeasurably through their resonance with verses such as: 'Is one who knoweth that what is revealed to thee from thy Lord is the truth like one who is blind? But only men of substance take heed, those who abide by the covenant ('ahd) of God, and break not the tryst (mithaq)' (13: 19–20); and 'Approach not the wealth of the orphan except with that which is more right, until he attains maturity; and abide by the covenant — truly, the covenant will be asked about' (17: 34).

It is also significant that, immediately before the instructions cited above, the Imam advises Malik to spend much time with the 'scholars' (al-ulama) and to hold much discourse with the 'sages' (al-hukama). This is to be done in order to 'consolidate that which brings well-being to your lands, and to further entrench that which has already been established by your predecessors.' The distinction between scholars and sages is important. It appears to hint at the difference between formal rules as established in the Qur'an and the Sunna and the spirit behind them — the spirit of wisdom without which those rules cannot be applied with care and sensitivity.

There follows a passage in which the interdependence of each class or segment of society is succinctly described. It opens with the soldiers as the foundation of the state and closes with the governor himself as the centre around which the different elements revolve. Yet this horizontal centre is nothing without the vertical axis that connects it — and thereby the whole of society — to the Real. The soldiers are described as the 'fortresses' of the subjects, and the governor is told, at the close of this passage, that he cannot accomplish what God requires of him without resolute determination and the seeking of God's assistance (al-ihtimam and al-isti'ana). This crucial point is reinforced later in the letter when the needs of the poor are addressed. As regards the instructions pertaining to the different categories of state and society, it suffices here to draw attention to a few salient points. First, concerning the commander of the troops: Malik is told to select for this position the person who is 'the most sincere in relation to God, the Prophet and your Imam, the purest of heart, the one most excellent in forbearance (hilm), who is slow to anger, happy to pardon, kind to the weak, severe with the strong; one who is neither moved by violence, nor held back by weakness.'

This description of the ideal commander, needless to say, reflects the Imam's own character. What is to be noted is the importance placed on kindness, gentleness, and forbearance — qualities not typically associated with fighting men. One observes here a spiritually refined attitude toward an activity that, left to its own devices, runs roughshod over the norms of decency and propriety. One cannot help noticing, once again, the resemblance between the Imam's portrait of the perfect warrior and Plato's description of the 'guardian', the protector of the ideal state. Plato frames the challenge of forming a protective force as follows: how to ensure that men whose natural disposition is spirited and courageous are 'to be kept from behaving pugnaciously to one another and to the rest of their countrymen'? For 'they must be gentle to their own people and dangerous only to their enemies.' This echoes the Qur'anic description of those who are with the Prophet as 'severe against the disbelievers, compassionate amongst themselves' (48: 29) — which is precisely why the Imam places such emphasis on the gentle qualities required in a warrior as the essential complement to the martial virtues.

Turning to the next category, the judges, the Imam identifies the following as the key virtues that must be present in those who dispense legal justice: they must not be confused by complexities or angered by litigants; they must be unflinching in the face of truth, free from greed, dissatisfied with superficial solutions, rigorous in argumentation, steadfast in their search for the truth of every matter, and immune to praise and temptation. 'Such people,' the Imam adds, 'are indeed rare.' He also recommends that judges be paid generously, 'so that any deficiency will be removed, thus diminishing the need for help from people' — a prerequisite for maintaining the incorruptibility of any judiciary.

As regards the administrators, Malik is told to test their honesty before employing them, taking care that no partiality or favouritism influences his choices. He is to continue checking and investigating their conduct. As noted earlier, stringent punishments are to be administered at any evidence of abuse of privilege or misappropriation of public funds.


In relation to those who till the soil and from whom the land tax is collected, the Imam offers this wise maxim: 'Let your concern with the cultivation of the land outweigh your concern with the collection of the tax, for no tax will be collected if there be no cultivation. And whoever exacts the tax without cultivating the land ruins the land and destroys the people.' His further exhortations to attend to the needs of the tillers of the soil, and to be fair to all who seek redress of wrongs, are concluded with the following warning:

The devastation of the land only comes about through the destitution of its inhabitants; and the destitution of its inhabitants only comes about when the desire to amass wealth rules the souls of the governors, when they have doubts about what endures, and when they profit little from exemplary teachings.

As regards the phrase 'having doubts about what endures (su' zannihim bi'l-baqa)', the commentators suggest it may mean either that the governors believe they will endure forever, forgetting that they will die and be judged in the Hereafter, or that they doubt their position will last long and therefore exploit every opportunity while they can. The first explanation seems more likely, given how closely it echoes the following Qur'anic verses, which may themselves be counted among the 'exemplary teachings' (ibra) the Imam mentions at the close of that sentence: 'Woe be to every slandering backbiter, who amasseth wealth and counteth it. He thinketh that his wealth will render him immortal. Nay, but he will be flung to the all-consuming Fire' (104:1–4).

The Imam uses the same word jama'a for the amassing of wealth that appears in this verse. The lesson is plain: greed is a symptom of spiritual myopia. Forgetfulness of what truly endures gives rise to varying degrees of greed — understood here as the desire for a false material richness in place of true spiritual plenitude — and this in turn breeds exploitation and oppression. Once again we see that, in the Imam's view, the vice of injustice not only produces corruption but is itself the product of a chain of causes rooted in a fundamental inversion of spiritual values: a failure to discern the true nature of things.

The advice on the appointment of scribes is similarly sealed with a spiritual message. Malik is told to resist the temptation of appointing only those who have shown him their best side. The governor must examine their prior record and the impression they have made on ordinary people, and appoint only those known for their trustworthiness. Doing all of this, the Imam says, is 'proof of your sincerity towards God.'

Merchants and craftsmen are then described as those who bring diverse benefits to society and must therefore be treated well. On the whole they are peaceable and should be left in peace to do their work, but the vices to which they are prone — hoarding, miserliness, and cheating — must be kept in check.

The poor: those 'most in need of justice'

The Imam now turns to a point in the letter on which he wishes Malik to concentrate fully. He opens the passage with the exclamation 'Allah, Allah' to convey its urgency. The instruction concerns those who have no resources whatsoever — the destitute, the crippled, the orphans, the elderly — those who are 'most in need of justice from you' and who should be treated in a manner such that 'God may excuse you on the day you meet Him.' All of these, together with others who are in need but will not beg, should be helped by the governor; he should appoint an officer with the specific task of bringing the needs of the most destitute to light and providing for them. The Imam then adds these crucial words: 'This is onerous for the governors, and [the fulfilment of] all rights is onerous, but God makes it light for those who aspire to the Hereafter, who restrain their souls in patience, and trust in the truth of that which is promised them by God.'

What deserves emphasis here is that the spiritual element is precisely what makes an otherwise crushing ideal practicable: divine assistance is assured for those whose attention is not confined to this world alone but whose aspirations reach beyond it to the Hereafter. It is only when this world is seen through to the next that a fully just attitude toward this world becomes possible. Those whose talab, or aspiration, does not extend beyond this world will be far more likely to take the easy way out and neglect the destitute — because, on the horizontal scales of political calculation, the costs to a ruler of pursuing a policy of charity toward the poor may well outweigh the benefits to him. This is where an ethical policy tied exclusively to this-worldly appraisal, to pragmatic politics, or to personal interest reveals its inherent flaws. But when the notion of right in relation to the poor is imbued with firm faith in God's inexorable justice and infinite compassion, and suffused with aspiration for the Hereafter, an attitude of unflinching fidelity to the needs of the destitute in society will be generated and continually renewed.

Such an attitude reaches far beyond conventional notions of charity or generosity. The person who has fully realized it will not be vulnerable to any contrary suggestion arising from self-serving pragmatism or individualism, still less from indifference or willfulness. With this attitude firmly in place, the next quality the Imam mentions — patience — follows naturally. For, in the light of a concrete rather than merely notional apprehension of the absolute values at stake, patience can be extended indefinitely: it is no longer dependent on personal will alone but is sustained by the empowering grace that flows from heartfelt faith. The Imam completes this point by referring to those with right aspirations as having, as a consequence, confidence in the truth of God's promise. If that promise — of His reward, His unfailing justice, and His infinite compassion — is true, then the motivation behind the governor's effort to show mercy to, and deal justly with, the poor will be immeasurably deepened. The task the Imam describes as 'onerous' — the obligation to help those who are weak and helpless, those from whose assistance no political benefit may be derived — is transformed into an inescapable duty flowing from one's spiritual conviction. That conviction rests on the certainty that not only is such generosity and compassion good, right, and proper, but that these virtues are in harmony with the true and inalienable substance of ultimate reality. These moral implications of spiritual reality — and equally, the spiritual repercussions of virtue — are hidden from worldly rulers by their covert prejudices at best and their open vices at worst. Such rulers are blind not only to the truth of the divine promise of ultimate felicity but also to the warnings and threats of retribution for the oppressors, al-zalimun. The zalim here is one who disobeys the divine commandments on justice and who, more fundamentally still, violates the quality of compassion that lies at the heart of the divine nature.

Realization of intention

These considerations can be taken further by introducing the altogether crucial question of intention. Even when a just man's efforts to live according to justice are frustrated by the unpredictabilities of worldly life, the right intentions that motivate him — together with his awareness of the immutable divine qualities as models and principles guiding his endeavour — produce a real inward 'taste' of the grace that furnishes the spiritual foundation of his moral life. If God's promise is true and one is absolutely certain of that truth, then its impact on the soul of the sincere believer transforms a personal ethic into an unconditional imperative and a desirable value into an incontrovertible reality. Morality is thus deepened by — and into — spirituality: an existential and all-encompassing mode of being within which the burden of individual 'duty' is not merely lightened immeasurably but is transformed into an accompaniment of one's 'taste' of spiritual reality. One then acts justly out of gratitude for what one has already received — the 'joy of spiritual certainty,' as explored in Chapter 1 — and not only out of hope for something not yet attained.

The Qur'an speaks of the paradisal reward as something already granted to the true believer; the future has already come to pass for one whose certainty of Paradise is absolute: 'We have given thee [the paradisal fountain of] al-Kawthar; so pray to thy Lord and sacrifice ...' (108:1–2). Worshipping God in consequence of this certainty of a beatitude to come — which has thus, in one respect, already come — is well expressed in the following saying of the Imam:

Indeed there is a group who worship God out of desire [for something not yet attained]; and this is the worship of the merchants. And there is a group who worship God out of fear, and this is the worship of the slaves. And there is a group who worship God out of gratitude, and this is the worship of the free.

Furthermore, the just man is liberated from the material consequences of his actions — and this in the measure of the rectitude and sincerity of his intentions, since in the Islamic perspective actions are evaluated not by their consequences but by the intentions behind them. One's intention is to be just, not for the sake of some earthly reward or tangible outcome in this world, but purely for the sake of justice itself; and this essence or principle of justice is in turn inseparable from the divine nature. For justice is at one with God not simply because whatever God commands is just; rather, God commands just acts precisely because they are just, and because that justice is one with His own nature, as we noted at the outset: 'I bear witness that He is Justice and He acts justly.' If God acts justly as a result of His very nature — which is pure justice — the spiritual man will act justly out of a desire to conform to God's nature, and not merely out of obedience to God's commands, and never out of desire for any earthly reward. As the Qur'an says:

The most pious will be far removed from it [the punishment of Hell], he who giveth of his wealth to purify himself. And nobody possesseth, in the sight of such a person, any favour that might be bestowed on him as reward [for his generosity]. [His action is performed] seeking only the Countenance of his transcendent Lord, and he will be content (92:17–21).

<!-- page 180 -->

The same principle is expressed in the description of the righteous, the abrar. The commentators of the Qur'an are agreed that the occasion for the revelation (sabab al-nuzul) of the following verses was when Ali (ع) and his family went three consecutive days without food. At the end of each day, just as they were about to break their fast, someone more in need than themselves came asking for food. The Qur'an relates this as follows: 'They feed, out of love for God, the needy, the orphan and the prisoner, saying, "We feed you only for the sake of God; we desire from you neither reward nor thanks"' (76:8–9).

In the very measure that a good action is performed for the sake of that absolute goodness which God is, the person performing it remains unshakeably content, untouched by others' blame or praise, neither deflated by outward adversity that thwarts his good actions nor inflated by their successful outcomes. This understanding of the relation between action and intention helps us appreciate the Imam's saying cited above: 'their hearts are in the Gardens [of Paradise] while their bodies are at work.' Here again we see the contrast between a secular ethic of justice — one stripped of all spiritual or transcendental points of reference — and a spiritual conception of justice. A secular ethic stands or falls according to how successfully it realizes its ideal of justice in the world; a spiritual conception already sees the fulfilment of its ideal in the sincere intention to be just. Yet that fulfilment still requires an unyielding struggle to achieve justice in practice, for without such struggle the sincerity of the intention is called into question. Without concordant action, aspirations and intentions remain shallow and purely theoretical. To hold an intention with total sincerity demands diligent and ceaseless striving toward its realization. Therefore, however formidable the obstacles, however compelling the arguments of political pragmatism, however thoroughly one's strenuous efforts are undone by the unavoidable pressures of outward life, the just man never despairs and never abandons the effort to achieve justice — precisely because achieving justice in this world is not the whole of his aim. He knows that the intention is already, in an invisible but spiritually palpable way, its own fulfilment; and he knows, with sober realism, that the outward realization of any intention depends on circumstances beyond his control and ultimately on the grace of God.

This may appear, on the surface, to excuse the failure to achieve justice in society — and might therefore be exploited as a subterfuge by which actual injustice is legitimized by appealing to good but thwarted intentions. In fact the very opposite is true. Far from implying any weakening of resolve in one motivated by a sacred conception of justice, such a person's efforts will be as ceaseless and unyielding as the eternal and unfailing reward in which he fervently believes. Those efforts will also be sustained by the consciousness of the subtle continuity between just intentions and the divine quality of justice — and thereby the divine nature itself. An inalienable sense of the sacred, then, sustains the just man in his pursuit of justice. This is not to say that all secular or atheistic conceptions of justice are without value; only that, compared with a sacred conception of justice, they will lack the immeasurable and inexhaustible spiritual resources nourished by sincere faith. 'With men this is impossible, but with God all things are possible,' as Jesus says in the Gospel. Applied to the present context, the principle might be stated as follows: when justice is conceived in isolation from spiritual principles, personal idealism is acutely vulnerable to outward contingencies and all too likely to crumble under the pressure of difficulties and disappointments; in a sacred conception of justice, by contrast, divine grace upholds the incorruptible ideal of justice, holding it far above the frailties and vicissitudes to which both the human soul and human society are subject. To the extent that one's ideals are rooted in spiritual knowledge, one's susceptibility to disappointment and despair in the face of difficulties is diminished.

<!-- page 181 -->

The salience of this ideal of justice is clearly visible in the Imam's own experience as caliph: a continuous series of bloody conflicts, waged against adversaries who professed Islam, prevented him outwardly from putting into effect many of the principles and policies that flowed from his understanding of truth and justice — yet none of this produced dejection, despair, or cynicism, nor the slightest diminution in his efforts to achieve justice whenever and wherever possible, as is amply demonstrated in the character of his own administration and in the stream of letters and counsels to his officers and governors, including the one presently under consideration. Such despair is almost inconceivable for one whose inner life is shaped by the conviction that 'No act is negligible if it is accompanied by piety.' For such a person, despair is excluded by piety in the deepest sense of the term — that is, the permanent awareness of divine Reality — for this degree of piety is the makhraj, the 'way out,' from the darkness of all earthly injustice. As the Imam says: 'He who has pious awe towards God — for him God establishes a way out of tribulation, and a light out of darkness (man yattaqi'Llah yaj'al lahu makhrajan min al-fitan wa nuran min al-zulam).'

### The Virtue Of Worship

We now come to what is arguably the lynchpin of the entire epistle — the most important means by which the sacred substance of all the virtues is assimilated by the ruler, namely, prayer. We saw at the beginning of the letter how the Imam opened with a counsel to observe the obligatory prayers. At this point, after stressing the need to perform all administrative tasks at their proper times, the Imam instructs Malik to set apart the 'most excellent' portion of his available time for 'what is between you and God' — that is, acts of worship and devotion. Malik is urged to pay special attention to the obligatory prayers as the means by which he 'purifies' his religion for God, or renders his devotion to God sincere. This instruction should not be read merely as an exhortation to perform the formal prayers; it must above all be understood in light of the deeper meaning of 'purifying one's religion for God' — that is, purifying the very substance of the relationship between the soul and God, for it is the depth of that relationship which determines the quality of all human virtue. As the Imam put it in the saying noted earlier: 'Know that your every act is dependent upon your prayer.' What matters is not only the formal, physical, and verbal accomplishment of prayer, but the inward quality that accompanies it and enlivens it from within. The sincerity, intensity, and frequency of prayer are essential to its efficacy. The Imam continues: 'Give unto God of your vital energy in your nights and your days, and perform fully that by which you draw near to God, doing so perfectly, without becoming dull or deficient, taking your body to its limits (balighan min badanika mā balagha).'

All the emphasis here should fall on the word taqarrub — 'drawing near'. One attains divine nearness most directly through deep devotion, offered in solitude, in the still hours of the night, with one's whole heart. Once that nearness is realized, all of one's other actions in the world become suffused with something of the spiritual quality gained through divine proximity. It is in this way that all action can be assimilated to 'devotion' in its wider sense, every outward deed truly governed by the sincere intention to give oneself to God. If one's inner life is dominated by prayer, prayer comes to penetrate one's outer life as well. Hence, as the Imam said immediately after telling Malik to set apart the best of his time for God: 'all times — and the actions performed within them — are for God, if the intention underlying them is good and if your subjects derive security as a consequence.' In other words, all of a ruler's actions partake of the sacred to the degree that his devotion truly enters the source of the sacred. One can then envision an overflow, as it were, from the fountain of obligatory and voluntary prayer into the outward domain of action. This subtle truth is beautifully expressed in the famous hadith qudsi of the Prophet, often called the hadith of taqarrub:

My slave draws near to Me through nothing I love more than that which I have made obligatory for him. My slave never ceases to draw near to Me through supererogatory acts until I love him. And when I love him, I am his hearing by which he hears, his sight by which he sees, his hand by which he grasps, and his foot by which he walks. 107

To 'see' and act through God can be understood, at one level at least, as seeing things as they truly are and then acting upon that vision — in other words, it implies perfect justice, placing each thing in its rightful position. It is worth noting in the hadith qudsi above the distinction between obligatory and supererogatory acts of devotion: both are means of 'drawing near', but it is primarily through the latter that the slave comes to perceive and act through the divine. It is equally important to note the Imam's phrase 'taking your body to its limits'. Strenuous effort is called for here; it is far from sufficient to perform the prayers and devotions in a mechanical or perfunctory way. 'He who prays without making an effort,' says the Imam, 'is like one who shoots arrows without a bow.' As noted earlier, the sincerity (ikhlas) demanded by the Imam's understanding of divine oneness calls for the integration of the soul, and it is in prayer that the soul's deepest dimensions are expressed, cultivated, and unified. The prayer must therefore be accomplished not merely with one's tongue or body, but with all that one is.

Elsewhere, Ali (ع) points to the key supererogatory practice by which the spiritual station of 'nearness' is attained — doing so in a commentary on the following Qur'anic words: '... men whom neither commerce nor trade diverts from the remembrance of God' (24:36).

Truly God has made the remembrance (al-dhikr) a polish for the hearts, by which they hear after suffering from deafness, and see after being blind ... There have always been slaves of God ... with whom He held intimate discourse through their thoughts and spoke with them through the essence of their intellects. They diffused illumination through the awakened light in their hearing and their seeing and their hearts, calling unto the remembrance of the days of God.

The importance of dhikr — both as a principle of awareness of ultimate reality and as a concrete means of attaining that awareness — is clearly of the utmost significance in the Imam's perspective. It should be stressed that by the word dhikr the Imam implies a methodic practice of invoking a divine Name, as well as a principle of permanent God-awareness embracing all modes of worship, meditation, and reflection. It is to be understood not only as being aware of God or remembering God, but as an intense mode of prolonged, prayerful meditation on ultimate reality, represented both symbolically and sacramentally by one or more of the divine Names. (This dimension of remembrance will be addressed more fully in the following chapter.) The relationship between the remembrance of God and the cultivation of justice is made explicit in this sermon. The Imam describes those illuminated by the dhikr as follows:

Truly there are people who belong to the dhikr; they have adopted it in place of the world, such that 'neither commerce nor trade' distracts them from it; they spend the days of their life in it ... they instruct people about justice and themselves are steadfast therein.

It is those souls who truly 'belong to the dhikr' who are not only 'steadfast' in justice, but able to 'instruct people about justice' — putting first things first, they can show how to place all things in their proper order. Fervent, lived spirituality is therefore not opposed to dedicated activity in the world; rather, it infuses that activity with an ethical depth that springs from direct contact with the divine, a contact articulated through prayer in general and through dhikr in particular. Just as it was seen earlier that the sincerity of a good intention is proven by the effort to realize it, so it is clear now that the sincerity of prayer is likewise proven and rendered efficacious through the sustained effort to prolong and intensify it — 'taking your body to its limits'. What this has to do with justice should be clear: if justice is grasped as a quality rooted in the Real (al-Haqq), then one's nearness to the Real cannot but draw one nearer to justice and to all positive qualities — spiritual, intellectual, and moral. The relationship between worship and justice must therefore be firmly established if the conception of justice is to be genuinely 'sacred'. From this perspective, justice is both cause and consequence of worship. On one hand, justice requires us to give everything its due, and so God must be given His due — which entails, sacramentally, worship and prayer; on the other hand, justice emerges, ethically, as a consequence of that worship, as do all the virtues, in proportion to the 'nearness' granted by God to His worshipper and in accordance with the radiant repercussions of that nearness.

Gazing on the Good

We have made occasional references above to similarities between principles expressed in the Imam's letter and those discussed in Plato's The Republic. This chapter can be brought to a close by proposing that what the Imam gives us in his stress on prayer — together with its essence, the remembrance of God — is a devotional principle that illuminates and, in a sense, sublimates Plato's philosophical position, and might be seen as rendering explicit in spiritual terms what is only implied in Plato's pedagogy. After discussing justice at great length, Socrates arrives, in Chapter 23 of The Republic, at the crux of the matter: the nature of the Sovereign Good, which is the primary concern of the ideal ruler — the 'guardian' — whose moral and intellectual formation lies at the heart of the dialogue's practical dimension:

'... the highest object of knowledge is the essential nature of the Good, from which everything that is good and right derives its value for us ... I need not tell you that, without that knowledge, to know everything else, however well, would be of no value to us ...'

Yet Socrates declines to respond to Glaucon's entreaties to describe the nature of this Good, offering instead to speak of its 'offspring'. This leads him to draw an analogy between the Good and the Sun: what the Sun is to visibility, the Good is to intelligibility and being. He then proceeds with the famous allegory of the cave, followed by a description of the educational programme that would turn the attention of his prospective guardians from the world of shadows to the world of real objects — a programme comprising mathematics, arithmetic, geometry, solid geometry, astronomy, harmonics, and dialectic. Having successfully passed through all stages of their education, 'when they are fifty, those who have come safely through and proved the best at all points in action and in study must be brought at last to the goal. They must lift up the eye of the soul to gaze on that which sheds light on all things; and when they have seen the Good itself, take it as a pattern for the right ordering of the state and of the individual, themselves included.' Yet Plato nowhere specifies what this 'gazing' actually consists in, or by what means — intellective, contemplative, or methodic — it is to be achieved.

At an important moment in The Republic, Glaucon presses Socrates directly: 'But, Socrates, what is your account of the Good? Is it knowledge, or pleasure, or something else?' Socrates refuses to answer, saying after further discussion, 'I am afraid it is beyond my powers; with the best will in the world I should only disgrace myself and be laughed at.' It is interesting to note in this connection that Dhi'lib al-Yamani, very much in the manner of Glaucon, provocatively asked Ali (ع) whether he had seen the object of his devotion. The Imam replied: 'I would not be worshipping a lord whom I have not seen.' Dhi'lib then asked, 'O Commander of the Faithful! How did you see Him?' The Imam replied with the significant sentence that serves as one of the leitmotifs of this book: 'O Dhi'lib! Eyes see Him not through sight's observation, but hearts see Him through the verities of faith.'

We are reminded here of the 'polishing' of the heart effected by remembrance, and this very word evokes the essence of Plato's approach to knowledge as 'recollection', anamnesis. Yet, as noted above, Plato does not in any of his dialogues make direct reference to the devotional, methodic, or contemplative counterpart of his educational, ethical, and intellective endeavour. There is no explicitly postulated link between 'remembrance' of the 'forms' or 'ideas' that engender true knowledge and the 'gazing upon the Good' — the contemplation of 'that which sheds light on all things' — in other words, not merely knowing or remembering the 'forms', but knowing the 'Bestower of Forms', referred to in Islam by the divine Name al-Musawwir. There is speculation as to whether any spiritual method existed in Plato's teaching, and if it did, why he chose not to divulge it. One answer can be inferred from a passage in Plato's famous Seventh Letter, where he denies that any exposition of philosophy's true reality can be given, and

points instead to the 'disciplined way of life' and rigorous 'daily routine' which the subject demands if philosophy is to be realized in its deepest sense. 'No treatise by me concerning it exists or ever will exist. It is not something that can be put into words like other branches of learning; only after long partnership in a common life devoted to this very thing does truth flash upon the soul, like a flame kindled by a leaping spark, and once it is born there, it nourishes itself thereafter.' This brings us back to the importance of active spirituality for the practice of authentic virtue. If the Real — indefinable in its essence — is the foundation of all good, then the means of assimilating the Real existentially, rather than merely comprehending it mentally, cannot be dismissed. Plato's philosophical approach to the Good can be augmented and deepened by an appreciation of Ali (ع)'s means of 'drawing near' to the Real, for in the Imam's perspective intellectual comprehension and spiritual devotion are inseparable and mutually reinforcing. Integral, deep-rooted justice emerges as one of the fruits of this 'drawing near', just as, for Plato, gazing upon 'that which sheds light on all things ... the Good itself' issues in the capacity to take the Good 'as a pattern for the right ordering of the state and of the individual'.

In this chapter we have attempted to sketch the outlines of a spiritual approach to the theme of justice as it presents itself principally in the Imam's letter to Malik al-Ashtar. We are fully aware that we have only skimmed the surface of this important subject, but hope nonetheless to have indicated at least that the Imam's illumination of the principle of justice — though addressed in the first instance to a prospective governor of Egypt in the first century of the Islamic era — is by no means outmoded. Whether one operates in an avowedly Islamic or an overtly secular environment, whether rule is autocratic or democratic, whether political institutions are strong or weak: wherever individuals are vested with power and authority — and by that very fact exposed to the temptations power offers — the approach to justice articulated in the Imam's ethos remains relevant. For this ethos presupposes no particular form of government; it engages the universal theme of moral responsibility, but does so in terms of that which transcends morality itself, offering insights derived from a direct vision of ultimate spiritual realities. The principles contemplated through that vision surpass the domain of politics and, for precisely that reason, are capable of penetrating deeply into, and refashioning from above, the moral substance of political consciousness. Thus, although the Imam's perspective is especially pertinent to the interface between spirituality, morality, and governance within Islamic discourse and practice, its relevance extends to every context in which a sense of the sacred plays a part in the articulation of moral sensibility and the cultivation of political responsibility.

We also hope to have demonstrated that the Imam's letters and discourses constitute a vast and largely untapped source of inspired ideas capable of illuminating the role of spiritual principles in forming ethical values and shaping the quality of political practice. In particular, it should be clear that an integral 'Islamic' approach to politics and society must take into account both the requirements and the fruits of 'worship' — understood in its widest sense. Sincere worship transforms the notion of the Absolute into a concrete reality that imparts to the soul an existential impetus and a spiritual resource that buttresses, stabilizes, and deepens one's moral life. What is derived from our consciousness of divine Reality is not so much a philosophy of ethics as a 'spiritual morality' — an existential orientation that is simultaneously the consequence of a lived spirituality and the cause of a continuous, dynamic, and ever-deepening realization of spiritual truth: the Real as such, al-Haqq. This means that the haqq — the right due to each person or group — will be given, in accordance with the principle of justice, by 'putting each thing in its proper place'. Virtue, in such a perspective, is a form of 'ethical realism' — understanding by 'realism' an assimilation of spiritual reality — and morally correct behaviour translates that realism into action across all planes: individual, social, and political.


If it be objected that this perspective is hopelessly utopian — that the harsh exigencies of politics and the sublime ideals of spirituality are poles apart — the response must be this: if no effort is made to comprehend the deeper dimensions of Islamic piety and spirituality, then any ostensibly 'Islamic' discourse, whether in politics, sociology, ethics or any other field, will be reduced to an outer shell, stripped of the inner life that gives it meaning and, more importantly, its transformative power. For Islamic concepts to be properly grasped, they must be assimilated not merely by the mind but by the heart — failing which, they risk becoming pure abstraction. As the Imam insists, it is the heart — the inmost point of one's consciousness — that 'sees' the true nature of things. Without this vision of God 'as if one could see Him', there is no ihsan in the ethico-spiritual sense discussed earlier; and without ihsan, the very integrity of the Islamic way of life is fatally undermined. This has been amply demonstrated by a tiny minority of Muslims in our own time who betray the highest ideals of the Islamic ethical tradition by resorting to unbridled violence in its name. Without ihsan, religion is left in a position of acute vulnerability: it becomes an ideology, driven by individual passion and subject to political manipulation. It comes as no surprise that those most prone to terrorism in the name of Islam have emerged from the currents most hostile to the ihsanī or mystical tradition.

This brings us back to the devotional practices that are all too often regarded as the preserve of a minority — viewed as either mystical or misguided, depending on one's standpoint — rather than being recognised as God-given means of closing the gap between what one aspires to and what one actually is; of moving from the actual toward the ideal; of giving concrete form to what would otherwise remain abstract; of living what is merely thought; and of rendering existentially galvanising what might otherwise remain only theoretically compelling. While the principles set out here are universal in their import and applicability, it would be a mistake to try to 'de-contextualise' the Imam's perspective — if that means dismissing as superfluous his repeated, insistent and uncompromising emphasis on the imperative of prayer. The principle of justice requires that every dimension of one's life — devotion, worship, ethics, action — be bound together in a harmonious, indissoluble and interconnected whole, in accordance with the ideal of tawhid. The domain of the virtues cannot be detached from that of spirituality, for in the Imam's perspective the only true virtue is one that is rooted in, sustained by, and oriented toward the divine Reality. One must therefore conclude that justice, together with all the other virtues, can only come to full fruition in a soul — and in a society — that is permeated by spirituality, the essence of which is the principle and practice of the remembrance of God. In the final analysis, it is the remembrance of God that determines the quality of human virtue. It is toward the spiritual realization that this remembrance intends and cultivates that the following chapter is addressed.

## Realization through Remembrance: Ali (ع) and the Mystical Tradition of Islam

This chapter explores the meaning of dhikru'Llah — 'the remembrance of God' — one of the most fundamental principles in Islamic spirituality. It does so by focusing on the Imam's sayings, understood essentially as commentaries on the Qur'anic notion of dhikru'Llah, while also engaging with relevant doctrinal formulations that have emerged from within the mystical traditions of Islam. At the outset of this book it was stated that to speak of Ali (ع) is to speak of the quintessential spirituality of the Islamic faith. His influence can be felt wherever spirituality has flourished in Islam — whether in the more clearly defined spiritual disciplines of Sufism and Shi'i 'Irfan, or in the realms of futuwwa (spiritual chivalry), the craft guilds, music, poetry and the arts.1 His presence, as the 'gate' to the city of prophetic wisdom, leaves its mark on all the noblest expressions of the spirit of Islam. Within the Sufi tradition in particular, one discerns an especially organic connection with the Imam. For whether they be Shi'i or Sunni, Arab or Persian, Turk or Malay, Mauritanian or Bosnian, the Sufi orders are united in the figure of Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع), who stands at the summit of all the chains of affiliation (salasila, sing. silsila) through which the Sufi masters trace their spiritual lineage back to the Prophet. Ali (ع)'s unifying role is equally apparent with respect to the most significant division within Islam — that between Sunnism and Shi'ism. Beyond the vast common ground shared by the two traditions in essential beliefs and practices, these two branches of Islam are brought together in a particularly direct and existential manner by their shared orientation toward sanctity (walaya) and gnosis (ma'rifa) as the goal of the religious life. In this connection, Seyyed Hossein Nasr writes: 'Islamic esotericism or gnosis crystallized into the form of Sufism in the Sunni world, while it poured into the whole structure of Shi'ism, especially during its early period.' Thus the esoteric core of Islam expresses itself in Sunni Sufism and in Shi'ite 'Irfan with different 'accents', taking on different forms and expressions, without this spiritual attunement to the essence of the Islamic message of tawhid being fundamentally altered. Indeed, according to one of the great figures in whom Shi'ism and Sufism are united — Sayyid Haydar Āmulī (d. 787/1385) — the true Shi'i cannot but be a 'Sufi' if he plumbs the mystical depths of the Imams' teachings; conversely, the Sufi is a Shi'i if he comes to recognise that the most important roots of Sufism lie in the Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt, the first poles (aqtab, sing. qutb) of the spiritual tradition through whom the mystical blessings of the Prophet were transmitted in an initiatic context. For Āmulī, the outward differences between the two traditions are dissolved in the fire of walaya — sanctity, the true substance and goal of both Sufism and Shi'ism alike. And for both traditions, Ali (ع) is the wali Allah, the 'friend and saint of God', par excellence.

The following passage by Ali (ع) Hujwirī (d. 464/1071) — author of the first manual of Sufism written in Persian, the Kashf al-mahjub — may be taken as wholly representative of how Ali (ع) is regarded within the Sufi tradition:

Among them [i.e. the Sufis] is the son of Muhammad's uncle, he who drowned in the ocean of trying affliction and was consumed in the fire of loving passion (ghariq-i bahr-i bala' wa hariq-i nar-i wala') — the leader of the saints and the pure ones (muqtada-yi awliya' wa'l-asfiya'). In this Path he holds a place of tremendous honour and elevated degree. He had complete mastery over the precise expression of the principles of the supernal realities, such that Junayd said of him, 'Ali is our master as regards principles and affliction (shaykhuna fi'l-usul wa'l-bala')' … That is, Ali (ع) is the Imam of this Path in respect of both theoretical knowledge and practical application (andar 'ilm wa mu'amalat imam-i in tariqat), for the adepts of Sufism call the knowledge of this Path its 'principles', while its practice is summed up in the endurance of affliction.

The mention of 'the endurance of affliction' recalls the Imam's reference to the sages as those who 'befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged', noted in Chapter 1 above. One possible meaning of this elliptical phrase, as suggested earlier, is that among the things the sages embrace and the ignorant flee is prayer — and more specifically, the act of pure concentration on the Absolute, to the exclusion of all other thoughts. This is a kind of death: the ego and all its preoccupations are, as it were, sacrificed before the divine Reality on which concentration is exclusively focused. The remembrance of God, the focus of this chapter, may be understood both as the consummation of such concentration and as the path leading to the summit of spiritual realisation: self-effacement before the sole reality of God.

The corpus of Ali (ع)'s teachings illuminates in a remarkable way the notion, the principle and the practice of dhikru'Llah — a theme of central importance in both the Qur'an and the Sunna of the Prophet. The Imam's sayings constitute a valuable commentary on the Qur'anic verses relating to the dhikr; and, given the centrality of the dhikr within Sufi practice and doctrine, these sayings also help one appreciate why the Sufis regard the Imam as the first spiritual Pole (qutb) of Sufism after the Prophet himself. The close relationship between remembrance and spiritual realisation will be explored in the light of both the Imam's sayings and the expositions of representatives of the Sufi tradition — particularly those belonging to the school of Ibn al-'Arabi, known in the tradition as al-Shaykh al-akbar, 'the Greatest Master', on account of his preponderant influence on subsequent expressions of Sufi thought.2 These expositions may be read as doctrinal reverberations of the dense, often elliptical pronouncements of the Imam. The Imam's sayings, together with the Sufis' elaborations on the meaning and implications of 'the remembrance of God', bring key aspects of Islamic spirituality into sharp focus. Before proceeding, one must first consider what the term dhikr actually means and why it occupies such a prominent place in Islamic devotional practice. The term is extraordinarily rich, and no single English word can capture its full range of meanings. Lexically it encompasses: remembrance, recollection, invocation, repetition, incantation, recitation; it can also mean memory, mention, telling, relating (as of a story), praise, glorification, celebration, fame, eminence, honour, and exhortation or warning; it may likewise denote the Qur'an itself,3 or any book of revelation or religion. Yet the definition that brings us closest to the spiritual principle the term expresses is that given by al-Raghib al-Isfahānī in his Qur'anic dictionary. He writes that dhikr is that by means of which 'man preserves that knowledge (ma'rifa) which he has attained, so it is akin to memory (hifz), except that memory is spoken of in respect of its function of acquisition (ihraz), whereas dhikr is spoken of in respect of its function of rendering present (istihdar). Dhikr is also used to refer to the presence of a thing in one's consciousness (lit. "heart") or in one's speech. Thus, it is said that there are two types of dhikr: dhikr of the heart and dhikr of the tongue, and each comprises two further kinds: a dhikr which follows forgetfulness, and a dhikr which does not follow forgetfulness but which, on the contrary, expresses a continuous remembering.'

Much in this definition of dhikr resonates with the Imam's perspective, as will be seen later. What must be stressed at this point is that when the thing remembered — or 'rendered present' to consciousness — is God, the importance of the principle of dhikru'Llah becomes self-evident. Dhikru'Llah should therefore be understood, in the first instance and above all else, as a principle of awareness, a deep assimilation of the divine presence; but it must equally be understood as an action, a practice or series of practices,10 and hence as a spiritual discipline. In other words, dhikru'Llah can be conceived both as the end and as a means to that end — at once spiritual awareness and the methodical practice oriented toward that awareness. It is within the Sufi tradition that the relationship between the practice of the dhikr

and the principle of consciousness that the practice is intended to cultivate has been most fully elaborated. The dhikr is presented both as a contemplative discipline centred on the methodical invocation or repetition of a Name of God, and as the cognitive consequence and mystical fruit of that discipline.

The dhikr is thus the spiritual method par excellence of the Sufis — a discipline that works toward the realisation and deep assimilation, within the heart, of what is present to the mind, thereby transforming an abstract, notional grasp of certain ideas into a concrete perception of spiritual realities. Insofar as dhikru'Llah is conceived as the permanent awareness of the divine Reality, it can be presented as the very quintessence of religion as such — that which 're-binds' (re-ligare) the human being to God, and thus the raison d'être of all religious rites and practices. If prayer constitutes the core of religious practice, then dhikru'Llah is, as the Qur'an states simply, akbar — that is, 'greater' or 'greatest': 'Truly, prayer keepeth [one] away from lewdness and iniquity, and the remembrance of God is greater' (29:45).

Numerous sayings of the Prophet attest to the pre-eminence of the remembrance of God. It is related, for example, that the Prophet asked his companions: 'Shall I not tell you about the best and purest of your works for your Lord, the most exalted of them in rank, and the work that is better for you than giving silver and gold, and better for you than encountering your enemy — you striking their necks and they striking yours?' Those addressed replied: 'What is that, O emissary of God?' He said: 'The perpetual invocation of God — exalted and glorious (dhikru'Llah 'azza wa jalla da'iman).'

Likewise, Ali (ع) affirms: 'Perpetuate the dhikr, for truly it illumines the heart, and it is the most excellent form of worship (huwa afdal al-'ibada).' The performance of dhikr is accorded great importance in the Shi'i tradition, which preserves many sayings of the early Imams on the virtues of invoking the Names of God and various sacred phrases. Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 148/765) described his followers in terms of dhikr: 'Our Shi'a are those who, when alone (idha khala'u), invoke and remember God much.' The khalwa — the spiritual retreat in which one seeks to be 'alone' with God — is described as follows by 'Allama Tabataba'ī, one of the most important masters of Shi'i gnosis in the twentieth century: 'As to the particular spiritual retreat (khalwat-i khass), it is distancing oneself from people … it is especially required during certain types of verbal invocations (adhkar-i kalamiyya) … and is considered essential by the masters of the path.'

As regards the Sufi tradition, the following statement by Shaykh Ahmad al-'Alawi (d. 1934) on the dhikr may be taken as altogether representative:

Remembrance is the mightiest rule of the religion … The law was not enjoined upon us, neither were the rites of worship ordained, but for the sake of establishing the remembrance of God … In a word, our performance of the rites of worship is considered strong or weak according to the degree of our remembrance while performing them.


<!-- page 218 -->

Al-Ghazālī poses the question of why the dhikr should be the most meritorious practice when it is so simple to perform compared to all the other acts of worship, which are so demanding. He replies that a full understanding of this superiority can only be reached through the 'science of unveiling' (*'ilm al-mukashafa*). What can be said from the standpoint of the practical religious sciences (*'ilm al-mu'amala*), however, is that the most efficacious dhikr is that which is performed continually with the presence of the heart (*hudur al-qalb*). This is the aim of the dhikr, and it is this quality that 'takes precedence over all acts of worship; nay, it is through this that the other acts of worship are dignified; this is the ultimate fruit of the practical acts of worship.' One is therefore justified in asserting that the principle of dhikru'Llah touches the highest levels of religious practice, contemplative discipline, and spiritual realization — and thus the very heart of Islamic spirituality. Despite all their variety across time and place, their divergent perspectives and emphases, the Sufis are unanimous on the centrality of the dhikru'Llah to their way. Likewise, as seen above, despite all their differences of name and orientation, school of thought or mode of practice, the Sufi orders are united in the pivotal figure of Ali (ع) as the one who connects all later masters of the spiritual path to the Prophet himself. This convergence between the Imam and the dhikru'Llah in the Sufi tradition is far from accidental. The Imam's elucidation of the meaning of the dhikr — together with his actual initiation of disciples into its methodic practice — constitutes a key aspect of his role as spiritual guide, the *walī Allāh*, or the Pole (*qutb*) of his age; it is thus central to his esoteric function within the spiritual tradition of Islam in both its Sunni and Shi'i manifestations.

<!-- page 219 -->

Regarding the Imam's esoteric function, it is true that we have little historically firm information about his initiatic role in this domain — that is, his actual initiation of disciples into the practice of invocation (known in the tradition as *talqīn*) — just as we have precious little about his own initiation into the esoteric practice by the Prophet. This is hardly surprising, given that the practice is, precisely, esoteric; it is not enjoined upon all (the exoteric majority) as an obligatory rite. Rather, within both Sufism and Shi'i 'Irfān, the invocation is bestowed upon those with a particular vocation — those who have received a calling to practise something beyond the obligatory rites binding on all. Nonetheless, there are historical accounts that provide prima facie evidence of the Imam's role in transmitting the dhikr. The individuals described in the earliest sources as devoted to the dhikr as a particular practice were known as close associates, if not disciples, of the Imam. His role in this initiatic domain is emphasized in the orally transmitted sources of the Sufi way and is also attested in several written sources, which record reports of the transmission of the dhikr from the Prophet to the Imam, and from him to several disciples who went on to initiate others into what later came to be called the way of *taṣawwuf*.

<!-- page 220 -->

Despite the scarcity of historically sound material on the earliest period of the dhikru'Llah's use and transmission, we do have the extremely important literary evidence provided by the sayings recorded in the *Nahj al-balāgha* and the *Ghurar al-hikam*, as well as in the supplications attributed to the Imam. Taken together, these sayings form a coherent corpus — a distinct body of doctrine — that offers the researcher a rich source of philosophical reflection, spiritual meditation, and metaphysical insight into the nature and meaning of a practice occupying so central a place in the mystical tradition of Islam.

The sayings examined below shed light not only on the meaning of the dhikr as a practice, but also on the philosophy and epistemology underlying it; and they offer precious intimations of the fruits of that practice — its consummation in spiritual realization. Particular attention will be given to the way in which the Imam's sayings, on one hand, constitute an important source of commentary on the Qur'anic verses relating to this theme, and on the other, present a clear foreshadowing of the more elaborate doctrines pertaining to the dhikr found in the later Sufi and 'Irfānī traditions. The Imam's sayings in this domain thus serve as a kind of bridge, connecting what came before him — prophetic revelation — to what came after him, in the form of mystical elaboration. The texts and manuals of the mystical tradition seem to make explicit what was more often than not implicit in the Imam's sayings. In other words, the mystics elaborated on the esoteric truths alluded to by the Imam in sayings that were concise, often paradoxical and elliptical, but always intellectually demanding and spiritually fruitful. Reflection on these sayings — read in the context of the Qur'an and the Sunna that they illuminate, and in light of the later Sufi doctrine they anticipate — can bring one closer to a proper understanding of the role of dhikru'Llah in the practical, operative, and visionary dimensions of Islamic spirituality.

### Dhikru'Llah As A Polish For Hearts

<!-- page 221 -->

The most fitting place to begin this discussion is the Imam's comment on the dhikr in sermon 213 of the *Nahj al-balāgha*. The Imam first quotes words from verse 37 of Sūra 24, entitled *al-Nūr* ('The Light'), in which the invocation of God's Name is mentioned, and then proceeds to comment on them. The words in question are: '… men whom neither trade nor merchandise diverts from the remembrance of God'. Since this fragment of the verse is organically linked with the preceding 'verse of light' — the *āyat al-nūr*, so rich in mystical symbolism — the Imam's comment carries considerably deeper resonance. The Imam clearly knew that his recitation of these words from verse 37 would call to his listeners' minds the verses preceding them; his deliberate omission of the light verse in full only heightens the rhetorical effect of the ellipsis, for such hints, implications, evocations, and allusions are characteristic of Arabic *balāgha*. To understand the context of the Imam's words, it is worth citing the light verse in full:

God is the light of the heavens and the earth. A similitude of His light is a niche wherein is a lamp; the lamp is enclosed in a glass; the glass is as it were a shining star. [The lamp] is lit [by the oil of] a blessed olive tree, neither of the East nor of the West. The oil well-nigh shineth forth, though fire touch it not. Light upon light! God guideth to His light whom He will; and God striketh similitudes for mankind; and God knoweth all things (24:35).

After this verse come the following two:

In houses which God has allowed to be elevated, and that His Name be remembered therein, He is glorified in the mornings and in the evenings by men whom neither trade nor merchandise diverteth from the remembrance of God, and from establishing the prayer, and from the bestowing of alms … (24:36–7).

<!-- page 222 -->

After reciting the words 'men whom neither trade nor merchandise diverteth from the remembrance of God', the Imam begins his sermon:

Truly, God has made the remembrance (*al-dhikr*) a polish for the hearts, by which they hear after being deaf, and see after being blind, and yield after being resistant … There have always been slaves of God … with whom He held intimate discourse through their thoughts and spoke with them through the essence of their intellects. They diffused illumination through the awakened light in their hearing and their seeing and their hearts, calling unto the remembrance of the days of God … Indeed, there is a special group (*ahl*) who belong to the dhikr; they have adopted it in place of the world, such that 'neither trade nor merchandise' distracts them from it. They spend the days of their life in it … It is as though they had left this world for the Hereafter, and they are there, witnessing what is beyond this world …

In this passage it is important to note the Imam's description of the dhikr as a 'polish for hearts' (*jalā'an li'l-qulūb*), alongside the reference to the 'awakened light' in the hearing, sight, and hearts of those who have applied this polish. The relationship between the illumination of the heart and the light verse is transparent. The latent light of the heart is disclosed by the remembrance of God; the light within shines together with the light from on high: 'light upon light'. Once the heart is illumined, all the other faculties share in the luminosity generated by the invocation — again, 'light upon light'. Furthermore, the very word for 'polish', *jalā'*, can be read as an implicit commentary on the light verse, since it derives from the verb *jalā*, whose primary meaning is 'to become clear' — to be exposed to view, disclosed, brought to light. The Qur'an refers to 'the night when it enshroudeth in darkness, and the day when it shineth forth in brightness (*idhā tajallā*)' (92:1–2), and likewise: 'By the day, as it bringeth to light [the sun] (*idhā jallāhā*)' (91:3). The consciousness lying hidden within the heart is 'brought to light' through the effect of the dhikr polishing it.

<!-- page 223 -->

Here the concern is with clarifying the organ of consciousness — the spiritual 'heart' — both enabling it to function as a mirror reflecting truths from above, and revealing its own hidden depths, thereby disclosing those concealed contents of consciousness the Imam calls the 'buried treasures of the intellect' (*dafā'in al-'uqūl*). The dhikr is presented here as a means of clarifying this spiritual mode of perception — the 'heart' — which cannot be identified simply with the rational faculty, still less with the organs of sensory perception, as discussed in Chapter 1.

The Qur'an alludes to the deeper significance of the heart and its particular mode of vision: 'Have they not travelled in the land, that they might have hearts with which to comprehend, and ears with which to hear? Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but blind are the hearts that are in the breasts' (22:46). And again: 'They have hearts with which they understand not, eyes with which they see not, ears with which they hear not. They are like cattle — nay, even further astray. They are the heedless (*al-ghāfilūn*)' (7:179). The reference at the end of verse 179 to the 'heedless', the *ghāfilūn*, brings us back to the dhikr, for the *ghāfil* is the very opposite of the *dhākir*, the 'rememberer'. Indeed, the verse immediately following constitutes one of the most important methodic foundations of the mystical practice of dhikr: 'Unto God belong the most beautiful Names, so call Him by them. And leave behind those who desecrate His Names; they will be requited for what they do' (7:180). The connection between remembrance and the heart is affirmed clearly in the following verses: '… Those who believe and whose hearts are at peace in the remembrance of God — is it not in the remembrance of God that hearts are at peace?' (13:28); 'Truly, therein is a reminder (*dhikrā*) for him who hath a heart' (50:37); 'Those are true believers whose hearts quake with awe when God is invoked' (8:2); 'Is he whose breast God hath opened up to submission (*islām*), such that he followeth a light from his Lord [like he who remains closed to submission and in darkness]? So woe be to those whose hearts are hardened against the remembrance of God, they are in plain error' (39:22).

<!-- page 224 -->

The verse immediately following refers to hearts that 'soften' to the dhikru'Llah: 'God hath revealed the most beautiful saying, a Book [containing] similitudes and paired [threats and promises] at which the skins of those who fear their Lord quiver; then their skins and their hearts soften to the remembrance of God' (39:23).

The Imam's comment on verse 37 of the 'Light' chapter, cited above, should thus be understood as also shedding light on all those Qur'anic verses that speak of the understanding or spiritual vision to which the heart has access in principle, and that allude to the dhikru'Llah as the means of translating this potentiality into actuality. The heart must be polished by the invocation of God. The Imam's comment here echoes and reinforces a prophetic saying frequently quoted in Sufi circles: 'For everything there is a polish (*siqāla*), and the polish of the hearts is the dhikru'Llah.'

A concrete discipline — a practice, an action, or a regular series of actions — is clearly being referred to here, without this in any way excluding the meaning of dhikr as an awareness or a state of recollectedness in the divine presence, a disposition of soul shaped by the consciousness that 'if you cannot see Him, He sees you'. The 'polishing' in question can be understood both as purifying thought, reflection, and meditation on the one hand, and as a regular methodic practice on the other, whether it takes the form of glorification (*tasbīh*), magnification (*tamjīd*), praise (*tahmīd*), sanctification (*taqdīs*), the declaration of divine unity (*tahlīl*), or the dhikr in the strict sense — the invocation of God's Names — by means of which the 'rust' of forgetfulness is removed. Ali (ع) also refers in his comment to a particular group who 'belong' to the dhikr, calling them an *ahl*. It is worth noting that the Prophet likewise referred to the *ahl al-dhikr*: 'God has angels who rove the pathways, seeking out the *ahl al-dhikr*, and when they find a group of people (*qawm*) invoking God, they call out to one another, "Come to that which you desire!"'

<!-- page 225 -->

Moving forward in time to one of the greatest members of the *qawm* — one of the names by which the Sufis refer to themselves — Rumi offers a poetic rendering of the image of the heart polished by the dhikr. It is the custom of kings, he tells us in the *Mathnawī*, to place the Sufis directly before them:

for they are a mirror for the soul, and better than a mirror:
breasts polished by invocation and meditation,
such that the mirror of the heart receives the pure, virginal image.

Another dimension of this image of the polishing of the heart deserves reflection — one that centres on the nature of the vision experienced by the heart purified through the dhikr and vivified by faith. In the Imam's perspective, this vision is divinely inspired; it is nothing less than the spiritual sight of God to which we have already referred: 'Eyes see Him not through sight's observation, but hearts see Him through the verities of faith.'

In another saying the Imam refers to 'pure hearts' as the 'places' from which God can be seen: 'whoever purifies his heart sees God'. The connection between the purification of the heart and the remembrance of God is made explicit in this further saying: 'Where are they whose actions are accomplished purely for God, and who purify their hearts [so that they become] places for the remembrance of God?'

Taken together, these sayings of the Imam on the impact of the dhikr on the heart can be read as a commentary on the important *hadīth qudsī* cited in Chapter 2: 'My slave never ceases to draw near to Me through supererogatory acts (*nawāfil*) until I love him. And when I love him, I am his hearing by which he hears, his sight by which he sees, his hand by which he grasps, and his foot by which he walks.' The Sufis regard this divine utterance as defining a foundational principle of their metaphysical doctrine while simultaneously pointing to the goal of their mystical practice.


One observes here a mutual confirmation between the Imam's saying and the hadith qudsi. Through the dhikr — the supererogatory act par excellence — the perceptions and actions of the individual are mysteriously assimilated to the divine Reality, and the result is that the heart attains spiritual vision. Yet the heart, as the centre of consciousness, can no longer be regarded in its exclusively relative, human dimension alone. Its inmost essence, or secret (sirr), pertains in truth to the divine, and it is this alone that can see as God sees and, ultimately, 'see' God Himself. As the Sufi master Abu Talib al-Makkī wrote, 'None sees Him to whom nothing is similar but Him to whom nothing is similar.' If it is true that God enters into the human acts of perception proper to the outward senses, then a fortiori the supreme act of spiritual awareness — the vision of God — is in reality not a vision of God by man but a vision of God by God, effected through rather than by the individual's 'heart'. The Imam tells us, 'Know God through God (i'rafu'Llah bi'Llah).' In the final analysis, then, it is God who both 'sees' and is 'seen.' According to a typical Sufi interpretation of the Qur'anic verse 'by a witnesser and that which is witnessed' (85:3), it is God Himself who is both the 'witnesser' (shahid) and the 'witnessed' (mashhud). Al-Kashanī, for example, writes in his esoteric exegesis — until quite recently wrongly attributed to Ibn al-'Arabi — that the human witness is effaced in his witnessing, so that it is God who witnesses Himself through the individual whose separative consciousness has been extinguished, such that 'He is the identity of the witness, there being no difference between the two except in terms of notional expression.'

This discussion anticipates the final section of this work, where the ultimate fruits of invocation will be examined in terms of spiritual realization through self-effacement. For now, the focus remains on a more relative aspect of the heart's vision of God — that whereby the reality of God is rendered clear and present to the heart through the 'verities of faith'. Here the agent is the inner dimension of human consciousness, and what is 'perceived' is the divine Reality itself. To put it another way, profound faith reveals the objective presence of God — a presence that is ubiquitous and inalienable, constituting the ontological reality of all existing things. As the Imam's famous Du'a' Kumayl puts it, cited earlier: 'By Thy tremendousness ('azmatika) which has filled all things … by Thy Names which have filled the foundations of all things, by Thy knowledge which encompasses all things, by the light of Thy face, through which all things are illumined …' (emphasis added).

Nothing can exist without the 'foundation' — the ontological infrastructure, as it were — that the Name, or combination of Names, of God furnishes for it. What constitutes the true being and the positive qualities of each manifested thing is, therefore, ultimately a divine Name or combination of Names. To see 'things' correctly is to see the divine attributes manifested by and through them — to see them as existential reflections of principial realities, as so many tajalliyyat, or theophanies, self-disclosures of the divine Reality. The heart purified by invocation comes to see God everywhere.

This vision of the divine in all things flows from two principles affirmed in the Qur'an: the first concerns the divine 'object' — that is, the objective, metaphysical nature of reality — and the second concerns the human subject and the mystical assimilation of that reality. On the one hand, nothing that exists, not even the most outward aspects of material existence, can be excluded from the divine Reality, for 'He is the First and the Last, and the Outward

<!-- page 243 -->

and the Inward' (57:3), and 'Wherever ye turn there is the Face of God' (2:115). On the other hand: 'We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and in their own souls, until it be clear to them that He is the Real' (41:53).

The heart polished and purified by the dhikr can decipher these signs and thereby 'see' God everywhere and in everything; yet the possibility of such a vision in no way implies a reduction of the divine Reality to the level of sensible phenomena. As noted earlier, the first sermon of the Nahj al-balagha declares that 'God is with every thing, but not through association; and other than every thing, but not through separation.' This 'withness' (ma'iyya) of God implies no relativization of Him, no association or conjoining (muqarana) of the divine Reality with created things, and no diminution of divine transcendence. The subtle interplay of ideas here illuminates both the Qur'anic verses proclaiming divine immanence — the presence of God within all things — and those stressing divine transcendence, which surpasses and is incomparable to all things. God may be 'absolutely' close to us, yet we remain infinitely far from Him. On the one hand, 'We are closer to man than the neck-artery' (50:16); on the other, '… there is none comparable unto Him' (112:4).

Nothing that exists can be altogether separate from the all-encompassing reality of God, and yet that reality shares no common measure with anything that exists. His oneness both includes and excludes all things. The affirmation of God's immanence within the world — His being 'with every thing' — therefore implies no diminution of His transcendence; and conversely, the affirmation of His transcendence above the world — His being 'other than every thing' — implies no absence from it. The oneness of God implies all of this, just as it negates any merely numerical oneness — a point touched on briefly in the first chapter. In the Imam's perspective, God is One not in the sense of a countable or merely numerical 'one', a unit among other units, but in the sense that His oneness admits of no 'other'. As he says, 'That which has no second does not enter into the category of numbers.'

This can be read as an esoteric commentary on the Qur'anic verse 'Say: He, God is One' (112:1). The opposite of God's oneness — the multiplicity of gods implied in the error of shirk — is refined by this conception and transformed into a statement about being, not merely about theology. That is to say, the act of shirk, of associating 'partners' with God, is taken to a deeper level: it implies not simply the affirmation of false deities but the attribution to God of a merely numerical oneness, which reduces His oneness to the wholly relative category of number and confines it to the level of determinate entities. Each such entity is a unit, and from that standpoint God would be no more than one among other similar 'beings'. But the Imam insists that God 'has no second'; there is no other 'being' in being but Him. No entity or unit that apparently exists can be compared, in reality, with His being — a being that is one in an infinitely all-encompassing sense and unique in an absolutely exclusive sense: a oneness inclusive of all reality and exclusive of all alterity. This radical notion of a oneness that absolutely excludes any 'otherness' in Being implies that created entities are, to the very extent that their apparent 'oneness' in existence is set beside God's true oneness, so many false gods — or rather, pale reflections of His 'one-and-onliness'.

In another sermon, Ali (ع) addresses the aspect of divine immanence, this time focusing on the kind of awareness of the divine Reality that the human intellect is capable of — which brings us back to the question of what vision of God is possible for the human being:

Praise be to God who is present in the hidden inwardness of things,

<!-- page 245 -->

and whose being is indicated by the signs of manifest things. The eye of the onlooker cannot behold Him, so the eye of one who does not see Him cannot deny His reality, nor can the heart of one who affirms His reality see Him. He is utmost in elevation, so no thing is more elevated than He; but He is also close in His nearness, so no thing is closer than He … God has not made the intellects capable of defining His qualities, but He has also not veiled the intellects from essential knowledge of Him.

We are told, on the one hand, that human intellects cannot define — and a fortiori cannot fully know — the divine attributes, and on the other that 'essential knowledge' of Him remains attainable. How can this apparent contradiction be resolved? Grappling with this paradox leads us to another key dimension of dhikr: that of recollection, remembrance, and recognition — in other words, the re-constituting of a knowledge that already exists but has been forgotten. One recognizes what one has always known, and it is precisely for this reason that one is able to know it 'once again'. This does not mean that the knowledge in question is made up of fragments to be reassembled, which would make it empirical in character; rather, the remembrance at issue is more like an awakening, an illumination that dawns as the clouds of congenital forgetfulness are dispersed.

This approach to knowledge through remembrance recurs throughout the Imam's sayings. In Sermon 1 of the Nahj, for instance, we find the phrase: 'Foremost in religion is knowledge of Him (awwal al-din ma'rifatuhu).' The word awwal literally means 'the first' and can be understood as the starting-point, the foundation, and hence the foremost element of religion. As for ma'rifa, this denotes not merely knowledge but also carries the sense of 'recognition'; the implication is clearly that the basis of all religious orientation is man's pre-existing knowledge of the divine Reality. One 'recognizes' — that is, 're-cognizes' — what one has always known at the deepest level of consciousness; one 'ac-knowledges' what one already 'knows' but had apparently forgotten.

Further on in the same sermon, as noted earlier, we are told that God sent to mankind 'His messengers, dispatching prophets in succession, in order to claim from them [His creatures] the fulfilment of the covenant of His creation; to remind them of His forgotten graces; to remonstrate with them through communication [of His revelation]; to unearth for them the buried treasures of the intellects …' (dafa'in al-'uqul) (emphases added).

<!-- page 246 -->

These 'buried treasures' can be identified with the knowledge of divine realities ingrained in man's deepest nature — those realities to which mankind was made to testify at the dawn of creation: 'And when thy Lord brought forth from the children of Adam, from their loins, their seed, and made them testify of themselves, "Am I not your Lord?" They said, "Yea, we testify." [This was] lest ye say on the Day of Resurrection, "Truly, of this we were unaware"' (7:172).

These divine realities can also be understood in relation to the divine spirit breathed into man at the creation of the human soul: 'Then He fashioned [man] and breathed into him of His Spirit' (32:9); to the 'names' taught by God to Adam: 'And He taught Adam the names, all of them …' (2:31); and to the fitra, the primordial nature of man, patterned upon the divine nature itself: '… the nature of God (fitrat Allah), that according to which He created man' (30:30). The primordial nature of the human soul can thus be seen to comprise the very element — knowledge of God — to which all of humanity is made to bear witness in the pact (mithaq) mentioned in the Qur'anic verse above. This identification between the fitra and the knowledge of God is made explicit by Imam Muhammad al-Baqir, in a saying quoted by al-Khu'i as commentary on that sentence from the first sermon. Al-Baqir was asked about the meaning of the Qur'anic words 'the nature of God, that according to which He created man'. He replied, 'He created them [such that their primordial nature was] in conformity with tawhid, at the covenant regarding knowledge of Him, [when they testified] that He is indeed their Lord.'

Knowledge of God, then, essentially defines human nature; it is for this reason that the human intellect cannot be veiled, as the Imam affirmed, from 'essential knowledge of God.' Yet even though this knowledge is ingrained in human nature, forgetfulness (ghafla) has become man's second nature. In Qur'anic terms, the very passage of time smothers and thereby 'hardens' the heart: 'Hath not the moment come for the believers, that their hearts be humbled to the remembrance of God, and to what hath been revealed of the truth; and that they not become as those who were given the Book aforetime. Then the term was prolonged for them, so their hearts were hardened; and many of them are evil-doers' (57:16, emphasis added).

The verse speaks both of this hardening of the heart over time — identifiable with the ghafla of second nature — and of its opposite: the heart's being present, here and now, to the dhikru'Llah. To remedy forgetfulness, something more than a simple factual reminder is needed — more than an item of information, some element of formal, conceptual, discursive knowledge. No accumulation of empirical data, arriving exclusively from without, can bring man to knowledge of God. What is required is integral remembrance, a spiritual awareness welling up from within and crystallizing upon contact with revelation from without, which renders present once again to the heart and mind the innate knowledge of God — that knowledge 'buried' deep in the intellect. There is no common measure between this consciousness of God and ordinary empirical knowledge of phenomena. The Imam expresses this incommensurability in sayings such as, 'Praise be to God who is well-known (ma'ruf) without being seen.' The paradox sharpens in this formulation of the same principle: 'By things their Creator manifests Himself to the intellects; and by things He is guarded from the sight of the eyes.' This brings us to a complex theme in the Imam's perspective — the nature of the 'veil' constituted by created phenomena, and how it is to be understood in the light of the infinite and inalienable reality of God. On the one hand, all created phenomena are a veil, in that they prevent the divine Essence from being perceived by outward vision; on the other hand, all phenomena cannot but manifest the divine, bearing witness to its creative power and revealing something of its reality — a truth that is clear to the intellect in its normative state and dazzlingly evident to the heart illumined and purified by the remembrance of God.

<!-- page 247 -->

The following aphorisms of Ibn 'Ata'illah, one of the great masters of Sufi gnosis or ma'rifa, are entirely pertinent here. They read as a series of reflections on the paradox expressed so succinctly by the Imam. Ibn 'Ata'illah writes: 'That which shows you the existence of His omnipotence is that He veiled you from Himself by that which has no existence alongside of Him.' In one respect, God is 'guarded from the eyes', in the Imam's words, and thus veiled by 'things'; but insofar as God alone truly 'is', these 'things' have no real existence, and so God is veiled by 'nothing'. Through their very 'appearance' — both in the sense of their coming into existence and in the sense of their 'apparent' existence — it is 'by things' that 'their Creator manifests Himself'. The mystery of this self-manifestation of God by and through all things is pressed home with compelling force through the following rhetorical questions of Ibn 'Ata'illah:

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the one who manifests everything?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the one who is manifest through everything?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the one who is manifest in everything?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the Manifest to everything?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He was the Manifest before the existence of everything?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is more manifest than anything?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the One alongside of whom there is nothing?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is nearer to you than anything else?

How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since were it not for Him the existence of everything would not be manifest?

It is a marvel how Being has been manifested in non-being, and how the contingent has been established alongside of Him who possesses the attribute of Eternity!


The import of all of these sayings can be grasped within this paradox, tersely expressed by the Imam: 'The veils do not veil Him (la tahjubuhu al-hujub).' The veils cannot help revealing what they are supposed to conceal; they are not merely transparent to the reality they cannot hide — they actively reveal that reality rather than conceal it. This calls to mind the famous saying of the Imam noted earlier, so often quoted by the Sufis: 'Were the veil to be removed, I would not increase in certitude.' The veils of creation ultimately reveal what they initially conceal, but this revelation is perceived only by the eye of the heart that has been purified through the dhikr — the heart that now 'sees after being blind'. The Imam continues, deepening the initial paradox still further:

The veil between Him and His creatures is His very creation of them. [This veil is] for the sake of His prevention of that which is possible in their essences, and for the sake of making possible that which is prevented in regard to Him; and to distinguish the Creator from the created, and the limit from the limited, and the Lord from the vassal.

All of creation — every 'veil' constituted by creation — is thus understood as nothing other than the divine dimension of al-Zahir: the Apparent, the Clear, the Evident, the Manifest. Yet it is not on its own account that creation is al-Zahir, but by virtue of the divine reality of which creation is a projection, a form of tajallī. God, as al-Zahir, projects something of His 'hidden treasure' within and as the creation. The world manifests the 'apparent' aspect of God — that which 'appears' from Him, that which can be manifested or created by Him — even while concealing the mystery of God: that which remains 'hidden', al-Batin, the supra-manifest source of being, the uncreated essence. The veil of creation is therefore nothing other than the self-revelation of God. It is important to stress, however, that it is so only by virtue of what pierces the veil of its own apparent otherness — that divine creativity which creation cannot in fact conceal, even though in principle it bears the nature of a veil.

The veil constituted by creation prevents what the essences of creatures make possible. What exactly do the essences make possible? The realization of inmost identity with the divine Reality — tawhid at the very highest level — a possibility that flows from the fact that this same divine Reality is the ontological foundation of 'their essences'. As noted above, it is the divine Names, qualities, and attributes that 'fill the foundations of all things'. At those foundations — in the 'essences' — there is only the one divine Reality. If the realization of this truth of identity is thus 'possible', what then does the veil 'prevent'? It prevents the affirmation of this identity at the wrong level of being — that of empirical existence — rather than at the deepest level, where the 'essences' of things are nothing other than the divine qualities and thus the divine Reality itself. To equate created phenomena with God is tantamount to pure shirk, the cardinal sin of 'association', and it is precisely this against which a vision of divine oneness must guard. One must always 'distinguish the Creator from the created, and the limit from the limited, and the Lord from the vassal'.

The veil of creation, in other words, acts as a barrier against pantheism — the reduction of the infinite transcendence of the Creator to the finite forms constituted by creatures. Yet insofar as the veil has no existence apart from the self-manifestation of the Real, it paradoxically also makes possible 'that which is prevented in regard to Him': rendered transparent to the divine Reality it expresses, the veil cannot prevent the immanent reality of God from shining through and within all things. On the one hand, there is essential identity, without this implying any diminution of divine transcendence; on the other hand, there is existential distinction, without this attenuating divine immanence in any way. We return to the foundational sentence of the first sermon of the Nahj al-Balagha: 'He is with every thing, but not through association; and other than every thing, but not through separation.' We are reminded as well of a basic theme in Sufi metaphysics: the dynamic relationship between tanzih — the declaration of divine incomparability and transcendence — and tashbih — the affirmation of similarity and, at the deepest level, of immanence. In the words of Ibn al-'Arabi:

He is not declared incomparable in any manner that will remove Him from similarity, nor is He declared similar in any manner that would remove Him from incomparability. So do not declare Him nondelimited and thus delimited by being distinguished from delimitation! For if He is distinguished then He is delimited by His nondelimitation. And if He is delimited by His nondelimitation, then He is not He.

To return to the powerfully evocative statement of the Imam: 'The veils do not veil Him.' Both tanzih and tashbih can become veils unless each is seen in the light of the other. Both transcendence and immanence can be veils unless the two perspectives are united in spiritual vision. Both creation — as divine manifestation — and the uncreated Essence — above and beyond all manifestation — can be veils unless they are integrated in the spiritual vision proper to tawhid, rendered transparent, each 'revealing' the other. Likewise, a one-sided emphasis on God as 'the Apparent' will itself become a veil, blinding us to God as 'the Hidden'; and conversely, for even the hidden aspect of God, considered in isolation, will — despite its non-manifest nature — constitute a conceptual veil that prevents us from seeing God's 'evidence', His 'evident-ness', in the very heart of the created world. In other words, 'veils' are not only created phenomena but also the limitations inherent in all human conceptions of God, including the divine qualities and attributes — hence the need, as we shall see shortly, for a radical negation, on the plane of human conception, of the divine attributes. Only on the basis of such an apophasis can consciousness begin to fathom the true nature of divine transcendence and see through the veils that 'do not veil Him'.

It is clear that these perspectives on the nature of the 'veil' are better understood as the evocation of a spiritual vision than as the logical expression of empirical observation. They are the fruits of contemplative synthesis, not the conclusions of rational analysis. This vision resolves apparent contradiction through intuitive awareness of the unifying simplicity of reality; it is not the result of tortuous mental effort to reconcile the complexities of existence. It is at once a vision reflected by the heart burnished by the remembrance of God and itself a form of that very remembrance. What the rational faculty will only ever perceive as irreducible contradiction, the spirit of the intellect can apprehend as a fruitful paradox. This dimension of the Imam's teaching finds apt expression in the words of the famous Sufi Abu Sa'id al-Kharraz, who, when asked how he came to know God, replied: 'through the fact that He brings opposites together (jam'uhu al-diddayn).' He then recited the Qur'anic verse: 'He is the First and the Last, and the Outward and the Inward.'

It is spiritual intuition that imparts the capacity to see as one — in the unifying vision of tawhid — what appears on the surface as many. In sharpening the paradox on the conceptual plane, 'Ali (ع) is, to use contemporary language, 'deconstructing' the artifices of mental conception, thereby paving the way for receptivity to intuitive apprehension and spiritual contemplation. It is only at these deeper levels of consciousness that the complementary process of 'reconstruction' can take place — a 're-cognition' of the Real, al-Haqq, arising from the heart's vision of God through 'the verities of faith' (haqa'iq al-iman). Only these haqa'iq are truly commensurate with al-Haqq; only these realities hidden within the depths of faith, brought to light in a heart polished by the remembrance of God, can do justice to the reality of God.

### Remembrance As The Quintessence Of Worship

To focus now on the actual performance of the dhikru'Llah, we turn first to certain Qur'anic verses that allude to the dhikr as a practice:

And invoke the Name of thy Lord morning and evening (76: 25).

And invoke the Name of thy Lord, devoting thyself to Him [or: it] with utter devotion (73: 8).

O ye who believe! Invoke God with much invocation (33: 41).

Call upon thy Lord in humility and in secret (7: 55).

And invoke thy Lord within thyself, in humility and awe, and beneath thy breath, in the morning and in the night (7: 205).

All of these verses point to an actual practice, one to be maintained according to different modes ('within thyself', 'in humility', 'in awe', 'in secret', 'beneath thy breath', and so on). But perhaps most important of all, the invocation is to be performed much, in abundance — dhikran kathiran. As noted earlier, the intensity and duration of the devotions of the Prophet and the first Muslims is referred to in the Qur'anic chapter al-Muzzammil ('The Enwrapped'), where the long night vigils of these early Muslims are mentioned: 'Truly, thy Lord knoweth that thou standest in prayer for almost two-thirds of the night, and a half of it, and a third of it — as doth a group from those with thee' (73: 20). It is worth noting that the first part of that sura contains the verse quoted above, instructing the Prophet to 'remember/invoke the Name of thy Lord, and devote thyself to Him with utter devotion' (73: 8). The pronoun in the word ilayhi, 'to Him', can refer either to 'Him' as 'thy Lord' or to 'it' as 'the Name of thy Lord', so that the operative meaning of the verse becomes: 'Invoke the Name of God and devote yourself to this Name with utter devotion.' This recalls the explicit injunction in the verse, 'Glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Exalted' (87: 1). On the plane of devotional practice, then, devotion to the Name of the Absolute is tantamount to devotion to the Absolute itself. The Absolute in itself is above and beyond all forms, and thus all verbal forms; yet It is nonetheless made accessible through the divine Name — a Name not shaped by human linguistic invention but bestowed through divine self-disclosure: God reveals the sacrament of His own Name. Distraction from the remembrance or invocation of the Name is, likewise, distraction from God Himself, as these verses suggest: 'The devil hath overcome them, causing them to forget the remembrance of God' (58: 19), and 'O ye who believe, let not your wealth nor your children distract you from the remembrance of God' (63: 9).

<!-- page 246 -->

This identification between devoting oneself to God and devoting oneself to His Name flows naturally from the principle enunciated by 'Ali (ع) in the following simple affirmation: 'He who loves a thing dedicates himself fervently to its invocation (man ahabba shay'an lahija bi-dhikrihi).' The lover of God should therefore devote himself utterly to the invocation of the Name of God, giving himself to it in abundance (kathiran). The combination of the word lahija — meaning both 'to be devoted to' and 'to mention repeatedly' — with dhikr, which clearly indicates the constant verbal repetition of the invocation, appears also in the Imam's Du'a' Kumayl, there with an additional explicit reference to the tongue: '... my tongue has devoted itself to thy invocation (lahija bihi lisani min dhikrika).' He implores God: 'Make my times in the night and the day inhabited by Thy remembrance', and 'make my tongue invoke Thee with utter dedication (ij'al lisani bi-dhikrika lahijan).' Finally, the invocation as a practice is confirmed plainly in these words: 'O He whose Name is a remedy and whose invocation is the cure (ya man ismuhu dawa' wa dhikruhu shifa').'

Here the Name of God is more than a name among other names, a word or sound among other symbols: it is the remedy for man's congenital forgetfulness — the spiritual amnesia, or ghafla, that has become his 'second nature', as noted above. But to be effective, the Name must be actively invoked and not merely contemplated, just as a medicine must be taken, not merely thought about, if it is to exercise its healing power. As the Imam says in one of his sermons, describing the greatness of the Qur'an: 'It has made the dhikr incumbent upon your tongues.' And, as we have seen, the invocation is to be done kathiran — 'much', in abundance. The following hadith of the Prophet is also worth noting here, for it makes absolutely clear that dhikr has an operative, practical dimension and is not to be confined to a purely theoretical interpretation of its meanings and implications:

'Abd Allah b. Busr reported that a man said, 'O Messenger of Allah, the laws of Islam are too many for me, so tell me something that I may cling to.' He replied, may God bless him and give him peace, 'Let your tongue never cease to be moist from invoking Allah.'

<!-- page 247 -->

It is also worth quoting the verses that follow the Qur'anic exhortation to invoke much: 'O ye who believe, invoke God with much invocation, and glorify Him early and late. He it is who blesseth you, as do His angels, so that He may bring you forth from darkness into light; and He is ever merciful to the believers' (33: 41–43).

Here the reciprocity between the dhikr of the believers and the salat — the prayer or blessing of God — over the believers deserves attention. The relationship between the human act of invocation and the divine act of blessing is, however, more complex than it first appears. Exploring this relationship and delving into the ontological dimensions of the invocation brings us close to the mystery expressed by the verse, 'Remember Me, I will remember you' (2: 152).

We shall turn to the possible meanings of this reciprocity below. For now, remaining with the Qur'anic verses cited above (33: 41–43), the relationship to note is that between invocation and illumination — being brought from darkness into light. The Imam speaks to the way in which human invocation results in divine illumination, and his words can be read as a comment on this dimension of those verses: 'Whoever invokes God, glorified be He, God enlivens his heart and illuminates his inner substance (lubb).' The word lubb (pl. albab) evokes the Qur'anic verse describing the ulu'l-albab, the 'possessors of substance', in which the dhikr once again figures prominently: 'Truly in the creation of the heavens and the earth, and in the alternation between night and day, there are signs for those of substance, those who invoke God standing, sitting and reclining on their sides, and reflect upon the creation of the heavens and the earth ...' (3: 190–191).

Beyond underlining the practical and methodic aspect of remembrance, this passage also points clearly to the relationship between dhikr and fikr — between invocation and reflection or meditation. Returning to the observations above: the practice of invocation is held to polish the innermost organ of awareness, producing an enhanced capacity for meditation upon the 'signs of God' in creation and within oneself. When one places love between invocation and meditation — for 'he who loves a thing dedicates himself fervently to its invocation' — the possibility opens of entering a virtuous circle in which each element strengthens and deepens the other two. The more seriously one ponders the 'signs' of God and appreciates their beauty, the more one comes to love God; the more one loves God, the more one invokes His Name; the more one invokes, the more sharply one perceives the sacred message of the 'signs' in the universe. Thus fikr intensifies dhikr, and dhikr deepens fikr.

<!-- page 248 -->

These verses (3: 190–191) are also significant in that they highlight the supra-formal aspect of the invocation. The dhikr is presented here as the quintessence of all religious activity — the spiritual act par excellence. Its universality of modes — standing, sitting, reclining, as this verse says, and 'within thyself', 'in humility', 'in awe', 'in secret', 'beneath thy breath', according to the verses cited above — transcends the formal rules governing the fixed canonical prayers, which involve prescribed words, movements, and conditions. The dhikr, by contrast, is to be performed at all times, in all places, in all postures; it is to be woven into the very texture of everyday life rather than superimposed upon it as an extraneous, formalistic practice. It is worth noting here that, according to Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, the invocation is the sole practice that should be performed in all circumstances and at all times, with no limit placed upon it: 'Everything has a limit at which it terminates, except the dhikr Allah. God has made obligatory the religious duties (al-fara'id), so whoever fulfils them, that fulfilment constitutes their limit. For the month of Ramadan, whoever fasts therein, that constitutes its limit ...' He proceeds to mention other rites, then cites the Qur'anic verse, 'O ye who believe, invoke God with much invocation', and adds that his father, Imam Muhammad al-Baqir, never ceased invoking, even while eating and walking.


In this light it is easier to understand those referred to in the Qur'an as 'men whom neither trade nor merchandise diverts from the remembrance of God'. The verse also helps to explain the description of the believers as those who are 'perpetually at prayer (*'ala salātihim dā imun*)' (70: 23). They are not only regular in performing the canonical prayer at the appointed times; for men of substance, there is no moment when they are not 'at prayer' — if one understands 'prayer' as the remembrance of God. The Qur'an instructs mankind to 'establish the prayer for the sake of My remembrance' (20: 14). The very purpose and goal of prayer, its spiritual value and substance, is thus the remembrance of God.

The following saying of Ali (ع) is relevant here: 'Continuous invocation is the food of the spirit and the key to prayer (*miftah al-salat*).' This helps us understand why the Qur'an tells us — as was noted at the outset of this essay — that 'prayer keepeth [one] away from lewdness and iniquity, and the remembrance of God is greater' (29: 45). The formal, canonical prayer is described here in negative or constraining terms; it is a preventative. Its very formality is defined as a ritual necessity, but it is also, unavoidably, an existential limitation — it is performed and operative at one time and not another, expressed in certain forms and movements and not others. The remembrance of God is greater in that it is the positive, enlivening substance of prayer; it is what liberates consciousness from the limits — verbal, mental, existential — by which all formal prayer is defined. Since all formal prayer is inescapably defined within certain limits, its *baraka*, or blessedness, can all too easily become confined, subjectively, within those same limits. One feels close to God only during formal prayer, and not in everyday life outside the prayer-times. It is *dhikr*, in the sense of recollectedness and constant awareness of God, that allows prayer — or the consciousness shaped by prayer — to open out into its intended essence: an awareness of the unconditional presence of God. It thus transforms a preventative measure against moral transgression into a positive apprehension of spiritual reality. One might say that *dhikru'Llah* is 'greater' than ritual or canonical prayer in the measure that spiritual awareness transcends moral conscience, or in the measure that essence transcends form. The 'lesser' is not negated by the 'greater' but is rather comprised within it, enriched by it, attaining a fullness and scope it could never reach on its own. In this light one can better understand at least some of the reasons why the Imam, following the Prophet and the Qur'an, presents *dhikru'Llah* as the quintessence of worship and devotion.

THE REALITY OF REMEMBRANCE

In this final section, our discussion turns to the spiritual fruits of the remembrance of God, and in particular to 'realization', *tahqiq* — the process by which *al-Haqq*, the Real, is 'made real' within oneself. This is closely related to the process referred to earlier, which the Imam described as *tawahhud*: self-integration, the spiritual assimilation of oneness within oneself, as opposed to the simple theological affirmation of oneness, *tawhid*, in its conventional sense. In keeping with the Imam's own use of paradox, the fruit of remembrance can be approached first by examining its very opposite: *ghafla*, heedlessness, or *sahw*, forgetfulness and distraction. In following this line of inquiry, we will observe another striking example of how the Imam's sayings function implicitly as a commentary on Qur'anic verses, while opening onto a rich terrain of mystical speculation and spiritual contemplation concerning the ultimate consequences of the invocation and remembrance of God. The Qur'an is severe in its condemnation of those who pray in an ostentatious and hypocritical manner — those who 'give the lie to religion': 'Woe to those who pray, those who are heedless of their prayer, those who make a show ...' (107: 4–6). The Imam likewise warns against remembering God heedlessly or with distraction, and in describing the opposite — perfect or complete *dhikr* — expresses a paradoxical return to a kind of 'forgetting' that goes to the very heart of the Sufi principle of 'extinction in remembrance' (*al-fanā' fī'l-dhikr*):

Do not remember God absent-mindedly (*sahiyan*), nor forget Him in distraction; rather, remember Him with perfect remembrance (*dhikran kamilan*), a remembrance in which your heart and tongue are in harmony, and what you conceal conforms with what you reveal. But you will not remember Him according to the true reality of remembrance (*haqiqat al-dhikr*) until you forget your own soul in your remembrance.

This is a profoundly important saying, one that contains within it a summary of the entire mystical path. What it implies is the whole series of spiritual 'stations' (*maqamat*, sing. *maqam*) through which total sincerity is attained — Sufism is itself often referred to simply as 'sincerity', that is, *sidq* or *ikhlas*. On the one hand, the *dhikr* itself helps to realize sincerity by 'polishing' the heart; on the other, it is the virtue of sincerity that bestows 'perfection' or 'completion' upon the spiritual efficacy of the *dhikr*. What matters at the stage of 'perfect' *dhikr* is that the outward verbal form of the invocation must harmonize with heartfelt intention. There must be no contradiction between what the tongue articulates and what motivates the heart. Bringing the heart and tongue into harmony means that the divine Reality invoked by the tongue reverberates in the heart, from which arise the soul's fundamental dispositions — the virtues.

The divine qualities comprised within the Name of the Absolute must, to some degree at least, be reflected in the heart and soul of the invoker, in the form of virtues that are summed up in the sincerity or 'purity' of soul to which *ikhlas* refers. When the soul is completely pure — sincerely and utterly at one with the highest ideals proclaimed by the tongue and the intellect — it becomes transparent to the spiritual qualities embedded within its own substance, the *fitra*, or primordial nature. These are the qualities 'brought to light', and also brought to life, by the *dhikr*; these are the qualities that shine forth from the heart once it is polished by the *dhikr*. In the absence of these moral and spiritual qualities, however, the purely technical act of invocation is 'heedless' and devoid of all efficacy. One who makes no effort to attain sincerity — who does not engage with the 'greater' *jihad* referred to in Chapter 1 — is heedless of God, neglecting the moral concomitants of authentic remembrance and ignoring the moral prerequisites for its efficacy.

But what is most remarkable in this saying of the Imam is the presentation of the ultimate stage of *dhikr* in terms of its apparent opposite. The 'reality' of *dhikr*, its *haqiqa*, opens out into the supreme *haqiqa* — the divine Reality as such. This degree of *dhikr* demands the 'forgetting' of the individual: the veil of the self must be cast aside if the spiritual vision of divine Reality is to be attained. There can be no *ma'rifa* without self-effacement, initially, and 'extinction' (*fana'*), ultimately. The reality of 'remembering' calls for the most radical 'forgetting'.

In other words, there is a clear distinction between 'perfect' *dhikr* and the 'reality' of *dhikr* — indeed, a radical disjuncture between them, such that the move from 'perfection' to 'reality' — from human perfection to divine Reality, one might say — requires an ontological leap of consciousness. In the first degree, where the aim is the elimination of hypocrisy and the attainment of sincerity, integrity, and wholeness, the individual as agent of the *dhikr* retains his identity. The harmonious reverberation between hidden intent and outward act is here given the name of 'perfect' *dhikr*. But it remains a *dhikr* that pertains to the individual as agent. In the higher degree, by contrast, the *dhikr* can no longer be defined in relation to the individual, since the individual has been 'forgotten' — by himself. This can only mean that the centre of consciousness has shifted from the invoker, *al-dhakir*, to the Invoked, *al-Madhkur*. The *haqiqa* of the *dhikr* is thus one with the object of invocation — the Real itself, *al-Haqq*. Esoterically, then, it can be said that the true agent of the invocation is God Himself, the apparent invocation by man being no more than an outward appearance, an illusion extinguished by the very act of invocation itself. This brings the discussion back to the question of agency raised earlier. The *hadith qudsi* quoted earlier — 'My slave never ceases to draw near to Me through supererogatory acts (*nawafil*) until I love him ...' — is worth recalling here. The Imam's commentary on the Qur'anic words about those who are not distracted by the world from the remembrance of God made clear that the *nawafil* in question are centred on the practice of invocation. What is to be stressed at this point is that the invocation can be seen as disclosing the reality of God's ontological agency within all acts: God is the hearing and the seeing of the individual who is loved by God as a result of his devotions. It must be stressed, however, that this account of the Divine penetrating the faculties of the soul and body of the individual describes a universal reality — the true state of affairs — and not any change of state, either for God or for the individual; for God does not 'change' in order to enter into the acts and perceptions of one person as opposed to another.

His 'becoming' one with the acts of perception is to be understood in terms of a reality 'becoming clear' to the unveiled soul, so that it sees through the illusory appearance of its own agency. By virtue of the invocation, the soul is enabled to see the deeper meaning of the Qur'anic verses: 'God hath created you and what ye do' (37: 96), and 'thou didst not throw when thou threwest [the stones], but God threw' (8: 17).

To return to the kind of 'vision' made possible by the *dhikr*: the very title of one of Ibn al-'Arabi's treatises, *Kitab al-fana' fi'l-mushahada* (The Book of Extinction in Contemplation), appears to point directly to the Imam's paradox of forgetting in the very bosom of remembrance, for being extinguished in contemplation is akin to the state of being forgotten in invocation. At the outset of this important treatise, Ibn al-'Arabi writes that the divine Reality is too elevated to be witnessed by the creaturely eye. The vision of God is impossible for as long as there subsists a 'trace of the condition of the creature in the eye of the witness'. It is only towards the end of the treatise that this apparently insuperable barrier before the vision of God is removed. Ibn al-'Arabi resolves the problem by means of the Prophet's definition of *ihsan*, discussed in Chapter 1 above: 'It is to worship God as though you see Him, and if you see Him not, yet truly He sees you.' The Arabic wording is such that, by placing a pause in the middle of the phrase 'if you see Him not (*in lam takun: tarahu*)', the meaning is completely transformed into: 'if you are not, you see Him.'

This vision of God cannot, then, pertain to the individual, who is 'forgotten' in the Imam's words, or non-existent according to Ibn al-'Arabi. The vision of God can only be by God, not by man. We will return to this possibility of 'ontological transference' and its metaphysical underpinnings in relation to the divine Names shortly. But here it is useful to reinforce the message of 'extinction in contemplation' by referring to the discourse — as narrated by Ibn al-'Arabi — that took place between him and Moses in the sixth heaven. Ibn al-'Arabi presents this within his account of his own visionary ascent, his own spiritual *mi'raj* through the heavens to the divine presence within his own soul.

He addresses Moses: 'You requested the vision [of God], while the Messenger of God (Muhammad) said that "not one of you will see his Lord until he dies."'

Moses replies: 'And it was just like that: when I asked Him for the vision, He answered me, so that I "fell down stunned" (7: 143). Then I saw Him in my [state of] being stunned.'

Ibn al-'Arabi asks: 'While [you were] dead?'

Moses: 'While [I was] dead ... I did not see God until I had died.'

We have here a 'supra-personal vision' entirely congruent with Ali (ع)'s 'un-self-conscious remembrance'. What appears to be implicit in both types of spiritual assimilation is what was called above an 'ontological transference' of agency. The true vision of God cannot take place for as long as anything creaturely subsists in the 'eye' of the beholder. It is instructive to see how the spiritual principle — and even some of the language — of this dialogue is expressed in condensed form in a supplication attributed to the Imam. He pleads with God to make him 'one of those whom You have called and he has replied to You, upon whom You have glanced and who falls unconscious before Your majesty.' It should be noted that the word *sa'iqa* ('to fall unconscious', 'be stunned'), used in this supplication, is the same word used in the Qur'anic verse (7: 143) to describe Moses' state when God revealed Himself to the mountain. A single 'glance' of the Real is enough to bring about this dissolution of self-awareness — the shadow cast, on the plane of individual consciousness, by the fulgurating light of God's self-disclosure, the *tajalli*.

To speak of this divine 'glance' (*lahiza*) also takes us back to Ibn al-'Arabi's evocative phrase 'extinction in contemplation'. In his *Tarjuman al-ashwaq*, he refers to divine wisdom as a peacock that 'kills with her glances', and adds in his own commentary that this refers to 'the station' of *al-fana' fi'l-mushahada* (extinction in contemplation). Earlier in the same commentary on this poem, he refers to the 'murderous glances' of this divine wisdom 'which accrues to a man in his hours of solitude, and which assaults him with such violence that he is unable to behold his personality, and which exercises dominion over him.' The commentary on the following lines is equally instructive in underlining the relationship between *dhikr* and *fana'*: 'Wild is she, none can make her his friend; she has gotten in her solitary chamber (*bayt khalwatiha*), a mausoleum for remembrance (*dhikr*).' The commentary adds: '... because contemplation of the Essence is a passing away (*fana'*) ... Her solitude is her looking on herself, for God says, "Neither My earth nor My heaven contains Me, but I am contained by the heart of My believing servant," and since the heart which contains this essential wisdom of the race of Jesus is bare and empty of all attributes, it is like a desert and she is like a wild animal.' Realization through remembrance, then, strictly implies the extinction of individual consciousness. This principle is far from being purely speculative or theoretical. Quite apart from the wealth of Sufi literature on the states of consciousness (*ahwal*, sing. *hal*) into which the mystic passes, we have the following evidence of the Imam's own immersion in such states. Abu'l-Darda', an eminent companion of the Prophet, reported that he once found Ali (ع) lying in one of the palm orchards of Medina, his body completely lifeless and as stiff as wood. He went to condole with Ali (ع)'s family, but was told by his wife Fatima: 'My cousin [that is, Ali (ع), her 'cousin' by virtue of being the cousin of the Prophet] has not died. Rather, in fear of God he has fainted. This condition overcomes him often.' The 'fear' in question here is evidently to be understood in the sense of 'awe', and is to be related to the heavenly dimension of *khawf* — heavenly because Paradise is said to be the consequence of realizing to perfection the kind of fear indicated by the Qur'an: 'But whoso feareth (*khafa*) the station of his Lord and restraineth his soul from caprice, verily, the Garden will be his abode' (79: 40–41). 'Fear of God', in the spiritual rather than moralistic sense of the term, does not so much terrify as beatify; it produces not merely quaking awe before the transcendent Absolute, but also an intimate absorption within the immanent divine presence. As is often quoted in Sufism: when one fears creatures one flees from them, but when one fears God one flees to Him. 'There is no refuge from God except [fleeing] to Him' (9: 118), as the Qur'an puts it.

This report also shows that the following saying of the Imam is not extravagant rhetoric. It appears rather to allude to an authentic state of spiritual intoxication — a total re-absorption of consciousness within its divine source — and to describe the conditions leading up to the experience of the state that 'overcomes him often', as Fatima put it:


Truly, God has a drink for His friends (awliya'ihi). When they drink it, they are intoxicated (sakara); and when they are intoxicated, they are enraptured ((araba); and when they are enraptured, they are blessed (taba); and when they are blessed they dissolve (dhaba); and when they dissolve, they are free (khalaşti); and when they are free, they devote themselves purely (akhlasa); and when they devote themselves purely, they seek (alaba); and when they seek, they find (wajada); and when they find, they arrive (wasala); and when they arrive, they are at one (ittasala); there is no difference between them and their Beloved.

This saying of the Imam evokes that of the Prophet, who claimed that he had states or 'moments' (waqt) that no 'angel brought close' nor 'prophet sent forth' could encompass — the implication being that neither angel nor prophet can subsist as such within the ineffable 'moments' of absolute tawhīd. And tawhīd, it must be stressed, is not 'union': it is the realization of oneness. Union implies two entities becoming one, whereas God is, as the Imam put it, 'that which has no second'. This ultimate realization of tawhīd can be seen as the fruit of the invocation — the most potent of all intoxicating 'drinks', if prayer may be described as drink — and it leads one to attend carefully to the true agent of the invocation, the true 'drinker'. For what appears to be man's invocation of God is transformed into the divine invocation of man. At the outset it seems that man 'drinks' God through His Name; but the ultimate result of the invocation — unitive realization — reveals that it is in fact God who 'drinks into Himself' the essence of human consciousness, an essence from which the impurity of self-consciousness has been purged. The true nature of the dhikr, masked by the outward form of man's invocation of God, is alluded to in another saying of the Imam: 'The invocation is not a formality of speech nor a way of thinking; rather, it comes forth firstly from the Invoked, and secondly from the invoker (awwal min al-madhkur wa thanin min al-dhakir).'

It is thus God Himself who performs the invocation, first and foremost; man's invocation is but a shadow, a reflection, a consequence of this divine invocation. The polyvalence of the Imam's brief sentence opens out into a range of fruitful meditations on the meaning, the origin, the end, and the very substance of the dhikr. Among other things, one recalls here the Qur'anic verse cited earlier: 'Remember Me, I will remember you.'

Before addressing one of the metaphysical implications of the fact that God is Himself the Invoker, it is important to appreciate how this perception of the reality of the invocation reveals its spiritual efficacy more clearly. For the reality denoted by the term dhikr is no longer confined within the boundaries of the individual, still less is it definable in terms of his verbal or mental activity. Instead, dhikr in its primary, metaphysical sense opens out into the realm of engendering — implying the function of existentiation, the effusion of Being. The creative Word uttered by God, 'Be! (kun)', which brings the possibilities of being into existence, can be seen in this light as a form of dhikr: God utters the 'name' of the possible thing, 'calling' it from non-existence into existence. This passage from nothingness to being is what renders the possible thing 'remembered', madhkur. Here again, the Imam's sentence can be read as a commentary on a Qur'anic verse and as an anticipation of the elaborate cosmogony associated with Ibn al-'Arabi.

The Qur'an poses the question: 'Hath there come upon man any moment in time when he was not a thing remembered?' (76: 1). Man's being 'a thing remembered' is here tantamount to his coming into being, and his nothingness is equivalent to his being 'not remembered' — that is, not yet called forth from the abyss of pre-existential hiddenness into the light of manifest existence. In this pre-existential state, the human being is, according to Ibn al-'Arabi, 'known', ma'lum, but only by God. He refers to the above verse in the following lines of al-Futūḥat al-makkiyya (The Meccan Illuminations):

God said of Himself that He is al-Badi', the Originator — that is, He created that which had no like or exemplar in all the degrees of being, for He knows, by His all-encompassing nature, every single thing that enters into the degrees of being. Therefore He says, as regards the creation of man, that he 'was not a thing remembered', for His is the remembrance … so a thing has being in the remembrance of the one who remembers it.

God's 'remembrance' of man thus precedes man's remembrance of God — ontologically, not merely temporally — constituting another interpretation of the verse cited earlier: '… and the remembrance of God is greater', infinitely greater than man's remembrance of Him. The true being of man resides in the 'remembrance' that God has of him. While man's devotion to God is articulated through the utterance of the divine Name, God's creation of man is articulated through His utterance of the creative Word. In Ibn al-'Arabi's cosmogony, the utterance of this Word is not simply an event in time; rather, that Word never ceases to reverberate, furnishing each human soul with its divine substrate, support, or foundation — its mustanad ilahi. This Word 'breathes' existence upon the immutable entity, al-'ayn al-thabita, which, though inexistent in itself, determines the contours, the form, and the configuration of being that will be manifested through it — just as a prism refracts the light that passes through it, or just as water takes on the colour of the cup, an image used by Junayd and often quoted by Ibn al-'Arabi.

It is also worth noting that the hadith al-haqīqa, attributed to Ali (ع), contains the essence of the Akbari doctrine of the tajalli of the unique Light of God upon the differentiated 'receptacles' — the loci of manifestation for that Light. The Imam is questioned by his disciple Kumayl on the meaning of al-haqiqa. His response includes the following affirmations:

Al-haqiqa is the unveiling of the splendours of Majesty, without any allusion … the effacement of that which is erroneously imagined (al-mawham), together with the clarity of that which is truly known (al-ma'lam) … the attraction of absolute unicity to the quality of tawhīd … a light dawning from the morn of eternity, its traces shimmering on the temples of tawhīd …

Al-Kashanī comments on this last image: 'That is, the manifestation of the Light of the one Essence … upon the loci of manifestation of the qualities of the Real, the Essence, these being the loci of the entities of existents (a'yan al-mawjudat), which the Imam calls "temples of tawhīd" — that is, the forms of the divine Names in the station of tawhīd.' This mustanad ilahi, the divine support of each individual soul, is thus its particular Lord, the divine Name that engenders and sustains it. The soul is nothing other than the locus of manifestation, the mazhar, where God discloses Himself in a particular, unique mode.

The very first words of the khuṭba with which Ibn al-'Arabi opens the Futūḥat al-makkiyya are these: 'Praise be to Him who engendered things from nothing, then negated it; and established their being through the orientation towards them of His Words.' This orientation (tawajjuh) of the divine Word towards the possible thing results in the manifestation of the Word's properties in accordance with the receptivity of that possible thing; the possibility is then 'breathed' into existence and becomes akin to a 'body', the 'heart' of which is the divine Name that existentiates it: '… every slave has a Name which is its Lord; so it becomes the "body" of that Name, which is its "heart".'

This analogy recalls the Imam's image of the dhikr as a 'polish' for hearts. The Name revealed from 'above' is mirrored by a Name that resounds from 'within'. The reciprocal reflection appears when the rust obscuring the surface of the heart is removed by the polish of the dhikr. A mode of union between two divine realities is evoked here, which again brings to mind the phrase 'light upon light'.

Earlier we saw that the Imam referred to the Names of God 'which have filled the foundations of all things' — a statement that clearly foreshadows Ibn al-'Arabi's conception of the divine Names as the supports or substrata of all things. But a question arises here, the answers to which may take us another step closer to comprehending the powerful and mysterious efficacy of the dhikr. What is the difference between the divine Names conceived as the metaphysical roots of all things, and the particular Names of God as revealed in the Qur'an? On one hand, the whole of creation is the 'Book of God', all phenomena being so many 'words' of God; on the other, the revealed text contains specific words and Names, and it is these that are to be recited and invoked. Why? One clear answer is given in the following verse: 'Call upon Allah or call upon al-Rahman. By whatever Name you call upon Him, His are the most beautiful Names' (17: 110). The Qur'an itself enjoins believers to invoke these specific, revealed Names, and not others. These Names are therefore entirely unlike the names of the idolators' gods: 'They are but names that ye have named, ye and your fathers, for which God hath revealed no authority' (53: 23).

The names of idols resemble conventional names of phenomena in that they are merely arbitrary labels or signs with no intrinsic relationship to the phenomena themselves, let alone to the principles underlying those phenomena. By contrast, the Names of God, revealed by God, are penetrated by the very substance of the reality they denote. These Names therefore possess 'authority' (sultan), by virtue of which they have the power to transform, not merely to inform. They are, in other words, impregnated with a theurgic power — the capacity to render present to one's mind and heart the immanent reality of God. Though God's presence is inalienable — for nothing existent can possibly lie 'outside' His all-encompassing presence — that presence can nonetheless become veiled, at least subjectively. The heart can be veiled from God by the 'rust' of forgetfulness that covers it, so that the divine Reality comes to be 'veiled' by the very phenomena that manifest it. As noted earlier, the Imam challenges us to consider how it is 'by things' that the Creator manifests Himself, and yet it is also 'by things' that He is hidden from sight.

This being so, what is needed to open oneself to the divine presence within and beyond all things is both a polishing of one's own 'within' by the dhikr, and an unveiling or revelation from that 'beyond'. This latter form of disclosure may be viewed as a direct revelation of God within that indirect revelation of Him which consists in the entirety of the created cosmos — a cosmos that simultaneously reveals and veils the divine Reality. Even if, from one point of view, the two 'revelations' — cosmic and scriptural — are both defined as manifestations of God, from another point of view the scriptural revelation is charged with a spiritual power that makes it incomparably more 'revealing' than the created cosmos. There is no common measure, in terms of spiritual method, between the cosmos as the manifested divine 'Word' and Allah as a revealed Name of God. On one hand there is differentiated manifestation; on the other, a unique revelation. One might also say that the divine Name is the quintessential manifestation — simultaneously summing up the entire realm of manifestation and transcending it. The difference between cosmic manifestation and scriptural revelation might be seen, in one respect, as analogous to the difference between shadow and light. Revelation is like a projection of the pure Light of God and is therefore of one substance with the source from which it emanates, whereas manifestation is like the shadow cast by that light. Both are projections of one and the same reality, but this does not diminish the incommensurability between light and darkness. In the words of Ibn al-'Arabi: 'The very Being of the Real is a verified Light; the very existence of creation is a shadow following after.'

There is a remarkable supplication attributed to Ali (ع), the Du'a' al-Mashlul, 'supplication of the lame man'. It consists of 122 verses containing a multitude of divine Names, attributes, and titles. Towards its end, one reads a verse that may be taken as the key to the entire text: 'I ask Thee by every Name with which Thou hast named Thyself.' It is the fact that God 'names Himself' that imparts to His Names their theurgic power, their sacramental substance, their mystical efficacy. It is the revealed substance of the Names that accounts for these distinctive properties and renders them incomparable to other names. It is for this reason that, as Sufi texts so commonly assert, the Name is the Named (al-ism huwa'l-musamma).

These observations on the ontological relationship between the Names of God and the nature of being, while helpful in explaining certain aspects of the dhikr as a mystical practice, nonetheless fall short of accounting for that ontological leap of consciousness — that extinction of self-awareness — to which the Imam refers in the words: 'you will not remember Him according to the true reality of the remembrance until you forget your own soul in your remembrance.' One may ask why and how the dhikr produces this extinction of consciousness, or this effacement of individual being, especially since the function of dhikr appears to be precisely the opposite: to actualize or precipitate a heightened mode of consciousness, and to enhance or deepen one's quality of being.

The first point to be made in response is this: the individual cannot on his own make this leap from remembrance to forgetfulness. It is impossible for a conscious person, concentrating on the invocatory act, to make himself unconscious of himself in the midst of his invocation. It should be noted carefully that the Imam says the soul is to be forgotten 'in' the remembrance. Any effort to will oneself into unconsciousness emanates from a conscious will and thus entrenches, rather than effaces, self-consciousness. So this cannot be a matter of forcing oneself to forget oneself while invoking God; it is, rather, an effort of total concentration on the Name — an attempt to be so focused on the Name that there remains no 'space', mentally or existentially, for anything but the Named, the reality being invoked. A certain initial degree of self-effacement can thus be seen as a natural accompaniment of total concentration. But the ultimate consummation of self-effacement in fana' comes about as a divine grace, something that surpasses all individual effort, even if the act of invocation is held to attract that grace — or to remove from the heart the 'rust' that obscures the vision of one's nothingness in the face of the sole reality of God, 'that which has no second'. This part of the response can be summed up by an esoteric reading of the Qur'anic words: '… thou wilt not forget, unless God willeth it so' (87: 6–7). It is also noteworthy that al-Kalabadhi, author of one of the most important early manuals of Sufism, opens his chapter on dhikr with words that echo those of the Imam: 'The haqiqa of the dhikr is this: that you forget everything other than the madhkur (the Invoked) in the dhikr. For He says: "So remember thy Lord when thou hast forgotten" (18: 24). That is, when you have forgotten what is not God, then you have remembered God.'

The second response to the question above can be framed in terms of the principle of inverse analogy. The human invocation is analogous to the divine invocation — being, as noted earlier, a kind of reflection thereof — and, like any reflected image, the human act of invocation positively participates in what it reflects while also inverting it. In the first respect, the human act of invocation leads to a greater plenitude of consciousness and a fuller participation in being for the invoker; in the second, the human invocation finds its consummation in what appears to be the opposite: the effacement of the individual in the object invoked. Yet this is only apparently opposed to the enhancement of consciousness and being. In reality it is the fulfilment of that very process — though its final stage involves a quantum leap, a negation of the limits that constitute individuality as such. Insofar as the individual is limited, and insofar as limitation is a negation, the negation of this negation amounts to pure affirmation: the affirmation of unconditional, supra-individual being. This negation of negation, on the plane of the self, can be seen as the reflection of that negation of nothingness to which Ibn al-'Arabi alludes in the line quoted above: 'Praise be to Him who engendered things from nothing, then negated it.' In this connection it is worth recalling the enigmatic saying of the Imam cited earlier: 'I am the death of the dead.' This recalls the words that Rumi puts in the Imam's mouth in the following celebrated lines of the Mathnawi:

He said, 'I am wielding the sword for God's sake, I am the servant of God, I am not under the command of the body.

I am the Lion of God, I am not the lion of my passion: my deed bears witness to my religion.


In war I am (manifesting the truth of) *thou didst not throw when thou threwest*: I am (but) as the sword, and the wielder is the (divine) Sun.

I have removed the baggage of self out of the way, I have deemed (what is) other than God to be non-existence.

I am a shadow, the Sun is my lord; I am the chamberlain, I am not the curtain (which prevents approach) to Him.

I am filled with the pearls of union, like a (jewelled) sword: in battle I make (men) living, not slain.

<!-- page 203 -->

In other words, whereas the cosmic unfolding of creation — the movement from God to man — requires the negation of that relative nothingness which is pregnant with the possibilities of being, the realization of God by man requires the negation of that same nothingness which God breathed into being. Returning to Ibn al-'Arabi's treatise *Extinction in Contemplation*, what is at issue here is 'the extinction of that which never was ... and the subsistence of that which never ceased to be'. The following Quranic verses are clearly implied: 'Everything thereon perisheth (*fana*), and there subsisteth (*yabqa*) only the Face of thy Lord, possessor of glory and bounty' (55:26–27).

Yet all things, insofar as they exist, have two aspects or 'faces', as noted above: the divine aspect, which is the thing's true being, and the created aspect, which is 'nothing'. The 'Face' of the Lord that alone subsists is therefore not simply the divine Reality above and beyond all things, but equally the same reality *within* all things. This is the import of al-Ghazali's esoteric commentary on the Quranic verse 'Everything perisheth except His Face' (28:88). Al-Ghazali comments:

[it is] not that it perisheth at some particular moment, but rather it is forever perishing, since it cannot be conceived except as perishing. For each thing other than God is, when considered in and by itself, pure nothingness; and if considered from the face (*wajh*) to which existence flows to it from the Prime Reality, it is viewed as existing, but not in itself, solely from the face which accompanies Him Who gives it existence. Therefore, the divine face is the sole thing in reality.

He then connects the mystical realization of this oneness with the invocation of God and with the forgetting of the self in invocation, offering a valuable commentary on what the total dissolution of self-consciousness actually means. The Sufis who verify this oneness through mystical 'taste' (*dhawq*), he writes, are 'drowned in oneness' to the point of becoming 'dumbfounded':

No capacity remained within them save to recall Allah; yea, not so much as the capacity to recall their own selves ... when this state prevails, it is called in relation to him who experiences it Extinction, nay Extinction of Extinction, for the soul has become extinct to itself, extinct to its own extinction; for it becomes unconscious of itself and unconscious of its own unconsciousness, since were it conscious of its own unconsciousness, it would be conscious of itself.

<!-- page 204 -->

A saying attributed by al-Qushayri to Abu 'Uthman deserves attention here, as it illuminates another dimension of the 'unconsciousness' realized through invocation: 'Whoever has not tasted the loneliness of unconsciousness will not find the food of the intimacy of remembrance.' Losing consciousness of oneself is equated with 'loneliness', while becoming fully conscious of God through invocation produces 'intimacy'. One cannot find true intimacy with the divine Reality, in other words, except by stripping the self of its false 'intimacy' — that habitual self-satisfaction, that ingrained self-centredness which is the shadow cast by self-awareness. It is this awareness of self that — to return to the Imam's statement — must be forgotten. This higher forgetfulness cannot arise except on the basis of the remembrance of God, for it is the source of Being, unconditional Consciousness, and eternal Life that alone is capable of extinguishing, eclipsing, or effacing the relative existence, partial consciousness, and apparent life of the individual. This, the *haqiqa* of the *dhikr*, is expressed with economy in the Imam's simple words: '... forget your own soul in your remembrance'.

The state of *fana'*, the ultimate degree of self-forgetting, is therefore a grace; it cannot be seized by individual effort alone, however sustained, as was seen earlier. Yet this does not call for any abandonment of effort on the invoker's part. On the contrary, as the Sufi tradition attests abundantly, *fana'* is intimately bound up with the practice of *dhikr*, being its culmination. Following this state, or *hal*, of extinction comes the station, or *maqam*, of subsistence, *baqa'* — a return of consciousness to the self, a self now transfigured by the knowledge of reality that was impressed on the soul during *fana'*. For it is only in the state of *fana'* that the ultimate 'vision' of absolute oneness is attained, a vision that leaves no room for the ontological duality inescapably bound up with individuality. To posit the 'individual' is to posit its correlate, the Lord, and thus to affirm duality.

<!-- page 205 -->

Al-Kashani addresses this in his commentary on the Quranic verse 'Remember Him as He hath guided you' (2:198), tracing the guided ascent through the degrees of *dhikr*. The first is the *dhikr* of the tongue, corresponding to the soul (*al-nafs*); then that of the heart (*al-qalb*), corresponding to the divine acts and their manifest graces; then that of the 'secret' (*al-sirr*), corresponding to the vision of the divine acts together with 'the unveiling of the sciences of the manifestation of the divine qualities'; then that of the spirit (*al-ruh*), corresponding to 'the contemplation of the lights of the manifestations of the divine qualities, with an apprehension of the light of the Essence'; then the *dhikr* of 'the hidden' (*al-khafi*), which is 'the contemplation of the beauty of the Essence along with the subsistence of duality'; and finally the *dhikr* of the Essence, 'which is essential vision, attained through the removal of duality.'

The same point emerges from the following counsel of Ibn 'Ata'illah, which shows how the *dhikr* generates receptivity to the divine presence and to the grace by which alone the individual can truly 'forget' himself — in the sense of becoming 'unconscious of his own unconsciousness', as al-Ghazali put it:

Do not abandon the invocation because you do not feel the Presence of God therein. For your forgetfulness of the invocation of Him is worse than your forgetfulness in the invocation of Him. Perhaps He will take you from an invocation with forgetfulness (*ghafla*) to one with vigilance (*yaqaza*), and from one with vigilance to one with the Presence of God (*hudur*), and from one with the Presence of God to one wherein everything but the Invoked (*al-Madhkur*) is absent. 'And that is not difficult for God.' [emphasis added]

<!-- page 206 -->

In his commentary on the *Hikam*, Ibn 'Ajiba adds: 'So, in the measure that one is extinguished in the Name, one is extinguished in the Essence.' This follows from what was stated above: the Name is the Named. In another treatise — a commentary on a poem by Shushtari on the divine Name Allah — he states this cardinal tenet of the Sufi invocatory discipline more explicitly. The poem's first line, referring to the letters composing *Allah*, reads: 'The *alif* before two *lam*s, then a *ha*': coolness of the eyes.' Ibn 'Ajiba comments: 'The "coolness of the eyes" — this is joy, for tears of joy, as one knows by experience, are cool. The author means this: this Name is the joy of my heart, its happiness, its beatitude.' He then cites lines from al-Hallaj on the letters of the Name *Allah*, adds a verse of his own, and concludes: 'The Name is not other than the Named, for joy cannot come from anything but the essence [of a thing], not from the name [as such].'

This sums up the doctrine of identity between the Name and the Named — the rationale for the Sufi practice of *dhikru'Llah*. But here a certain difficulty arises. However congruent the Imam's pronouncements on the *dhikr* may be with these later Sufi articulations of the principles underlying the practice, this particular stress on the identity of Name and Named appears, on the surface, to conflict with numerous sayings of the Shi'i Imams that explicitly deny such identity. Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, for instance, is reported as saying:

The Name of Allah is other than Him. For everything to which the name of a thing is applicable is a creation, hence other than God. For whatever the tongue expresses, or is worked on by the hand, is a creation ... everything described is created, and the Creator of things cannot be described within the limits of a named object.

<!-- page 207 -->

Allah has ninety-nine Names. If the Name were the Named, then every one of the Names would be a god. But Allah is the one meaning — or spiritual reality, *ma'na* — indicated by the Names, and all of them are other than Him.

This apparent contradiction can be resolved in two ways. First, we need to attend carefully to precisely what 'Name' means in each context: that of doctrinal distinction, in which the Absolute must be rigorously distinguished from the relative and the Essence from all forms; and that of methodic concentration, in which the Absolute is understood as the reality of the very form through which it reveals itself. We can then turn to one of the most esoteric dimensions of the Shi'i Imams' self-understanding — their references to themselves as the 'most beautiful Names' (*al-asma' al-husna*).

On the question of what 'Name' means, Ibn al-'Arabi offers a precise definition: 'The Names have two connotations; the first connotation is God Himself who is what is named, the second that by which one Name is distinguished from another ... As being essentially the other, the Name is the Reality, while as being not the other, it is the imagined Reality.' Each Name is, on the one hand, identified with every other Name by virtue of its essential identity with the Named — the one Essence, source of all Names. On the other hand, it carries its own specific meaning and quality, the relationship by which the one Reality engages with the created cosmos. In this latter respect each Name is distinguished from the others, and distinction implies limitation, hence relativity and, ultimately, a kind of illusion before the one and only Reality; it is in this sense that the Name takes on the character of 'imagined Reality'. In Ibn al-'Arabi's perspective the Names possess no distinct ontological entities of their own; they all revert to the self-same, one and only Reality of the divine Essence. This resolves the problem — raised in the saying of Imam Ja'far — of different Names implying different 'gods'.

<!-- page 208 -->

In the Sufi lexicon *Lata'if al-i'lam fi isharat ahl al-ilham* (Subtle Disclosures in the Allusions of the Folk of Inspiration), attributed to al-Kashani, one finds a definition of the divine Names that further clarifies the two connotations. Under the entry 'Names of the Divinity' we read:

These are, in the terminology of the Sufis, expressive of the manifest aspect of being in respect of its determination within a specific meaning. For every divine Name is the manifest aspect of being — which is in turn identical to the Essence — but not as the Essence is in itself; rather, in respect of its specification and determination within a particular meaning, or quality. For example, the Name *al-Hayy* ('The Living') is a Name of manifest, determined being, but in respect of its specification as a particular meaning it is Life. So in regard to being itself, *al-Hayy* is identical to the Essence, whilst in regard to its determination as a particular meaning it is other than the Essence. If you have understood what we have said, you will know the meaning of their [i.e., the Sufis'] saying: the Name is not identical to the Named, neither is it other than It.

There is thus a nominal and cognitive distinction between Name and Named — in terms of which the Name is not the Named — and at the same time a spiritual assimilation between them — in terms of which the Name is the Named. A further distinction follows from this: the verbal form of the Name must be distinguished from the Name as an attribute of the Essence. As attribute, the Name is nothing but the divine quality it designates, which is in turn identical to the Essence; but alongside this there is the 'name' of this Name, which is merely a verbal form. Qaysari, another key commentator on Ibn al-'Arabi, brings out this distinction in the introduction to his commentary on the *Fusus al-hikam* — a major work in its own right and a masterly, concise exposition of the essential elements of the Akbari perspective as a whole — where he writes:

The Essence, considered along with a determined quality and in relation to one of Its theophanies, is named by a Name; so 'The Compassionate' has an essence, which is compassion; 'The All-Conquering' has an essence, which is all-conquering power. These spoken Names are Names of Names; from this it can be understood what is meant by the saying: The Name is identical to the Named.

<!-- page 209 -->

In his own monumental commentary on Qaysari's introduction, Jalal al-Din Ashtiyanī — a contemporary authority on Shi'i *'Irfan* — makes an important observation after citing the passage above. His remark brings the discussion back both to the principle of extinction through the invocation of the Name and to the second point mentioned earlier: the Imams themselves as the 'most beautiful Names'. Ashtiyanī notes that the saints frequently speak of the Names as being one with the Named because they are implicitly referring to the referent (*misdaq*), which is the *sifa*, the divine attribute or quality: 'The *sifat* are in one respect the specifications of the Essence, while in the station of unicity they are annihilated in the Essence, and are [thus] identical to the Essence.'

This metaphysical account of the relationship between Name, *sifa*, and Essence can be seen as mirroring, objectively and universally, the path traced subjectively and mystically by the soul extinguished in and through the invocation of the Name. Just as the *sifat* are 'annihilated' in the Essence — with which they are therefore absolutely and unconditionally one — so the soul extinguished by the Name simultaneously achieves its total nullification and its ultimate realization, though no longer as an individual soul. It is only by virtue of the divine theophany, or the particular divine Name — the 'heart' that constitutes the soul's true being, its *mustanad ilahi* — that this 'extinction' at the level of the individual soul is equivalent to 'realization' at the level of transcendence. The deepest reality of *tawhid* is thus revealed: the individual no longer affirms the proposition that 'there is no god but God', for the individual has been extinguished by the reality that 'there is no reality but the one and only Reality'. God is 'that which has no second'. Just as 'No one has truly said Allah but Allah', likewise no one realizes God but God. It is God alone who comes to 'know' or 'be' Himself — and this constitutes one aspect of the purpose of creation, as indicated in the *hadith qudsi* cited earlier: 'I was a hidden treasure and I loved to be known.'

<!-- page 210 -->

In this light the saints (*al-awliya'*) can be understood as perfectly self-realized theophanies — and thus as utterly effaced in that of which they are theophanic manifestations, their very effacement in the divine Reality being the ultimate source of their knowledge of that Reality. This applies, a fortiori, to the Prophet himself, prototype of all the saints and the 'perfect man', *al-insan al-kamil*. In this connection, Sabziwari cites the saying of Ali (ع) — 'We are the most beautiful Names' — in his commentary on the divine Names: 'There is no doubt that they [the Imams] are His most exalted indicators and His greatest signs (*'ala'imihi'l-'uzma wa ayatihi'l-kubra*), as the Prophet said, God bless him and his family, "Whoso sees me has seen the Truth (*al-Haqq*)."' He then equates the spiritual station and inner reality of the Imams with those of the divine Names and attributes, while stressing that the divine Essence transcends all Names and attributes and remains forever ineffable, known only to itself. The Prophet, the Imam, the saint, the 'perfect


man', thus manifest those aspects of the divine nature that can be perceived on the human plane. The more the divine Essence is exalted in transcendence above all Names and attributes, the more fulgurating is the reflection of those Names and attributes in the mirror constituted by the 'perfect man'. The unknowable transcendence of the hidden 'treasure' is, to some degree, compensated by the faithful reflection of the communicable aspects of that treasure in the 'friend of God'. It is worth dwelling, in conclusion, on the implications of identifying the Imam with the divine Name or attribute; for in one respect this identification would appear to be shirk — the 'association' of a creature, however exalted his station, with the transcendent Reality. The same applies to the ecstatic utterances (shathiyyat) of many of the most celebrated Sufis, such as al-Hallaj's ana'l-Haqq ('I am the Real') and Bayazīd's subhanī ('Glory to me'). 133 Yet when one adopts a viewpoint shaped by the Imam's own conception of the divine attributes, a very different picture emerges — one that, far from being tainted by shirk, in fact rejoins the most profound application of tawhid. The first sermon of the Nahj al-balagha contains, as we have seen, the following remarkable and highly important statements regarding the attributes of God:

The perfection of purification (ikhlas) is to divest Him of all attributes (sifat), because every attribute testifies that it is other than the object of attribution, and every such object testifies that it is other than the attribute. So whoever ascribes an attribute to God — glorified be He! — has conjoined Him with something else; whoever so conjoins Him has made Him twofold; whoever makes Him twofold has fragmented Him; and whoever fragments Him is ignorant of Him. 134

When the Imam says that pure devotion to God requires divesting God of all attributes, the negation here is not of the divine attributes as such — rather, it is a negation of them as separate, autonomous, or super-added realities conceived as being substantially 'other than', or ontologically distinct from, the Essence of God. For the reality of God's attributes is clearly affirmed earlier in the same sermon: 'He whose attribute has no binding limitation (alladhi laysa li-sifatihi hadd mahdud).' What is denied is the possibility of confining any divine attribute within the boundaries of human conception and language. Since no divine attribute can be distinct from the divine nature, and since that nature is infinite, every attribute is likewise of infinite scope and thus beyond all the limitations presupposed by formal conception and linguistic definition.

In one respect, then, the attribute or Name is to be affirmed, and in another respect negated: affirmed as not being other than the Essence, negated insofar as it is conceived in itself and on its own account. This metaphysical perception of the relationship between the attribute or Name and the Essence can also be applied, mystically, to the relationship between the individual soul and the divine Reality. On its own account the individual is nothing, while in respect of the divine substrate — the divine Name, the mustanad ilahi that constitutes its essential reality — it is not other than the unique Real. According to Ibn al-'Arabi, 'The final end and ultimate return of the gnostics ... is that the Real is identical with them, while they do not exist.' This is a perfect expression of the dual aspect of the soul of the realized sage: insofar as he is aware that 'he does not exist', he knows that the Real is his true identity. However paradoxical it may appear, the mystic's realization of absolute Reality goes hand in hand with his realization of his own non-existence. These two aspects of one and the same realization are elliptically expressed by Ibn al-'Arabi. He describes the climax of his spiritual ascent — his mi'raj — when he claims that God removed from him his 'contingent dimension (imkan)', resulting in the attainment of 'the inner realities of all the Names'. He continues:

Thus I attained in this nocturnal journey the inner realities (ma'ani) of all the Names, and I saw them returning to One Subject (musamma) and One Entity ('ayn): that Subject was what I witnessed and that Entity was my Being. For my voyage was only in myself and pointed to myself, and through this I came to know that I was a pure 'servant' without a trace of lordship in me at all. 136

This passage helps explain the meaning behind so many of the ecstatic utterances of the Sufis and the apparently self-deifying statements of the Imams. Their personal servitude to God is not denied but fully realized through knowledge of their true identity: for that identity as the Named is attained only upon the effacement of their individuality. Like the divine attributes, they are mustahlak — annihilated — in the divine Essence, leaving no room for any individualistic appropriation of God's glory. When the Imam therefore appears to speak of his own greatness, it is to be understood as nothing other than the greatness of God being expressed through him as a mazhar, a locus for the theophanic manifestation of the divine — precisely in the measure of his own effacement within that greatness.

The same point is reinforced by what 'Attar recounts in his Tadhkiratu'l-awliya' about Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq. When someone accused him of pride, the Imam replied: 'I am not proud, for pride pertains to the divine greatness. Since I have gone beyond the pride of self, His pride has come and taken the place of my own pride. With pride of self present, there can be no divine greatness, but through the divine greatness, pride will be present.' The divine qualities shine with greater, not lesser, brilliance through the prism of a soul rendered transparent to the divine Reality — a transparency achieved through the dissolution of all the clouds of egoism and passion.

Let us recall the holy utterance cited earlier: 'My slave draws near to Me through nothing I love more than that which I have made obligatory for him. My slave never ceases to draw near to Me through supererogatory acts (nawafil) until I love him. And when I love him, I am his hearing by which he hears, his sight by which he sees, his hand by which he grasps, and his foot by which he walks.'

In the light of the foregoing, one can now resolve the paradox contained in this divine utterance. On the one hand, the most beloved thing to God appears to be the obligatory rites; on the other, it is the supererogatory rites that lead the slave to draw ever nearer to God until He loves him — a love that is apparently greater insofar as it results in the divine penetration of the human faculties, the unitive realization proper to tawhid. The paradox resolves, however, when one understands that God's 'love' for the obligatory rites is a love directed toward something apart from Himself, whereas His love for the 'slave' — the pure slave effaced in his Lord — is infinitely greater, insofar as it partakes of the love of God for His own Self-manifestation, and thus for Himself. In the beginning, God 'loved to be known', and so He created the world. In 'knowing' Himself through the effaced slave — the apparent 'other' — His love to be known is realized through the saint whose greatest glory is precisely his own effacement, poverty, and emptiness of self.

It is in this sense that the following statement of the Imam is to be understood: 'Down from me torrents do pour; up to me no bird can soar.' Torrents flow down from him because — and in the measure that — he is extinguished in the source of the torrent. This image recalls the Qur'anic description of the 'slaves of God' who, unlike the 'righteous', drink directly from the celestial spring of Kafur — identified ultimately with the divine Essence: 'Truly, the righteous shall drink from a cup flavoured with Kafur, a spring wherefrom the slaves of God drink, making it gush forth abundantly' (76: 5–6). These slaves cause the flow of the spring to increase because, according to al-Kashani, 'they are [themselves] the sources of this spring; there is no duality or otherness ... were it otherwise, it would not be the fountain of Kafur, because of the darkness of the veil of egoity (ana'iyya) and duality.'

Thus we return to the final degree of the dhikr as al-Kashani defined it above: the dhikr of the Essence, 'which is essential vision, attained through the removal of duality'. The dhikr 'polishes' away not only the impurities of the heart and mind, but ultimately — through the grace of the madhkur — all taint of duality, including above all that primary postulate of duality, the individual as such. It removes every subtle or hidden form of shirk — the 'partners' in being that are non-existent before the One 'that has no second' — and establishes the spiritual principle of tawhid, such that the heart transfigured by knowledge of the sole Reality comes to perceive the light of that Reality reflected in all the mirrors of the created realm. To realize — to 'make real' in the fullest sense — this one and only Reality is, by the same token, to realize the unreality, or merely apparent reality, of everything else.

At this level of realization there is an inescapable degree of perplexity that accompanies the revelation of the divine Reality all around one. The elevation of the divine Essence beyond all comprehension makes the utter annihilation of creaturely consciousness all the more unavoidable if that Essence is to be 'realized' and gnosis of it attained. On the one hand, one 'knows' God by knowing that His Essence is unknowable, utterly other, utterly transcendent, the essence of the Creator lying beyond the veil constituted by creation itself; on the other hand, the very distinction between creature and Creator is effaced when the creature has 'forgotten' itself — not merely notionally but existentially, in a state of authentic contemplative extinction. Then it is no longer a question of one form of self-consciousness replacing another, but of the 'self' being removed from the equation altogether, leaving only pure consciousness as such, which cannot 'belong' to any entity without being reduced to the relativity proper to that entity. Thus, consciousness of the Absolute — that is, 'being conscious of the Absolute' — is transmuted into absolute consciousness; the Real is 'realized' not by another, but through another. Hence the designation al-'arif bi'Llah, literally 'the knower through God' rather than simply 'the knower of God.' As we noted earlier, the Imam tells us, 'Know God through God.' Knowledge of God at this deepest level is the Real knowing itself through the creature; and to say 'through' implies unimpeded passage — the creature being characterized by perfect faqr, poverty, self-effacement, and ultimately extinction.

Realization through remembrance, however, implies not only 'forgetting' everything in existence except God, but also finding that in reality there is nothing but God in being. Returning to the Imam's image of the 'polish' effected by remembrance: the heart is rendered capable of 'seeing' and 'hearing' after having been blind and deaf. The heart sees the Face of God wherever it looks and hears the cosmic hymn of remembrance and praise chanted by the whole of creation. For: 'Wherever ye turn, there is the Face of God' (2: 115), and '... whosoever is in the Heavens and the earth praises God; and the birds in flight with outstretched wings — each knoweth its prayer and glorification' (24: 41).

We conclude this chapter with a reflection on a passage from the Imam's famous and much-disputed Khutbat al-bayan, 'The Sermon of Clarifying Expression':

I am the Sign of the All-Powerful. I am the gnosis of the mysteries. I am the Threshold of Thresholds. I am the companion of the radiance of the divine Majesty. I am the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden. I am the Face of God. I am the mirror of God, the supreme Pen, the Tabula secreta. I am he who in the Gospel is called Elijah. I am he who is in possession of the secret of God's Messenger. 141

This extraordinary affirmation of the Imam's inmost reality may appear shocking to the exoteric mind, and is all too easily misread as a blasphemous self-deification in the manner of al-Hallaj. One key to understanding these expressions of 'his' true nature correctly is the line: 'I am the gnosis of the mysteries.' Once this is grasped, all the other statements fall naturally into place. For here the Imam is identifying himself not with God, but with the knowledge of God — and not with his own knowledge of God, since that would remain a relative knowledge belonging to himself as a relative being. Only the knowledge itself — the knowledge that God has of Himself, not the knowledge that man has of God — can be fully identified with the object known. The distinctions between knowledge, knower, and known are dissolved only at this transcendent level, just as the distinction between knowledge and being ceases to operate at this level of consciousness. The knower is the known when the knowledge in question is that consciousness which the divine Reality has of itself; and individual consciousness can access this knowledge only on condition of the extinction of all trace of individual, creaturely selfhood. To 'remember' God, one must have 'forgotten' oneself. To 'be' the knowledge of God is, by metaphysical reverberation, to 'be' everything that God is, for the consciousness absolutely at one with the Absolute is nothing other than 'the radiance of the divine Majesty'. Insofar as this consciousness is one with the 'gnosis of the mysteries', and these mysteries (asrar) are all rooted in the ultimate mystery (al-sirr) — the one and only Essence of God — it is perforce one with all the divine attributes. It is thus that the Imam can proclaim: 'I am the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden.'

Analogous observations can be made about the claim to be 'the Face of God' (wajh Allah). This is comprehensible only if one first understands that the Imam refers to himself as the 'mirror' of God. A mirror loses its identity as mirror when attention is focused exclusively on the object reflected in it — that is, to the total exclusion of the mirror itself: the object alone is seen, and the surface of the mirror becomes, as it were, invisible. Conversely, when attention falls on the mirror itself, the face within it can no longer be seen. Thus the mirror is identified with the Face projected upon it — which, in a certain sense, is 'in' the mirror — without the mirror ceasing to be a mirror and without the Face ceasing to be absolutely independent of it. 142 The 'veil' here is the mirror itself: when attention is directed toward it as such, this very orientation prevents one from seeing the object reflected in it; but once attention is fixed on the object whose image the mirror 'contains', the mirror ceases to be a veil and instead reveals the object itself. In terms of this symbolism, forgetting the mirror is tantamount to the effacement of the self, and fixing one's gaze on the image within the mirror is tantamount to the remembrance of God.

But this leads to another paradox: the deepest effacement of individuality goes hand in hand with the most perfect expression of humanity. The 'friends of God', who know they are nothing before the Absolute, are the very ones who most faithfully reflect the divine Names and qualities — which belong not to them but to God. It is for this reason that the great saints and sages are also exemplars of virtue, whose true foundations are the 'most beautiful Names' and attributes of God. The reflections of these divine attributes shine through them most clearly precisely because the mirrors of their hearts have been polished by the remembrance of God and the most persistent stains — those imprinted by egotism — have been removed. The creativity of the saint is raised to the highest possible pitch precisely because the divine qualities reflected by the heart are no longer impeded, subjectively, by the individualistic coagulations generated by egotism, nor constrained, objectively, by the cosmic coagulations of this lower world. Such saints are the ones whose 'hearts are in the Gardens [of Paradise] while their bodies are at work'.

Such souls have not only understood but have been existentially seized by the principle expressed in the Qur'anic verse: 'And the life of this world is naught but sport and play; and truly, the abode of the Hereafter — that is true life' (29: 64). Their instincts are therefore always attuned to that higher reality. When Ali (ع) was struck by the poisoned sword of Ibn Muljam, he instinctively uttered the cry, 'I have triumphed, by the Lord of the Ka'ba!' At the very moment of death there was nothing but the remembrance of God, and the Imam passed away — it is said — with his tongue repeating the words la ilaha illa'Llah. His death was thus consummated just as his life had been lived, in accordance with the wisdom of the Prophet, who, upon being asked what was the most meritorious of all acts, replied: '[That] you die while your tongue is moistened with the invocation of God.'

## The First Sermon of Nahj al-Balagha

Praise be to God, whose laudation no speaker can deliver, whose graces no one can number, and whose rightful due no striving can fully render; He who cannot be grasped by far-reaching aspirations, nor fathomed by the deepest intuitions; He whose attribute admits no binding limitation, no existing description, no appointed time, no extended term. Through His power He originated all created things; through His mercy He diffused the comforting winds; and He entrenched His swaying earth with rocky pillars.

Foremost in religion is knowledge of Him, and the perfection of this knowledge is believing in Him, and the perfection of this belief is affirming His oneness, and the perfection of this affirmation is to purify one's devotion to Him, and the perfection of this purification is to divest Him of all attributes — because every attribute testifies that it is other than the object of attribution, and every such object testifies that it is other than the attribute. So whoever ascribes an attribute to God — glorified be He — has conjoined Him with something else; whoever conjoins Him has made Him twofold; whoever makes Him twofold has fragmented Him; and whoever fragments Him is ignorant of Him. Whoever points to Him confines Him; whoever confines Him counts Him; whoever asks 'in what?' encloses Him; and whoever asks 'upon what?' isolates Him.

Being, but not by way of any becoming; existing, but not from having been non-existent; with every thing, but not through association; and other than every thing, but not through separation; acting, but not through movements and instruments; seeing, even when nothing of His creation was yet to be seen; solitary, even when there was none whose intimacy might be sought or whose absence might be missed.

He brought forth creation, originating it from the very beginning, without pondering any deliberation or drawing on any prior experience; without initiating any movement or engaging in any unsettling exertion. He assigned to all things their span of time and harmonized their differences; He instilled within them their natural dispositions, impressing upon them their respective forms; knowing them before their genesis, encompassing their limits and their ends, and aware of their configurations and their contours.

Then He — glorified be He — opened a fissure in the atmospheres, cleaving the directions and the uppermost reaches of air; then He caused water to flow therein, a current of surging waves swelling and swirling in overflowing abundance. Then He mounted it upon a tempestuous wind, a thunderous gale; then He made the wind hold back the water's return, enabling it to dominate the violence of the water's flow, and aligning it with the water's boundaries. The air beneath the wind spread wide open, while the water above flowed copiously.

Then He raised up another wind, making it sterile, fixing its course, intensifying its current, and distancing it from its source. Then He ordered it to agitate the swelling waters and to stir up the waves of the deep. The wind churned the waters — as one churns milk in a milkbag — imparting its own violence to the water in the atmosphere, turning the foremost back upon the hindmost, and stillness into motion, so that its torrents surged and its swelling waves threw up foam. Then He elevated that foam within a cleft air and a vast open atmosphere, and formed from it the seven heavens, making the lowest a suspended wave and the highest a protected shelter and elevated ceiling, without any pillars to support them or any fastening to transfix them. Then He adorned the heavens with the ornaments of the stars and the radiance of the firmament, and made a radiant lamp — the sun — and an illuminating moon to follow an orbit in a revolving sphere, beneath a roof in motion and a firmament in flux. Then He parted the highest heavens and filled them with angels of different dispositions: some prostrating and never bowing; others bowing and never standing upright; some arrayed in ranks, never separating from one another; others glorifying, never wearying — not overcome by sleepiness in the eyes, distraction in the mind, fatigue in the body, or the negligence of forgetfulness. Among them are custodians of His Revelation, conveyors of His Words to His Messengers, and those who come and go with His Decree and His Command. Among them are the guardians of His slaves and the wardens of the gates of His Gardens. And among them are those whose feet stand firmly on the lowest worlds, whose necks surpass the highest heaven, whose sides exceed all bounds, and whose shoulders are proportioned to the pillars of the Throne — their eyes cast down before it, wrapping themselves in their wings beneath it. Between them and all others there is a barrier: veils of Glory and screens of Might. They do not imagine their Lord through formal representation, nor ascribe to Him created attributes; they do not confine Him to places, nor allude to Him through similitudes.

Then He — glorified be He — gathered a quantity of dust from hard earth and soft, from sweet earth and salty, and poured water onto it until it was pure; then He kneaded moisture into it until it held together. Then He fashioned from this a form with curves and joints, limbs and parts. He compacted it until it held tightly, solidified it until it became sounding clay, and assigned to it a measured duration and a known end. Then He breathed into it of His spirit, and it stood up as a human being — endowed with intellectual powers for reflection, thoughts by which to conduct himself, limbs to put to service, instruments at his disposal, and knowledge with which to discern between the true and the false, between different tastes and fragrances, and between different colours and categories; a being whose constitution was compounded of different hues, unified forms, contrasting oppositions, and distinct admixtures of heat and cold, moisture and dryness.

And God — glorified be He — demanded from the angels the fulfilment of what He had charged them with, and their compliance with His injunction, by ordering their prostration before Adam and humble reverence of him. So He said, 'Prostrate to Adam', and they prostrated — all except Iblis. Passionate scorn unhinged him; wretchedness overwhelmed him. He prided himself on his constitution of fire and disdained the creature made of clay. Then God granted him a respite, so that His wrath would be fully warranted, the affliction consummated, and the promise fulfilled. So He said, 'You are among those given respite until a day whose time is appointed.'

Then He — glorified be He — settled Adam in an abode where his life was made comfortable and his domain secure, and He warned him of Iblis and his enmity. Yet Adam's enemy beguiled him, driven by envious desire for Adam's immortal dwelling and his intimacy with the righteous. So Adam exchanged certainty for doubt and resolution for weakness, and replaced joy with dread and self-esteem with remorse. Then God accepted his repentance, enveloped him in a word from His Mercy, and promised him a return to His Garden. Then He caused him to descend to the abode of tribulations and to the begetting of progeny. From his offspring God chose prophets, taking from them a pledge regarding the Revelation and entrusting to them the conveyance of His Message — at a time when most of His creatures had deviated from their covenant with God, ignoring His due, setting up peers alongside Him, while the demons distracted them from knowledge of Him and sundered them from His worship. So He sent His messengers among them, dispatching prophets in succession, in order to claim from His creatures the fulfilment of the covenant of creation; to remind them of His forgotten graces; to remonstrate with them through the communication of His Revelation; to unearth for them the buried treasures of the intellect; and to show them the signs of His omnipotence: the outspread earth laid low beneath them, provisions that sustain their life, finalities that mete out death, ailments hastening their decrepitude, and a succession of events that flows over them.

God never deprived His creation of a prophet sent forth, a revealed scripture, an imperative proof, or a clear path — messengers undimmed by the scarcity of their number or the multitude of their deniers. Among them, those who came before were told the names of those who would follow, and those who came after were informed of those who came before. In this way generations were begotten and ages elapsed, fathers passing away and sons succeeding them, until God sent forth Muhammad, Messenger of God, in fulfilment of His promise and completion of His cycle of prophecy — he regarding whom a pledge had been taken from the prophets: renowned through signs, of noble birth. At that time the people of the earth were divided into different religious communities; caprice was widespread and pathways were disordered — among them paths such as likening God to His creation, distorting His Names, and alluding to other than Him. So God guided them through the Prophet out of error and, through his presence, delivered them from their ignorance.

Then God chose for Muhammad the meeting with Him and desired for him what was in His own keeping; He removed him with dignity from the abode of this lower world and drew him away from any link with the domain of trial, taking him to Himself honourably. He left behind among you that which the prophets leave for their communities, lest they abandon them to wander without a clear path or a visible beacon: the scripture of your Lord, clarifying through it what is lawful and unlawful, the obligatory and the recommended, the abrogating verses and the abrogated, the conditionally permissible and the unconditionally compulsory, the specific and the general — His admonitions and analogies, undelimited and delimited ordinances, unambiguous and equivocal verses — elucidating what is dense and clarifying what is obscure therein.

In it are matters knowledge of which is binding upon His slaves, and others whose ignorance is overlooked; injunctions affirmed as obligatory in the scripture yet known to be abrogated by the Sunna; and actions made obligatory by the Sunna yet permitted to be set aside by the scripture; duties incumbent at their prescribed time but which cease to be so at a later time; the distinction between different forbidden acts — the major ones which He has threatened to punish with His fires, and the minor ones for which He has prepared His forgiveness; and acts that are acceptable when performed to a modest degree, and praiseworthy to a higher degree, without being obligatory.


## The Letter of Ali (ع) to Malik al-Ashtar

(Written when the Imam appointed him as governor of Egypt and its regions, at a time when the rule of its governor, Muhammad b. Abi Bakr, was unstable; it is the longest of the letters written by the Imam, and the most all-embracing as regards beauty of form and excellence of meaning.)

In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful.

This is what the servant of God, Ali (ع), Commander of the Faithful, enjoins upon Malik b. al-Harith al-Ashtar, in his mandate ('ahd) to him, appointing him as governor of Egypt: to collect its revenues; to fight its enemies; to establish the welfare of its inhabitants; and to bring prosperity to its lands.

He enjoins him to fear God; to place obedience to God above all else; and to abide by what He has commanded in His Book — both the obligatory and the recommended — for no one prospers except by abiding by them, and no one is brought to ruin except by repudiating and neglecting them. He further enjoins him to assist God with his heart, his hand, and his tongue, for God — majestic is His Name — has undertaken to grant victory to whoever assists Him and to elevate whoever exalts Him. He enjoins him to curb the passionate desires of his soul and to restrain it when seized by whim and caprice, for the soul inclines toward evil unless God grants His mercy.

Know, Malik, that I am sending you to a land that has been governed by rulers — just and unjust — before you. The people will assess your conduct just as you have assessed the conduct of those who governed before you. They will speak of you just as you spoke of them. The righteous are recognized as such only through what God causes to flow from the tongues of His servants. Let the treasure you cherish most be the treasure of righteous deeds. Master your desires, and exercise restraint before what is forbidden to you — for self-restraint cultivates within the soul a fitting balance between what it craves and what it rejects.

Fill your heart with mercy toward your subjects, love for them, and kindness to them. Do not be like a predatory beast looming over them, looking to devour them. They are of two kinds: either your brother in faith or your equal in creation. Faults slip from them; failings emerge from them, deliberately or by accident. Grant them your forgiveness and pardon, just as you would have God grant you His forgiveness and pardon. You are placed above them, and the one who appointed you is placed above you, and God is above the one who appointed you. God expects you to fulfill their needs, and through them He puts you to the test.

Do not set your soul on a course of war with God, for you have no defense against His retribution and no independence from His forgiveness and mercy. Feel no regret when you pardon, and take no pleasure when you punish. Do not let impulse drive you rashly into any course of action when you can see an alternative. Do not say, "I have been given authority; I command and am obeyed," for this corrupts the heart, erodes religion, and draws the adversities of fate closer. If the power of your position breeds vanity and arrogance, look to the grandeur of God's dominion above you, and to His power to do for you what you cannot do for yourself. This will calm your ambition, check your vehemence, and restore what had slipped from your reason. Beware of comparing yourself with God in greatness or likening yourself to Him in might, for God humbles every tyrant and disgraces every braggart. Be just toward God and be just toward people — rendering what is due from yourself, from your close relatives, and from those among your subjects to whom you feel most attached. Fail in this and you will be an oppressor. Whoever oppresses the servants of God will find that both God and His servants turn against him. God invalidates the argument of whoever He opposes, and the oppressor remains at war with God until he desists and repents. Nothing more surely strips away God's grace and hastens His retribution than persistence in oppression, for God hears the cry of the oppressed and keeps a vigilant watch over the oppressors.

Let the affairs dearest to you be those most firmly grounded in what is right, most comprehensive in justice, and most inclusive of the contentment of your people. The discontent of the common folk (al-'amma) undermines the contentment of the elite (al-khassa), while the discontent of the elite is offset by the contentment of the common folk. In times of ease, no subjects weigh more heavily on the ruler in their demands for his favour than the elite, and none offer less help when times grow hard; none are more averse to justice, more insistent in their demands, less grateful for what is given, slower to forgive when denied, and less patient before the turns of fortune. The common folk, by contrast, are the pillar of the religion, the cohesion of the Muslims, and the instrument for fighting the enemy — so be well disposed toward them and incline your heart to them.

Keep farthest from you, and hold in lowest regard, those of your subjects who are most eager to seek out the faults of others. People do have faults, and it befits the governor above all others to conceal them. Do not expose what remains hidden from you — your duty is only to address what has become plainly apparent. God will judge what remains concealed. Veil deficiencies as much as you are able, so that God may veil from your subjects what you wish to keep veiled in yourself. Untie every knot of resentment among the people, and sever from yourself every rope of rancour. Disregard whatever is obscure to you, and never be quick to believe a slanderer — for a slanderer is a deceiver, even when he presents himself as a sincere adviser.

Admit no misers into your circle of counsel, for they will turn you away from generosity and threaten you with poverty. Admit no cowards, for they will weaken your resolve in your affairs. Admit none who are avaricious, for they will dress up greed as justice before your eyes. Miserliness, cowardice, and avarice are so many distinct expressions of a poor opinion of God. The worst of your ministers is the one who served evil rulers before you and shared in their sins — do not admit such people into your inner circle, for they are helpers of wrongdoers and companions of tyrants. The best replacements for them are those who are equally capable and intelligent, but carry none of their burden of sin and have aided neither tyrant in his tyranny nor sinner in his sin. Such people will cost you less in terms of provision, render you more effective assistance, incline toward you more sincerely in affection, and feel less attachment to anyone outside you. Choose such people as your intimate companions, both in private and in public. Within this group, give preference to the one who speaks truth most plainly to you, however bitter it may be, and who least supports you in whatever God dislikes for His friends, however sharply that may cut against your desires.

Seek out those who are known for their piety and sincerity, and cultivate in them the habit of not flattering you or heaping praise on you for something you have not actually done. Excessive praise breeds pride and carries a person headlong toward vainglory. Do not place the virtuous and the wicked on the same footing before you, for this leads the virtuous to think little of their virtues while the wicked become further entrenched in their vices. Require of each what their own conduct has earned. Know that nothing so effectively builds a governor's confidence in his subjects as his treating them well, easing their hardships, and refraining from burdening them beyond their capacity.

Let there arise a state of affairs in which you can rely on your subjects with confidence, for that will spare you a great deal of trouble. The person most worthy of your trust is the one through whom your trials have gone well; the one most deserving of your suspicion is the one through whom your trials have gone badly.

Do not break any beneficial tradition established by the leaders of this community through which unity has been harmoniously sustained and from which the subjects have prospered. Do not introduce any new practice that damages what is already established. If you do so, the reward for observing those traditions will belong to whoever established them, while the burden of their destruction will fall upon you.

Study much with the scholars (al-'ulama) and hold much discourse with the sages (al-hukama), in order to consolidate what brings well-being to your lands and to strengthen what your predecessors have already put in place. Know that your subjects are made up of various classes, none of which can thrive unless the others do, and none of which can function independently of the rest. Among these classes are the soldiers of God (junud Allah); the scribes (kuttab, sing. katib) who administer the affairs of both the common people and the elite; the judges (qudat al-'adl); officials responsible for upholding fairness and establishing the right of redress (rifq); those who pay the jizya (poll-tax) and the kharaj (land-tax) from among the protected people (ahl al-dhimma) and the Muslims; the merchants and artisans; and the lowest class, consisting of the needy and destitute. God has assigned each class its share and has ordained — as a binding covenant ('ahdan) with us from Him — its limits and its duties, according to His Book or the Sunna of the Prophet, God bless him and his family.

As for the soldiers, they are — by the grace of God — the fortresses of the subjects, the adornment of the governors, the strength of religion, and the pathways to security (subul al-amn). The subjects cannot sustain themselves without the soldiers, and the soldiers cannot be sustained without the revenues of the kharaj which God provides for them. With these revenues they have the means to fight their enemies, attend to their welfare, and meet their needs. Neither of these two classes — the soldiers and the peasants — can be properly sustained without the third: the judges, administrators, and scribes, who uphold all contracts, harmonize and organize all interests, and are entrusted with both the particular and general affairs of state and society. All of these classes in turn depend on the merchants and artisans, who gather the necessary goods and establish the markets, fulfilling through their particular functions the needs that no one else's work can supply. Finally, there is the lowest class — the needy and the destitute, those who deserve assistance and favour. For each class there is abundance with God, and each has, in relation to the governor, a right proportioned to the requirements of its welfare. The governor cannot fully discharge the duties God has placed upon him without firm resolve and reliance on God's help (al-ihtimam wa'l-isti'ana), readying himself for the demands of upright conduct, and bearing patiently with whatever ease or hardship comes.

Appoint as commander of your soldiers the person you feel most deeply to be the most sincere toward God, the Prophet, and your Imam — the purest of heart; the most excellent in forbearance (hilm); slow to anger; ready to pardon; kind to the weak; firm with the strong; one who is neither driven by recklessness nor held back by timidity. Favor those of noble lineage, belonging to pious families of established name and repute, and men known for their bravery, courage, generosity (al-sakha'), and tolerance (al-samaha) — for they form a community shaped by nobility and a company built on honor. Then attend to their affairs as a parent would attend to a child. Let no act through which you strengthen them seem too great in your eyes, and do not dismiss any kindness — however slight — that you have promised them, for even small kindnesses draw them to offer you good counsel and to place their trust in you. Do not set aside close attention to their affairs in favor of what may seem weightier, for there are moments when they benefit greatly from even a small act of kindness, and others in which they cannot do without what is most substantial.

Let the commander you favor most be the one who is most generous to the soldiers and most giving from his own means, so that both they and their dependents after them have enough. Let their energies converge on a single purpose: fighting the enemy. Your generosity toward the soldiers will cause their hearts to turn toward you.

The greatest source of joy for any governor is the establishment of justice throughout the land and the outpouring of love from his subjects. Such love will not become evident until their hearts feel secure. And sound counsel from them will not flow unless the governor supervises those who administer the subjects' affairs, ensures that the burden of governance rests lightly upon the people, and removes the anxious hope that his rule might come to an end. Raise their hopes, sustain your praise of them, and enumerate the courageous deeds of those who have acted bravely — for abundant acknowledgment of noble acts rouses the brave and spurs on the sluggish, God willing. Recognize the courageous deed of every man; do not attribute the deed of one to another, and do not fall short in granting due recognition. Do not let a man's high standing lead you to count a small accomplishment as great, and do not let a man's lowly station lead you to count a great one as small. Refer to God and His Messenger any matter that weighs heavily upon you or remains unclear, for God the Exalted has declared to a people He loved to guide: "O ye who believe, obey God and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. And if ye dispute with one another over anything, then refer it to God and the Messenger" (Qur'an, 4:59). To "refer to God" means following what is clear and unequivocal in His Book; to "refer to the Messenger" means following that part of his Sunna which unites rather than divides.

Select as judges those you consider the most excellent of your subjects — those who are not confused by complex matters, nor provoked to anger by disputants; who do not persist in error and are not reluctant to turn toward truth once they perceive it; whose souls are not susceptible to greed; who, dissatisfied with a surface understanding, will probe more deeply; who are most careful in the face of ambiguities; most rigorous in argumentation; least unsettled by the appeals of litigants; most patient in working to uncover the true state of affairs; most resolute when the right judgment becomes clear; and who are neither flattered by praise nor led astray by blandishment. Such people are rare indeed.

Then examine carefully and frequently how the judge's verdicts are carried out, and be generous in his remuneration so that any material shortfall is removed and his need to seek assistance from others is diminished. Grant him a rank of closeness to you that none among your companions can aspire to match. He must feel secure in your presence and be shielded from the encroachments of powerful men. Subject the judiciary to penetrating scrutiny, for this religion was once held captive in the hands of evil people, who abused it according to their whims and exploited it for worldly gain.

Then consider your administrators. Appoint them only after proper examination, and do not assign them posts out of partiality or favoritism, for such indulgence breeds injustice and treachery in various forms. Seek out men of proven experience and integrity from among righteous households — families with a distinguished standing in Islam — for they are the most noble in character, the most firmly rooted in dignity, the least susceptible to greedy desires, and the most keenly aware of the consequences of all things. Provide them with a generous


stipend, for this will strengthen them, enabling them to attend to their own welfare, obviating any need to appropriate what lies within their power, and standing as an argument against them should they disobey your orders or betray your trust. Monitor their conduct carefully by appointing supervisors known for their sincerity and reliability to watch over them. Your covert observation of their affairs will motivate them to fulfil their trust and to deal considerately with those in their charge. Pay attention also to your assistants: should any one of them reach toward some treacherous act — and your observers are unanimous in their reports, which you deem sufficient evidence — then impose corporal punishment upon him. Let him be chastised for the harm his act has caused. Let him be disgraced, branded a traitor, and made to bear the ignominy of accusation.

Examine the question of the kharaj land-tax with care, so that those subject to it are kept in a proper state of well-being, for it is from their welfare, and from the sound collection of the tax itself, that the benefit of others flows. There can be no welfare for anyone else without them — the peasants — for all without exception depend on the revenues of the kharaj and those who pay it. Let your concern for the cultivation of the land outweigh your concern for the collection of the tax, for no tax can be collected where there is no cultivation. Whoever exacts the tax without cultivating the land ruins the land and destroys the people, and his rule will not endure long.

So if they complain of a heavy burden — whether from some deficiency, the cutting of irrigation supplies, lack of rain, or a change in the condition of the soil through flooding or drought — then lighten their burden, to the extent that you hope their situation may be set right. Do not let any favour you grant them by way of relief weigh heavily upon you, for this is an investment that will yield returns in the prosperity of your land and the adornment of your rule, through the finest praise they will render you and the pride you will take in dispensing justice among them. You can then count on an increase in their capacity as a result of what you have invested in them when you gave them respite, and you can trust them because you have accustomed them to receiving your justice and kindness.

There may come a time when you must rely upon them, and they will gladly bear whatever burden you place on them, for a prosperous land can sustain what a devastated one cannot. The devastation of the land comes only from the destitution of its people, and the destitution of its people comes only when the desire to amass wealth takes hold of the souls of the governors — when they grow uncertain about what endures, and when they profit little from the lessons of history. Now attend carefully to the situation of your scribes, and appoint the best of them to manage your affairs. Entrust the most sensitive strategies and secrets to the scribe in whom you find the most complete moral qualities: one who will not be emboldened by his elevated position to contradict you before others in a public assembly; one who is not negligent in delivering correspondence from your officials to you, nor in properly dispatching your replies on what he handles on your behalf. He must not weaken any agreement contracted on your behalf, nor be incapable of repudiating what has been contracted to your detriment. He must not be ignorant of the value of his own capabilities, for one who is unaware of his own worth will be even more ignorant of the worth of others. Do not allow yourself to rely solely on your own judgment, confidence, and trust in selecting such a person, for men know how to present themselves favorably to rulers through pretence and attentive service, behind which there may be no sincerity or fidelity at all. Instead, investigate them carefully by looking at how they performed in the service of good people before you, and place your trust in the one whose virtue left the deepest impression on ordinary people and whose reputation for integrity is most widely known. This will be proof of your sincerity toward God and toward the one who appointed you.

Appoint for each of your affairs a senior officer among your officials — one who will not be daunted by the magnitude of affairs or scattered by their multiplicity. Any fault of your scribes that you overlook will be attributed to you. Then attend to merchants and artisans, and treat them well, urging others to do the same. Among them are those who are settled, those who travel with their goods, and those who earn their living by their hands. They are sources of benefit and the means by which necessities are obtained. Through them, goods are procured from distant and remote places — brought by land and sea, from plains and mountains, from places where men do not settle and do not dare to venture. They live in a state of reconciliation (silm), from which no calamity need be feared, and in a state of peace (sulh), from which no disturbance need be feared. Examine their affairs attentively, both those close to you and those in every corner of your land. Be aware, however, that despite all of this, many of them harbour a despicable stinginess, a repugnant greed, a tendency to hoard goods, and an inclination to fix prices arbitrarily. All of this brings loss to the populace and is a source of shame for the governors. So prohibit hoarding, for the Messenger of God — God bless him and his family — prohibited it. Let trade be conducted with propriety and fairness, at prices that do not harm either party, buyer or seller. If any one of them yields to the temptation to hoard after you have prohibited it, inflict upon him an exemplary — but not excessive — punishment.

Then — O God, O God! — give particular attention to the lowest class: those who have nothing, the destitute, the needy, the afflicted, the disabled. Within this class are those who beg, and those whose wretchedness calls out to be relieved but who do not beg. Be mindful of God regarding their rights, for He has entrusted those rights to your care. Assign them a portion from your public treasury (bayt al-mal), and a portion of the produce of what the Muslims take as booty in every region, for those who are furthest have the same rights as those who are nearest. Upholding the right of each of them is incumbent upon you. Do not let haughtiness cause you to neglect them, for you will not be pardoned even the smallest shortcoming in fulfilling your obligations toward them on account of having attended to some seemingly important matter. Do not turn your concern away from them, nor adopt a contemptuous attitude toward them. Keep watch over the affairs of those who have no access to you and who are looked down upon by men of high standing. Appoint from among those you trust a God-fearing and humble person to be responsible for bringing their affairs to your attention. Treat these people in a manner for which God may excuse you on the Day you meet Him, for they stand in greater need of justice from you than any others among your subjects. Offer your excuse to God concerning each of them in the fulfilment of their rights. Take responsibility for the orphans and the elderly — those who have no resources yet cannot bring themselves to beg. This is burdensome for governors, and the fulfilment of all rights is burdensome, but God makes it light for those who aspire to the Hereafter, who restrain their souls in patience, and who trust in the truth of what God has promised them. Set aside a portion of your time for those with special needs, making yourself freely available to them in person, sitting with them in a public assembly with all due humility before God, your Creator. Keep your soldiers, guards, and officers away from them so that they may speak to you without constraint — for I heard the Messenger of God say — God bless him and his family — on more than one occasion: 'A nation in which the rights of the weak are not wrested freely from the strong will never be blessed.'

Bear patiently whatever coarseness or inarticulate expression they may show; do not display irritation or disdain toward them — for by this God will extend the most expansive dimensions of His compassion to you and make the reward for your obedience to Him certain. Give whatever you give with generosity, and if you must withhold, do so with grace and apology.

There are certain matters you must attend to personally — among them replying to your officers when your scribes are not capable of doing so, and responding to the requests of people when they are brought before you directly because your aides find themselves unable to handle them.

Perform each day the task proper to it, for each day has its own particular task. Reserve the finest portions of your available time — the greatest and most excellent — for your own soul and for what is between you and God, even though all times and all actions performed within them are for God when the underlying intention is sound and when your subjects are at peace as a result. Let your observance of those duties (fara'id) relating exclusively to God be the special means by which you purify your religion for His sake. Give to God of your vital energy in your nights and your days, and perform in full what draws you near to God — doing so perfectly, without becoming dull or falling short, taking your body to its limits.

When you lead the people in prayer, do so without driving them away or making light of it, for among them are those with infirmities or special needs. Indeed, I asked the Messenger of God — God bless him and his family — when he sent me as his representative to Yemen, 'How should I lead them in prayer?' He replied, 'Lead them according to the prayer of the weakest among them, and be merciful to the believers.'

Beyond all of this, do not allow any prolonged absence from your subjects, for the isolation of governors from those they govern is a kind of constriction that causes a deficiency in awareness of their affairs. Such isolation cuts rulers off from knowledge of what is hidden from them, so that great things appear small and small things appear great, the beautiful appears ugly and the ugly beautiful, and truth becomes mixed with falsehood. A governor is only human: he cannot know what people conceal from him. The truth bears no visible signs by which genuine expressions of sincerity can be distinguished from falsehood.

You can only be one of two kinds of man: either one who gives himself generously for the sake of the truth — in which case why seclude yourself and thereby prevent yourself from bestowing a necessary right or performing an honourable deed? Or else you are a man afflicted with a refusal to give of himself — in which case, how quickly people will stop making requests of you, despairing of your generosity. And this despite the fact that most of the needs people bring to you are not burdensome, whether they be complaints against injustice or demands for fairness in transactions.

A governor has favourites and intimate companions, among whom some are prone to presumptuousness, arrogance, and unfairness in their dealings. Sever the root of these tendencies by eliminating their causes, and do not allot landed estates to anyone among your entourage or friends. Do not allow them to entertain any hope that you might grant them an estate, for were they to receive one it would be detrimental to those living nearby — regarding their access to water resources or any shared undertaking — the burden of which would inevitably fall on those neighbours while the profit would flow to those who received the estate and not to you. Upon you would fall only the resulting blame ('ayb), in this world and the next. Impose what is right upon whoever it falls upon, whether he is close to you or distant. Be steadfast and vigilant in this, regardless of how it affects those near and dear to you. Always seek the outcomes this principle produces, however heavily it may weigh upon you at the time, for its fruit will be praiseworthy.

If any of your subjects suspect you of an injustice, present your case clearly so that you may be exonerated, thus dispelling their suspicions through plain explanation. Such conduct is a means of self-discipline, a form of kindness toward your subjects, and a way of presenting your case that will help you to keep them upright in accordance with the truth.

Never reject a call to peace made by your enemy if God's acceptance of that call is apparent, for truly in peace lies rest for your soldiers, relief from your concerns, and security for your lands. But remain fully vigilant regarding your enemies once you have concluded a peace with them, for it is possible that the enemy seeks peace only to lull you into a false sense of security. Proceed therefore with all due caution, and guard against placing too much trust in such circumstances. But if you and your enemy enter into a solemn agreement, or if he obtains from you the right of protection (dhimma), then faithfully abide by what you have promised and honourably uphold your obligation of protection. Make your very life a shield for what you have pledged, for there is no divine obligation that so powerfully unites people — despite their diverse inclinations and differing opinions — as the honouring of one's pledges. Even the polytheists observed this among themselves, apart from their dealings with Muslims, such was their dread of the consequences of treachery. So do not violate your pledge of protection, do not break your promises, and do not be treacherous toward your enemy — for only an ignorant wretch (jahil shaqiyy) dares to oppose God. God has made His pledge and His protection a means of security, spreading it over His servants by His mercy — a sanctuary in whose impregnability they find peace, and toward whose protective power they hasten. So let there be no corruption, no treachery, and no deception. Do not enter into any agreement that contains defects, nor fall back on ambiguous connotations once an agreement has been confirmed and solemnized. Do not let any difficult situation lead you to break unfairly an agreement that God has made binding upon you. For your patience in the face of difficulty — hoping for its resolution and its positive outcome — is far better than acting treacherously and then living in fear of its consequence: being overwhelmed by an exacting demand (tilba) from God, from which you will find no exemption in this life or the next.

Beware of unlawfully shedding blood, for nothing is more conducive to retribution, more momentous in consequence, more deserving of the cessation of blessings, or more liable to cut short one's span of life than the unjust shedding of blood. On the Day of Resurrection, God — glorified be He — begins His judgment of His servants with the blood they have shed. So do not seek to strengthen your authority through unlawful bloodshed, for such action in truth weakens and debilitates it, brings it to an end, and removes it entirely.

You have no excuse before God or before me if you intentionally murder anyone, for this demands the ultimate penalty. If you fall into some error and your tongue or your hand goes too far in inflicting punishment — for even a blow with the fist and other such assaults can be a cause of death — do not let pride in your power lead you to seek any way of avoiding the payment of what is rightfully owed as recompense to the relatives of the person killed.


Beware of self-satisfaction, of overconfidence in what you admire about yourself, and of loving flattery, for these are among Satan's most reliable openings to erase the virtue (ihsan) of the virtuous (al-muhsinin). Beware of making your subjects feel indebted to you for your goodness toward them, of exaggerating your own deeds, and of making promises you do not keep. Making people feel beholden corrupts virtue; exaggeration extinguishes the light of truth; and breaking promises earns you the hatred of both God and men. God the Exalted has said: "It is indeed hateful to God that ye say that which ye do not" (61:3). Beware of rushing matters before their proper time, of letting opportunities slip away through neglect, of stubbornness when affairs prove difficult, and of weakness when they become plainly clear. Handle every matter in its proper place and at its proper time. Beware of seizing what all people hold in common, and of neglecting what is urgent and self-evident, for that harms you and benefits others. Soon enough, the veil covering all affairs will be lifted, and those who have been wronged will demand justice from you. Master the zeal of your pride, the force of your censure, the power of your hand, and the sharpness of your tongue. Guard against these vices by restraining every impulse and deferring every resort to force until your anger subsides and you recover self-control. But such self-mastery is unattainable without deepening your preoccupation with remembrance of your return to your Lord.

It is incumbent upon you to remember what transpired in earlier times regarding just rule and virtuous conduct, the practices of the Prophet — God bless him and his family — and the obligations enshrined in the Book of God. Follow faithfully what you have witnessed us doing in these matters, and strive with all your soul to act in accordance with the injunctions contained in this mandate of mine. I am confident that this mandate will stand as an argument for myself against you, so you will have no excuse if your soul instead rushes to satisfy its own caprice.

I beseech God, by the abundance of His compassion and the magnitude of His power, to fulfill every desire, to cause me and you to do that which pleases Him, to present a clear justification before Him and His creatures, to earn the fairest praise among His servants, and to leave behind us the most beautiful traces in the land. I beseech Him to grant us perfect blessings and ever-increasing honor, and to seal the lives of myself and yourself with felicity (al-sa'ada) and the testimony (al-shahada). "Truly unto Him we are returning" (2:156).

And peace be with the Messenger of God — God bless him and his good and pure progeny.
