## Source chapter 1 — Introducing Ali (ع) and his Spiritual Ethos

`ch01a-ali-and-the-prophet.txt` — Source chapter 1 opens with the "Introducing Ali" and "Biographical Sketch" material and the list of prophetic testimonies (source lines 5–114), which together form one whole thematic unit: who Ali (ع) is and why his sayings carry weight. Episode 1 keeps that unit intact across three concepts — the affinity and identity between Ali (ع) and the Prophet (interiorization of the Qur'an and the prophetic Sunna, the gate of knowledge); the biographical arc from the Ka'ba to Khaybar, Fatima, and the Ahl al-Bayt; and the prophetic testimonies (fada'il) culminating at Ghadir Khumm, where walaya is framed as universal spiritual sanctity rather than sectarian politics. The noise rule was applied lightly here: the source's own front-matter about the corpus of sayings and the historiographical caveats were compressed to the minimum needed to introduce the testimonies, and the deliberate setting-aside of the Sunni–Shi'i political dispute was preserved as the author's own move, not reconstructed as book-object apparatus. Landed at 5,699 words, inside the extended band.

`ch02b-why-intellect-not-reason.txt` — "The Spirit Of The Intellect" ran to roughly 6,250 words across source lines 117–181, exceeding the 6,000 band ceiling and carrying four distinct teachings, so it was split at the seam between the intellect/reason argument and the apophasis. Episode 2 takes the first half (lines 117–144): why the author prefers "intellect" (intellectus/nous, contemplative vision) over "reason" (ratio/dianoia, discursive comparison) as the rendering of 'aql; the bedouin at the battle of the Camel and the oneness beyond number ("that which has no second"); and ta'wil as the return to al-awwal, with revelation and intellect in creative reciprocity and the intellect as itself a mode of revelation. The forward-looking sentence that previews the discourse to Kumayl was left as a functional gesture rather than reproduced, so no anchor material was lost. Landed at 5,827 words.

`ch03c-paradox-and-the-inbreathed-spirit.txt` — Episode 3 takes the second half of "The Spirit Of The Intellect" (source lines 146–181): the continuity and discontinuity between the intellect's depth and its rational surface (hadd as both definition and limit; the heart that sees through the verities of faith); the apophatic discourse of the first sermon, where the divine attributes are simultaneously affirmed and negated on the model of "no god except God"; and the in-breathing of the spirit into man, which makes the intellect a living, spiritual faculty and defines the true intellectual (al-'aqil) as one who puts each thing in its proper place. The concept-2 heading was shortened from the source's own phrasing to a concise noun phrase ("The depth of paradox") to satisfy the heading-length rule; no content was dropped. The excerpts of the first sermon here are quoted for commentary and are not the whole sermon — the complete first sermon is its own primary text later in the book — so this episode is not a verbatim-sermon episode. Landed at 5,996 words.

`ch04d-the-lordly-knower-and-the-inner-struggle.txt` — "The Discourse To Kumayl" and its commentary run about 9,800 words (source lines 183–262 and beyond), a floor of two episodes, split at the seam between the inner struggle and the epistemology of joy. Episode 4 takes the first half (lines 183–254): the discourse to Kumayl itself, rendered whole as a verbatim sermon section per the sermon rule (the three types of people, knowledge above wealth, and the bearers of God's proof); the commentary on knowledge storming the heart (zahir/batin, the battlefield sense of hajama, realization versus verification); and the greatest struggle (al-jihad al-akbar) against the soul, with the intellect leading the forces of the Compassionate against caprice, and the integration (tawhid) and pure sincerity (ikhlas) that win the whole soul. The contract records the sermon section explicitly so the framing author instructs the hosts to recite it before discussing it. Landed at 5,576 words.

`ch05e-joy-virtue-and-the-hereafter.txt` — Episode 5 takes the closing movement (source lines 256–344): the epistemology of joy (worship named first, the joy of certainty, knowledge as a homecoming, the faqih who never makes people despair of God's mercy); beauty as virtue (ihsan as "making beautiful," assuming God's character traits, the prophetic virtues, and the counter-image of vice as blindness and pretension as hidden idolatry); and the befriending of hardship and death (zuhd as freedom from worldliness rather than world-hatred, death as intimacy and fana', and the Hereafter foretasted here and now, where goodness already participates in its reward). As the final episode of the source chapter it was given a consummating shape, gathering the four movements into the completed portrait of the ethos and the theme of salvation here and now. Landed at 5,820 words.

## Source chapter 2 — A Sacred Conception of Justice in the Letter of Ali (ع) to Malik al-Ashtar

`ch06a-the-sacred-conception-of-justice.txt` opens the second source chapter by establishing its governing thesis: that justice in Ali (ع)'s letter to Malik al-Ashtar is not the secular fairness of the dictionary but a reality rooted in the divine nature. It carries the source's framing material — MacIntyre's critique that modern ethics has drifted from any anchor in the Good, the three components of the spiritual life (islam, iman, ihsan) anchored in the hadith of Gabriel, the definition "justice puts everything in its right place," the letter's historical setting and Malik's standing, and the human vocation to emulate the divine Names while remaining God's slave. This is the natural first cut because everything after it is application: the episode teaches the principle, and the four that follow enact it. Its three concepts (justice beyond fairness, justice as the nature of God, becoming like God) sit at the concept ceiling and stay inside the extended band at 5,534 words. Callbacks to beauty-as-virtue and the buried treasures of the intellect are kept to a line each, since both were taught in the first source chapter.

`ch07b-pride-as-war-with-god.txt` takes the letter's sternest warning — "Do not set your soul up for war with God" — and unfolds the negative counterpart of sacred justice: pride as the usurpation of a divine attribute. It was split from the surrounding material at the seam where the source turns from the grounding of justice to the anatomy of tyranny, because pride is a self-contained teaching with its own archetype (Iblis), its own scriptural spine (the fall in Surat Sad, the verses on perishing and on istidraj), and its own inner logic (hidden idolatry, arrogance as intellectual defect, slow ruin). The three concepts (the cloak of pride, Iblis and hidden idolatry, arrogance and slow ruin) run to 5,652 words. Keeping this as its own episode prevents the diagnosis of corruption from being compressed into a footnote of the positive account, which is how the source itself weights it.

`ch08c-conscience-and-compassion.txt` follows the war with God inward, to the war a ruler wages against his own conscience, and then outward again into mercy. The cut here honours the source's own hinge — the section titled "Moral Conscience and Spiritual Consciousness" and the mercy passages that follow it — pairing conscience (the primordial covenant, self-reform as the root of justice) with compassion (mercy as intrinsic to justice, rigour toward wrongdoers even among one's own kin, the universal reach of "your brother in religion or your like in creation"). The two belong in one episode because the source presents mercy as the direct fruit of the ruler's identification with the poor and his awareness of his own lowliness before God; separating them would sever cause from consequence. Three concepts, 5,588 words. The combat against the soul receives a one-line callback rather than re-exposition, since the greatest struggle was taught in the first source chapter.

`ch09d-the-classes-of-society-and-the-poor.txt` gathers the letter's practical constitution — the wheel of society turning on the vertical axle of the Real, each class owed its due, and the poor as the sharpest test of justice — into a single episode, because the source runs these as one continuous movement from the al-Shiqshiqiyya sermon through the anatomy of the classes to the passage on the destitute and the primacy of intention. It was kept whole rather than split at the class boundaries because the wheel image is the organising figure and the treatment of the poor is its culmination; a mid-section cut would have orphaned the argument that a merely this-worldly ethics cannot honour the claim of the poor. At 5,996 words it sits at the top of the extended band, which the density of concrete counsel justifies. The joy of spiritual certainty gets a one-line callback; the three types of worshipper and acting from gratitude are the fresh material.

`ch10e-prayer-as-the-source-of-justice.txt` closes the source chapter on what Shah-Kazemi calls the lynchpin of the whole epistle: prayer and the remembrance of God as the source from which justice flows, sealed by the comparison with Plato's gazing on the Good. This is the final cut because the source explicitly reserves prayer for last, presenting it as the engine behind everything the earlier sections describe, and because the Plato comparison functions as the essay's coda. The three concepts (drawing near in prayer, the polish of remembrance, gazing on the Good) run to 5,919 words. Remembrance as recollection of what is inscribed in the intellect receives a one-line callback to the first source chapter's teaching on the intellect and the buried treasures, and the leitmotif that the heart, not the eye, sees God is echoed as a touchstone rather than re-explained.

## Source chapter 3 — Realization through Remembrance: Ali (ع) and the Mystical Tradition of Islam

`ch11a-dhikr-the-polish-for-hearts.txt` — Source chapter 3 opens with a long framing movement (Ali (ع) at the head of the Sufi silsilas, walaya and ma'rifa as the shared goal of Sunni Sufism and Shi'i gnosis, al-Raghib's definition of dhikr, its supremacy as akbar) that flows directly into the first titled section, "Dhikru'Llah As A Polish For Hearts." This episode takes that framing plus the opening arc of the polish section — sermon 213, the verse of light, the heart's recovered senses, and the heart's vision of God ("hearts see Him through the verities of faith," "know God through God," the witnesser and the witnessed). Three concepts: the mystical-tradition framing with the meaning and supremacy of dhikr; the polish for hearts; and the heart's vision of God. Because "the polish of remembrance" was previewed functionally in the earlier prayer-as-source-of-justice episode (source chapter 2), the polish is carried here by brief callback while the genuinely new weight falls on the epistemology of the polished heart — the vision that is finally God's own. The episode ends by setting up the veils paradox for episode 12. Lands at 5,546 words, extended band.

`ch12b-the-veils-that-do-not-veil.txt` — This episode takes the second arc of the polish section (source pp. 289–292: "The veils do not veil Him," Ibn 'Ata'illah's cascade of rhetorical questions, al-Zahir/al-Batin, tanzih and tashbih, Ibn al-'Arabi's non-delimitation, Abu Sa'id al-Kharraz on the coincidence of opposites) together with the whole of the source's second titled section, "Remembrance As The Quintessence Of Worship" (the practice verses, the Name as remedy, the dhikr–fikr circle, dhikr as greater than the ritual prayer). The numerical-oneness "no second" and the intellect-binding-into-tawhid material is carried by callback (both were taught in source chapters 1–2), and the "drawing near in prayer" overlap with the prayer episode is likewise a callback, leaving the practice-of-invocation itself as the new teaching. Lands at 5,523 words, extended band.

`ch13c-forgetting-the-self-in-remembrance.txt` — This episode takes the first two-thirds of the source's long final section (source pp. 230–250): the movement from heedless remembrance (Qur'an 107) through perfect remembrance to the reality of remembrance, the mystical extinction (fana) with its stations and degrees, al-Ghazali's "extinction of extinction," Ibn al-'Arabi's Book of Extinction in Contemplation and the mi'raj discourse with Moses, Ali (ع)'s fainting in the palm orchard, the drink for God's friends, and the disclosure of God as the true invoker (the creative Word as dhikr, the "thing remembered" of Qur'an 76:1, "Remember Me, I will remember you"). `ch14d-the-name-and-the-named.txt` — This episode takes the final third of the source's closing section (source pp. 246–end): the theurgic power of the revealed Names, the Du'a al-Mashlul, the tension between the Sufi maxim "the Name is the Named" and Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq's denial and its resolution (Ibn al-'Arabi, al-Kashani, Qaysari, Ashtiyani), the mustanad ilahi cosmogony, the divesting of attributes from the first sermon, the Imams and saints as the most beautiful Names, the shathiyyat and their reconciliation with tawhid, the resolution of the hadith qudsi, the Khutbat al-bayan with the mirror symbolism, and the death of Ali (ع) with the testimony of faith on his tongue. ## Source chapter 4 — The First Sermon of Nahj al-Balagha

`ch15-the-first-sermon-of-nahj-al-balagha.txt` — Source chapter 4 is a single complete primary text: the opening sermon (khutba) of Nahj al-Balagha in Ali (ع)'s own words, running the whole way from the oneness of God divested of every attribute, through the making of the cosmos and the ranks of the angels, the fashioning of Adam and the pride of Iblis, to the succession of prophets that ends in Muhammad and the Book. Because it is a sermon, R-SERMON-VERBATIM governs: the sermon is rendered whole in refined English as its own concept section (`## The First Sermon`), never fragmented or summarized, and the contract carries `sermon: {present: true, section_title: "The First Sermon"}` so the framing author can instruct the hosts to recite it aloud before discussing it. The design is one Extended episode with exactly three concept sections around the fixed verbatim core: `## The overture of negation` teaches why the collection opens on apophasis and how the sermon's rhetoric turns negation into affirmation; `## The First Sermon` is the whole text; and `## Creation and the prophets` teaches the single descending-and-returning arc — the genuinely new synthesis of this chapter. Much of the sermon's doctrine (oneness beyond number, the depth of paradox, the in-breathed spirit, Iblis and hidden idolatry, revelation and the buried treasures) was already taught in full in earlier chapters, so per R-NO-DOCTRINE-REPEAT those are given one-line callbacks rather than re-taught; the chapter's real new value is the primary sermon itself heard whole, its craft, and the cosmology-to-Book arc. Format is `deep_dive` (a single unfolding voice, no named opposition), essentiality `core` (the fountainhead the earlier exposition mined in fragments), landing at 5,571 words inside the Extended band (5,500–6,000). Arabic script and Qur'anic verses are rendered as refined English only per R-NO-TRANSLIT-FORMULA and R-QURAN-CITATION-FORMAT, matching the discipline of the fourteen shipped chapters; the one cited verse (chapter 23, verse 91) carries its plain-English reference.

## Source chapter 5 — The Letter of Ali (ع) to Malik al-Ashtar

`ch16a-letter-to-malik-the-rulers-soul.txt` — Source chapter 5 is a single primary text, the Letter of Ali (ع) to Malik al-Ashtar (his mandate on appointing Malik governor of Egypt), 6,007 words — seven words over the 6,000 band ceiling, so the density floor is ceil(6007/6000)=2 and one episode is not permitted. This first episode takes the letter's opening half (source lines 5–53), cut at the natural seam between the governing pillars and the productive classes. It carries exactly three concept sections: `## The ruler's soul` (the mandate's charges subordinated to welfare, curbing the nafs, mercy to subjects as brothers-in-religion or equals-in-creation, and the antidote to the pride that wars with God); `## The company a ruler keeps` (the common folk over the flattering elite, the concealment of faults, the counsellors to banish, and the highest place given to the speaker of bitter truth); and `## The pillars of rule` (the classes as a covenant, then the gentle commander, the rare and shielded judge, and the tested-and-watched administrator). A critical de-dup point governs the whole design: source chapter 2 (episodes 6–10) was Shah-Kazemi's scholarly commentary ON this same letter, so its doctrines — justice as the nature of God, pride as war with God, the wheel of society, each class its due, the bad opinion of God behind the vices, the scholars-versus-sages distinction — are already on the R-NO-DOCTRINE-REPEAT list. Those are given one-line callbacks here, not re-taught; the chapter spends its new words on the letter's own concrete instructions the commentary passed over (the four charges of the mandate, the elite/common-folk calculus, the concealment reciprocity, the worst-ministers rule, the commander raised like a child, the joy-of-governors passage, the judge's rank of nearness and generous pay, and the administrator's supervisors and disgrace). Format is `deep_dive` (Ali is the sole unfolding voice, no named opposition to adjudicate), essentiality `core`, source_type `letter`, landing at 5,584 words inside the Extended band (5,500–6,000). R-SERMON-VERBATIM is deliberately NOT invoked: the letter is a formal address but exceeds 6,000 words and is split across two episodes, so it cannot be rendered whole as one concept section in one episode — it is expounded across the pair exactly as the source's essay chapters were. Qur'anic verses render as refined English with plain-English references (chapter 4, verse 59); Arabic-script terms the source explicitly supplied (kharaj, jizya) are preserved beside their transliteration, and no verbatim run of a neighbouring episode's prose is reused (R-NO-VERBATIM-BRIDGE), the callbacks being freshly worded.

`ch17b-letter-to-malik-land-poor-and-covenants.txt` — This second episode takes the letter's second half and close (source lines 55–105), the descent from the machinery of the state to its soil and its edges, and it is the final episode of the whole series.
