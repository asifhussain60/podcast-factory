# The Greater Jihad and the World

*Reza Shah-Kazemi's Justice and Remembrance reads the spiritual ethos of Ali (ع) not as a code of outward rules but as an intelligence that must be won from the inside out. This closing movement of the opening chapter follows that intelligence into its hardest terrain: the battle a person wages against his own soul. Here the study gathers the Imam's sayings on the "greater struggle," on sincerity that makes a person purely for God, on the strange joy that certainty brings, on beauty as a function of the intellect, and on the detachment that lets the wise befriend what others flee. It ends on a promise the whole book will keep — that goodness does not merely earn a reward later but already carries that reward within itself, so that deliverance is something tasted here and now.*

## Where this episode picks up

What if the fiercest war a person ever fights has no enemy in it but himself? The tradition Shah-Kazemi is unfolding gives that intuition a name and a rank. It calls the war against the self the greater struggle — greater than any clash of armies, greater than any campaign against an outward foe — and it hands the command of that war not to the strong arm but to the intellect. This is the inversion the episode is built around: the summit of the spiritual life is not conquest of the world but conquest of the one who wants to conquer the world.

We arrive here having watched the Imam's counsel to Kumayl come to life. That discourse was not about accumulating facts; it was about a knowledge that refuses to stay on the surface of words and instead storms past the ear and the tongue to reach the heart, taking hold of "the reality of insight." Knowledge of that kind cannot be held by the mind alone. It asks for the whole person — will, character, and the soul's deepest disposition all bent toward the same truth. And the moment you ask a person to give the whole of himself to the truth, you have named the obstacle. The whole of a person is not obediently available. Part of him is caprice, appetite, self-regard, the ego's low pull toward its own comfort and its own glory. So the very demand that knowledge be interiorized turns out to require a struggle, because the soul that must do the interiorizing is itself divided.

That is the door this episode walks through. The integration the tradition calls tawhid — the making-one of knowledge within a person's being — is unreachable, Shah-Kazemi writes, without the participation of the whole soul, and the whole soul cannot participate while it is still ruled by the vices that flow from self-centeredness. So the true intellectual, the one the Imam calls al-'aqil, is redefined before our eyes. He is not the man with the most information. He is the man perpetually at war with his own lower inclinations — because he has grasped that those inclinations are not merely moral failings on some separate ethical ledger but clouds across the eye of the heart, rust on the very surface that was meant to reflect the Real.

Three movements carry the chapter to its close. First, the anatomy of that inner war: why the greatest jihad is the jihad of the soul, and what it means that the aim is not to kill the soul but to win it over. Second, the fruits that ripen once the soul is won — sincerity that makes a person purely for God, the joy that pours from certainty, and the beauty that virtue works upon the soul. Third, the detachment that follows from all of it: a loosening of the grip of the world, a friendship with hardship and even with death, and the final teaching that the good a person does is already, in its own substance, the reward he is promised.

## The greater jihad

Begin with the figure at the center of it all. The 'aqil, the true intellectual, is described by the Imam as someone constantly engaged in struggle against his own soul — against the lower inclinations, the passionate whims, the distracting caprices of the ego. What makes this description startling is where the Imam locates the stakes. We are used to filing our vices under morality: a bad temper, a streak of greed, a taste for flattery, each a mark against one's character. Shah-Kazemi presses the Imam's point that the vices do not stay so conveniently filed. They encroach on the domain of knowledge itself. Once knowledge is understood as a concrete vision of things rather than a merely notional grasp of concepts, the vices are seen for what they are — clouds obscuring the vision proper to the reality of insight.

The Qur'an gives the image its sharpest form. Evil actions do not simply blemish the record; they corrode the organ of sight. "Nay, their hearts have become rusted by that which they have done" (chapter 83, verse 14). The heart is a mirror meant to catch the light of the Real, and each wrong act lays down another film of rust across it, until the surface that was made to reflect God reflects nothing at all. So the 'aqil opposes vice on two levels at once. As a virtuous man he opposes it because it is contrary to virtue. But he opposes it for a deeper reason as well, one that sits on a higher plane: vice contradicts the nature of the Real, it obscures the reality of insight, it blinds the eye of the heart which alone can see God. This is not a rejection of the moral view but a deepening of it. Morality becomes the fruit; spirituality is the seed. And by drawing attention to the intellectual dimension of the struggle, the Imam shows how immorality is finally rooted in ignorance — so that for the true intellectual, uprooting that ignorance is nothing less than dissolving the very substance of the vices.

Such uprooting is not a tidy act of resolve. It demands a relentless battle within, indeed the most formidable of all battles — al-jihad al-akbar, the greatest struggle, as the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) defined it. Around this definition the Imam's sayings cluster with unusual intensity, and they repay being heard one after another. "Struggling against the soul through knowledge — such is the mark of the intellect." "The strongest of people are those who are strongest against their own souls." "Truly, one who fights his own soul in obedience to God, and does not sin against Him, has the rank of the righteous martyr in the sight of God." "The ultimate battle is that of a man against his own soul." "He who knows his soul fights it." "No jihad is more excellent than the jihad of the soul." Read together, they do not merely praise self-discipline; they promote it to the highest station in the moral world, ranking the man who conquers himself above every other kind of strong man, and awarding the victor the rank of the martyr.

But a crucial nuance lifts this above mere self-punishment. In another saying the Imam explains what the Prophet meant, and the explanation changes the whole picture. It is not so much a matter of fighting against the soul, he clarifies, as of fighting for the soul itself. He casts the struggle as a battle between two armies over a contested prize. The intellect, al-'aql, leads the forces of al-Rahman, the Compassionate. Whim, caprice, desire — all gathered under the word al-hawa — commands the forces of al-shaytan, the devil. And the soul, al-nafs, stands between them, drawn by both, falling under the sway of whichever army prevails. The soul is not the enemy. The soul is the territory. The aim is not to destroy it but to conquer it and enlist it on the side of the intellect — to free it from the destructive influence of its own caprice, its egoism, its subjectivism, and to redirect its fundamental energy away from the transient objects of individual desire and toward the one true object named by al-Rahman.

There is a further name the Imam gives the intellectual engaged in this battle, and it fuses two meanings that the tradition holds together: al-shahid, at once the martyr and the witness. The one who fights his soul in obedience to God attains the rank of the righteous martyr because he has sacrificed his lower, egoistic life; but he is also a witness, one who bears witness to God through the intellect's sacrifice of everything that opposes God's truth — and the chief thing sacrificed is his own egotism, his own false life. To possess perfect sincerity, on this reckoning, is to be rid of all hypocritical self-preoccupation and to put in its place a mode of being centered on God rather than on oneself. The martyr and the witness are the same person seen from two sides: what he gives up is the self, and what he thereby attests is the Real.

This reframing lets Shah-Kazemi draw out the contrast the Imam is after. Revelation itself, the Qur'an tells us, does not come from hawa: it "is naught but a revelation revealed" (chapter 53, verses 3-4). Divine objectivity stands over against human subjectivity, the passing whim of the self. The inner war reproduces that same polarity: the objectivity of the intellect, which makes present the power of al-Rahman, must prevail over the subjectivity of individual caprice, which carries the forces of al-shaytan. And the danger of losing that war is graver than it first appears. Hawa is the very thing the Qur'an describes when the ego swells into a false god: "Hast thou seen him who maketh his desire his god?" (chapter 25, verse 43; almost the same words at chapter 45, verse 23). This is the subtle idolatry, al-shirk al-khafi, which the Prophet said is more hidden than a black ant crawling on a dark stone on a moonless night. It is idolatry without an idol — the worship of one's own appetite, so quiet it never announces itself as worship at all.

Precisely because the idol here is the self, the self cannot dethrone it. It is impossible, the argument runs, for the ego to be controlled by anything belonging to the ego; a higher force must be brought to bear, and that force is the divine Reality, reached through the power of the intellect and the graces of faith. The struggle is therefore never merely an act of willpower straining against itself; it is effort met by grace, and the Qur'an names both sides of the meeting: "As for he who fears the station of his Lord, and restraineth his soul from whim, verily, Paradise will be his abode" (chapter 79, verses 40-41). The soul's energy is not to be broken but converted — turned from the objects of self-love toward the one object that can hold it. And this conquest is far more than arriving at a mental conception. It is not only mind, will, and character that must change; the soul's very deepest disposition must be integrated into the truth the intellect perceives, if a person is to perceive that truth in all its dimensions across all the planes of his being. The greater jihad, in the end, is the labor of becoming whole enough to see.

## Sincerity and its fruits

Why must the soul be won in its entirety, and not merely disciplined here and there? Shah-Kazemi answers by returning to the principle that anchors everything, tawhid. Rendered flatly as "oneness" or "unity," the word loses its force. It is a verbal noun; it means "affirming one" — believing in God's oneness, the outward theological sense — and "realizing one" — the inner recognition that there is but one Reality. On the Imam's reading the absolutely Real is not only one but making one; not only unique but unifying. To know God, then, is to become integrated oneself, to be one so as to reflect the One. Here Shah-Kazemi notes a quiet link to the root of 'aql, whose primary meaning is "to bind": the intellect, in this light, is not what encloses and limits meaning but what binds together the scattered elements of the soul into a single organic whole. Four of the Imam's sayings carry this into practice, each worth long reflection: "He who knows God integrates himself." "He who knows his soul disengages himself." "He who knows people isolates himself." "He who knows the world withholds himself." Knowing the highest gathers a person together; knowing the lesser things loosens his attachment to them, one after another.

This total adherence to the Real the Imam calls sincerity — sidq or ikhlas — words that mean far more than telling the truth. The verb behind ikhlas means to be utterly pure, unadulterated by anything extraneous, and the Imam spells out how wide that purity must reach: "Make purely for God your action and your knowledge, your love and your hatred, your taking and your leaving, your speech and your silence." To be sincere is to make oneself purely for God — to carry no stain of the other-than-God in one's devotion, intention, or even sentiment, loving and hating only in relation to one's bond with Him. Of all the impurities that creep into a person's motives, the most poisonous is egotism, which is why the Imam ranks the war within as the ultimate battle and its victor as the righteous martyr: he has sacrificed his lower, false life for the sake of the real life, the life witnessed by the intellect. Ikhlas is finally being for God — as the Qur'an puts it in the verse the Imam echoes, "Say: Truly my worship and my sacrifice, and my living and my dying, are for God, Lord of the universes" (chapter 6, verse 162).

That worship stands first in the verse is no accident, and Shah-Kazemi lingers on it, because worship is integral to sincerity. Something in the soul yearns to worship and is fed by nothing else; deny it its due and one's sincerity is compromised — one is no longer being just to God, no longer rendering Him what is His. And the Imam's spiritual logic runs straight from that injustice to the outward world: "Whoso establishes well-being between himself and God, God establishes well-being between him and mankind." One cannot be just to creatures while unjust to the Creator. Worship here is taken in its widest sense, from formal prayer and supplication to meditation, reflection, and remembrance — every means by which a person is turned toward the Real. And it must be worship with presence, not mechanism: "He who prays without exertion is like one who shoots arrows without a bow." Nothing in this ethos comes cheap; even knowledge "calls out for action; if it is answered it is of avail, otherwise it departs." Knowledge not taken seriously stays undigested, unable to reach the heart — the innermost faculty that alone has access to the reality of insight and that "sees" God not by eyesight but by the spiritual insight (بصیرۃ) generated through the verities of faith.

The quest for such knowledge is therefore inseparable from prayer in its deepest mode. "Worship thy Lord until certainty cometh to thee" (chapter 15, verse 99), the Qur'an says, and the Imam takes the command literally: knowledge yields its inner riches only to total dedication, the kind of concentration on the divine Reality in which nothing else can share the field of consciousness. The remembrance of God, dhikr, strictly implies the forgetting of everything else — above all, oneself; its spiritual reality is reached only when "you forget your soul in your remembrance." And at a certain point the direction of the effort reverses. At first a person seeks knowledge and cultivates consciousness; then, the Imam says, "Acquire knowledge, and true life will acquire you" — being makes itself more real within him until it is no longer he who assimilates knowledge but knowledge, the reality expressed through it, that assimilates him to itself. The most worthy knowledge, on this measure, "is that by virtue of whose presence action is accepted by God."

That standard exposes a counterfeit the Imam met in his own lifetime, and Shah-Kazemi lets the episode stand as a warning. Among those who rebelled against him were many reciters of the Qur'an, men of real religious prestige on account of their Qur'anic knowledge and their fierce worship. Yet their piety was a "zeal of bitterness," a caricature of the real thing — knowledge without the intellect that would have integrated it. When his supporters praised the long night-vigils of that faction, the Imam answered sharply: "Sleeping with certainty is better than praying with doubt." Certainty brings peace and joy, so that even the bodily rest of a soul immersed in the spirit of certainty deepens its happiness, while frantic worship without inner light is worth less than sleep. The true intellectual, whom the Imam defines as the one who puts each thing in its proper place, obeys the command to pray — but he knows that obedience is a necessary and not a sufficient condition, that there must be consciousness of what one is doing and why, for it is that awareness, together with inward beauty, that draws divine acceptance.

From this soil grows the most unexpected fruit of all: joy. The Imam tells Kumayl that those who reach the life of the spirit "rejoice in their intimacy with the spirit of certainty," and Shah-Kazemi draws out how much that single word carries. To touch the spirit of certainty is not to reach a cold conclusion at the end of an argument; it is to enter into the very object of one's certainty, which is ultimately nothing less than the divine. And since that Reality is more beatific than any imaginable paradise — for the Qur'an affirms that the divine beatitude, ridwan, is "greater" than Paradise itself (chapter 9, verse 72) — it cannot but bestow joy on the soul that truly opens to it. Hence the Imam's astonishing claim, and Shah-Kazemi's name for it, an epistemology of joy: "the most joyous of mankind is the intellectual." Engaging with truth ought to make a person happy, and it does so exactly to the degree that the truth touches the ground of his own being. True knowledge is knowledge of Being, and so it is always, at bottom, a kind of self-discovery — a homecoming from the exile of ignorance, since the most intellectual of people is the one closest to God and therefore the one most alive. To be close to God is to be fully alive, and that life is joy.

Because this joy rests on an unshakeable certainty of the divine goodness, it reshapes even the definition of the true faqih — not so much "the jurist" as the one who genuinely understands. Such a person, the Imam says, is "he who does not make people despair of the mercy of God, and does not make them lose hope in the gracious spirit of God." Despair of that mercy is itself a mark of unbelief — "who despaireth of the mercy of his Lord except those who stray in error?" (chapter 15, verse 56) — while the Qur'an's great assurances, "My mercy encompasseth all things" (chapter 7, verse 156) and "thy Lord has prescribed for Himself mercy" (chapter 6, verse 54), come fully alive only in the souls that know the beatific nature of the Real not as a notion but as a taste.

Joy of this kind is inseparable from beauty, which is itself, in the Imam's perspective, a function of the intellect rather than a matter of private taste. The Arabic ihsan, often flattened to "virtue" or "excellence," carries the literal sense of "making beautiful," since husn means beauty; virtue is that which makes the soul beautiful. "The excellence of the intellect," says the Imam, "is in the beauty of things outward and inward." Outward beauty of form and action must be completed by inward beauty, the beauty of the soul. Two sayings of the Prophet stand behind this: "Truly God is beautiful and He loves beauty," and "Assume the character traits of God." To make oneself beautiful in the fullest sense is to make oneself lovable to God — which is why the drawing of divine love runs through the imitation of the Prophet: "Say: O ye who believe, if ye love God, follow me; God will love you" (chapter 3, verse 31). And the virtues that make a person beautiful in this way are the prophetic ones — wise forbearance, generosity, modesty, kindness, contentment, courtesy — none of which can be absent from the true intellectual, for without them the intellect stays trapped on the flat plane of bare reasoning, blind to its own hidden treasures.

The counterfeit of all this is what the Imam warns against most sharply. When formal compliance is drained of sincerity, it becomes mere hypocrisy and ostentation, riya — and riya, the Imam says, is the subtlest form of idolatry: "the slightest pretension is polytheism." A short sura makes the point mercilessly, condemning those "who pray, those who are heedless of their prayer, who want only to be seen, and withhold small kindnesses" (chapter 107, verses 1-7). Acts of devotion have no value at all unless they are woven into a life of virtue and offered with the right intention, for "it is not their flesh nor their blood that reacheth God, but it is piety from you that reacheth Him" (chapter 22, verse 37). This is why the Imam's ethos is called not merely religious but spiritual: it drives religion to its deepest application. "There is no religion for one who has no intellect."

## Detachment and the Hereafter

If the soul has been won, sincerity made pure, and joy tasted at the root, one consequence follows that looks at first like its opposite — a cooling of the whole grip on the world. The Imam calls it zuhd, a word no single English term captures: asceticism, austerity, abstention, detachment, "making little of." Crucially, it is not the denial of the physical world but the overcoming of worldliness, the egotistic attachment to it. The Nahj al-balagha states the attitude plainly: "The world is an abode for which annihilation is ordained, and for its people departure from it is decreed. It is sweet and green, and it hastens to one who seeks it, and confounds the heart that gazes upon it. So set out from it with the best of the provision available to you, and ask not from it above what suffices." The best provision, the Imam says elsewhere, is taqwa — piety, awe, God-consciousness, all at once: "I exhort you, O slaves of God, to taqwa, which is the provision." And that provision is gathered only in the measure of one's detachment from the perishable side of the life of this world.

The Imam's contempt for that perishable side is expressed in images no reader forgets. This world, he says, "is more contemptible in my eyes than a bone of a pig in the hand of a leper"; it is "as worthless to me as that which drips from the nose of a goat"; an image of it is "the snake — soft to touch, but virulent poison within." Yet — and this is where Shah-Kazemi insists the doctrine is misread if taken as mere world-hatred — the same Imam waxes lyrical over the beauty of untouched nature, describing the peacock and the birds and the whole marvel of creation with evident wonder. There is no contradiction. What renders the world small is not that it is ugly but that the grandeur of its Creator dwarfs it: "The greatness of the Creator for you belittles creatures in your view." Seen in the light of the Absolute, every created thing shrinks to a fleeting shadow destined for annihilation — "Everything perishes except His Face" (chapter 28, verse 88) — and yet loses none of its meaning as a sign, for "there is nothing that does not hymn His glory with praise" (chapter 17, verse 44), and even "the birds in flight — each one knoweth its prayer and glorification" (chapter 24, verse 41). The world is worth little in itself and everything as a pointer beyond itself.

This detachment gives the wise a strange capacity: they "make easy what the extravagant find harsh; they befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged." What the worldly find harsh is adversity — fear, hunger, loss — the trials the Qur'an says inevitably accompany this life: "And We shall try you with something of fear and hunger and loss of property and life and fruits; but give glad tidings to the patient" (chapter 2, verses 155-157). The wise perceive the blessing hidden inside affliction, because their patience flows from knowledge of the truth — "By the Age, truly man is in a state of loss, except those who believe and do virtuous deeds and exhort one another to truth and exhort one another to patience" (chapter 103, verses 1-3). They can make the trials easy because they discern the ease folded into every hardship: "Verily, with hardship cometh ease; verily with hardship cometh ease" (chapter 94, verses 5-6). And they go further than merely awaiting that ease as hardship's eventual fruit; they taste it already as the seed hidden within the hardship itself, actually befriending what others flee.

What makes such friendship possible is a certainty so complete that it has been assimilated rather than merely believed. It is easy, the Imam observes, to hold at one level of the mind that "there is no god but God." He draws attention to what that simple testimony should mean when it is truly sincere: "I bear witness that there is no god but God alone Who has no partner — a testimony whose sincerity has been tested and whose essence has been assimilated with conviction." The word he chooses points to the choicest, purest extract of a substance, the essence pressed out of it; he is stressing the spiritual substance of the testimony as against the bare utterance of its words. The words are an echo of the reality they convey and must not be mistaken for it. "We cling to it always," he continues, "storing it up against the terrors that will befall us. For truly, it renders faith resolute, opens up spiritual virtue, pleases al-Rahman, repels al-shaytan." It is from this tested certainty that deliverance itself becomes present ahead of time: "He attains deliverance whose intellect dominates his caprice." Deliverance — falah, carrying at once the sense of salvation and of beatitude — is thus accessible here and now, not a grace withheld until after death.

Nowhere does this friendship reach further than toward death. "Death is more intimate to me than the breast of the mother is to the suckling babe," the Imam says. Shah-Kazemi reads this alongside the Qur'an's word that those slain in the path of God are not truly dead — an allusion, on the deeper level, to fana', extinction in God: the dying-before-death in which egoistic consciousness is effaced before the Absolute. Because life in the world is itself a kind of death — a limitation, a privation set against the plenitude of the divine realms — dying to that death is tantamount to true life: "the life of this world is nothing but sport and play, and verily, the abode of the Hereafter, that is true life, did they but know" (chapter 29, verse 64). For the wise, death is release, encounter, a deeper degree of integration; for the ignorant it is sheer disintegration, the end of their world. One tastes the ocean of the Real, Shah-Kazemi writes, only by being drowned in it. This is why the wise keep company with the world in their bodies while their spirits are tied to the transcendent realm — why the Imam can describe those "whose hearts are in the Gardens while their bodies are at work." Their consciousness is substantially, not merely conceptually, determined by higher realities; they act in the world yet are not bound by the fruits of their action, knowing that "action — all action — is dust, except what is purified within it."

What role, then, does the Hereafter really play in this ethos? Not, Shah-Kazemi argues, the crude one of dangling reward and threatening punishment to force good behavior — or not only that. The Hereafter becomes a living dimension within the believer's own soul, an orientation and a foretaste rather than a distant hope. Its mere anticipation marks every believer; its realization marks the friend of God, whose immersion in the reality of which he is certain the Imam expresses in his most famous line: "Were the veil to be removed, I would not increase in certitude." The foretaste of the Hereafter deepens with virtue rather than fading once its moral work is done. At the summit of this state the soul is already, in the Qur'an's phrase, al-nafs al-mutma'inna (chapter 89, verse 27), "the soul at peace in absolute certainty" — so utterly content in the presence of God that it desires nothing more. The Hereafter is "better and more lasting" (chapter 87, verse 17), and even the thought of it purifies: "We purify them with a pure quality — remembrance of the Abode" (chapter 38, verse 46). This is why the Imam can turn from his images of the world's worthlessness to a vision of Paradise so vivid it is plainly no rhetorical flourish but a described reality: "If you were to cast the eye of your heart towards what is described for you of the Garden, your soul would become averse to the marvels of this world — its passions, its pleasures and its embellished scenery. And your soul would be rapt in contemplation of the swaying trees whose roots lie hidden in dunes of musk on the banks of the rivers of the Garden, and the clusters of dazzling pearls hanging down from the branches of its trees." The remembrance of the Abode is not a threat held over the soul; it is a foretaste offered to it, and the offering itself cleanses.

All of this converges on the teaching that closes the chapter, and it is the hinge of the entire ethos. A person's well-being in this life lies in doing good, and that well-being is proportioned to the depth of his belief that "Whatever good ye send before for your souls, ye will find it with God, better and more tremendous as reward" (chapter 73, verse 20). A sincere orientation toward the Hereafter is transformed into a perception of heavenly beatitude already here — because good acts share the very nature of the felicity they lead to. Goodness does not merely earn a reward; it already participates in that reward: "Is the reward of goodness aught but goodness?" (chapter 55, verse 60). The Imam's own saying seals it — "The fruit of good action is like its root" — and the Qur'an gives the image of the blessed tasting the fruits of the Garden and saying, "This is what we were given to eat before" (chapter 2, verse 25). The good done in this world is the same fruit, tasted early. On the ethical plane, then, the realization of intrinsic virtue, ihsan, is not merely a prerequisite for salvation later; it is already a deliverance now — a release, here and now, from the imprisonment of sin outwardly and the bondage of egocentricity within.

## What this episode lands

Across this opening chapter, Shah-Kazemi has been building a single portrait, and this final movement completes it. The intellectual he set out to define has turned out to be, at his core, a fighter — but a fighter turned inward, engaged in the greater jihad against his own soul, aiming not to destroy that soul but to win it whole and enlist it on the side of the Real. We have seen why that battle sits at the summit of the moral world: because the vices are not merely lapses of character but rust on the heart, and the eye that would see God cannot see through rust. We have seen the shape of victory — sincerity that makes a person purely for God in action and intention and even sentiment; the joy that certainty pours into the soul, so that the most alive of people is the most joyous; the beauty that virtue works upon the inner form; and the detachment that lets the wise make hardship easy and death intimate, because their hearts are already in the Gardens while their bodies are at work.

Most of all, the chapter lands its governing claim: that deliverance is not deferred. The good a person does already carries within it the reward he is promised, so that falah, deliverance, is tasted here and now — a freedom from sin without and from the ego within. This is the thread the rest of the book will follow, and it is the reason the Imam's ethos is called spiritual rather than merely religious: it takes the ideals of faith to their most profound application, where the reward and the deed are one substance.

And it points forward. Everything gathered here has been interior — the intellect, the soul, sincerity, the eye of the heart. The book's next movement carries that inner order outward. The very virtue named at this chapter's close, ihsan — worshipping God as if one saw Him — becomes the root of the cardinal virtue the next chapter takes up: justice. The question shifts from how a person orders his own soul to how that ordered soul orders the world around it, and it is pursued through the Imam's great letter of counsel to his governor, Malik al-Ashtar. The ethos of the intellect and the soul is about to become an ethos of governance and justice — the inner remembrance flowering into an outward order.
