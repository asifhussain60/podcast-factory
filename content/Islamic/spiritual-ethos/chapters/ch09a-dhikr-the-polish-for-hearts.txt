This episode opens the book's meditation on the remembrance of God, dhikru'Llah, by asking why so simple a practice — the invocation of a divine Name — is set by the Imam above every other act of worship. We follow how Ali (ع) stands at the head of the whole mystical tradition of Islam, what the word dhikr actually means, why the Imam calls remembrance a polish for the hearts, and how the polished heart comes to see God with a sight that has nothing to do with the eye.

## Where this episode opens

What could be easier than to remember something — and how could an act that costs no money, demands no journey, and can be done while walking or eating be the single most exalted thing a human being does? That is the paradox the Imam plants at the center of Islamic spirituality, and it is the paradox this episode begins to open. Of all the works a person can offer to God — fasting, almsgiving, pilgrimage, the fixed prayers with their prescribed words and postures — the tradition singles out the one that looks least strenuous, the bare turning of the heart and tongue toward God, and calls it the greatest. The Qur'an says it plainly: the remembrance of God is greater.

In the closing movement of the letter to Malik al-Ashtar, followed earlier in this series, the Imam had already reached toward this claim, resting the whole edifice of just rule on prayer and on the remembrance at prayer's heart. There the remembrance was seen chiefly through its fruit in the world — through the justice that overflows from a soul drawn near to God. This chapter turns from the fruit to the root. It follows the remembrance inward, past its outward consequences, to ask what dhikr is in itself: what it means, why it holds so central a place, and what it accomplishes in the depths of a human being. To do so it draws not only on the Imam's own dense and often elliptical sayings but on the vast mystical tradition — Sufism and Shi'i gnosis alike — for which those sayings are a founding source. For the corpus of Ali (ع)'s teachings on remembrance forms a bridge: it gathers up the Qur'anic verses that speak of dhikr and, at the same time, foreshadows the elaborate doctrines the later mystics would build. What the Imam conveyed in a few paradoxical words, the tradition would spend centuries making explicit.

## Ali and the mystical tradition

To grasp why a book on the spirituality of Islam should place the remembrance of God at its heart, one has first to see how completely Ali (ع) belongs to the mystical tradition that made dhikr its central discipline. His presence, as the gate to the city of prophetic wisdom, marks every noble expression of the spirit of Islam — the spiritual chivalry of the craft guilds, the poetry, the music, the arts — but nowhere more organically than in Sufism. Whether they are Arab or Persian, Turk or Malay, Sunni or Shi'i, the Sufi orders are united in the figure of Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع), who stands at the summit of nearly all the chains of affiliation by which the masters trace their spiritual lineage back to the Prophet. He is, for the whole of that tradition, the friend of God par excellence — the wali Allah in whom sanctity and knowledge are perfectly joined.

This unifying role reaches across the deepest division within Islam, the split between Sunnism and Shi'ism. Beyond the enormous common ground the two branches share, they are drawn together in a peculiarly direct and existential way by a shared orientation toward sanctity, walaya, and gnosis, ma'rifa, as the very goal of the religious life. Seyyed Hossein Nasr puts the point crisply: Islamic esotericism crystallized into Sufism in the Sunni world, while it poured into the whole structure of Shi'ism, especially in its early period. The esoteric core of Islam thus expresses itself in Sunni Sufism and in Shi'i gnosis with different accents, without its inmost attunement to the message of divine oneness being altered. One of the great figures in whom the two streams converge, Sayyid Haydar Amuli, drove the point to its edge: the true Shi'i cannot but be a Sufi if he plumbs the mystical depths of the Imams' teachings, and the Sufi is a Shi'i if he comes to recognize that the roots of his own way lie in the Imams of the Prophet's house. The outward differences between the traditions are, for Amuli, dissolved in the fire of walaya — the sanctity that is the true substance and goal of both.

The clearest single testimony to how Ali (ع) is regarded in that tradition comes from Ali Hujwiri, author of the first Sufi manual written in Persian. He calls the Imam one who drowned in the ocean of trying affliction and was consumed in the fire of loving passion, the leader of the saints and the pure ones, holding in the mystical path a place of tremendous honor. Hujwiri reports the great master Junayd's own verdict — that Ali (ع) is the guide of this path in respect of both its principles and the endurance of affliction — and concludes that the Imam is the master of the way both in its theoretical knowledge and in its practice. It is worth dwelling on that pairing of knowledge and affliction, for it recalls a phrase followed earlier in this series: the Imam's description of the sages as those who befriend the very things from which the ignorant flee. One reading of that elliptical saying is that among the things the sages befriend is precisely this — pure concentration on the Absolute, to the exclusion of every other thought, a concentration that is a kind of death, since the ego and all its preoccupations are as it were sacrificed before the divine Reality. The remembrance of God is at once the summit of that concentration and the path that leads to it.

But what, exactly, is dhikr? The word is extraordinarily rich, and no single English term can carry its range. It means remembrance, recollection, invocation, repetition, recitation; and also memory, mention, telling, praise, glorification, honor, exhortation — and it denotes the Qur'an itself, and indeed any book of revelation. The definition that comes closest to the spiritual principle is the one given by the lexicographer al-Raghib al-Isfahani. Dhikr, he writes, is that by which a person keeps present the knowledge he has attained. It is akin to memory, except that memory is spoken of in respect of acquiring knowledge, whereas dhikr is spoken of in respect of rendering it present. This is the hinge of the whole idea. To remember, in this sense, is not to store a fact away but to make a reality present to consciousness here and now. Al-Raghib adds that there are two kinds of dhikr, that of the heart and that of the tongue, and that each divides again into a remembrance that follows forgetfulness and a remembrance that follows no forgetfulness at all but expresses a continuous, unbroken presence. When the thing rendered present is God, the importance of remembrance becomes self-evident. Dhikru'Llah is, first, a principle of awareness, an in-depth assimilation of the divine presence; but it is also an action, a discipline, a practice or series of practices. It is at once the end and the means to that end — both the spiritual awareness itself and the methodic work that cultivates it. It is this double character, end and means woven together, that the Sufi tradition would elaborate most fully, presenting the invocation of a Name of God as both a contemplative discipline and the very fruit that discipline ripens.

Once this is seen, the supremacy of remembrance is no longer surprising. If dhikr is the permanent awareness of the divine Reality, then it is the quintessence of religion as such — that which binds the human being back to God, the reason every other rite exists. The Qur'an states it with great economy: prayer keeps one away from lewdness and iniquity, and the remembrance of God is greater (chapter 29, verse 45). The Prophet made the same ranking unmistakable. He once asked his companions whether he should tell them of the best and purest of their works, more exalted in rank than giving gold and silver, better than meeting the enemy in battle — and when they asked what it was, he answered: the perpetual invocation of God. The Imam echoes him exactly: perpetuate the remembrance, he says, for truly it illumines the heart, and it is the most excellent form of worship. This emphasis runs through the Shi'i tradition, which records many sayings of the early Imams on the virtues of invoking the divine Names. Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq even defined his followers by it: our Shi'a, he said, are those who, when they are alone, invoke and remember God much. That being alone with God has its own name in the tradition — the retreat, the khalwa — and one of the great masters of Shi'i gnosis in the twentieth century, Allama Tabataba'i, describes it as a distancing of oneself from people, required above all for certain kinds of verbal invocation and held essential by the masters of the path. Al-Ghazali pressed the natural objection — why should the simplest of all the acts of worship be the most meritorious, when the others demand so much more effort? A full answer, he grants, belongs only to the science of unveiling; but from the standpoint of practice one can say that the most powerful remembrance is the one performed continuously with the presence of the heart. That presence of the heart is the aim of the whole discipline, and it is through it, al-Ghazali says, that every other act of worship is dignified. The one work that looks like the least turns out to be the one through which all the rest are made whole. As the Sufi Shaykh Ahmad al-'Alawi says, taking the point to its limit: remembrance is the mightiest rule of the religion, and the law and its rites were ordained only for the sake of establishing it, so that our performance of every act of worship is strong or weak exactly according to the degree of our remembrance while performing it.

There is a further reason Ali (ع) stands at the head of this tradition, and it has to do not only with what he taught about remembrance but with what he did. Beyond expounding the meaning of dhikr, he initiated others into its methodic practice — the bestowal of the invocation on a disciple that the tradition calls talqin — and this initiatic role is a key part of his function as spiritual guide, the pole of his age. Here the historian must be honest about the limits of the record. We have very little firm historical information about this initiatic activity, just as we have almost none about the Imam's own initiation by the Prophet. That is hardly surprising, for the practice is by its nature esoteric: it is not laid on everyone as an obligatory rite but bestowed on those who have received a particular calling, a vocation to something beyond the duties binding on all. What we do possess, and possess in abundance, is the literary evidence — the sayings gathered in the Nahj al-balagha and the Ghurar al-hikam, and the supplications attributed to the Imam. Taken together these form a homogeneous corpus, a distinct body of doctrine, rich in philosophical reflection and metaphysical insight into the nature of a practice that stands at the very center of the mystical tradition. It is this corpus, read against the Qur'an it illuminates and the later Sufi doctrine it anticipates, that the rest of this book unfolds. The dhikr, in that tradition, is the spiritual method above all others — a discipline that works the in-depth assimilation, within the heart, of what is merely present to the mind, transforming an abstract and notional grasp of ideas into a concrete perception of spiritual realities.

## The polish for hearts

The Imam's own teaching on this polish is best approached through a sermon in which he comments on a Qur'anic description of men whom neither trade nor merchandise diverts from the remembrance of God (chapter 24, verse 37) — the same verse, and the same saying, followed earlier in this series in its bearing on justice: that God has made the remembrance a polish for the hearts, by which they hear after being deaf and see after being blind. What deserves fresh attention here is not the saying itself but the verse just before it, the one the Imam's hearers would have supplied without his needing to recite it in full. It is the great verse of light: God is the light of the heavens and the earth, a light likened to a lamp in a niche, glass like a shining star, lit from a blessed olive tree, light upon light (chapter 24, verse 35). The Imam's comment on remembrance is set deliberately against this verse, and once that is seen, the whole saying opens further than its bearing on justice alone had shown.

The latent light of the heart, on this reading, is disclosed by the remembrance of God; the light within shines together with the light from on high, so that the same phrase — light upon light — describes both the lamp of the verse and the heart the Imam is describing. Once the heart is illumined, every other faculty shares in the luminosity remembrance has kindled. Even the word the Imam chooses for polish carries this meaning: it derives from a root whose primary sense is to become clear, to be exposed to view, brought into the open — the same root the Qur'an uses of the day when it brings the sun to light. The consciousness hidden within the heart is, quite literally, brought to light by the polishing that remembrance works.

There is a second aspect of this polishing worth pausing over: the double work the cleared heart performs. On one side, the heart purified by remembrance becomes a mirror, able to reflect truths that come to it from above; on the other, it discloses its own hidden depths — what the Imam elsewhere calls the buried treasures of the intellect, followed earlier in this series as the knowledge of God ingrained in the very substance of the soul. The polish works in both directions at once: it clarifies the organ so that it can receive the light from on high, and it uncovers the light already latent within, as though the heart, cleared of its rust, were remembering something it had always known. The Imam's words here simply echo a saying of the Prophet often repeated in Sufi circles: for everything there is a polish, and the polish of the hearts is the remembrance of God.

What is being purified here is a particular organ — the spiritual heart, which cannot be identified with the reasoning faculty, still less with the organs of sense. The Qur'an points repeatedly to this deeper seat of vision. Have they not travelled the land, it asks, that they might have hearts with which to comprehend and ears with which to hear? For it is not the eyes that are blind, but blind are the hearts within the breasts (chapter 22, verse 46). Outward sight reaches only the surface of things; the vision proper to the heart penetrates beneath the surface to the reality concealed there. And the Qur'an sets the remembering heart against its opposite, the heedless: those who have hearts and understand not, eyes and see not, are called the heedless, the ghafilun — and the heedless one is the exact opposite of the one who remembers (chapter 7, verse 179). The verse that follows supplies one of the great foundations of the whole practice of invocation: to God belong the most beautiful Names, so call Him by them (chapter 7, verse 180). The bond between remembrance and the heart is stated as plainly as it can be: those who believe, and whose hearts are at peace in the remembrance of God — is it not in the remembrance of God that hearts find peace (chapter 13, verse 28)?

Return, briefly, to the people the Imam describes in that same sermon — those who have taken the remembrance in place of the world, so wholly reoriented that trade and commerce no longer divert them, though their outward life continues unchanged. This chapter's angle on that portrait is not, as with the letter to Malik, what such a soul does for others once returned to the world, but what has happened within it: a consciousness so cleared and illumined that remembrance has become its steady condition rather than an occasional act, and the divine presence, once glimpsed only in set moments, has become the abiding air the soul breathes.

The Imam's own further sayings confirm that the polish is a real discipline, not a metaphor for pious feeling — he speaks of a people, an ahl, who belong to the remembrance, exactly as the Prophet spoke of the people of the remembrance sought out by roving angels, who call to one another, come to what you long for, whenever they find a company invoking God. And the image outlived the Imam by centuries. Rumi, one of the greatest of those the Sufis call simply the folk, gives it poetic form in his Mathnawi: it is the custom of kings, he says, to seat the Sufis directly before them, for they are a mirror for the soul, and better than a mirror — breasts polished by invocation and meditation, so that the mirror of the heart receives the pure, unspotted image. The Imam's dense saying and the poet's image are one teaching, and neither confines the polishing to a single technique: it is at once the work of reflection and meditation and a regular methodic practice — glorification, magnification, praise, the declaring of God's oneness, or the invocation of the Names in the strict sense — and all of them, done with the presence of the heart, do the one work of rubbing away the rust of forgetfulness. The point is never the motion of the tongue as such but the clearing of the heart, and any practice that clears it belongs to the family of remembrance.

## The heart's vision of God

If remembrance recovers the heart's lost sight, the natural question is what, exactly, the polished heart then sees. The Imam's answer is as bold as it is precise: it sees God. But it does not see Him as the eye sees a thing. Eyes see Him not through sight's observation, he says, but hearts see Him through the verities of faith. This single sentence, which has served this whole series as a touchstone, marks off two orders of vision. The physical eye, bound to the surfaces of the sensible world, can never reach the divine Reality; to expect it to would be a category mistake. The heart, purified by remembrance and vivified by faith, has access to a seeing of another kind altogether — an inward vision whose organ is the deepest faculty of consciousness and whose object is God Himself. In another saying the Imam calls pure hearts the very places from which God can be seen: whoever purifies his heart, he says, sees God. And he ties that purity directly to remembrance, asking where are those whose deeds are done purely for God, who have purified their hearts so that they become places for the remembrance of God.

Read together, these sayings can be taken as a commentary on the great saying in which God speaks in the first person — the saying of drawing near, followed earlier in this series in its bearing on justice, in which God tells of the servant who draws near to Him through devotion until He loves him, and then becomes the very hearing by which he hears and the sight by which he sees. Here the same saying is read for what it discloses about vision alone. Through the remembrance — the voluntary devotion par excellence — the individual's very acts of perception are mysteriously assimilated to the divine Reality, and the result is that the heart attains spiritual sight. But then the heart, as the center of consciousness, can no longer be regarded in its merely human, relative dimension. Its inmost secret pertains in truth to the divine, and it is this alone that can see as God sees and, in the end, see God. The Sufi master Abu Talib al-Makki put the paradox at its sharpest: none sees Him to whom nothing is similar, he wrote, but He to whom nothing is similar. If God enters even into the outward acts of the senses, then the supreme act of spiritual awareness — the vision of God — is in reality not man's vision of God at all, but God's vision of God, effected through the individual's heart rather than by it.

This is why the Imam gives a command that sounds, at first, impossible: know God through God. Knowledge of the divine cannot be built up from below, out of the creature's own resources, as one builds knowledge of a created thing; it is the Real coming to know itself in the heart it has purified. There is no common measure between this consciousness of God and ordinary empirical knowledge of things — a point the Imam presses through his characteristic paradoxes. Praise be to God, he says, who is well known without being seen; and again, by things their Creator manifests Himself to the intellects, and by things He is guarded from the sight of the eyes. No amount of information gathered from outside can bring a person to the knowledge of God, because that knowledge is of a wholly different order; it wells up from within and crystallizes upon contact with revelation from without. This is what marks off the seeing of the heart from every other kind of knowing: it is not the acquisition of a fact but the presencing of a reality, and its true agent is not the knower but the Known — a further mystery about the Name itself, and about the self that falls away in the act of remembering it, waits for a later episode in this arc. For the moment it is enough to hold the more relative aspect of the heart's vision: the seeing by which the reality of God is rendered clear and present to the heart through the verities of faith, where the inner dimension of human consciousness is the one that perceives and the divine Reality is what it perceives — a presence that is not far off but ubiquitous and inalienable, the very ground of everything that exists. As the Imam's own supplication to Kumayl, followed earlier, addresses God by His tremendousness which has filled all things, by His Names which have filled the foundations of all things, by His knowledge which encompasses all things, by the light of His face through which all things are illumined.

For nothing can exist without the foundation that a divine Name, or a combination of Names, furnishes for it. What constitutes the true being and the positive qualities of anything is, in the end, a divine Name. To see things correctly, then, is to see the divine attributes manifested in and through them — to perceive them as reflections of principial realities, self-disclosures of the divine, so many theophanies in which the one Reality shows itself. The heart purified by invocation comes to see God everywhere. This vision draws on two principles the Qur'an affirms together. On one side stands the objective nature of reality: nothing that exists, not even the most outward face of the material world, can be shut out of the divine Reality, for God is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward, and wherever one turns there is His Face. On the other side stands the human subject and the assimilation of that reality: God will show His signs on the horizons and in the souls of men until it is clear to them that He is the Real. Everything around us, on this reading, is a sign pointing back to its Creator, and the heart polished by remembrance can read the signs and so see God in everything.

Yet — and this is the guard the next episode will have to secure — such vision in no way drags the divine down to the level of the sensible, or confuses the Creator with the creature. It sees God through things without mistaking things for God. The Imam holds the two truths together in a single sentence from the first of his sermons: God is with everything, he says, but not through association, and other than everything, but not through separation. His being with all things does not compromise His transcendence, and His being other than all things does not entail His absence. The Qur'an itself sets the two poles side by side. On one side it proclaims a nearness closer than the neck-artery — We are closer to man than his jugular vein — and on the other an utter incomparability: there is none like unto Him. Nothing that exists can be wholly separate from the all-encompassing reality of God, and yet that reality has no common measure with anything that exists. It is precisely this double truth, immanence and transcendence held together without either canceling the other, that the vision of the polished heart must keep intact — and it is precisely here, in the effort to hold them together, that the paradox of the veils arises.

## What this episode lands

This episode has laid the ground on which everything to come is built. The remembrance of God is the beating heart of the whole mystical tradition of Islam, and Ali (ع) stands at its source — the friend of God in whom that tradition finds its founding master, the one figure who unites Sunni Sufism and Shi'i gnosis in a common orientation toward sanctity and knowledge. Remembrance itself, dhikr, is that by which the knowledge already attained is rendered present to consciousness; it is at once the end and the means, the awareness of God and the discipline that cultivates that awareness, and for this reason it is set above every other act of worship — the one thing through which all the rest are dignified. Its central image is the Imam's own: remembrance is a polish for the hearts, burnishing the clouded mirror until it recovers a hearing and a sight it had lost. And what the polished heart recovers is a vision — not the eye's seeing, which can never reach God, but the heart's seeing through the verities of faith, a vision that turns out, at its depth, to be less man's sight of God than God's own sight, effected through the heart He has purified. Everything the rest of this book will say about remembrance grows from these roots: that dhikr renders the divine present, that it clears the heart, and that the cleared heart sees. What remains is to follow each of these claims to its end — into the paradox of a world that hides and reveals God at once, into the self-effacement in which alone the vision is completed, and into the divine Name whose invocation sets the whole discipline in motion.

## Closing

But the moment one says that the purified heart sees God everywhere, a problem rears up that cannot be left unanswered. For the world is also, on every account, precisely what stands between the creature and God — the veil that hides the divine Essence from every gaze. How can the same created order be at once the veil that conceals God and the mirror that reveals Him? The Imam met this head-on with one of his most compressed and startling sayings: the veils do not veil Him. The next episode takes up that paradox, following the Imam and the mystics who elaborated him into the strange logic by which the very things that seem to hide God cannot help disclosing Him — and into the delicate balance, between affirming God's nearness to all things and His utter incomparability with them, on which the whole vision of divine oneness turns.
