# A Sacred Conception of Justice

*Reza Shah-Kazemi's Justice and Remembrance turns from the interior life of the intellect to the cardinal virtue that orders the world: justice. This first movement of the study's central essay refuses the dictionary sense of the word — fairness, equality, fair play — and works instead to align justice with the sacred, locating its very roots inside the nature of God. Reading Ali (ع)'s letter to his governor Malik al-Ashtar against Alasdair MacIntyre's critique of modern ethics and against Plato's vision of the Good, the chapter argues that the moral effort to be just is immeasurably deepened when it is consciously tied to the divine, and that the Imam's most exact definition of justice — putting everything in its right place — begins with putting one's own relationship to God in its right place first.*

## Where this episode opens

What if justice were not first of all a rule about how we treat one another, but a name for the nature of God? We are used to hearing the word as a settlement between people — fairness, equality, the even hand, the level playing field. It is a serviceable meaning, and nothing in this chapter throws it away. But Shah-Kazemi opens his study of justice by insisting that this everyday sense is only the outermost skin of the word, and that beneath it lies something of an altogether different order: a justice aligned with the sacred, with al-Quds, the All-Holy, and rooted in the divine nature itself. The claim reorders everything. If justice belongs first to God and only then to us, then to be just is not merely to be fair — it is to become, in the measure a creature can, like the Real.

We arrive at this from the ethos we have already been tracing. The opening chapter followed the Imam's intelligence inward, to the war a person wages against his own soul and the deliverance tasted here and now when goodness and its reward turn out to be one substance. That was interior work — the ordering of a single soul. This chapter carries the same intelligence outward, into the ordering of the world, and it does so through the most celebrated document the tradition preserves on the subject: the long letter of counsel Ali (ع) wrote to Malik al-Ashtar when he appointed him governor of Egypt. But the letter is not read here as a manual of statecraft. It is read as a commentary on revelation, a statement of ethics as much as of politics, in which the outward rules of good rule keep opening onto the inner nature of the God who is their source. That reading is deliberate, and it sets the essay apart from the usual scholarship. Read as a constitution, the letter is a remarkable but dated artifact — a first-century document about the governance of a province long since transformed. Read as a commentary on the divine nature, it becomes something that cannot date: an account of what it means for any soul, in any age and under any form of government, to give each thing its due once "each thing" is understood to include one's own relationship with the Real.

The argument has a precise shape, and it is worth naming before we enter it. Justice, the Imam teaches, is putting everything in its right place. That sounds like an administrative maxim until one asks what "right place" means and where the standard of rightness comes from. Shah-Kazemi's answer is that the standard is the divine nature — that a thing is in its right place when it stands as it stands in God, and therefore as it ought to stand here on earth. Three movements carry us there. First, why a sacred conception of justice differs from the modern secular kind, and why virtue cut off from the divine turns situational and hollow. Second, the metaphysics that makes this possible — the Imam's teaching that God has no opposite, so that evil is not justice's rival but its mere negation. And third, the vision that grounds the whole thing: ihsan, worshipping God as if you could see Him, embodied in the letter to Malik and in the life of the man who wrote it.

## Justice and the sacred

Begin with what the sacred conception is not. In everyday speech, justice means little more than fairness, equality, and fair play — a workable but essentially formal account, the kind one finds in a dictionary. Shah-Kazemi does not dispute that this describes something real. His point is that it describes only the horizontal face of justice, the settling of accounts between one person and another, and that it leaves the deeper question untouched: why be just at all, and by what measure? The conception at work in the Imam's ethos is of a different order entirely. It aligns justice with the sacred and locates the roots of justice within the divine nature itself — so that the first act of justice is not something done between people but something done between the soul and God. "Be just with God," the Imam instructs Malik, "and be just with people from yourself." The order is deliberate. Being just with God means conforming, as far as one can, to His nature — and that nature, the Imam insists, is not merely the source of justice but is justice. "I bear witness," he says in one of his sermons, "that He is Justice, and He acts justly."

That single word — is — carries the weight of the whole chapter. Justice, on this reading, is not one attribute of God standing alongside His mercy, His wrath, His love, as items on a list. It is the very essence of His nature, so that every other quality flows from it: His mercy is an expression of His justice, and so too are His anger, His love, His creating, and His destroying. Everything He does is the placing of each thing where it belongs. And because the soul was made from that nature and retains a buried affinity with it, the human effort to be just is not the imposition of an external rule but the recovery of something innate — a spiritual predisposition toward justice, waiting to be actualized. There is a real difference between the divine and human sides of this. In God, justice means that everything already is in its right place, an immutable metaphysical fact; in a person, justice is the effort to put everything in its right place, a dynamic and volitional labor. But the effort expresses the principle, and the principle is one. When a person acts justly, he does not merely make a moral choice; he reaches toward the way things truly are in God, and therefore the way they ought to be on earth.

Shah-Kazemi offers an image to make the relation concrete. Imagine the shari'a, the revealed law, as having a body and a spirit, just as a person does. Its body is a set of actions — the prayer, the fast, the paying of the alms — the visible observances a Muslim performs. But the spirit animating those actions rests on the nature of the divine, which is justice. Adherence to the law, on this view, is a training in becoming like the divine through the principles of justice, and so outward action must be complemented by inward contemplation of the divine. The two are not opposed but complementary — and their complementarity goes to the heart of the message of tawhid, an integrating oneness that makes theory and practice one, so that a person becomes a true muwahhid, one who not only affirms the divine unity with his tongue but embodies it in the unity of his own being. This is why contemplation and action, being and doing, ideal and reality, run together through the letter to Malik rather than pulling against each other. The document is not a compromise between piety and practicality; it is a demonstration that at the deepest level the two are the same movement.

The distinction the essay is reaching for here is the one Amyn Sajoo draws, in a thoughtful study of Muslim ethics, between ethics and morals. Morals, on this account, pertain to rules about right and wrong — the outward code of conduct. Ethics is concerned with the deeper thing, with conceptions of the good, with the soul and spirit from which the rules are born; it deals with the batin, the inner dimension, and it is out of ethics, so understood, that morals arise. The concepts overlap in practice, but keeping the more encompassing sense of ethics in view matters, because it grounds the whole discussion in a more organic framework. Situating ethics, in turn, within the still wider frame of spiritual virtue — ihsan — expands the compass of both ethics and morality and lends them a dimension of existential depth. Abstract questions of right and wrong come to rest on a more definitive principle, the one that emerges in answer to the deepest question a person can ask: what is reality? Justice, in the sacred conception, is finally the answer to that question translated into action.

This is where the chapter's engagement with modern ethics does its work. Shah-Kazemi reads the Imam against the background of Alasdair MacIntyre's critique of contemporary moral discourse — one of the most searching indictments of how the modern West came to talk about virtue. MacIntyre's charge, put simply, is that for several centuries ethical discourse has drifted from its moorings in absolute values, no longer grounded in any conception of "the good life" or of "the Good" as such. Cut loose from that ground, morality has been left to be filled in by whatever is at hand — relativism, which holds that truth and morality exist only in relation to a culture or an age; nihilism, which rejects all moral principle and finds life meaningless; utilitarianism, which counts an act right only if it profits a majority, reducing good and bad to a calculation of gain and loss; and opportunism, which seizes whatever advantage circumstances offer, the way of the manipulator who flatters and performs to get what he wants. Whatever occasional value these theories yield, none of it is rooted in the divine; and so even where they happen to coincide with the values of faith, they do so by accident and remain, in Shah-Kazemi's stark image, dead corpses — the right shape with no life in it.

The reason is not hard to see once the Imam's principle is in view. Virtue practiced on an ad hoc basis is not virtue at all; it is a series of pragmatic adaptations to changing circumstances in the absence of any genuine principle. Take truthfulness. If one resolves always to speak the truth without grasping why truth matters — that al-Haqq, the absolute truth, is the very nature of the Creator, and that the whole aim of this life is to take on the qualities of that Creator — then truthfulness inevitably becomes situational. It will be spoken when it profits and abandoned when it costs, compromised whenever circumstances seem to warrant. But once truth is seen as the nature of the divine, it becomes everlasting and indifferent to consequence, because one now speaks it in emulation of the immortal nature of God. The virtue itself becomes immortal; the need to calculate whether to practice it falls away. MacIntyre, from within the Western tradition, was searching for exactly this — "a conception of the good," he writes, "which will enable us to order other goods," to understand "the place of integrity and consistency in life." Shah-Kazemi's suggestion is that the Imam's legacy offers not a theory answering that search but something more: a life in which the answer was lived.

For this is the first thing the Imam contributes, beyond any argument. He was himself a paragon of justice — a living embodiment of the virtue, not a theorist of it. In his life one observes an unflinching adherence to this cardinal quality, a life not governed by his own nafs, his lower self, but governed entirely by the divine nature. The remarkable deeds the tradition remembers were the fruit of an irresistible attraction toward the nature of al-Haqq: his attention was never fixed on the action itself but on the source of the principles generating it, so that by gazing on the beauty of the divine nature and striving to take it on, good actions arose from him naturally and inevitably. Alongside that living example stands the second contribution — the discourses, letters, and sayings, which offer a rich reflection on the dynamic spirituality at the root of a life of virtue. Through them one glimpses a way of life totally governed by the sacred, in which the desire for the good life emerges as the fruit of an attraction toward the Good as such — which is identical, in this vision, to the absolutely Real. One is ethically inspired by the Good in exact proportion to one's existential orientation toward the Real. Situated this way, ethics is no longer a sealed compartment. It rests, as the tradition frames it, on three components of the spiritual life: islam, which supplies the outer aspects of morality; iman, which is the framework of ethics; and the union of the two, issuing in ihsan — the good life, the beautiful life — which is where the chapter is ultimately headed.

## God has no opposite

To ground justice in the nature of God, the chapter first has to correct how we habitually picture that nature. We think in opposites. We set good against evil, God against Satan, light against dark, and imagine the moral world as a contest between two matched powers. The Imam's teaching on divine oneness dismantles that picture at its foundation. In his vision of tawhid, one perceives — according to one's capacity — an ultimate reality that not only "has no second" but has no moral opposite at all. God does not stand against anything, because nothing has the capacity to stand against God. This is not wordplay; it changes the whole meaning of justice, because it changes what evil is.

Consider Iblis, the figure our imagination most wants to cast as God's adversary. He does not stand against God, the Imam teaches, for nothing can; he stands against God's creation. His entire effort is to drive people away from their inherent divine nature by beautifying its opposite — making arrogance attractive where God's nature is modest, lewdness attractive where it is decent, boastfulness attractive where it is humble. Evil, then, is not a rival principle with its own standing in being. The goodness proper to God is not the moral opposite of evil the way white is the opposite of black; it occupies an entirely different plane, the plane of al-Haqq, the absolutely Real, whose only "opposite" is unreality, nothingness. Goodness is at one with the pure positivity of the Absolute. Evil derives solely from a negative capacity — the capacity of a free creature to negate the Real. The essence of all goodness and beauty is identified with the Creator; evil is a modality of the created, the corrupted state of a creation that has deviated from the divine nature.

Shah-Kazemi finds this precise metaphysics implied in a verse the whole tradition knows, the opening of the sura of the daybreak, which calls the believer to seek refuge in God "from the evil of what He has created." The wording repays attention. It is often mistranslated as "from the evil which He created" — which is wrong, Shah-Kazemi argues, both theologically and lexically. The Arabic phrase refers to the evil that derives from creation, not to creation itself as evil. In theological terms, God creates morally free agents, and it is they who author evil; when a creature chooses, through its own free will, to negate the divine nature of its Creator, evil is born. God may be said to "create" all actions only in the metaphysical sense that He is the source of all being and thus of every modality within being, and action is one such modality. But God cannot be said to have created evil as such — only the conditions within which evil can emerge. Evil is the negation of the Good, which is at one with the Real; and as negation it has no ultimate ontological principle of its own. It is a temporary state of deviation from that pure and divine nature, an illusion, witnessed for what it is by the eye of the heart.

Far from making evil a small thing to be shrugged at, this vision intensifies the resolve to overcome it. The verse becomes a call to seek refuge in the Real from the unreal, there being no common measure in being between goodness and evil. Goodness is opposable only by the appearance of evil, an appearance the eye of the heart grasps as illusion — that inner eye which truly sees God through the verities of faith. The moral effort to be good grows stronger, not weaker, in proportion to one's conviction that goodness is more real than evil — a certainty carried inward from the mind to the heart, nourished by the spiritual vision of the good as the Real. Perceiving the radical incommensurability between good and evil does not lead to indifference in the face of evil; it leads to a more existentially compelling orientation toward the good. One does not fight evil less for knowing it is unreal; one fights it with the whole weight of the Real behind the effort.

There is a deeper claim folded into this, and it is the hinge on which the sacred conception turns: one has to be good before one can do good. This is not a play on words. If justice is finally attunement to the Real, then just action is not a technique that a wicked soul could master; it flows from what a person is. The only way "to be," in the fullest sense, is to be at one with the Real — that which alone truly is. So the spiritual conception of virtue does not merely regulate what one does to others; it engages the whole of what one is. It asks not only for right conduct but for a shift in the center of gravity of the soul, away from the ego and toward the transcendent reality that is the source of all virtue and all truth. This is why the chapter can insist that justice, properly understood, is never merely horizontal. To give every being its due, one must first stand in the right relation to Being itself; and to stand in that relation is already to have begun placing oneself, the most stubborn of all things, in its proper place beneath God.

Now the definition can carry its full charge. Justice, the Imam says, "puts everything in its right place." One is just insofar as one gives everything its proper due, renders the haqq owed to each person — indeed to every single thing in existence. And the capacity to be just in this demanding, universal sense is enhanced in exact proportion to one's attunement to al-Haqq, the Real. The word haqq itself holds both halves of this: it means what is right, and it means the rightful due, and both are infused with an aura of absoluteness. Understanding what is right leads to the commitment to fulfill one's obligations; the rightful due is the restoration of reality through the elimination of illusory evil. Both are equally charged with reality — which is justice. This is why the chapter can say that the moral notions of rectitude and propriety are expressions of a principle that transcends morality, a principle pertaining to ultimate reality itself. One's resolution to give every being its due is made more absolute by the very quality of absoluteness proper to the Real.

Here the chapter draws its first bridge to Plato, and it is worth marking because the whole essay will return to it. The Imam's definition evokes the famous account in the Republic, where justice is "that everyone ought to perform the one function in the community for which his nature best suited him." The parallels between Plato's dialogue and the Imam's letter run deep, and Shah-Kazemi will return to them; within Islamic ethical discourse, justice was often understood, in a Platonic key, not as one virtue among several but as the perfection and sum of all the others — of temperance, courage, and wisdom, which must order the appetitive, the spirited, and the rational dimensions of the soul. When every part of the soul, filled out by its proper virtue, stands in its right place, the soul may be called just. The point of noting the parallel is not to reduce the Imam to Plato but to mark, in advance, the one thing the Imam supplies that Plato leaves implicit: how a person actually attains the vision of the Real from which just ordering flows — a question the chapter will answer, at its close, through remembrance and prayer. As established in the opening chapter's teaching on the intellect, this is not the learning of something new but the recollection of principles already inherent in the substance of the intellect, rooted in the Sovereign Good itself.

## The vision and the letter

Everything so far rests on a single relationship — between worship and virtue, between devotion to the Creator and goodness toward His creatures — and the tradition fixes that relationship in one famous exchange. It is called the hadith of Gabriel, because the questioner who approached the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) as he sat among his Companions, unrecognized until he departed, was the angel Gabriel in human form. He asked in turn about submission, about faith, and about virtue. Told of submission — the testimony that there is no god but God and Muhammad is His messenger, the prayer, the alms, the fast of Ramadan, the pilgrimage — he said, astonishingly to the Companions, "You have spoken truly." Told of faith — belief in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and the divine decree of good and evil alike — again he confirmed it. And then he asked about virtue, ihsan, and received the definition that stands at the root of the whole ethos: "It is to worship God as though you see Him; and if you see Him not, yet truly He sees you."

From that single sentence the crucial relationship becomes visible. Ihsan can be literally rendered "doing what is beautiful," for its root is tied to husn, beauty, and virtue and vice are named in Islamic ethical discourse as husn and qubh — beauty and ugliness. It is ultimately the vision of divine beauty that inspires virtue, which is the beauty of the soul, just as departure from the divine qualities is what leaves the soul ugly. This is why, broadly, it fell to the mystics of the tradition — the custodians of the inner life — to be the specialists in "making beautiful," where the jurists tended the domain of outward action and the theologians the domain of formal belief; and why so many of the tradition's artists were themselves practitioners of that inner path. Worshipping God as if one could see Him means composing oneself in the constant consciousness of standing in the divine presence, for even if you cannot see Him, He sees you. That awareness, Shah-Kazemi argues, lies at the very root of a life of virtue in the spiritual universe of Islam — and its denial, he adds pointedly, is what allows some in every age to commit atrocities in the name of a religion whose deepest spirit they have abandoned. The Imam repeatedly draws the reader of his sermons back to this vision of God as the true basis of virtue, and warns just as repeatedly of its opposite — the engrossing vision of oneself, the vanity and self-praise that give Satan his most reliable opening to efface the very virtue of the virtuous. The first vision leads to justice, putting all things in their proper place, the ego most of all; the second leads to every injustice that flows from self-aggrandizement, including the fanaticism the Imam names by calling Satan the leader of the bigots and the forerunner of the proud.

That the theory and the practice of ethics have always been integral to this inner tradition is easy to miss, and Shah-Kazemi takes a moment to make it plain. Even figures who wrote in more philosophical and less explicitly spiritual terms were often themselves practitioners of the inner path. The most influential meeting-place of spirituality and ethics is al-Ghazali, whose great work, the Revival of the Religious Sciences, stands as a monument to the impact of Islam's deepest spirit on the ethical disposition of the believing soul; he has been called the greatest thinker on ethics in the tradition, and the fact that his thought is so thoroughly shaped by the inner path is itself testimony to how close ethics and that path have always been. Indeed, the majority of the treatises the mystics wrote are, in effect, treatises on ethics — if ethics is understood not narrowly, as a code of conduct, but as the fruit of ihsan, of spiritual virtue. The very spiritual stations these writers describe bear the names of particular virtues: patience, gratitude, contentment, generosity. And the thread running through all of them is the same transformative awareness of God, the principle of acting as if one could see Him, conducting oneself in the constant consciousness of the divine presence. This is the imperative that lies at the root of a life of virtue in this universe — the same imperative the letter to Malik will apply, relentlessly, to the exercise of power.

It is this vision, and this ethos, that the letter to Malik al-Ashtar embodies. The epistle appears as number fifty-two in most editions of the Nahj al-balagha, and across the centuries it has been read as an ideal constitution for just governance, complementing the framework of the Constitution of Medina with its detailed account of the duties and rights of the ruler, the functionaries of the state, and the main classes of society. But its counsel far exceeds the parameters of a political or legal text. Much of what is addressed to the governor applies just as fully to the governed, for it concerns universally applicable ethical principles; the letter is as much a statement of ethics as of politics — not a system or theory, but an inspiring expression of the spiritual ethos the Imam embodied and radiated, flowing directly from the sources of revelation. One can read it, accordingly, as itself a commentary on revelation, one that sends the reader back into the revealed texts to delve deeper. Its recipient was no ordinary appointee. Malik al-Ashtar was among the Imam's most devoted followers, called one of his "right-hand" men — a phrase even his enemies used. When Mu'awiya, the Imam's chief antagonist in the civil war that consumed his caliphate, learned that Malik had been poisoned before he could take up his post in Egypt, he is reported to have said that Ali (ع) "had two right hands. One of them was cut off at Siffin, and the other today." The Imam's own response to the news measured the loss differently: "Malik — and what was Malik! By God, were he a mountain, he would be the solitary one soaring above all others, and were he a rock, he would have been impenetrably solid. No horse could ascend such a mountain, no bird could fly up to it."

To read the letter's opening command — "Be just with God, and be just with people from yourself" — is therefore to hear the whole metaphysics in a single instruction. Being just with God means conforming to His nature, which is justice; and everything else in the letter derives from first putting that relationship in its right place. The Imam roots even the reciprocity of ruler and ruled in the divine: the most momentous of the mutual rights God has made binding, he says in another sermon, is the right of the ruler over the subjects and of the subjects over the ruler — a reciprocal obligation God Himself has ordained. Applied to state and society, one of the most immediate consequences is the necessity of piety and rectitude in the ruler, for as the Imam says, the subjects will not be righteous except through the righteousness of the rulers, and the rulers not except through the uprightness of the subjects; when each renders the other its due, right is honored, the ways of religion are upheld, and the banners of justice are raised. But this is far more than an exhortation to obey the formal rules, and the Imam's own life proved it. When he assumed the caliphate, his cousin Ibn al-'Abbas advised him, as sound strategy, to confirm the previous governors in their posts temporarily and remove them only after consolidating power. The Imam refused: "I do not doubt that this would be best for the sake of reconciliation in this world. But there is my obligation to the Truth, and my knowledge of those governors — so, by God, I shall never appoint one of them." He knew the refusal made revolt all but inevitable; he knew Mu'awiya wanted only worldly power and would have been content with Syria had he allowed it. Commentators have read the decision as political naivety. Shah-Kazemi reads it as disdain for worldly ease bought at the price of principle — for, as the Imam said, people renounce no part of their religion to rectify their world without God opening them to something worse than the very defect they meant to fix. To confirm governors whose corruption was beyond doubt would have been, for him, to renounce a part of his religion — for religion without justice is not religion. Supporting unjust governors, even as a tactical maneuver, was a compromise of religious principle simply inconceivable to him; and this attitude was embodied not in one decision but in countless episodes of his life, all of them demonstrating that the principles enshrined in the letter to Malik were far from merely theoretical. The whole of his life, Shah-Kazemi writes, can be seen as a quest to conform as perfectly as possible to the divine nature, and to this quality of justice in particular — which is precisely why the letter he sent to his governor reads less like the instruction of a magistrate than the testimony of a man describing the only world he could bear to live in.

## What this episode lands

This opening movement of the essay has done one thing above all: it has moved justice off the horizontal plane and rooted it in the vertical. Justice is not first a rule about people; it is the nature of God, and to be just is to conform to that nature by putting everything — the ego most of all — in its right place. We have seen why a secular ethics, however useful, lacks the resource a sacred conception has: cut from the divine, even a genuine virtue turns situational and hollow, a dead corpse of the right shape. We have seen the metaphysics that makes the sacred conception coherent — that God has no opposite, that evil is negation rather than a rival power, so that the effort to be good carries the whole weight of the Real. And we have seen the vision that grounds it all: ihsan, worshipping God as if you could see Him, the root from which justice grows, embodied in the letter to Malik and in the life of the man who would sooner lose his empire than confirm an unjust governor.

The chapter has also set its own agenda. Having established what a sacred conception of justice is, the essay now turns to its clearest test — the ruler, and the temptation to injustice that power sets before him. Shah-Kazemi announces the plan plainly: the analysis will look first at a dimension of justice that is political on the surface, ethical in its outward expression, and spiritual in its depth — the justice of the ruler, seen against its clearest opposite, which is tyranny and oppression. The wager throughout is that a deep assimilation of the Imam's spirituality bestows an existential power on the moral effort to live justly, a power simply absent when justice is conceived in purely horizontal, secular terms, cut off from any spiritual principle. The next movement follows the letter into its sombre opening, where the Imam warns Malik about the soul that incites to evil and about the subtlest of all vices, the pride that sets a person, however quietly, at war with God. Justice put everything in its right place; tyranny is what happens when a soul puts itself in the place of God.
