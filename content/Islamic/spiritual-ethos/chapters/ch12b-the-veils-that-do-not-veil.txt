This episode takes up the paradox left standing at the end of the last: how the created world can be at once the veil that hides God and the mirror that reveals Him. We follow the Imam's compressed saying that the veils do not veil Him, the strange logic by which the very things that seem to conceal God cannot help disclosing Him, the balance between His nearness to all things and His incomparability with them, and then the practice of invocation itself — the actual work of remembrance that a life is meant to be woven around.

## Where this episode picks up

If God is closer to a person than the vein in their own neck, and if wherever one turns there is His Face, then what is left for the whole visible world to be — except a veil drawn across a presence that was never actually absent? That is the knot this episode sets out to untie. The previous episode ended with the claim that the heart polished by remembrance comes to see God everywhere, in and through all things; but the same created order that the purified heart reads as a book of signs is, on every ordinary account, precisely what stands between the creature and the Creator, hiding the divine Essence from every gaze. Creation both shows God and screens Him, and the two facts seem flatly to cancel each other. The Imam met this contradiction not by softening it but by sharpening it, in one of the most compressed sayings in his whole corpus: the veils do not veil Him. It is the kind of saying that stops the mind, because it appears to deny the obvious — of course veils veil, that is what the word means. And that is exactly why it repays slow attention. The Imam is not being careless with language; he is pointing to a truth about the world that only shows itself once the ordinary meaning of "veil" has been turned inside out. This episode follows that saying, and the mystics who elaborated it, into the heart of the paradox — and then turns from the vision to the practice, from what the remembering heart sees to the actual discipline of invocation by which a life is polished at all.

## The veils that do not veil

Begin with the ordinary intuition the Imam means to overturn. We take it for granted that the world hides God: that the sheer solidity and self-evidence of things — their colors, weights, and surfaces — draw the eye and hold it, so that we see the creation and not its Creator. On this picture the world is a curtain, and the spiritual task is to see past it. The Imam does not simply deny this; he transforms it. The veils, he says, do not veil Him. They cannot help revealing what they are supposed to conceal. They are not merely transparent to the reality behind them, as thin cloth is transparent to a lamp; in and of themselves, they reveal rather than hide it. This recalls another of his sayings, endlessly quoted by the Sufis and followed earlier in this series — that were the veil to be lifted, he would gain no increase in certitude, so completely does he already see through it. The veils of creation reveal what they first conceal, though this revelation is perceived only by the eye of the heart, purified through remembrance, the heart that now sees after having been blind.

To grasp how a veil can reveal, one must ask what the veil actually is. The Imam gives a startling answer. The veil between God and His creatures, he says, is nothing other than His very creation of them. Creation itself is the veil. He puts it in dense, deliberate terms: this veil exists for the sake of preventing what is possible in the essences of creatures, and for the sake of making possible what is prevented in regard to Him — and so as to distinguish the Creator from the created, the limit from the limited, the Lord from the vassal. Set aside the compression for a moment and take the plain sense first. To be a creature is to be distinct from God; that distinctness is the veil. God's act of creating something is the very act by which it stands apart from Him as a separate thing — and it is this apartness, this being-a-limited-thing, that keeps the divine Essence from being simply seen in it. The veil is not an object laid between God and the world; it is the createdness of the world as such.

And yet — here is the turn — everything that is created, every veil, is nothing other than a face of the divine name the Apparent, al-Zahir: the Manifest, the Evident, the one who shows Himself. Creation is manifest not on its own account but by virtue of the divine Reality it projects, a self-disclosure, a theophany. As the Apparent, God projects something of His hidden treasure within and as the creation; the world manifests that aspect of God which can appear, which He can bring into being, even while it conceals the other aspect that remains forever hidden — the Hidden, al-Batin, the supra-manifest source of being, the uncreated Essence. The veil of creation is therefore, in the end, the self-revelation of God. It hides Him only in the way a word both is and is not the mind that speaks it; it reveals Him by the very act in which it seems to screen Him.

It helps to hold both of the divine names together here, for they are two faces of one reality. God is the Apparent and the Hidden at once — the one who shows Himself in all things and the one who remains, in His Essence, forever beyond all showing. Creation is His self-disclosure precisely as the Apparent; it is a projection outward of something from the inexhaustible depth that stays hidden, the way light streaming from a source discloses the source without emptying it. This is why the mystics could call the whole of creation the Book of God, every thing in it a word He speaks. A word both hides and reveals the one who utters it: it hides him in that it is not himself, a mere sound or mark set apart from the speaker; and it reveals him in that it carries his meaning, is charged with his intent, and would not exist at all if he had not spoken. So the world stands in relation to God. It is other than He, and to that extent a veil; it is spoken by Him, and to that extent nothing but His self-utterance. The Apparent shows what can appear of God; the Hidden keeps what cannot. Neither face cancels the other, and it is the failure to hold them together that turns the veil opaque.

No one pressed this mystery harder than the Sufi master Ibn 'Ata'illah, whose aphorisms read like a series of meditations on the Imam's terse saying. God, he writes, has veiled you from Himself by that which has no existence alongside of Him — so that in one respect God is guarded from the eyes, veiled by things, yet insofar as God alone truly is, these things do not really exist, and so He is veiled by nothing at all. From that paradox Ibn 'Ata'illah unfolds a cascade of rhetorical questions, each of which pulls the veil away as it names it. How can it be conceived that something veils Him, he asks, since He is the one who manifests everything? How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the one who is manifest through everything, manifest in everything, the Manifest to everything? How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He was manifest before the existence of everything, more manifest than anything, the One alongside of whom there is nothing, nearer to you than anything else — since, were it not for Him, the existence of everything would not be manifest at all? He ends not with an answer but with a marvel: it is a wonder, he says, how Being has shown itself in non-being, and how the contingent has been made to stand alongside the One who alone possesses eternity. Every question drives the same point deeper. The thing you took for a veil is only there because God makes it appear; to see it at all is already to be seeing by His light. What could such a thing possibly hide?

Notice how completely this reframes the spiritual task. On the ordinary picture, the work is to fight past the world, to push the curtain of things aside in order to reach a God concealed behind it. On the Imam's picture, there is no God concealed behind the world in that sense at all; the world is the showing of God, and the only thing that hides Him is the way we look — the eye fixed on the surface of things as though the surface were the whole of them. The veil is real, but it is a veil the way a written word veils the meaning it also carries: to one who cannot read, the marks on the page are an opaque barrier; to one who can, the very same marks are pure transparency, nothing but the meaning made visible. The heart polished by remembrance is the heart that has learned to read. It looks at the same world everyone looks at and sees, in and through it, the creativity that everyone else takes for a wall. Nothing about the world has changed; what has changed is the organ of sight.

The whole force of these reflections gathers back into the Imam's four words: the veils do not veil Him. The created order cannot finally conceal the divine creativity of which it is the projection; it has the character of a veil, and yet it reveals. But — and the Imam is emphatic about this — it reveals only by virtue of what pierces the veil of its own apparent otherness: that divine creativity which the creation cannot conceal even though, in principle, it has the form of a concealment. The world is not God, and to mistake it for God would be the gravest of errors; and yet the world cannot help pointing beyond itself to God. Both truths must be held at once, and holding them at once is exactly what the next movement of the Imam's teaching requires.

## Nearness and incomparability

The Imam's account of the veil turns on two possibilities held in tension, and it is worth drawing them out, for the whole balance of the vision depends on them. The veil of creation, he says, prevents what the essences of creatures make possible. What do those essences make possible? The realization of the inmost identity of things with the divine Reality — oneness at its very highest level — a possibility grounded in the fact that this same divine Reality is the foundation of their essences. As followed in the last episode, it is the divine Names and qualities that fill the foundations of all things; at the root of anything there is, in truth, only the one Reality. If the realization of that truth is possible, what then does the veil prevent? It prevents the affirmation of this identity at the wrong level — at the level of ordinary, empirical existence, rather than at the deepest level where the essences of things are nothing other than the divine qualities. To equate created things as they stand with God is the cardinal error of association, of setting up partners beside Him, and it is against precisely this that a true vision of oneness must guard. One must always distinguish the Creator from the created, the limit from the limited, the Lord from the vassal.

So the veil of creation acts, first, as a barrier against pantheism — against the reduction of the Creator's infinite transcendence to the finite forms of creatures. That is its protective work. And yet, because the veil has no existence apart from the self-manifestation of the Real, it also, paradoxically, makes possible what would otherwise be prevented: rendered transparent to the divine Reality it expresses, the veil cannot in the end keep the immanent presence of God from shining through and within all things. On one side stands essential identity, without any diminishing of God's transcendence; on the other stands existential distinction, without any weakening of His immanence. We return to that foundational sentence from the first sermon, followed at the close of the last episode: God is with every thing, but not through association, and other than every thing, but not through separation. His being with all things does not lower Him into them; His being other than all things does not exile Him from them.

The mystics gave this double truth a pair of names that the whole of Islamic metaphysics turns on. There is the declaration of God's incomparability, His absolute transcendence above everything — the way of saying what God is not, that nothing whatever is like Him. And there is the affirmation of similarity, and at the deepest level of immanence — the way of recognizing the divine presence disclosed in and through the world. The trap, the Imam and the mystics insist, is to seize one of these and let go of the other. Ibn al-'Arabi states the balance with almost vertiginous precision: God is not to be declared incomparable in any way that removes Him from similarity, nor declared similar in any way that removes Him from incomparability. Do not, he warns, make Him boundless in a way that then bounds Him — for if His unboundedness is set off against boundary, it has itself become one more limit, and then He is not truly He. Transcendence pressed one-sidedly becomes a cage; immanence pressed one-sidedly becomes an idol. Each is true only in the light of the other.

There is a further consequence here, and it concerns God's oneness. As established earlier in this series, the oneness the Imam affirms is not the oneness of a countable unit — not God as one thing set alongside other things and outnumbering them — but a oneness that admits no second at all, that includes all reality and excludes all otherness. That teaching bears directly on the veil. For if God's oneness were merely numerical, then the created world would be a genuine second alongside Him, a real other that could stand between the creature and its Lord, and the veil would be an unbreachable wall. But the Imam insists that God has no second — there is no other being in being but Him — and so the created world, however solid it appears, cannot be a true second that shuts God out. Its apparent otherness is exactly what the veil consists of, and exactly what the polished heart sees through. The world is not another thing beside God; it is His self-showing, and to see it as a rival reality is already to have misread the veil.

This is why the Imam can say that the veils do not veil Him — for both transcendence and immanence, both the created world as divine manifestation and the uncreated Essence beyond all manifestation, become veils in their turn unless they are held together in a single vision. A one-sided emphasis on God as the Apparent blinds us to God as the Hidden; a one-sided emphasis on the Hidden, considered in isolation, becomes its own conceptual veil, hiding God's very evidentness in the heart of the created world. The veils, in other words, are not only the outward things of creation but also the limitations built into every human conception of God — including our conceptions of His own qualities and attributes. And this points ahead to something the later episodes must take up: the need for a radical negation, on the plane of thought, even of the divine attributes as we conceive them, so that consciousness can begin to fathom a transcendence that no concept can hold. Only on the far side of that letting-go do the veils that do not veil Him become, at last, transparent.

It is worth naming the two errors this balance guards against, for they are the perennial temptations of the religious mind. The first is the error of collapse — of so stressing God's nearness and presence in things that the distinction between Creator and creature dissolves, and the world itself, or some part of it, is quietly divinized. This is the idolater's mistake in its subtlest form: mistaking the reflection for the source, the word for the speaker, and so worshipping a face of God as though it were God. The second is the error of exile — of so stressing God's transcendence and incomparability that He is pushed clean out of the world, sealed off in an unreachable beyond, present nowhere in the things He has made. This is the mistake that empties creation of its signs and leaves a person alone in a world gone mute. The Imam's teaching refuses both. God is genuinely with all things, so the world is never mute; God is genuinely other than all things, so the world is never God. And the vision that holds both at once is not a cautious compromise, a splitting of the difference that waters each truth down; it is the affirmation of each at full strength, in the light of the other. Nearness at its fullest and incomparability at its fullest turn out not to fight but to require one another — for only what is utterly beyond all things could be utterly present within them without being diminished, and only what is utterly present within all things could be utterly beyond them without becoming absent.

It should be clear that this is the language of spiritual vision, not of logical demonstration. These perspectives on the veil are the fruit of contemplative synthesis, not the conclusions of rational analysis; they resolve apparent contradiction not by a clever argument but through an intuitive awareness of the simple unity of the Real. As established earlier in this series, where the reasoning faculty perceives only irreducible contradiction, the deeper intellect apprehends a fruitful paradox — and it is this deeper faculty, the heart burnished by remembrance, that does the seeing here. The famous Sufi Abu Sa'id al-Kharraz, asked how he had come to know God, answered: through the fact that He brings opposites together. And then he recited the verse the whole paradox rests on — He is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward (chapter 57, verse 3). It is spiritual intuition that gives the power to see as one what the surface shows as many; and in sharpening the paradox to its edge, the Imam is not tormenting the mind but clearing it, dismantling the constructions of mere conception to make room for a vision that sees the unity of the Real directly. The vision is itself a form of the very remembrance that made it possible: the heart polished until it can hold together what the mind alone must tear apart.

## The quintessence of worship

Having followed the vision to its edge, we should now turn to the practice that grounds it — the actual, bodily, repeated work of invocation, without which all this talk of polished hearts remains a theory. The Qur'an is full of the command to do it. Invoke the Name of your Lord morning and evening (chapter 76, verse 25). Invoke the Name of your Lord, and devote yourself to Him with utter devotion (chapter 73, verse 8). O you who believe, invoke God with much invocation (chapter 33, verse 41). Call upon your Lord in humility and in secret (chapter 7, verse 55). Invoke your Lord within yourself, in humility and awe, beneath your breath, morning and night (chapter 7, verse 205). Every one of these points to an actual practice, to be kept up in different modes — within oneself, in humility, in awe, in secret, under the breath. But the note struck most insistently is abundance: the invocation is to be performed much. The early Muslims are remembered in the Qur'an for their long night vigils, standing in prayer through two-thirds of the night, and a half of it, and a third; and the same passage that recalls those vigils opens with the command to invoke the Name of one's Lord and devote oneself to Him with utter devotion. On the plane of devotion, giving oneself utterly to the Name is giving oneself utterly to the Named. The Absolute in itself lies beyond every form, including every verbal form, and yet it makes itself accessible through the Name — a Name not invented by human beings but bestowed by God, who reveals the sacrament of His own Name. And so distraction from the Name is distraction from God Himself: the devil, the Qur'an says, made them forget the remembrance of God (chapter 58, verse 19); let not your wealth nor your children distract you from the remembrance of God (chapter 63, verse 9).

The insistence on abundance is not incidental; it belongs to the very logic of the remedy. A medicine taken once and then forgotten does not heal a chronic condition, and the condition remembrance treats is chronic — the forgetfulness that has become a second nature, closing over the heart again as soon as the invocation lapses, the way rust returns to an untended blade. This is why the command is always to invoke much, and why the Prophet, asked for a single thing to hold onto, gave the image of a tongue kept perpetually moist with the invocation of God: not a dose but a continual moistening, a practice that never fully stops. The abundance is the medicine's dosage. The heart clouds over by the simple passage of time, and only a remembrance renewed as steadily as the clouding can keep pace with it. Understood this way, the Qur'an's repeated summons to invoke morning and evening, by night and by day, within oneself and beneath the breath, is not a heaping-up of pious extras but the prescription for a cure that has to be taken continually to work at all.

This identification of devotion to God with devotion to His Name flows from a simple, penetrating saying of the Imam: he who loves a thing dedicates himself fervently to its invocation. The word he uses carries both senses at once — to be devoted to something and to mention it constantly — and it names exactly what a lover does, forever bringing the beloved's name to his lips. The lover of God, then, gives himself wholly to the invocation of the Name of God, in abundance. The same pairing runs through the Imam's own great supplication to Kumayl, followed earlier, where he speaks of his tongue devoting itself to God's invocation and begs that his nights and days be inhabited by remembrance, that his tongue be made to invoke God with utter dedication. And he gives the Name a title that reframes the whole practice: O He whose Name is a remedy and whose invocation is the cure. Here the Name of God is far more than a word or a sound among other symbols. It is the remedy for the spiritual amnesia that has become the human being's second nature — the forgetfulness that dulls the heart. But a remedy, to work, must be taken, not merely contemplated; a medicine thought about but never swallowed heals no one. This is why the invocation must be actively performed and performed abundantly, and why the Imam says of the greatness of the Qur'an that it has made the remembrance incumbent upon our tongues. The Prophet made the same point to a man who complained that the laws of Islam were too many for him to hold and asked for one thing to cling to: let your tongue, the Prophet told him, never cease to be moist with the invocation of God.

There is something worth dwelling on in that list of modes — within oneself, in humility, in awe, in secret, beneath the breath, standing and sitting and lying down. It marks off the remembrance from every other rite by its sheer freedom of form. The fixed prayers prescribe particular words, particular movements, particular conditions of time and purity; they are, by their nature, bounded. The remembrance is bounded by none of this. It is to be performed at all times, in all places, in all postures — woven into the texture of an ordinary life rather than laid upon that life as an external, formal duty discharged and then set down. This supra-formal character is the whole point of the modes the Qur'an keeps naming. Remembrance is not one more scheduled obligation competing for a slot in the day; it is the practice that can fill the day, the invocation that continues while a person eats, walks, works, and rests. And it is exactly this unboundedness that lets it become the abiding awareness the last episode described — the condition of a heart for which no moment is empty of God.

The practice does not stand alone; it is bound up with reflection, and the two deepen each other. The Qur'an describes the possessors of substance as those who invoke God standing, sitting, and reclining on their sides, and who reflect upon the creation of the heavens and the earth (chapter 3, verses 190 and 191) — setting invocation and reflection side by side. The Imam draws the connection out: whoever invokes God, he says, God enlivens his heart and illuminates his inner substance. Invocation polishes the inmost organ of awareness, and the polished organ can meditate more deeply on the signs of God in creation and in the soul; and when love is set between the two, the three enter a single turning circle. The more one ponders the signs of God and tastes their beauty, the more one loves Him; the more one loves Him, the more one invokes His Name; the more one invokes, the more keenly one reads the sacred message written in those signs. Reflection intensifies remembrance, and remembrance deepens reflection, and love drives the wheel. And because remembrance can be carried out standing, sitting, reclining, in every posture and at every hour, it escapes the fixed conditions of the formal, canonical prayer. Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq observed that everything has a limit at which it stops — the fasts of Ramadan end when the month ends, the prescribed duties are discharged when they are fulfilled — everything except the remembrance of God, on which no limit is set; his own father, he said, never ceased invoking God, even while eating and walking.

This opens onto the deepest thing the Imam says about the practice — the sense in which remembrance is greater than the ritual prayer itself. The Qur'an tells us to establish the prayer for the sake of My remembrance (chapter 20, verse 14): the very purpose of prayer, its inward substance, is the remembrance of God. And a saying of the Imam presses the relation further — continuous invocation, he says, is the food of the spirit and the key to prayer. This is why the Qur'an can say, in the verse this whole book keeps returning to, that prayer keeps one from lewdness and iniquity, and the remembrance of God is greater (chapter 29, verse 45). Formal prayer is described there in negative terms, as a preventative; its formality is a ritual necessity but also, unavoidably, a limitation — performed at one time and not another, in these words and not those. The remembrance of God is greater because it is the positive, enlivening substance of prayer, what frees consciousness from the verbal and existential limits that define all formal prayer. Because formal prayer is bounded by such limits, its blessing can too easily feel bounded too, so that one feels near to God only in the prayer-times and not in the life around them. It is remembrance, in the sense of constant awareness of God, that lets prayer open outward into what it was always for — an awareness of the unconditioned presence of God at every moment. As followed earlier in this series through the letter to Malik, this is why the truly devout are described as perpetually at prayer (chapter 70, verse 23): not that they never rise from the prayer-mat, but that for them there is no moment that is not, in the deepest sense, prayer. The greater does not cancel the lesser; it comprises it, enriches it, and carries it to a fullness it could never reach alone.

It is worth being precise about this ranking, because it is easy to hear "the remembrance of God is greater" as a demotion of the ritual prayer, as though the invocation made the five daily prayers optional. The Imam means the reverse. The formal prayer is described as a preventative — it holds a person back from transgression, fences the soul against its own worst impulses — and that fencing is indispensable, the necessary discipline without which the higher awareness has no ground to stand on. What remembrance adds is not a replacement for the fence but the living presence the fence was built to protect. One might say remembrance is greater than ritual prayer in the exact measure that spiritual awareness is greater than moral restraint, or that the inner essence of a thing is greater than its outer form. The essence does not abolish the form; it needs the form as its vessel, and pours itself into it. And so the person who has grasped that remembrance is greater does not pray less; he prays with the whole of what the prayer was always for, and then carries that same awareness out of the prayer-times and into the ordinary hours, until the boundary between the prayer and the life around it begins to dissolve. The lesser is not left behind. It is taken up, filled, and completed by the greater it exists to serve.

## What this episode lands

This episode has held two things together that the mind wants to pull apart. The first is the paradox of the veil: the created world both hides God and reveals Him, and the Imam's four words — the veils do not veil Him — insist that the revealing is the deeper truth, since every apparent veil is only a face of the divine Apparent, projected from the hidden divine Reality it can never finally conceal. The second is the balance the paradox requires: God is with all things and yet other than all things, immanent without ceasing to be transcendent, near without ceasing to be incomparable — and to seize either pole alone is to erect a new veil, whether the idolatry that mistakes the world for God or the abstraction that exiles God from the world. Both truths are seen at once only by the heart's intuition, not the mind's analysis. And undergirding the whole vision is the practice: the actual, abundant invocation of the Name that is at once remedy and cure, woven with reflection and love into a single turning discipline, and greater even than the ritual prayer whose inner substance it is. The vision is real, but it is grown in the soil of a practice. Hold onto that pairing, because it protects against two opposite mistakes — the one that treats the metaphysics of the veil as an idle speculation with no bearing on how a person actually lives, and the one that treats the invocation as a mere technique with no vision behind it. In the Imam's teaching the two are one thing seen from two sides: the practice polishes the heart, and the polished heart sees through the veil, and what it sees drives it back to the practice. Neither half stands without the other.

## Closing

Yet everything said so far has assumed a self who invokes — a person who takes up the Name, polishes their heart, and comes to see. The Imam's most radical teaching about remembrance dismantles even that assumption. For he says, in a sentence that the whole mystical path is folded into, that one does not truly remember God until one forgets one's own soul in the remembrance. Perfect remembrance, it turns out, is not the summit; beyond it lies the reality of remembrance, and the passage from the one to the other demands nothing less than the effacement of the self that was doing the remembering. The next episode follows that vertiginous turn — from the polishing of the self to its forgetting, from remembrance as an act of the individual to an extinction in which the individual is no longer there to perform it.
