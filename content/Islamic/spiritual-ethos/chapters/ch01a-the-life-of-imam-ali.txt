# The Gate to Prophetic Wisdom

*The opening of Reza Shah-Kazemi's study of the spiritual ethos of Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع) — cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him), and the figure the tradition calls the Father of Imams. This episode does not argue a doctrine; it introduces a life. It follows Ali (ع) from his birth inside the Ka'ba, through his childhood in the Prophet's own household, to the battlefields of Badr, Uhud, and Khaybar, and on to the marriage that made him head of the Prophet's family. Along the way it gathers the prophetic sayings the tradition — Sunni and Shi'i alike — has meditated on for fourteen centuries: that Ali (ع) is "as my own soul," that he is "with the Qur'an and the Qur'an is with him," and the declaration at the pool of Ghadir Khumm that has been argued over ever since. The aim is to show why, before a single teaching of his is examined, this one companion is regarded as the gate through which prophetic wisdom passed into Islamic history.*

## Where this series opens

What kind of man is described by his own teacher as a second self — "he is as my own soul"? Not a favored student, not a gifted disciple, but a soul the Prophet Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him) spoke of as though it were continuous with his own. That claim is where any honest introduction to Ali ibn Abi Talib (ع) has to begin, because everything the tradition later built around him rests on it, and because it is so extravagant a thing to say of another human being that we are forced to ask what could possibly justify it.

To speak of Ali (ع) at all is to speak of the very heart of Islamic spirituality. In this early figure the tradition finds an integral meeting of its two fundamental sources: the Qur'anic revelation and the inspired example, the Sunna, of the Prophet. By Sunna the book does not mean the outward copying of the Prophet's gestures — a reductionism it regards as all too common in our own time — but the spiritual substance of prophetic perfection, the inner reality to which the Qur'an itself points when it tells the Prophet, "Verily, thou art of a tremendous nature" (chapter 68, verse 4). The Prophet's words and deeds express that substance without exhausting it. To assimilate it inwardly, rather than merely imitate its outer forms, is the goal of every spiritually serious Muslim, and it rests on a mysterious affinity between the believer's soul and the prophetic nature — the closeness the Qur'an names when it says, "The Prophet is closer to the believers than they are to themselves" (chapter 33, verse 6). The Prophet, in other words, outwardly manifests a perfection that the spiritually sensitive believer already intuits, however faintly, as the very essence of his or her own soul.

Read against that background, the epigraph the whole book hangs from stops being a slogan and becomes a thesis: "I am the city of knowledge and Ali is its gate; so whoever desires knowledge, let him enter the gate." This episode sketches the life that earned such words — the childhood in the Prophet's shadow, the valor in battle, the place at the head of the Prophet's household, and the sayings through which the tradition has always seen him. We stay deliberately with the early period, the years of nearness, and leave the later controversies aside; what we are after is not a verdict in an old dispute but the portrait of the man the tradition means when it calls him the gate.

## The gate and the two sources

Begin with the claim that unsettles everything else, because the Prophet made several versions of it and each raises the stakes. "Ali is as my own soul," he said — an affirmation not only of an extraordinary bond between the two men but of an affinity, and ultimately an identity, between every sanctified soul and the prophetic nature itself. The word to weigh is *soul*. He does not say Ali (ع) is his dearest friend or his ablest lieutenant; he locates him at the level where a person is most inwardly himself. More mysteriously still, he told Ali (ع) directly, "You are from me, and I am from you." Notice that the derivation runs both ways. A son is from his father, but a father is not, in the same sense, from his son; here the two clauses are symmetrical, and the symmetry is the point. It refuses to let the relationship be read as mere descent or mere discipleship. Something in each is said to originate in the other — which is exactly the kind of paradox, as later episodes will show, that the Imam's own teaching delights in.

The same reciprocity appears the moment the subject shifts from the Prophet's person to the Qur'an. "Ali is with the Qur'an and the Qur'an is with Ali," the Prophet said; "they will not separate from each other until they return to me at the paradisal pool." Again the two are set side by side as equals in a bond, not ranked as reader and book. On the book's reading, the spirit of the Qur'an and the soul of the Prophet were both fully interiorized by Ali (ع) — taken in so completely that they became the substance of his own consciousness rather than an external authority he consulted. And it is precisely that double interiorization of the twin sources of revelation, the Qur'an on one side and the prophetic nature on the other, that the book identifies as the spiritual path of Islam in its essence. The path is not first a set of rules; it is this inward assimilation, of which Ali (ع) is the exemplar.

This is why the tradition treats him not as one companion among many but as a unique case, and the evidence is not merely devotional but quantitative. Our principal way of investigating his spirituality is to engage the enormous corpus of teachings that bears his name. Even granting — as the book candidly does — that not every one of the thousands of sayings attributed to him can be confidently traced to his lips, the sheer magnitude of the corpus testifies that he must have articulated a very large number of profound teachings; no other companion of the Prophet possesses anything approaching this body of work. A comparable pattern holds across the sciences. Whether or not his foundational role can be established beyond dispute in every field, later authorities across an astonishing range of disciplines — jurisprudence (fiqh), theology (kalam), Qur'anic exegesis (tafsir), rhetoric (balagha), grammar (nahw), calligraphy (khatt), the mystical knowledge later gathered under Sufism, and even the arcane sciences of numerology (jafr) and alchemy (al-kimiya) — look back to him as the original impulse behind their fields. One can argue the historical details; what is not in doubt is the pull. That so many disciplines instinctively trace themselves to a single man says something about the reach of both his formal teaching and his personal radiance, the two working together.

There is a quiet honesty in how the book handles this, and it is worth noticing because it sets the tone for everything that follows. It does not claim that every saying in the corpus is authentic, nor that Ali (ع) single-handedly founded each of these sciences in the way a modern reader might mean by "founder." It grants the historical uncertainties openly. And yet it observes that the uncertainties do not dissolve the phenomenon; they only sharpen the real question. Why should so vast a body of teaching have gathered around this one name and no other companion's? Why should fields as different as grammar and alchemy, law and mysticism, each look back to the same figure as their origin? The magnitude itself is the evidence — not of this or that particular attribution, but of a source powerful enough to leave so wide a wake. The tradition's instinct, in other words, is not a naïve one; it survives the scholar's scruples. And it points beyond mere influence to the thing the book actually wants to talk about: a spiritual substance in Ali (ع) so concentrated that it could radiate outward into the most diverse forms and still be recognized, everywhere, as one and the same light. That is the substance the later episodes will try to describe from the inside — first as the intellect, then as the discipline of knowledge, then as remembrance and justice.

Given how thoroughly the Islamic intellectual sciences came to be identified with him, the Prophet's description of Ali (ع) as the gate to prophetic wisdom does double work at once. It describes what Ali (ع) actually was in relation to the Prophet during their shared lifetime — the one who stood at the threshold, taking in what the Prophet gave — and it prophesies the role he would play across the long unfolding of the tradition's sciences afterward. For all those sciences are understood here as so many outward, formal expressions of one inward spirit: the essential spirit of the Islamic revelation, which Ali (ع) synthetically assimilated and then faithfully transmitted. The metaphor of the gate repays a moment's attention. A gate is not a wall; its whole nature is to be passed through. To call Ali (ع) the gate to the city of knowledge is therefore not to make him a guard who rations entry but a passage through which the inner substance of revelation moved into the disciplines that would carry it forward. Everything the rest of the book explores — the theology of the intellect, the discourse on knowledge, the doctrine of remembrance, the sacred vision of justice — stands downstream of this single image.

It is worth being exact about what interiorization means here, because it is the load-bearing idea of the whole introduction. To interiorize the Qur'an is not to memorize its words, and to interiorize the Sunna is not to catalog the Prophet's habits. A person can hold the entire text in memory and remain, spiritually, outside it — treating it as an object consulted from a distance. Interiorization, in the book's sense, is the opposite movement: the revealed substance passes from being something one has to being something one is, so that it now works from within as the very grammar of one's perception and action. That is why the sayings keep reaching for language of identity rather than possession — "as my own soul," "you are from me and I am from you," "with the Qur'an." They are trying to name a case in which the distance between the knower and the known has closed. And it is precisely that closing which qualifies a man to be a gate. A gate does not contain the city; it is simply the place where inside and outside meet, the point at which what is within becomes accessible to what is without. Ali (ع) can transmit the inner spirit of revelation into the later sciences only because that spirit is not, for him, external cargo to be handed on, but his own interior life made available to others.

Before any of that can be taken up, though, the outlines of the life itself have to be drawn, for the tradition never separated the teaching from the man who lived it. The nearness came first; the wisdom is its fruit.

## The follower in the shadow

Ali (ع) was a follower of the Prophet in the deepest sense of that word: he lived physically in the Prophet's shadow and absorbed spiritually everything that radiated from him. He captures the intimacy himself in one of his sermons, recalling how, when he was still a small child, the Prophet took him under his wing — "I would follow him as a baby camel follows its mother." The image is worth pausing on, because a baby camel does not follow at a respectful distance or out of calculated obedience; it follows because separation is unthinkable, orienting its whole small body to the mother's movement. That is the register in which the Imam describes his own formation. The Prophet, he says, held up for him each day some sign of his noble character and commanded him to follow it; took him along into his yearly seclusion at the mountain of Hira', where no one else saw them, at a time when no single household in all of Islam was gathered together save that of the Messenger, Khadija, and Ali (ع) as the third among them. "I saw the light of the revelation and the message," he remembered, "and I smelt the fragrance of prophecy." The nearness was not incidental to his later stature; on the book's account it *is* his stature, in seed.

The historical outline of that early life is soon told, and the book deliberately restricts itself to it — from Ali (ع)'s birth around 599 of the common era to the Prophet's death in the year 11 of the Muslim calendar. The later periods, the years leading to his own caliphate and the brief, tragic caliphate itself, are set aside, not because they lack importance but because the controversies they raise would only deflect attention from the book's real aim: to introduce a spiritual ethos whose relevance transcends the political disputes on which the outward Sunni–Shi'i divergence rests. What the book wants to highlight instead is the unifying force of his teaching, whose immense importance Muslims of every school affirm. So the biography here is not a full life but a portrait of the years of nearness.

Even those years carry markers of the singular. According to all Shi'i authorities and several Sunni sources, Ali (ع) held the unique distinction of being born inside the Ka'ba in Mecca — the holiest shrine of the tradition becoming, quite literally, his birthplace. His mother was Fatima bint Asad; his father, Abu Talib, was a leading member of the Hashimite clan who had raised the orphaned Muhammad and would become his chief protector once Islam was preached openly. During a season of drought some years before the mission began, the Prophet eased his uncle's burden by taking the boy Ali (ع), then about five, into his own household — and from that point on the two were rarely apart. So it is not surprising that Ali (ع) is regarded as the first male to embrace Islam, though he was only nine or ten at the time; Abu Bakr was the first adult man, and Khadija, the Prophet's wife, the first woman. The immediacy of the boy's response is not a minor biographical detail but part of the argument. When revelation later commanded the Prophet to "warn thy nearest kin" (chapter 26, verse 214), he gathered the leading men of his clan to a feast and asked which of them would help him, and be his brother, his executor, and his successor among them. All fell silent — the seasoned elders weighing the risk — except the youth, who spoke up: "O Prophet of God, I will be thy helper in this." The Prophet placed his hand on Ali (ع)'s neck and said, "This is my brother, mine executor and my successor amongst you. Hearken unto him and obey him." The clan's elders laughed at the time; the years took the declaration more seriously than they had, as the boy's character, nobility, and intelligence grew ever more dazzlingly evident.

The same readiness reappears at the hinge of Islamic history. On the night of the migration, the hijra, from Mecca to Medina in 622 — the event that begins the Muslim calendar — the Prophet's enemies had plotted to kill him in his bed, and Ali (ع) risked his own life by lying down in that bed in his place, so that the Prophet's departure could go unseen. It is a small scene with an enormous logic: the follower puts his body exactly where the danger is aimed, so that the one he follows can slip free. In Medina the Prophet took Ali (ع) as his brother in the pact of brotherhood he forged between the emigrants who had made the hijra and the helpers, the Medinan tribes who had embraced the faith — singling him out, again, from all the others being paired. And through the Medinan years Ali (ع) distinguished himself three ways at once: as a warrior, as a scribe of the unfolding revelation, and as a leading companion. His courage against the overwhelmingly superior forces of the Meccan Quraysh and their allies became legendary. He stood at the front of nearly every major engagement, was frequently the standard-bearer, and was never once defeated in the single-combat duels with which battles then began — indeed, never defeated in any battle he fought. At Uhud a heavenly voice was said to proclaim, "There is no chivalric knight but Ali, no sword but Dhu'l-Faqar" — the sword the Prophet had given him. And at Khaybar in the year 7, when the Muslims could make no headway against an apparently impregnable fortress, the Prophet declared he would give the banner to one "who loves God and His Messenger and is loved by God and His Messenger," through whom victory would be granted — and sent for Ali (ع), who was absent with an affliction of the eyes. The Prophet healed the inflammation with his own saliva and sent him forward; in the thick of the fighting, having lost his shield, Ali (ع) is said to have torn a gate from its hinges and used it as a shield — a gate that eight men could barely lift afterward. The tradition lingers over such feats, but it never lets them stand alone; they are read as the outward face of an inward fidelity, the same fidelity that lay in the Prophet's bed and answered first at the feast.

That reading — valor as the visible edge of something invisible — is the key to how the book uses the whole biography, and it is worth making explicit. The first response at the feast, when every seasoned elder held his tongue and a boy of ten spoke; the willingness to become the target in the Prophet's bed; the years of following "as a baby camel follows its mother" — these are not a series of unrelated virtues but a single disposition seen from different angles. In each case Ali (ع) puts himself, without reserve and without calculation of his own advantage, at the exact point where the Prophet's need is greatest. That is why the book can treat a child's quickness to believe as spiritually significant rather than merely charming. A child has not yet learned the adult art of hedging; the immediacy of the young boy's assent, and the unflinching loyalty that followed it, show a soul with no gap between recognition and commitment. The battles simply carry the same disposition into the arena where it costs the most. Read this way, the standard-bearer who is never defeated and the deputy who weeps to be left behind at Tabuk are the same man: one whose entire orientation is fixed on the Prophet, so completely that his courage and his obedience are two names for one thing. The book will argue, in the episodes to come, that this same undivided orientation is exactly what the Imam later calls the mark of the true intellect — the soul so integrated around its center that thought, action, and being no longer pull apart.

## The house and the pool

His valor, though, is only one thread, and by the book's own reckoning far from the most significant. What matters more is his closeness in every respect to the Prophet and the sanctity that flowed from it. Ali (ع) was given the immense honor of marrying the Prophet's daughter Fatima — reckoned, alongside her mother Khadija, the most saintly woman in Islam, and named in the tradition Majma' al-Nurayn, "Confluence of the Two Lights": the light of prophecy (nubuwwa) and the light of sanctity (walaya). The phrase is precise. Two currents that might be imagined apart — the prophetic and the saintly — are said to meet and mingle in this marriage, so that after the Prophet himself, Ali (ع) becomes head of the Ahl al-Bayt, the People of the House, the Prophet's family. And that family is addressed directly in the verse of purification — "God only wisheth to remove from you all impurity, O People of the House, and to purify you with a complete purification" (chapter 33, verse 33) — which, according to several of the most authoritative early sources, the Prophet applied specifically to Fatima, Ali (ع), and their two sons Hasan and Husayn, gathering these four beneath his cloak and reciting these very words over them. The occasion had teeth: a debate with a Christian delegation from Najran had escalated to a proposed mutual imprecation, a solemn calling-down of God's judgment on whichever party was lying; the Prophet brought forward exactly these four for the challenge, and the Christians, seeing whom he staked, declined it.

The Qur'an raises the standing of the House again where the Prophet is told to say, "I ask you for no reward, save love of the near of kin" (chapter 42, verse 23) — a phrase that several major commentators identify with the Ahl al-Bayt, so that love of the Prophet's family becomes something the revelation explicitly asks for, without cancelling its broader sense of love for all close kin. The reverence goes further still. Blessings are invoked upon the Prophet's progeny as part of the very blessings invoked upon him, so that when the command came, "O ye who believe, bless him and greet him with peace" (chapter 33, verse 56), and the believers asked how, the Prophet taught them to say, "O God, bless Muhammad and the progeny of Muhammad, as Thou hast blessed Abraham and the progeny of Abraham." The family is woven into the daily prayer itself. Even Imam al-Shafi'i, founder of one of the four Sunni schools of law, put it in verse: to love the family of the Prophet's household is an obligation revealed in the Qur'an, and one who withholds the blessing upon them has, in effect, no prayer at all. To all this the tradition adds the saying of the two weighty things, the thaqalayn: "Truly, I am leaving behind amongst you the two weighty things: the Book of God and my Ahl al-Bayt. They will not be parted from each other until they return to me at the pool." The Book and the House are bound together and entrusted together — the same inseparability the Prophet had already voiced between the Qur'an and Ali (ع) himself.

Beyond his place in the House, the Prophet spoke of Ali (ع) in his own right, and the tradition has always preserved these sayings as a kind of prism through which it views him — meditating on them, drawing from them diverse interpretations and endless inspiration. Their reach is what makes them weighty: they come down through the most authoritative Sunni traditionists as much as through the Shi'i collections, which is why Ahmad ibn Hanbal, founder of another of the four Sunni schools, could say plainly that no companion of the Prophet had ever had such distinctions and merits ascribed to him. The list runs deep, and a listener should hear it as a single accumulating weight rather than a set of isolated proofs. Ali (ع) is "the wali, the patron and spiritual master, of every believer after me." "Gazing upon Ali is an act of worship." God is asked to "make the truth revolve around Ali wherever he turns." Whoever obeys him obeys the Prophet, and whoever disobeys him disobeys the Prophet. He will "clarify for my community that over which they will differ after me," and he will fight for the inner interpretation, the ta'wil, of the Qur'an as the Prophet fought for its outward revelation, its tanzil — a saying that will become the seed of the next episode. When the Prophet left for the expedition to Tabuk and set Ali (ع) over Medina as his deputy, and Ali (ع) grieved at being left behind from the campaign, the Prophet told him, "Are you not content to stand in relation to me as Aaron stood in relation to Moses — except that there is no prophet after me?" — placing him, that is, in the rank of the prophet's closest partner and successor, short only of prophecy itself. And to his wife A'isha, asked to summon the leader of the Arabs, the Prophet answered, "I am the leader of the children of Adam, and Ali is the leader of the Arabs."

The same accumulating weight carries through a further cluster of sayings that the tradition treasures. "Whoever desires to live my life and to die my death," the Prophet said, "and to take his rest in the eternal Garden my Lord has promised me, let him orient himself towards Ali ibn Abi Talib, for truly he will never lead you away from right guidance, nor lead you into error." Of the inner test of allegiance he said that none but a believer would love Ali (ع) and none but a hypocrite would hate him, and that whoever separates himself from Ali (ع) separates himself from the Prophet, as whoever separates himself from the Prophet separates himself from God. Commenting on the verse "thou art a warner, and for every people there is a guide" (chapter 13, verse 7), he said, "I am the warner; you are the guide, O Ali. After me, the rightly-guided shall be guided by you." He even set beside Ali (ع) a warning drawn from the fate of an earlier prophet: "O Ali, there is in you something akin to Jesus, on whom be peace — the Jews hated him so intensely that they slandered his mother, and the Christians loved him so intensely that they ascribed to him a rank he did not possess," a caution against the excess of both hostility and adoration. And when the Prophet once prayed that God would bring "the most beloved of Thy creatures" to share a meal with him, the tradition records that it was Ali (ع) who came. Heard together, these sayings are less a set of separate proofs than a single portrait, drawn by the Prophet's own hand, of a man bound to him at the level of guidance itself.

Above all there is the word spoken at the pool. When Ali (ع), bowing in prayer, gave his ring to a beggar who had asked for alms, the verse came down — "Verily your wali is only God and His Messenger and those who believe, establish the prayer and give alms while bowing in prayer" (chapter 5, verse 55) — and the Prophet added what are perhaps the most famous words ever spoken about him: "For whomever I am the mawla, the guardian and master and close friend, Ali is his mawla." He said it on several occasions, but the most celebrated came after his final pilgrimage in the year 10, at a pool between Mecca and Medina called Ghadir Khumm, where he assembled the thousands of returning pilgrims, had a pulpit raised, and closed his address with that declaration. Here the interpretations part, and the book names the parting honestly. For Shi'is it is an unambiguous designation of Ali (ع) as successor to the Prophet; for Sunnis it marks his special closeness without amounting to a nomination in the political sense. That the word carries the highest spiritual significance, though, is not seriously disputed on either side — even Umar is reported to have gone to Ali (ع) and congratulated him, "You have become my mawla and the mawla of every Muslim." The long polemic turns only on the political implications of his spiritual authority, his walaya, and the book steps deliberately around that quarrel. What it insists on instead is that devotion to Ali (ع) was never the property of one faction. Already in the Prophet's own lifetime a group was known as his shi'a, his partisans — Salman al-Farisi, Abu Dharr al-Ghifari, Ammar ibn Yasir, and Miqdad ibn Amr among the four remembered as its prototypes — yet fidelity to him runs across the whole of the faith, and is especially deep wherever Sufism flourishes, where he is honored as the first spiritual pole (qutb) after the Prophet and stands at the summit of nearly every chain (silsila) through which the orders trace their lineage back to the Prophet. To speak of Ali (ع), the book therefore insists, is to speak not of exclusivist sectarianism but of a universal spirituality.

The word walaya deserves one further look, because it holds together two things that a listener might otherwise keep apart. On one side it names an attitude in the believer: fidelity, loyalty, love — the orientation of the heart toward Ali (ع) and, through him, toward the Prophet and toward God. On the other side it names a reality in Ali (ع) himself: spiritual authority, sanctity, the standing of one who is genuinely a master of souls. These two are the complementary faces of a single word, and the book is careful to keep them joined. Devotion that fastened on Ali (ع) for factional or sentimental reasons, cut loose from any real sanctity in its object, would be mere partisanship; and sanctity that called forth no answering loyalty would leave the believer untouched. Walaya is precisely the circuit that runs between them — a real authority evoking a real love, and a real love opening onto a real authority. Held this way, it also explains why the book can locate the shi'a of Ali (ع) already in the Prophet's lifetime, among men like Salman and Abu Dharr, and yet refuse to make walaya the possession of the later sect that took its name from the word. The early love for Ali (ع) is not to be simply identified with the theological doctrines elaborated in subsequent generations; and neither the love nor the spiritual orientation it expresses is confined to any one branch of the community. Wherever the inner dimension of the faith is taken seriously — and nowhere more visibly than in the Sufi orders that place him at the head of their chains — this orientation toward the walaya Ali (ع) embodies is found. That is the sense in which the gate opens onto something universal rather than sectarian.

## What this episode lands

The life sketched here is not offered as history for its own sake. It is the indispensable background against which one image has to be understood: the gate. Born in the most sacred shrine of Islam, raised inside the Prophet's own household, first among men to answer the call, willing to lie in the Prophet's bed under threat of murder, unbeaten in battle, married into the confluence of the two lights, set at the head of the purified House, and named in saying after saying as the one whose relation to the Prophet was that of a second self — this is the figure the tradition means when it calls him the gate through which the city of knowledge is entered. Everything gathered so far is the outward frame of that one claim. Notice, too, how consistent the sources are with one another across an otherwise divided tradition: the birth in the Ka'ba, the merits Ahmad ibn Hanbal conceded, the verse of purification, the blessing woven into the daily prayer, the word at Ghadir — these come to us through Sunni and Shi'i channels alike, and the book leans on that convergence deliberately. It is building its portrait from what the community holds in common, not from what divides it, precisely because the ethos it wants to introduce is one all Muslims recognize even where they disagree about its consequences.

But a frame is not yet the picture. We have heard, without unfolding it, the saying that Ali (ع) fights for the inner meaning of the Qur'an as the Prophet fought for its letter, and that saying opens directly onto the real subject of the book: not who Ali (ع) was, but what kind of knowing this gate actually transmits — a knowing the tradition insists is something other, and deeper, than the ordinary work of the reasoning mind. The next episode steps through the gate and takes up exactly that question, asking what the Imam meant by the intellect, why he preferred that word to "reason," and how, in his hands, the intellect turns out to be less a faculty of the head than a spiritual faculty of the whole person — the inner counterpart, within each of us, of the very revelation that comes from above.
