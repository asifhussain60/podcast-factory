This episode traces the ruler's inner war on both fronts it is fought: outward, as pride's war against God, and inward, as pride's war against conscience. We follow Ali (ع)'s stern warning to Malik al-Ashtar against pride — the vice that usurps a greatness belonging to God alone, that corrupts the heart before it corrupts religion, and that carries within itself the seed of its own humiliation — from the cloak of divine majesty, through the disgrace of Iblis and the hidden idolatry of vanity, to the slow ruin of the arrogant soul. Then we follow the same war to its source, in the conscience every tyrant must silence before he can wrong anyone else, in the self-reform and self-lowering that alone reverse it, and in a compassion that is not softness but the very shape justice takes when a ruler has finally remembered his own poverty before God.

## Where this episode picks up

How can an inner attitude — a mere feeling of superiority, a way of carrying oneself in front of others — be described, without exaggeration, as war against God? It sounds like a category mistake: war is waged against an enemy one can see, with armies and weapons, and God is neither an equal nor a rival to be fought. Yet this is exactly the language the Imam reaches for when he turns, in his letter to Malik al-Ashtar, from the duties a ruler owes his people to the state of the ruler's own soul. "Do not set your soul up for war with God," he tells Malik — and the phrase is arresting precisely because it names something invisible, something interior, and treats it as an act of open hostility toward the Absolute.

Having watched, in the letter's earlier counsels, how justice is not merely a policy a ruler adopts but a reflection of God's own nature — how putting each thing in its proper place is a participation in the way reality itself is ordered — we are now led to look at the reverse. If justice aligns the soul with the real, there must be a disposition that sets the soul against the real, and the Imam locates it with precision. It is not, first of all, the gross violations he catalogued at the letter's outset — the many breaches of religious obligation that are the obvious meaning of "fighting" against God. Those are the visible surface. The allusion here reaches deeper, into subtler modes of opposition to divine reality: attitudes that can accompany even outward acts of obedience and yet quietly contradict their very spirit.

Chief among them stands the cardinal vice against which the Imam warns again and again: pride. Not pride as a passing mood, but pride as a settled psychic state — the condition in which a person feels himself superior and behaves highhandedly toward those around him, its signs so legible in his conduct that they cannot be concealed. For a person invested with power, this is not a small moral blemish among others. It is a misappropriation of something that belongs to God alone, an arrogation of a greatness no creature may claim — and for that reason a covert declaration of war against the one from whom all greatness derives.

But the war does not stay fixed on that plane, the ruler's relation to a distant, transcendent God. Traced far enough, it turns out to be fought in a much smaller, much closer place: the ruler's own conscience, the inner witness that must be silenced before pride can do any damage in the world at all. This episode follows the war in both directions it runs — outward, into its first and most extravagant form in the disgrace of Iblis and the slow ruin of the ordinary tyrant; and inward, into the conscience it must conquer, the self-reform that alone reverses it, and the compassion that is its final undoing.

## The cloak of pride

The word the Imam reaches for is takabbur, sometimes rendered kibr: the state in which a person feels superior to others and carries himself highhandedly among them. It is worth pausing on how visible this vice is. Pride is not a secret of the heart that leaves no trace; its marks show in a man's bearing, his tone, the way he receives those beneath him and the way he answers those who question him. The proud man advertises himself whether he means to or not. But the Imam is not finally interested in pride as a social unpleasantness, an unattractive manner. He is interested in what pride is, beneath its symptoms — and what it is, when the one who is proud holds power, is theft. A ruler's pride is a misappropriation of a divine attribute, an arrogation to himself of a greatness that belongs exclusively to God.

To see why this is theft and not merely arrogance, listen to the divine word the tradition places at the center of this teaching: "Pride is My cloak and greatness is My robe; whoever contends with Me over either of them, I shall cast him into the Fire." The image is exact and worth dwelling on. A cloak and a robe are what a king wears to signify his majesty; they are not shared, not lent out, not worn by a subject in the king's own hall. Here the cloak of pride and the robe of greatness are said to be God's own regalia — the outward signs of a sovereignty that is His and His alone. To take up pride, then, is not simply to think too well of oneself. It is to reach for a garment that belongs to God and to drape it over one's own shoulders, and the warning attached to that act is not a mild rebuke but the Fire. The person who contends with God over pride is described as a rival contending for a throne.

This is why the tradition draws so sharp a line around the word itself. God alone has the right to takabbur, and God alone may be called, without any offence, al-Mutakabbir — the Proud, the one whose greatness is real because it is underived and absolute. When the name is spoken of God it is pure praise; there is nothing in Him that greatness could distort, because His greatness is simply the truth of what He is. But the very same disposition, transferred to a creature, becomes the worst of vices, because a creature has no greatness of its own to be proud of. Everything he has, he has received; every faculty by which he might exalt himself was given to him. For such a being to vaunt himself is to lay claim to a self-sufficiency he does not possess, to behave as though the borrowed were his own. The name that is perfection in God is corruption in man, and the difference is the whole difference between the Creator and the created.

It is worth staying with that difference a moment longer, because the whole warning depends on it. The proud man's error is not that greatness is a bad thing to possess; it is that he ascribes to himself a greatness he does not, and cannot, possess. Consider what a ruler actually holds. His power was entrusted to him and can be withdrawn; his strength was lent and will fail him; his very existence is held in being, from one instant to the next, by the one who fashioned him. Nothing in this inventory is his own. To be proud of any of it is to mistake a loan for a possession, a gift for an achievement — and to forget the giver in the act of admiring the gift. This is why pride, at its root, is a lie about reality before it is a fault of character.

From here the logic of "war" becomes clear. To vaunt oneself before others is tantamount to inwardly defying God's authority; it amounts to an implicit denial of His unique and incomparable majesty. Think of how a subject relates to a king. A loyal subject acknowledges, in his very bearing, that the greatness in the room is not his; he defers, he lowers himself, he keeps his proper place. But a subject who openly exalts himself, who behaves as though the majesty were his to display, is by that very posture refusing the king's supremacy — and a subject who refuses his king's supremacy is, in effect, in rebellion, waging war against him. Pride does exactly this to God. Enmity toward transcendent reality is the invisible substance that pervades all pride, and it is present in every act of arrogance or oppression a ruler commits against those beneath him.

And here is the paradox that only a sense of spiritual values can illuminate, for it turns the ordinary picture of pride inside out. We tend to think of arrogance as something aimed downward — the strong man's contempt for the weak, the ruler's disdain for the ruled, a sin committed against those "below." But an attitude of superiority toward those below is, in fact, an act of rebellion against that which is above. The injustice a proud ruler does to the people beneath him is not merely a horizontal wrong, a crime against fellow creatures that will one day draw down upon him the justice of the Creator. It is that, but it is also more: in and of itself it manifests outright opposition to the Creator. What looks like a horizontal vice — belonging to this world, played out between a man and his neighbors — is in reality a spiritual disorder, a fracture in the soul's relation to the Absolute.

This is why the Imam can treat the outward immorality of corruption as a mere symptom. The oppression, the highhandedness, the abuse of the weak — these are the visible eruptions of an inner spiritual malaise that lies deeper than any of them: an implicit denial of the absoluteness, and hence the uniqueness, of the source of all power and authority. The corrupt ruler has forgotten something, and his corruption is the shape his forgetting takes. What he has forgotten is the truth voiced in the Qur'anic question and answer: "To whom belongeth the dominion this day? To God, the One, the All-Conquering" (chapter 40, verse 16). Dominion belongs to God — not tomorrow only, not at the reckoning only, but this day. And the tradition insists that "this day" is to be heard as every day, not merely the Day of Judgement. The proud man's whole error is contained in that single lapse of memory. He behaves as though the sovereignty of this day were his to wield, when the sovereignty of this day, and of every day, belongs to God alone.

## The slow ruin of the proud

If pride is the misappropriation of a divine attribute, the tradition has a name for the being who committed that theft first and most completely, whose refusal became the archetype of every arrogance since. The Imam devotes to him one of his most searching sermons, known as al-Qasi'a — "the Abasement," so named because its whole purpose is to humiliate the proud, to bring the arrogant soul low by showing it the fate of the first arrogant one. That figure is Iblis, Satan, described as the first of those who competed with God in grandeur, and thus the very embodiment of the principle of pride. Every later act of arrogance is, in a sense, a repetition of his; he is the pattern, and the sermon holds him up so the listener may recognize the same disease at work, in miniature, in himself.

The scene of his fall turns entirely on a refusal to bow. "When your Lord said to the angels: Truly I am going to create man from clay; and when I have fashioned him and breathed into him of My spirit, then fall down before him prostrate. So the angels prostrated themselves, all of them together; save Iblis: he was proud and was one of the disbelievers" (chapter 38, verses 71 to 74). The whole company of angels obeys as one, and a single exception breaks the harmony. The command was to prostrate before the newly fashioned man, into whom God had breathed of His own spirit — the prostration a recognition of what God had placed in Adam. Iblis would not, because, the tradition says, a vehement passion overcame him: he deemed himself superior to Adam on a ground that seemed to him self-evident. He had been created from fire, Adam merely from clay; fire, he reasoned, is nobler than clay, therefore he was nobler than the one he was told to honor, therefore he would not bow.

This is the exact anatomy of pride, laid bare in a single refusal. Iblis took the fact of a difference — fire against clay — and converted it into a claim of superiority, and that claim into a defiance of God's own command. Taking pride in a presumed excellence, and rebelling against God on the strength of it, he became not only the first enemy of God but, as the Imam names him, the leader of the bigots and the forerunner of the proud. Every proud soul that has come after walks in a procession he leads — which is why the Imam turns to his listeners with an urgent, practical warning: beware lest Iblis infect you with his disease. The disease spreads by insinuation, by whispering, by machination; it does not announce itself as pride but slips in disguised as self-regard, as wounded dignity, as the reasonable sense that one deserves better. The Imam's counsel against it is vividly physical: be resolute in placing self-abasement over your heads, in casting self-glorification beneath your feet, in removing pride from your necks, and take up humility as the fortified watchtower between you and your enemy.

That image tells us what humility actually is in this contest. It is not a mood of self-deprecation but a defensive position — a watchtower, a fortress — the one ground on which the assault of Iblis cannot land. For the whole aim of the enemy is, inversely, to inculcate pride, since pride is not merely the worst of vices but the poison that infects and destroys every other virtue. A person may possess real virtues — courage, generosity, learning, devotion — and pride will convert each of them into a vice the moment it attaches itself, because a virtue one is proud of has already been turned into an occasion for self-exaltation, the very thing the virtue was meant to overcome. Hence the startling measure the tradition offers: "The sin that grieves you is better, in the sight of God, than the virtue that makes you proud." A sin that leaves you humbled has kept you in the right relation to God; a virtue that puffs you up has quietly reversed it — which is also why excessive praise is so dangerous, among Satan's most reliable opportunities to efface the virtue of one who is genuinely virtuous.

These teachings amount to a commentary on the disgrace of Iblis, and they open onto a danger the Prophet named with unforgettable precision: "The creeping of shirk in my community is more hidden than the creeping of a black ant over a hard rock on a dark night." A black ant is already hard to see; on a dark rock, harder; on a moonless night, all but invisible — and the movement of shirk within the soul is more hidden still. The superficial notion of shirk is the worship of material idols, shirk al-jali, the open and manifest form the tradition condemns without difficulty. But that is not what the Prophet is warning his own community about, for they had already renounced idols. He means shirk al-khafi, the hidden form: false gods concealed not in temples but within one's own intentions and attitudes — the whole apparatus of hidden pride, vanity, ostentation, and pretension.

The Imam makes the connection unmistakable: "Know that the slightest pretension is polytheism." Pretension here is riya — acting so as to be seen, performing one's virtue for the eyes of others — and it counts as polytheism because to act for the sake of earning others' praise is to act for others rather than for God, setting up an audience whose approval one really seeks in the place that belongs to God alone. The deed may look devout, but its true object is the admiring gaze of one's fellows, and that gaze has become a rival god. The consequence is stated with terrible symmetry: "Whoso acts for other than God, God entrusts him to those for whose sake he acted." The man who worked for human praise is handed over to the humans he worked for, left in the end with exactly what he sought and nothing more. What makes this form of idolatry so treacherous is precisely its hiddenness — the worshipper of a stone idol at least knows what he is doing, while the one who has slipped into riya may perform every outward act with scrupulous care and never suspect that the true recipient of his worship is his own reputation.

Read in this light, the disobedience of Iblis discloses its full meaning: the outward moral consequence of an inner spiritual malaise, and the malaise was precisely the pride that amounts to hidden polytheism — the exalting of the self into a god beside God. This is why the Imam presses the point the sermon of Abasement is named for. Iblis is disgraced because he disobeyed God, and he disobeyed God on account of pride; therefore pride is, in and of itself, a disgrace — a fall out of grace that carries within it the seed of its own inevitable inversion. "Do you not see how God humiliated him through his pride?" the Imam asks. The proud one imagines he is exalting himself; in reality he is bringing himself low, for the very act of self-exaltation is what cast him down. Being proud is already a form of humiliation, because self-aggrandizement is nothing other than self-destruction dressed up as glory.

Return now from the archetype to the ordinary case: the ruler on the slope toward oppression, and the small sentence he tells himself as he begins to slide. The Imam catches the very train of thought that sets a man in motion toward tyranny: "Do not say, 'I have been given authority, I order and am obeyed,' for this leads to corruption in the heart and the erosion of religion." It is a small, almost reasonable-sounding thought — I hold the office, I give the command, the command is carried out — but the Imam treats it as the first step onto the incline, because in it the ruler has begun to locate the source of his authority in himself, in the bare fact that his orders are obeyed, rather than in the God from whom all authority is on loan. Hold onto that sentence; it will return, read from a deeper angle, once we follow the war inward.

Notice, too, the order in which the Imam says the damage arrives: this self-glorification produces first an inner, spiritual corruption — the corruption of the heart — and only then the erosion of religion. The sequence is the whole diagnosis. The rot begins on the inside, in the ruler's relation to God, and works outward to the practice of his faith, not the other way around; when power goes to the head, faith departs from the heart, and for faith to be manifested truly in the world it must flow from a heart suffused with humility. This too is the principle running through the whole letter — putting each thing in its proper place — for a heart in its proper place is a heart that knows it is not the source of its own greatness.

What, then, is the remedy the moment vanity begins to rise? The Imam prescribes a deliberate act of attention: "If the authority of your position engenders vanity and arrogance, then look at the grandeur of God's dominion above you — this will calm your ambition, restrain your vehemence, and restore to you what had strayed from your intellect." What strays is precisely the part of the intellect conscious of God's absolute reality — and of the relative, derivative, ultimately illusory character of everything else, the ruler's own power included. Arrogance, in other words, is not merely a failure of humility; it is a failure of intelligence, a darkening of the very faculty by which the soul perceives what is truly real.

The tradition even preserves this in the shape of the words. The term the Imam uses for arrogance, makhila, springs from the same root as the word for imagination, khayal: arrogance belongs to the realm of the imaginary, the fanciful, the unreal — what happens when the imagination fastens upon something not truly there and swells with it. So arrogant pride is at bottom an intense form of self-delusion rooted in an intellectual defect: not merely an overestimation of oneself but, more fundamentally, an underestimation of the Absolute. The arrogant man overrates himself only because he has first underrated God; the swelling of the self is the exact measure of how small the Real has grown in his sight.

This is why the sage alone is free of it, having genuinely assimilated the truths the tyrant has forgotten. "Everything is dying away except His countenance" (chapter 28, verse 88). "Everything that is thereon is perishing, and there subsisteth but the Face of thy Lord, possessor of Glory and Bounty" (chapter 55, verses 26 to 27). "The Truth hath come and falsehood hath passed away; indeed, falsehood is ever passing away" (chapter 17, verse 81). To take these words into oneself is to see the whole created order, and one's own self within it, as passing and contingent — and to see that only one Face endures. From such a vision, pride cannot be drawn, because there is nothing in a perishing self to be proud of; only the humility that dissolves all individualism and egocentricity can be drawn from it, and that humility is the antidote to the very roots of pride, vanity, and arrogance — which are, in their turn, the lifeblood of every tyranny.

With this the Imam's most direct warning falls into place: "Beware of comparing yourself with God in greatness and likening yourself to Him in might, for God abases every tyrant and disgraces every braggart." The tyrant sets himself up as a rival in majesty, and the outcome is fixed by the nature of things: a creature who reaches for the incomparable can only be broken by the reach. There is a further consequence. The one who fails to act justly toward God and toward God's creatures finds that not only the creatures but God Himself becomes his adversary — and the one whom God opposes has his every argument struck down. Such a man remains at war with God until he desists and repents: the war we began with is now revealed in its full duration, not a single act but a standing condition, ended only when the tyrant lays down his pride, even if that surrender is compelled only at the very edge of death and judgement.

Yet the deepest teaching here concerns how the war is waged before repentance or judgement overtakes the tyrant — how his punishment is already underway while he imagines himself untouched. The tradition names this istidraj, the drawing out of punishment by degrees: a punishment that does not fall all at once but is administered gradually, stage upon stage, often without the one being punished perceiving it. The Qur'an sets it in stark contrast: "Of those We have created are people who guide with truth and dispense justice therewith. But those who reject Our signs, We will lead them on by degrees to punishment from whence they know not" (chapter 7, verses 181 to 182).

Here the teaching reaches its most unsettling point, the one that overturns every worldly measure of how things stand. The punishment meted out by degrees can take the form of apparent worldly success. "Let not their wealth nor their children please thee. God desireth only to punish them thereby in the world, that their souls shall pass away while they are disbelievers" (chapter 9, verse 85). The wealth that grows, the family that flourishes, the power that consolidates — these need not be signs of favor at all. They may be the very instrument of a punishment drawn out by stages, a prosperity that hardens the soul in its rejection until it passes away still turned from God. What looks like the tyrant's triumph may be the slow closing of a trap. This is why the tradition can say, without contradiction, that a tyrant or oppressor may be a Muslim in the formal sense — bearing the name, performing the outward acts — and yet be simultaneously guilty of a mode of disbelief. The word for it is kafir, taken in its strict, etymological sense: not merely one who denies a creed, but one who covers over the truth, in his own being and in his actions, burying under his arrogance the reality he was made to acknowledge.

## The voice of conscience

Follow the war with God as far as it goes, and it leads somewhere smaller and closer than the throne room or the field of oppression. Before a ruler can set his soul against God and wrong the people beneath him, something must first be overruled within — a voice that knows better, that recognizes the injustice for what it is even as the will proceeds to commit it. The tradition calls this the conscience, the inner witness pride most needs to quiet, because as long as it speaks, the soul cannot rest easy in its arrogance. The tyrant does not usually begin by disbelieving that he does wrong; he begins by silencing the part of himself that says so. In Urdu it is called the damir; in English, conscience — the inner monitor that recognizes good and evil. The tyrant's first act of violence is against it: he does not so much argue with his conscience as learn to no longer hear it, until the voice that once objected falls silent from disuse.

Reza Shah-Kazemi's argument here is that we have badly misunderstood what that voice actually is. Secular ethics treats conscience as the bedrock of individual morality, the private sense of right and wrong each person carries and answers to. That is true as far as it goes, but it stops far too short. For the Imam, conscience is not something the individual generates on his own; it is infused into the soul from a source infinitely above the individual — the divine breath of discernment that lets a creature tell right from wrong at all. To override one's conscience, then, is not merely to betray oneself. It is to declare war on the trace of God written into the very structure of the human soul — which is to say that the war we have been tracing outward, in the cloak of pride and the disgrace of Iblis, has an inward face as well, fought in this quieter court.

That reframing changes everything about how we read the letter's warnings. The Imam is not simply advising a governor to keep a clear head; he is describing a spiritual physics in which the corruption of a ruler begins as an interior event — a soul that has stopped obeying its own better knowing and started obeying its appetites instead. Seen this way, tyranny stops being the exclusive vice of kings. The seed of the tyrant is planted in any soul that learns to ignore the voice it was given.

Begin with that voice, and with the claim that it comes from somewhere far higher than the individual who hears it. When the Imam warns his governor against the corruption of the soul, he is drawing on a Qur'anic picture of what the soul is and how it is furnished. The soul is not a blank slate onto which a society writes its rules; it arrives already inscribed. Woven into its very making is a capacity to recognize the difference between wickedness and righteousness — a gift breathed into it, not the soul's own achievement. The Qur'an puts it with startling economy: "And the soul and that which perfected it, inspiring it with consciousness of its wickedness and its righteousness" (chapter 91, verses 7 to 8).

Read that slowly, because everything turns on it. The soul is not merely made; it is perfected, brought to its proper completion — and the perfecting includes an inspiration, a divine infusion, by which the soul comes to know its own wickedness and its own righteousness, placed within at the moment of the soul's completion, as part of what makes it a soul at all. This is why Shah-Kazemi insists that the divine quality of justice is translated, at the level of the responsible human being, into the voice of inner conscience. Justice above becomes conscience below.

And there is a deeper layer still. The soul does not only carry a sense of right and wrong; it carries a memory of God's lordship that predates the soul's arrival in the world. The Qur'an evokes a moment at the very dawn of things, often called the primordial covenant: "And when thy Lord brought forth from the children of Adam, from their loins, their seed, and made them testify against their souls, saying, 'Am I not your Lord?' They said, 'Yes, verily, we testify.' This was lest ye say on the Day of Resurrection, 'Truly, of this we were unaware'" (chapter 7, verse 172). At the moment He created them, He made them witness His glory and acknowledge it, so that on the Day of Judgement no one could plead ignorance.

Notice what this does to our idea of conscience. The voice within is not an arbitrary feeling; it is the echo of a promise the soul once made. Spiritual consciousness — the innate awareness of divine lordship and of one's own creaturely dependence — is the source from which moral conscience springs. To know right from wrong is, at bottom, to remember Whose one is. So the source of morality turns out to transcend the human being even while forming part of the very definition of what a human being is.

Seen this way, the war against one's conscience is graver than self-betrayal. It is a war against the very reality that defines the soul as a soul: for the soul contains, beneath the moral conscience, an innate consciousness of divine lordship and of its own answering servitude, and it is out of that spiritual consciousness that the moral conscience grows. To silence the conscience is to attack the root as well as the branch — to deny not only that this act is wrong but that there is any Lord to whom one owes an accounting for it. And this is the point at which tyranny stops being the special vice of rulers and comes uncomfortably close to every one of us. If the tyrant is, first and foremost, one who violates his own soul, then the difference between the despot on his throne and the ordinary person who habitually overrides his own better knowing is a difference of scale and opportunity, not of kind. That is a sobering thought, but it is also the pastoral heart of the teaching: the letter is not only a manual for governors. It is a mirror held up to anyone who has ever quieted the voice within in order to get what he wanted.

Recall now the sentence we heard the ordinary ruler telling himself as he began his slide into oppression — "I order and am obeyed" — and the corruption it was said to work first upon the heart and only afterward upon religion. That reading told us the sequence of the damage. What it did not tell us is where, inside the soul, that sentence is first spoken. Before he oppresses anyone else, the tyrant lets his own soul be dominated by what the tradition calls the soul which commands evil — al-nafs al-ammara, the appetitive self that issues orders and demands to be obeyed. The Imam's sentence, read microcosmically, is exactly what this evil element within the soul says: the nafs al-ammara literally orders, and every other faculty of the soul — intelligence, imagination, will, feeling, the senses — falls into line and obeys. The tyranny outside is a projection of a tyranny already established within. The man who says "I order and am obeyed" to a nation has first said it to his own conscience, and won.

What, then, is the answer to a soul like that? The tradition's answer is the combat against one's own soul, mujahadat al-nafs — met already, in an earlier episode, as the greater struggle the Prophet named on returning from battle, the war against the lower self that outweighs any war against an external enemy. It is enough to place it here as the interior discipline that keeps the commanding soul from seizing the throne.

What is fresh is the consequence the Imam draws from it: that self-reform is not a private spiritual luxury but the very root of public justice. Placing each thing in its proper place, which is what justice means, has to begin with placing oneself rightly before presuming to order anyone else. The Imam states it without hedging: "If your aspiration is to reform the people, begin with yourself, for your pursuit of the reform of others while your own soul is corrupt is the greatest of faults." A ruler who would straighten a crooked society while his own soul is bent has, in the Imam's eyes, committed the gravest error of all, because he has inverted the order of repair.

And the larger the power entrusted to a man, the more urgent this becomes. In his letter appointing Muhammad b. Abi Bakr — Malik's own predecessor as governor of Egypt — the Imam ties the gift of authority directly to the duty of self-restraint: "you are obliged to oppose your soul and protect your religion." The greater the power at one's disposal, the greater the damage should the soul yield to its desires and gratify them through that power, for then it is not only the man who is corrupted but the very religion he was charged to uphold — the principle of proper placement again, applied to the ruler himself: the outward form is only as sound as the inward disposition that carries it.

The alternative is the soul that fancies itself self-sufficient, and the Qur'an draws it with lapidary precision: "Truly man is rebellious, in that he deemeth himself independent" (chapter 96, verses 6 to 7). The moment a soul imagines it can stand on its own, severed from its total dependence on God, it sets itself up as a god in its own right — the true portrait of the tyrant, one who, as the Qur'an says, "taketh his own desire as his god" (chapter 45, verse 23). Knowledge of one's own nothingness before God, and of one's unconditional need of Him, is the one thing that loosens the grip of the commanding soul. Only the soul that already commands itself to evil will command others to it; only the soul that is lowly before God will be humble, merciful, and just toward people.

That last thought — that only the lowly soul rules justly — is the bridge into the letter's next counsel, which at first sounds merely practical. "Let the most beloved of affairs to you be those most centred upon the right, the most comprehensive in justice, and the most inclusive of popular approval, for the disapproval of the common folk undermines the approval of the elite." On the face of it this is shrewd advice about governance: keep the ordinary people with you, because a court that pleases only the powerful sits on nothing. But Shah-Kazemi reads it as a spiritual imperative to recognize man's actual lowliness before God — a summons to the ruler to identify himself with the poor rather than the rich.

The principle beneath it is a Qur'anic verse that levels every human being at a single stroke: "O mankind, ye are the poor before God, and God, He is the Rich, the absolutely Praiseworthy" (chapter 35, verse 15). Before God, everyone is destitute. The wealth a ruler commands is not his own richness but borrowed against an absolute poverty he shares with the beggar at his gate. A leader who has taken that verse to heart will instinctively side with the poor — not because it is politically canny, but because he knows himself to be one of them in the only accounting that finally matters. Their visible poverty is a plain image of the invisible poverty in which every soul, ruler included, stands before God. To side with the poor is to align oneself with reality as it actually is; to side with the rich, and prize their approval, is to be seduced by the illusion of self-sufficiency the wealthy are most tempted to believe about themselves.

The Imam's own austerity was proverbial — the rough clothing, the coarse food, the refusal of comfort others in his position took for granted — and there is a wonderful exchange that shows it was never a shunning of the world for its own sake. A man named Asim b. Ziyad had thrown himself into a severe asceticism, and his family came to the Imam complaining that he had cut himself off from ordinary life. The Imam told Asim to think of his family and not to deny himself the good things God has permitted. Asim, quick on his feet, turned the argument back: "and yet here you are, in your rough clothes and your coarse food!"

The Imam's reply draws the crucial distinction. "Woe to you — I am not like you. God has made it incumbent on true leaders — a'immat al-haqq, the leaders of the truth — that they proportion themselves to the weakest among the people, so that the poverty of the poor will not engender covetousness." The ascetic denies himself the permitted good things and calls it piety, a private indulgence of a different kind. But the leader's austerity is not for himself: it is a duty laid on those who lead in the name of the truth, to lower themselves, in material terms, to the level of the weakest they govern — so that no poor man looking at his ruler need feel the sting of a comfort he cannot share. Same austerity, opposite spirit: one turned inward toward the self, the other turned outward toward the people. To stand among the a'immat al-haqq, a man must manifest his attachment to al-Haqq, the Real, the ultimate spiritual reality in whose light material wealth does not register as wealth at all — a heart fixed on the Truth itself, to which gold is not worth turning the head.

The most powerful and the most powerless may both be materially poor — the ruler by choice, the beggar by circumstance — but that shared poverty is not merely a leveling downward. It opens the same door for both of them onto the treasures of the spiritual domain, in proportion to how deeply each has realized the truth of that verse. The self-lowering of the ruler is thus not a loss but an entry: by refusing the false richness of the world he claims the real richness the world cannot give, and holds it on exactly the same terms as the weakest of his subjects. This is not political theatre — a ruler who dresses plainly as a calculated performance while his heart remains fixed on power would be miming the opposite of what the Imam describes. The plain living is real because the detachment behind it is real, and only genuine detachment opens that door; it does not open to a pose.

And on the plane of ordinary social psychology, the effect is precisely the one the Imam named. A society will always have its inequalities; what determines whether those inequalities breed resentment is whether the people at the top hold themselves apart or hold themselves alongside. The Imam's insistence that rulers live like the poor — an insistence he embodied in his own person before he ever wrote it in a letter — is the most effective guard against envy a society can have. Living like the poor, in the end, is not only a discipline of the ruler's soul; it is the quiet mortar that keeps a community from cracking along the fault line of wealth.

## The compassion of justice

Now watch how this self-lowering opens outward into something larger. Identifying with the poor does not only humble the ruler; it deepens his awareness of his own perpetual need of God, teaching him to live in reliance on divine mercy, in hope and trust. And the natural fruit of a life lived in reliance on mercy is the giving of mercy to others. A man who knows how much he depends on being forgiven becomes, almost inevitably, a man who forgives.

This is where Shah-Kazemi corrects a stubborn misunderstanding. We tend to think of compassion and justice as pulling in opposite directions — justice as the stern one, compassion as the soft one, forever having to be balanced against each other. But the Imam's vision refuses that opposition. Compassion in no way conflicts with justice; it is an intrinsic dimension of justice, once justice is conceived ontologically — in accordance with the fundamental nature of reality itself. To act with compassion is to put things in their right place; it is also to act in accord with the intrinsic character of the Real. Mercy is not a concession that softens justice. Mercy is what justice looks like when it is faithful to the nature of the God whose justice it is.

The proof is written across the Qur'an itself. At the head of every chapter but one, the name Allah is followed immediately by the words "the infinitely Compassionate, the ever-Merciful" — al-Rahman, al-Rahim. Mercy is the first thing said about God at the opening of nearly every revelation. The capacity for compassion, then, is an innate affinity with the very nature of divine reality — a reality in which, as the Qur'an says, "My mercy encompasseth all things" (chapter 7, verse 156); a reality in which "thy Lord has prescribed for Himself mercy" (chapter 6, verse 54); a reality whose deepest law is stated in the divine saying, "My mercy takes precedence over My wrath." Wrath is real, but it is not final; mercy is the older and the higher word.

The Imam's own sayings press the same truth into daily conduct. "The dispensing of mercy brings down divine mercy," he says, as if every act of compassion below draws a corresponding compassion from above. "As you grant mercy, so will you be granted mercy." And with genuine astonishment: "I am astounded by the person who hopes for mercy from one above him, while he is not merciful to those beneath him." The man who begs God's forgiveness while withholding his own from those in his power has not understood the terms of the exchange he is asking to enter.

There is a kind of spiritual arithmetic in these sayings. Mercy is not simply commended as a virtue; it is described as a channel — to show mercy is to open a conduit through which mercy flows back, and to withhold it is to seal that conduit shut with one's own hand. A person who reflects the predominance of mercy in his conduct comes to stand more fully within the merciful nature of things; to be merciful is to become more real, more aligned with the deepest grain of existence, and to be harsh is, correspondingly, to shrink oneself toward the thin and passing region of things subject to wrath.

But — and this is essential — mercy so understood does not dissolve rigour. The rigorous side of things is not denied; it is subordinated to the higher principle of mercy, not abolished by it. There remains a place for the firm application of penalty where it is genuinely unavoidable. In the same letter to Malik he orders corporal punishment for any officer found misappropriating public funds, and his correspondence bristles with warnings of swift and severe retribution to governors who would enrich themselves at the people's expense. To Ziyad b. Abihi, a deputy governor in Basra, he swears a plain oath: "if news reaches me that you have misappropriated the revenue of the Muslims, whether small or large, I shall inflict a severe punishment upon you." The mercy is real; so is the iron.

And the iron came down hardest, tellingly, on those closest to him. In one sermon the Imam recalls how his own blind brother, Aqil b. Abi Talib, once asked for more from the public treasury than was his due — thinking, the Imam says, that he would sell his religion for the sake of family feeling. The Imam's answer was to bring a piece of red-hot iron close to his brother's body, telling him that by making such a request he was urging Ali (ع) himself into a far more terrible fire. When his cousin Abd Allah b. al-Abbas was implicated in misusing funds, the Imam met him with a thundering remonstrance, ordering him to fear God and return the people's property, warning that if he did not, the sword would answer for it. Then came the line that leaves no room for favouritism at all: "even if Hasan and Husayn had done the like of what you have done, they would not have been granted any leniency by me." That he names the Prophet's own grandsons is the whole point. No closeness of blood, no exaltation of status, buys the smallest relaxation of justice.

This is exactly what the Qur'an commands, and the Imam is simply living it out: "O ye who believe, be staunch in justice, witnesses of God, even though it be against yourselves, your parents or your near of kin" (chapter 4, verse 135). Justice that bends for one's own people is not justice; it is nepotism wearing justice's clothes.

And yet the final word is not severity but a breathtaking statement of human unity. The Imam writes to Malik: "Infuse your heart with mercy for the subjects, love for them and kindness towards them. Be not like a ravenous beast of prey above them, seeking to devour them. For they are of two types: either your brother in religion or your like in creation." The people you govern are either your brothers in faith or your fellows in the sheer fact of being created — no third category, no group of persons who fall outside the reach of your mercy. He closes the passage with a reminder of why: the governor is above the people, but the one who appointed him is above the governor, and God is above them all — testing the ruler through the very subjects he is tempted to devour.

There is one last incident that shows the whole teaching in a single scene. The Imam once came upon an old, blind beggar and asked about him, and was told the man was a Christian. "You have employed him until he is old and infirm," the Imam said to those around him, "and now you refuse to help him. Give him maintenance from the public funds" — the bayt al-mal, the treasury of the whole community. In one sentence he founds a principle of social welfare — in Arabic, maslaha — rooted in redistributive justice and a strict refusal to discriminate between Muslims and others. The beggar's religion was not the question; his humanity was. He was, in the Imam's own phrase, "your like in creation," and that was claim enough on the community's care.

## What this episode lands

The listener now holds a single, unbroken thread. Pride is war with God, and not by metaphor: it usurps a divine attribute — the cloak of greatness that belongs to God alone — and in doing so denies, in the very posture of the soul, the sole and incomparable reality of God. In the disgrace of Iblis, pride is shown to be its own humiliation, a hidden idolatry in which the self, or the praise of others, is set up as a god beside God. And in the ruler on the slope toward oppression, pride corrupts the heart first and religion afterward, darkens the very intelligence that perceives the Real, and works a slow ruin that can wear the mask of worldly success.

But traced to its root, that same war turns out to be fought closer to home than the throne room. The tyrant's war against God is finally a war against his own conscience — against the divine discernment breathed into his soul and the covenant his soul once made when it testified that God was its Lord. That is the seed of every outward oppression: a soul that has learned to override the voice it was given, the very soul the Imam catches in the sentence "I order and am obeyed," first spoken to one's own conscience and only then to a nation. And so justice cannot start with reforming other people; it starts with reforming oneself, and with the deliberate self-lowering that puts the ruler shoulder to shoulder with the weakest of those he governs, remembering that before God every one of us is poor.

From that lowered ground, compassion rises — not as a soft feeling set against the demands of justice, but as justice being faithful to the merciful nature of the Real, the God whose name is joined to mercy at the head of nearly every chapter of His book. That is a compassion with iron in it: impartial even toward the Prophet's own grandsons, unbending toward a governor's misused coin, yet open-handed to a blind Christian beggar grown old with no one to help him. Pride and conscience, self-reform and mercy — they are not scattered counsels but one continuous movement of the soul, first the disease and then its cure, the war with God and the peace that ends it only when a ruler remembers Whose he is.

## Closing

For the Letter to Malik does not end in the ruler's soul. Having established that justice begins within — first as war against pride, then as reform of conscience, then as mercy — the letter goes on to lay out an anatomy of the whole society the ruler holds in trust: the ranks and callings that make a community work, each with its proper place and its proper due. The army that defends it, the judges who weigh its disputes, the scribes who keep its records, the tillers who feed it, the merchants who supply it — and, standing at the sharpest edge of justice's practical demand, the destitute who have no one to speak for them but the ruler himself. It is one thing to teach that mercy is the nature of the Real; it is another to build a government in which that mercy reaches the man at the very bottom.

The inner disciplines traced in this episode — the refusal of pride, the listening to conscience, the reform of the self, the deliberate self-lowering, the mercy that answers mercy — were never meant to stay locked inside the ruler's heart. They were meant to become a structure, a working arrangement of offices and duties through which a whole people is held in justice. That is where the letter goes next: from the inner discipline of the just ruler to the ordered classes of a just society — the soldiers, the judges, the scribes, the farmers, the traders — and, at the sharpest edge of the whole design, to the standing claim of the poor, the one measure that finally tells us whether any of the rest of it was ever real.
