# Ali: A Biographical Portrait

*This episode introduces the figure at the heart of the whole book: Ali ibn Abi Talib (peace be upon him), cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him), and the man the tradition remembers as the gate to prophetic wisdom. It follows his early life lived in the Prophet's shadow — from a birth said to have taken place inside the Ka'ba, through his boyhood in the Prophet's household, to his place at the head of the Prophet's family. Along the way it gathers the sayings by which the Prophet described him, the verses in which the tradition heard him named, and the events on which every school of Islam has meditated for centuries. The claim it sets out to establish is deceptively simple. To understand the spirituality of Islam at its source, one begins with this single figure — a figure claimed by every school of thought yet reducible to none of them.*

## Where this episode opens

Why would a religion whose deepest treasure is knowledge hand the key to that treasure to a boy who first entered history as a warrior — and why would the schools that spent centuries disagreeing about almost everything agree, without exception, about him? The Prophet Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him) once said, "I am the city of knowledge and Ali is its gate; so whoever desires knowledge, let him enter the gate." That single image sets the terms of everything that follows. Knowledge, in this saying, is not a shelf of books but a city — an inhabited whole, with streets and doorways and an interior — and the way into it runs through one person. To speak of Ali ibn Abi Talib (peace be upon him), cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet, is therefore to speak of nothing less than the quintessential spirituality of the Islamic tradition itself.

The strangeness of the claim is worth sitting with. Here is a figure whom the Shi'a revere as the first of their Imams and whom the Sunni tradition honours as the fourth of the rightly guided caliphs; whom the Sufi orders place at the summit of nearly every chain through which they trace their descent; whom jurists, theologians, grammarians, and even the practitioners of the most arcane sciences name as the originating impulse of their disciplines. He is claimed by everyone. And yet the deepest thing said about him belongs to none of these later divisions. It is older than the schools and larger than their quarrels.

This episode does not attempt the whole of that life. The years of political conflict, the caliphate, the civil wars, the assassination — all of that is set aside, not because it is unimportant but because it draws the eye toward disputes that obscure the essential message. What this episode holds up instead is the early period: the boy in the Prophet's house, the young man on the battlefield, the head of a sacred family. This is the ground on which the title "gate to prophetic wisdom" must be understood. The aim is to introduce a spiritual ethos whose relevance is universal — within Islam, and beyond it.

## The gate to prophetic wisdom

To call Ali the gate to prophetic wisdom is to make a specific claim about what he carried and how he carried it. In this seminal figure of nascent Islam one finds an integral expression of the two fundamental sources of Islamic spirituality: the Qur'anic revelation and the inspired Sunna of the Prophet. Everything depends, however, on what is meant by that second word. By Sunna the tradition here does not mean the mere outward imitation of the Prophet's actions — a reductionism all too common in the present day — but rather the spiritual substance of prophetic perfection to which the Qur'an itself points when it says of the Prophet, "Verily, thou art of a tremendous nature" (chapter 68, verse 4). The Prophet's words and deeds express that substance; they do not exhaust it. To assimilate the substance inwardly, rather than to copy its outward forms, is the goal of every spiritually inclined Muslim.

There is a profound affinity, in this understanding, between the soul of the believer and the prophetic nature — a mysterious proximity that transcends time and space. The Qur'an gives it voice: "The Prophet is closer to the believers than they are to themselves, and his wives are their mothers; and by the ordinance of God, blood relatives are more entitled to one another than other believers and emigrants, unless you would show kindness to your close associates — this is decreed in the Record" (chapter 33, verse 6). The Prophet outwardly manifests a perfection that the spiritually sensitive believer intuits, to one degree or another, as the very essence of his or her own soul. The prophetic nature is not, on this reading, something alien and distant that one admires from below; it is the truth of one's own interior, seen in its fullness in another.

It is against this background that a cluster of prophetic sayings about Ali takes on its weight. The Prophet said that Ali "is as my own soul" — a phrase that affirms not merely an extraordinarily close bond between the two men but something deeper: the affinity, and ultimately the identity, between every sanctified soul and the prophetic nature. More mysterious still, the Prophet said to him, "You are from me, and I am from you." These are not the words of ordinary kinship. They describe a shared substance, a single reality expressed in two persons. And when the tradition turns from the Prophet's soul to the Qur'an itself, it preserves a saying that binds Ali to the revealed text with the same intimacy: "Ali is with the Qur'an and the Qur'an is with Ali. They will not separate from each other until they return to me at the pool." The spirit of the Qur'an and the soul of the Prophet were thus, in this vision, fully interiorized by a single man — and it is precisely this interiorization of the twin sources of the Islamic revelation that constitutes the spiritual path of Islam.

The most direct way to investigate that interiorization is to engage with the corpus of teachings attributed to him — and here the sheer scale of the thing becomes an argument in itself. Even granting that not every one of the thousands of sayings bearing his name can be confidently ascribed to him, the magnitude of the corpus testifies that he must indeed have articulated a very large number of profound teachings. No other companion of the Prophet possesses anything approaching this body of work. When a name gathers that much wisdom around it across the centuries, the accumulation is not an accident of transmission; it points back to a source that could sustain it.

There is a saying that draws this bond with the Qur'an tighter still, and it names the exact register in which he carried the text. Speaking of a coming trial, the Prophet said, "There is one amongst you who will fight for the ta'wil of the Qur'an as I have fought for its tanzil." Tanzil is the revelation as it came down, the literal descent of the text; ta'wil is its inward, spiritual interpretation, the return of the words to their deeper sense. Abu Bakr asked whether he was the one meant, and the Prophet said no; 'Umar asked, and again the Prophet said no — the one intended, he said, was the man mending a sandal nearby, for the Prophet had handed Ali his sandal to repair. To fight for the ta'wil is to labour for the inner meaning of the revelation as the Prophet had laboured for its outward coming. That is the exact task of a gate to prophetic wisdom: to guard and open the interior of a text whose surface has already been given.

The same pattern repeats across the intellectual disciplines. Even if his foundational role in the development of the Islamic sciences remains debatable in its details — sciences such as jurisprudence, theology, Qur'anic exegesis, rhetoric, grammar, and calligraphy, the mystical knowledge associated with Sufism, and such arcane arts as numerology and alchemy — the striking fact is that later authorities in all these fields regard him as the original impulse behind their disciplines. Grammarians trace the ordering of Arabic grammar to a directive of his. Exegetes look to him for the inner sense of the verses. The theologians, the jurists, the masters of eloquence — each lineage turns back toward the same figure and finds him standing at its head. This does not require that he composed a treatise in every field. It reflects the far-reaching and penetrating influence of both his formal teachings and his personal radiance — the way a single luminous presence can become the acknowledged origin of things it did not itself write down.

Given how closely the Islamic intellectual sciences as a whole came to be identified with him, the Prophet's description of Ali as the gate to prophetic wisdom reads on two levels at once. It describes what he was in relation to the Prophet during their shared lifetime — the one who stood at the doorway of the Prophet's own knowledge, the nearest to its interior. And it reads as a prophecy of the role he would play in the later unfolding of the tradition's sciences, when discipline after discipline would trace its lineage back through him. All such sciences are understood as so many formal, outward expressions of the essential, inward spirit of the Islamic revelation — a spirit that he synthetically assimilated and faithfully transmitted. The gate, in other words, opens in both directions: inward, toward the city of the Prophet's wisdom, and outward, toward the centuries of knowledge that would flow from that source.

What holds this whole conception together is the relationship between the outward form and the inward substance. A science is a form; the spirit it articulates is the substance. An imitation of the Prophet's conduct is a form; the prophetic perfection it gestures toward is the substance. The distinctive thing about the figure at the centre of this book is that he is remembered, above all, as one who never stopped at the form. He is the one who went through the gate to what lay within — and who, having gone through, could open it for others. That is why the tradition, in all its branches, keeps returning to him. It is not looking merely for a hero or a jurist or a saint. It is looking for the interior of its own revelation, and it has learned to look for it through him.

## A life in the Prophet's shadow

Ali was a follower of the Prophet in the deepest sense of that word. He lived physically in the Prophet's shadow and absorbed spiritually everything that radiated from him. The intimacy of that relationship is preserved in the Imam's own words, in one of his sermons: "When I was but a child he took me under his wing. I would follow him as a baby camel follows its mother. Every day he would hold up for me a sign of his noble character and command me to follow it. Each year he would go into seclusion at the mountain of Hira'. I saw him there, and no one else did. At that time no single household was gathered together in the religion of Islam, save that of the Messenger of God, Khadija, and myself as the third among them. I saw the light of the revelation and the message, and I smelt the fragrance of prophecy." It is a portrait of a formation so early and so complete that the boy could describe himself as present at the very dawn of the revelation, when the household of faith numbered three.

The outlines of that early life can be sketched. According to all Shi'i authorities and several Sunni sources, Ali had the unique distinction of being born inside the Ka'ba in Mecca, around the year 599 of the common era. His mother was Fatima bt. Asad; his father, Abu Talib, son of 'Abd al-Muttalib, was a leading member of the Hashimite clan. Abu Talib had raised the young orphan Muhammad — the son of his brother — and would later become his chief protector when Islam was openly preached in Mecca. Some years before the mission began, during a time of drought, the Prophet eased the burden on his uncle's household by taking the young Ali, then about five years old, into his own home. From that point on the boy was like a son to him, and the two were rarely apart. The detail matters: before there was any revelation to receive, there was already a household, and this child was inside it.

Ali is regarded as the first male to embrace Islam, though he was only nine or ten years old at the time. Abu Bakr was the first adult male to enter the new religion, and Khadija, the Prophet's wife, was the first woman. The immediacy of the boy's response to the prophetic message, and his unflinching loyalty ever after, are of great significance — a child recognizing at once what many grown men would resist for years. About three years later, when the Prophet was instructed by revelation to "warn thy nearest kin" (chapter 26, verse 214), he invited the leading members of his clan to a feast and then addressed them: "O sons of 'Abd al-Muttalib, I know of no Arab who hath come to his people with a nobler message than mine. I bring you the best of this world and the next. God hath commanded me to call you unto Him. Which of you, then, will help me in this, and be my brother, mine executor and my successor amongst you?"

All remained silent — except the youthful Ali, who spoke up: "O Prophet of God, I will be thy helper in this." The Prophet then placed his hand on Ali's neck and said, "This is my brother, mine executor and my successor amongst you. Hearken unto him and obey him." The clan's leaders greeted this with mockery at the time; a boy had answered where the elders sat mute, and the elders laughed. But the declaration was taken far more seriously as the years passed and the extraordinary character, nobility, and intelligence of the Prophet's young cousin became ever more dazzlingly evident. What looked like a child's impulsiveness at a feast came, in retrospect, to look like the first public naming of a destiny.

Also of great importance in this early period is the role Ali played in the migration from Mecca to Medina in 622 — the hijra, the event that marks the beginning of the Muslim calendar. The Prophet's enemies had plotted to assassinate him. Knowing this, Ali risked his own life by pretending to be the Prophet, sleeping in his bed on the night of his departure so that the watchers outside would believe their target still lay within while the Prophet slipped away. Once in Medina, the Prophet adopted Ali as his brother in the pact of brotherhood he established between the emigrants — the Muslims who had accompanied him on the hijra from Mecca — and the helpers, the Medinan tribes who had embraced the new faith. When the whole community was being knit together, pair by pair, into a single body, the Prophet took Ali for himself. The bed and the brotherhood belong together: in the first, Ali offered his life to shield the Prophet's; in the second, the Prophet drew him nearest of all. What the boy at the feast had promised — to be the Prophet's helper — the young man now made good with his body and his place.

During the Medinan period Ali distinguished himself on the battlefield, as a scribe of the unfolding Qur'anic revelation, and as a leading companion of the Prophet. His courage and heroism in the battles against the overwhelmingly superior forces of the Meccan Quraysh and their allies were second to none. He stood at the forefront of nearly all the major engagements fought under the Prophet's banner and was frequently appointed standard-bearer. He was never defeated in any single-combat duel of the kind with which battles customarily began — indeed, he was never defeated in any battle he fought at all. His indomitable courage and his incomparable skill as a warrior became legendary. It is said that during the battle of Uhud a heavenly voice was heard proclaiming, "There is no chivalric knight but Ali, no sword but Dhu'l-Faqar" — the latter being the name of the sword the Prophet had given him. The proclamation is worth hearing carefully: it does not merely praise a fighter, it sets him apart from every other, as the sword was set apart from every other blade.

The most celebrated of all the battles that established his reputation as the pre-eminent — indeed invincible — warrior of Islam was Khaybar, in the year 629. The Muslims had been unable to make any headway against the heavily fortified, apparently impregnable defences of their opponents. The Prophet then declared that he would give the army's banner to one who "loves God and His Messenger and is loved by God and His Messenger," through whom victory would be granted. He sent for Ali, who was absent because of an affliction of the eyes. The Prophet healed him by placing saliva upon his eyes, and Ali went on to lead the Muslims to victory. In the thick of the fighting, having lost his own shield, he is said to have wrenched a gate from its hinges and used it as a shield — a gate which, after the battle, eight men could barely lift. The image is almost too fitting to pass without notice: the man remembered as the gate to a city of knowledge lifting an iron gate as though it were a plank, at the battle that sealed his name.

And yet his role as a warrior is only one of many factors that account for his unique place in the tradition. Far more significant is his closeness, in every respect, to the Prophet, and the wisdom and sanctity that flowed from that proximity. Ali was accorded the immense honour of marrying the Prophet's daughter Fatima — considered, alongside her mother Khadija, the Prophet's first wife, to be the most saintly woman in Islam. One of the epithets the tradition bestows on Fatima is Confluence of the Two Lights: the two lights of nubuwwa, prophecy, and walaya, sanctity. In her the light of her father's prophethood and the light of her husband's sanctity are said to meet. And so, after the Prophet himself, Ali became the head of what would come to be known as the Ahl al-Bayt, the People of the House — the Prophet's family. The battlefield, for all its legend, was not the ground of his stature. The house was.

## The People of the House

The People of the House are addressed in one of the most consequential verses of the Qur'an, which declares them purified of all defilement: "God only wisheth to remove from you all impurity, O People of the House, and to purify you with a complete purification" (chapter 33, verse 33). According to several of the most authoritative early sources, the Prophet called for Fatima, Ali, and their two sons Hasan and Husayn, and declared, "This is my Ahl al-Bayt." On one well-attested occasion he gathered these members of his family beneath his cloak and recited the words of that verse over them. The episode has a precise setting. A Christian delegation from the city of Najran had come to debate religion with the Prophet, and a revelation was received calling for a mutual imprecation — a solemn challenge in which each side would invoke God's curse upon the liars among them. This is the event the tradition calls the mubahila. The Prophet brought forward those same four persons for it; the Christians, seeing whom he brought, declined the challenge. Whatever else the moment meant, it identified beyond dispute who counted as his house: not a wide circle of followers, but this small family gathered under one cloak.

The standing of that family is deepened by a further verse, in which the Prophet is instructed, "Say: I ask you for no reward, save love of the near of kin" (chapter 42, verse 23). Several of the most significant Qur'anic commentators identify the near of kin here as the Prophet's People of the House. Love of the Prophet's family is thus, on their reading, explicitly demanded by the revelation itself — and demanded not as sentiment but as the one recompense the Prophet asks for delivering the message. This does not cancel the verse's broader sense, which embraces love for all those bound to one by close kinship; but at its heart, the commentators say, stands this house. The reverence owed to the family is indicated in yet another way by the blessing Muslims are taught to invoke. The Qur'an instructs the believers, "Truly, God and His angels bless the Prophet. O ye who believe, bless him and greet him with peace" (chapter 33, verse 56). When this verse was revealed and the Prophet was asked how the blessing should be performed, he replied that it was to be invoked upon him and upon his progeny together: "O God, bless Muhammad and the progeny of Muhammad, as Thou hast blessed Abraham and the progeny of Abraham. Truly, Thou art the Praised, the Glorious." The family is folded into the very prayer said upon the Prophet, in the canonical worship, five times a day.

The obligation binds far beyond any single school. Imam al-Shafi'i, founder of one of the four schools of Sunni jurisprudence, gave it memorable expression in verse. Loving the household of the Prophet of God, he wrote, is an obligation laid down by God in the Qur'an He revealed; and it is enough to show the grandeur of their dignity that whoever sends no blessing upon them has, in truth, no prayer at all. That such a line should come from a founder of a Sunni school of law is precisely the point the whole chapter is building toward: devotion to the People of the House is not the property of one faction. It runs through the tradition as a whole.

The spiritual significance of the family is underscored by a saying the tradition preserves with particular care. "Truly, I am leaving behind amongst you the two weighty things: the Book of God and my Ahl al-Bayt. They will not be parted from each other until they return to me at the pool." Two weighty things, left together, never to be separated — the revealed Book and the Prophet's family, bound in a single trust. The echo is deliberate: it is the same image, the same inseparability, that the tradition used of Ali and the Qur'an. What was said of the man is here said of the house he heads.

Alongside these sayings about the family stand the sayings the Prophet spoke of Ali specifically — and here the tradition prefers to let them speak with minimal commentary, as they appear in the standard collections, because their force is in their plainness. It is worth noting where they are drawn from. Though they fill any Shi'i collection, they are gathered here from two of the most authoritative Sunni traditionists, so that no one may suppose them the property of a single branch of Islam; and it was a leading Sunni traditionist and founder of one of the four Sunni schools of law who observed, "No companion of the Prophet has had such distinctions ascribed to him as those which have been ascribed to Ali ibn Abi Talib." The distinctions themselves — the fada'il, the merits and virtues — are best heard directly. "Truly, Ali is from me and I am from him, and he is the wali of every believer after me." "Three things were revealed to me regarding Ali: he is the leader of the Muslims, the guide of the pious, and chief of the radiantly devout." "Gazing upon Ali is an act of worship." "May God have mercy on Ali. O God, make the truth revolve around Ali wherever he turns." "O Ali, you are a leader in this world and the Hereafter. Your beloved is my beloved, and my beloved is the beloved of God; your enemy is my enemy, and my enemy is the enemy of God." "Whoever desires to live my life and to die my death and to take his rest in the eternal Garden my Lord has promised me, let him orient himself towards Ali ibn Abi Talib, for truly he will never lead you away from right guidance, nor lead you into error."

The sayings knit obedience to Ali into obedience to the Prophet, and knowledge of the community's future into his keeping. "Whoever obeys Ali obeys me, and whoever disobeys him disobeys me." "You will clarify for my community that over which they will differ after me" — a charge that looks past the Prophet's lifetime and entrusts to Ali the resolving of the disputes to come. To one who reveres prophetic guidance, the cumulative weight of these words is hard to set aside: to take the Prophet seriously is, on their own terms, to take Ali seriously. It is largely for this reason that countless Muslims across the centuries have meditated on them, drawing from them diverse interpretations and incalculable inspiration. In a certain sense the sayings form the prism through which the tradition views him, and they serve to deepen one's spiritual receptivity to his teachings.

The sayings reach toward the other prophets to place him. "O Ali, there is in you something akin to Jesus: the Jews hated him so intensely that they slandered his mother, and the Christians loved him so intensely that they ascribed to him a rank he did not possess" — a warning, in the same breath, against both the hatred and the excess he would provoke. And when the Prophet departed on the expedition to Tabuk and left Ali behind as his deputy in Medina, and Ali was saddened not to be going with him, the Prophet said, "Are you not content to stand in relation to me as Aaron stood in relation to Moses — except that there is no prophet after me?" Aaron to Moses: brother, deputy, the one left in charge of the people — everything but a prophet in his own right, since prophecy had reached its seal.

The single most celebrated of all these traditions concerns a verse and an act of quiet charity. Of the words, "Verily your wali is only God and His Messenger and those who believe, establish the prayer and give alms while bowing in prayer" (chapter 5, verse 55), the commentators relate that the occasion was this: Ali, while bowing in prayer, extended his ring to a beggar who had asked for alms, giving even as he worshipped. On being told of it, the Prophet recited the verse and then added what are perhaps the most quoted words ever spoken about him: "For whomever I am the mawla, Ali is his mawla." The word mawla carries a spread of meanings at once — guardian, master, protector, close friend — and the saying places Ali in that relation to the believers as the Prophet himself stands in it.

He spoke these words on more than one occasion, but the most famous by far came after his final pilgrimage to Mecca, in the year 632, at a pool midway between Mecca and Medina known as Ghadir Khumm. There the Prophet assembled the pilgrims, had a pulpit erected, and addressed the thousands gathered around him. The address culminated in the declaration: "For whomever I am the mawla, Ali is his mawla." For the Shi'a this is an unambiguous designation of Ali as the Prophet's successor. For the Sunni tradition it marks his special closeness to the Prophet without amounting to a nomination in the political domain. That the words are of the highest spiritual significance, however, is not seriously disputed by either — the polemic has always turned on their political implication, on the walaya and what it entails for authority, never on whether the bond they name is real. One of the companions is reported to have approached Ali afterward with an expression of congratulation: "You have become my mawla and the mawla of every Muslim."

It is not the purpose of this book to enter the Shi'i–Sunni dispute, but one historical fact deserves note. Ali's charisma was such that even during the Prophet's own lifetime a group came to be known as the shi'a, the partisans, of Ali. The first great commentator on the Qur'an reports that the Prophet interpreted the verse "Those who have faith and do righteous deeds, they are the best of created beings" (chapter 98, verse 7) as referring to Ali and his shi'a. Four individuals in particular were renowned for their devotion to him in those years and are remembered as the prototypes of that early loyalty: Salman al-Farisi, Abu Dharr al-Ghifari, 'Ammar b. Yasir, and Miqdad b. 'Amr. This early love for Ali should not be confused with the theological doctrines elaborated by later generations; but it is worth emphasizing that at the core of every later expression of Shi'ism lies the same thing that was already present here — fidelity to Ali. That fidelity, that love, is one aspect of walaya; the complementary aspect is the spiritual authority and sanctity of Ali himself.

And here the widest point must be made plainly, because it is where the whole portrait is meant to land. Neither devotion to Ali nor the orientation toward the walaya he embodies is the exclusive province of Shi'ism. It runs across the whole of the Islamic faith. Devotion to Ali as head of the Prophet's People of the House is found throughout the Muslim world, and it is especially deep wherever Sufism flourishes — for Ali is regarded as the first spiritual pole, the first qutb, of Sufism after the Prophet himself, and he stands at the summit of nearly all the spiritual chains, the salasil, through which the Sufi orders trace their lineage back to the Prophet. Follow almost any of those chains upward, link by link, and it arrives at the same doorway. To speak of Ali, then, is not to speak of exclusivist sectarianism. It is to speak of universal spirituality — of the one figure through whom the whole tradition, in all its branches, reaches back toward the interior of its revelation.

## What this episode lands

The portrait assembled here makes a single, cumulative claim. Ali is the gate through which the Qur'an and the Sunna — the two fundamental sources of Islamic spirituality — were fully interiorized, taken inward as living substance rather than copied as outward form. Everything gathered in this episode serves that claim: the boy raised inside the household of revelation, the man the Prophet called as his own soul and bound to the Qur'an, the head of the purified family beneath the cloak, the figure at the summit of the Sufi chains and at the core of Shi'i fidelity alike. He is claimed by all the schools because he stands, in a sense, before them — at the source they all draw from.

But to say that the revelation was interiorized through him raises the next question, and it is the question the following episode takes up. If Ali interiorized the twin sources, by what faculty did he do it? What is the human power that receives a revealed truth not as information to be repeated but as reality to be known from within? The tradition has a name for that faculty, and the whole spiritual ethos of the Imam turns on how he understood it. The next episode leaves the biography behind and enters the interior — turning to the faculty of knowing itself, the intellect, and to the distinctive vision of it that the Imam's teachings unfold.

## Closing

Two images frame this life, and they answer each other. The Prophet called Ali the gate to the city of his knowledge; and at Khaybar, having lost his shield, Ali lifted a fortress gate that eight men could barely raise and carried it into battle as though it weighed nothing. The gate of knowledge and the gate of iron belong to the same man, and the tradition has never quite let them come apart. To enter the city of prophetic wisdom, it says, one goes through him — and to see how, one must look not at the battles but at what he understood, and how. That is where the next episode begins: at the threshold he holds open, turning inward, toward the intellect by which the whole of it was known.
