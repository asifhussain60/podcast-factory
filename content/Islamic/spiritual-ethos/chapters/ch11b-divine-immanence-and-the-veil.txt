# Divine Immanence and the Veil

*This episode follows the cleared heart out into the world it now beholds. Reza Shah-Kazemi shows how, for Ali (ع), the heart purified by remembrance sees the divine in all things — every existent a theophany, an existential reflection of a divine Name — and yet without the least reduction of God to the things in which He is seen. It then unfolds the Imam's most vertiginous paradox: that creation, which seems to be the veil hiding God, is in truth the self-disclosure that reveals Him, so that, as the Imam says, the veils do not veil Him at all. And it holds together the two truths the paradox rests on — divine incomparability and divine similarity, transcendence and immanence — as a single vision that the reasoning mind can only take for contradiction and the spirit of the intellect apprehends as fruitful truth.*

## Where this episode picks up

Consider the strangest consequence of everything we have established so far. If the heart, once polished by remembrance, sees God — and if it sees Him not by its own light but by His — then where, exactly, does it look to find Him? The natural expectation is that it turns away from the world: closes its eyes to the crowded surfaces of things and seeks God somewhere behind them, above them, apart from them. The Imam's answer runs the opposite way. The heart cleared by remembrance, he teaches, comes to see God precisely in the world — in the neck-vein and the olive tree and the shining star, in the most outward aspects of material existence — and finds that there is nowhere it can turn without meeting the Face of God. And yet, in the same breath, the tradition insists with absolute firmness that nothing whatever resembles God, that He is beyond all comparison, that no thing in which He is found contains or limits Him. The heart sees God everywhere; God is like nothing anywhere. How can both be true at once, and how can a single vision hold them without splitting in two?

This is not a riddle to be dissolved by a clever formula. It is, Shah-Kazemi argues, the very structure of reality as the Imam perceives it, and learning to hold both halves of it together without letting go of either is the whole education of the spiritual vision. Let go of immanence, and God becomes a distant abstraction with no presence in the world He made; let go of transcendence, and God dwindles into the world, becomes one more thing among things, and the door swings open to the gravest of all errors, the idolatry that mistakes a creature for the Creator. The Imam holds the line between these two failures with a precision that the closing sermons of the Nahj al-balagha make almost unbearable in its tautness, and the mystics who came after him — above all the school of Ibn al-'Arabi — spent centuries drawing out what he had compressed into a few dense, paradoxical sentences.

Notice, before we begin, why the stakes are so high on both sides, for it is tempting to think one of the two errors safer than the other. They are not. The person who keeps only transcendence, who insists that God is so far above the world that no trace of Him can be met within it, ends by worshipping an absence — a God so removed that the world becomes godless in practice, a mere machine of matter with the sacred exiled to some unreachable elsewhere. And the person who keeps only immanence, who feels God so present in things that God simply is the things, ends by worshipping the world — and that, in the Qur'an's unsparing verdict, is the one sin that is never forgiven, the association of a creature with the Creator. The Imam's vision is not a comfortable middle between two extremes but a razor's edge above two abysses, and what keeps a soul on the edge is not cleverness but the cleared sight of a heart that has been taught, by remembrance, to see both truths as one. This is why the doctrine of the veil is not an abstract puzzle for metaphysicians; it is the practical safeguard of a lived vision, the discipline by which a person learns to find God everywhere without ever mistaking anything for God.

Three movements carry the episode. We begin with the vision itself — how the heart purified by remembrance comes to see God in all things, reading every existent as a theophany, a self-disclosure of a divine Name, without collapsing God into the creation that discloses Him. We turn then to the paradox at the heart of the matter — the veil that does not veil, creation understood not as the barrier that hides God but as the very appearance through which He shows Himself. And we close on the two truths the whole vision rests upon, incomparability and similarity, held together as one, and on why this seeing belongs to the heart and not to the reasoning mind — which can only take for contradiction what the heart takes for the deepest unity.

## God seen in all things

We must begin from a principle the Imam states and the whole vision presupposes: that the true being of every existing thing is, at its root, a divine Name. In his famous supplication to his disciple Kumayl the Imam addresses God by His tremendousness which has filled all things, by His Names which have filled the foundations of all things, by His knowledge which encompasses all things, by the light of His face through which all things are illumined. The phrase to weigh is that the Names of God have filled the foundations of all things. Nothing can exist without the foundation — the ontological infrastructure, one might say — that a Name, or a combination of Names, of God supplies for it. What constitutes the true being and the positive qualities of any manifested thing is therefore, in the last analysis, a divine Name. And this changes what it means to see a thing correctly. To see a thing correctly is to see the divine attributes manifested by and through it, to perceive it as an existential reflection of a principial reality — as one of what the tradition calls the tajalliyyat, the theophanies or self-disclosures of the divine Reality. The heart purified by invocation comes, on this account, to see God everywhere, because everywhere it looks it reads the divine Name that furnishes each thing with its very being.

This vision rests on two principles the Qur'an affirms, and it is worth seeing how they fit together, for one concerns the object seen and the other the subject who sees. The first concerns the metaphysical nature of reality itself: nothing that exists, not even the most outward aspect of material existence, can be excluded from the divine Reality, for God "is the First and the Last, and the Outward and the Inward" (chapter 57, verse 3), and "wherever ye turn, there is the Face of God" (chapter 2, verse 115). The second concerns the human subject and the assimilation of that reality: "We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and in their own souls, until it be clear to them that He is the Real" (chapter 41, verse 53). Everything around us, and everything within us, is thus a sign pointing back to its Creator, and the cleared heart is the faculty that can at last decipher the sign — read the outward form as the disclosure of the reality it manifests, and so see God in it. The two principles meet in the act of vision: the world is objectively a tissue of theophanies, and the polished heart is the subject at last able to perceive them as such.

It is worth being exact about what this seeing is and what it is not, because a careless version of it slides straight into error. To see the divine in all things is not to look at a tree and mistake the tree for God, nor to feel a vague sacred glow suffusing the landscape. It is to perceive the tree as an existential reflection of a principial reality — to read in its being, its life, its form, the divine qualities of which those are the finite echoes, and so to see through it to the Names that furnish it. The outward eye, the Imam has taught, reaches only the surface of things; it sees the tree and stops at the tree. The vision proper to the heart penetrates the surface to the reality concealed within, and what it finds there is not a second object hidden inside the first but the divine attribute the first object exists to manifest. This is why the possibility of seeing God everywhere carries with it no reduction of God to the level of sensible phenomena. The heart that deciphers the sign is not adding God to the inventory of visible things; it is perceiving that the visible thing has no standing of its own except as the disclosure of a reality that infinitely exceeds it. The more truly a person sees God in a thing, the less the thing detains him as a thing — the more transparent it becomes to what shines through it.

But here the whole difficulty announces itself, and the Imam meets it head-on. That the cleared heart can see God in all things does not mean, and must never be allowed to mean, that God is reduced to the level of the things in which He is seen. The very first sermon of the Nahj al-balagha states the guard with a precision that has echoed through the tradition ever since: God, it proclaims, is "with every thing, but not through association; and other than every thing, but not through separation." Both halves are indispensable. God's withness — His being present to and within all things — implies no relativization of God, no conjoining of the divine Reality with created things, no diminution of His transcendence. He is "closer to man than the neck-artery" (chapter 50, verse 16), as near to us as our own jugular vein — and at the very same time "there is none comparable unto Him" (chapter 112, verse 4). Nothing that exists can be altogether separate from the all-encompassing reality of God, and yet that reality has no common measure with anything that exists. His oneness both includes and excludes all things: the affirmation that He is with every thing does not diminish His transcendence, and the affirmation that He is other than every thing does not imply His absence from the world. God may be absolutely close to us, even as we remain infinitely far from Him.

The ground of this whole way of speaking, as the book established at its outset, is the Imam's radical conception of divine oneness — a oneness that is not the number one. God is one, in his perspective, not in the sense of a countable unit, one entity standing among other entities, but in the sense that His oneness admits no other beside it; and "that which has no second," the Imam says, "does not enter into the category of numbers." This has been taught already and needs no re-opening here; but its bearing on the present vision must be marked, for it is exactly this non-numerical oneness that makes it possible to see God in all things without idolatry. If God's oneness were the oneness of number, then to find Him in a thing would be to find one countable being lodged inside another, and that would indeed be to compromise both. But a oneness that includes all reality and excludes all otherness is not a unit that could be housed in a thing or set beside it; it is the very reality of which each thing is a fleeting reflection. To see God in the world, on this understanding, is not to spot a hidden object among the world's objects but to perceive that the world's objects have no reality of their own apart from the One they reflect. The vision of immanence and the safeguard of transcendence turn out to be, at bottom, a single perception — which is why the Imam can hold them together in a single sentence without strain.

## The paradox of the veil

Now the Imam presses the matter to its sharpest point, and it is here that his teaching becomes genuinely vertiginous. If God is present to all things and disclosed through all things, then in what sense is He hidden at all? The ordinary religious intuition is that creation is a veil — that the crowded, glittering surface of the world stands between us and God, screening Him from view, and that the spiritual task is to see past the veil to the God concealed behind it. The Imam accepts the language of the veil and then turns it inside out. In one sermon he praises "God who is present in the hidden inwardness of things, and whose being is indicated by the signs of manifest things," adding that "the eye of the onlooker cannot behold Him, so the eye of one who does not see Him cannot deny His reality." And he compresses the whole paradox into a single astonishing phrase: "the veils do not veil Him." The veils cannot help revealing what they are supposed to conceal. They are not merely transparent to the reality behind them; in and of themselves they reveal rather than conceal it. This is the sense of another saying of his that the Sufis endlessly quote — that were the veil to be removed, he would not increase in certitude. There is nothing more to be shown him by lifting the veil, because the veil was already showing it.

The Imam himself sets the two sides of the paradox in a single balanced sentence that repays slow reading: "By things their Creator manifests Himself to the intellects; and by things He is guarded from the sight of the eyes." The same things do both. It is by the created world — its order, its beauty, its sheer contingent existence — that the Creator makes Himself evident to the intellect in its normative state, and dazzlingly evident to the heart illumined by remembrance; the world is God's testimony to Himself, and the mind that reads it aright cannot but be led back to its Author. And yet it is by that same created world that God is guarded from the outward eye, which reaches the things and stops at them, taking the surface for the whole and never suspecting the reality it screens. So the phenomena are, at one and the same moment, the disclosure of God to the seeing heart and the concealment of God from the unseeing eye — and which they are depends entirely on the state of the one who confronts them. The world does not change; the beholder does. To the eye it is an opaque wall of things; to the polished heart it is a transparency through which the light pours.

The masters of the school of Ibn al-'Arabi took this paradox and turned it in the light like a jewel. The aphorisms of Ibn 'Ata'illah read almost as a set of variations on the Imam's terse phrase. "That which shows you the existence of His omnipotence," he writes, "is that He veiled you from Himself by that which has no existence alongside of Him." God is, in the Imam's words, guarded from the eyes and thus veiled by things; but insofar as God alone truly is, these things do not really exist — so He is veiled, in the end, by nothing at all. And then Ibn 'Ata'illah presses the point home with a cascade of rhetorical questions that leave no room to stand: How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the one who manifests everything? How can it be conceived that something veils Him, since He is the one who is manifest through everything? Since He is manifest in everything? Since He was the Manifest before the existence of everything? Since He is more manifest than anything? Since He is the one alongside of whom there is nothing? Since He is nearer to you than anything else? Since, were it not for Him, the existence of everything would not be manifest? It is a marvel, he concludes, how Being has been manifested in non-being, and how the contingent has been established alongside of the Eternal.

What all these sayings press upon us can be grasped through the pair of divine Names the Qur'an supplies: al-Zahir, the Apparent, the Manifest, the Evident; and al-Batin, the Hidden, the Inward. All of creation, every veil it seems to constitute, is nothing other than the divine dimension of the Apparent — but it is Apparent not on its own account, but by virtue of the divine reality of which it is a projection, a form of self-disclosure. God as the Apparent projects something of His hidden treasure within and as the creation; the world manifests that aspect of God which can appear, can be brought into being, even while it conceals the supra-manifest source, the Hidden, the uncreated Essence. The veil of creation is therefore nothing other than the self-revelation of God — though it is so, the Imam is careful to add, only by virtue of what pierces the veil of its own apparent otherness: that divine creativity which the creation cannot in fact conceal, even though in principle it has the character of a veil.

But if the veil reveals, why speak of a veil at all — what does it prevent? Here the Imam's precision is at its finest, and it is what keeps the whole vision from toppling into error. The veil between God and His creatures, he says, is His very creation of them, and it exists for a double purpose: to prevent what is possible in the creatures' essences, and to make possible what is prevented in regard to God, so as "to distinguish the Creator from the created, and the limit from the limited, and the Lord from the vassal." What do the creatures' essences make possible? The realization of an inmost identity with the divine Reality — the deepest level of oneness — a possibility that follows from the fact that at their very foundations the essences of things are nothing other than the divine Names, and so the divine Reality itself. If that identity is possible, what then does the veil prevent? It prevents the affirmation of that identity at the wrong level — at the level of empirical existence, where a created thing would simply be equated with God, which is the cardinal sin of association, of idolatry. The veil is, precisely, the barrier against pantheism, against the reduction of the Creator's infinite transcendence to the finite forms of creatures; one must always distinguish the Creator from the created, the Lord from the vassal. Yet because the veil has no existence apart from the self-manifestation of the Real, it also makes possible what would otherwise be prevented: rendered transparent to the divine Reality it expresses, the veil cannot prevent the immanence of God from shining through and within all things. On one side stands essential identity, without any diminution of transcendence; on the other stands existential distinction, without any attenuation of immanence. The double function of the veil deserves to be held still a moment longer, because it is easy to hear it as a paradox for its own sake and miss how exactly it works. Think of what the veil is protecting on each side. Looking from the creature toward God, the veil prevents the creature from claiming, at the level of its everyday existence, an identity with God that would be sheer blasphemy — it keeps the drop from announcing itself the ocean, the lamp from declaring itself the sun. That is its guarding, negative office: it holds the distinction between Lord and vassal that idolatry would erase. But looking from God toward the creature, the same veil, precisely because it is nothing but God's own self-disclosure and has no substance of its own to interpose, cannot actually keep God out; it lets the divine presence flood through it, so that what looked like a wall turns out to be a window. The single veil does both at once, and this is why it can be said neither simply to hide nor simply to reveal. It hides God from the arrogance that would seize Him at the wrong level, and reveals Him to the humility that seeks Him at the right one. And so we return, by the longest of routes, to the first sermon's foundational sentence: He is with every thing, but not through association; and other than every thing, but not through separation.

## Incomparability and similarity

The paradox of the veil rests, finally, on two truths that the tradition names with two technical terms, and everything depends on refusing to let go of either. There is tanzih — the declaration of God's incomparability, His transcendence, His utter unlikeness to anything that exists. And there is tashbih — the affirmation of similarity, and, at the deepest level, of immanence, God's presence within and likeness reflected by the things He makes. The whole error the Imam guards against is the error of choosing one and discarding the other. Ibn al-'Arabi states the balance with unimprovable exactness: God "is not declared incomparable in any manner that will remove Him from similarity, nor is He declared similar in any manner that would remove Him from incomparability." Do not, he warns, declare Him unbounded in such a way that you thereby bound Him — for if He is distinguished from all limitation, then He is limited by that very distinction, delimited by His own non-delimitation, and then, he says, "He is not He." To make God purely transcendent, cut off from all likeness, is itself to have drawn a limit around Him — the limit of pure transcendence — and so to have failed of the very transcendence one was reaching for.

This is why the Imam can say that the veils do not veil Him, and mean by it that both transcendence and immanence can themselves become veils unless each is held in the light of the other. A one-sided emphasis on God as the Apparent becomes a veil, by blinding us to God as the Hidden; and a one-sided emphasis on God as the Hidden becomes a veil no less, by blinding us to His evident presence in the very heart of the created world. The creation as divine manifestation can veil, and the uncreated Essence above all manifestation can veil, unless the two are integrated in the single vision proper to divine oneness and rendered transparent, each revealing the other. The veils, in the end, are not only the created phenomena but the limitations inherent in every human conception of God — including our conceptions of the divine qualities and attributes themselves — which is why the tradition will insist, as the book has taught before in speaking of the Imam's way of affirmation and denial, on a radical negation of those attributes as we conceive them, so that consciousness may fathom what transcendence truly is and see through the veils that do not veil.

It should be clear by now that this is not a set of propositions the reasoning mind assembles but a vision the heart receives — and the distinction is decisive. These perspectives on the veil, Shah-Kazemi observes, are better understood as the evocation of a spiritual vision than as the conclusions of logical analysis; they are the fruit of contemplative synthesis, not the product of some tortuous mental effort to reconcile the complexities of existence. The vision resolves apparent contradiction through an intuitive awareness of the unitive simplicity of reality — it does not argue its way to a resolution but simply sees the unity that was there before the contradiction arose. It is at once a vision reflected by the heart burnished by remembrance and itself a form of that very remembrance. And here the two faculties the book has distinguished from the first part company in the plainest way: what the rational faculty will always perceive as irreducible contradiction, the spirit of the intellect apprehends as a fruitful paradox. The famous Sufi Abu Sa'id al-Kharraz, asked how he had come to know God, answered, "through the fact that He brings opposites together" — and then recited the verse, "He is the First and the Last, and the Outward and the Inward." The mind sorts and separates; it cannot hold first and last, outward and inward, as one without protest. The heart, given the capacity to see as one what appears on the surface as many, holds them without strain.

The difference is not that the heart is careless where the mind is rigorous, as though the vision were merely a licence to ignore contradictions the mind is honest enough to feel. The point is subtler and more demanding than that. The contradiction the mind registers is real — on the plane of concepts, transcendence and immanence, hiddenness and manifestation, do genuinely pull against each other, and a thinker who pretended otherwise would only be papering over the difficulty with a word. What the heart perceives is that the plane of concepts is not the deepest plane. Beneath the level at which the opposites are opposed lies the unitive simplicity of the Real, where they were never two things needing to be reconciled but one reality seen, at the surface, under two aspects. The heart does not resolve the contradiction by a superior argument; it sees past the level on which the contradiction arises. This is why the tradition insists that the vision is a fruit of contemplation and not of analysis, and why it ties the vision so tightly to remembrance. The polishing that clears the heart is the same operation that lifts consciousness from the plane where reality fractures into opposites to the plane where it is simply one. To remember God, in this sense, is already to begin to see as one what the unremembering mind can only see as many.

There is a quiet grandeur in al-Kharraz's answer that should not be passed over. Asked the most searching question one could put to a mystic — how did you come to know God? — he does not describe an experience, or name a method, or recount a vision. He gives, instead, a definition of God: the one who brings opposites together. He knows God, that is, as the reality in which first and last, outward and inward, transcendence and immanence, are not warring principles to be balanced but a single unity from which the appearance of opposition falls away. To know God is to know the ground on which the opposites are reconciled — or rather were never sundered. And the verse he recites to seal the answer, that God is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward, is the very verse the Imam's teaching on the veil has been circling all along: the four Names that the reasoning mind must hold apart and the seeing heart holds together. The knower of God is simply the one in whom that holding-together has become vision. Everything the school of Ibn al-'Arabi elaborated over the centuries, and everything the Imam compressed into the phrase that the veils do not veil Him, is contained in al-Kharraz's five plain words — the whole doctrine of immanence and transcendence folded into a single answer given without hesitation.

There is a fine irony, which Shah-Kazemi does not miss, in what the Imam is doing when he sharpens these paradoxes to their finest edge. In pressing the contradiction on the conceptual plane — insisting that the veils both hide and reveal, that God is both utterly beyond and utterly near — he is, one might say in modern terms, dismantling the artifices of mental conception, breaking the mind's habit of resolving everything into tidy, separated categories, and so clearing the way for a receptivity to intuition and contemplation that the categories had blocked. The very word for intellect in Arabic, from a root meaning to bind or to restrain, points to its proper work: not the fragmenting analysis of the reasoning faculty but a binding-together, a gathering of the realities the heart discloses through intuition into the organic unity of oneness. The realities the heart brings to light are drawn together by the intellect through a movement of negation and affirmation, of constriction and expansion, into that unity — and it is only at these deeper levels of consciousness that the true re-cognition of the Real can take place, arising from the heart's vision of God through the verities of faith. Only those verities are commensurate with the Real; only the realities hidden in the depths of faith, and brought to light in a heart polished by remembrance, can do justice to the reality of God. The mind reaches the veil and stops at it; the heart, cleared, sees that the veil was never a veil.

## What this episode lands

This episode has taken the cleared heart out into the world and shown what it beholds there. We have seen that for the Imam the heart purified by remembrance sees God in all things — every existent a theophany, its very being a divine Name filling its foundations — and that it does so without the least reduction of God to the things in which He is found, because His oneness is not the oneness of number but a reality that includes all and resembles nothing. We have followed the Imam's most dizzying paradox to its center: that creation, which seems the veil that hides God, is in truth the self-disclosure through which He shows Himself, so that the veils do not veil Him at all — the veil existing only to keep us from the one fatal error of equating a creature with the Creator, while it makes the divine presence shine through everything else. And we have held together the two truths on which the whole vision rests, incomparability and similarity, transcendence and immanence, learning that to seize either alone is to turn it into a veil, and that the seeing which holds both belongs to the heart and not to the mind that can only take them for contradiction.

What this leaves us needing is the practice itself. We have spoken at length of the heart cleared by remembrance and of the world it then beholds, but we have not yet looked closely at what remembrance concretely is as a discipline — how it is actually done, in what measure, by what means, and why the tradition insists that it must be an active practice of the tongue and not merely a contemplation held in the mind. The vision is the fruit; the coming episode turns to the labor that bears it. If the heart is a mirror that remembrance polishes, we turn now to ask how, precisely, the polishing is performed — and why the Qur'an commands that it be performed not sparingly but in abundance, woven into the whole texture of a life.

There is a reason the sequence runs in this order and not the reverse. Had we begun with the mechanics of the practice — the times, the modes, the measure of invocation — we would have had a technique in search of a purpose, a set of instructions with nothing to explain why anyone should trouble to follow them. Having first seen what the cleared heart beholds — God present in all things and beyond all things, the veils that do not veil, the opposites brought together — we now know exactly what the practice is for and what is at stake in it. The invocation the next episode describes is not a pious exercise added to a full life; it is the polishing of the one organ by which a person can see the reality this episode has laid open. And that is why the Qur'an does not counsel a little of it, now and then. What is at stake is nothing less than whether a person spends his days on the opaque surface of things or in the depth that the surface conceals — and a vision of that magnitude is not won by an occasional gesture. It is won, if at all, by a remembrance made continual, poured into every hour and posture of a life, which is exactly what the verses we turn to next command. The vision this episode has described is not a view a person visits and departs; it is a country he must learn to live in, and one learns to live in it only by the daily labor to which we now turn.
