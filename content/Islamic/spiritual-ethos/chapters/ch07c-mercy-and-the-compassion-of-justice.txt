# Mercy and the Compassion of Justice

*If tyranny is the ego usurping the place of God, its cure is a self-lowering that mirrors the soul's true poverty before the Absolute — and the first form that cure takes, in Ali (ع)'s letter to Malik al-Ashtar, is mercy. This episode of Reza Shah-Kazemi's study argues that compassion is not the softening of justice but an intrinsic dimension of it, rooted in the nature of a God whose mercy takes precedence over His wrath. It follows the letter's great commands to infuse the heart with mercy for the governed, watches the Imam live those commands out with unbending severity toward his own kin and unexpected tenderness toward a blind stranger, and closes on the subtle teaching that every vice, at bottom, is a bad opinion of God.*

## Where this episode picks up

Doesn't mercy soften justice — bend the rule for the sympathetic case, let the guilty off, tilt the scales toward pity? That is how we usually set the two words against each other, as if compassion were the humane loosening of a justice that would otherwise be hard. The letter to Malik al-Ashtar refuses the opposition outright. Mercy, in the Imam's ethos, is not what tempers justice from outside; it is justice seen from within, an intrinsic dimension of the same reality. To act with compassion accords with justice in the plain sense of putting things in their right place — one must extend mercy wherever and whenever it is called for — but it accords with justice at a deeper level too, by conforming to the true nature of things, the intrinsic character of the Real. And the Real, in this vision, is merciful before it is anything else.

We reach this having watched the letter clear away the great obstacle to just rule: the pride by which a soul sets itself in the place of God and turns, in doing so, against its own conscience and its own deepest nature. The cure for that disorder, we saw, had to be its opposite — a self-lowering that reflects the soul's actual poverty before the Absolute. Mercy is where that self-lowering first becomes visible in the world. A ruler who has truly grasped his own nothingness before God cannot look down on those beneath him as a beast of prey looks down on what it means to devour; his awareness of his own need of divine mercy above becomes the spring of the mercy he extends to those below.

There is a warning built into that image, and the letter states it in almost these words: be not like a ravenous beast of prey above the people, seeking to devour them. The temptation of power is precisely to see those beneath one as prey — as material to be used, taxed, and discarded. The whole burden of this movement of the letter is to replace the predator's gaze with another, in which the governed are seen as brothers or as equals in creation, and in which the ruler's height over them is felt not as license but as exposure, since someone stands higher over him, and God higher than all. Mercy, then, is the corrected vision of a man who remembers where he actually stands.

Three movements carry the episode. First, why compassion is an intrinsic edge of justice and not its opposite — a claim tested against the Imam's own startling severity, the red-hot iron he brought near his own brother and the sword he promised his cousin, which show that mercy and impartial rigor spring from one root. Second, the universal reach of that mercy: the great command to infuse the heart with love for the subjects, the reminder that every person is either a brother in faith or a like in creation, and the blind Christian beggar the Imam ordered maintained from the public treasury. And third, the hidden root of the whole thing — a good opinion of God, the accurate perception of the divine nature from which every generous act flows, and whose absence turns quietly into every vice.

## Mercy as an edge of justice

Start with the ontology, because everything else rests on it. The capacity to act with compassion, the Imam teaches, stems not so much from personal sentiment as from an innate affinity with the very nature of divine reality. This is why compassion belongs to justice conceived not merely as fair dealing but ontologically — in accordance with the fundamental character of the Real. Shah-Kazemi points to the sign the Qur'an itself places at the head of nearly every one of its chapters: after the name of God come the words "the infinitely Compassionate, the ever-Merciful." Mercy is not one item in a list of divine qualities; it is the atmosphere in which all the others are named. The single chapter of the Qur'an that omits the invocation is the exception that proves how deliberate the pattern is: everywhere else, before God is invoked as creator, judge, sovereign, or avenger, He is invoked as the infinitely Compassionate and the ever-Merciful, so that mercy frames the reading of every page. The Qur'an makes the priority explicit — "My mercy encompasses all things"; "thy Lord has prescribed for Himself mercy" — and the tradition preserves the divine word that settles the order once and for all: "My mercy takes precedence over My wrath." Neither the rigorous side of things nor the wrathful side of God is denied; both are real. But both are clearly subordinated to the higher ontological principle of mercy. There is no common measure between the relativities that are subject to wrath and punishment and the intrinsically beatific realities to which mercy gives access. One is therefore more real, in this precise sense, insofar as one's soul reflects this predominance of mercy — in spiritual orientation and in moral conduct alike.

From that principle the Imam draws a cluster of sayings on the reciprocity of mercy, each turning the divine order into a human imperative. "The dispensing of mercy brings down divine mercy." "As you grant mercy, so will you be granted mercy." And, most pointedly for a man about to hold power over others: "I am astounded by the person who hopes for mercy from one above him, while he is not merciful to those beneath him." The logic is not sentimental but structural. A person stands in a chain of dependence — below God, who is above all, and above those he governs, who depend on him as he depends on God. To withhold mercy from those below while begging it from above is to break the very symmetry the universe is built on. This is why the identification with the poor that the previous episode named as the antidote to pride flows so naturally into mercy: living in the awareness of one's own perpetual need of God, in hope and trust, has as its natural concomitant the bestowal of mercy on one's fellow human beings.

It is worth being precise about what "more real" means here, because it is the phrase that carries the ontology. In this ethos a quality is not merely praised or condemned on a moral ledger; it is measured by how much reality it participates in. Mercy, being at one with the deepest nature of the Real, is more real than the wrath and rigor that answer only to the passing relativities of the created world. So a soul that reflects the predominance of mercy is not simply a nicer soul; it is a soul standing closer to what truly is. Cruelty, by the same measure, is not only wrong but thin — an alignment with the less real, the merely relative, the destined-to-pass. This is the quiet reason the Imam can ask a governor to lead with mercy without any fear that he is asking him to be weak. To be merciful is to be aligned with the strongest thing there is, the mercy that encompasses all things and takes precedence over all wrath; and there is no firmer ground on which a ruler could plant his authority.

Yet Shah-Kazemi is careful not to let this collapse into softness, and the letter itself will not permit it. It lies in the very nature of justice, conceived in this sacred way, to tend toward compassion wherever possible — but there must also be a place for the rigorous application of corrective penalty where that is unavoidable. The Imam who commands mercy also instructs Malik to inflict corporal punishment on any officer found guilty of misappropriating public funds, and his letters are replete with stern warnings of swift and severe retribution to his governors, the severity always proportioned to his unflinching fidelity to fairness and honesty in the handling of public revenue. To a deputy in Basra he wrote, in an oath sworn in all sincerity, that if news reached him of even a small misappropriation of the Muslims' revenue he would inflict a punishment that would leave the man's wealth light, his back burdened, and his affair degraded. Mercy toward the vulnerable and severity toward the corrupt are not in tension here; they are the same fidelity to putting each thing in its right place, applied to two different objects.

The way to hold mercy and severity together without contradiction is to remember what justice is: the placing of each thing in its right place. Mercy is right in its place — toward the vulnerable, the erring, the weak from whom mistakes slip deliberately or by accident. Corrective penalty is right in its place too — toward the officer who plunders the public trust, the powerful man who exploits his position. To extend mercy where rigor is required, or rigor where mercy is required, would be an injustice of exactly the kind the whole letter is written to prevent: a thing put in the wrong place. So the Imam's tenderness and his severity are not a temperament that swings between poles; they are a single fidelity, discriminating in each case what the situation truly calls for and giving it precisely that. Sacred justice is not the absence of severity, and it is not the absence of mercy; it is the wisdom that knows, in each instance, which the moment demands, and the will to render it without regard to the cost to oneself.

The most searching proof of this unity is what the Imam was willing to do to his own family. In one sermon he recalls how his blind brother, Aqil ibn Abi Talib, asked for funds from the treasury beyond what was his due. "He thought I would sell my religion for him," the Imam said — and his answer was to bring a red-hot piece of iron close to his brother's body, telling him that by making such a request he was urging the Imam himself to enter a far more intense fire. When his long-standing ally and cousin, Abd Allah ibn al-Abbas, was found to have misappropriated funds, the response was a thundering remonstrance: fear God and return the people's property; if you do not, and God gives me authority over you, I shall excuse myself before Him concerning you and strike you with my sword, "with which whomever I have struck enters the fire of Hell." And then the sentence that removes any doubt about how far the principle reaches: "By God, even if Hasan and Husayn had done the like of what you have done, they would not have been granted any leniency by me." The mention of the Prophet's grandsons is deliberate and unmistakable. However close the relationship with a wrongdoer, however exalted his status, the principle of justice demands absolute impartiality — no favors for one's favorites. This is the precise opposite of the nepotism that had undermined the administration before him, and it is exactly what revelation commands: "O you who believe, be staunch in justice, witnesses of God, even though it be against yourselves, your parents, or your near of kin" (chapter 4, verse 135). Mercy for the weak and impartiality toward the powerful, including one's own, turn out to be two names for one virtue.

## Brother in religion, like in creation

Now the letter states the command that gives the whole subject its scope, and it is one of the great sentences in the literature of governance. "Infuse your heart with mercy for the subjects, love for them and kindness towards them. Be not like a ravenous beast of prey above them, seeking to devour them. For they are of two types: either your brother in religion or your like in creation. Mistakes slip from them, defects emerge from them, deliberately or accidentally. So bestow upon them your forgiveness and your pardon, just as you would have God bestow upon you His forgiveness and pardon; for you are above them, and the one who has authority over you is above you, and God is above him who appointed you." Shah-Kazemi dwells on this passage because of how far it reaches. It is one of the Imam's most important statements on the unity of the human race and the equality of all human beings, and a corrective to every form of prejudice, particularism, and sectarianism that would restrict justice or compassion to members of one's own group, however that group is defined.

The reach is in the phrase "either your brother in religion or your like in creation." There is no third category — no one who falls outside the claim of mercy. Those who share your faith are your brothers; everyone else is your equal in the fact of being created, and that alone establishes the claim. The passage binds this universal compassion to a reminder of God's absolute sovereignty: the governor is above his subjects, but the ruler who appointed him is above him, and God is above them all. No one in the chain is anything other than a slave of God, utterly dependent on His mercy. So every person who finds himself in a position of relative superiority must constantly remember his own inferiority before the Absolute — an awareness that both moves him to compassion toward those below and draws to himself the compassion of God above.

There is a final clause in that great command that Shah-Kazemi will not let pass, because it reframes the whole relation of a ruler to those he rules: "and through them He tests you." The subjects are not merely the objects of the governor's mercy; they are the instrument of his own trial before God. Power over others is not a privilege to be enjoyed but a test to be undergone, and the people placed under one's authority are precisely the ground on which one is examined. This is the Imam's own attitude to rulership, and it changes the emotional texture of authority entirely. A ruler who understands that his subjects are his examination cannot regard them with the cool proprietorship of an owner surveying his holdings; he must regard them the way a person under scrutiny regards the very thing his judgment turns on. Every act of mercy or cruelty toward them is an answer he is giving to God about himself. The powerful man, on this reading, is the most exposed man in the realm, not the most secure — and it is exactly this reversal that keeps sacred justice from ever hardening into the complacency of office.

The Imam lived this out in an incident Shah-Kazemi lets stand as a vivid gloss on "your like in creation." Coming across an old, blind beggar, the Imam inquired about him and was told the man was a Christian. His response was immediate: "You have employed him until he is old and infirm, and now you refuse to help him. Give him maintenance from the public treasury." The instruction expresses, in a single stroke, the principle of social welfare rooted in redistributive justice and a policy of strict non-discrimination between Muslims and others. Social justice and religious equality flow here from compassion conceived not as a passing sentiment but as an intrinsic dimension of the Real — and therefore translate into a fundamental religious duty binding on all Muslims, rulers and citizens alike. The beggar's faith is beside the point; his likeness in creation is the whole point. And notice the exact shape of the Imam's rebuke: he does not merely order charity, he indicts a society that used a man's labor while he was strong and abandoned him when he was old and blind. The claim on the public treasury is not presented as generosity but as something owed — the settling of a debt the community incurred by benefiting from the man in the first place. This is redistributive justice in its most literal sense, the placing of resources where they are owed rather than where they are politically convenient, and it falls on the ruler as a duty precisely because the destitute have no power to compel it. Mercy toward the powerless is, once again, not the softening of justice but its sharpest application, in the one arena where nothing but the ruler's own fidelity to God will make it happen.

Behind this universal mercy stands the Imam's own manner of life, which made the teaching credible. His poverty and austerity were proverbial, but Shah-Kazemi is careful to show this was no mere world-hatred for its own sake. When an ascetic named Asim ibn Ziyad took abstemiousness to an extreme that distressed his family, the Imam told him to think of his family and not cut himself off from the good things God has permitted. Asim shot back: "O Commander of the Faithful, and yet here you are, in your rough clothes and your coarse food!" The reply drew the crucial distinction: "Woe to you — I am not like you. God has made it incumbent on true leaders that they proportion themselves to the weakest among the people, so that the poverty of the poor will not engender covetousness." Here is a rigorous expression of the duty of the most powerful to identify themselves, in material terms, with the most powerless. He who could have the most chooses to have the least — not from contempt for the world as such, but because a leader who would stand among the true leaders must manifest his attachment to al-Haqq, the ultimate reality in whose light material wealth is of no consequence. The powerful and the powerless may both be poor in material terms, yet both have equal access to the treasures of the spiritual domain, in proportion to their realization of the truth the Qur'an states: "O mankind, you are the destitute before God, and God is the Self-sufficient, the Praiseworthy" (chapter 35, verse 15). And on the plane of social psychology, the Imam's insistence that rulers live like the poor, as he himself did, is a powerful means of ensuring that the inevitable inequalities of society do not breed envy.

This posture toward power has a name in the Western tradition — noblesse oblige — and Shah-Kazemi invokes it, along with the Imam's own most famous statement of it. When one has been granted the grace of knowledge together with a clear sense of justice, a correspondingly greater obligation is created; one's duty to God grows in proportion to the graces bestowed. The Imam gave this memorable form after being praised by a supporter: "The blessings of God upon a person do not become immense without the right of God over him becoming immense." The greater the gift, the heavier the debt. And this attitude shaped how the Imam regarded rulership itself — not as a prize but as a burden accepted only under obligation. In the sermon known for its bitter eloquence, he said he would have flung the reins of the caliphate back upon its own withers, finding this world of ours as insignificant as what drips from the nose of a goat, had God not taken from the learned a covenant that they would not acquiesce in the rapacity of the tyrant nor in the hunger of the oppressed. Shah-Kazemi hears in this a striking embodiment of the ideal ruler Plato imagined — the true philosopher who takes power not as a distinction but as an unavoidable task, caring only for the right and for justice as the one indispensable thing in whose service he would reorder the state. Rulership, for such a man, is something to be borne for the sake of the governed, never seized for the sake of the self. The convergence is not incidental to Shah-Kazemi's argument; it is one of the places where the letter and the Republic most clearly illuminate each other. Plato worried that the men best fitted to rule would be exactly the ones least willing, since a good man does not hunger for power; and he concluded that the only safe rulers are those who consent to govern reluctantly, as a duty owed rather than a prize won. The Imam gives that Platonic intuition a spiritual ground it lacks in Plato. The reluctance is not merely the good man's distaste for power; it is the servant's knowledge that authority is a trust from God that will be asked back, a weight accepted under covenant with the learned and borne toward the day of reckoning. What in Plato is a psychological observation about the right temperament for rule becomes, in the Imam, a fact about the soul's standing before its Maker. And this, again, is why mercy rather than self-interest is the native language of such a ruler: a man who holds power as a burden owed to God and the poor has no motive to hoard its advantages for himself, and every motive to spend them on those in his charge.

## A good opinion of God

The letter now turns to a point so concisely put that it is easily missed, and Shah-Kazemi treats it as the hidden root of everything the episode has traced. Advising Malik on which counsellors to keep close, the Imam warns him away from three kinds of men: "Do not include among those you consult a miser who would hold you back from generosity and warn you of destitution; nor a coward who would make you feel too weak for your affairs; nor a greedy person who would make the amassing of wealth by evil means seem attractive to you. For miserliness, cowardice, and avarice are so many diverse inclinations, all united by a bad opinion of God." That last clause is the key. Three different vices — meanness, timidity, greed — are traced to a single source, and the source is not a moral failing at all but a misperception of the divine nature. A bad opinion of God: that is what miserliness, cowardice, and avarice have in common.

Unfold the diagnosis and it is startlingly precise. When the divine nature is misunderstood, these very vices follow, each a distorted inference from a false picture of God. The miser holds the bad opinion that God may not provide in the future, and so he hoards. The coward believes that God may not protect him, and so he shrinks. The greedy man feels that God has given him less than he needs, and so he grasps. Every one of them is acting on a theology, whether or not he knows it — a defective account of who God is and what He may be trusted to do. From this vantage, all human vices trace back to an intellectual dysfunction, itself the consequence of weak faith, or even of outright disbelief, since to deny God is the ultimate bad opinion of Him. And the converse follows just as logically: a strong and deep faith generates a good opinion of God — an accurate intellectual and contemplative perception of the divine Reality — and this good opinion in turn causes the soul to be genuinely impressed, not superficially but to its depths, by the divine qualities that Reality contains.

Notice why these three vices in particular are so dangerous in a counsellor, and how exactly they corrode just rule. Each of them, whispered into a ruler's ear at the right moment, wears the costume of prudence. The miser counsels against generosity in the name of guarding the treasury and warns perpetually of destitution; the coward counsels against necessary action in the name of realism and makes his ruler feel too weak to do what justice requires; the greedy man dresses up the amassing of wealth by wrongful means as sensible self-interest. All three give advice that sounds like worldly wisdom and is in fact a failure of faith projected outward — a bad opinion of God offered as statecraft. This is why the Imam bars them from the circle of counsel altogether. A ruler surrounded by such voices will slowly be argued out of every just and generous impulse, each retreat justified by a plausible fear; and behind every one of those fears stands the same false picture of a God who cannot be trusted to provide, to protect, or to suffice. Sacred justice, then, has an intellectual prerequisite: the ruler must first get God right, or the corrupt inferences will follow on their own.

Those qualities, Shah-Kazemi is careful to say, must be grasped in two registers at once, and holding them together is the whole art of the spiritual life. They are transcendent — absolutely one with the divine Essence, utterly beyond the reach of the human soul. But they are also models, exemplars, patterns of perfection the soul is meant to emulate as far as it can. Miss the first register and one falls into the tyrant's error, imagining that a divine quality one has managed to reflect is now one's own possession — the generous man who begins to say "I am generous," which is the first step toward comparing oneself with God in greatness. Miss the second register and one falls into the opposite error, a false humility that treats the divine qualities as so utterly beyond reach that no effort to embody them is worth making, and the world is left unserved while the soul congratulates itself on its lowliness. The good opinion of God keeps both errors at bay. It sees the qualities as truly God's, so it never claims them; and it sees them as truly exemplary, so it never stops reaching for them. This is why a good opinion of God is not a comfortable optimism but a demanding discipline — the constant work of reflecting the divine without appropriating it. This is the very opposite of the tyrant's error of "comparing oneself with God in greatness." One must be as much like God as possible, yet this development unfolds entirely within the unalterable context of one's servitude to Him: it is the emulation of God's qualities, never the personification of Him. One is generous because God is the Generous, and merciful because He is the Compassionate — not out of any claim that "I am generous" or "I am merciful." And precisely because the aim is to emulate and not to rival, the same movement that reaches up toward the divine qualities must also bend down into humility and self-effacement. No degree of virtue or intelligence can reduce the incommensurability between the Absolute and the relative, between the Creator and the created; so the virtues of lowliness and self-effacement are simply the truthful acknowledgment of that gap, ways of expressing the human being's absolute dependence on God.

Shah-Kazemi captures the double movement in an image the Imam's ethos keeps returning to. Between the human and the divine there is both a positive and an inverse analogy. In the positive sense, one reflects the divine qualities directly: "He who shows no mercy will not have mercy shown to him." In the inverse sense, one reflects the divine transcendence precisely by acknowledging one's own nothingness — as the Qur'an says, "O mankind, you are the destitute before God, and God is the Self-sufficient, the Praiseworthy" (chapter 35, verse 15). There must therefore be a synthesis between the active human virtues, as positive reflections of the divine qualities, and the spiritual consciousness of one's nothingness before the Real. It is like looking into a mirror: what one sees is oneself, yet seen in reverse. When one looks into the divine mirror, the aim is to catch, on the human plane, an inverted reflection of divine transcendence. For the human being is at once two things the tradition names in a single breath — slave of God and vicegerent of God, His servant and His representative on the earth. The consciousness of servitude ensures that, while representing God, one never slides into rivalling Him in grandeur, which is the tyrant's fall. The active commitment to representing God's attributes ensures that this awareness of servitude never collapses into a paralysing neglect of one's duties toward the world. The equilibrium between these two dimensions — slave and representative, effacement and action — is itself another aspect of sacred justice, another placing of things in their right order, and it attunes the quality of moral action to the harmony of pure being.

With this the episode's own circle closes. It began with mercy and asked where mercy comes from; it ends by locating the source. Mercy is not, at bottom, a warm feeling a ruler happens to have or lack. It is the fruit of a good opinion of God — of an accurate perception of a Reality whose mercy takes precedence over its wrath — worked out through the double discipline of reflecting the divine qualities while confessing one's nothingness before them. A ruler who has that perception cannot help being merciful, because he is trying to emulate a God who is merciful; and he cannot help being just, because the same perception forbids him to appropriate what is God's or to neglect what God has entrusted to him. The miser, the coward, and the greedy man are merciless in their several ways precisely because their opinion of God is bad. Get the opinion of God right, and mercy follows as surely as the corrupt inferences followed from getting it wrong. The letter has thus tied the ruler's gentleness toward the weak to the deepest thing in him — his picture of the divine nature — and left no room to treat compassion as an optional grace note over the hard business of rule. It is the hard business of rule, done rightly.

## What this episode lands

This episode has answered the tyrant's disorder with its cure. Where pride made the ego swell until it eclipsed God, mercy is the ego shrinking to its true size before Him — and that shrinking, far from weakening the ruler, is what makes him just. The whole movement has argued a single, counterintuitive claim: that mercy and justice are not rivals to be balanced against each other but one reality seen from two sides, both grounded in the nature of a God who is Justice and whose mercy takes precedence over His wrath. We have seen that compassion is not the softening of justice but an intrinsic edge of it, rooted in a God whose mercy takes precedence over His wrath; and we have seen that this same root produces, without contradiction, the Imam's unbending severity toward corruption, even the red-hot iron near his own brother and the refusal of leniency he would have extended to no one, not even to Hasan and Husayn. We have seen how far the mercy reaches — to every person as either a brother in faith or a like in creation, to the blind Christian beggar maintained from the public purse, to the weakest of the people whose level the true leader lowers himself to share. And we have found the hidden root of it all: a good opinion of God, the accurate perception of the divine nature from which every generous act flows and whose absence turns quietly into every vice.

The letter has now given the ruler the two dispositions that matter most — freedom from the pride that wars with God, and the mercy that mirrors the soul's poverty before Him. What remains is to put those dispositions to work in the actual machinery of a society: its classes and offices, its soldiers and judges and scribes, its farmers and merchants, and above all its poor. The next movement follows the letter into that machinery, where sacred justice meets the hard particulars of administration — and where, the Imam insists, the same vertical axis that ordered the ruler's soul must order the whole turning wheel of the state. There the mercy we have just traced will be tested against tax registers and troop commanders, judges and scribes and cultivators, and above all against the destitute, whose care the Imam will press upon Malik with a special urgency. The disposition is now in place; what remains is to watch it do the work of governing.
