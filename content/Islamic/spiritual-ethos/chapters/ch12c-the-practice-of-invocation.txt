# The Practice of Invocation

*Having established the vision that remembrance yields, this episode turns to the labor that yields it — the actual practice of invoking God. Reza Shah-Kazemi gathers the Qur'anic commands to invoke God's Name morning and evening, in humility and in secret, and above all in abundance, and sets beside them the Imam's insistence that remembrance is a remedy that must be taken, not merely a meaning to be contemplated. It then traces the living circle of invocation, love, and reflection in which each deepens the other, and closes on the Imam's teaching that remembrance is "greater" than the formal prayer — the positive substance that liberates prayer from the very limits that define it, so that a person may become one who is perpetually at prayer.*

## Where this episode picks up

We have spent two episodes on what the eye of the cleared heart beholds — God in all things, the veils that do not veil — and almost nothing yet on the plainest question a listener might ask: what does one actually do? For all the grandeur of the vision, the practice at its root is startlingly humble. It is a Name, spoken. The Qur'an does not command that we achieve a state of mystical illumination; it commands, in the simplest imperative, that we invoke the Name of the Lord, and that we do so much. And the Imam, for all the metaphysical depths his sayings open, is relentless on this homely point: the remembrance of God is a remedy, and a remedy does no good in the cupboard. It must be taken.

There is a temptation, especially for a thoughtful person, to treat all of this as a set of ideas — to grasp the doctrine of the polished heart, to admire the paradox of the veil, and to suppose that having understood remembrance one has in some sense already practiced it. The whole weight of this episode falls against that temptation. Remembrance, in the Imam's teaching and the Qur'an's, has an operative, practical dimension that cannot be reduced to a theoretical grasp of its meanings. A man once came to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, and said, "O Messenger of God, the laws of Islam are too many for me, so tell me something I may cling to," and the Prophet answered, "Let your tongue never cease to be moist from the invocation of God." Not: understand the following doctrine. Not: hold the following intention. Keep your tongue wet with the Name. The practice is bodily, verbal, repeated, and continual, and it is this concrete discipline — not merely its beautiful rationale — that polishes the heart. One does not think one's way to a clean mirror; one wipes it, and the wiping is a motion of the hand, repeated, not a conclusion of the mind.

There is a certain humility demanded in accepting this, and it is not accidental that the tradition demands it. The very simplicity of the practice is a kind of test. A person drawn to the grandeur of the vision may find it faintly disappointing that the way to it should be so plain — a Name, on the tongue, again and again — and may look about for something more commensurate with the height of the goal, some subtler exercise worthier of a subtle mind. But the plainness is deliberate. The Name is given precisely so that the way should be open to everyone and closed to pride, so that the shepherd and the scholar stand on the same ground before it, and so that the one thing required is not cleverness but faithfulness — the willingness to keep doing a small thing without cease. The heart is polished by persistence, not by ingenuity; and the humble willingness to repeat a plain Name through the long unremarkable hours is itself part of what the practice works in the soul. To accept the smallness of the means is already to have begun the surrender the means is meant to accomplish.

Three movements carry the episode. We begin with the Qur'anic command to invoke, and to invoke in abundance — the modes it prescribes and the astonishing measure it sets, drawn from the night vigils of the first Muslims. We turn then to the Imam's insistence that the Name is a remedy to be actively taken, and to the circle of love, invocation, and reflection in which the practice comes alive. And we close on his teaching that remembrance is greater than the formal prayer itself — not because prayer is dispensable, but because remembrance is the living substance that prayer exists to carry, the essence that liberates the form from its own limits.

## Invocation in abundance

The Qur'an's commands to invoke are direct, repeated, and specific about the manner of the practice, and it is worth hearing several of them together, for their cumulative force is part of their meaning. "And invoke the Name of thy Lord morning and evening" (chapter 76, verse 25). "And invoke the Name of thy Lord, devoting thyself to Him with utter devotion" (chapter 73, verse 8). "O ye who believe! Invoke God with much invocation" (chapter 33, verse 41). "Call upon thy Lord in humility and in secret" (chapter 7, verse 55). "And invoke thy Lord within thyself, in humility and awe, and beneath thy breath, in the morning and in the night" (chapter 7, verse 205). Read in sequence, these verses do two things at once. They establish that remembrance is an actual practice, something done at set times and in set manners — morning and evening, within oneself, in humility, in awe, in secret, beneath the breath. And they establish that the practice has many modes: it may be voiced or silent, public in its discipline but inward in its focus, spoken aloud or breathed almost soundlessly. The tradition does not reduce remembrance to a single technique; it opens a whole range of ways in which the Name may be carried.

But among all the qualifications the verses attach, one stands out and governs the rest: the invocation is to be performed abundantly. "Invoke God with much invocation" — the Arabic word is kathiran, much, in abundance, without stint. This is not a practice measured out in careful spoonfuls. The measure the Qur'an sets is startling, and it is best seen in the chapter called The Enwrapped, which recalls the long night vigils of the Prophet and the first Muslims. "Truly, thy Lord knoweth that thou standest in prayer for almost two-thirds of the night, and a half of it, and a third of it — as doth a group from those with thee" (chapter 73, verse 20). The devotions of the earliest community were not a brief daily observance fitted into the margins of an ordinary life; they consumed the night. And the opening of that same chapter contains the very command we began from — to invoke the Name of the Lord and devote oneself to Him with utter devotion — so that the abundance and the devotion are set side by side as two faces of one thing. To invoke much is to devote oneself utterly; the measure and the manner are inseparable.

It is worth pausing on the manners the verses prescribe, because they are not incidental decorations but the shape the practice is meant to take. Several of the commands turn the invocation inward and downward, away from display: call upon the Lord in humility and in secret; invoke Him within thyself, in humility and awe, and beneath thy breath. There is a reason the Qur'an so often presses the invocation toward silence and secrecy. A remembrance performed to be seen has already been diverted from its object, for its object is God alone, and the moment another set of eyes is courted the heart has divided its attention. The secret invocation, breathed almost soundlessly within the self, is remembrance purified of the wish to be observed — and in that it is the natural companion of the abundance the Qur'an commands, for what is done continually, through the ordinary hours of an ordinary life, is by that very continuity mostly hidden. One cannot make a public performance of a thing one is doing at every moment; abundance drives the practice inward. And the awe the verses join to the humility keeps the inwardness from turning cozy or slack. To invoke in humility and awe is to hold, in the very act, the two truths the last episode set side by side: God nearer than the jugular vein, and God incomparable to anything that is. The manner of the practice thus rehearses, in the body and the breath, the vision it is meant to yield.

There is a subtlety in that opening verse worth drawing out, because it discloses what the invocation is finally reaching toward. When the Qur'an says "devote thyself to Him with utter devotion," the pronoun — to Him, or to it — can refer either to the Lord or to the Name of the Lord, so that the operative sense becomes: invoke the Name of God and devote yourself to this Name with utter devotion. This is echoed in another command, "Glorify the Name of thy Lord, the Exalted" (chapter 87, verse 1). On the plane of devotional practice, then, devotion to the Name of the Absolute is tantamount to devotion to the Absolute itself. This is a point the next episode will unfold to its metaphysical root; here it is enough to register its practical force. The Absolute in itself is above and beyond all forms, including all verbal forms — no word can capture it — and yet it is made accessible through the divine Name: a Name not invented by human ingenuity but bestowed through divine self-disclosure, God revealing the sacrament of His own Name. And so distraction from the Name is distraction from God Himself, as the Qur'an makes plain when it warns that "the devil hath overcome them, causing them to forget the remembrance of God" (chapter 58, verse 19), and, "O ye who believe, let not your wealth nor your children distract you from the remembrance of God" (chapter 63, verse 9). To turn from the Name in abundance is to turn from God in abundance; to cling to the Name is to cling to Him.

One might still ask why the measure must be abundance — why the Qur'an is not content to command a daily remembrance at fixed hours, as it fixes the hours of prayer, but presses instead for a remembrance without stint. The answer lies in the malady the remedy is meant to cure. Forgetfulness, the book has taught, is not an occasional lapse but a second nature — a standing condition of the soul, the film that returns to the mirror as fast as it is wiped away. A malady so constant cannot be met by an occasional dose. If forgetfulness is the soul's default, the counter to it must be at least as continual as it is, or the ground gained in a moment of remembrance is lost again in the long stretches of heedlessness between. This is why abundance is not excess or scrupulosity but simple proportion: the remembrance must be as unbroken as the forgetting it heals. A person who invokes God for a measured interval and then returns to a wholly unremembering day has treated a chronic condition with a single application, and the rust closes over the mirror before the next. The Qur'an commands much invocation because only much invocation answers to the depth and constancy of the forgetting it is set against.

## The Name as remedy

Why should devotion to the Name be devotion to God? The Imam gives the principle in a single luminous affirmation: "He who loves a thing dedicates himself fervently to its invocation." This is a truth anyone can verify in ordinary experience — the beloved's name is never far from the lover's lips, the name of what we treasure recurs in our speech without our willing it — and the Imam turns it toward God. The lover of God will devote himself utterly to the invocation of God's Name, giving himself to it in abundance. He voices the same pairing in his great supplication to Kumayl, there with an explicit reference to the tongue: my tongue, he says, has devoted itself to Thy invocation. He implores God to make his times in the night and the day inhabited by His remembrance, and to make his tongue invoke Him with utter dedication. And he names the practice, unmistakably, as a practice: "O He whose Name is a remedy and whose invocation is the cure."

That word — remedy — carries the whole burden of this concept, and the Imam presses it exactly as one would press a medical fact. The Name of God is not a name among other names, a sound or symbol among symbols; it is the remedy for man's congenital forgetfulness, the spiritual amnesia that has become, as the book has taught, a kind of second nature. But a remedy has one absolute condition: to work, it must be taken. A medicine only thought about, admired for its efficacy, kept sealed in its bottle, heals no one. It must be swallowed. So too the Name must be actively invoked, not merely contemplated — the healing power is released in the taking, not in the understanding. This is precisely why the Prophet, asked by a man overwhelmed by the multitude of Islam's laws for one thing he could hold to, did not offer a doctrine or a resolution but a bodily instruction: let your tongue never cease to be moist from invoking God. The image is deliberately physical. A dry tongue and a wet one are not two opinions; they are two conditions of the body, and the Prophet locates the whole of what the overwhelmed man needs in the difference between them. The Imam says the same when, describing the greatness of the Qur'an, he observes that it "has made the remembrance incumbent upon your tongues." Remembrance is laid, first of all, upon the tongue — and only through the faithful tongue does it reach and heal the heart.

This is the point at which a certain refined error is most likely to creep in, and it is worth naming plainly. A thoughtful person, having grasped the meanings of remembrance — its polishing of the heart, its rendering-present of the divine — may conclude that what matters is the understanding, and that the outward repetition is a crutch the understanding can dispense with. He may suppose that to hold the meaning of the Name steadily in mind is to have done, in a higher key, what the simple repeater does with his moving lips. The Prophet's answer to the overwhelmed man forecloses exactly this. He did not say, grasp the meaning of My names; he said, keep your tongue moist. The invocation has an operative dimension that cannot be dissolved into a theoretical interpretation of its meanings, however subtle. The reason lies in what the practice is for: not to inform the heart of something it did not know, but to work on the heart, to scour it, and scouring is done by an act repeated, not by a proposition entertained. A medicine understood is not a medicine taken. The tongue's humble labor is not a beginner's substitute for the mind's contemplation; it is the very instrument by which the polishing is performed, and the most learned understanding of remembrance, unaccompanied by the act, leaves the mirror as dull as it found it.

Once the practice is established as an active discipline, the Imam shows that it does not stand alone but enters a living circle with two other movements of the soul, each strengthening the others. The Qur'an itself joins remembrance to reflection when it describes the people of substance: "Truly in the creation of the heavens and the earth, and in the alternation between night and day, there are signs for those of substance, those who invoke God standing, sitting and reclining on their sides, and reflect upon the creation of the heavens and the earth" (chapter 3, verses 190 to 191). Here invocation and reflection — remembrance and meditation — are named in a single breath, and the Imam draws the connection tight: "Whoever invokes God, glorified be He, God enlivens his heart and illumines his inner substance." The practice of invocation polishes the inmost organ of awareness, and the polished organ is better able to reflect on the signs of God in creation and within the soul. And when love is set between the two — for he who loves a thing dedicates himself to its invocation — the three enter a virtuous circle in which each deepens the others. The more seriously one ponders the signs of God and tastes their beauty, the more one comes to love God; the more one loves God, the more one invokes His Name; and the more one invokes, the more keenly one perceives the sacred meaning of those signs. Reflection intensifies invocation, and invocation deepens reflection, and love binds them and is fed by them both.

The Qur'an gives this circle a name for those who move within it — the people of substance, the possessors of the inmost core, whom it praises in the same verse for invoking God in every posture and reflecting on the creation of the heavens and the earth. And the Imam ties the invocation directly to that inmost core: whoever invokes God, he says, God enlivens his heart and illumines his inner substance — the very word the Qur'an uses of these possessors of substance. The circle, in other words, is not a technique laid over an ordinary soul from outside; it works upon and awakens the deepest layer of the person, the core that was made to know God and had forgotten. Reflection without invocation risks becoming mere speculation, the mind turning over the signs of the world as puzzles to be solved rather than messages to be received; invocation without reflection risks becoming mechanical, the tongue moving while the mind sleeps. Bound together by love, each rescues the other. The one who reflects on the signs is moved to invoke their Author; the one who invokes is given eyes to read the signs; and the love that grows between them is the warmth in which both quicken. This is why the practice, far from dulling the intellect, sharpens it — the polished heart reflects more keenly, and the keener reflection feeds a fuller invocation, in a spiral that has no natural end.

This is why the tradition sets no ceiling on the practice, when it sets a ceiling on everything else. Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq drew the contrast exactly: "Everything has a limit at which it terminates, except the remembrance of God. God has made obligatory the religious duties, so whoever fulfils them, that fulfilment is their limit. For the month of Ramadan, whoever fasts therein, that is its limit." The fast ends at sunset and at the month's close; the pilgrimage ends when its rites are done; every obligation has a boundary at which it is complete and beyond which it need not be pressed. Remembrance alone has no such boundary. It is the one practice, al-Sadiq taught, to be carried on in all circumstances and at all times, with no limit set upon it — and he added that his own father never ceased invoking, even while eating and walking. The remembrance the Qur'an commands in abundance is, in the end, a remembrance without terminus: not a rite performed and completed but a condition sustained without end, woven into eating and walking and every waking hour.

## The essence of prayer

This brings us to the boldest of the Imam's claims about remembrance, and it is one that must be handled carefully, for it can be misheard. The Qur'an says that "prayer keepeth one away from lewdness and iniquity, and the remembrance of God is greater" (chapter 29, verse 45). Remembrance is greater than the formal, canonical prayer itself. This does not mean, and the Imam never means, that the ritual prayer may be set aside — he calls remembrance, in another saying, "the food of the spirit and the key to prayer," and a key does not abolish the door it opens. What then does "greater" mean? Shah-Kazemi draws out the answer from the Qur'an's own way of describing the two. The formal prayer is described here in negative, constraining terms: it is a preventative, something that keeps a person away from transgression. Its formality is a ritual necessity, but it is also, unavoidably, a limitation — performed at one time and not another, expressed in certain fixed words and postures and not others, bounded on every side. Remembrance is greater in that it is the positive, enlivening substance of which prayer is the vessel; it is what liberates consciousness from the verbal, mental, and existential limits by which all formal prayer is defined.

The distinction reaches to something the whole tradition treasures. Because the ritual prayer is inescapably bounded, its blessedness can all too easily become confined, in a person's own experience, within those same bounds. One feels close to God during the prayer, at its appointed times, and then feels that closeness lapse the moment the prayer is finished and ordinary life resumes. It is remembrance — in the sense of a constant, recollected awareness of God — that allows the prayer, or rather the consciousness that prayer shapes, to open outward beyond its appointed times into its intended essence: an awareness of the unconditional presence of God at every hour. Remembrance thus transforms a preventative against transgression into a positive apprehension of spiritual reality. One might say, Shah-Kazemi suggests, that remembrance is greater than the ritual prayer in the measure that spiritual awareness transcends moral conscience, or in the measure that essence transcends form. And crucially, the lesser is not negated by the greater but comprised within it, enriched by it, brought to a fullness and scope it could never attain on its own. The formal prayer is not left behind; it is opened up, its blessing set free to flow through the whole of a life.

It is important to feel the balance the Imam keeps here, because the claim can be pulled in a dangerous direction. There have always been those who, hearing that the essence is greater than the form, conclude that the form may be discarded — that one who has attained the inner substance of prayer is excused its outer discipline, free to remember God in the heart and neglect the appointed rite. The Imam allows no such inference, and his own images forbid it. Remembrance is the key to prayer, he says — and a key is not a rival to the door but the very thing that opens it; to prize the key is not to despise the door but to value what the door gives onto. Remembrance is the food of the spirit — and food does not abolish the body it nourishes but sustains it. The relation of essence to form here is not that of a superior thing replacing an inferior one, but that of a soul animating a body: the essence needs the form as its vessel and vehicle, and the form needs the essence as its life. A ritual prayer without remembrance is a body without a soul, a shell of correct motions emptied of the awareness they exist to carry; but remembrance that scorns the ritual prayer is a soul that has thrown away its body, and cannot long survive the loss. The Imam's teaching exalts the essence precisely in order to fill the form, not to empty it — to send the blessing that gathers in the appointed hours pouring outward into every hour, so that the ritual becomes the fountainhead of a continual prayer rather than an island of devotion in a sea of forgetting.

The same verse that joins invocation to reflection also names the postures in which the invocation is to be kept, and the list is telling: those of substance invoke God "standing, sitting and reclining on their sides." Set beside the modes the other verses prescribe — within thyself, in humility, in awe, in secret, beneath thy breath — these postures disclose the supra-formal character of remembrance. The fixed canonical prayer prescribes specific words, specific movements, specific conditions, all of which must be met for the rite to be valid; remembrance, by contrast, is to be performed in every posture, at every time, in every place. It is standing, sitting, and lying down; it is aloud and silent; it is in the mosque and in the marketplace and on the sickbed. Where the ritual prayer is a defined act set into the day, remembrance is something woven into the very texture of a life rather than imposed upon life from without. This is exactly why al-Sadiq could say it alone has no limit: a thing done only in one posture, at one time, under set conditions, terminates when those conditions lapse, but a thing done in all postures at all times has no natural stopping-point at all. The universality of its modes is the outward sign of its inward supremacy — the practice that can be done always is the practice meant to be done always.

This is what makes intelligible a phrase the Qur'an uses that would otherwise puzzle us — its praise of believers who are "perpetually at prayer" (chapter 70, verse 23). Taken of the ritual prayer alone, the phrase is impossible; no one stands in formal prayer without cease. But for the people of substance there is no moment when they are not at prayer, if one understands prayer as the remembrance of God. The Qur'an itself points to this identification when it commands, "establish the prayer for the sake of My remembrance" (chapter 20, verse 14). The very purpose and goal of prayer, its spiritual value and substance, is thus the remembrance of God; the form exists for the sake of the essence. This is also why the Imam can call remembrance "the key to prayer" — the continuous invocation is what unlocks the ritual's inner meaning, the recollected awareness without which the fixed words and postures would remain a shell. The command to "establish the prayer for the sake of My remembrance" reverses the order in which we habitually rank the two. We are inclined to think of remembrance as a good disposition to bring to prayer, a helpful frame of mind that improves the rite; the Qur'an says the opposite — that the prayer is established for the sake of the remembrance, the rite serving the awareness and not the other way around. Prayer is the means; remembrance is the end. The fixed rite is a discipline appointed to plant and water in the soul a thing that must then grow beyond the rite's own bounds, spreading into the unappointed hours until it fills them. A person who prayed with perfect outward correctness and carried nothing of God into the rest of his day would have kept the means and missed the end — would have tended the vessel and left it empty. The whole thrust of the Imam's teaching is to keep the end in view: the prayer is for the remembrance, and the remembrance is for the constant, unconditional awareness of God that is the soul's true home. And it illuminates once more the men the Qur'an described at the outset of this whole discussion, whom neither trade nor merchandise diverts from the remembrance of God: they are not merely punctual in the canonical prayer at its times, but so continually at prayer, in the sense of unbroken remembrance, that their commerce in the marketplace never so much as interrupts their awareness of God. The abundant remembrance the Qur'an commanded turns out to be nothing less than this — a whole life made perpetual prayer, the appointed hours of ritual overflowing into every unappointed hour, until there is no time that is not, in the deepest sense, prayer.

The image of those men is worth dwelling on, for it corrects a misunderstanding that dogs the whole ideal of continual remembrance. One might imagine that to be perpetually at prayer a person must withdraw from the world — abandon trade, forsake the marketplace, retreat to a cell where nothing competes for his attention. But the men the Qur'an praises are men of trade and merchandise; they are in the marketplace, buying and selling, and it is there, amid the noise of commerce, that neither the buying nor the selling diverts them from the remembrance of God. Their continual prayer is not the fruit of leaving the world but of carrying God through it — of a remembrance so deeply set that it survives the very activities that would seem most opposed to it. This is the abundance made perfect: not a remembrance walled off from ordinary life in a protected quiet, but one so woven into the substance of a person that it goes on beneath the transactions of the day like an underground stream beneath a busy street. The withdrawal the practice finally asks is not of the body from the marketplace but of the heart from forgetfulness, and that withdrawal can be accomplished, the Qur'an insists, in the thick of the world's affairs. The night vigil trains it; the marketplace proves it.

## What this episode lands

This episode has come down from the summit of the vision to the ground of the practice, and shown that the ground is where the vision is actually grown. We have heard the Qur'an's commands to invoke the Name of the Lord morning and evening, in humility and in secret and beneath the breath, and above all in abundance — a measure set not by the careful minimum but by the night vigils of the first Muslims, who gave the better part of their nights to it. We have seen why the Imam calls the Name a remedy that must be taken and not merely contemplated, why the Prophet reduced the whole of what an overwhelmed man needed to a tongue kept moist with the Name, and how invocation, love, and reflection turn together in a single circle, each deepening the others, so that the practice has no ceiling and terminates in no limit. And we have weighed the Imam's boldest claim — that remembrance is greater than the formal prayer — learning that it means not the abandonment of prayer but its liberation: remembrance is the living essence that prayer exists to carry, the awareness that frees the ritual's blessing to flow through every hour, until a person becomes, in truth, perpetually at prayer.

What we have not yet asked is the deepest question the practice raises, and it is the one the next episode takes up. We have spoken throughout of a person invoking the Name of God — the lover on his tongue, the servant in his vigil, the man keeping his tongue moist with remembrance. But the Imam drops a strange hint, more than once, that the invocation does not finally originate with the one who seems to be invoking. The Name a person calls upon, he suggests, is uttered first from the side of the One who is called; and the reciprocity the Qur'an names when God says "Remember Me, I will remember you" runs deeper than a simple exchange of favors. Who is the true agent of the invocation? What is the relation between the Name that is spoken and the God who is named by it? This is the point at which the practice we have been describing turns inside out. Everything in this episode has assumed a plain and reassuring picture: a human being, on one side, reaching toward God, on the other, by the labor of his tongue and the abundance of his invocation. The next episode will disturb that picture at its root, not to deny the labor but to disclose what lies beneath it — that the very Name a person raises toward God was first spoken from God's side toward him, and that the reciprocity the Qur'an names runs in an order we would never have guessed. To those questions — the reciprocity of remembrance, and the mystery that the Name is the Named — we turn next. What has looked, through this whole episode, like a person's own diligent reaching toward God will prove to be the near end of a movement whose far end is God Himself, and the discovery will change what the labor of the tongue was all along — and turn the whole practice, at its root, into something other than an act a person performs.
