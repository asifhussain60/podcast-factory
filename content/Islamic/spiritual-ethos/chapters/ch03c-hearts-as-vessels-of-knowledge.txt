# Hearts as Vessels of Knowledge

*Among the sayings of Ali ibn Abi Talib gathered by later masters, few have drawn more commentary across the centuries than the private counsel he gave to his disciple Kumayl ibn Ziyad in a graveyard outside Kufa. In this episode of Reza Shah-Kazemi's study of the Imam's spiritual ethos, we follow that discourse whole — the human heart imagined as a vessel that is only as good as the knowledge it can hold, the division of all people into three kinds, the insistence that knowledge outranks wealth, and the Imam's own lament that he carries a treasure of knowledge he can find no one worthy to bear. From there we watch a single verb — hajama, to storm — open into a whole theory of how knowledge must break past the surface of words and take the heart of meaning by force.*

## Where this episode picks up

What if most of what passes for knowledge never actually arrives — never reaches the one place it was meant to lodge? The Imam's answer begins with an image so plain it is easy to walk past: the human heart is a vessel, a container, and like any container it is worth exactly what it can hold. Some vessels are wide and deep; some are cracked, or shallow, or already full of other things; and most, on this reckoning, sit half-empty while their owners imagine themselves full. That single picture governs everything that follows in this episode, and it turns the ordinary question about knowledge inside out. The question is not first of all what a person has learned. It is what a person is capable of receiving — how much truth the vessel of the heart can take in before it overflows or breaks.

The prior episode built the ground this one stands on. There the Imam unfolded his theology of the intellect, and it was a far larger thing than the reasoning mind we usually mean by the word. The intellect, in his account, is a spiritual faculty — the breathing of the divine spirit into the clay of man, the light by which the soul recognizes its origin. And the truly intelligent person, al-'aqil, is not the cleverest debater but the one who puts each thing in its proper place, who orders his loves and his fears and his acts according to the real weight of things. That definition matters here, because the discourse to Kumayl is about who can carry such an intellect to its depth and who cannot. If the intellect is a light breathed into man, then the heart is the vessel that light must fill — and the question of which vessels can hold it is the question this discourse exists to answer.

Now the scene itself. The setting is spare and deliberate. The Imam takes Kumayl ibn Ziyad by the hand — an intimate gesture, master to disciple — and leads him out of the city of Kufa altogether, past the last of the houses, to a graveyard. Only when the noise and traffic of the living city lie behind them, when the two of them stand among the dead, does he stop. He draws a deep breath, a sigh that the commentators have never quite let go of, and begins to speak. Everything about the staging says that what is coming is not public teaching but private counsel — something meant for one prepared heart, delivered in the one place that strips away every illusion about wealth and rank and the crowd's approval. The graveyard is the great leveler. It is exactly where a discourse about the vanity of hoarded wealth and the endurance of the knowers belongs.

This discourse has been the subject of numerous commentaries through the centuries, and its importance lies, among other things, in its highly esoteric character — its habit of pointing past what it plainly says toward something it will only indicate. In this episode we take it in three movements. First, the discourse itself, rendered whole, in the Imam's own sequence: the vessels, the three kinds of people, the superiority of knowledge to wealth, the lament over his breast, and the promise that the earth is never empty of one who upholds God's proof. Second, we slow down over the heart of the argument — why knowledge stands above wealth, and why the true bearers of that knowledge are always the fewest in number yet the greatest in the sight of God. And third, we follow a single word the Imam chooses at the climax, hajama, to storm, and let it open into his teaching on how knowledge must break past the surface and seize the heart of meaning from within.

## The discourse to Kumayl

When the city lay behind them and the graves stood all around, the Imam drew his deep breath and imparted the following counsel.

O Kumayl ibn Ziyad, truly these hearts are vessels — containers for knowledge and wisdom — and the best of them are those which hold the most. So retain from me that which I say to you. People are divided into three types: a lordly knower, an 'alim rabbani; one who seeks knowledge, a muta'allim, for the sake of deliverance; and the common folk, the hamaj ra'a — a word that names the moth which hurls itself at any flame — following just anyone, swaying with every current, neither desiring to be illumined by the light of knowledge nor seeking refuge in any strong support.

O Kumayl, knowledge is better than wealth, for knowledge guards you, while you must guard your wealth; and wealth diminishes as it is spent, while knowledge only increases as it is disbursed, replenishing itself the more it is given away. The fruits of wealth vanish when the wealth itself is gone — the friends bought with money, the comforts it purchased, all fall away with it — while no one can take a man's knowledge from him. O Kumayl ibn Ziyad, the wise apprehension of knowledge is a religion by which God is worshipped; it is by acquiring knowledge that a man repays the debt his own intellect has incurred to its Maker, and through it he acquires obedience in his life and a good name after his death. And knowledge is a judge, while wealth is judged: knowledge is active and wealth passive, for the one who knows takes command of what he owns, his knowledge directing him how to handle it, whereas wealth is forever something merely ruled upon by its possessor.

O Kumayl ibn Ziyad, those who hoard wealth perish even while they live, but the knowers endure for as long as time itself subsists: their material forms are absent, yet their images in the hearts of men are present. Ah, what abundant knowledge is here — and he pointed to his own breast — would that I could find one to bear it! Yes, I found one who was quick to understand, but he could not be trusted with it, exploiting the tools of religion for the sake of worldly gain, empowering himself with the graces of God against His servants and with His proofs against His friends; for when the vessel of the heart is corrupt, this knowledge — pure and precious as it is — becomes a weapon turned against the very friends of God. Then there was another, obedient to the bearers of truth but lacking heartfelt insight, in whom, at the first appearance of obscurity, doubt was kindled in the heart. So alas, neither this one nor that! Nor one who is greedy for pleasure and submissive to passion, nor one obsessed with acquisition and accumulation — neither of these is a guardian of religion in any respect. They resemble nothing so much as grazing cattle. Thus does knowledge die with the death of its bearers, when its trust falls to the worthless and the self-serving.

But indeed, O my God, the earth will never be empty of one who establishes the proof of God, whether overtly and in the open or fearfully and in obscurity, lest God's proofs and elucidations come to naught. Yet how many are such as these, and where are they? By God, they may be the smallest in number, but with God they are the greatest in rank — for here authority is not the possession of the many, and truth is not settled by the count of those who hold it; the guardians of the proof are few, and it is they who stand highest in the sight of God. Through them God preserves His proofs and elucidations, so that they may entrust them to their peers and sow them in the hearts of those who resemble them. Through them, knowledge penetrates the reality of insight; they take hold of the very spirit of certainty; they make easy what the extravagant find harsh, and they befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged; with their bodies they keep company with the world, while their spirits are bound to the transcendent realm. They are the vicegerents of God upon His earth, the summoners to His religion. Ah, how I long to see them! Go now, Kumayl, as you will.

With that valediction the counsel ends. He does not draw out a moral or repeat himself; he releases his disciple back into the world — "Go now, Kumayl, as you will" — and leaves the rest of the work to the one prepared heart he had led out to the graves. Everything the rest of this episode does is commentary on what he has just said in these few dense paragraphs.

## Knowledge above wealth

Begin where the Imam begins, with the vessels — because the three types of people are not three arbitrary sorts but three answers to the single question the vessel-image raises. If a heart is a container for knowledge and wisdom, then the whole of a person's spiritual standing turns on what that container does with what is poured into it. The first type, the lordly knower, the 'alim rabbani, is the vessel that has been filled and made whole; his knowledge is not borrowed information but a settled state, knowledge that has become a quality of his very being, so that he stands as one who bears the proof of God. The second type, the seeker, the muta'allim, is the vessel in the course of being filled — the one still on the road, but on the road for the sake of deliverance, oriented toward the right end even if he has not yet arrived. And the third, the common rabble, the hamaj ra'a, are the vessels that receive nothing and hold nothing. The Imam's chosen word for them is unforgettable: the moth that hurls itself at any flame. They follow whoever calls, sway with whatever wind is blowing, and seek refuge in no firm support at all — not because they lack the vessel of a heart, but because that vessel is turned to catch every passing gust rather than the one steady light. Notice that only two of the three are on a ladder that rises; the third is not lower on the same ladder but off it entirely, driven by impulse rather than pointed toward any goal.

From this division the Imam turns to his central comparison, and he presses it with the patience of a man laying out an argument he expects to win: knowledge is better than wealth. He does not merely assert it; he proves it feature by feature, and each contrast is exact. First, the matter of guarding. Knowledge guards you, he says, while you must guard your wealth. Turn a coin over in your mind and you feel it at once — wealth is a burden of vigilance, forever needing walls and locks and watchfulness, a thing that makes its owner its servant. Knowledge asks no such guarding; on the contrary, it stands sentry over the one who has it, warning him, directing him, keeping him from ruin. The relation is simply reversed. With wealth, the man protects the possession; with knowledge, the possession protects the man.

Second, the matter of spending. Here the Imam reaches for a paradox that has never lost its force: wealth diminishes as it is spent, while knowledge only increases as it is disbursed. Every coin given away is a coin gone. But every truth taught is a truth more deeply held; the teacher who gives his knowledge away a hundred times finds it clearer and firmer in himself each time, replenishing itself the more it is given. Generosity impoverishes the wealthy and enriches the knowing. The two obey opposite laws. And a third contrast follows from it — the matter of what survives. The fruits of wealth vanish when the wealth itself is gone: the friends money bought, the comforts it purchased, the standing it propped up, all of them fall away the moment the fortune does, because they were never attached to the man but only to his money. Knowledge, by contrast, cannot be taken from him at all. No thief reaches it, no reversal of fortune strips it, no creditor seizes it. It is the one possession fused to the possessor.

Then the Imam lifts the comparison from prudence to worship, and this is the pivot of the whole passage. The wise apprehension of knowledge, he says, is a religion by which God is worshipped. Shah-Kazemi draws out the resonance the Imam is working with here: din, religion, is kin to dayn, a debt. To acquire knowledge is to repay a debt — the debt the intellect itself owes to its Maker for having been given the light to know at all. So knowledge is not merely useful and not merely durable; it is an act of worship, the settling of an account the soul opened at its creation. And its returns run in both directions of a man's existence: through it he acquires obedience in his life — for one cannot obey rightly what one does not rightly know — and a good name after his death, when the body is gone but the image remains.

Hold that phrase — the wise apprehension of knowledge, ma'rifat al-'ilm — a moment longer, because it is doing careful work. The Imam does not say that mere information is a form of worship; he says that the wise apprehension of it is. There is a way of possessing facts that never rises to worship at all, and there is a way of knowing that is itself an act of obedience, a bending of the whole self toward the truth it has grasped. The difference between the two will become the entire subject of the discourse's climax. For now it is enough to see that the Imam has quietly raised the stakes: to pursue knowledge rightly is not a preliminary to the religious life but already, in itself, a discharge of the debt religion exists to settle.

The Imam then sets down the sharpest of his formulas: knowledge is a judge, while wealth is judged. It is a single clean line and it carries an entire hierarchy. Knowledge is active; wealth is passive. The one who knows takes command of what he owns, his knowledge telling him how to use it, when to hold and when to give, where the thing belongs in the order of his life. Wealth has no such capacity; it is inert, forever waiting to be ruled upon by whoever possesses it, unable to direct even itself. So the man of knowledge governs his wealth, but the man of wealth without knowledge is governed by nothing wiser than appetite. Put the two in one person and knowledge sits on the bench while wealth stands in the dock. The ordering is not a matter of taste; it is a matter of which faculty commands and which is commanded.

And this is why, the Imam continues, the fates of the two kinds of men diverge so completely. Those who hoard wealth perish even while they live — a startling phrase, for they are breathing and walking and counting their stores, and yet by the measure that matters they are already gone, because they have staked their whole selves on the one thing that dies with them and adds nothing to the world's stock of light. The knowers, by contrast, endure for as long as time itself subsists. Their bodies are absent — buried, perhaps, in a graveyard like the one the Imam and Kumayl are standing in — but their images live on in the hearts of men, carried forward, taught onward, present long after the hoarders and their hoards have alike turned to dust. It is a quiet devastation of everything the marketplace calls success.

All of this is prelude to the passage the commentators love most: the Imam's lament. Ah, what abundant knowledge is here, he cries — and points to his own breast — would that I could find one to bear it. Here the man who has just proven knowledge the supreme possession confesses that its supremacy is also its tragedy, because a treasure this pure needs a vessel this rare, and the vessel is almost never to be found. He runs through the candidates the way a man sorts through keys that will not fit the lock. There was one quick to understand — the sheer intelligence was there — but he could not be trusted, for he would turn the tools of religion to worldly gain, arming himself with the graces of God against God's own servants, wielding the very proofs of God against His friends. This is the most chilling figure in the discourse: knowledge as a weapon in a corrupt hand, the sacred learning turned against the sacred, precisely because the vessel that held it was foul. Then there was another, obedient and willing, but lacking heartfelt insight, so that at the first shadow of obscurity doubt caught in his heart like a spark in dry grass. And then the lowest sort, greedy for pleasure, submissive to passion, obsessed with getting and keeping — of these the Imam says flatly that they resemble nothing so much as grazing cattle. So knowledge dies with the death of its true bearers whenever its trust falls to the worthless; the treasure is not lost because there is no treasure, but because there is no worthy vessel to receive it.

Yet the discourse does not end in despair, and this is the turn Shah-Kazemi asks us to dwell on. But indeed, O my God, the Imam says, the earth will never be empty of one who establishes the proof of God — whether overtly and in the open, or fearfully and in obscurity — lest God's proofs and elucidations come to naught. Rare as the true bearer is, he is never wholly absent; the world is never for a moment left without at least one who upholds the divine proof, even if he must do so in hiding. And then the Imam sets down the principle that overturns every worldly measure of authority. How many are such as these, and where are they? By God, he answers, they may be the smallest in number, but with God they are the greatest in rank — for authority here is not the possession of the many, and truth is not settled by counting the heads that hold it. This is the passage Shah-Kazemi lingers over: the description of the lordly knower, the 'alim rabbani, as those fewest in number yet greatest in the sight of God, and through whom His religion is sustained. And by "His religion" the Imam means something precise — not the outward, historical religion whose institutions shift with the times, but the divine reality of religion itself, the pure and immutable essence of the faith, perfect and unchanging. From Adam to the Seal of the prophets the outward forms have altered with the conditions of men, yet the essence has never changed: the salvation of the soul through willing submission to God. As the Quran declares, "Truly, religion in the sight of God is submission" (chapter 3, verse 19). It is only through the knowledge of the true knowers that religion stays true to itself, and only through such sages that it truly lives in the world at all. The few, in other words, are not a remnant clinging to the edges of the faith. They are the channel through which the faith remains itself.

## Storming the heart of meaning

The climax of the discourse is a single sentence, and the whole of Shah-Kazemi's commentary turns on one word inside it. Through them, the Imam says, knowledge penetrates the reality of insight. It is worth noticing exactly what he does and does not say. He does not say that their knowledge penetrates the reality of insight, as though the achievement belonged to the knowers as a private possession. He says that through them knowledge penetrates — the knowers are the channel, the means by which knowledge itself breaks through to something deeper than the surface it usually rests on. Knowledge, on this account, can remain merely abstract, sitting at a distance from the reality of insight, a set of true statements a man holds without ever being changed by them. There is the outward, surface knowledge of a thing — its zahir — and there is the inner, hidden knowledge of the same thing — its batin. And knowledge carries within it a potential that calls out to be realized, a charge that begs to be released: it needs to be enlivened so as to press past the words and concepts that make up its own outer form, past the meaning that lies ready on the surface, to the meaning that waits underneath.

The Imam gives an everyday illustration of the gap. A man may have a perfectly good surface knowledge of a verse of the Quran, of a saying, of why he prays and fasts and how many cycles each prayer requires — all of it accurate, all of it examinable. But let him press past that surface, let him ask what the bowing and the prostration actually represent, what the fast is really doing to the soul, and a world of knowledge opens that is wholly unlike the bare information he began with. The facts were never wrong. They were simply the outermost shell of a reality that runs down through layer after layer. Degrees of knowledge are plainly implied here, and with them the refusal of all literalism, all superficiality, the whole facile habit of mistaking factual information for actual knowledge, let alone for wisdom. And the shift of consciousness this penetration requires is shown, Shah-Kazemi points out, by the Imam's own gesture in the lament — his pointing toward his breast. For it is not the chest that holds knowledge but the heart hidden behind it; the chest is the outward form, the zahir that conceals the heart in its batin, and to reach the heart one must first break through the chest. The gesture is itself the teaching.

Now comes the word on which everything turns. The verb the Imam uses, translated here as "penetrate," is hajama, and its primary sense is not gentle at all: it means to assault, to take by storm, to charge. Shah-Kazemi asks us to hear it against the warfare of the age. The battle lines were drawn as a right wing, a left wing, and a center — and the center was called the heart of the army, the qalb al-'askar, the place where the king or the commander stood. The whole aim of an assault was to break through the wings and reach that heart, for once the king was taken the battle was over and the victor took command of everything the defeated had held: the kingdom, its treasury, its army, its people. The chessboard, Shah-Kazemi notes, preserves the very same rule — take the heart of the position and all its resources fall to you. So when the Imam says that through the true knowers knowledge storms the reality of insight, he is telling us that there is in conceptual knowledge a latent force, almost an explosive power, deriving from the reality of knowledge itself — its haqiqa, its spiritual essence. Knowledge is not the inert catalogue of facts it looks like from outside. It is a charge waiting to be detonated toward the heart of meaning.

That is why two words that share the same root become so important here. The latent force of knowledge calls out to be made real through tahaqquq — realization in depth, as opposed to mere verification on the surface. And the striving by which a person storms the outer wall of words to reach the heart of their meaning is tahqiq, true investigation. The distinction is the whole difference between two ways of holding a truth. One man verifies a proposition — confirms that it is so, files it away, and is unchanged. Another realizes it — storms past its verbal wall, seizes the reality it names, and is transformed by what he has taken. The haqiqa, the essence, can be indicated by the words and concepts that make up its communicable form, but it can never be captured by them, just as the chest can be opened to reach the heart but the chest is not the heart. The words are the wall; the meaning is the king within.

It is worth pausing on how much the military image is being asked to carry, because it is not a decoration but the whole logic of the passage. In the warfare the Imam and his listeners knew, reaching the heart of the army was not one objective among several; it was the objective, the single point on which the entire battle turned, and everything else — the wings, the skirmishing, the maneuver — existed only to open a road to it. Transpose that back onto knowledge and the lesson is severe. The words and concepts of a teaching, the citations a man can recite and the definitions he can reproduce, are the wings and the outworks; they are real and they must be engaged, but they are not the prize. To stop among them, satisfied, is to win the flanks and never reach the king — to mistake the taking of the outer positions for victory when the commander still stands untouched at the center. This is exactly the complacency the Imam is warning against, and it explains why he reaches for so violent a verb to describe an act we usually imagine as quiet and studious. Real knowing, on his account, has the structure of a charge that does not stop until the heart is taken.

There is a further edge to the distinction that Shah-Kazemi is careful not to let slide past. It would be easy to hear "sight versus insight" and assume that insight is simply the safe, superior faculty — that once a man has any inner perception at all he has crossed to the right side of the line. The Imam does not allow that comfort. Even insight, he warns, can remain at the surface; there is an ordinary insight that goes a little beneath the visible and then stops, resting content with a slightly deeper appearance, no more stormed to its root than the abstract knowledge it looks down upon. The reality of insight is something else again — the intelligence opened to reality as such, not to one more layer of representation. The degrees of knowledge, in other words, do not run out after the first descent below the surface. They continue down, and the danger of stopping too soon attends every level, not merely the lowest.

By means of this inner force — which the outer conceptual form of knowledge at once hides and evokes — knowledge is able to shift a man's consciousness from one degree of knowing to another. And the change is not academic; it reaches into how the world itself appears. A man in whom knowledge has stormed the heart begins to see the very realities of this world differently from those who rest in the surface play of cause and effect, so that the same act which is a heavy burden to one person is a delight to another. An indirect, mental conception of reality paves the way for a direct, existential perception of it. This, Shah-Kazemi says, is precisely the difference between sight and insight. Sight reaches only the outward, superficial reality; the reality of insight opens the intelligence to reality as such — and even ordinary insight, like abstract knowledge, can remain stuck at the surface if it is never stormed to its depth. The Imam himself expresses the same idea elsewhere in a saying about the understanding that comes to plumb, and so truly to know, the buried treasures of knowledge: he who understands knows the depth of knowledge. And the effort such knowing demands, as against the mere piling up of facts, he indicates in another saying — that it is through learning and assimilating knowledge, until it becomes deeply rooted in one's consciousness, that knowledge is truly attained. Not registered. Rooted.

Which brings the whole movement to its point. The urgency of releasing this immanent power within knowledge — of moving from the abstract to the concrete, the potential to the actual, the static to the dynamic, the formal to the essential — is exactly what the war-word hajama is chosen to evoke. It warns against a double complacency: against taking the first understanding of an idea for its full comprehension, and against taking even its full intellectual comprehension for its living interiorization in the spirit. For when knowledge at last storms the surface of religion and takes the heart, a transformation begins from within. The heart, now illumined by the realities it has seized, takes command of the limbs and the actions — which are the soul's own resources — exactly as the victor takes command of the kingdom he has won. This is the deepest sense in which knowledge must be interiorized rather than merely collected. Interiorization is a process of infinite unfolding within, not the filing-away of some fact from without. It demands the effective integration of knowledge within one's very being: knowledge from without must be transformed into being from within, woven into the deepest texture of a person's existence. And so the Imam's whole discourse arrives at a demand that is total. Knowledge does not ask merely for the attention of the mind. It asks for the entire orientation of the soul.

## What this episode lands

Stand back from the graveyard scene and take stock of what the Imam has established in these few dense paragraphs. He has given us the governing image of the whole discourse: the heart as a vessel, worth exactly what it can hold, so that the measure of a person is not what he has been told but what he is able to receive and keep. From that image he has drawn the three kinds of people — the lordly knower who has been filled, the seeker still rightly on the road, and the rabble driven like a moth at every flame. He has proven, feature by feature, that knowledge stands above wealth: it guards its owner rather than needing to be guarded, it grows rather than shrinks in the spending, it cannot be taken away, and above all it is the judge while wealth is merely judged. He has confessed the tragedy folded inside that supremacy — that a treasure so pure needs a vessel so rare that he can point to his own breast and lament finding no one worthy to bear it — and he has promised, against all appearances, that the earth is never for a moment emptied of one who upholds the proof of God. And he has let a single war-word, hajama, open the final teaching: that knowledge is a latent force which must storm the wall of words and take the heart of meaning by force, transforming a man from within until his heart commands his limbs as a victor commands a conquered kingdom.

But that last image also marks where this episode has to stop, because it raises a question it does not yet answer. If knowledge must be interiorized — if it must be transformed from information held in the mind into being woven through the whole texture of a person — then what stands in the way? Why do so few vessels ever fill? The Imam's own catalogue of failed bearers already hinted at the answer: the one who exploited religion for gain, the one submissive to passion, the one greedy for pleasure and lost in accumulation. In every case it was not the intellect that failed but the soul — the appetites, the self-regard, the crookedness of a heart bent toward itself. So the storming of the heart of meaning cannot be the work of the mind alone. It requires the whole soul, and it requires the uprooting of the egocentric vice that keeps the vessel foul and the wall standing. That is the next front in the Imam's teaching, and it is the greater struggle — the jihad against the soul itself — to which everything in this discourse has quietly been pointing. The knowledge has been shown to be a conquest. The next episode asks what army it takes to win it.
