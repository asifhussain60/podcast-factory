This closing episode follows the remembrance of God to its final mystery: the relationship between the Name that is invoked and the God who is named by it. We take up the bold Sufi maxim that the Name is the Named, the seeming contradiction of the Imams who deny it, the resolution that holds both together, the astonishing claim that the friends of God become themselves the most beautiful Names — and, at the last, the death of Ali (ع) with the invocation on his tongue, in which the whole teaching of this book is gathered into a single act.

## Where this episode picks up

When a person invokes the Name of God, is he merely uttering a word that points at a reality beyond his reach — or is he, in some real sense, already touching the reality itself? Everything in this book has been driving toward that question, and this episode answers it. The last episode ended by unveiling God as the true one who invokes, and by understanding the creative Word — the calling of things into being — as itself a kind of remembrance. But that raises the sharpest question of all about the practice at the center of the whole mystical tradition. If remembrance works its transforming power through the invocation of a Name, then everything depends on what a Name actually is. Is the Name of God a label, arbitrary and external, like the names we give to ordinary things? Or is it charged with the very reality it names, so that to invoke it is to make that reality present? The Sufis gave the boldest possible answer — the Name is the Named — and the Shi'i Imams appear flatly to deny it. Everything turns on which of them is right, for if the Name is only a label the whole practice of remembrance is a kind of pointing at an absent God, while if the Name is charged with the reality it names, then to invoke it is already to have arrived. Following that tension to its resolution will carry us to the summit of the whole teaching: the doctrine of the divine Names, the effacement of the self within the Name, and the mystery of the friends of God who become the Names they invoke.

## The Name is the Named

Start with the difference between two kinds of name. The names we give to things, and the names the idolaters gave to their gods, are arbitrary labels — signs with no intrinsic bond to the things they mark, still less to the realities underlying those things. The Qur'an says exactly this of the idols: they are but names that you have named, you and your fathers, for which God has revealed no authority (chapter 53, verse 23). A false god's name is empty precisely because it is a human invention pinned onto nothing. The Names of God are of a wholly different order, because they are not human inventions at all. Call upon God, or call upon the Merciful, the Qur'an says; by whatever Name you call upon Him, His are the most beautiful Names (chapter 17, verse 110). These Names are revealed by God, and revelation is the point. Because God Himself has disclosed them, they are penetrated by the very substance of the reality they denote; they carry, in the Qur'an's word, an authority — a sultan — by virtue of which they have the power not merely to inform the mind but to transform the soul. They are, one might say, charged with a real presence: the capacity to render the immanent reality of God present to heart and mind.

This is why the tradition can say that the Name is the Named. The great supplication attributed to the Imam, the supplication of the lame man, consists of a hundred and twenty-two verses crowded with divine Names, attributes, and titles, and near its end comes a verse that is the key to the whole: I ask Thee by every Name with which Thou hast named Thyself. That is the decisive phrase — with which Thou hast named Thyself. It is the fact that God names Himself that gives His Names their transforming power, their sacramental substance, their mystical efficacy. A name a creature invents is a mere sound stuck onto a thing from outside; a Name God gives Himself is a self-disclosure, and to invoke it is to lay hold of something of the reality that disclosed itself in it. This is the ground of the whole practice of remembrance: the invocation works because the Name is not other than the reality it names. As followed in an earlier episode, the Name revealed from above is mirrored by a name that resounds from within the heart, and when the rust is rubbed from the heart's surface the two meet — light upon light. The remembrance is powerful because it joins a Name that God gave to a heart that God made to receive it.

There is a further depth here, and it belongs to the mystics' account of how a soul comes to be at all. Every soul, on this vision, has its own particular divine Name that engenders and sustains it — its Lord in the most intimate sense, the divine support at the very root of its being. Ibn al-'Arabi puts it vividly: every servant has a Name which is its Lord, and the servant becomes, as it were, the body of that Name, while the Name is its heart. A thing comes into existence when the divine Word turns toward its latent possibility and breathes being into it, and what shape that being takes is determined by the possibility itself — the way a prism gives its own form to the light passing through it, or, in an image the tradition loved, the way water takes on the color of the cup that holds it. The Name is the light; the soul is the cup. This means that when a person invokes a Name of God, he is not reaching for something foreign to himself; he is invoking the very reality at his own root, the divine support that is more truly his heart than his own ego is. The remembrance is a homecoming to the Name one already, secretly, is. And this is why the invocation can undo the invoker — for to sink into the Name is to sink toward one's own ground, where the separate self was never anything but the cup, and the water is God's.

The mystics found the same truth foreshadowed in the Imam's discourse to his disciple Kumayl, followed earlier in this series, in which he describes the reality as a light dawning from the morning of eternity, its traces shimmering upon what he calls the temples of tawhid — the loci where the one Light discloses itself in the many. Each soul is one such locus, one temple, a place where God shows Himself in a particular and unrepeatable mode. To invoke the Name is to let that showing come to its fullness.

And yet — here the difficulty arises that this episode exists to face — the Shi'i Imams themselves are recorded denying, in the plainest terms, that the Name is the Named. Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq is reported to have said: the Name of God is other than Him. Everything to which the name of a thing applies is a creation, and therefore other than God; whatever the tongue expresses or the hand works upon is a created thing, and the Creator cannot be confined within the limits of a named object. He presses the point with a stark argument: God has ninety-nine Names, and if the Name were the Named, then every one of those Names would be a god — an intolerable multiplicity of deities. Rather, he insists, God is the one meaning, the single spiritual reality, indicated by all the Names, and every one of the Names is other than Him. On its face this contradicts the Sufi maxim head-on. One tradition says the Name is the Named; the other says the Name is emphatically not the Named. How can both be true?

The resolution turns on seeing that the word Name is being used in two different senses. Ibn al-'Arabi draws the distinction with great precision. A Name, he says, has two connotations. In the first, the Name is nothing other than God Himself, the very reality that is named; in the second, the Name is that by which one Name is distinguished from another — the specific meaning that marks the Merciful off from the All-Conquering. Taken in the first sense, as essentially identical with the Named, the Name is the Reality itself; taken in the second sense, as this particular determination and not that one, the Name is a kind of imagined reality, real only as a facet, a relation, a way the one Reality engages the created world. The Names, in Ibn al-'Arabi's vision, have no separate existence of their own; they all revert to the single reality of the divine Essence. And this dissolves Imam Ja'far's objection exactly. The Names do not imply many gods, because in the respect in which each Name is a distinct meaning — the respect in which there are ninety-nine of them — each is precisely not the Essence, but a determination, a relation, an imagined reality that reverts to the One. It is only in the other respect, as identical with the Essence they all name, that the Name is the Named — and in that respect there are not ninety-nine but only One. A lexicon of the Sufis attributed to al-Kashani puts it with a clean example. Take the Name the Living. Considered simply as manifest being, it is identical with the Essence; but considered as this particular meaning, namely Life, it is other than the Essence. And so, the lexicon concludes, one understands the saying that the Name is not identical to the Named, nor other than it. There is a distinction of concept and word, in which the Name is not the Named; and there is a spiritual identity, in which the Name is the Named. The two traditions were never in conflict; they were speaking of the Name in its two senses.

## The Imams as divine Names

This resolution rests on a way of thinking about the divine attributes that the Imam himself set out, in the first of his sermons, with startling force — and it is worth following, because it is the key that unlocks the most audacious claims in the whole tradition. The perfection of sincerity, the Imam says, is to divest God of all attributes; for every attribute bears witness that it is other than the thing it is attributed to, and every such thing bears witness that it is other than the attribute. So whoever ascribes an attribute to God has conjoined Him with something else; and whoever conjoins Him has made Him twofold; and whoever makes Him twofold has fragmented Him; and whoever fragments Him is ignorant of Him. Read carelessly, this sounds like a denial that God has any attributes at all. But that is not what the Imam means, and the same sermon elsewhere plainly affirms the reality of the divine attributes, speaking of the One whose attribute has no binding limit. What the Imam negates is not the attributes themselves but the attributes conceived as separate, super-added realities, standing over against the Essence as though they were something other than it. Since no divine attribute can really be distinct from the divine nature, and since that nature is infinite, every attribute is likewise infinite, and so bursts every boundary that human conception and language would put around it. In one respect the attribute is to be affirmed — as not other than the Essence; in another respect it is to be negated — insofar as we conceive it in itself, as a thing on its own.

Now apply that same double logic, not to the divine attributes, but to the human soul, and the deepest teaching of the tradition comes into view. On its own account, considered as a separate thing standing on its own, the individual is nothing — pure nothingness, as the last episode found. But considered in respect of its divine ground, the Name that engenders and sustains it, its true and inmost reality, the soul is not other than the one Real. Ibn al-'Arabi states the paradox at its sharpest: the final end of the knowers of God, he says, is that the Real is identical with them, while they themselves do not exist. Both halves must be held together. Insofar as the knower is aware that he does not exist — that on his own account he is nothing — he knows that the Real is his true identity. His realization of absolute Reality goes hand in hand with the realization of his own non-existence; the two are one insight seen from two sides. Ibn al-'Arabi describes reaching this at the climax of his own visionary ascent, when God stripped from him his contingent dimension and he attained the inner realities of all the Names, and saw them all returning to a single Subject and a single Entity — and came to know, in that moment, that he was a pure servant, without a trace of lordship in him at all. The height of the ascent is not a self-deification but its exact opposite: the discovery of one's own utter servitude, and precisely thereby of the Reality that alone truly is.

It is worth pausing on how radical the Imam's negation of the attributes really is, because it is the hinge of everything that follows. He is asking, on the plane of human thought, for a genuine apophasis — a stripping-away even of the divine qualities as we conceive them, mercy and power and life and the rest, insofar as we take them to be distinct realities we can hold in the mind. Not because those qualities are unreal, but because every concept we form of them draws a boundary, and the divine reality of each is boundless. To think God is compassionate, and to stop there, as though compassion were a bounded thing added to a bounded God, is already to have shrunk Him. The Imam's demand is that thought let go of its own limits, so that consciousness can begin to fathom a transcendence no concept can contain. Only on the far side of that letting-go — the same letting-go the previous episode traced as the extinction of the self — do the divine qualities appear as what they truly are: not separate things, but the one infinite Essence, seen now under this aspect and now under that.

This is what makes sense of the most shocking sayings in the whole tradition — the Imam's own declaration, reported of Ali (ع), that we are the most beautiful Names. And it is what makes sense of the ecstatic utterances of the great Sufis: al-Hallaj crying out that he is the Real, Bayazid exclaiming glory to me. To the outward ear these sound like blasphemy, a creature usurping the place of God. But in the light of the Imam's teaching on the attributes, they mean the reverse of what they seem. When the Imam appears to speak of his own greatness, it is not his own greatness he is claiming; it is the greatness of God being expressed through him as a locus of manifestation, a place where the divine shows itself — and expressed precisely in the measure of his own effacement in that greatness. The saints are, in the tradition's word, annihilated in the divine Essence, just as the attributes are; there can be no question of any private, individual appropriation of the glory of God, because there is no longer a private individual there to appropriate it. The perfect man — the Prophet foremost, and the friends of God after him — reflects the divine Names and qualities exactly because his own self has been polished away, so that nothing of his egoity clouds the mirror. The more the divine Essence is exalted beyond all Names, the more brilliantly those Names shine in the mirror the perfect man has become.

It is essential to see that this effacement does not cancel the saint's servitude to God — it perfects it. One might expect that a soul which has come to know the Real as its own true identity would have left servanthood behind, risen above the humble station of the worshipper. The tradition insists on the opposite. The friends of God realize their servitude most completely at the very moment of their effacement, for it is only when their individuality has been annihilated that their true identity as the Named can shine through, and that identity belongs to God, not to them. Like the divine attributes, which are annihilated in the Essence they never really stood apart from, the saints are annihilated in the divine Reality — and just for that reason there can be no private seizing of God's glory, no creature setting itself up beside the Creator. Ibn al-'Arabi found this at the summit of his own ascent, when he came to know himself a pure servant with not a trace of lordship in him at all. The higher the friend of God rises, the more purely a servant he becomes; the exaltation and the servitude are not opposites but a single reality, and it is only the outward eye, which sees the mirror and not the image, that mistakes the one for a denial of the other.

'Attar preserves a saying of Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq that catches this with perfect economy. Accused of pride, the Imam answered: I am not proud, for pride belongs to the divine greatness; since I have gone beyond the pride of self, His pride has come and taken the place of my own. Where the pride of self is present, there can be no divine greatness; but through the divine greatness, a kind of pride will be present. The divine qualities shine with greater, not lesser, brilliance through a soul rendered transparent to the Real — a transparency won by the dissolving of every cloud of egoism and passion. This is the resolution of the whole apparent scandal of the saints' utterances. Far from being tainted with the association of a creature with God, which is the cardinal sin, these sayings reach the very deepest application of the divine oneness. The one who has been effaced does not compete with God for glory; he has become a clear window through which God's own glory passes undimmed. As followed earlier in this series in the Imam's discourse to his disciple Kumayl, the one Light of God dawns upon the differentiated loci of manifestation, the temples of tawhid — and the friends of God are the clearest of those loci, the mirrors that have been most perfectly cleared.

## Dying with the Name

All of this at last resolves the great saying of drawing near that has run through the whole book — the saying in which God declares that His servant draws near to Him through the voluntary devotions until He loves him, and that when He loves him He becomes the servant's hearing, sight, hand, and foot. There was a paradox buried in it. On one side, God says that nothing He loves is greater than the obligatory acts He has laid down; on the other, it is the voluntary, supererogatory devotions that carry the servant into the intimacy where God becomes his very faculties. How can the freely offered devotion lead to a love that seems greater than the love reserved for the obligatory? The resolution is this. God's love for the obligatory acts is a love directed toward something apart from Himself — the acts of a servant still standing as a servant. But God's love for the servant himself, once that servant has been effaced in his Lord, is a love of another order entirely, because it partakes of God's love for His own self-manifestation, and so of His love for Himself. In the beginning, the tradition says, God loved to be known, and so He created the world — I was a hidden treasure, runs the divine saying, and I loved to be known. In coming to know Himself through the effaced servant, the apparent other, God's own longing to be known is fulfilled; and the servant's greatest glory turns out to be exactly his own emptiness, his poverty, his effacement of self, through which alone the hidden treasure comes to light.

It is in this sense that a startling saying of the Imam is to be understood: down from me torrents pour, he says, up to me no bird can soar. The torrents flow down from him because — and in the exact measure that — he is extinguished in the source of the torrent; he gives abundantly because he keeps nothing back for a self. The image recalls the Qur'anic description of the friends of God who drink from the celestial spring, causing it to gush forth abundantly (chapter 76, verses 5 and 6). They make the spring flow more, the tradition explains, because they are themselves the sources of that spring — there is no otherness, no duality left in them to dam it; were it otherwise, were there still the darkness of the veil of egoity, it would not be that pure spring at all. Here we reach the final degree of the remembrance, the one the tradition names last: the remembrance of the Essence, which is essential vision, attained through the removal of duality. The remembrance polishes away not only the impurities of the heart but, in the end, through the grace of the One remembered, all trace of duality itself — including that most stubborn duality of all, the individual self standing over against God. It removes every subtle, hidden association of partners with God, the phantom others that have no existence before the One that has no second, and it establishes the pure oneness in which the transfigured heart sees the light of the one Reality reflected in every mirror of creation. To make the one Reality fully real is, by the same stroke, to realize the mere apparent-reality, the unreality, of everything else.

At this height there is a change in what knowledge itself means. So long as a self stands facing God, there is a knower here and a known there, and knowledge is the bridge between them. But when the self has been taken out of the equation, there is no longer one consciousness replacing another; there is only pure consciousness, which cannot belong to any particular entity without being cut down to that entity's measure. Being conscious of the Absolute is transmuted into absolute consciousness. The Real is realized not by another but through another — and this is why the tradition calls the one who has arrived not simply the knower of God but the knower through God, the creature having become so poor, so empty of self, that God's knowledge of Himself passes through him without obstruction. Recall the Imam's command, followed at the beginning of this whole meditation: know God through God. At its deepest level, knowledge of God is the Real knowing itself through a creature grown perfectly transparent — and to say through, rather than by, is to name the unimpeded passage that opens only where the self is no longer in the way.

There is a reason the revealed Name has this power to draw a soul back to its ground, when the whole created cosmos, for all that it too manifests God, does not carry the same transforming charge. The world and the scripture are both, in a sense, revelations of God — the cosmos an indirect one, every thing in it a word, and the revealed Name a direct one. But between them there is no common measure. The difference, the tradition says, is like the difference between shadow and light. Revelation is a projection of the pure Light of God, of one substance with the source from which it streams; the created cosmos is the shadow that light casts. Both are projections of one reality, but a shadow cannot do what light does. In Ibn al-'Arabi's words, the very Being of the Real is a verified Light, and the existence of creation is a shadow following after. This is why the practice of remembrance fastens on the revealed Name and not on the general spectacle of the world: the Name is light where the world is shadow, and only light can dispel the darkness of forgetfulness in the heart.

It is with a passage from the Imam's most famous and most disputed sermon, the sermon of the clarifying declaration, that this teaching reaches its peak — and its most shocking expression. I am the Sign of the All-Powerful, the Imam says there; I am the gnosis of the mysteries; I am the companion of the radiance of the divine Majesty; I am the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden; I am the Face of God; I am the mirror of God. To the outward mind this can only sound like the blasphemy of a man deifying himself in the manner of al-Hallaj. But one phrase is the key that unlocks all the rest: I am the gnosis of the mysteries. The Imam is not identifying himself with God; he is identifying himself with the knowledge of God — and not with his own knowledge of God, which would still be the relative knowledge a creature has, but with knowledge itself, the knowledge God has of Himself. Only knowledge of that kind can be fully identical with its object, for at that transcendent level the distinctions between knower, knowing, and known dissolve, just as the distinction between knowledge and being ceases to hold. To be the knowledge of God is, by a kind of metaphysical resonance, to be everything that God is — for a consciousness that is wholly one with the Absolute is nothing other than the radiance of the divine Majesty. This is how the Imam can say I am the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden: not as a man claiming divinity, but as the gnosis of the mysteries claiming its natural inheritance. And the claim to be the Face of God, and the mirror of God, points the same way. A mirror loses its own identity when the eye fixes only on the image within it; the mirror is then identified with what it reflects, without ceasing to be a mirror and without the Face reflected in it ceasing to be wholly independent of it. Forgetting the mirror is the effacement of the self; fixing on the image within it is the remembrance of God. The friend of God is the mirror in which the Face is seen — precisely because he has ceased to draw attention to the mirror.

There is an inescapable perplexity at this summit, and it is worth naming honestly rather than smoothing over. The very elevation of the divine Essence beyond all comprehension is what makes it inescapable that creaturely consciousness must be utterly annihilated if that Essence is to be realized at all. On one side, one knows God by knowing that His Essence is unknowable — utterly other, utterly transcendent, lying beyond the veil that creation itself constitutes. On the other side, the distinction between creature and Creator is effaced the moment the creature has genuinely forgotten itself, not as an idea entertained but existentially, in real contemplative extinction. These two are not a contradiction to be resolved by cleverness; they are the two faces of a single realization that only the extinguished self can hold. To know God as unknowable and to be effaced before Him are not two acts but one, and the perplexity that accompanies them is not confusion but the mind's honest register of a reality that exceeds it on every side.

And this yields the last and most beautiful paradox of the whole book: that the deepest effacement of the self goes hand in hand with the most perfect flowering of the human. The friends of God, who know themselves to be nothing before the Absolute, are exactly the ones who reflect the divine Names and qualities most faithfully — qualities that belong not to them but to God. This is why the great saints and sages are also the great exemplars of virtue, for the true foundation of every virtue is one of the most beautiful Names of God, and those Names shine through them undimmed because the mirrors of their hearts have been polished by remembrance until the most stubborn stains, the stains of egotism, are gone. Their creativity, their goodness, is raised to its highest pitch precisely because nothing of self obstructs the divine qualities flowing through them; their hearts, the tradition says, are already in the Gardens of paradise while their bodies still labor in this world. And nowhere is this seen more clearly than in the manner of the Imam's death. When Ali (ع) was struck by the poisoned sword of his assassin, the cry that broke from him was not one of pain but of triumph: I have triumphed, by the Lord of the Ka'ba. At the very threshold of death there was in him nothing but the remembrance of God, and he passed away, it is said, with his tongue still repeating the testimony that there is no god but God. His death was consummated exactly as his life had been lived — in remembrance — fulfilling the Prophet's own answer, when he was asked which of all deeds is the most meritorious: that you die, he said, while your tongue is moist with the invocation of God.

## What this episode lands

This episode has followed the remembrance of God to its final resolution and its final mystery. The Name is the Named, and the Name is not the Named — both are true, once one sees that a Name is at once the very reality it names and a distinct meaning that reverts to that reality; and it is because God names Himself that His revealed Names carry the transforming power on which the whole practice of invocation rests. The same double logic, carried from the divine attributes to the human soul, unlocks the most audacious claims of the tradition: that the individual is nothing on his own account and yet, in respect of his divine ground, not other than the Real; that the friends of God, effaced in Him, become themselves the most beautiful Names, so that their apparent self-exaltation is in truth the deepest confession of God's oneness, the shining of the divine glory through a mirror wiped perfectly clean. And all of it comes to rest in a single image: Ali (ع) dying with the invocation on his tongue, the whole teaching of remembrance gathered into one act — a life so given over to the Name that its last breath was the Name, a self so effaced that only the Remembered remained.

## Closing

And so this long meditation on remembrance closes where it began, at the figure who stands at the head of the whole mystical tradition of Islam. Every noble expression of the spirit of Islam — the poetry and the music, the arts, and above all the spiritual chivalry of the guilds, whose every virtue was traced back to him — was understood to flow from Ali (ع) as from a fountain, and it is fitting that it should be so. For the remembrance this book has followed is not, in the end, a technique or a doctrine but a way of being: the way of a heart so polished that it sees God everywhere, a self so forgotten that God becomes its very sight, a soul so given to the Name that it has become the Name. The whole of creation, the Qur'an says, glorifies its Creator, though we do not understand its glorification (chapter 24, verse 41); every creature in existence is already, in its own way, at remembrance. The friend of God is the one who has awakened to that cosmic remembrance and joined it consciously — and, joining it, has vanished into it, so that there is at last no difference between the one who remembers and the One remembered. That is the summit toward which the entire spiritual ethos of Ali (ع) has been pointing, and it is where this book lays down its pen.
