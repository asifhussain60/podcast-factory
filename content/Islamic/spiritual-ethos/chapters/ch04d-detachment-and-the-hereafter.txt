# Detachment and the Hereafter

*A spirituality forged in the hardest inner warfare arrives, at its far end, not at grimness but at joy. This closing movement of the introduction traces how the certainty won through struggle becomes a felt happiness, an eye for beauty, and a freedom from the world so complete that the sage befriends the very hardships the worldly flee. It follows Reza Shah-Kazemi's reading of Ali ibn Abi Talib (peace be upon him) into the strangest of its claims: that death can be more intimate than a mother's breast to a nursing child, and that the Hereafter is not a distant reward but a life already tasted here and now. Third-person throughout, it lets the Imam's own sayings and the verses of the Qur'an carry the weight. The exposition moves from an epistemology of joy, through the detachment called zuhd, to the abode of true life. It is the last of four episodes on this opening chapter.*

## Where this episode picks up

Why should a path defined by the fiercest struggle against the self end in delight rather than in exhaustion? The previous episode left Ali (peace be upon him) describing the war within the soul as the ultimate battle, and sincerity, ikhlas, as the purity won by fighting it. One might expect the fruit of so severe a discipline to be a kind of stern austerity. What Shah-Kazemi shows instead is that the certainty earned in that struggle blossoms into its opposite: a joy so deep that the sage can greet hardship as a friend and death as a homecoming.

This episode picks up exactly where that hard-won certainty begins to bear fruit. The struggle against the self was never an end in itself; it cleared the ground. Once the ego's false life has been sacrificed, what remains is direct contact with the object of certainty — and that contact, in Ali's teaching, cannot but bestow happiness on the soul that opens to it. The Imam's guidance to his companion Kumayl, which threaded the earlier episodes, reaches its climax here in a single sentence about those who "rejoice in their intimacy with the spirit of certainty."

From that rejoicing the whole closing arc unfolds. Joy leads to beauty, because the intellect that touches the Real perceives beauty inwardly and outwardly, and virtue itself turns out to mean the making-beautiful of the soul. Beauty in turn leads to detachment, because a heart filled with the grandeur of God finds the world small, and can therefore let it go without bitterness. And detachment opens onto the Hereafter — not as a bribe or a threat held over moral conduct, but as a reality the wise assimilate now, so fully that its foretaste already colours their acts and attitudes in this life. The thread running through all of it is a single conviction: that deliverance is not only posthumous. It is accessible here and now, to the pure of heart. This episode follows that conviction to its close.

## An epistemology of joy

The turning point in the discourse to Kumayl is a sentence about happiness. Ali (peace be upon him) says of the true knowers that they "rejoice in their intimacy with the spirit of certainty." Shah-Kazemi lingers on the verb, because in the original its primary sense is to have intimate experience of something, to come into direct contact with it — and the same form carries connotations of joy. To touch the spirit of certainty is not to reach a cold logical conclusion at the end of a mental process. It is to enter deeply into the very object of one's certainty, which is ultimately nothing less than the divine itself.

That is why the contact bestows joy. The reality one touches is more beatific than any conceivable paradisal bliss, for the Qur'an affirms that the divine ridwan — God's good pleasure, His contentment — is "greater" than Paradise itself: "and greater, is God's good pleasure" (chapter 9, verse 72). A soul that opens itself effectively to a reality of that order cannot help but be filled with delight. The Imam states it directly: "Learning and reflecting upon knowledge is the delight of the knowers."

Here is what Shah-Kazemi calls an epistemology of joy. It stands in sharp contrast to the modern picture of knowledge as dry, cold, and abstract. Ali says, "the most joyous of mankind is the intellectual" — the aql, the one who truly understands. Engaging with truths is meant to impart happiness, and it does so exactly in the measure that those truths are fundamental to one's own being, or to Being as such. And these two turn out to be the same. Being is one; that which has no second cannot be divided or counted. So the being of the individual cannot finally differ from Being itself, however vast the disparity between the creature and God. True knowledge, being knowledge of Being, is therefore inevitably a form of self-discovery — even though knowledge of the Absolute is impossible without absolute self-abandonment. The highest discovery calls for the deepest effacement.

It is worth pausing on how sharply this cuts against a familiar modern instinct. Under a scientistic picture, to know something is to stand outside it, to measure and manipulate it from a safe distance; the knower stays cold, the object stays inert, and feeling is treated as noise that clouds the result. Ali's teaching inverts this at the root. The deepest truths are not external objects at all but the very ground of the knower's own being, so that to know them is to draw nearer to oneself, and to draw nearer to oneself, at that depth, is to draw nearer to God. Knowledge of this order cannot leave the knower unmoved, because there is finally no gap across which to remain detached. The warmth is not a distortion of the knowing; it is the knowing arriving at its proper object. This is why Shah-Kazemi insists that the joy is not decoration added to knowledge after the fact — it is the very sign that the knowledge has reached something real.

In its earlier stages, this self-discovery brings the particular happiness of a homecoming: a return from the exile of ignorance. The Imam links the terms tightly — the most intellectual of people is the one who is closest to God, and the one who is most alive. To be near God is to be fully alive, and that fullness of life is itself joy, the sheer joy of living. It is a joy proper to existence as such, and, as the rest of the arc will show, it does not depend on whether particular experiences happen to be pleasant.

This is why the earlier thread about life and consciousness returns here. Those who are true to the spirit of God within them genuinely possess joy, and their joy rests on an unshakeable certainty of the divine goodness and compassion. On that certainty Ali builds his definition of the faqih. The word is usually translated "jurist," but Shah-Kazemi renders it more literally as one who truly understands, and the Imam's definition has nothing juridical about it: the faqih is "he who does not make people despair of the mercy of God, and does not make them lose hope in the gracious spirit of God." The sage is so immersed in the joyous certainty of God's mercy that there is simply no room for despair.

Despair of that mercy is, in fact, a mark of unbelief. The Qur'an asks, "and who despaireth of the mercy of his Lord except those who stray in error?" (chapter 15, verse 56). The divine assurances come fully alive only in souls that know the beatific nature of the Real not notionally but profoundly: "My mercy encompasseth all things" (chapter 7, verse 156), and "thy Lord has prescribed for Himself mercy" (chapter 6, verse 54). These, for Ali, are the ones who truly understand — the real fuqaha. Their understanding is measured not by mastery of rulings but by the depth of their assurance that God's mercy has the first and the last word.

From this opening onto joy through the knowledge of mercy, Shah-Kazemi draws out a second, unexpected concomitant of the intellect: beauty. The perception of beauty, in Ali's perspective, is not merely a matter of subjective taste. It is an objective dimension of the intellect's own functioning. And it leads directly into the heart of ethics through a single Arabic word. Ihsan is often translated simply as "virtue" or "excellence," but one of its key literal meanings is "making beautiful" — for husn means beauty. Virtue, then, can be understood as that which makes the soul beautiful. In this light the relationship among intellect, ethics, and aesthetics becomes clear. The Imam says, "The excellence of the intellect is in the beauty of things outward and inward." Outward beauty of forms and actions must be completed by inward beauty — beauty of soul, which is virtue in its most penetrating sense.

All of this follows from two sayings ascribed to the Prophet Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him). The first: "Truly God is beautiful and He loves beauty." The second: "Assume the character traits of God." To cultivate beautiful traits within oneself is to make oneself lovable to God, since He loves beauty — and the process of becoming lovable to Him is inseparable from emulating the virtues brought to perfection in the soul of the Prophet. The Qur'an ties love to that following: "Say: O ye who believe, if ye love God, follow me; God will love you" (chapter 3, verse 31).

Ali gives this a precise name. He says, "The foundation of the intellect is the assimilation of love," using the form tawaddud rather than the plain word for love. Shah-Kazemi draws attention to the choice. Tawaddud is not love as such; it is the active work of making oneself lovable to God, integrating and realizing the principle of love within oneself, and then expressing outwardly the love experienced inwardly. Love is at once an inner attraction and an outward radiance, and the intellect is founded on the labour of drawing it in and giving it out.

To follow the Prophet, and so become receptive to that divine influx of love, means among other things cultivating the prophetic virtues. They are called prophetic not because they belong to the Prophet alone, but because he realized virtue in all its plenitude and is therefore the exemplar of virtue as such. And so beauty and love are not the only qualities that make the intellect receptive to the Real. Kindness and contentment, courtesy, generosity and modesty, and wise forbearance — hilm — are equally essential. None of these is absent from the soul of the true intellectual. There can be no wisdom in the fullest sense if the soul lacks them.

Their presence is what gives the intellect depth and breadth. Without them it stays confined to the flat, horizontal plane of its own operations — to the activity of reason alone, which, until touched by faith and deepened by grace, remains unaware of what lies beneath its own surface, blind to the hidden treasures buried within it. The virtues let the intellect expand to its full dimensions. Shah-Kazemi frames this as a two-way movement: truth must inspire the soul, but equally, virtue must aerate the intelligence, providing the leaven that lets it rise. This integration, this making-one, is fundamental to tawhid itself, now grasped not as a mere doctrine but as a dynamic process of spiritual unification demanded by the very idea of God's oneness. The intellect that touches the spirit of certainty, then, is not a bare faculty of reasoning. It is a whole soul made joyful, made beautiful, and made one — rejoicing in its intimacy with the Real, and drawn by that intimacy into every virtue the Prophet embodied.

## Detachment from the world

If virtue is the making-beautiful of the soul, then vice, in Ali's perspective, is something more than moral failure. It is a kind of blindness. Shah-Kazemi frames the sins not only as vices but as intellectual dysfunctions, blind spots in the very organ of vision. The heart is diseased by them, and its eye is thereby rendered unable to see the Real. The Qur'an supplies the imagery: "In their hearts is a disease and God increased them in disease" (chapter 2, verse 10), and "It is not the sights that are blind, but blind are the hearts" (chapter 22, verse 46).

The vices are all comprised within egotism, which Ali often describes simply as caprice. To conquer caprice is, in one of his sayings, tantamount to salvation: "He attains deliverance whose intellect dominates his caprice." And the word for deliverance carries the double sense of salvation and beatitude — which means, Shah-Kazemi stresses, that deliverance is to some degree accessible here and now. It is not a grace granted only after death. It is bound up with tasting the joy that flows from certainty, from intimate contact with the spirit of that certainty. What cultivates receptivity to that spirit, over and above obedience to the revealed Law, is the same purity of intention and sincerity of devotion the earlier episode named: ikhlas.

Without that sincerity, compliance with the rules is nothing but hypocrisy — nifaq — and pretension, riya. And Ali defines pretension as the gravest of sins: shirk, the attribution of partners to God. "Know that the slightest pretension is polytheism." Shah-Kazemi reads this as a commentary on one of the Qur'an's sharpest condemnations of hypocrisy, a short sura aimed at those who deny the essence of faith while outwardly conforming to it: "Hast thou seen the one who belieth religion? He is the one who repelleth the orphan and urgeth not the feeding of the poor. So woe be to those who pray, those who are heedless of their prayer, who want only to be seen, and withhold small kindnesses" (chapter 107, verses 1-7).

The lesson is that formal acts of devotion have no value whatsoever unless they are woven into a life of virtue and performed with the right intention. This is why Shah-Kazemi calls Ali's ethos not merely religious but spiritual: it takes the ideals of religion to their most profound application. To be spiritual in this sense is precisely to be true to one's intellect — to the treasures buried deep within it, not merely to the rational functions running on its surface. Without that depth, religion is empty. "There is no religion for one who has no intellect," says the Imam. And likewise: "Action without knowledge is error." What counts is the intention behind the act, for "action — all action — is dust, except what is purified within it."

No group, Shah-Kazemi observes, better illustrated this principle than the Kharijites who rebelled against Ali. Among them were many "readers" of the Qur'an, men who enjoyed real religious prestige for their Qur'anic learning and intense worship. Yet their piety was more apparent than real — a zeal of bitterness, a caricature of the genuine article. When his supporters spoke admiringly of the long night vigils these rebels performed, the Imam answered sharply: "Sleeping with certainty is better than praying with doubt." Certainty imparts peace and joy; so the sleep of one immersed in the spirit of certainty is a bodily repose that only deepens the happiness of a good conscience. Spiritual certitude is itself a kind of permanent wakefulness — being awake to higher realities — and bodily sleep does not contradict it. As the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) said, "My eyes sleep, but my heart sleeps not."

This is the setting for the sentence in the discourse to Kumayl that governs the whole movement of detachment: the true knowers, Ali says, "make easy what the extravagant find harsh; they befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged." What the avid, worldly types find harsh is outward adversity, difficulty, and deprivation. But the Qur'an views precisely these as the trials that inevitably accompany life in this world: "And We shall try you with something of fear and hunger and loss of property and life and fruits; but give glad tidings to the patient — those who, when struck by affliction say: 'Verily, we belong to God and verily unto Him we are ever-returning'" (chapter 2, verses 155-157).

The rightly-guided perceive the blessings showered on those who are patient under affliction — a patience that is itself a fruit of their knowledge of the truth. The whole of an entire sura turns on it: "By the Age, truly man is in a state of loss — except those who believe and do virtuous deeds and exhort one another to truth and exhort one another to patience" (chapter 103, verses 1-3). Firmly rooted in the awareness that unto God they are ever returning, they can make easy the trials the extravagant experience only as bitterness. The ease hidden in every hardship is invisible to the ignorant but discerned by the wise: "Verily, with hardship cometh ease; verily with hardship cometh ease" (chapter 94, verses 5-6). And the wise go further than merely anticipating that ease as hardship's eventual fruit. They taste the fruit already, as a seed hidden within the hardship itself — which is why they actually befriend what estranges the ignorant.

At its deepest level this befriending reaches all the way to death. Ali says, "Death is more intimate to me than the breast of the mother is to the suckling babe." Shah-Kazemi connects the saying to the verse cited just above, where the mention of trials is preceded by a reminder that those slain in the path of God are not truly dead — "nay, they are alive but ye perceive not." That is not only an affirmation of the life hereafter; it is an allusion to the esoteric sense of being slain for God, extinction in God, fana. The Imam's intimacy with death is intimacy with this death-while-alive, which he names elsewhere: "I am the death of the dead." Because life in the world is itself a kind of death — a limitation, and so a privation, against the infinite plenitude of the divine — dying to that death is nothing but true life. The Qur'an says it plainly: "And the life of this world is nothing but sport and play, and verily, the abode of the Hereafter — that is true life, did they but know" (chapter 29, verse 64).

Here Shah-Kazemi enters an essential clarification. It is not the physical world as such that is to be negated. It is worldliness — egotistic attachment to the world — that must be overcome. This is the principle of zuhd, stressed repeatedly throughout the Nahj al-balagha, and no single English word renders it: it gathers asceticism, austerity, abstention, detachment, "doing without," and "making little of." A passage from the Nahj conveys the attitude of the zahid: "The world is an abode for which annihilation is ordained, and for its people departure from it is decreed. It is sweet and green, and it hastens to one who seeks it, and confounds the heart that gazes upon it. So set out from it with the best of the provision available to you, and ask not from it above what suffices and seek not from it more than what can be attained."

And what is that best provision, that zad? Throughout the Nahj it is named taqwa — another word resisting simple translation, encompassing piety, fear of God, awe, and God-consciousness. "I exhort you, O slaves of God, to taqwa, which is the provision," says the Imam. This provision for the next world is gathered only in the measure of one's detachment from the perishable aspects of this one, for "whoso desireth that life which hasteneth away, in that life We hasten for him what We will" (chapter 17, verse 18).

Ali describes the world's contemptibility in the most vivid terms. "By God, this world of yours is more contemptible in my eyes than a chewed-up bone of a pig in the hand of a leper." He calls its image the snake, soft to touch but virulent poison within. What renders the world so small is not hatred of creation but the sheer grandeur and beauty of God: "The greatness of the Creator for you belittles creatures in your view." It is only in the light of the Absolute that the world's pleasures appear fleeting and illusory. And so the Imam praises a brother in God with a saying that turns entirely on this reversal of scale: "What made him great in my eyes was the smallness of the world in his."

This, Shah-Kazemi notes, is the Qur'anic meaning of the very word zahid. In the sura named after Joseph, the brothers who sold him for a small price "deemed him of little value" — and the word used of them is zahidin, those who make light of a thing (chapter 12, verse 20). In Ali's perspective the world is likewise deemed of little value: not valueless, but rendered all but insignificant against the Hereafter, against eternity, against ultimate reality. All created things are reduced to fleeting shadows destined for annihilation, for "Everything perishes except His Face" (chapter 28, verse 88).

Yet — and this is the crucial balance — the world is not stripped of all value. Its significance lies precisely in what it cannot help but signify. "And there is nothing that does not hymn His glory with praise" (chapter 17, verse 44). Every creature in existence proclaims the infinite creativity of its Creator, and the Qur'an gives the imagination an image to grasp this universal praise: "And the birds in flight — each one knoweth its prayer and glorification" (chapter 24, verse 41). There is therefore no contradiction in Ali despising the world and, in the same breath, describing with lyrical wonder the beauties of virgin nature — the peacock, the birds, the ants and serpents and elephants of a famous sermon. The world scorned as an object of attachment is the same world revered as a hymn of signs. There is no contradiction between the two because they answer different questions. Asked whether the world deserves the heart's ultimate devotion, the answer is that it is smaller than a chewed bone in a leper's hand. Asked what the world is for, the answer is that it exists to proclaim its Maker, and every creature in it is already doing so. Detachment, in the end, is not a flight from creation. It is the freedom to see creation for what it truly is: a passing shadow, and a ceaseless praise.

## The abode of true life

The freedom won through detachment issues in a distinctive way of being present in the world. Ali (peace be upon him) describes the true knowers in a single sentence: "With their bodies, they keep company with the world, while their spirits are tied to the transcendent realm." Shah-Kazemi pairs it with another of the Imam's descriptions of the group to which he belongs — those whose "hearts are in the Gardens while their bodies are at work." To be tied to that realm means to be already within it more fully than within this earthly one. Those whose spirits are in the transcendent domain have a consciousness substantially, not merely conceptually, determined by transcendent realities. Their outward actions in the world belie their inward transcendence of all action; they act, and yet are not bound by the fruits of their action, because they know that "action — all action — is dust, except what is purified within it."

This raises the question that governs the closing of the whole chapter: what role does the Hereafter actually play in Ali's ethics? Is it simply a matter of dangling a heavenly reward and threatening a hellish punishment as levers for moral action? Even if that is partly true, Shah-Kazemi argues, there is a far deeper dimension at work. The ethical orientation here is defined not merely by belief in God but by the spiritual reality of the Hereafter — both objectively, in itself, and subjectively, insofar as the individual assimilates it. The Hereafter thereby becomes a dynamic dimension within the believer's own soul: a process, an orientation, a movement, rather than a bare belief or hope.

The distinction is sharp. Anticipation of the Hereafter characterizes every believer in principle; its realization characterizes the soul of the wali Allah, the friend of God, the saint and the sage. And this is the meaning of the Imam's most famous testimony to his own total immersion in the reality of which he is certain: "Were the veil to be removed, I would not increase in certitude." His certainty is already so complete that no unveiling could add to it. The foretaste of the Hereafter deepens, rather than fades, in proportion to one's virtue and sanctity. It never dwindles into a mere heuristic device whose usefulness is exhausted once its moral impact has landed.

To show what such a foretaste is, Shah-Kazemi turns to a passage from the Nahj that is not rhetorical exhortation but the description of an actual vision: "If you were to cast the eye of your heart towards what is described for you of the Garden, your soul would become averse to the marvels of this world — its passions, its pleasures and its embellished scenery. And your soul would be rapt in contemplation of the swaying trees whose roots lie hidden in dunes of musk on the banks of the rivers of the Garden, and the clusters of dazzling pearls hanging down from the branches of its trees." Once the eye of the heart is turned toward that Garden, the marvels of this world lose their grip of themselves. Renunciation, at this level, is not an act of will forcing the soul away from the world. It is the natural aversion of a soul that has seen something incomparably greater.

It is worth being precise about what this reframing does and does not claim. It does not reduce the Hereafter to a psychological state, as though Paradise were merely a way of feeling in the present. The Hereafter remains, for Ali, an objective reality — better and more lasting than this world in its own right. What changes is the relation of the soul to that reality. For the ordinary believer the Hereafter is an object of anticipation, held at a distance in time; for the friend of God it is an object of realization, present now to a consciousness already shaped by it. The difference is not that the sage believes more firmly in a future event, but that the future has, in a sense, stopped being merely future for him. He lives from it. And because he lives from it, the promise and the threat that work on others as external levers work on him as the felt texture of the reality he already inhabits. The moral force is not lost; it is transmuted from calculation into contemplation.

That state of soul has a name in the Qur'an. When the highest degrees of realization are attained, the soul is so utterly content in the beatific presence of God that it desires nothing more — and this is al-nafs al-mutma'inna, the soul at peace in absolute certainty, addressed at its return to its Lord (chapter 89, verse 27). Here the desire for Paradise and the fear of Hell are transcended, not by indifference but by fulfilment: the soul's very state is already paradisal.

Ali constantly echoes the Qur'anic principle that "the Hereafter is better and more lasting" (chapter 87, verse 17). Shah-Kazemi reads the Hereafter here not only as the time at which the divine warnings and promises reach their consummation, but as the space in which all positive qualities — human, angelic, divine — are manifested in their fullness, the qualities that appear on earth only as reflections of higher realities. The Qur'an furnishes the imagination with elaborate descriptions of paradisal bliss for a reason it names of itself: "Assuredly, We purify them with a pure quality — remembrance of the Abode of the Hereafter" (chapter 38, verse 46). The mere thought of Paradise is itself a purification of mind and heart, a guard against the ever-present temptation to seek one's ultimate happiness in this world alone.

And here the whole arc closes on its central claim. One's well-being in this life, in regard to action, lies in the performance of good deeds — and such well-being is proportioned to the depth of one's belief that "Whatever good ye send before for your souls, ye will find it with God, better and more tremendous as reward" (chapter 73, verse 20). A sincere orientation toward the Hereafter is thereby transformed into a perception of heavenly beatitude already in this life, in the very form of the acts and attitudes which, being good, share the same nature as the felicity they lead to. Morally good actions are grasped by one who sees beyond the veil as vivid prefigurations of heavenly realities, not merely as ethical prerequisites for salvation. Goodness does not simply lead to a reward; it already participates in that reward. The Qur'an asks, "Is the reward of goodness aught but goodness?" (chapter 55, verse 60). And the Imam's saying condenses it: "The fruit of good action is like its root." The end is already present in the means that lead to it.

Shah-Kazemi gives the last word to the verse he calls a fitting close to the whole chapter: "And give good tidings to those who believe and perform virtuous deeds, that for them are Gardens underneath which rivers flow. Every time they are given to eat from the fruits thereof, they say: 'This is what we were given to eat before.' And they were given the like thereof" (chapter 2, verse 25). The fruits of the paradisal gardens are experienced already here below, in the form of all goodness, beauty, and truth, and the modes of happiness that flow from them. Each such experience is a foretaste of the ultimate beatitude. On the ethical plane, then, the performance of good deeds — and still more directly, the realization of intrinsic virtue, ihsan — is not simply a prerequisite for a salvation deferred to after death. It is already a kind of deliverance, here and now: deliverance from the imprisonment of sin on the outward plane, and from the bondage of egocentricity on the inward plane. Salvation in this life, grounded in unshakeable certitude, is the beating heart of the Imam's teaching, and the note on which this introductory chapter comes to rest.

## What this episode lands

The ethos traced across these four episodes turns out to be, at its far end, a spirituality of joy, of beauty, of detachment, and of a Hereafter already tasted. The severe inner warfare of the greater struggle does not end in grimness; it ends in a happiness so grounded in certainty that the sage befriends hardship, greets death as intimate, holds the world as small, and yet reads every creature as a hymn to its Maker. The deliverance the Imam teaches is not withheld until after death. It is available here and now, to the pure of heart, as goodness that already participates in the reward it leads to.

This closes the book's introductory chapter — the portrait of Ali's spiritual ethos as a universal spirituality of realization. Everything that follows now builds on this foundation. The book turns next from the inward architecture of the soul to its outward consequence: the cardinal virtue of justice. And that turn is not a change of subject but a deepening of the same one, for the justice to come is rooted in ihsan itself — the making-beautiful of the soul, defined by the Prophet as worshipping God as if one saw Him, and affirmed by the Imam as real and not merely hypothetical, since God is indeed seen by the pure of heart through the verities of faith. The joy, beauty, and detachment of this episode are the soil in which that justice grows.

## Closing

The closing movement of the introduction has followed one conviction to its end: that the fruit of the hardest struggle is joy, and that the Hereafter better and more lasting is not only awaited but tasted. Ali ibn Abi Talib (peace be upon him) rejoices in intimacy with the spirit of certainty, sees virtue as beauty, befriends death, and holds the world as small against the grandeur of God — and in all of it teaches a deliverance offered in this life, not only the next. What began as an anatomy of the soul ends as an invitation. The next stage carries that invitation outward, into the justice rooted in this very beauty of the soul, where the inward realization traced here becomes a way of living rightly among others.
