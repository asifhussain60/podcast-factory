# The Name and the Named

*This episode turns the picture of invocation inside out. Reza Shah-Kazemi follows the Imam's startling claim that the invocation issues first from the One who is invoked and only second from the one who invokes — that God's remembrance of man precedes man's remembrance of God, ontologically and not merely in time. It unfolds the cosmogony the later mystics built on the Imam's hints: creation itself as a kind of divine remembrance, the world called from nothing by God's utterance of its name. And it reaches the cardinal tenet of the whole invocatory discipline — that the Name is the Named — resolving the apparent clash between this Sufi principle and the Imams' own insistence that the Name of God is other than God.*

## Where this episode picks up

Here is a sentence of the Imam's that quietly overturns everything the last episode built. We have spoken throughout of a person invoking God — the lover with the Name on his tongue, the worshipper in his night vigil, keeping his tongue moist with remembrance. The Imam says: "The invocation is not a formality of speech nor a way of thinking; rather, it comes forth firstly from the Invoked, and secondly from the invoker." Read that slowly. The invocation comes first from the One who is invoked. Before ever a human tongue moves to call upon God, the call has already gone out from God's side — and the human invocation is a second thing, a shadow, a reflection, an answer to a summons that was issued before the answerer existed. It is God, first and foremost, who performs the invocation; man's invocation is but a consequence of the divine invocation.

This is not a pious flourish. It reaches to the ground of what remembrance is, and it is anchored in a verse the Qur'an states with disarming simplicity: "Remember Me, I will remember you" (chapter 2, verse 152). We are apt to read that as a bargain — you remember God, and in return God remembers you — with the human act first and the divine response following. The Imam's teaching, and the whole tradition that develops it, reads the reciprocity the other way down. God's remembrance is the deeper and the prior term; man's remembrance is the echo it awakens. And if that is so, then something must be said about how God "remembers" a thing at all — for when God remembers what does not yet exist, His remembrance does not find the thing but makes it. To be remembered by God, the tradition will say, is to be called out of nothing into being. Creation itself is a kind of divine remembrance, and the invocation a human being raises is a late reverberation of the Word by which he was first called forth.

This reversal is not meant to diminish the human effort but to place it rightly, and it carries a strange consolation. If the whole initiative lay with the human being — if a person had to reach across an infinite distance to a God who waited, indifferent, to see whether He would be remembered — the practice would be a lonely and precarious thing, its success hanging on the strength of a creature's will. The Imam's teaching removes that loneliness at the root. The person who turns to invoke God is not initiating a contact from nothing; he is answering a call that has been sounding toward him all along, that indeed gave him the very being with which to answer. His remembrance is possible only because he is already remembered; his reaching toward God is possible only because God has already reached toward him and is holding him, at this moment, in existence. The tradition does not thereby make the human effort superfluous — the effort is the answering, and an unanswered call awakens no echo — but it strips the effort of its illusion of self-sufficiency and sets it within a prior, encompassing act of God. To invoke is to answer; and one can only answer because one has first been addressed.

Three movements carry the episode. We begin with the reciprocity of remembrance — the human invocation answered by a divine blessing, and the deeper truth that the invocation issues first from the Invoked. We turn then to creation as remembrance — the world called from nothing by God's utterance of its name, and the cosmogony the mystics built on the Imam's hints. And we close on the cardinal tenet that the Name is the Named, and on how it can be reconciled with the Imams' equally firm insistence that the Name of God is other than God. Each movement takes us a step further from the reassuring picture of a lone human being reaching upward, and a step closer to the disclosure that the reaching was, all along, God's own act turning back toward Himself through the creature He had called into being to perform it.

## The reciprocity of remembrance

Begin with the reciprocity as the Qur'an first presents it, at its gentlest. When it commands abundant invocation, it immediately adds a promise: "O ye who believe, invoke God with much invocation, and glorify Him early and late. He it is who blesseth you, as do His angels, so that He may bring you forth from darkness into light; and He is ever merciful to the believers" (chapter 33, verses 41 to 43). Set the human act beside the divine act: the believers invoke, and God — with His angels — blesses. The word rendered "blesseth" is the same word used for the human prayer; God, as it were, "prays" upon the believers, and the fruit of His prayer upon them is that they are brought forth from darkness into light. There is already something remarkable here. The illumination that remembrance yields — the polishing of the heart into a thing that sees — is here attributed not to the human invocation as its own achievement but to the divine blessing that answers it. The Imam draws the line directly: "Whoever invokes God, glorified be He, God enlivens his heart and illumines his inner substance." The human being invokes; God enlivens and illumines. The light is God's gift, not the invoker's manufacture.

It is worth lingering on that movement from darkness into light, because it is easy to pass over as a familiar phrase and miss what it settles. In the ordinary way of thinking about spiritual effort, a person labors and, by his labor, produces a result — as a farmer sows and reaps, or a craftsman works and makes. Applied to remembrance, this would mean that the invoker, by the diligence of his practice, manufactures his own illumination, earns it as the wage of his effort. The Qur'an's grammar quietly refuses this. It is God who brings the believers forth from darkness into light; the believers invoke, but the bringing-forth is His act, His blessing upon them, seconded by His angels. The human effort is real and indispensable — the last episode spent itself insisting on that — but it is not the cause of the light in the way a craftsman is the cause of what he makes. It is more like the opening of a shutter than the kindling of a flame: the invoker clears the way, removes what obstructs, keeps his tongue moist and his heart turned; but the light that then floods in was not made by him, and does not belong to him, and could not have been produced by any exertion however great. This will matter enormously when we reach the summit of the path, for it is the first hint of a truth the whole tradition will press to its limit — that the decisive thing in the spiritual life is finally given and not achieved, a grace and not a wage.

But the Imam presses past this exchange of acts to something stranger and more fundamental. The reciprocity of "Remember Me, I will remember you" is, he suggests, more complex than a simple trade of favors, because the two remembrances are not equal partners taking turns. His saying that the invocation comes first from the Invoked and second from the invoker names an order of priority that is not temporal but ontological. It is not that God happened to remember first and man second, as two people might greet each other, one a moment before the other. It is that the divine remembrance is the ground of the human one — that man can remember God at all only because God has first "remembered" man in the deeper sense that gives man his very being. What appears, from the human side, as a person taking the initiative to call upon God is, from the deeper side, a response drawn out of him by a call that reached him before he could call anything. The Qur'an's "Remember Me, I will remember you" thus discloses, on this reading, not a bargain between equals but the awakening of an echo: God's prior remembrance sounding, and the human remembrance rising in answer.

Everything turns, then, on what it means for God to "remember" — and this is where the teaching opens onto its widest horizon. When a human being remembers something, the thing remembered already exists; remembrance renders it present to the mind but does not bring it into being. When God "remembers" a thing that does not yet exist, His remembrance is not the finding of something already there but its very calling-into-existence. To be present in the remembrance of God is, for a creature, to be — and to be absent from it is not to be. This is the sense in which the Imam's brief sentence opens onto the whole mystery of existentiation, the effusion of being: the invocation, in its deepest metaphysical meaning, is no longer confined to a human being's verbal or mental activity but reaches into the realm of engendering, the calling-forth of things from nothing. And it is here that the later mystics found in the Imam's hint the seed of an entire cosmology, which the next movement takes up. For if to be remembered by God is to be called into being, then the creation of the world is itself a kind of divine remembrance — God uttering the name of each possible thing, and by that utterance summoning it from the abyss of non-existence into the light of the real.

## Called forth from nothing

The Qur'an poses a question that the mystics took as the key to this whole cosmology: "Hath there come upon man any moment in time when he was not a thing remembered?" (chapter 76, verse 1). Read in the ordinary way, it asks whether there was a time when man was nothing worth mentioning. Read in the deeper way the tradition proposes, it identifies man's coming-into-being with his becoming "a thing remembered," and his prior nothingness with his being "not remembered" — not yet called forth from the hiddenness of pre-existence into manifest being. To exist is to be remembered by God; to be nothing is to be, as yet, unmentioned. Ibn al-'Arabi read the verse exactly so. In his great work of illuminations he wrote that God named Himself the Originator — al-Badi' — the one who creates that which had no like or exemplar in all the degrees of being, because He knows, by His all-encompassing nature, every single thing before it enters into existence; and therefore, of the creation of man, God says that he "was not a thing remembered," for the remembrance is God's — "so a thing has being in the remembrance of the one who remembers it." A thing exists in being remembered. God's remembrance of a creature is that creature's existence.

From this follows the reversal that crowns the whole reciprocity. God's remembrance of man precedes man's remembrance of God — not merely in time but in the order of being, since man's very capacity to remember is itself a gift of the being that God's remembrance conferred. And this yields another reading of the verse we have already met, that the remembrance of God is greater: it is greater, infinitely greater, than man's remembrance of Him, because God's remembrance is the creative act that gives man the being by which he can remember at all. The true being of a person resides in the remembrance God has of him. Weigh the two remembrances against each other and the disproportion is absolute. Man's remembrance of God is the movement of a tongue and a heart across a span of years, intermittent, effortful, forever lapsing and recovering; God's remembrance of man is the sustaining of that man in existence from before his birth and at every instant of his life, the very ground on which his frail remembering stands. To call God's remembrance "greater" is almost to understate it, for the two are not even quantities on the same scale — the one is a creature's act within being, the other is the act that confers being. The verse, read this way, is not comparing two remembrances of the same kind and finding one larger; it is pointing past the human act to the divine act that underlies and infinitely exceeds it. Man's devotion to God is articulated through the utterance of a divine Name; God's creation of man is articulated through His utterance of the creative Word — the Word by which, in the Qur'an, He says to a thing "Be!" and it is. And in the mystics' cosmogony this Word is not an event over and done with at some first moment of time; it never ceases to reverberate, furnishing each soul, at every instant, with its divine substrate, its foundation and support. The Word "breathes" existence upon what the tradition calls the immutable entity — the archetype of a thing, inexistent in itself, which yet determines the particular contours and configuration of the being that will be manifested through it, as a prism refracts the light passing through it, or as water takes on the color of the cup that holds it.

The point that the creative Word never ceases to sound is worth pausing on, for it changes how one must think of one's own existence. We are inclined to picture creation as a single act completed long ago — a first moment at which God spoke, the world sprang into being, and thereafter it continues under its own momentum, as a clock, once wound, runs on without the winder. The mystics' reading of the creative Word forbids this picture. The "Be!" by which a thing is called forth is not spoken once and finished; it is uttered continuously, at every instant furnishing each soul afresh with the divine support on which its being rests. A creature does not possess its existence as a thing owned outright and thereafter independent; it receives its existence, moment by moment, as a gift renewed — and were the Word to fall silent for an instant, the thing would lapse back into the nothing it was drawn from. This is the deepest sense in which to be is to be remembered by God: not that God remembered us once, at our origin, but that He is remembering us now, and that our present existence is the present tense of His remembrance. Our own remembrance of God, raised on the tongue, is thus set within a vaster remembrance that is holding us in being even as we speak — the small echo sounding inside the great Word that never stops.

The Imam had foreshadowed this whole conception in an image we have met before — the Names of God that "have filled the foundations of all things." The later masters made explicit what the phrase had held in reserve: that the divine Names are the very supports and substrata of all that exists, each thing resting on a Name as on its foundation. So Ibn al-'Arabi can say that every servant has a Name which is its Lord, so that the servant becomes the "body" of that Name, and the Name is the servant's "heart" — the inner reality that gives the outer form its life. And here the image of the polish returns from a new direction and clicks into place. The Name revealed from above is mirrored by a Name that resounds from within; the reciprocal reflection appears when the rust obscuring the surface of the heart is scoured away by the polish of remembrance. What the polished heart discloses within itself and what it receives from above are, at the root, the same divine Name — the Name that furnishes the soul's own foundation meeting the Name invoked upon the tongue — and their meeting is once again that phrase the verse of light supplied: light upon light. The whole doctrine of the polished mirror and the whole cosmogony of the creative Word turn out to be one teaching seen from two sides: the soul is the locus in which a divine Name discloses itself, and remembrance is the clearing of that locus until the Name it was founded upon shines out.

The tradition has a precise term for the soul in this role — the locus of manifestation, the place where God discloses Himself in one particular, unrepeatable mode. Each soul rests upon its own divine support, its own particular Lord, the Name that engenders and sustains it; and the soul is nothing other than the site at which that Name comes to light. The image of water taking the color of its cup captures the whole relation. The light, or the water, is one; what differentiates one soul from another is not a difference in the light but the particular vessel through which it passes, the archetype that shapes how the one Reality is refracted there. This is why no two souls manifest God identically, and why the effacement the path aims at is never a blank uniformity: what shines through a soul cleared by remembrance is the one Light, but shaped by the unrepeatable Name that soul was founded upon, as a single sun strikes a thousand windows and each returns a light that is at once wholly the sun's and unmistakably its own. The Imam himself foreshadowed this doctrine in a saying preserved in answer to a question about the nature of ultimate reality, in which he spoke of a light dawning from the morning of eternity, its traces shimmering upon what he called the temples of oneness — which the commentators read as the manifestation of the Light of the one Essence upon the loci where the divine qualities appear, the very forms of the divine Names. The cosmogony the mystics elaborated was, once again, the drawing-out of what the Imam had folded into an image.

## Name and Named

We arrive now at the cardinal tenet of the whole invocatory discipline, the principle that makes sense of why one invokes a Name at all: that the Name is the Named — al-ism huwa'l-musamma. If the Name were merely a label, an arbitrary tag pinned to God from outside, then to invoke it would be to handle a sound and no more, and the practice would be empty. The tradition insists it is not. But we must first see why the revealed Names are unlike any other names. The names of the idols, the Qur'an says, "are but names that ye have named, ye and your fathers, for which God hath revealed no authority" (chapter 53, verse 23) — arbitrary signs with no intrinsic bond to what they name, invented by human beings and pointing to nothing real. The Names of God are the opposite. "Call upon Allah or call upon al-Rahman," the Qur'an commands; "by whatever Name you call upon Him, His are the most beautiful Names" (chapter 17, verse 110). These Names are revealed by God, not devised by men, and they are penetrated by the very reality they denote. They possess, in the tradition's word, authority — sultan — the power to transform and not merely to inform, charged with a theurgic force that can render present to mind and heart the immanent reality of God. This is why the practice is the invocation of these specific, revealed Names, and not of names of our own choosing. A supplication attributed to the Imam, dense with the divine Names, supplies the key to it in a single verse: "I ask Thee by every Name with which Thou hast named Thyself." It is because God names Himself that His Names carry their sacramental substance; a Name God has spoken of Himself is of one substance with the reality it names.

This also answers a question that might otherwise nag at the whole practice. If, as the last episodes taught, the entire created cosmos is a kind of speech of God — every phenomenon a "word," the world a book in which the divine is everywhere disclosed — then why invoke a particular revealed Name at all, rather than simply contemplate the signs of God spread through creation? Why single out the scriptural Name from the cosmic manifestation, when both are disclosures of the one Reality? The tradition answers by distinguishing two orders of revealing that are not of equal power. The cosmos is a differentiated manifestation of God, refracted and scattered through countless forms; the revealed Name is a unique, concentrated revelation, charged with a spiritual force that makes it incomparably more revealing than the created world. The difference, Ibn al-'Arabi suggests, is something like the difference between light and shadow. The revealed Name is like a direct projection of the pure Light of God, of one substance with its source; the cosmos is like the shadow that light casts — real, and a projection of the same reality, but at a further remove, dimmer, more veiled. "The very Being of the Real," he writes, "is a verified Light, the very existence of creation is a shadow following after." Both the Name and the world reveal God; but to invoke the revealed Name is to lay hold of the Light at its point of nearest approach, while to read the cosmos alone is to trace God only in His shadow. This is why the practice concentrates on the specific Names God has spoken of Himself: they are the quintessence of manifestation, summing up the whole realm of disclosure and surpassing it, the Light itself rather than the shadow it throws.

Yet at exactly this point a real difficulty rises, and Shah-Kazemi does not smooth it over. However well the Imam's teaching on remembrance agrees with the later Sufi doctrine, this particular stress on the identity of the Name and the Named seems to collide head-on with numerous sayings of the Shi'i Imams that deny precisely that identity. Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq is reported to have said, flatly: "The Name of Allah is other than Him. For everything to which the name of a thing is applicable is a creation, hence other than God. For whatever the tongue expresses, or is worked on by the hand, is a creation; everything described is created, and the Creator of things cannot be described within the limits of a named object." And again: "Allah has ninety-nine Names. If the Name were the Named, then every one of the Names would be a god. But Allah is the one meaning indicated by the Names, and all of them are other than Him." Here is a plain contradiction on its face: the Sufis say the Name is the Named; the Imam says the Name is other than the Named, and warns that to identify them would multiply gods. Which is it?

The resolution lies in attending carefully to what the word "Name" means in each of the two contexts, for they are not speaking of the same thing under the same aspect. There is the context of doctrinal distinction, in which the Absolute must be rigorously distinguished from the relative and the Essence from every form — and there the Name, taken as a describable, utterable thing, is emphatically other than the indescribable Essence, exactly as al-Sadiq says; to confuse the two would indeed be to lodge a creature in the place of God. And there is the context of methodic concentration, in which the Absolute is understood to be the very reality of the form through which it reveals itself — and there the Name, taken as the divine attribute it designates, is not other than the Essence, for that attribute is identical with the Essence. Ibn al-'Arabi gives the distinction its edge: the Name, he says, has two connotations — the first is God Himself, who is what is named; the second is that by which one Name is distinguished from another. As being essentially the Named, the Name is the Reality; as being the distinguishing mark that sets one Name apart from the rest, it is an "imagined" reality, a relation, a determination, and in that respect it has no separate ontological standing of its own. The Names have no independent entities; they all revert to the one Reality of the Essence — which dissolves al-Sadiq's worry that different Names would mean different gods, since the Names are not separate deities but the one Essence considered under its several disclosures.

The lexicon of the school makes the point with a precise example. Consider the Name "the Living." In respect of being itself, the Living is identical with the Essence — for the Essence is living, and its life is nothing other than itself. But in respect of its determination as a particular meaning — Life, as distinguished from Knowledge or Power — the Living is other than the Essence, a specification within it, a facet marked off by the mind. And so, the definition concludes, the Name is not identical with the Named, neither is it other than It: not identical, in respect of the specific meaning that distinguishes it; not other, in respect of the being that is the Essence itself. A further distinction sharpens this still more. One must separate the verbal form of a Name — the spoken word "the Living," a set of sounds — from the Name as an attribute of the Essence, which is nothing but the divine quality it designates, itself identical with the Essence. The spoken words, as Qaysari put it, are "Names of Names": the audible "the Compassionate" is the name of an essence which is compassion, and it is from this that one understands in what sense the Name is the Named. The verbal Name on the invoker's tongue points to the attribute; the attribute is not other than the Essence; and so the humble sound a person repeats is bound, through the attribute it names, to the very reality of God. The identity and the distinction are both preserved, each in its own respect — and the whole rationale of invoking a Name stands vindicated: to invoke the Name is, through the attribute, to reach toward the Named, because at the deepest level the Name is the Named.

There is a reason the tradition refuses to collapse the distinction even as it affirms the identity, and it is the same reason the Imam's whole teaching keeps transcendence and immanence together. To say only that the Name is the Named, without the qualifying distinction, would open the very door al-Sadiq shut — it would let the ninety-nine Names harden into ninety-nine realities, so many gods, or let the spoken word on the tongue be worshipped as though the sound itself were divine. And to say only that the Name is other than the Named, without the qualifying identity, would empty the practice of its power, reducing the invocation to the handling of a mere label with no bond to what it names, no better than the idolaters' arbitrary signs. The truth the discipline lives by lies exactly in holding both: the Name is other than the Named as a spoken form and a distinguishing relation, and the Name is the Named as an attribute identical with the Essence. It is because both are true that invocation works as it does. The invoker's tongue moves a spoken word — which is other than God, a created sound — yet that word names an attribute, and the attribute is not other than God; so the humble act of repetition, beginning in the created and the outward, reaches through the attribute to the uncreated and the inward. The Name is the bridge precisely because it stands on both banks at once, its verbal form planted in the world of creation and its inner reality rooted in the Essence — which is why a sound a child could pronounce can carry a soul toward the Absolute.

## What this episode lands

This episode has turned the practice of invocation inside out and shown what lies beneath it. We have followed the Imam's overturning of the picture we began with: the invocation, he says, comes first from the One who is invoked and only second from the one who invokes, so that the reciprocity of "Remember Me, I will remember you" is not a bargain between equals but the awakening of an echo — God's prior remembrance sounding, and the human remembrance rising in answer. We have seen what it means for God to remember: that His remembrance of a creature is that creature's very existence, so that creation itself is a kind of divine remembrance, the world called from nothing by the utterance of its name, each thing resting on a divine Name as on its foundation. And we have reached the cardinal tenet of the whole discipline — that the Name is the Named — and resolved its apparent clash with the Imams' insistence that the Name is other than God, by distinguishing the verbal form and the distinguishing relation, which are other, from the attribute and the being, which are not.

But a shadow has fallen across the last stretch of this argument, and the next episode must reckon with it. If the invocation is finally God's and not man's; if the true being of the invoker resides in the remembrance God has of him; if the Name he raises is, at the root, not other than the Named — then what becomes of the invoker himself in all this? The Imam has said, in a saying we have so far only glanced at, that a person "will not remember God according to the true reality of remembrance until he forgets his own soul in his remembrance." The whole tendency of this episode has been to disclose God as the deeper agent behind the human act — and that disclosure, pressed to its end, must mean the effacement of the human agent who thought the act his own. To that effacement — the forgetting of the self in remembrance, the extinction that the tradition calls fana, and the realization that it opens onto — we turn in the final episode.

There is even a clue, in the very doctrine we have just unfolded, to what that effacement will look like. We saw that a divine attribute — the Living, the Compassionate — is, in respect of its distinguishing meaning, marked off from the Essence, yet in the deepest reckoning is annihilated in the Essence, one with it, so that its apparent separateness dissolves in the unity of God. Now set beside the attribute the human soul. On its own account the soul is nothing; in respect of the divine Name that founds it — its support, its true reality — it is not other than the Real. What the mystics glimpsed is that the soul's path traces, on the plane of a single life, the very relation that holds objectively between the attribute and the Essence: just as the attribute is annihilated in the Essence and thereby shown to be one with it, so the soul, extinguished by the Name it invokes, attains at once its total nullification and its ultimate realization — no longer as a separate self, but as the locus in which the one Reality knows itself. The metaphysics of the Name and the Named, in other words, is the objective map of a journey the soul is meant to make subjectively; and the name of that journey's summit is the word the next episode is given wholly to unfold. What the metaphysician sees written across the whole of being, the traveler must undergo in the narrow compass of his own soul; and the undergoing has a cost the map could not convey, for what is annihilated on the page is only a word, and what is annihilated in the soul is the whole self that reads it — and that is a passage no doctrine can spare a person from making for himself.
