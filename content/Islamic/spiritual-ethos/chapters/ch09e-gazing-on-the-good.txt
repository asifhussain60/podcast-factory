# Gazing on the Good

*The final movement of Reza Shah-Kazemi's essay on justice asks where the whole disposition of the just soul is actually drawn from — and answers with what the letter to Malik al-Ashtar calls its lynchpin: prayer, and its inner essence, the remembrance of God. This episode follows the letter's instruction to give God the best of one's time and vital energy, unfolds the hadith of drawing near in which God becomes the very faculties of the one He loves, and shows how remembrance makes a person able not only to be just but to teach justice. It closes on the essay's boldest stroke: the parallel with Plato's Republic, where Socrates brings his guardians to the threshold of "gazing upon the Good" and falls silent — and where the Imam supplies the devotional method Plato left unspoken.*

## Where this episode opens

How do you actually see the Good — not think about it, not define it, but see it? That is the question on which the whole essay finally turns, and it is the question the greatest philosopher of the ancient world reached and could not answer. When Socrates, in the Republic, brings his readers to the very summit of knowledge, the vision of the Sovereign Good from which everything else derives its value, he stops. Pressed to say what the Good is, he confesses it is beyond his powers and offers instead to speak only of its offspring. He knows the vision is the source of all right ordering in the state and the soul; he cannot say how it is attained. The letter to Malik al-Ashtar, Shah-Kazemi argues, answers precisely there — not with a better definition but with a method, a way of actually drawing near to the Real, which the Imam names in his final and most insistent counsel: prayer, and its essence, the remembrance of God.

We reach this having watched sacred justice descend from the nature of God all the way into the tax registers and grievance-hearings of a province — and having seen that the just person acts from an inner taste of divine reality rather than for any outcome the world can grant. That left one question open: how is such a taste acquired? A disposition cannot be legislated, and it cannot be willed into being from nothing. It has to be drawn from a source. This episode names the source. If justice is a quality rooted in the Real, then nearness to the Real cannot but draw a person nearer to justice and to every other positive quality — spiritual, intellectual, and moral. And nearness to the Real is attained, most directly, in prayer offered with the whole of oneself, and in the sustained remembrance that is prayer's living core.

Three movements carry the episode, and the book, to their close. First, prayer as the lynchpin — why the Imam makes worship the most important means by which the sacred substance of all the virtues is assimilated, and what the astonishing hadith of drawing near reveals about where such worship leads. Second, remembrance and justice — how the practice the Imam calls the polish of hearts makes a person not only steadfast in justice but able to teach it. And third, gazing on the Good — the Platonic parallel that crowns the essay, where the Imam's devotional method illuminates what remains implicit in the philosopher's pedagogy, and where the whole argument returns, at last, to the vision that sees God through the verities of faith.

## Prayer, the lynchpin

Shah-Kazemi calls it, without hedging, the lynchpin of the entire epistle: the most important means by which the sacred substance of all the virtues is assimilated by the ruler — prayer. The letter had opened with a counsel to observe the obligatory prayers; now, after all the instructions on administering the classes of society at their proper times, the Imam returns to worship and gives it the place of honor. Malik is told to set apart the most excellent portion of his available time for what is between him and God, and to pay special attention to the obligatory prayers as the means by which he purifies his religion for God. And Shah-Kazemi insists this is not merely an exhortation to perform the formal prayers. It must be understood in the light of the deeper meaning of purifying one's religion for God — purifying the very substance of the relationship between the soul and God, for it is the depth of that relationship which determines the quality of all human virtue. "Know that your every act is dependent upon your prayer," the Imam says: your every action depends upon the way you gaze upon God, who is the source of all good. What matters is not only the formal, physical, verbal performance of the prayer but the inward quality that accompanies and enlivens it.

That single instruction — that every act depends on prayer — reorders the relation between worship and work that we ordinarily assume. We tend to think of prayer as one activity among many, a duty performed and then set aside so that the real business of the day can proceed. The Imam reverses the hierarchy. Prayer is not one item on the list; it is the thing on which every other item depends, because the way a person gazes upon God determines the quality of everything he then does. A ruler's justice in the courtroom, his honesty with the treasury, his mercy toward the destitute — none of these is generated in the courtroom or the treasury; they are generated in his prayer and merely expressed in his work. This is why the letter, having spent so many pages on the concrete offices of the state, returns at its climax to worship: not as a pious afterword but as the disclosure of where all the rest was coming from all along. Get the prayer right, and the administration follows; get the prayer wrong, and no set of rules will save the administration, because the source that was supposed to feed it has run dry.

That inward quality demands everything a person has, and the Imam's language for it is strikingly physical. "Give unto God of your vital energy in your nights and your days," he tells Malik, "and perform fully that by which you draw near to God, doing so perfectly, without becoming dull or deficient, taking your body to its limits." The whole emphasis falls on that phrase — drawing near. One attains divine nearness most directly through deep devotion, offered up alone, in the dead of night, with all one's heart. And once that nearness is realized, all one's other actions in the world are imbued with something of the spiritual quality gained through divine proximity. This is how all action can be assimilated to devotion in the wider sense — every outward act truly governed by the sincere intention to give oneself to God. If one's inner life is dominated by prayer, prayer comes to penetrate one's outer life as well; there is an overflow, as it were, from the fountain of obligatory and voluntary prayer into the whole domain of action. Hence the Imam can say, immediately after telling Malik to reserve the best of his time for God, that all times and the actions performed in them are for God if the intention behind them is good and if the subjects derive security as a consequence. The ruler's every act partakes of the sacred to exactly the degree that his devotion truly reaches into the source of the sacred.

There is a further reach in that overflow, and the Imam names it in a single phrase whose implication the whole essay has been preparing. All the emphasis, he says, should fall on drawing near — and the drawing near is achieved most directly through deep devotion offered in solitude, in the dead of night, with the whole heart. Why the night, and why alone? Because it is there, stripped of the world's noise and of the observing eyes of others, that the soul can give itself to God without the contamination of self-display that is the ruin of virtue. The night vigil is the purest form of the sincerity the whole book has been tracing: an act done for God alone, witnessed by God alone, and therefore uncorrupted by the desire to be seen. And the fruit of that solitary nearness is not private; it flows outward. Once nearness is realized in the secret hours, it colors the public ones, so that a ruler's dealings with his subjects by day carry something of the quality won in his prostrations by night. This is the mechanism by which prayer becomes the lynchpin of governance and not merely of piety: the treasury and the courtroom are administered justly to the exact degree that the administrator has drawn near to the source of justice in the dark, where no one but God could see him do it.

The deepest expression of this overflow is a saying the tradition treasures above almost any other — the divine word on drawing near, spoken by God in the first person through the Prophet. "My slave draws near to Me through nothing I love more than that which I have made obligatory for him. My slave never ceases to draw near to Me through supererogatory acts until I love him. And when I love him, I am his hearing by which he hears, his sight by which he sees, his hand by which he grasps, and his foot by which he walks." Shah-Kazemi reads this as the key to the whole relation between worship and justice. To see and act through God, at one level at least, is to see things as they really are and then to act upon that vision — which is nothing other than perfect justice, the placing of each thing in its proper order. This is why the Imam repeatedly draws the reader of his sermons back to the vision of God as the true foundation of a life of virtue: in prayer we efface the ego through prostration, and that submission through self-effacement is itself what puts all things in their right place, the ego most of all. The hadith also marks a crucial distinction — between the obligatory acts and the supererogatory ones. Both are means of drawing near, but it is primarily through the voluntary, freely offered devotions that the slave comes to perceive and act through the divine. And it insists again on effort: it is far from enough to perform prayers mechanically or perfunctorily. "He who prays without making an effort," the Imam says, "is like one who shoots arrows without a bow." The sincerity that the oneness of God demands calls for the integration of the whole soul, and it is in prayer that the deepest dimensions of the soul are expressed, cultivated, and integrated — so the prayer must be accomplished not with the tongue or the body alone but with all that one is.

## Remembrance and justice

At the heart of prayer, as its living essence, lies the practice the Imam calls remembrance — dhikr — and he introduces it here, in relation to justice, though its full unfolding waits for the chapter to come. He does so in a commentary on the Qur'anic phrase describing men whom neither commerce nor trade diverts from the remembrance of God. "Truly God has made the remembrance a polish for the hearts," the Imam says, "by which they hear after suffering from deafness, and see after being blind. There have always been slaves of God with whom He held intimate discourse through their thoughts and spoke with them through the essence of their intellects." Remembrance, in this teaching, is not the acquisition of new information but the polishing of a mirror already there — the clearing of the heart until it can once more perceive the reality it was made to reflect. And by remembrance the Imam means two things held together: a methodic practice of invoking a divine Name, and a principle of permanent awareness of God embracing every mode of worship, meditation, and reflection. It is to be understood not merely as recalling God but as an intense, prolonged, prayerful meditation on ultimate reality, represented symbolically and sacramentally by one or more of the divine Names. For this remembrance, as the opening chapter's teaching on the intellect already established, is not learning from scratch but the recollection of immutable principles inherent in the very substance of our intellects, by which everything can be understood, and which are rooted in the divine nature of our Creator. The Imam himself signals that this aspect will be treated more fully further on; here his concern is its bearing on justice.

The image of the polish repays a moment's attention, because it carries the whole doctrine in a figure. A mirror does not generate the images it shows; it reflects what is set before it, and it does so faithfully only when its surface is clear. The heart, in this teaching, is such a mirror, made to reflect the Real — and remembrance is the polishing that removes the film obscuring it, so that it hears after deafness and sees after blindness. The knowledge thus recovered was never absent; it was only occluded. This is why the Imam can speak of slaves of God with whom He held intimate discourse through their very thoughts and spoke through the essence of their intellects: once the mirror is clear, the divine converse that was always addressed to it can finally be received. And the point for justice is exact. A clouded heart cannot see things as they are, and a person who cannot see things as they are cannot place them in their right order — cannot, that is, be just. The clearing of the heart and the capacity for justice are therefore one work seen from two sides, which is why the practice that polishes the mirror is the same practice that produces the just soul.

And that bearing is direct, for the Imam draws an explicit line from remembrance to the capacity for justice. Describing those illuminated by the practice, he says: "Truly there are people who belong to the remembrance; they have adopted it in place of the world, such that neither commerce nor trade distracts them from it; they spend the days of their life in it; they instruct people about justice and themselves are steadfast therein." The sequence is the whole point. It is those who truly belong to the remembrance who are not only steadfast in justice but able to teach it to others — for, putting first things first, they alone can show how to place all things in their proper order. A person who has cleared his own heart until it perceives the Real can order his own soul justly; and only a soul so ordered can order the world around it or instruct another soul in the ordering. Justice, in other words, is not a technique that can be transmitted apart from the inner state that produces it. One teaches justice by first embodying it, and one embodies it by remembrance.

Shah-Kazemi draws out the mutual dependence this creates between worship and justice, and it closes the circle the whole essay has been drawing. Fervent, lived spirituality is not opposed to dedicated activity in the world; it infuses that activity with an ethical depth springing from direct contact with the divine, a contact articulated by prayer in general and by remembrance in particular. Just as the sincerity of a good intention is proven only by the effort to realize it, so the sincerity of prayer is proven — and rendered effective — by the sustained effort to prolong and intensify it, taking one's body to its limits. And the relation to justice becomes exact. If justice is grasped as a quality rooted in the Real, then nearness to the Real cannot but draw one nearer to justice and to every positive quality, spiritual, intellectual, and moral. The relationship between worship and justice must therefore be firmly established for the conception of justice to deserve the name sacred. From this vantage, justice is at once cause and consequence of worship. On one hand, justice requires that everything be given its due, and God must accordingly be given His due — which entails, sacramentally, worship and prayer; to fail to worship is itself an injustice, a withholding from God of what is His. This is a striking consequence of the sacred conception, and it is easy to miss. If justice is giving each thing its right place and its rightful due, then the very first debt of justice is owed not to any creature but to the Creator, who is owed worship; a person who deals scrupulously with every human claim while withholding what he owes to God has not achieved justice but only a fragment of it, and a fragment resting on a void. The prayer the Imam presses on Malik is thus not an addition to his justice but its foundation — the settling of the primary debt, without which every other account is out of true. On the other hand, justice emerges ethically as a consequence of that worship, as do all the virtues, in proportion to the nearness God grants His worshipper and the radiant repercussions of that nearness. Worship makes the just, and justice is owed to the worshipped; the two are bound in a single circle whose center is the Real.

## Gazing on the Good

Now the essay reaches its crowning comparison, and it is worth stating plainly what Shah-Kazemi claims and what he does not. He does not reduce the Imam to Plato or Plato to the Imam. He argues that the Imam's stress on prayer and remembrance illuminates, and in a sense completes, a position Plato reached and could not carry further — that the Imam makes explicit, in spiritual terms, what remains implicit in the philosopher's pedagogy. The Republic, after its long inquiry into justice, comes to the crux: the nature of the Sovereign Good, "the highest object of knowledge," from which everything good and right derives its value, and without which all other knowledge is worthless. Socrates draws his famous analogy between the Good and the Sun — what the Sun is to visibility, the Good is to intelligibility and being — and then the allegory of the cave, and then the long educational program, mathematics through dialectic, designed to turn the prospective rulers from the world of shadows to the world of real things. At the summit, when they are fifty and have proved themselves at every stage, they must, he says, "lift up the eye of the soul to gaze on that which sheds light on all things; and when they have seen the Good itself, take it as a pattern for the right ordering of the state and of the individual." But Plato nowhere states what this gazing actually means, nor by what means — intellective, contemplative, or methodic — it is to be achieved. Pressed by Glaucon to say whether the Good is knowledge or pleasure or something else, Socrates demurs: "I am afraid it is beyond my powers; with the best will in the world I should only disgrace myself and be laughed at."

It is exactly here that Shah-Kazemi sets the Imam's answer to a strikingly parallel question, and the resonance is uncanny. A man named Dhi'lib al-Yamani, very much in the manner of Glaucon, provocatively asked the Imam whether he had seen the object of his devotion. The Imam replied, "I would not be worshipping a lord whom I have not seen." Dhi'lib pressed: "O Commander of the Faithful, how did you see Him?" And the Imam gave the sentence that serves as one of the whole book's leitmotifs: "Eyes see Him not through sight's observation, but hearts see Him through the verities of faith." Where Socrates fell silent before the how of the vision, the Imam answers it — not by describing the Good, which no eye can observe, but by naming the organ and the means through which it is genuinely seen. The heart sees, and it sees through the verities of faith. And this returns us at once to the polishing of the heart effected by remembrance, for that very word — remembrance — evokes the essence of Plato's own account of knowledge as recollection. For Plato, learning is anamnesis, the soul remembering what it already knew; for the Imam, remembrance is the recollection of the immutable divine principles inherent in the substance of the intellect. The two traditions meet on the word. Yet Plato, Shah-Kazemi notes, never makes explicit the devotional or contemplative counterpart of his intellectual program; there is no stated link in his dialogues between remembering the forms that engender knowledge and gazing upon the Good that sheds light on all things — no passage from knowing the forms to knowing, as Islam would put it, the Bestower of Forms.

The gap is worth naming precisely, because it is the exact space the Imam's teaching fills. Plato has an epistemology of ascent — the soul is turned, stage by stage, from shadows to realities, until it can bear to look at the source of all light. What he lacks is a method of contact with that source once the soul arrives before it. He can describe the climb; he cannot describe the seeing. His analogy of the Sun is telling in just this way: the Good is to the intelligible world what the Sun is to the visible, the source by which all else is known and is — but one does not reach the Sun by argument, and Plato offers no discipline by which the eye of the soul is actually opened upon it. In Islamic terms, he teaches the knowing and remembering of the forms, the ideas that engender true knowledge; he does not teach the knowing of the One who bestows the forms, the reality the tradition names in the divine Name the Fashioner, al-Musawwir. To know the forms is to reach the threshold; to know the Bestower of Forms is to cross it. And crossing it, on the Imam's account, is not a further act of intellection at all but an act of devotion — the heart, polished by remembrance, seeing what the eye cannot observe.

Why Plato left the method unspoken is itself a question, and Shah-Kazemi finds a clue in the philosopher's own most personal text. In his Seventh Letter, Plato denies that any exposition can be given of the true reality of philosophy, pointing instead to a disciplined way of life and a rigorous daily routine that the subject demands. "No treatise by me concerning it exists or ever will exist," he writes. "It is not something that can be put into words like other branches of learning; only after long partnership in a common life devoted to this very thing does truth flash upon the soul, like a flame kindled by a leaping spark, and once it is born there, it nourishes itself thereafter." This is as close as Plato comes to acknowledging what the Imam makes central: that the Real, indefinable in its essence, cannot be assimilated by the mind alone but only existentially, through a way of life. The means of that assimilation cannot be set aside as mere superstructure. And this, Shah-Kazemi argues, is exactly what the Imam supplies and Plato withholds — the actual method of drawing near, prayer and remembrance, through which intellectual comprehension and spiritual devotion become inseparable and complementary. Integral, deep-rooted justice emerges as one of the fruits of that drawing near, just as, for Plato, gazing on the Good yields the capacity to take it as the pattern for the right ordering of the state and the soul. The philosopher pointed to the summit; the Imam described the ascent.

Before that descent, Shah-Kazemi pauses to state how far the Imam's illumination of justice reaches, and the claim is deliberately expansive. The letter was addressed, in the first instance, to a prospective governor of Egypt in the first century of the Islamic era; but its approach to justice, he argues, is by no means outmoded. Whether one operates in an avowedly Islamic or an overtly secular environment, whether the rule is autocratic or democratic, whether political institutions are strong or weak — wherever individuals are vested with power and authority, and by that very fact exposed to the temptations power offers, the approach to justice articulated in the Imam's ethos will not be irrelevant. For that ethos presupposes no particular form of government. It engages the universal theme of moral responsibility, but does so in terms of that which transcends morality, offering insights drawn from a direct vision of ultimate spiritual realities. And precisely because the principles contemplated through that vision surpass the domain of politics, they are capable of penetrating it from above and refashioning the very substance of political consciousness. The relevance of the Imam's perspective, in short, extends to every context in which a sense of the sacred plays any part at all in the shaping of moral sensibility and the cultivation of political responsibility — which is to say, potentially, to all of them.

From this height the essay makes its final descent into conclusion, and gathers the whole book's argument into a few strokes. Any integral approach to politics and society in Islam, Shah-Kazemi insists, must take into account the requirements and the fruits of worship in the widest sense. For sincere worship does something no argument can: it transforms the notion of the Absolute into a concrete reality, imparting to the soul an existential impetus and a spiritual resource that buttresses, stabilizes, and deepens the whole moral life. What is derived from consciousness of the divine Reality, then, is not so much a philosophy of ethics as a spiritual morality — an existential orientation that is at once the consequence of a lived spirituality and the cause of its ever-deepening realization of the Real, al-Haqq. The difference is decisive. A philosophy of ethics is a set of propositions one holds in the mind; a spiritual morality is a mode of being one lives from the heart. The first can be sound and still leave the person who holds it unmoved and unchanged; the second is nothing until it has changed the one who lives it. This is why the Imam's ethos cannot finally be extracted as a theory and studied at a distance. It exists only where it is enacted, in a soul actually drawing near to God — which returns the whole argument, one last time, to remembrance, the practice by which that drawing near is done. Virtue, from this perspective, is a form of ethical realism, where realism means the assimilation of spiritual reality; and morally correct behavior translates that realism into action across every plane, individual, social, and political. Against the charge that this is hopelessly utopian — that the harsh exigencies of politics and the sublime ideals of spirituality are poles apart — the reply has already been given by the Imam's own caliphate, run amid civil war without cynicism or surrender. And there is a warning in the conclusion that echoes the essay's opening, now sharpened to a point. For an Islamic concept to be grasped rightly, it must be assimilated not by the mind alone but by the heart, the inmost point of consciousness that sees the true nature of things. Without the vision of God as if one could see Him, there is no ihsan in the ethico-spiritual sense; and as the opening of this discussion argued, it is precisely the denial and subversion of this inner tradition that leaves religion an empty shell, transformed into an ideology, driven by passion, and open to political manipulation — the soil in which those who would hurt creation in the name of pleasing the Creator take root. Behind this stands the principle the whole book has been driving toward, the integration named by tawhid. The word does not mean only the affirmation that God is one; it means the making of many into one — and it lays a demand on the person who affirms it. The principle of justice requires that all the elements of a life — devotion, worship, ethics, action — be bound together into a harmonious, indissoluble whole, in accordance with that oneness; and tawhid means precisely this integration, so that when a person's theory and practice have themselves become one, that person has become a true bearer of the oneness he professes. This is why there can be no detaching the domain of the virtues from the domain of spirituality. The only true virtue, in the Imam's perspective, is one rooted in, sustained by, and oriented toward the divine Reality; a virtue not so rooted — even one that happens by coincidence to mirror an Islamic value — is baseless and without spirit, and, cut off from its source, dwindles back into the situational, self-serving thing the opening of the essay diagnosed. And remembrance is the practice that performs the integration: it is not the learning of something new but the recollection of the immutable principles inherent in the substance of our intellects, the principles by which everything else is understood, rooted in the divine nature of our Creator. It is, in the fullest sense, the cognitive and philosophical foundation of ethical thought.

Justice, together with all the other virtues, can be brought to fruition only in a soul, and a society, permeated by spirituality, whose essence is the remembrance of God. In the last analysis, Shah-Kazemi concludes, it is that remembrance which determines the quality of human virtue.

## What this episode lands

This final movement has named the wellspring the whole essay had been circling. Sacred justice descends from the nature of God, orders the ruler's soul, and reaches into the machinery of the state — but the disposition that makes all of it possible is drawn, in the end, from prayer and its living essence, remembrance. We have seen why the Imam calls prayer the lynchpin of the entire letter: because it is the means by which the sacred substance of every virtue is assimilated, and because, as the hadith of drawing near reveals, the one who never ceases to draw near is finally loved by God, who becomes the very hearing and sight and hand by which he acts — which is to say, becomes his capacity to see things as they are and place each in its proper order. We have seen that remembrance makes a person able not only to be just but to teach justice, since one instructs others in right order only by first embodying it. And we have watched the essay reach its boldest stroke: the parallel with Plato, who brought his guardians to the threshold of gazing upon the Good and fell silent, where the Imam supplied the method the philosopher could not — the heart seeing God through the verities of faith.

With this the book's central essay on justice closes, and its argument is complete: that the ethical orientation toward justice is immeasurably deepened when consciously linked to the divine, and that the deepening comes not through better theory but through the lived practice of drawing near. The essay ends by pointing beyond itself. Everything has finally rested on remembrance — the polishing of the heart, the recollection of the Real, the practice at the core of prayer — and the Imam himself deferred its full treatment to what comes next. The book now turns to make remembrance its whole subject: what dhikr is, how it polishes the heart until the divine is seen in all things, and how a person is at last effaced in the very Reality he remembers. The justice traced through the letter to Malik was one fruit of drawing near to God; the coming chapter follows the drawing near itself, all the way to its root. Where this essay treated remembrance as the source of a particular virtue, the next will treat it as the heart of the whole spiritual life — the polish of the heart become the theme rather than the tool, pursued until the one who remembers is himself effaced in the Reality he had set out only to recall.
