# The Spirit of the Intellect

*This episode enters the heart of Reza Shah-Kazemi's argument about Ali ibn Abi Talib (peace be upon him): that the faculty at the very center of his spiritual life is the intellect — but not the intellect as the modern world understands it. For the author, the Arabic word aql names something far larger than the reasoning mind, a contemplative faculty capable of seeing the divine directly, and standing in creative harmony with revelation rather than in opposition to it. The episode traces why the author prefers the English word 'intellect' over 'reason', how the Qur'an becomes the center around which that faculty revolves, and what it means to say the intellect holds buried treasures waiting to be unearthed. It moves through Ali's answer to a bedouin on the edge of a battlefield, the apophatic opening lines of the first sermon of the Nahj al-balagha, and the Qur'anic image of God breathing His spirit into clay. Through all of it runs a single claim: the intellect is first and foremost a spiritual faculty, and the spirit, in turn, is its source and its life.*

## Where this episode opens

What if the faculty modern people dismiss as cold, calculating reason is only the thin surface of something that can actually see God? That is the provocation at the center of this episode. The previous episode closed by placing Ali ibn Abi Talib at the summit of the spiritual chains that later generations would trace back to the Prophet Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him) — the gate through which knowledge passes, the first pole around which a whole tradition of inner life would turn. This episode asks what actually flows through that gate. And the answer the author gives is unexpected: what flows through it is intellect.

The word invites a modern reader to picture argument, calculation, the clever manipulation of concepts. Shah-Kazemi wants to dismantle that picture before it can settle. When Ali speaks of aql, the author argues, he is not naming the reasoning mind at all, or at least not only that. He is naming a spiritual faculty — one that surpasses the operations of the rational mind while containing them, and that reaches into domains a modern person would never file under intelligence: moral conduct, aesthetic sensibility, the direct perception of transcendent reality. This is the faculty the author calls the spirit of the intellect, the living relationship between the intellect and the spirit that animates it.

The episode builds this case in three movements. First, it establishes why 'intellect' is the right word and 'reason' the wrong one — a distinction that will govern everything else. Second, it shows how this intellect stands in harmony with revelation rather than against it, so that the Qur'an becomes not an external decree imposed on the mind but a reminder of what the intellect already, secretly, knows. Third, it follows the author into the strangest and most paradoxical territory — the language in which Ali affirms and negates the divine attributes in the same breath — and out again into the Qur'anic account of creation, where God breathes His spirit into clay and it stands up a human being. The whole arc bends toward one conclusion: to be true to one's intellect is to be spiritual.

## Intellect versus reason

The author begins with a translation choice, because the whole argument depends on it. Why prefer the English word 'intellect' to the word 'reason' as a rendering of aql? The answer reaches back past ordinary English usage to the original meaning of intellectus in Latin Christendom — a meaning, Shah-Kazemi notes, virtually identical to that of nous in the Patristic Greek tradition. The Latin intellectus means 'understanding', and the author draws out something hidden inside that plain word. To understand a thing is, quite literally, to stand under it: to place oneself beneath a perception of reality and allow that perception to overshadow and govern one's actions. This is not a pun for its own sake. Human beings always act in the shadow of how they perceive things; conduct follows vision. So the faculty of understanding, of standing under a truth, is the faculty by which a perceived reality comes to command a life.

Intellectus, or nous, is therefore the faculty capable of a direct, contemplative vision of transcendent realities — the very realities of which the Qur'an speaks but which lie hidden from the senses. Reason is something else. Reason translates the Latin ratio and the Greek dianoia, and it is indirect and discursive by nature. It works through logic. It arrives only at mental concepts of those realities, never at the realities themselves. The author points to the root of ratio, which denotes a relation or a comparison between two numbers, and finds in it the whole character of reason's method: reason proceeds by comparison, by a binary logic of affirmation and negation, weighing one thing against another until it has defined what it is considering.

Here the distinction sharpens to a point. With the intellect, a person is able to contemplate or, in the author's word, to see the Absolute. With reason, a person can only think about it. To know about a reality is one thing; to contemplate and experience that reality directly is quite another — and it is the intellect, not reason, that is capable of that experience. Reason can carry a person only as far as the outward form of a thing, as far as the mental concept or image of a reality, and no further. It stops at the surface.

This does not make reason worthless. The author is careful, and the care matters for everything that follows. Reason, so understood, is the instrument the intellect employs. It is not a rival faculty to be discarded but a tool to be picked up. The intellect is brought to its contemplation through reason, even though reason on its own could never reach the destination. The relationship is one of mode to principle: reason is one of the modes of the intellect, one expression of a faculty far larger than itself. Reason no more contradicts intellect, the author insists, than intellect contradicts revelation. To equate the two — to say that the intellect is nothing more than the reasoning mind — would be to deny the very existence of what the author will keep calling the buried treasures of the intellect, those supra-rational depths that lie hidden, initially, beneath the rational surface.

The distinction has a texture worth dwelling on, because it is easy to hear it as a simple hierarchy and miss the subtlety. Between the spiritual depths of the intellect and its rational surface there is both continuity and discontinuity. The continuity is plain: all thinking begins with concepts that carry intelligible meaning. Thought sets out from ideas that are rationally discernible, and those ideas must bear some real relation to the realities they express, or they collapse into notions that lead nowhere. So the rational surface is genuinely continuous with the depth below it; one begins there. But there is discontinuity too, and the author locates it precisely. Reason stops short at the ideas it has, in his phrase, de-fined. The Arabic word for definition, hadd, carries — like its English counterpart — two senses at once: the sense of a conceptual definition, and the sense of a limitation, a boundary. To define a thing is to draw a boundary around it, to fix it by weighing its features until it is bounded and set. And a boundary, by its nature, keeps things out. To go beyond the limits imposed by this rational act of definition, something more than reason is necessary. That something more is exactly what makes the ethos under discussion a spiritual one and not a rationalistic one.

The author is precise about the difference between those two words. This ethos is certainly rational, in the sense that its outward form is fully susceptible to rational scrutiny — it can be examined, argued, laid out in orderly terms. But it is not rationalistic, because its essence is not predicated on reason alone. A rationalist in the strict sense treats intellect and revelation as mutually exclusive, two authorities that cannot both be sovereign. In the perspective the author is describing, they are nothing of the kind.

This translation choice, then, is not a matter of pedantic preference. It opens onto three factors that the author names as inherent to the whole intellectual perspective he is trying to convey, and the rest of the episode is, in effect, an unfolding of these three. The first is the centrality of revelation — the Qur'an as the fixed center around which the intellect must revolve. The second is the harmony between revelation and the intellect — the creative reciprocity in which each requires the other. And the third is precisely this distinction just drawn, between the intellect conceived as the principle of the articulation of consciousness, and reason understood as one expression of that principle. The distinction between intellect and reason, in other words, is not the whole argument; it is the doorway into it. What lies on the other side is a claim about how the human mind and the revealed word belong to one another.

## Revelation and the inner prophet

Everything about Ali's life and teaching, the author argues, is centered on the Qur'anic revelation and its embodiment in the conduct of the Prophet. The Qur'an is the center around which the intellect must revolve — and this metaphor of a center is exact, not decorative. The intellect needs a center. It needs principles and definitions to hold fast to if it is to be guided rightly, and left to spin without one it loses its bearing. Both the thought and the life of Ali can be read, the author suggests, as a kind of spiritual commentary on the sacred text — a work of ta'wil, of interpretation that returns things to their origin. On the doctrinal plane, he states it plainly: Ali possessed an innate aptitude for the work of ta'wil. And on the existential plane too, the whole span of his life — from his birth within the most holy shrine of the Ka'ba to his martyrdom in the mosque of Kufa — reads as a dramatic unfolding of the inner meaning of the revealed word. Ali's refusal to stop at the superficial surface of things, whether religious or ethical or political, expressed an unflinching fidelity to the sacred substance of the religion — a fidelity that meant both inward intellectual penetration and outward combative resistance.

The word ta'wil deserves its weight here, because the author gives it a literal sense. To perform ta'wil is to retrace appearances back to their source and origin — to go back to the awwal, the First. This is not fanciful reading imposed on a text. It is a movement of return, and the author grounds it in a scene that shows the whole doctrine in action.

Just as the battle of the Camel was about to begin, a bedouin approached Ali and asked him a question. "O Commander of the Faithful! Do you say that God is one?" Those standing nearby rebuked the man, telling him this was neither the time nor the place for such a question. Ali overruled them. "Leave him," he said, "for surely what the bedouin wishes is what we wish for the people." The author reads this moment as a key to Ali's entire spiritual ethos. Had the Imam been guided by reason alone, he would have agreed with the ones who rebuked the bedouin and sent him off to find a fitting time. But he saw with the intellect that stands above reason. For Ali, the most fundamental intention behind every action — religious or, as here, military — is knowledge of the true nature of ultimate reality. What he wishes for the people is precisely this knowledge, which must lie at the heart of all concerns and all actions, on the battlefield exactly as on the prayer-rug. There is therefore no time and no place where the question of what it means to say God is one becomes irrelevant.

So on the very field of battle, Ali delivers a discourse on the meaning of divine oneness — and its first words carry the whole. He refutes any notion that God's oneness is numerical or countable. It is not permissible, he tells the bedouin, to say 'one' while holding any number in mind, for, in the phrase the author returns to again and again, that which has no second does not enter into the category of numbers. The moment a thing can be counted as one, it admits the possibility of two, of three, of four — and a oneness understood in that numerical way is undone by the very number meant to affirm it. That which has no second, the author explains, is not merely a divinity to which no partner may be ascribed, as a theologian might phrase it. It is that reality which has no otherness at all — the Real, at once transcendent and immanent, beyond all things and manifested by all things. To say God has no otherness is not to say that other beings exist which might have been His partners but which the believer politely declines to associate with Him. It is to affirm that nothing whatsoever in existence bears His likeness, nothing that could conceivably be set beside Him.

In those few words, the author says, Ali effects — for those who have ears to hear — a shift of consciousness: from the dogmatic picture of one God against many gods, to the mystical apprehension of the one and only Reality, in the face of which everything else is mere appearance. And once that shift occurs, the whole visible world changes character. All things come to be apprehended as outward phenomena calling to be deciphered, as signs pointing beyond themselves to that of which they are the manifestations. A sign, an aya, is precisely a meaningful token that points past itself to something else. Retracing these appearances back to their source is the literal meaning of ta'wil — going back to the First. On one side, the Real is the beginning and the end of all things, the apparent and the hidden: "He is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward" (chapter 57, verse 3). On the other side, apparent multiplicity is to be brought back to real oneness: "We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and in their own souls, so that it be clear to them that He is the Real" (chapter 41, verse 53).

The author reinforces the point with the tradition surrounding this gift of interpretation. In one saying attributed to Ali, the Prophet is described as the first of those whom God taught the science of ta'wil, and who in turn taught it to Ali. According to the Prophet, in a saying already noted in the portrait, Ali is with the Qur'an and the Qur'an is with Ali — the two are not to be separated. In another saying often quoted by later commentators, Ali claimed he could load seventy camels with the pages of commentary he could offer on the opening chapter of the Qur'an alone. And Ali's own cousin, often called the first commentator of the tradition, is reported to have said he learned the science of interpretation from Ali himself. The picture the author assembles is of a man for whom the sacred text was never a flat surface but a well of inexhaustible meaning, and whose whole life was a descent into it.

That inexhaustibility is why the author insists the harmony of intellect and revelation exposes the hollowness of the false dichotomies later imposed on Islamic thought. The apparently insuperable opposition between intellect and revelation shows up in a whole family of polarized forms — reason against faith, convention against nature, imitation against verification, piety against inquiry. In Ali's ethos, the author argues, none of these hold as true oppositions. Intellect and revelation are not mutually exclusive but complementary, and at the highest level inseparable, truth being one. The human intellect plainly cannot be equated with divine revelation; but the spirit that animates the intellect is not, in the final analysis, confined by human limitation, and it is this spirit that perceives and grasps the divine quality of revelation in the first place. Far from curtailing the operations of reason and imposing some unintelligible decree, revelation presupposes a creative engagement with all the resources of the intelligence — precisely those processes of reflection and meditation, of fikr, that the Qur'an repeatedly calls for. Sustained reflection does not enclose the meaning of the text within its literal surface; it discloses it, opening the inner horizons of meaning folded within the letter.

This is where the theme of interpretation deepens into something structural. There is a saying of Ali the author treats as fundamental: "The Qur'an consists of a book inscribed, between two covers; it speaks not with a tongue, it cannot do without an interpreter." The revealed text is silent. It is inscribed between two covers and it does not speak with a tongue of its own — it speaks only through its interpreter, and that interpreter is the intellect. From this follows the whole reciprocity the author is after. He sets beside it a second saying: the prophet of a man is the interpreter of his intellect. And a third, more explicit still: the intellect is the inner messenger, the messenger of the Real. The silent scripture on the page and the interpreting intellect within the person are two poles of a single act; the outer revelation requires the inner faculty to become speech, and the inner faculty requires the outer revelation to know what it is hearing.

It is against this background that the author sets the saying he treats as one of the keys to the entire ethos — a line from the first sermon of the Nahj. One of the purposes for which God sent forth the prophets to mankind, Ali says, was to unearth for them the buried treasures of the intellects. The intellect is not stifled by revelation; it is brought to life by it. Revelation is a clarion call to the spiritual depths of the intellect, cultivating and bringing to fruition seeds of consciousness — the seeds of tawhid — that God has already planted there and that lie buried within. For the spirit of a person's intellect, the author argues, already carries the initial imprint of its Creator. In the world of the spirit it witnessed its Lord and testified to His lordship, and revelation — the dhikr, the reminder — does no more than remind it of that primordial testimony. A reminder, after all, is never a new piece of information. It is knowledge one already possesses but has forgotten, for a person can only be reminded of what he already knows.

The author draws the image out with a physician's analogy. Human beings were created into this world in a kind of amnesia. And just as a physician tells the family of a man who has lost his memory to surround him with familiar things, with signs out of his past, to jog his memory, so God has set His reminders in the horizons and in the souls. The spirit of one's intellect, then, cannot be alien to what descends as revelation from on high. On the contrary, it must be fundamentally akin to it — and it is precisely for this reason, the author says, that a person can be certain the revelation truly comes from the Absolute. If the human intellect can perceive and recognize revelation, it is because both are rooted in one and the same ultimate source. Both came from a spiritual realm; the intellect was sent into the body and the revelation was sent into the world, each to remind of what was once witnessed and has since been forgotten. The point, the author stresses, is not to set up a dichotomy between intellect and revelation but to see the intellect as revelation, or at least as one mode of it — the God-given means by which a person comes to discern, affirm, and plumb the revelations outside himself, both those of scripture and those constituted by the signs of the natural world. Prophetic revelation awakens and quickens the depths of the human intellect; it does not replace or displace it. The messengers do for these buried seeds what the farmer does for what lies hidden beneath the soil, what the miner does when he digs into the mountain for its gold and silver, what the diver does when he descends to the sea-bed to bring up its buried treasures. Whereas the strict rationalist regards intellect and revelation as mutually exclusive, in this perspective they are perfectly harmonious — two things rooted in one source, each requiring the other for completeness, for the making-one that is the very essence of tawhid.

## The breath of the spirit

If the intellect is a faculty of contemplation, the author now asks what kind of language could possibly serve it — and the answer is a language that seems, at first, to defeat itself. Ali frequently hints, in highly allusive terms, at the unearthed treasures of the intellect that he has witnessed directly by the eye of his purified heart. But he also alludes to that supra-rational awareness indirectly, through a bewildering use of apophatic discourse and paradoxical utterance, of which the corpus attributed to him is full. In this ethos, the author observes, the higher the truth, the deeper the paradox. The sheer incommensurability between reality as it is in itself and the human conceptions formed of it is evoked through this creative use of paradox — and the paradox is not destructive. Unlike a modern deconstruction so radically corrosive that it has nothing left to reconstruct, Ali's apophasis feeds directly into contemplation and realization of what reason, on its own, could never attain.

Nowhere is this style more apparent than in the famous opening lines of the first sermon of the Nahj al-balagha:

"Praise be to God, whose laudation those who speak cannot deliver, whose graces those who count cannot number, whose rightful due those who strive cannot render; He who cannot be grasped by far-reaching aspirations, nor fathomed by profound intuitions; He whose attribute has no binding limitation, no existing description, no time appointed, no term extended."

The author works patiently through the paradox folded into those lines. The divine attribute, the sifa, has no limit in terms of which it could be defined — and yet it is affirmed as real. It has no existing description, and yet in some sense it is described, by the very words of the Qur'anic revelation, where God is called the Merciful, the Compassionate, the Almighty. He possesses these attributes, and yet Ali affirms that the very same attributes cannot be defined, bounded, or described. The paradox sharpens further still when the Imam speaks of the perfection of ikhlas, the rendering-sincere, the purification of one's devotion to God and of one's conception of Him. This perfection, he says, is to divest God of all attributes. On one hand the attributes are affirmed; on the other they are to be negated. The rational mind can only think of God in terms of the attributes given verbal expression in the Qur'an — and here Ali appears to negate those same attributes, stripping the mind of any convenient conceptual handle by which to grasp the nature of the divine Reality.

The author insists this contradiction is not a failure of clarity but an instrument. The simultaneous affirmation and negation of the attributes raises an apparently irresolvable contradiction, and in doing so it enhances receptivity to a deeper mode of knowing. This kind of discourse seems designed to awaken in the listener some latent awareness inscribed in the heart, in the depths of the intellect, and to invite the seeker to dive into those depths, into the knowledge buried in the intellect. The very shock of the apparent contradiction is meant to precipitate a leap of consciousness — from the formal activity of reason to the supra-rational contemplation of the spirit.

The structure of the paradox, the author shows, is the structure of the testimony of faith itself. In Ali's discourses one meets a repeated combination of affirmation and denial that mirrors the negation and affirmation of the first testimony of Islam: first the negation, 'no god', the nafy; then the affirmation, 'except God', the ithbat. This is the key that resolves what looked irresolvable. The negation of the attributes that Ali calls the perfection of ikhlas is in fact an affirmation of the infinitude of the divine Reality. Each attribute is to be negated — but only insofar as it is conceived by the mind. Such negation is necessary because, at the mental level, each attribute excludes the others, and excludes even the object of attribution itself, the divine Essence, since the mind cannot help distinguishing between the quality and the One it qualifies. The reality of the attribute, however, is unlimited, by virtue of its absolute identity with the Essence. The attribute that has been negated is paradoxically affirmed — not in itself, but inasmuch as it is nothing but God. One can therefore place each negated attribute in the position of the negation in the testimony and arrive at formulations like: there is none all-compassionate except God, none all-merciful except God. The apparent negation of the attributes becomes, in truth, an affirmation of their real identity, cleansed of any restrictive association with the poverty of mental conception. The attributes are not added onto the Essence; they are one with it, distinguishable only in thought, not in reality. Ali's apophasis, the author suggests, reads as a commentary on the Qur'anic refrain, "Glorified be God above what they describe" (chapter 23, verse 91). What must be negated is all that the mind can conceive through reason — but not all that the heart can see through spiritual vision.

This deeper mode of knowing has, in Ali's vocabulary, an organ — and it is not the eye. The author connects the buried treasures of the intellect to what Ali calls the heart, the qalb, that innermost mode of perception capable of seeing God. It is the heart that can see God — not with eyesight, not with basira in the sense of ordinary sight, but with insight, with the spiritual vision the author names basira, generated by the verities of faith. When a questioner asked Ali whether he had seen his Lord, his answer drew the distinction exactly: "Eyes see Him not through sight's observation, but hearts see Him through the verities of faith." The negations of the apophatic discourse, then, are not the last word. They call out to something deeper, and that something can only be understood in the setting of spiritual practice — the reflective, devotional, contemplative life assumed as the context of everyone who heard these sermons. The heart, the seat of contemplative intelligence, asks to be polished, and what polishes it is the remembrance of God, the dhikr, which Ali calls the most excellent form of worship. Even there a further paradox waits: the reality of the remembrance is not reached until, in Ali's words, you forget yourself in your remembrance. It is not only the reasoning mind and its concepts that are finally to be negated, but the individual soul itself as the locus of all knowing — the ultimate form of apophasis, the fana' toward which the whole movement tends.

And this is where the whole doctrine of the intellect turns back toward the account of what a human being is. The author reads the first sermon as, among other things, a commentary on the Qur'anic verses that describe the creation of man in terms of God's breathing of His spirit into him: "He fashioned him and breathed into him of His spirit" (chapter 32, verses 7-9). Ali's own words expand the scene. God gathered up dust from hard earth and soft, from sweet earth and salty, poured water onto it until it was pure, kneaded moisture into it until it stuck together, fashioned from it a form with curves and joints, limbs and parts, compacted it until it held tightly and solidified it until it rang like sounding clay. "Then He breathed into it of His spirit," Ali says, "and it stood up as a human being, endowed with intellectual powers with which to reflect, thoughts by which to conduct himself, limbs to put to service, instruments at his disposal, and knowledge with which to discern between the true and the false."

The author lingers on the exact hinge of that sentence. The body had been fashioned whole — its eyes, its ears, the organ of thought all in place — and yet it could neither hear nor see nor reflect until God breathed His spirit into it. Only then did it stand up as a human being. It is this in-breathing, this literal in-spiration, the insertion of the spirit, that produces consciousness and life. Death, by contrast, is the ex-piration, the departure of the spirit; and a life lived without aspiration — without the intention to be true to one's spirit — is a kind of death. It is through the spirit that inanimate, unconscious matter is brought to life and to awareness of itself, generating not only knowledge but self-knowledge. This intimate intertwining of the gift of life and the capacity to know — existence that is conscious, knowledge that is existential — is, the author writes, one of the foundations of any truly spiritual perspective. The question is never one of setting spirit against body. It is the infusion of the spirit into the body that brings the body to life, and it is for this reason that knowledge itself vivifies, in the very measure that it is spiritual: "O ye who believe, respond to God and His Messenger when they call you to that which vivifies you" (chapter 8, verse 24). And one of the most important aspects of that call, according to Ali, is to remind human beings of the buried treasures of the intellect.

So existentiation — the bringing of a being into existence — is nothing other than the bestowal of the spirit, which is consciousness. The aql, the intellect, the principle that articulates the spirit, is therefore not merely a mental faculty but is itself constitutive of a person's true life. The mental faculty is one formal expression of the intellect, one mode of its operation, and by no means the only one. The aql is many-faceted, comprising aspects that a modern age rarely associates with intelligence at all. To be true to one's intellect — true to the treasures buried deep within it, and not merely to the rational functions running along its surface — is, the author concludes, tantamount to being spiritual. This is why, for Ali, the true intellectual is not the clever reasoner but the one who thinks correctly, acts ethically, and, at the deepest level, seeks to realize ultimate Reality. The intellectual is defined as one who puts all things in their proper place — one who aligns his acts with the intentions of his Creator. That, the author notes, is also the very definition of justice: only the one who sees things as they truly are can put them in their right place, and so only the true intellectual can be fully just. Thought, action, and realization are all bound together in the one faculty.

## What this episode lands

The episode lands on a single, load-bearing claim. The intellect, in Ali's perspective, is first and foremost a spiritual faculty — and the spirit, in turn, is the source both of the intellect and of life itself. Without the intellect, the author writes, the spirit is reduced to unconscious existence; without the spirit, the intellect tends toward lifeless thought. The two need each other. And because they do, the three things a modern reader would keep apart — correct thinking, impeccable virtue, and authentic being — turn out to be inseparable, all of them the concern of the true intellectual, the one who puts each thing in its place. This reframing of what intelligence even is prepares the ground for everything the book will argue about Ali's ethos as a universal spirituality.

These aspects come into sharper focus, the author says, in the light of a particular discourse — the private counsel Ali gave to one of his closest disciples. The next episode follows him as he takes that disciple by the hand and leads him out of the city to a graveyard, and, once the city lies behind them, draws a deep breath and begins to speak. It is there, in a conversation held among the dead, that the doctrine of the intellect meets its most searching test: what kind of heart is fit to receive knowledge, and what happens to knowledge when it falls to those who are not.

## Closing

The wager of this episode is easy to state and hard to absorb. The faculty a modern person is most tempted to dismiss as cold — reason, calculation, the shuffling of concepts — is, in Ali's understanding, only the outermost surface of something immeasurably deeper: a spiritual faculty that can contemplate the Real directly, that carries within it the buried memory of a testimony given before birth, and that revelation exists to reawaken rather than to override. To think well, on this account, is not finally different from living well or seeing truly; they are one act performed by one faculty. The intellect must be negated in its pretensions precisely so that it can be fulfilled in its depths. What that fulfilment demands of a particular human heart — and what corrupts it — is the question waiting in the graveyard outside Kufa.
