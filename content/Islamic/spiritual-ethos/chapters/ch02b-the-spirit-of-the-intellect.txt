# The Spirit of the Intellect

*This second episode of Shah-Kazemi's study enters the very heart of the Imam's ethos through a single word: the intellect. Where the previous episode traced the outline of the Imam's life and gathered the sayings that name him the gate to prophetic wisdom, here the study asks what kind of knowing that gate actually transmits. The answer turns on a distinction most of us never draw — between intellect and reason — and on three convictions that hold the Imam's worldview together: that revelation stands at the centre, that revelation and the intellect are in harmony, and that the reasoning mind is only one mode of a far deeper faculty. Along the way we will meet a bedouin's question thrown out on the edge of a battlefield, the buried treasures of the intellects, and God affirmed and denied in the very same breath.*

## Where this episode picks up

What if the deepest knowing you are capable of is not the work of your reasoning mind at all — what if the mind that weighs, compares, and concludes is only the shallow surface of a faculty whose depths it can barely sound? That is the inversion this episode is built on, and it is a strange thing to say, because we are so used to treating clear reasoning as the summit of the human intelligence rather than its outer edge. The study we are following insists on the reverse. There is a mode of knowing that stands above reasoning, that sees rather than infers, that contemplates a reality directly instead of assembling a concept of it — and it is this higher faculty, not the discursive mind, that the tradition means when it speaks of the intellect.

The previous episode did the work of introducing Ali (ع) to us: it sketched the arc of his life and set out the cluster of sayings by which he is remembered as the gate through which the wisdom of the Prophet Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him) passes to the community. It closed on the most pointed of those sayings — that Ali (ع) would fight for the inner meaning of the Qur'an, its ta'wil, exactly as the Prophet had fought for its outer revelation, its tanzil. That saying left a question hanging in the air, and this episode is the answer to it. If Ali (ع) is the gate, and if what he guards and transmits is the hidden meaning beneath the letter of the revealed text, then everything depends on the character of the knowing that reaches that inner meaning. A gate is only as valuable as what passes through it. So we now turn from the man and his titles to the faculty by which he knew — the faculty the study calls the spirit of the intellect, a spirit that surpasses while containing the ordinary operations of the rational mind, and that reaches into domains we no longer tend to associate with intelligence at all, such as moral bearing and the sense of beauty.

This episode moves through three linked ideas, each of them a facet of that single theme. First, it explains why the study reaches for the word "intellect" rather than "reason," and lays out the three framing convictions that govern the whole perspective. Second, it shows the Qur'an standing at the centre of the intellect's work, with the intellect as its appointed interpreter, and it draws out what it means to call the revelation a reminder of something the soul already knows. Third, it enters the paradoxical style for which the Imam is renowned — the simultaneous affirming and denying of God's attributes — and traces that style down to its root in the breathing of the divine spirit into the human being, and to the definition of the true intellectual as one who puts every thing in its proper place. By the end you will see why, in this ethos, being true to one's intellect and being spiritual turn out to be one and the same thing.

## Intellect, not reason

It is worth beginning exactly where the study itself begins, with a deliberate choice of translation. The Arabic word at the centre of everything that follows is al-'aql, the intellect (al-'aql, العقل), and the study explains at the outset why it prefers to render it as "intellect" rather than "reason." What it wants to recover is the older meaning that the word intellectus carried in Latin Christendom, a meaning very nearly identical to that of nous in the Greek of the early Church. Intellectus means understanding, and buried in the word is the sense of standing under a thing. To understand something, on this reading, is to stand beneath a perception of reality and let that perception overshadow and govern what one does — for our actions are always performed in the shadow of how we see things. Intellect, so understood, is the faculty capable of a direct, contemplative vision of transcendent realities: the very realities the Qur'an speaks of, which are hidden from the senses. It does not reach them by inference. It sees them.

Reason is something else. It translates the Latin ratio and the Greek dianoia, and it is indirect and discursive by nature; it works through logic and arrives only at mental concepts of those realities. The very root of ratio is telling — it denotes a relation or comparison between two numbers — and this captures precisely how reason proceeds: by comparison, by a binary logic of affirmation and negation, weighing one thing against another until it has fixed and defined what it is considering. Reason, on this account, is the instrument the intellect employs, and an indispensable one; but it can carry us only as far as the outward form of a thing, only as far as the mental image or concept of a reality. The difference the study is pressing can be put very simply. With the intellect one is able to contemplate, to "see," the Absolute; with reason one can only think about it. To know about a reality is one thing; to contemplate and experience it directly is quite another — and it is the intellect, not reason, that is capable of that experience, even though it is often through reason that the intellect is first brought to it.

Before entering the Imam's own words, the study asks us to hold three framing convictions steadily in mind, because the whole perspective rests on them. The first is the centrality of revelation. Everything about the life and teaching of Ali (ع) is centred on the Qur'anic revelation and its embodiment in the conduct of the Prophet. The Qur'an is the centre around which the intellect must revolve, for the intellect needs a centre — principles and definitions to hold fast to if it is to be guided rightly. The second conviction is the harmony between revelation and the intellect. Where a strict rationalism treats intellect and revelation as mutually exclusive, this perspective sees them as perfectly harmonious, subsisting in a state of creative reciprocity, each requiring the other for its completeness, for the very "making one" that is the essence of tawhid. The third conviction is the distinction we have just drawn — between the intellect, conceived as the principle by which consciousness is articulated, and reason, understood as one expression of that principle. Reason is a mode of the intellect, not its equal; to collapse the two is to deny that the intellect has any depths beneath its rational surface at all.

That last point deserves to be pressed, because it is where the spiritual character of this ethos is decided. Between the depths of the intellect and its rational surface there is both continuity and discontinuity. The continuity is plain: all thinking begins with concepts that carry intelligible meaning, and these concepts must bear some relation to the realities they express or they lead nowhere. But there is discontinuity as well, in that reason stops short at the ideas it has defined. Here the study turns on a single Arabic word and its English counterpart alike. The word for definition, hadd (حد), carries two senses at once — a conceptual definition, and a limitation or boundary. To define is to draw a limit; and the very boundary reason draws around an idea, weighing its pros and cons until the idea is fixed, is also the boundary at which reason halts. To go beyond that limit, something more than reason is required, and it is this "something more" that makes the ethos a spiritual one and not a rationalistic one. The perspective is certainly rational, in that its form can be examined by reason; but it is not rationalistic, because its essence is not predicated on reason alone.

What lies beyond the boundary the study calls the "buried treasures" of the intellect — the supra-rational dimensions that lie, at first, hidden beneath the rational surface. And it names the innermost of these depths with a word that will recur through the whole book: the heart (qalb, قلب). This is not the seat of sentiment but the innermost mode of perception, the faculty capable of "seeing" God. It sees not with eyesight, not with the bodily faculty the tradition calls basara (بصارۃ), but with insight, with basira (بصیرۃ) — that spiritual seeing generated by the verities of faith (haqa'iq al-iman, حقائق الایمان). The Imam himself puts the whole distinction into a single answer. Asked whether he had seen his Lord, he replied that eyes do not see God through sight's observation, but that hearts see Him through the verities of faith. That is the intellect at its deepest reach: not the mind reasoning about God, but the heart contemplating Him. And it is why the study insists from the start on the higher word. To call al-'aql "reason" would be to shut the seeker inside the very surface the Imam is forever trying to open.

## The Qur'an at the centre

If the intellect needs a centre to revolve around, that centre is the Qur'an — and the intellect's task before it is not to stand over the text but to interpret it. Both the thought and the life of Ali (ع) can be read as a single extended spiritual commentary, a ta'wil, upon the sacred text. On the doctrinal plane he possessed an innate aptitude for that work; on the plane of his very existence, his life reads as a dramatic unfolding of the inner meaning of the revelation, from his birth within the sanctuary of the Ka'ba to his martyrdom in the mosque of Kufa. His refusal ever to stop short at the surface of things — religious, ethical, or political — was a fidelity to the sacred substance of the religion that demanded both inward penetration and outward, combative resistance. This is the sense in which he would fight for the ta'wil of the Qur'an as the Prophet fought for its tanzil. His wars were not power struggles; the study makes the strong case that all three — the Camel, Nahrawan, and Siffin — were waged for the correct interpretation of the Qur'an and for the realization of its message of oneness on every level, spiritual and social at once.

Nothing shows this more vividly than a scene the study dwells on. Just as the battle of the Camel was about to begin, a bedouin approached the Imam and asked, "O Commander of the Faithful, do you say that God is one?" Those present rebuked him — this was neither the time nor the place. But Amir al-Mu'minin intervened: leave him, he said, for what the bedouin wishes is precisely what we wish for the people. Had the Imam been guided by reason alone, he would have sided with those who told the bedouin to find a fitter moment; but he saw with the intellect that stands above reason. For him the most fundamental intention behind any action — whether an act of worship or, as here, an act of war — is knowledge of the true nature of ultimate reality. What he "wishes for the people" is exactly this knowledge, which must lie at the heart of every concern and motivation. There is therefore no time and no place where the question "What do you mean when you say God is one?" is out of place. On the very field of battle, then, the Imam delivers a discourse on the meaning of divine oneness.

And the first thing he does is refuse any notion that God's oneness is numerical. It is not permissible, he tells the bedouin, to say "one" while holding any countable conception in mind, for that which has no second does not enter into the category of numbers. The moment a thing can be counted as "one" it admits the possibility of "two" and "three," and so a oneness understood in that arithmetical way is undone by the very number that would affirm it. "That which has no second" is not merely a divinity to which no partner may be ascribed, as a theologian might put it. It is that reality which has no otherness at all — the Real, al-Haqq, at once transcendent and immanent, beyond all things and manifested by all things, simultaneously inaccessible and inalienable. To say God has no otherness is to affirm that nothing whatever in existence bears His likeness, nothing that could conceivably be set beside Him. In these few words the Imam works, for those who have ears to hear, a shift of consciousness — from the doctrinal picture of one God against many gods, to the mystical apprehension of the one and only Reality, in the face of which everything else is mere appearance.

Once that shift has taken place, the world itself changes character. All things come to be read as outward phenomena calling to be deciphered, as signs (ayat, آیات) pointing beyond themselves to that of which they are the manifestations — for a sign is precisely a meaningful token that points past itself to something else. And retracing these appearances back to their source is the literal meaning of ta'wil: a return to the awwal, the First. On the one hand the Real is the beginning and the end of all things, the outward and the inward, so that there is nothing in being but this one Reality — "He is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward" (chapter 57, verse 3). On the other hand the apparent multiplicity of things is to be brought back to that real oneness — "We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and in their own souls, so that it becomes clear to them that He is the Real" (chapter 41, verse 53). The intellect's whole labour is this movement of return: from the letter of the revelation to its spirit, and from the spirit of the revelation to the spirit of the Revealer.

But the revealed text cannot make that movement on its own, and here the study places one of the Imam's most fundamental sayings. The Qur'an, he says, is a book inscribed between two covers; it does not speak with a tongue, and it cannot do without an interpreter. The revelation is silent; it speaks only through the intellect that interprets it. How deeply its meaning is plumbed depends entirely on the depth and penetration of the interpreter's own intellect, since the text carries many levels of meaning at once, and every literalism that would flatten it into a single surface reading is thereby excluded. The reciprocity runs in both directions: the interpreter of a man, the Imam says, is his intellect, and the intellect is itself the messenger of the Real — an inner messenger set within the soul, answering to the outer messenger who brought the book. At its deepest, this "prophetic" intellect rejoins the very source of the revelation, for it is this inner prophet, the inmost degree of consciousness, that is the ground on which a person can confirm the outward verses as true — not merely affirm them by dogma, but validate them through lived experience.

It is worth pausing on how completely Ali (ع) is bound to the text in the tradition's memory of him. In one saying the Prophet is described as the first whom God taught the science of ta'wil, and who in turn taught it to Ali (ع); in another, often quoted by Sufi commentators, the Imam declares that he could load seventy camels with the commentary he could offer on the Fatiha alone, the short opening chapter of the Qur'an. His cousin Ibn 'Abbas, remembered as the first of the commentators, claimed to have learned the science of interpretation from him. And the Prophet's own words seal the bond: Ali (ع) is with the Qur'an, and the Qur'an is with Ali (ع). This is not the relation of a reader to a book he happens to have studied closely. It is a mutual indwelling — the interpreter so wholly given over to the text that its inner meaning and his own intellect become, in the study's phrase, two faces of one reality.

Seen from this angle, the Imam's discourses expose the hollowness of a whole set of false or over-sharpened oppositions that later came to be imposed on Islamic thought. The apparently insuperable clash between intellect and revelation shows up in many polarized guises — reason against faith, convention against nature, blind imitation against genuine verification, piety against inquiry — and in each case the Imam's synthetic perspective dissolves the opposition, since for him truth is one and these are not rival goods but partners. Far from curtailing the operations of the mind, the revelation presupposes a creative engagement with all the resources of the intelligence. The Qur'an constantly demands fikr, deep and sustained reflection, and such reflection does not enclose the meaning of the text within its literal surface; it discloses it, opening the inner horizons folded within the letter. The letter is not a wall but a door — and the intellect is what walks through.

This is why the study can say something that at first sounds startling: that the intellect is not stifled by revelation but brought to life by it. In the first sermon of the Nahj al-balagha the Imam says that one of the purposes for which God sent the prophets was to unearth for people the buried treasures of the intellects, the dafa'in al-'uqul. Revelation, on this reading, is a call addressed to the spiritual depths of the intellect, cultivating seeds of consciousness — the seeds of tawhid — that God has already planted there and that lie buried within us. For the spirit of one's intellect already carries the initial imprint of its Creator: in the world of the spirit it witnessed its Lord and testified to His lordship, and revelation, the reminder (dhikr, ذکر), does no more than remind it of that primordial testimony. A reminder is never new information; it is knowledge one already possesses but has forgotten, for one can only be reminded of what one already knows. We were created into this world in a kind of amnesia — and just as a physician tells the family of a man who has lost his memory to surround him with familiar things out of his past to jog it, so God has set His reminders in the horizons and the souls. The spirit of the intellect cannot be alien to what descends as revelation; it must be fundamentally akin to it, and it is precisely this kinship that lets a person be certain the revelation truly comes from the Absolute. The messengers do for these buried seeds what the farmer does when he waters what lies hidden beneath the soil, what the miner does when he digs into the mountain for its gold and silver, what the diver does when he descends to the sea-bed to bring up its buried treasures. Revelation does not replace the intellect. It awakens it.

## Affirmation and denial

There is, however, a further and stranger feature of the Imam's discourse, and it is here that the spirit of the intellect shows most clearly how far it exceeds the reasoning mind. Ali (ع) speaks of God through a bewildering use of paradox — affirming and denying in a single movement — and the corpus attributed to him is full of it. In this ethos it seems that the higher the truth, the deeper the paradox, for the sheer incommensurability between reality as it is in itself and the concepts we form of it can only be evoked, never captured. Each human conception of the divine needs, in a sense, to be undone; but this undoing is nothing like a merely destructive scepticism. The Imam's negation feeds directly into contemplation, into modes of spiritual intuition that reason on its own could never reach. His negations do not so much cancel reason as situate it, revealing the limits of its proper sphere: reason alone cannot comprehend the higher realities, however indispensable it is in first setting the mind moving toward them.

Nowhere is this style more apparent than in the famous opening of the first sermon of the Nahj al-balagha, where the Imam praises God as the one whose full praise those who speak cannot deliver, whose graces those who count cannot number, whose rightful due those who strive cannot render; who cannot be grasped by far-reaching aspirations nor fathomed by profound intuitions; whose attribute has no binding limitation, no existing description, no appointed time, no extended term. The divine attribute has no limit by which it could be defined — and yet it is affirmed as real. It has no existing description, and yet in some sense it is described, by the very words of the Qur'an, for there God is named the Merciful, the Compassionate, the Almighty. He truly possesses these attributes; and yet the Imam affirms that these same attributes cannot be defined, bounded, or described. The paradox sharpens still further when he speaks of the perfection of ikhlas — the rendering sincere, the purifying of one's devotion to God and of one's very conception of Him. That perfection, he says, is to divest God of all attributes. On one side the attributes are affirmed; on the other they are to be negated. The reasoning mind can only think of God through the attributes the Qur'an gives it, and here the Imam appears to strip those attributes away, denying the mind any convenient handle by which to seize the divine nature.

This is not confusion; it is design. The simultaneous affirmation and negation of the attributes raises an apparently irresolvable contradiction, and in doing so it enhances the listener's receptivity to a deeper mode of knowing. The style is meant to awaken some latent awareness inscribed in the heart, and to invite the seeker to dive into the knowledge buried in the intellect. The very shock of the apparent contradiction is intended to precipitate a leap of consciousness — from the formal activity of reason to the supra-rational contemplation of the spirit. Consider the words the Imam speaks immediately after affirming that his heart had indeed witnessed God through the verities of faith: his Lord, he says, is not described in terms of dimension, nor motion, nor rest, nor through coming or going; He is subtle in subtlety but not described by subtlety, magnificent in magnificence but not described by magnificence, great in greatness but not described by greatness. Again the attributes are affirmed and denied at once. God possesses them but is not reducible to them; we may begin to think of Him through these categories, but He is not exhausted by our conceptions. The mental portrait must first be recognized as insufficient before it can be given the dimension of depth — and it is the cognitive shock of paradox that reveals its insufficiency.

The study finds the key to this whole style in the shape of the first testimony of Islam, the declaration that there is no god but God. That formula moves in two beats — first the negation, "no god," and then the affirmation, "but God." The Imam's affirming-and-denying of the attributes follows the same rhythm. The negation of the attributes, which he calls the perfection of ikhlas, turns out to be an affirmation of the infinitude of the divine Reality. Each attribute is negated, but only insofar as it is conceived by the mind — for at the mental level each defined attribute excludes the others and even excludes the Essence to which it is ascribed, since any concept, to be a concept, must have limits, and where there are limits there are exclusions. The reality of the attribute, however, is unlimited, by virtue of its absolute identity with the Essence. So the negated attribute is paradoxically affirmed — not in itself, but inasmuch as it is nothing other than God. One can place each attribute into the first, negating beat of the testimony and arrive at formulations such as "there is no All-Compassionate but God," "no All-Merciful but God." The apparent denial becomes, in truth, an affirmation of the attribute's real identity, stripped of any restrictive tie to the poverty of our concepts. The attributes are not added onto the Essence; they are absolutely one with it, distinguishable from it only in thought, never in being. The Essence comprises them all and is infinitely more than them; and the attributes, for their part, are infinite only within and as the Essence. The Imam's paradox is thus a living commentary on the Qur'anic refrain, "Glorified be God above what they describe" (chapter 23, verse 91). What must be negated is everything the mind can conceive through reason — not everything the heart can see through spiritual vision; and this negation calls out for something deeper, a polishing of the heart accomplished by the remembrance of God, which the Imam names the most excellent form of worship. Yet even here the paradox reaches one turn further, for the reality of that remembrance, the Imam warns, is not attained until you forget yourself in your remembering. It is not only the reasoning mind and its determinate concepts that must be undone, but the individual soul itself as the locus of all cognition, rational and supra-rational alike — the final and ultimate form of this apophasis, in which the one who remembers is no longer standing apart from what he remembers. The negation that began with a mere idea ends by consuming the self that had the idea.

This is why the study keeps insisting that the intellect must be understood, first and foremost, as a spiritual faculty, and that the spirit, in turn, is the source of the intellect just as it is the source of life. The two cannot be prised apart without ruin to both. Without the intellect the spirit is reduced to unconscious existence, mere life that does not know itself; and without the spirit the intellect drifts toward lifeless thought, a machinery of concepts turning over nothing. The whole burden of the apophasis — the affirming and denying, the shock of paradox, the polishing of the heart — is to keep the intellect from settling into that second, lifeless condition, forever pressing it back toward the living spirit that is its source.

All of this rests, finally, on the deepest thing the intellect is: not a faculty of the mind but a gift of the spirit. The first sermon can be read as a commentary on the Qur'anic account of the creation of man, in which God fashions the human form and breathes into it of His spirit (chapter 32, verses 7 to 9). The Imam describes it in his own words: God gathered dust from hard earth and soft, from sweet and salty, kneaded it with water until it held together, and fashioned from it a form with curves and joints, limbs and parts; He compacted it until it held firm and hardened it until it rang like sounding clay. Then He breathed into it of His spirit, and it stood up as a human being — endowed with intellectual powers by which to reflect, with thoughts by which to conduct itself, with limbs to put to service, with instruments at its disposal, and with knowledge by which to discern the true from the false. The body had been fashioned entire, its eyes and ears and organ of thought all in place, and yet it could neither hear nor see nor reflect until the spirit was breathed into it; only then did it stand up as a human being. It is this in-spiration — the in-breathing of the divine breath — that produces consciousness and life. Death, by contrast, is the ex-piration, the departure of the spirit; and a life lived without aspiration, without the intention to be true to one's spirit, is itself a kind of death. Knowledge vivifies exactly in the measure that it is spiritual — which is why the Qur'an calls, "Respond to God and His Messenger when they call you to that which vivifies you" (chapter 8, verse 24).

From this the study draws its culminating definition. To exist is to be given the spirit, which is consciousness; and so al-'aql, the intellect — the principle by which the spirit is articulated — is not merely a mental faculty but is itself constitutive of our true life, the mind being only one of its expressions. The intellect is many-sided, comprising aspects our own age rarely associates with intelligence at all. To be true to it — to the treasures buried deep within it, and not merely to the rational functions running along its surface — is what it means to be spiritual. And so the study can define the true intellectual, al-'aqil (العاقل), as one who not only thinks correctly but acts rightly and, at the deepest level, seeks to realize the ultimate Reality. The intellectual is the one who puts every thing in its proper place, aligning his own acts with the intentions of his Creator. That, as the following chapters will show, is also the very definition of justice — so that only the true intellectual can be fully just, for only one who sees things as they truly are can put them where they belong. Thought, action, and realization are all the concern of the true intellectual: correct thinking, impeccable virtue, and authentic being, all of them bound up together in the spirit of the intellect.

## What this episode lands

By the close of this episode the study has fixed several things firmly in place before turning to any single teaching of the Imam. It has established, first, why the whole perspective reaches for the word "intellect" over "reason" — because the faculty in question sees rather than infers, contemplates rather than merely thinks about, and reaches into depths that reason, halting at the boundary of its own definitions, can never enter on its own. It has established, second, that the Qur'an stands at the centre around which this intellect revolves, that the intellect is the revelation's appointed interpreter, and that the revelation itself is a reminder — a call to unearth the buried treasures already planted in the soul, awakening a knowledge we once possessed and have forgotten. And it has established, third, that the Imam's paradoxical style of affirming and denying God in a single breath is not a puzzle to be solved but a door to be walked through: the shock of the contradiction is meant to lift the seeker from the surface of reason to the contemplation of the spirit, the same spirit that was breathed into clay to make a human being in the first place. The true intellectual, on this account, is the one who puts every thing in its proper place — and so being true to one's intellect and being spiritual turn out to name a single thing.

All of this has been the necessary groundwork. The study has told us what the spirit of the intellect is; it has not yet let us watch the Imam teach it directly to a living disciple. That is where the next episode goes. It follows Amir al-Mu'minin as he takes one of his closest companions by the hand, leads him out beyond the edge of the city to a quiet place, draws a deep breath, and entrusts to him a private counsel that later generations would gloss for centuries. There the abstract faculty we have been circling becomes utterly concrete: the human heart is likened to a vessel, and everything turns on how much it is able to hold. People are sorted into kinds by their relation to knowledge; a living teacher is distinguished from the store of learning he carries; and knowledge itself is weighed against wealth and found to be the greater possession by far. Everything this episode has argued about the intellect as a spiritual faculty, and about the buried treasures waiting to be drawn up from within, is about to be spoken aloud, in the plainest and most intimate terms, to a single man standing among the graves outside Kufa.
