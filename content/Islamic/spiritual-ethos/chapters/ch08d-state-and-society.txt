# State and Society

*Having given the ruler the two dispositions that matter most — freedom from pride and a heart infused with mercy — Ali (ع)'s letter to Malik al-Ashtar turns to the concrete machinery of governance: the classes of society and their interdependence, the soldiers and judges and scribes, the cultivators and merchants, and above all the destitute. This episode of Reza Shah-Kazemi's study reads this administrative core as justice at its sharpest edge, showing how each practical instruction is sealed with a spiritual message, how the whole social wheel turns only on its unseen connection to the Real, and how the just person's motive — intention rather than outcome — makes fidelity to the poor unbreakable where a merely worldly ethics would give way.*

## Where this episode opens

A state is a wheel — and a wheel is worthless without the one part of it you cannot see. That is the image at the heart of this movement of the letter, and it captures the whole paradox of governance as the Imam understands it. The governor sits at the hub; around him the classes of society radiate like spokes — the army, the scribes, the judges, the executive officers, the tax-collectors, the cultivators, the merchants, the artisans, and the needy at the rim. A well-built wheel, evenly spoked, turning smoothly: it is a fine picture of a functioning state, and for most of political thought it is the whole picture. But a hub is useless without the axle it turns on, and that axle is nowhere in the visible machinery. It is the ruler's connection to the Absolute, the vertical axis without which all his horizontal effort spins to no purpose. The most practical section of the whole letter, the one that reads most like an administrative manual, turns out to rest on the least visible thing of all.

We arrive here having watched the letter equip the ruler inwardly — freeing him from the pride that wars with God, filling his heart with the mercy that mirrors his own poverty before the Absolute. Now the letter puts that equipped soul to work. It sets out, class by class, the rights and duties of every segment of society, and Shah-Kazemi calls this section justice at its sharpest edge in actual practice, where the sacred conception must take full account of the hard demands of social and political life. What makes it more than a table of organization is that every practical instruction is sealed with a spiritual message. The pattern repeats so consistently it becomes the argument itself: the Imam describes a class, states the governor's duty toward it, and then anchors that duty in the nature of God or the destiny of the soul. This is where the reader who came expecting statecraft discovers that the letter has been doing theology all along — that the same sacred conception which ordered the ruler's soul is now ordering the tax office and the barracks and the courts, and that there is, in the Imam's world, no seam between the two.

Three movements carry the episode. First, the classes and the covenant — how the whole social order is bound together not by contract or convenience but by a covenant with God who has assigned each class its share, and why the letter distinguishes the scholars who know the law from the sages who know how to apply it. Second, justice at its sharpest edge — the letter's actual counsel on soldiers, judges, administrators, cultivators, scribes, and merchants, culminating in its most urgent instruction, the care of the poor. And third, intention beyond consequence — the teaching that finally makes fidelity to justice unbreakable, because the just person acts for the sake of justice itself and not for any outcome the world can withhold.

## The classes and the covenant

The letter opens its account of society by naming its parts and then binding them together in a way that is easy to read past. The Imam describes the different classes — the army, the scribes, the judiciary, the executive officers, the tax-collectors, the peasants who till the soil, the merchants, the artisans, and finally the destitute — and assigns to each its particular rights and duties. But the essential character of the governor's relationship to society as a whole is determined, once again, by his primary duty to God. For it is God, the Imam insists, who has prescribed to each class its share and ordained, as a binding covenant, the limits and duties of each, according to His Book or the practice of His Prophet. The governor does not invent the social order or negotiate it; he administers a settlement that is already fixed, preserved as a trust from God. "God has ordained the share of every one of them," the letter says, "and laid down His covenant about the limits of each in His Book and the Sunna of His Prophet, by way of a settlement which is preserved with us."

The word the Imam reaches for here is the one the Qur'an uses again and again — covenant, the 'ahd — and Shah-Kazemi shows how much weight that single term is meant to carry. The governor's duty to society is not a matter of policy or social contract in the modern sense; it is part of the universal covenant between man and God. By choosing this word to reinforce the obligation of acting justly toward all classes, the Imam knew his counsel would resonate with the verses his readers held sacred: the assurance that the men of substance are those "who abide by the covenant of God and break not the tryst"; the warning that one must "abide by the covenant — truly, the covenant will be asked about." To treat a class of society unjustly, in this frame, is not merely to mismanage the state; it is to break a covenant with God that will be called to account. The whole social order is thereby lifted out of the realm of the merely political and set within the realm of the sacred. Every farmer's due and every soldier's right is a clause in an agreement whose other party is God.

This changes the standing of the humblest classes decisively, and it is worth pausing on the consequence. In an ordinary political order, the weak are those whose rights are least secure, because they have the least power to enforce them; a destitute man's claim is only as strong as his ability to press it. But if every class's share has been assigned by God and sealed in a covenant He will ask about, then the strength of a claim no longer depends on the claimant's power at all. The poorest cultivator's due is guaranteed by the same covenant that guarantees the army's, and its violation is answerable to the same God. The vertical relation to God thus does something no horizontal arrangement can do: it gives the powerless a guarantor infinitely stronger than themselves. This is why the letter can move so naturally from the machinery of state to the care of those who can do nothing for the ruler in return — because in the covenant frame their weakness is not a reason their claims can be safely ignored but the very thing that places those claims most directly in God's keeping.

Just before setting out these instructions, the Imam gives Malik a piece of advice that Shah-Kazemi treats as a key to the whole administrative section: to study at length with the scholars and to converse at length with the sages, so as to consolidate the well-being of his lands and further entrench what his righteous predecessors had established. The distinction between the two matters greatly. The scholars are those who possess knowledge of the formal rules established in the Qur'an and the Sunna — the letter of the law. The sages are those who know how to apply that knowledge — the spirit of wisdom, for wisdom is knowledge put into practice. It is the difference between the letter of the law and the spirit behind it, the spirit without which the rules cannot be applied with care and sensitivity. A governor who consulted only the scholars would have the rules and no feel for how to use them; a governor who consulted only the sages would have judgment untethered from the law. Sacred justice requires both — the exact knowledge of what is owed, and the wisdom to render it rightly in the endless particular cases the rules cannot anticipate.

Then comes the wheel. The Imam describes the interdependence of the classes with admirable concision, beginning with the army as the foundation of the state and ending with the governor himself as the center around which the other elements revolve. The soldiers are the fortresses of the subjects; the scribes, the executive officers, the dispensers of justice, the tax-collectors, the merchants, the artisans, and the lowest class of the needy — all are spokes radiating from the governor at the hub and reaching to the rim of society. On the horizontal plane, the governor operates with creation, the point from which the whole structure is organized. But Shah-Kazemi presses the image to its decisive point. The hub is nothing without the axle that turns it. The horizontal center is nothing without the vertical axis connecting it — and through it, the whole of society — to the Real. Just as a hub is useless without its connection to the axle, all the governor's efforts are useless without his connection to the Absolute, upon which he must be wholly dependent if society is truly to flourish. The letter says as much plainly: the governor cannot accomplish what God requires of him without resolute determination and recourse to Him. The machinery of state, however well-built, turns on a connection that no census could count and no ledger could record. Strip away the axle and the finest wheel is only a heap of well-shaped parts; strip away the ruler's dependence on God and the finest administration is only a mechanism with nothing at its center to give it purpose or direction.

## Justice at its sharpest edge

With the framework set, the letter descends into particulars, and here Shah-Kazemi's point about every instruction being sealed with a spiritual message proves itself class by class. It is worth naming the pattern before watching it repeat, because the pattern is itself the argument. In each case the Imam does three things: he identifies a class, he specifies the governor's concrete duty toward it, and then he roots that duty in something that transcends administration — the nature of God, the destiny of the soul, the covenant that will be asked about. A lesser manual of governance would stop at the second step, handing the ruler a set of rules. The Imam never stops there, because he does not believe rules can be applied justly by a soul that does not see why they matter. The spiritual seal on each instruction is not decoration; it is what keeps the instruction alive in the hands of the man who must carry it out, and it is why this most practical section of the letter is also, from first to last, a spiritual one.

Take first the commander of the troops. Malik is told to select for this post the man most sincere toward God, the Prophet, and his Imam, the purest of heart, the most excellent in forbearance, slow to anger, happy to pardon, kind to the weak and severe with the strong, one who is neither stirred by violence nor held back by weakness. The portrait, needless to say, reflects the Imam's own character. What deserves notice is where the emphasis falls: on kindness, gentleness, and forbearance — qualities not ordinarily associated with fighting men. One discerns here a spiritually refined approach to an activity that, left to its own devices, easily runs roughshod over decency. Shah-Kazemi again hears the echo of Plato, whose central difficulty in forming the guardians of his ideal state was exactly this — how to ensure that men whose nature is spirited and courageous are kept from behaving fiercely toward their own people, gentle to their own and dangerous only to their enemies. This is precisely the Qur'anic description of those with the Prophet, "severe against the disbelievers, compassionate among themselves," from which the Imam draws his insistence on the gentle qualities a warrior needs as the essential complement to the martial ones.

The judges receive a similar treatment. The Imam names the virtues that must be present in those who dispense legal justice: they must not be confused by complexities or provoked by litigants, must be unflinching before the truth and free from greed, dissatisfied with superficial solutions, rigorous in argument, steadfast in searching out the truth of every matter, and immune to praise and temptation. "Such people," the Imam adds dryly, "are indeed rare." And he pairs this ideal with a practical safeguard that shows how seriously he means it: judges should be paid generously, so that any deficiency is removed and their need for help from others diminished — a prerequisite, as any age discovers, for keeping a judiciary incorruptible. The administrators are handled the same way. Malik is to test their honesty before employing them, to guard scrupulously against partiality and favoritism in his choices, and to keep monitoring and investigating their conduct afterward — with the stringent punishments already noted awaiting any abuse of privilege or misappropriation of public funds. There is a consistent wisdom running beneath these appointments, and the case of the judges' pay makes it visible: the Imam does not rely on virtue alone but arranges the material conditions so that virtue is not needlessly strained. A judge paid too little is pressed toward the very corruption the law forbids him; a judge paid generously has that particular pressure removed and can more easily remain what justice needs him to be. This is neither cynicism about human nature nor naivety about it, but the sober realism of a ruler who knows that most people are corruptible and arranges his administration to place as little weight as possible on the fault-lines of the soul. Sacred justice, applied to institutions, does not merely demand good men; it builds the structures within which ordinary men are least tempted to be bad.

The counsel on the cultivators of the soil, from whom the land tax is collected, contains one of the letter's most quietly radical maxims. "Let your concern with the cultivation of the land outweigh your concern with the collection of the tax," the Imam says, "for no tax will be collected if there be no cultivation. And whoever exacts the tax without cultivating the land ruins the land and destroys the people." The instruction inverts the ordinary priorities of a treasury: the health of the productive base comes before the revenue extracted from it, because revenue is a fruit and cultivation is the root, and a ruler who devours the root to feed on the fruit will soon have neither. The Imam presses further into the spiritual diagnosis behind ruinous taxation: "The devastation of the land only comes about through the destitution of its inhabitants; and the destitution of its inhabitants only comes about when the desire to amass wealth rules the souls of the governors, when they have doubts about what endures, and when they profit little from exemplary teachings." That phrase — doubts about what endures — is the spiritual hinge, and Shah-Kazemi lingers on it. The commentators read it as forgetting that one will die and be judged, and this reading fits, for it echoes the Qur'anic warning against the man who amasses wealth and counts it, imagining his wealth will render him immortal, only to be flung into the all-consuming fire. The Imam even uses the same word for amassing that the verse uses. The lesson is plain: greed is a symptom of spiritual myopia. Forgetfulness of what truly endures breeds greed — the desire for a false material richness in place of true spiritual plenitude — and greed in turn breeds exploitation and oppression. Once more the vice of injustice is shown to be not only a producer of corruption but itself the product of a chain of causes rooted in a fundamental inversion of spiritual values.

The scribes and the merchants close out the survey, each again sealed with its spiritual point. On the appointment of scribes, Malik is warned not to be taken in by those who show him only their best side; he must examine each candidate's prior record and the impression left on ordinary people, and appoint only those well known for trustworthiness — an exercise the Imam describes, strikingly, as proof of the governor's sincerity toward God. The choice of a competent clerk is thereby made a spiritual act, a rendering to God of honest judgment. The merchants and craftsmen bring diverse benefits to society and must be treated well; on the whole they are peaceable and should be left in peace to pursue their work, though the vices to which their trade is prone — hoarding, miserliness, and cheating — must be kept in check. In each case the same rhythm holds: a class named, a duty stated, and the duty rooted in something deeper than administration.

Then the letter arrives at the class the Imam most wants Malik to take to heart, and his manner changes. He opens the passage with the exclamation "Allah, Allah" — a cry that signals its urgency — before turning to the poor: those with no resources whatsoever, the destitute, the crippled, the orphaned, the elderly. These, he says, are the ones "most in need of justice from you," and they must be treated so that "God may excuse you on the day you meet Him." All of them, along with those who are in need but too proud to beg, should be provided for; the governor ought even to appoint an officer charged specifically with uncovering the needs of the most destitute and attending to them. And then the sentence that goes to the heart of the matter: "This is onerous for the governors, and the fulfilment of all rights is onerous, but God makes it light for those who aspire to the Hereafter, who restrain their souls in patience, and trust in the truth of that which is promised them by God." Here the administrative section reaches its edge and passes into something else. Caring for those from whom no political benefit can ever be derived is, by any worldly calculus, a losing proposition — which is exactly why the Imam grounds it not in calculation but in the aspiration for the Hereafter. It is only when this world is seen through to the next that a fully just attitude toward this world becomes possible. The very placement of the poor is telling. They come last in the survey of the classes — after the army and the scribes, the judges and the merchants — and yet the Imam signals that they are first in his concern, breaking his measured administrative prose with that doubled cry of the divine name to mark the shift. The order is deliberate: the classes that hold power in society are addressed first because they run the machinery, but the class that holds no power at all is the one on which the ruler's standing before God most directly turns. It is with the destitute, from whom nothing can be extracted and for whom everything must be given, that a governor's justice is finally weighed on the day he meets his Lord.

## Intention beyond consequence

That last instruction opens onto the teaching that finally makes sacred justice unbreakable, and it turns on a single reorientation: the just person acts for the sake of justice itself, not for any result the world can grant or withhold. The point is sharpened by the very difficulty of caring for the destitute. Those whose aspiration does not reach beyond this world are far more likely to neglect the poor, because in the horizontal calculus of politics the cost of charity to a ruler may well outweigh any visible benefit to himself. This is exactly where an ethics tied to a purely this-worldly assessment — to pragmatic politics or personal interest — exposes its flaw. But when the notion of what is owed to the poor is charged with firm faith in God's inexorable justice and infinite compassion, and suffused with aspiration for the Hereafter, an unflinching fidelity to their needs is generated and continually renewed. Such an attitude goes far beyond conventional charity. A person who has realized it will not be swayed by any contrary suggestion rooted in self-serving pragmatism; and with it firmly in place, the patience the Imam names next follows naturally, no longer dependent on personal will alone but sustained by the empowering grace that flows from heartfelt faith. Patience of that kind can be extended indefinitely. This is the sense in which the spiritual dimension is precisely what makes an otherwise crushing ideal practicable. The Imam did not soften the demand — the care of the destitute, the fulfilment of all rights, remains onerous, and he says so plainly. What he did was locate the strength to bear it somewhere other than the ruler's own finite will. Divine assistance, he promises, is assured for those whose attention is not confined to this world but reaches beyond it to the Hereafter; God makes the onerous light for them. The task itself does not shrink; the one carrying it is enlarged, drawn up into a grace that turns an unbearable weight into something that can, in fact, be borne — and borne without the resentment or exhaustion that would otherwise be inevitable when a ruler pours effort into those who can never repay him.

The deepest ground of this is the Islamic principle that actions are evaluated not by their consequences but by their intentions. One's intention is to be just — not for the sake of any earthly reward or tangible outcome, but purely for the sake of justice itself. And this essence of justice is inseparable from the divine nature, for, as the previous discussion established, God commands just acts precisely because they are just, and because justice is one with His own nature: "I bear witness that He is Justice and He acts justly." If God acts justly by virtue of His very nature, then the spiritual person acts justly out of a desire to conform to that nature — not merely out of obedience to a command, and never out of desire for an earthly reward. This liberates the just person from the material consequences of his actions in exact proportion to the sincerity of his intentions. Even if his effort to live justly is frustrated by the unpredictabilities of worldly life, the right intentions motivating him — together with his awareness of the immutable divine qualities as the models guiding his effort — produce a real, inward taste of the grace that furnishes the spiritual foundation of his moral life. Morality is thus deepened into spirituality, and the burden of duty is not merely lightened but transformed into an accompaniment of one's taste of spiritual reality. One then acts justly out of gratitude for what one has already received, and not only out of hope for something not yet attained.

Shah-Kazemi grounds this in the way the Qur'an itself speaks of the highest reward — not as something merely promised for later but as something already granted to the true believer, so that for one whose certainty of it is absolute, the future has in a sense already arrived. "We have given thee the abundant fountain; so pray to thy Lord and sacrifice," the Qur'an says, speaking of the reward in the perfect tense, as a gift already made. Worshipping God as a consequence of a certainty so complete that the promised beatitude has, in one respect, already come — this is the disposition the Imam names when he divides the worshippers into three kinds. And it has a concrete face in his own household. The commentators hold that a set of verses on the righteous, the abrar, was revealed on the occasion of the Imam's family going three consecutive days without food: at the end of each day, just as they were about to break their fast, someone in greater need came and asked for what they had, and each time they gave it away. The Qur'an records their motive in their own words: "They feed, out of love for God, the needy, the orphan, and the prisoner, saying, 'We feed you only for the sake of God; we desire from you neither reward nor thanks.'" This is the purity of intention the whole teaching is reaching for — good done for the sake of the absolute goodness that God is, with no eye to reward or even to gratitude. The Qur'an describes such a person elsewhere as one who gives of his wealth to purify himself, seeking nothing but the Countenance of his transcendent Lord, and who, precisely for that reason, "will be content" — imperturbably so, untouched by the blame or praise of others, neither deflated when adversity thwarts his good actions nor inflated when they succeed.

That distinction — acting from gratitude rather than for reward — the Imam himself draws with unforgettable clarity, dividing all worshippers into three kinds. "There is a group who worship God out of desire for something not yet attained, and this is the worship of the merchants. And there is a group who worship God out of fear, and this is the worship of the slaves. And there is a group who worship God out of gratitude, and this is the worship of the free." The just ruler who cares for the poor out of gratitude belongs to the third and highest kind: he is not trading present cost for future reward like a merchant, nor avoiding punishment like a slave, but rendering justice as a free man renders thanks — for its own sake, because it is right, because it is the nature of the God he loves. This is the ethos that the opening chapter already touched in its teaching that goodness carries its reward within itself; here that principle is applied directly to the labor of governing, so that the just act is already its own fulfilment even before any outcome arrives. Shah-Kazemi marks the difference from a secular ethic exactly here: a secular conception of justice stands or falls by its outward success in realizing its ideal, while a spiritual conception already sees the fulfilment of its ideal in the sincere intention to be just.

But this must not be misheard as an excuse for failure, and Shah-Kazemi is emphatic on the point, because the teaching is open to a dangerous misreading — as though thwarted good intentions could be used to legitimate actual injustice. The very opposite is the case. Holding an intention with total sincerity strictly requires diligent, ceaseless striving toward its realization; without concordant action, aspirations remain shallow and merely theoretical, and the sincerity is shown to be false. The just person, far from relaxing his effort, strives all the harder precisely because he is not depending on outward success for his vindication. However formidable the obstacles, however compelling the arguments of political pragmatism, however thoroughly his efforts are undone by the exigencies of outward life, he never despairs and never abandons the effort — because achieving justice in this world is not the whole of his aim. He knows the intention is already, invisibly but palpably, its own fulfilment; and he knows, with sober realism, that the outward realization of any intention depends on circumstances beyond his control and, ultimately, on the grace of God. As the modern theories cannot supply this, the difference tells. The relativism, nihilism, utilitarianism, and opportunism that the opening of this discussion set out are, whatever occasional value they yield, cut off from the divine and therefore from the inexhaustible spiritual resources that sincere faith supplies; when justice is conceived in isolation from spiritual principles, personal idealism is acutely vulnerable to outward contingency and all too likely to disintegrate under difficulty. In a sacred conception, by contrast, divine grace holds the incorruptible ideal high above the frailties to which soul and society are subject. The Imam's own caliphate proved it: a continuous succession of bloody conflicts against foes who professed Islam prevented him from enacting much of what his conception of justice required, yet none of it produced dejection or cynicism or the slightest slackening of his effort. For one whose inner life is shaped by the conviction that no act is negligible if accompanied by piety, despair is simply excluded — for such piety, the permanent awareness of divine Reality, is itself the way out. As the Imam says, "He who has pious awe toward God, for him God establishes a way out of tribulation, and a light out of darkness."

This is what allows the just ruler to work in the world without being at the mercy of the world's verdicts, and the Imam captures the state in an image the opening chapter already touched: those whose "hearts are in the Gardens while their bodies are at work." The body labors at the tax registers and the grievances and the endless business of a province; the heart is anchored elsewhere, in the reality of which it is certain, so that the labor is done with full effort and yet without the anxiety of a man whose whole stake rides on the result. A ruler in that state can neither be bribed by success nor broken by failure, because his center of gravity is not in the ledger. And this, finally, is why the sacred conception of justice is not the utopian fantasy it might appear. The objection writes itself — that the brutal realities of political life and the lofty ideals of the spirit stand hopelessly far apart, and that no real government could be run this way. The Imam's answer is his own caliphate, which was run this way, amid civil war and betrayal, without cynicism and without surrender. What looks utopian from the outside is simply what becomes possible once a person's stake in his own action has been shifted from the outcome to the intention, and from the world to God.

## What this episode lands

This episode has followed sacred justice into the one place where lofty conceptions usually break down — the concrete administration of a state — and found that the Imam's conception does not break down there but sharpens. We have seen the whole social order bound together not by contract but by a covenant with God who assigns each class its due, and we have seen why a ruler needs both the scholars who know the law and the sages who know how to apply it. We have watched the letter descend into particulars — the gentle-yet-severe commander, the rare incorruptible judge, the tested administrator, the cultivators whose land must be nurtured before it is taxed, the trustworthy scribe, the peaceable merchant — and found each instruction sealed with a spiritual message, the whole turning on the unseen axle of the ruler's connection to the Real. And we have seen the letter's manner change at the mention of the poor, whose care is onerous by every worldly measure and possible only for those who aspire to the Hereafter.

The recurring pattern has been the lesson: a class named, a duty stated, and the duty rooted in the nature of God or the destiny of the soul — the administrative and the spiritual proving, in the end, to be one fabric rather than two. Above all, the chapter has landed the teaching that makes fidelity to justice unbreakable: intention beyond consequence. Because the just person acts for the sake of justice itself — as a free man worshipping out of gratitude, not a merchant trading for reward or a slave fleeing punishment — his effort survives every worldly defeat, since its fulfilment was never lodged in the outcome. Yet this same person strives all the harder, for sincerity of intention is proven only in ceaseless striving. This is the resource a secular ethics lacks and a sacred one supplies. But the letter has not yet named the wellspring from which this whole disposition is drawn. If the just person acts from an inner taste of divine reality, how is that taste actually acquired? The next and final movement of the essay answers that question, turning to what the Imam calls the lynchpin of the entire letter — prayer, and its essence, the remembrance of God — and following it all the way to the vision Plato called gazing upon the Good.
