This closing episode of the book's meditation on remembrance follows dhikr to its two final mysteries. First, why the highest form of remembering God turns out to be a kind of forgetting — the self-effacement the tradition calls fana, in which the one who remembers falls away and only the Remembered remains. Second, what the Name a person invokes actually is: whether it is a word pointing at a distant reality, or already, in some real sense, that reality's own self-disclosure, so that to invoke it is to touch what it names. We follow both mysteries to the single point where they meet — the friends of God, effaced in Him, who become themselves the most beautiful Names — and, at the last, to the death of Ali (ع) with the invocation still moving on his tongue.

## Where this episode picks up

This session has, so far, taken for granted that there is a self doing the remembering — someone who invokes the Name, whose heart gets polished, whose sight is restored, who learns at last to hold God's nearness and God's incomparability together without confusing the two. Everything in that picture is about to be dismantled from within. You will not remember God, the Imam says, according to the true reality of the remembrance, until you forget your own soul in your remembrance. Perfect remembrance — the harmony of heart and tongue already traced at this session's opening — is real, and it is not the summit. One further threshold remains, and crossing it costs exactly the thing that has, until now, been doing all the work: the self.

Why should the peak of remembering God be a forgetting? And once we have followed the Imam into that forgetting, a second and stranger question opens beneath it. If, at the summit, it is God who truly remembers and the human invocation only a shadow of that remembering, then everything turns on what exactly is being invoked. Is the Name of God a label, arbitrary and external, the way the names of ordinary things are — or is it charged with the very reality it names, so that to invoke it is already to have arrived? The two questions are not separate. They are the same mystery approached from either side: the forgetting of the self and the identity of the Name are the near and far faces of a single realization, and this episode follows both faces to where they meet.

## The reality of remembrance

The Qur'an is severe with those who go through the outward motions of worship while their hearts are elsewhere. Woe, it says, to those who pray, who are heedless of their prayer, who make a show of it, and who withhold simple assistance (chapter 107, verses 4 to 7). It is possible to pray and to be, at the same time, absent from one's prayer — present in body and posture, gone in mind and heart. Worse still is the prayer offered for show, whose real audience is other people rather than God; the Qur'an couples the two failings, the heedlessness and the showing, and calls those who combine them givers of the lie to religion. Both are forms of a single disease: a devotion in which the self, and not God, remains at the center — whether the self that has drifted off into its own distractions or the self that is performing its piety for an onlooker. The gap between tongue and heart is exactly the impurity that empties the act of its power.

The Imam gives one of the most concentrated sayings in his whole corpus on this point. Do not remember God absent-mindedly, he says, nor forget Him in distraction; rather, remember Him with perfect remembrance, a remembrance in which your heart and tongue are in harmony, and what you conceal conforms with what you reveal. But you will not remember Him according to the true reality of the remembrance until you forget your own soul in your remembrance.

Read slowly, the saying moves in two stages that must be carefully distinguished. The first is what the Imam calls perfect remembrance. Its mark is harmony — heart and tongue brought into a single accord, so that what a person conceals within matches what he reveals without. This is the elimination of every trace of hypocrisy, the slow work of a lifetime by which every motive that is not God is purged from a soul that begins riddled with them, until the concealed and the revealed no longer diverge at any point. The mystical tradition often names its whole aim with this one word, sincerity, or truthfulness: to orient oneself and every desire purely toward God, with no impurity mixed in — where impurity means the presence, in one's worship or intention or act, of any wish for something other than God. The remembrance both produces this sincerity, by polishing the heart, and is perfected by it in turn, since the invocation is only as spiritually effective as the sincerity of the heart from which it rises. One who makes no effort toward that sincerity — who has not taken up the greater striving against his own ego, followed earlier in this series — is heedless of God even while his tongue moves, and the technical act of invocation, in the absence of that effort, is devoid of power. For the divine qualities held within the Name of God must, to some degree, come to be reflected in the heart and soul of the one who invokes, in the form of virtues; when the soul is truly pure it becomes transparent to the spiritual qualities embedded in its own deepest substance, and those qualities are brought to light, and brought to life, by the remembrance that polishes the heart which holds them.

But then comes the second stage, and with it a break so sharp it amounts to a change of order. For all its perfection, the remembrance so far described still belongs to the individual as its agent — the person's own heart brought into harmony with the person's own tongue, the self that remembers still standing intact at the center of the act. And the Imam says that even this perfect remembrance is not yet the reality of remembrance. There is, then, a real disjuncture between human perfection and divine Reality. At the first level the individual as agent keeps his identity; the harmony of hidden intent and outward act is what earns the name perfect. At the second level the remembrance can no longer be defined in relation to the individual at all, because the individual has been forgotten — by himself. And that can only mean one thing: that the center of consciousness has shifted, moving from the one who invokes to the One who is invoked. The reality of remembrance is thus one with its very object, the Real itself. To pass from the perfection of remembrance to its reality is to cross from the human to the divine, and no amount of further polishing carries a person across that gap. It is a leap, and it costs the self.

## Extinction in remembrance

What the Imam is describing at this second level is what the whole mystical tradition means by extinction, fana — the perishing of egoistic self-awareness before the Absolute, the process by which the Real, al-Haqq, is made real within a person, a process the tradition calls tahqiq. There can be no true knowledge of God, it holds, without self-effacement at the beginning and this extinction at the end. But an obvious difficulty arises, and the Imam's own words already contain its answer. If the self must be forgotten, can a person simply set out to forget it? The answer is no. Any effort to will oneself into unconsciousness of oneself is itself an act of the conscious will, and so it entrenches the very self-consciousness it means to dissolve; the harder one tries to forget oneself, the more firmly one is there, trying. This is why the soul is to be forgotten in the remembrance rather than forgotten by direct assault: it is a matter of total concentration on the Name, so complete that no room remains in consciousness for anything but the Named. A first degree of self-effacement follows naturally from that absorption. But the ultimate consummation — full extinction — comes as a divine grace that surpasses all individual effort, even though the invocation is understood to attract that grace, to remove from the heart the rust that hides from a person their own nothingness before the sole reality of God.

The Qur'an itself hints at this when it says that a person will not forget unless God wills it so (chapter 87, verses 6 and 7), read here in an inner sense, of the forgetting of self that God alone can grant. One of the earliest masters of the Sufi tradition, al-Kalabadhi, opens his chapter on remembrance with words that echo the Imam almost exactly: the reality of remembrance, he writes, is that you forget everything other than the Invoked in the remembrance — for God says, remember your Lord when you have forgotten (chapter 18, verse 24), which means that when you have forgotten what is not God, then you have truly remembered God. Remembrance in its deepest sense is a double movement: to bring God to mind is, at the limit, to let everything else — the self above all — fall out of mind entirely.

There is a strict logic to how this self-forgetting can be the peak of remembrance rather than its collapse. The human invocation is a kind of reflection of the divine, and like any reflected image it both participates in what it reflects and inverts it. In one respect the human act of invocation leads to a greater fullness of consciousness; in the other it finds its consummation in what looks like the exact opposite — the effacement of the individual in the One invoked. Yet this is only apparently opposed to the heightening of consciousness; in truth it is that heightening carried to its end, where the final step requires a leap: the negation of the very limits that make up individuality as such. To be an individual is to be limited, and a limit is itself a kind of negation, a not-being-everything-else. To negate that negation is not to fall into nothingness but to arrive at pure affirmation — the affirmation of unconditioned, supra-individual being. The dissolving of the self is not a loss of reality but the removal of the one thing that was keeping reality bounded. This is the sense of the Imam's own enigmatic words, met earlier in this series, when he calls himself the death of the dead; and it is the sense of the lines Rumi places in his mouth in the Mathnawi — I have removed the baggage of self out of the way, the Imam says there, I have deemed all that is other than God to be non-existence; I am a shadow, the Sun is my lord; I am the chamberlain, I am not the curtain that prevents approach to Him.

The mystics found the same reality dramatized in the story of Moses on the mountain. When Moses asked to see God, the Qur'an says, he fell down stunned (chapter 7, verse 143). Ibn al-'Arabi, recounting his own visionary ascent through the heavens, tells of meeting Moses in the sixth heaven and questioning him about that request, reminding him that no one will see his Lord until he dies. And Moses answers: it was just so — when I asked for the vision, He answered me, so that I fell down stunned, and then I saw Him in my being stunned. Ibn al-'Arabi presses: while you were dead? And Moses replies, while I was dead; I did not see God until I had died. The vision of God could not belong to Moses the living, self-standing individual; it came only when that individual had, in the decisive sense, ceased to be. Ibn al-'Arabi built a whole treatise on this, titled the Book of Extinction in Contemplation, whose very name points to the Imam's paradox of forgetting in the heart of remembrance. At its opening he declares the divine Reality too exalted to be witnessed by any creaturely eye, so long as the least trace of the creature's own condition remains in the eye of the witness; and he resolves that impasse only by taking the Prophet's own definition of excellence in worship, met earlier in this book, and reading it with a pause in the middle that transforms its sense entirely: if you are not, you see Him. The vision of God is possible precisely where the self is not.

This is no mere speculation, and the surest proof is the Imam's own life. Abu'l-Darda, an eminent companion of the Prophet, once found Ali (ع) lying in one of the palm orchards of Medina, his body seemingly lifeless, as rigid as a piece of wood. Thinking him dead, he went to condole with the family, only to be told by Fatima: my cousin has not died; rather, in fear of God he has fainted, and this condition overcomes him often. The fear in question is not terror but awe — the awe of one who feareth the station of his Lord and restrains his soul from caprice, to whom the Garden is promised (chapter 79, verses 40 and 41). As the Sufis often say, when one fears creatures one flees from them, but when one fears God one flees to Him — for there is no refuge from God except to Him (chapter 9, verse 118). The Imam's recurring collapse in the orchard was the overflow of a soul so absorbed in God that its ordinary consciousness of itself gave way.

It is important not to mistake this extinction for a final annihilation, an ending in emptiness. In the Sufi tradition, the state of extinction, fana, is followed by a station of subsistence, baqa — a return of consciousness to a self now transfigured by the knowledge impressed upon it in the moment of extinction. Al-Ghazali describes what happens at the extreme of this state in unforgettable terms. Those who verify the divine oneness through direct mystical taste, he writes, become drowned in oneness, so utterly dumbfounded that no capacity remains within them save to recall God — not even the capacity to recall their own selves. This condition, in relation to the one who undergoes it, is called extinction, or rather the extinction of extinction: for the soul has become extinct to itself, and extinct even to its own extinction; it is unconscious of itself, and unconscious of its own unconsciousness — since, were it conscious of its unconsciousness, it would still be conscious of itself. The self does not merely fall silent; it falls silent about its own silence. There is a hard saying preserved from the early master Abu 'Uthman that names the price of this and its reward together: whoever has not tasted the loneliness of unconsciousness, he says, will not find the food of the intimacy of remembrance. One cannot have both the old self-satisfied intimacy — the habitual, comfortable self-regard that is the shadow cast by self-awareness — and the intimacy of God at once. The first must be surrendered for the second.

The tradition maps this ascent through graded degrees of remembrance itself, each deeper than the last: the remembrance of the tongue; then of the heart; then of the innermost secret; then of the spirit; then of the hidden; and finally the remembrance of the Essence, which is essential vision, attained only through the removal of duality altogether. The whole movement is a progressive descent inward, from the lips toward the ground of the soul, until nothing stands between the rememberer and the Remembered — because the rememberer, as a separate self, is no longer there. And yet, precisely because this final degree cannot be forced, the master Ibn 'Ata'illah counsels the seeker never to give up the practice for failing to feel God's presence in it. Do not abandon the invocation, he says, because you do not feel the Presence of God in it — for your forgetfulness of the invocation is far worse than your forgetfulness in the invocation. Perhaps God will carry you from an invocation clouded with heedlessness to one with vigilance, and from vigilance to the Presence of God, and from the Presence of God to a state in which everything but the Invoked is absent. The seeker's task is only to keep invoking; the passage through the degrees is God's to give.

And this is why one further saying of the Imam is no mere flourish of rhetoric but an account of a real state. God, he says, has a drink for His friends. When they drink it, they are intoxicated; and when they are intoxicated, they are enraptured; and when they are enraptured, they are blessed; and when they are blessed, they dissolve; and when they dissolve, they are free; and when they are free, they devote themselves purely; and when they devote themselves purely, they seek; and when they seek, they find; and when they find, they arrive; and when they arrive, they are at one — and there is no difference between them and their Beloved. This is not the union of two things becoming one, which would still leave two; it is the realization of oneness — the disclosure that there was never, at the deepest level, a genuine second at all. It leads one, inevitably, to ask the last and strangest question: who, in the end, is the one who truly invokes?

## The Name that is the Named

The Imam answers with a sentence that turns the whole practice of remembrance inside out. The invocation, he says, is not a formality of speech nor a way of thinking; rather, it comes forth firstly from the Invoked, and secondly from the invoker. The remembrance originates first from the One who is remembered, and only second from the one who remembers. It is God Himself who performs the invocation, first and foremost; a person's own invocation is but a shadow of it, a reflection, a consequence. This resolves the paradox of self-forgetting from the far side. If, at the summit, it is not really the individual who invokes but God who invokes through him, then the extinction of the individual is not the failure of the remembrance but its truth coming to light — the passing-away of an agent who was never, at the deepest level, the real agent at all.

This inverts the whole natural picture of prayer, in which a creature, standing at a distance, reaches across a gap toward a God who may or may not answer. On the Imam's account the movement runs the other way first: God's own reality is the origin of the remembrance, and the human invocation is the reflection that reality throws back into the mirror of a heart. When at last the mirror is perfectly clear — the self forgotten, the rust of egoity rubbed away — nothing of the reflecting surface intrudes, and what is seen in it is simply the light itself. Once remembrance is seen this way, its meaning reaches all the way down into the act of creation itself. God's creative Word — Be — which calls the possibilities of being into existence, can be understood as a form of remembrance: God utters the name of the possible thing, calling it out of nothingness into being, and that very passage from nonexistence to existence is what makes the thing remembered. The Qur'an poses the question exactly in these terms: has there come upon man any moment in time when he was not a thing remembered (chapter 76, verse 1)? Before he exists, the human being is known, but known only by God. So God's remembrance of man precedes man's remembrance of God, not merely in time but in the order of being itself.

There are two movements here that mirror each other. The first is the descent of creation, from God to man: to bring a thing into being, God negates the relative nothingness that was pregnant with its possibility, calling forth what was merely possible into the actual. The second is the ascent of realization, from man back to God: the very same nothingness that was breathed into being must be negated again, so that what was called forth returns to its source. This is why extinction is not a fall into the void but a homecoming. What is extinguished, in the words of Ibn al-'Arabi's treatise, is that which never truly was — the creature's illusory self-standing existence — while what subsists is that which never ceased to be, the divine reality that was always its ground. The Qur'an says it plainly: everything upon the earth perishes, and there subsists only the Face of your Lord, possessor of glory and bounty (chapter 55, verses 26 and 27). Al-Ghazali, commenting on the kindred verse that everything perishes except His Face (chapter 28, verse 88), insists that this perishing is true forever, not merely at some future moment: every thing, considered in and by itself, apart from God, is pure nothingness even now, and has existence only from the face turned toward the One who gives it being.

Remember Me, the Qur'an says, and I will remember you (chapter 2, verse 152). On the surface this reads as an even exchange: turn to God and He turns to you. But the order of the two remembrances is not level. God's remembrance of the human being is the prior, engendering reality — the very act by which the person is held in existence — and the human being's remembrance of God is the answering movement it makes possible, the shadow that follows the light. There is a genuine reciprocity in the exchange, but it is not a reciprocity between equals: the Qur'an describes how those who invoke much are blessed by God and by His angels, who send their blessing upon the believers so as to bring them out of darkness and into light, and that light is not a reward added onto an act a person completed alone but the very thing that made the act possible in the first place. The response, in a sense, comes before the call, since the capacity to call at all was itself the first gift. To remember God, then, is finally to be caught up into a remembrance that was already underway before one began: God remembering Himself, and holding the whole of creation in being, through the very creature who imagines he is the one doing the remembering. The self-forgetting this episode has traced so far is, seen from this height, only the moment when the creature stops imagining that and lets the true remembrance shine through unobstructed.

But this raises the sharpest question still to be answered. If remembrance works through the invocation of a Name, everything depends on what a Name actually is. Is the Name of God a label, arbitrary and external, like the names we give to ordinary things — or is it charged with the very reality it names? Start with the difference between two kinds of name. The names we give to things, and the names the idolaters gave to their gods, are arbitrary labels, signs with no intrinsic bond to the realities underlying them. The Qur'an says exactly this of the idols: they are but names that you have named, you and your fathers, for which God has revealed no authority (chapter 53, verse 23). The Names of God are of a wholly different order, because they are not human inventions at all. Call upon God, or call upon the Merciful, the Qur'an says; by whatever Name you call upon Him, His are the most beautiful Names (chapter 17, verse 110). Because God Himself has disclosed them, they are penetrated by the very substance of the reality they denote; they carry an authority — a sultan — by virtue of which they have the power not merely to inform the mind but to transform the soul.

This is why the tradition can say that the Name is the Named. The great supplication attributed to the Imam, the supplication of the lame man, consists of a hundred and twenty-two verses crowded with divine Names, attributes, and titles, and near its end comes the phrase that is the key to the whole: I ask Thee by every Name with which Thou hast named Thyself. It is the fact that God names Himself that gives His Names their transforming power. A name a creature invents is a mere sound stuck onto a thing from outside; a Name God gives Himself is a self-disclosure, and to invoke it is to lay hold of something of the reality that disclosed itself in it. As this session's opening episode found, the Name revealed from above is mirrored by a name that resounds from within the heart, and when the rust is rubbed from the heart's surface the two meet — light upon light.

There is a further depth here, in the mystics' account of how a soul comes to be at all. Every soul, on this vision, has its own particular divine Name that engenders and sustains it — its Lord in the most intimate sense, the divine support at the very root of its being. Ibn al-'Arabi puts it vividly: every servant has a Name which is its Lord, and the servant becomes, as it were, the body of that Name, while the Name is its heart. A thing comes into existence when the divine Word turns toward its latent possibility and breathes being into it, and what shape that being takes is determined by the possibility itself — the way water takes on the color of the cup that holds it. The Name is the light; the soul is the cup. When a person invokes a Name of God, he is not reaching for something foreign to himself; he is invoking the very reality at his own root, the divine support that is more truly his heart than his own ego is — the same reality the Imam names, in his discourse to Kumayl met earlier in this series, as a light dawning from the morning of eternity upon the temples of tawhid.

And yet the Shi'i Imams themselves are recorded denying, in the plainest terms, that the Name is the Named. Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq is reported to have said: the Name of God is other than Him. Everything to which the name of a thing applies is a creation, and therefore other than God; the Creator cannot be confined within the limits of a named object. He presses the point with a stark argument: God has ninety-nine Names, and if the Name were the Named, then every one of those Names would be a god — an intolerable multiplicity of deities. Rather, he insists, God is the one meaning indicated by all the Names, and every one of the Names is other than Him. On its face this contradicts the Sufi maxim head-on.

The resolution turns on seeing that the word Name is being used in two different senses. Ibn al-'Arabi draws the distinction with great precision. In the first sense, the Name is nothing other than God Himself, the very reality that is named; in the second, the Name is that by which one Name is distinguished from another — the specific meaning that marks the Merciful off from the All-Conquering. Taken in the first sense, the Name is the Reality itself; taken in the second, the Name is a kind of imagined reality, real only as a facet, a relation, a way the one Reality engages the created world. This dissolves Imam Ja'far's objection exactly. The Names do not imply many gods, because in the respect in which each Name is a distinct meaning — the respect in which there are ninety-nine of them — each is precisely not the Essence, but a determination that reverts to the One. It is only in the other respect, as identical with the Essence, that the Name is the Named — and in that respect there are not ninety-nine but only One. A lexicon of the Sufis attributed to al-Kashani puts it with a clean example. Take the Name the Living. Considered simply as manifest being, it is identical with the Essence; but considered as this particular meaning, namely Life, it is other than the Essence. So one understands the saying that the Name is not identical to the Named, nor other than it: a distinction of concept and word, in which the Name is not the Named, and a spiritual identity, in which the Name is the Named. The two traditions were never in conflict; they were speaking of the Name in its two senses.

## The effaced friend as the Name

This resolution rests on a way of thinking about the divine attributes that the Imam himself sets out, with startling force, at the very head of his first and most famous sermon — heard in full in the episode that follows this one. There he climbs a ladder that ends in a single demand: the perfection of sincerity, he says, is to divest God of all attributes. His reasoning is exact. Ascribe Him an attribute pictured as a separate thing standing over against the Essence, and a quality has quietly been set beside Him, another has been added to make two, and what is two has already been divided into parts a mind can no longer hold together as one. But the same sermon elsewhere plainly affirms the reality of the divine attributes. What the Imam negates is not the attributes themselves but the attributes conceived as separate, super-added realities. Since no divine attribute can really be distinct from the divine nature, and since that nature is infinite, every attribute is likewise infinite, bursting every boundary human conception would put around it. In one respect the attribute is affirmed, as not other than the Essence; in another it is negated, insofar as we conceive it in itself, as a thing on its own.

It is worth pausing on how radical this negation really is, because it is the hinge of everything that follows. To think God is compassionate, and to stop there, as though compassion were a bounded thing added to a bounded God, is already to have shrunk Him. The demand is that thought let go of its own limits, so that consciousness can begin to fathom a transcendence no concept can contain. Only on the far side of that letting-go — the same letting-go this episode has already traced as the extinction of the self — do the divine qualities appear as what they truly are: not separate things, but the one infinite Essence, seen now under this aspect and now under that.

Now apply that same double logic, not to the divine attributes, but to the human soul, and the deepest teaching of the tradition comes into view. On its own account, considered as a separate thing standing on its own, the individual is nothing — pure nothingness, as the last movement of this episode found. But considered in respect of its divine ground, the Name that engenders and sustains it, the soul is not other than the one Real. Ibn al-'Arabi states the paradox at its sharpest: the final end of the knowers of God, he says, is that the Real is identical with them, while they themselves do not exist. Both halves must be held together. Insofar as the knower is aware that he does not exist — that on his own account he is nothing — he knows that the Real is his true identity. Ibn al-'Arabi describes reaching this at the climax of his own visionary ascent, when God stripped from him his contingent dimension and he attained the inner realities of all the Names, and saw them all returning to a single Subject — and came to know, in that moment, that he was a pure servant, without a trace of lordship in him at all. The height of the ascent is not a self-deification but its exact opposite: the discovery of one's own utter servitude, and precisely thereby of the Reality that alone truly is.

This is what makes sense of the most shocking sayings in the whole tradition — the Imam's own declaration, reported of Ali (ع), that we are the most beautiful Names. And it is what makes sense of the ecstatic utterances of the great Sufis: al-Hallaj crying out that he is the Real, Bayazid exclaiming glory to me. To the outward ear these sound like blasphemy, a creature usurping the place of God. But in the light of the Imam's teaching on the attributes, they mean the reverse. When the Imam appears to speak of his own greatness, it is not his own greatness he is claiming; it is the greatness of God being expressed through him as a locus of manifestation, and expressed precisely in the measure of his own effacement in that greatness. The saints are annihilated in the divine Essence, just as the attributes are; there can be no question of any private, individual appropriation of the glory of God, because there is no longer a private individual there to appropriate it. The perfect man reflects the divine Names and qualities exactly because his own self has been polished away, so that nothing of his egoity clouds the mirror. The more the divine Essence is exalted beyond all Names, the more brilliantly those Names shine in the mirror the perfect man has become.

It is essential to see that this effacement does not cancel the saint's servitude to God — it perfects it. One might expect that a soul which has come to know the Real as its own true identity would have risen above the humble station of the worshipper. The tradition insists on the opposite. The friends of God realize their servitude most completely at the very moment of their effacement, for it is only when their individuality has been annihilated that their true identity as the Named can shine through, and that identity belongs to God, not to them. 'Attar preserves a saying of Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq that catches this with perfect economy. Accused of pride, the Imam answered: I am not proud, for pride belongs to the divine greatness; since I have gone beyond the pride of self, His pride has come and taken the place of my own. Where the pride of self is present, there can be no divine greatness; but through the divine greatness, a kind of pride will be present. The one who has been effaced does not compete with God for glory; he has become a clear window through which God's own glory passes undimmed.

All of this resolves the great saying of drawing near, met earlier in this series for what it discloses about vision: God declares that His servant draws near to Him through the voluntary devotions until He loves him, and that when He loves him He becomes the servant's hearing, sight, hand, and foot. Read now for what it discloses about love, the saying holds a paradox. God's love for the obligatory acts he has laid down is a love directed toward something apart from Himself — the acts of a servant still standing as a servant. But God's love for the servant himself, once that servant has been effaced in his Lord, partakes of God's love for His own self-manifestation, and so of His love for Himself. In the beginning, the tradition says, God loved to be known, and so He created the world — I was a hidden treasure, runs the divine saying, and I loved to be known. In coming to know Himself through the effaced servant, God's own longing to be known is fulfilled, and the servant's greatest glory turns out to be exactly his own emptiness, through which alone the hidden treasure comes to light.

It is in this sense that a startling saying of the Imam is to be understood: down from me torrents pour, he says, up to me no bird can soar. The torrents flow down from him because he is extinguished in the source of the torrent; he gives abundantly because he keeps nothing back for a self. The image recalls the Qur'anic description of the friends of God who drink from the celestial spring, causing it to gush forth abundantly (chapter 76, verses 5 and 6). They make the spring flow more because they are themselves the sources of that spring — there is no otherness, no duality left in them to dam it. Here is reached the final degree of remembrance named earlier in this episode: the remembrance of the Essence, essential vision, attained through the removal of duality — including that most stubborn duality of all, the individual self standing over against God.

At this height there is a change in what knowledge itself means. So long as a self stands facing God, there is a knower here and a known there. But when the self has been taken out of the equation, there is only pure consciousness, which cannot belong to any particular entity without being cut down to that entity's measure. This is why the tradition calls the one who has arrived not simply the knower of God but the knower through God, the creature grown so poor, so empty of self, that God's knowledge of Himself passes through him without obstruction. Recall the Imam's command, met at the beginning of this whole meditation: know God through God. Knowledge of God, at its deepest, is the Real knowing itself through a creature grown perfectly transparent — and to say through, rather than by, is to name the unimpeded passage that opens only where the self is no longer in the way.

There is a reason the revealed Name has this power to draw a soul back to its ground, when the whole created cosmos, for all that it too manifests God, does not carry the same transforming charge. The world and the scripture are both revelations of God — the cosmos an indirect one, every thing in it a word, and the revealed Name a direct one. But between them there is no common measure. Revelation is a projection of the pure Light of God, of one substance with the source from which it streams; the created cosmos is the shadow that light casts. In Ibn al-'Arabi's words, the very Being of the Real is a verified Light, and the existence of creation is a shadow following after. This is why the practice of remembrance fastens on the revealed Name and not on the general spectacle of the world: the Name is light where the world is shadow, and only light can dispel the darkness of forgetfulness in the heart.

It is with a passage from another of the Imam's sermons, his most disputed, the sermon of the clarifying declaration, that this teaching reaches its peak — and its most shocking expression. I am the Sign of the All-Powerful, the Imam says there; I am the gnosis of the mysteries; I am the companion of the radiance of the divine Majesty; I am the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden; I am the Face of God; I am the mirror of God. To the outward mind this can only sound like blasphemy, a man deifying himself. But one phrase is the key that unlocks all the rest: I am the gnosis of the mysteries. The Imam is not identifying himself with God; he is identifying himself with the knowledge of God — not his own relative knowledge, which would still be a creature's, but knowledge itself, the knowledge God has of Himself, in which the distinctions between knower, knowing, and known dissolve. To be that knowledge is, by a kind of metaphysical resonance, to be everything that God is. And the claim to be the Face of God, the mirror of God, points the same way. A mirror loses its own identity when the eye fixes only on the image within it, without ceasing to be a mirror and without the Face reflected in it ceasing to be wholly independent of it. Forgetting the mirror is the effacement of the self; fixing on the image within it is the remembrance of God. The friend of God is the mirror in which the Face is seen, precisely because he has ceased to draw attention to the mirror.

There is an inescapable perplexity at this summit, worth naming honestly rather than smoothing over. One knows God by knowing that His Essence is unknowable, utterly other, lying beyond the veil that creation itself constitutes. And the distinction between creature and Creator is effaced the moment the creature has genuinely forgotten itself, not as an idea entertained but existentially, in real contemplative extinction. These two are not a contradiction to be resolved by cleverness; they are the two faces of a single realization that only the extinguished self can hold. To know God as unknowable and to be effaced before Him are not two acts but one, and the perplexity that accompanies them is the mind's honest register of a reality that exceeds it on every side.

And this yields the last and most beautiful paradox of the whole meditation: that the deepest effacement of the self goes hand in hand with the most perfect flowering of the human. The friends of God, who know themselves to be nothing before the Absolute, are exactly the ones who reflect the divine Names and qualities most faithfully — qualities that belong not to them but to God. This is why the great saints and sages are also the great exemplars of virtue, for the true foundation of every virtue is one of the most beautiful Names of God, and those Names shine through them undimmed because the mirrors of their hearts have been polished by remembrance until the most stubborn stains, the stains of egotism, are gone. Their hearts, the tradition says, are already in the Gardens of paradise while their bodies still labor in this world. And nowhere is this seen more clearly than in the manner of the Imam's death. When Ali (ع) was struck by the poisoned sword of his assassin, the cry that broke from him was not one of pain but of triumph: I have triumphed, by the Lord of the Ka'ba. At the very threshold of death there was in him nothing but the remembrance of God, and he passed away, it is said, with his tongue still repeating the testimony that there is no god but God — fulfilling the Prophet's own answer, when asked which of all deeds is the most meritorious, that you die, he said, while your tongue is moist with the invocation of God.

## What this episode lands

This episode has followed remembrance to its two final mysteries and found them to be one. The self-forgetting the Imam demands is not the ruin of remembrance but the disclosure of its truth: that the invocation comes forth firstly from the Invoked and only secondly from the invoker, so that when the individual is at last effaced, nothing is lost but the illusion that he was ever the one doing the remembering. And the Name a person invokes is, in exactly the same sense, both other than God and not other than Him — a determination that reverts to the One, and, in the respect that matters most, the Reality itself, disclosed by God's own naming of Himself and not by any human invention. The two mysteries meet in the friend of God, who is nothing on his own account and, in respect of his divine ground, not other than the Real — effaced enough to become, in the tradition's boldest words, one of the most beautiful Names, a mirror so cleared of self that only the Face it holds is seen. What began, at the opening of this session, as a discipline a person takes up to polish a clouded heart ends here as a self so given to the Name that it has become the Name — sealed, at the last, in a life whose final breath was the invocation itself.

## Closing

Everything this session has said about remembrance, the self, and the Name has been commentary — a modern reader's careful walk through the sayings the Imam left scattered across sermons, letters, and prayers. The next episode sets that commentary aside and lets Ali (ع) speak whole. It opens the collection called Nahj al-Balagha at its very first page and lets that founding address sound entire — the same address this episode has already drawn on for its most demanding claim, that sincerity's perfection is to strip every attribute from God. There the argument that has run through this whole session is heard not as a modern reconstruction but at its source: a single unbroken address moving from an Essence no word can reach, through the shaping of the heavens and the fashioning of Adam, to the long succession that hands revelation down at last into human hands. Having followed the teaching in fragments, we now hear where it began.
