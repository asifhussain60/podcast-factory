# The Discourse to Kumayl

*On a night outside Kufa, Ali ibn Abi Talib (peace be upon him) took a single disciple by the hand and led him past the city walls to a graveyard, where he drew a deep breath and entrusted him with a counsel that later generations would treat as one of the most esoteric utterances in the whole tradition. This episode recites that discourse to Kumayl whole, and then follows the commentary into the interior of what it teaches. It listens for how knowledge is meant not merely to inform the mind but to storm the heart, breaking past the surface of words into the reality of insight. It follows the turn from knowing to being, where the intellect must win the whole soul for God through the greatest of all struggles, the struggle against the self. And it arrives at the ground on which that whole labor stands: sincerity, ikhlas, and the worship that sustains it.*

## Where this episode picks up

Why would the Father of Imams lead one man alone, at night, past the last houses of Kufa and out to a field of graves, only to draw a long breath and there impart the most dangerous knowledge he carried? The setting itself is the first lesson. This counsel was not proclaimed from a pulpit to a crowd but confided to a single vessel judged capable of holding it, in the one place where the vanities that the living chase after have already been answered by the dust. It is a teaching about who can bear knowledge and who cannot, delivered in the presence of those who can bear nothing any longer.

The previous episode traced how, for Ali, the intellect is first and foremost a spiritual faculty, not a mental one — the articulation of the very spirit that God breathed into the clay of man, the principle by which inert matter is brought to life and to awareness of itself. It followed the intellect to full stretch, past the surface functions of reason and down toward the buried treasures within it, and it defined the true intellectual, the aqil, as one who not only thinks correctly but acts rightly and, at the deepest level, seeks to realize the ultimate Reality. That definition left a question standing. If correct thinking, impeccable virtue, and authentic being are all bound up together in the intellect, what actually does the binding? What turns information into wisdom, and a knower of facts into a bearer of truth?

The discourse to Kumayl is where that question is answered. It sorts all people into three kinds by their relation to knowledge, it weighs knowledge against wealth and finds wealth wanting, and it culminates in the portrait of the rare few through whom, in the Imam's own words, knowledge penetrates the reality of insight. Those four sentences about the guardians of the proof become the seed of everything the commentary then unfolds: that knowledge must storm the heart, that the whole soul must be conquered for the intellect, and that the ground of it all is sincerity before God. The episode recites the discourse first, whole and uninterrupted, and only then turns to what it means.

## The counsel to Kumayl

This discourse has been the subject of numerous commentaries through the centuries, and its importance lies, among other things, in its highly esoteric character. Taking Kumayl by the hand and leading him out of Kufa to a graveyard, with the city behind them, the Imam drew a deep breath and imparted the following private counsel.

O Kumayl ibn Ziyad, truly these hearts are vessels — containers for knowledge and wisdom — and the best of them are those which hold the most. So retain from me that which I say to you. People are divided into three types: a lordly knower (alim rabbani); one who seeks knowledge (muta'allim) for the sake of deliverance; and the common folk (hamaj ra'a) — the word names the moth that hurls itself at any flame — following just anyone, swaying with every current, neither desiring to be illumined by the light of knowledge nor seeking refuge in any strong support.

O Kumayl, knowledge is better than wealth, for knowledge guards you, while you must guard your wealth; and wealth diminishes as it is spent, while knowledge only increases as it is disbursed, replenishing itself the more it is given away; the fruits of wealth vanish when the wealth itself is gone — the friends bought with money, the comforts it purchased, all fall away with it — while no one can take a man's knowledge from him. O Kumayl ibn Ziyad, the wise apprehension of knowledge (ma'rifat al-ilm) is a religion by which God is worshipped — for din, religion, is kin to dayn, a debt, and it is by acquiring knowledge that a man repays the debt his own intellect has incurred to its Maker. Through it man acquires obedience in his life and a good name after his death. And knowledge is a judge, while wealth is judged: knowledge is active and wealth passive, for the one who knows takes command of what he owns, his knowledge directing him how to handle it, whereas wealth is forever something ruled upon by its possessor.

O Kumayl ibn Ziyad, those who hoard wealth perish even while they live, but the knowers endure for as long as time subsists; their material forms are absent, but their images in the hearts of men are present. Ah, what abundant knowledge is here — and he pointed to his breast — would that I could find one to bear it. Yes, I found one who was quick to understand, but he could not be trusted with it, exploiting the tools of religion for the sake of worldly gain, empowering himself with the graces of God against His slaves and with His proofs against His friends; for when the vessel of the heart is corrupt, this knowledge — pure and precious as it is — becomes a weapon turned against the very friends of God. Then there was another, obedient to the bearers of truth but lacking heartfelt insight, in whom, at the first appearance of obscurity, doubt was kindled in the heart. So alas, neither this one nor that! Nor one who is greedy for pleasure and submissive to passion, nor one obsessed with acquisition and accumulation — neither of them is a guardian of religion in any respect. They resemble nothing so much as grazing cattle. Thus does knowledge die with the death of its bearers, when its trust falls to the worthless and the self-serving.

But indeed, O my God, the earth will never be empty of one who establishes the proof of God, whether overtly and in the open or fearfully and in obscurity, lest God's proofs and elucidations come to naught. Yet how many are such as these, and where are they? By God, they may be the smallest in number, but with God they are the greatest in rank — for here authority is not the possession of the many, and truth is not settled by the count of those who hold it; the guardians of the proof are few, and it is they who stand highest in the sight of God. Through them God preserves His proofs and elucidations, so that they may entrust them to their compeers and sow them in the hearts of those who resemble them. Through them, knowledge penetrates the reality of insight; they take hold of the very spirit of certainty; they make easy what the extravagant find harsh, and they befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged; with their bodies they keep company with the world, while their spirits are bound to the transcendent realm. They are the vicegerents of God upon His earth, the summoners to His religion. Ah, how I long to see them! Go now, Kumayl, as you will.

## Storming the heart

It suffices, for the purpose of understanding this discourse, to reflect on the four sentences of that final movement, in which the Imam describes those who bear the title of lordly knower, alim rabbani: those who are fewest in number yet greatest in the sight of God, and through whom His religion is sustained. His religion here means the divine reality of religion, the pure and immutable essence of the faith, as against religion in the ordinary sense, whose formal institutions are subject to the vicissitudes of human history. From Adam to the Seal of the prophets those outward institutions have shifted with the conditions of men, but the essence, the message, and the purpose have never altered: the salvation of the soul through willing submission to God, as the Quran declares, "Truly, religion in the sight of God is submission" (chapter 3, verse 19). It is by means of the knowledge of the true knowers that religion is kept true to itself, and only through such sages that it truly lives in the world.

The whole discourse is framed, from its first breath, by the image of the vessel. Truly these hearts are vessels, the Imam begins, and the best of them are those which hold the most. That opening is not mere preamble; it sets the terms for everything that follows. Knowledge is not weighed by how much is poured out into the air but by the capacity of the container that receives it, and capacity here means depth — the reach of the batin behind the chest. The three types of people are three kinds of vessel. The lordly knower is the vessel that holds and keeps; the seeker of deliverance is the vessel being made ready; and the common folk, the moths flinging themselves at any flame, are vessels that hold nothing, swaying with every current because there is no depth in them for anything to settle into. The graveyard makes the same point in stone: the bodies there held their knowledge only so long as the spirit remained to keep it, and the counsel is spoken precisely to the one vessel judged deep enough to carry what the Imam pointed to in his own breast. Read in this way, the sorting of humanity at the very start and the portrait of the guardians at the very end are seen to be one continuous meditation on which hearts can bear the reality of insight and which cannot.

Everything then turns on one line: "Through them, knowledge penetrates the reality of insight." The Imam does not say that their knowledge penetrates it, but that through them knowledge penetrates the reality of insight. Knowledge, on its own, can remain merely abstract, far removed from that reality. There is the outward, surface knowledge of a thing — its zahir — and the inner, hidden knowledge of the same thing — its batin. Yet knowledge contains a potential that calls out to be realized; it needs to be enlivened so as to penetrate to something deeper than the words and concepts that articulate its own outward form, the meaning that lies ready on the surface. A man may have a surface knowledge of a verse of the Quran, of a saying, of why he prays and fasts and how many cycles each prayer requires; but when he presses past that surface — asking what the bowing and the prostration truly represent — a world of knowledge opens that is wholly unlike the bare information he began with. Degrees of knowledge are plainly implied here, and with them the refusal of all literalism and superficiality, of the facile equation of factual information with actual knowledge, let alone with wisdom.

The shift of consciousness required for this penetration is shown graphically by the Imam's own gesture toward his breast when he cried out how much abundant knowledge was there. For it is not the chest but the heart hidden behind it that holds knowledge. The chest is the outward form, the zahir that conceals the heart within its batin, and to reach the heart one must first penetrate the chest. The gesture and the teaching are one: knowledge lives in the hidden interior, and reaching it is an act of penetration.

The word underneath the translation makes the point sharper still. The word chosen to render the Imam's verb is "penetrate," but the primary sense of the Arabic hajama is to assault, to take by storm, to charge. In the warfare of that age the battle lines were drawn as a right wing, a left wing, and a center — the heart of the army — where the king or commander stood. The aim of the assault was to break through the wings and reach that heart, for once the king was taken the battle was won and the victor took command of everything the defeated had held: the kingdom, its treasury, its army, its people. The chessboard preserves the same rule. So the word lets us see that there is in conceptual knowledge a latent force — one might almost call it an explosive power — deriving from the reality of knowledge, its haqiqa or spiritual essence. This essence can be indicated, but never captured, by the words and concepts that make up its formal, communicable aspect, just as the chest can only be opened to reach the heart.

Two motions follow from that image. The striving by which one storms the outer wall of words to reach the heart of their meaning is tahqiq, the investigation that presses inward; and the realization it aims at — realization in depth rather than mere verification on the surface — is tahaqquq. By means of this inner force, which its outer conceptual form at once hides and evokes, knowledge is able to effect a shift of consciousness from one degree to another. A man begins to see the very realities of this world differently from those who rest in the surface play of cause and effect, so that the same act which is a burden to one becomes a delight to another. An indirect, mental conception of reality thus paves the way for a penetrating, existential perception of reality. This is the difference between mere sight and true insight: sight reaches only the outward, superficial reality, whereas the reality of insight opens the intelligence to reality as such. The same idea is caught in a saying of the Imam about the true understanding that comes to plumb, and so truly to know, the buried treasures of knowledge: "He who understands knows the depth of knowledge." And the effort this demands, as against the mere accumulation of facts, is likewise indicated: knowledge is attained only through learning and assimilating it until it becomes deeply rooted in one's consciousness.

The rest of that final movement fills in the portrait of the ones in whom this happens. Having said that through them knowledge penetrates the reality of insight, the Imam adds that they take hold of the very spirit of certainty, that they make easy what the extravagant find harsh and befriend that by which the ignorant are estranged, and that with their bodies they keep company with the world while their spirits are bound to the transcendent realm. Each clause describes a consequence of the same interior conquest. The one whose knowledge has reached his heart no longer relates to certainty as a conclusion argued toward but as a reality laid hold of; what the undisciplined soul experiences as a hardship — the demands of devotion, the renunciations of the path — he finds light, and what estranges the ignorant, who rest in the surface play of things, is precisely what draws him. He walks among ordinary men, sharing their world bodily, and yet the center of gravity of his life lies elsewhere; he is, in the Imam's phrase, a vicegerent of God upon His earth and a summoner to His religion. The four sentences are thus not four separate praises but a single description of what a human being becomes once knowledge has stormed past the surface and taken the heart — and the Imam ends them with a cry of longing to see such people, which is itself a measure of their rarity.

The urgency of releasing this immanent power within knowledge — of moving from the abstract to the concrete, the potential to the actual, the static to the dynamic, the formal to the essential — is exactly what the language of battle evokes. It warns against taking the first understanding of an idea for its full comprehension, and its full intellectual comprehension for its dynamic interiorization in the spirit. For when knowledge at last storms the surface of religion and takes the heart, a transformation begins from within: the heart, now illumined by the realities it has seized, takes command of the limbs and the actions — which are the soul's own resources — exactly as the victor takes command of the kingdom he has won. This interiorization is a process of infinite unfolding within, not the mere registering of some fact from without. It requires the effective integration of knowledge within being; knowledge from without must be transformed into being from within, woven into the deepest texture of one's existence. Knowledge thus demands not merely the attention of the mind but the total orientation of the soul.

It is worth marking how far this reframes what learning is. On the surface model, a person stands over against a body of facts and takes them in, adding them to a store he possesses and can display. The Imam's counsel dissolves that picture. The two motions of the seeker, the storming inward of tahqiq and the realization in depth of tahaqquq, are not techniques for acquiring more but the labor of becoming other. The heart that has been reached is not a fuller container of the same kind of contents; it is a transformed organ of vision, and from it the transformation spreads outward to the limbs and the actions, which the discourse calls the soul's own resources, exactly as a conquered kingdom's whole wealth passes to the one who took its center. What began as words on a surface ends as a reordered life. This is why the same verse, the same prayer, the same act of worship can be a burden to one man and a delight to another: nothing in the outward fact has changed, but everything in the one who meets it has. Knowledge, in this ethos, is finally inseparable from what one is.

## The greater struggle

If knowledge is to become being, the whole of one's being must be available to it — and here the counsel opens onto its hardest demand. In this ethos the effective integration, the tawhid, of knowledge within being is unattainable without the participation of the whole soul; and this in turn requires the elimination of all the vices that flow from egocentricity. Thus the aqil, the true intellectual, is described by the Imam as one constantly engaged in struggle against his own soul — against the lower inclinations, the passionate whims, the distracting caprices of the ego. All these vices, far from being confined to the plane of morality, encroach upon the domain of knowledge, and this is understood in the very measure that knowledge is grasped as a concrete vision of things rather than a merely notional apprehension. The vices are then seen as so many clouds obscuring the vision proper to the reality of insight. According to the Quran, evil actions become a rust that covers over the heart: "Nay, their hearts have become rusted by that which they have done" (chapter 83, verse 14). The aqil is engaged in a constant inner struggle against the tendencies that not only sully the soul but blind the heart.

As a virtuous man he opposes vice on the moral plane, because it is contrary to virtue; but vice is opposed for a further reason, situated on the higher plane of spiritual reality — it contradicts the nature of the Real, it obscures the reality of insight, blinding the eye of the heart which alone can see God. This is not to deny the validity of the moral perspective; the stress falls rather on the deeper motivation that propels the aqil toward virtue, a motivation that transcends the moral plane while comprising it. Morality emerges here as the fruit or effect, and spirituality as the seed or cause. By drawing attention to the intellectual aspect of the aqil's motive for shedding the vices, the Imam shows how immorality is rooted in ignorance; and the uprooting of that ignorance is tantamount to dissolving the very substance of the vices. This uprooting demands a relentless battle within — indeed the most formidable of all battles, the greatest struggle, al-jihad al-akbar, as the Prophet Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him) defined it, naming the war against the soul greater than the war against any outward enemy.

The Imam's own sayings on this inner struggle press the point from every side. "Struggling against the soul through knowledge — such is the mark of the intellect." "The strongest of people are those who are strongest against their own souls." "Truly, one who fights his own soul in obedience to God, and does not sin against Him, has the rank of the righteous martyr in the sight of God." "The ultimate battle is that of a man against his own soul." "He who knows his soul fights it." "No jihad is more excellent than the jihad of the soul."

Yet the Imam also clarifies what the Prophet meant, and the clarification changes everything. It is not so much a matter of fighting against the soul as of the battle for the soul itself. This emerges from the metaphor by which the Imam defines the struggle: the intellect, the aql, is the leader of the forces of al-Rahman, the Compassionate; whim, caprice, desire — al-hawa — commands the forces of al-shaytan, the devil; and the soul, the nafs, vacillates between them, drawn by both, falling under the sway of whichever of the two prevails. The soul in its entirety must be conquered and enlisted on the side of the intellect. The aim is not to destroy the lower soul but to free it from the destructive influence of its caprice — its egoistic whims, its subjectivism, its individualism — all of which are connoted by the word hawa. The Prophet, the Quran tells us, does not speak from hawa; rather, "It is naught but a revelation revealed" (chapter 53, verses 3-4). The divine objectivity of revelation is set here against the human subjectivity of passional whim, and the Imam's sayings bring an analogous polarity into focus: the objectivity of the intellect must prevail over the subjectivity of individual caprice.

The word hawa carries a further and darker sense in the Quran, where it names the desires by which the ego makes itself into a false god: "Hast thou seen him who maketh his desire his god?" (chapter 25, verse 43; almost identical at chapter 45, verse 23). This is the subtle or hidden idolatry, the shirk khafi, which the Prophet described as more hidden than a black ant crawling on a dark stone in a moonless night. To eliminate this idolatry of the ego, a force higher than the ego itself must be brought to bear, since the ego can never be controlled by anything that belongs to it; and that higher force is the divine Reality, access to which is made possible through the power of the intellect and the graces of faith. The Quran expresses the complementarity between the human effort and the divine influx: "As for he who fears the station of his Lord, and restraineth his soul from whim, verily, Paradise will be his abode" (chapter 79, verses 40-41). The soul's fundamental energy is therefore not to be destroyed but converted and redirected — away from the transient objects of individualistic desire and toward the one true object expressed by al-Rahman. This conquest of the soul is far more than arriving at a mental conception; the soul's very deepest disposition must be integrated into the truth perceived by the intellect.

The need to win the soul over in its totality follows naturally from the Imam's understanding of tawhid, the most fundamental principle of Islam. Tawhid is a verbal noun, and to translate it as mere oneness or unity fails to capture its force: it means affirming one — belief in God's oneness, the outward theological meaning — and realizing one — the recognition that there is but one reality, the inner spiritual meaning. The absolutely Real is thus perceived not only as being one but as making one, as integrating; it is not only unique but unifying. Tawhid comes to mean not merely affirming the oneness of God but being one oneself so as to reflect the One perfectly — to know God is to be integrated. Here the etymology of the word aql, whose primary meaning is to bind, comes back into play, but as the intellect that binds together the disparate elements of the soul into an organic unity. This principle is expressed in a series of the Imam's sayings, each of which merits deep reflection: "He who knows God integrates himself." "He who knows his soul disengages himself." "He who knows people isolates himself." "He who knows the world withholds himself."

These four sayings are worth pausing over, for they map the whole movement of the soul that tawhid demands. To know God is to integrate oneself, to gather the scattered forces of the self into one; to know one's own soul is to disengage, to loosen the grip of identification with its passing states; to know people is to isolate oneself, to stand free of the crowd's swaying and its counting of heads; and to know the world is to withhold oneself, to hold back from the transient things that the hoarders of wealth clutch even as they perish. Each act of true knowing produces a corresponding freedom, and each freedom serves the one integration. Read against the opening of the discourse, they explain why the guardians of the proof can be the fewest in number and the greatest in rank: their knowledge has integrated them, disengaged them, isolated them, and withheld them, until nothing of the divided and borrowed self is left to obstruct the light.

Elsewhere this total adherence to the Real is described by the Imam as sincerity — sidq or ikhlas — terms that denote far more than simple truthfulness. To be totally sincere means to make oneself purely for God, to have no stain of impurity, understood as whatever is other than God, in one's devotion, action, intention, or even sentiment: "Make purely for God your action and your knowledge, your love and your hatred, your taking and your leaving, your speech and your silence." One must love and hate only in relation to one's bond with God, and of all the tendencies that insinuate themselves into one's motives the most pernicious is egotism. Ikhlas is the active effort to be, think, and act purely for God, with selflessness, which is why the Imam places such stress on the holy war within as the ultimate battle. The victor in it attains the rank of the righteous martyr, and this is exact: the one who bears witness, the shahid, is both martyr and witness, bearing witness to God through the intellect's sacrifice of whatever opposes the truth of God — the chief opponent being his own egotism, his own false life.

The image of the martyr repays a closer look, because it holds together the two halves of this whole teaching. To possess perfect ikhlas is to be rid of all hypocritical self-preoccupation and to replace it with a sincere theocentricity, a mode of being centered upon God rather than oneself. The one who wins the inner battle is a martyr in the truest sense: he has sacrificed his lower life for the sake of God, with whom is the real life — the life witnessed by the intellect, the only element within human consciousness capable of looking upon the soul as an object, as "the other," with genuine objectivity. This is why the word for witness and the word for martyr are one: the intellectual engaged in this battle bears witness to God through the intellect's sacrifice of whatever opposes the truth of God, and the chief thing opposed is his own egotism, his own false life. The sacrifice is not of the body but of the counterfeit self that had usurped the place of God. What is put to death is only what was never truly alive, and what is gained is the life that the spirit, breathed into man, was always meant to carry. The struggle against the soul, then, turns out to be the same act as the witnessing of God — the negative face of a single movement whose positive face is sincerity.

Worship is inseparable from all of this. Ikhlas means being for God, in the sense of the Quranic verse, "Say: Truly my worship and my sacrifice, and my living and my dying, are for God, Lord of the universes" (chapter 6, verse 162) — where worship is the very first thing named. There is a reason the ego cannot be left to reform itself. It is impossible for the ego to be controlled by anything belonging to the ego; a whim cannot police a whim, and a desire cannot renounce desire on desire's own authority. The idolatry of the self can only be broken by a force higher than the self, and that force is the divine Reality, reached through the power of the intellect and the graces of faith. This is why the whole labor cannot be reduced to moral resolve or strength of will, and why it necessarily passes into worship. Worship is an integral aspect of ikhlas precisely because it relates to and sustains a fundamental dimension of the human soul: that within the soul which yearns to worship, and is nourished only by worship, must be given its due. If worship is marginalized, sincerity is compromised; one is no longer being just to God, no longer rendering God His due — and by the Imam's own logic, if one is not just with God one cannot be just to His creatures: "Whoso establishes well-being between himself and God, God establishes well-being between him and mankind." The whole labor of storming the heart and conquering the soul thus rests on the ground of worship, and finds its horizon in the command, "Worship thy Lord until certainty cometh to thee" (chapter 15, verse 99).

## What this episode lands

The discourse to Kumayl and its commentary converge on a single demand. Knowledge that stays on the surface, however abundant, is not yet knowledge in the full sense; it must storm past the chest into the heart, past the zahir into the batin, past information into the reality of insight — and this penetration cannot happen unless the whole soul has been won for the intellect. That conquest is the greatest struggle, the battle for the nafs, in which the intellect leading the forces of al-Rahman must prevail over hawa and the subtle idolatry of the ego. Its aim is not destruction but integration, the making-one that tawhid truly names. And the ground beneath the whole enterprise is ikhlas, sincerity made pure for God, sustained by worship without which no sincerity survives. The hard, warlike labor described here is not grim for its own sake. It is the price of a certainty that, once attained, turns out to be pure joy — the delight the knowers take in their own knowledge, the happiness that flows from touching the spirit of certainty itself. That joy, and the beauty inseparable from it, is where the next episode begins.

## Closing

The counsel confided in a graveyard outside Kufa turns out to be a map of the interior life. It sorts humanity by its relation to knowledge, weighs knowledge above every possession, and holds up the rare guardians of the proof — those through whom knowledge penetrates the reality of insight — as the measure of what a human being can become. The commentary reads that portrait as a summons addressed to anyone who would bear knowledge truly: let it storm your heart, win your whole soul for your intellect, and stand on the ground of a sincerity made purely for God. What began as the most esoteric of private whispers proves to be the plainest of invitations, and the Imam's own longing closes it — how he longed to see such knowers, and how, having pointed to the abundance in his own breast, he sent his one disciple back into the world to seek them, or to become one.
