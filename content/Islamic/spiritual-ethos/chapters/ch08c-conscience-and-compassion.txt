This episode follows the Letter to Malik al-Ashtar down into its deepest layer, where the war against God turns out to be a war against the self. We trace how the enmity described earlier as pride now reveals itself as a quiet campaign against one's own conscience, how justice therefore has to begin with self-reform and self-lowering, and how it flowers, at last, into a compassion that is not soft feeling but fidelity to the very nature of the Real — impartial toward one's own kin and generous even to a stranger at the roadside.

## Where this episode picks up

What if the real tyrant is not the one who silences other people, but the one who has already silenced the small voice inside himself? The previous episode traced the arrogance of the oppressor outward — the swagger, the appetite for dominion, the readiness to trample whoever stands in the way — and named it for what it is: a form of war waged against God. But arrogance of that kind does not begin in the throne room. It begins in a far quieter place, and this is where we now turn: inward, to the place where a person first learns to stop listening to himself.

Because the enmity with God takes more than one shape, and the shape we meet here is the subtlest of them all. Before the tyrant ever raises a hand against a subject, he raises it against something within his own soul — the faculty that would have told him to stop. In Urdu it is called the damir; in English we call it conscience. It is the inner monitor that recognizes good and evil, and the tyrant's first act of violence is to override it. He does not so much argue with his conscience as learn to no longer hear it, until the voice that once objected falls silent from disuse.

Reza Shah-Kazemi's argument in this section is that we have badly misunderstood what that voice actually is. Secular ethics treats conscience as the bedrock of individual morality — the private sense of right and wrong that each person carries and answers to. That is true as far as it goes, but it stops far too short. For the Imam, conscience is not something the individual generates on his own. It is something infused into the soul from a source infinitely above the individual: the divine breath of discernment that lets a creature tell right from wrong at all. To override one's conscience, then, is not merely to betray oneself. It is to declare war on the trace of God written into the very structure of the human soul.

That reframing changes everything about how we read the letter's warnings. The Imam is not simply advising a governor to keep a clear head. He is describing a spiritual physics in which the corruption of a ruler begins as an interior event — a soul that has stopped obeying its own better knowing and started obeying its appetites instead. And once we see tyranny in this light, it stops being the exclusive vice of kings and comes uncomfortably close to home. The seed of the tyrant, this episode will argue, is planted in any soul that learns to ignore the voice it was given.

## The voice of conscience

So let us begin with that voice, and with the claim that it comes from somewhere far higher than the individual who hears it. When the Imam warns his governor against the corruption of the soul, he is drawing on a Qur'anic picture of what the soul is and how it is furnished. The soul, in that picture, is not a blank slate onto which a society writes its rules. It arrives already inscribed. Woven into its very making is a capacity to recognize the difference between wickedness and righteousness — and that capacity is not the soul's own achievement but a gift breathed into it. The Qur'an puts it with startling economy: "And the soul and that which perfected it, inspiring it with consciousness of its wickedness and its righteousness" (chapter 91, verses 7 to 8).

Read that slowly, because everything turns on it. The soul is not merely made; it is perfected, brought to its proper completion — and the perfecting includes an inspiration, a divine infusion, by which the soul comes to know its own wickedness and its own righteousness. The knowledge of good and evil is not something the soul reasons its way to from the outside. It is placed within, at the moment of the soul's completion, as part of what makes it a soul at all. This is why Shah-Kazemi insists that the divine quality of justice is translated, at the level of the responsible human being, into the voice of inner conscience. Justice above becomes conscience below. The same reality that orders the cosmos rightly orders the soul that will listen to it.

And there is a deeper layer still. The soul does not only carry a sense of right and wrong; it carries a memory — a memory of God's lordship that predates the soul's arrival in the world. The Qur'an evokes a moment at the very dawn of things, a moment that comes before the human condition as we know it yet enters into the making of every human soul. It is often called the primordial covenant: "And when thy Lord brought forth from the children of Adam, from their loins, their seed, and made them testify against their souls, saying, 'Am I not your Lord?' They said, 'Yes, verily, we testify.' This was lest ye say on the Day of Resurrection, 'Truly, of this we were unaware'" (chapter 7, verse 172). At the moment He created them, He made them witness His glory and acknowledge it, so that on the Day of Judgement no one could plead ignorance.

Notice what this does to our idea of conscience. The voice within is not an arbitrary feeling; it is the echo of a promise the soul once made. Spiritual consciousness — the innate awareness of divine lordship and of one's own creaturely dependence — is the source from which moral conscience springs. To know right from wrong is, at bottom, to remember Whose one is. And so the source of morality turns out to transcend the human being even while forming part of the very definition of what a human being is. What makes a soul fully human is precisely this element that infinitely surpasses it and yet, mysteriously, supplies its own deepest ground.

Seen this way, the war against one's conscience stands revealed as something graver than self-betrayal. It is a war against the very reality that defines the soul as a soul. For the soul does not merely happen to contain a moral conscience; it contains, deeper still, an innate consciousness of divine lordship and of its own answering servitude — and it is out of that spiritual consciousness that the moral conscience grows. To silence the conscience is therefore to attack the root as well as the branch: to deny not only that this act is wrong but that there is any Lord to whom one owes an accounting for it. The oppressor who barely acknowledges the small voice he is overriding is, without knowing it, tearing at the deepest structure of his own being.

And this is the point at which tyranny stops being the special vice of rulers and comes uncomfortably close to every one of us. If the tyrant is, first and foremost, one who violates his own soul, then the difference between the despot on his throne and the ordinary person who habitually overrides his own better knowing is a difference of scale and opportunity, not of kind. The political tyrant simply has more power through which to express an interior disorder that any soul can suffer. That is a sobering thought, but it is also the pastoral heart of the teaching: the letter is not only a manual for governors. It is a mirror held up to anyone who has ever quieted the voice within in order to get what he wanted.

That is why the tyrant, in this account, is first of all one who violates his own soul. Before he oppresses anyone else, he lets his soul be dominated by what the tradition calls the soul which commands evil — al-nafs al-ammara, the appetitive self that issues orders and demands to be obeyed. The Imam cites a single sentence as the epitome of the man on his way to becoming an oppressor: "I order and am obeyed." On the surface that is a boast about political power — the ruler who commands and watches his commands carried out. But Shah-Kazemi asks us to read it microcosmically, as a description of what has gone wrong inside the man. For the same sentence is exactly what the evil element within the soul says. The nafs al-ammara literally orders, and every other faculty of the soul — intelligence, imagination, will, feeling, the senses — falls into line and obeys. The tyranny outside is a projection of a tyranny already established within. The man who says "I order and am obeyed" to a nation has first said it to his own conscience, and won.

Which raises the obvious question: what is the answer to a soul like that? The tradition's answer is the combat against one's own soul, mujahadat al-nafs — and we have already met it, in an earlier chapter, as the greater struggle the Prophet named on returning from battle, the war against the lower self that outweighs any war against an external enemy. So we need not expound it again here. It is enough to place it where it belongs: as the interior discipline that keeps the commanding soul from seizing the throne.

What is fresh in this section is the consequence the Imam draws from it — that self-reform is not a private spiritual luxury but the very root of public justice. Placing each thing in its proper place, which is what justice means, has to begin with placing oneself rightly before presuming to order anyone else. The Imam states it without hedging: "If your aspiration is to reform the people, begin with yourself, for your pursuit of the reform of others while your own soul is corrupt is the greatest of faults." A ruler who would straighten a crooked society while his own soul is bent has, in the Imam's eyes, committed the gravest error of all — because he has inverted the order of repair.

And the larger the power entrusted to a man, the more urgent this becomes. In his letter appointing Muhammad b. Abi Bakr — Malik's own predecessor as governor of Egypt — the Imam ties the gift of authority directly to the duty of self-restraint: "you are obliged to oppose your soul and protect your religion." The reasoning is exact. The greater the power at one's disposal, the greater the damage should the soul yield to its desires and gratify them through that power; for then it is not only the man who is corrupted but the very religion he was charged to uphold. The integrity of religion outwardly depends on the integrity of the soul inwardly. Here is the principle of proper placement again: the outward form is only as sound as the inward disposition that carries it.

The alternative is the soul that fancies itself self-sufficient — and the Qur'an draws that soul with lapidary precision: "Truly man is rebellious, in that he deemeth himself independent" (chapter 96, verses 6 to 7). The moment a soul imagines it can stand on its own, severed from its total dependence on God, it sets itself up as a god in its own right. And that is the true portrait of the tyrant: not merely a cruel man, but one who, as the Qur'an says, "taketh his own desire as his god" (chapter 45, verse 23). Knowledge of one's own nothingness before God, and therefore of one's unconditional need of Him, is the one thing that loosens the grip of the commanding soul. For only the soul that already commands itself to evil will command others to it — and only the soul that is lowly before God will be humble, merciful, and just toward people.

## Living like the poor

That last thought — that only the lowly soul rules justly — is the bridge into the letter's next counsel, which at first sounds merely practical. "Let the most beloved of affairs to you be those most centred upon the right, the most comprehensive in justice, and the most inclusive of popular approval, for the disapproval of the common folk undermines the approval of the elite." On the face of it this is shrewd advice about governance: keep the ordinary people with you, because a court that pleases only the powerful sits on nothing. But Shah-Kazemi reads it as far more than statecraft. It reflects the spiritual imperative to recognize man's actual lowliness before God — and so it becomes a summons to the ruler to identify himself with the poor rather than the rich.

The principle beneath it is a Qur'anic verse that levels every human being at a single stroke: "O mankind, ye are the poor before God, and God, He is the Rich, the absolutely Praiseworthy" (chapter 35, verse 15). Before God, everyone is destitute. The wealth a ruler commands is not his own richness but borrowed against an absolute poverty he shares with the beggar at his gate. A leader who has taken that verse to heart will instinctively side with the poor, not because it is politically canny, but because he knows himself to be one of them in the only accounting that finally matters.

That is a striking way to read a piece of practical political advice. The Imam begins with something a hardheaded advisor might say — keep the common people content, because the goodwill of the elite cannot compensate for the resentment of the many — and then discloses the spiritual truth underneath it. The reason the common people matter is not merely that they are numerous. It is that they stand nearer to the truth of the human situation: their visible poverty is a plain image of the invisible poverty in which every soul, ruler included, stands before God. To side with the poor is to align oneself with reality as it actually is. To side with the rich, and to prize their approval, is to be seduced by the illusion of self-sufficiency that the wealthy are most tempted to believe about themselves.

The Imam's own austerity was proverbial — the rough clothing, the coarse food, the refusal of comfort that others in his position took for granted. But this was never a shunning of the world for its own sake, and there is a wonderful exchange that makes the point. A man named Asim b. Ziyad had thrown himself into a severe asceticism, and his family came to the Imam complaining that he had become excessively abstemious, cutting himself off from ordinary life. The Imam told Asim to think of his family and not to deny himself the good things that God has permitted. Asim, quick on his feet, turned the argument straight back: "and yet here you are, in your rough clothes and your coarse food!" If austerity is a fault, he was saying, why does the Commander of the Faithful practice it?

The Imam's reply draws the crucial distinction. "Woe to you — I am not like you. God has made it incumbent on true leaders — a'immat al-haqq, the leaders of the truth — that they proportion themselves to the weakest among the people, so that the poverty of the poor will not engender covetousness." The ascetic denies himself the permitted good things and calls it piety; that, the Imam implies, is a private indulgence of a different kind. But the leader's austerity is not for himself at all. It is a duty laid on those who lead in the name of the truth: to lower themselves, in material terms, to the level of the weakest people they govern. The reason is precisely social. If the ruler lives in visible luxury while the poor go without, their poverty curdles into covetousness and envy. If the ruler shares their condition, that poison has no soil to grow in.

Notice, too, how sharply the Imam distinguishes his own austerity from Asim's. Asim had renounced the good things of the world as a personal spiritual regime, and the Imam gently corrects him for it: the permitted goods of life are not to be spurned, and a man owes something to his family and to his own body. The renunciant who denies himself out of private zeal has, in a sense, made his abstinence about himself. The Imam's own plain living looks identical from the outside — the same rough clothes, the same coarse food — but its meaning is the reverse. It is not self-directed piety; it is an obligation owed to others, a discipline undertaken for the sake of the weakest, so that no poor man looking at his ruler need feel the sting of a comfort he cannot share. Same austerity, opposite spirit: one turned inward toward the self, the other turned outward toward the people.

It is hard to imagine a more demanding expression of the duty of the powerful. The most powerful man is to identify himself, in material fact and not merely in sentiment, with the most powerless. He who could have the most deliberately chooses to have the least. And Shah-Kazemi draws out what this reveals about the leader's inner orientation. To stand among the a'immat al-haqq — the leaders of the truth — a man must manifest his attachment to al-Haqq, the Real, the ultimate spiritual reality in whose light material wealth simply does not register as wealth at all. The word buried in the title is the same as the word for the Absolute. A leader of the truth is one whose heart is fixed on the Truth itself, and to a heart so fixed, gold is not worth turning the head.

There is a beautiful consequence in this. The most powerful and the most powerless may both be materially poor — the ruler by choice, the beggar by circumstance — but that shared poverty is not merely a leveling downward. It opens the same door for both of them onto the treasures of the spiritual domain, in proportion to how deeply each has realized the truth of that verse: ye are the poor before God, and God is the Rich. The self-lowering of the ruler is thus not a loss but an entry. By refusing the false richness of the world he claims the real richness that the world cannot give, and he holds it on exactly the same terms as the weakest of his subjects.

This is worth dwelling on, because it rescues the whole teaching from mere political theatre. One could imagine a cynical version of the same counsel — a ruler who dresses plainly and eats simply as a calculated performance, to flatter the poor and secure their loyalty while his heart remains fixed on power. The Imam's austerity is the opposite of that. It is not a costume worn over an unchanged interior; it is the outward trace of a heart that has genuinely turned from the world toward al-Haqq. The plain living is real because the detachment behind it is real. And only such genuine detachment produces the spiritual access the verse describes, because the door onto the treasures of the spiritual domain does not open to a pose. It opens to a soul that has actually let go.

And on the plane of ordinary social psychology, the effect is precisely the one the Imam named. A society will always have its inequalities; that is the nature of collective life. What determines whether those inequalities breed resentment is whether the people at the top hold themselves apart or hold themselves alongside. The Imam's insistence that rulers live like the poor — an insistence he embodied in his own person before he ever wrote it in a letter — is the most effective guard against envy that a society can have. When the powerful share the conditions of the weak, the gap between them stops being an insult and becomes bearable. Living like the poor, in the end, is not only a discipline of the ruler's soul. It is the quiet mortar that keeps a community from cracking along the fault line of wealth.

## The compassion of justice

Now watch how this self-lowering opens outward into something larger. Identifying with the poor does not only humble the ruler; it deepens his awareness of his own perpetual need of God, teaching him to live in reliance on divine mercy, in hope and trust. And the natural fruit of a life lived in reliance on mercy is the giving of mercy to others. A man who knows how much he depends on being forgiven becomes, almost inevitably, a man who forgives.

This is where Shah-Kazemi corrects a stubborn misunderstanding. We tend to think of compassion and justice as pulling in opposite directions — justice as the stern one, compassion as the soft one, forever having to be balanced against each other. But the Imam's vision refuses that opposition. Compassion in no way conflicts with justice; it is an intrinsic dimension of justice, once justice is conceived ontologically — that is, in accordance with the fundamental nature of reality itself. To act with compassion is to put things in their right place, yes; but it is also to act in accord with the intrinsic character of the Real. Mercy is not a concession that softens justice. Mercy is what justice looks like when it is faithful to the nature of the God whose justice it is.

The proof is written across the Qur'an itself. At the head of every chapter but one, the name Allah is followed immediately by the words "the infinitely Compassionate, the ever-Merciful" — al-Rahman, al-Rahim. Mercy is, so to speak, the first thing said about God at the opening of nearly every revelation. The capacity for compassion, then, is not so much a private tenderness of feeling as an innate affinity with the very nature of divine reality — a reality in which, as the Qur'an says, "My mercy encompasseth all things" (chapter 7, verse 156); a reality in which "thy Lord has prescribed for Himself mercy" (chapter 6, verse 54); a reality whose deepest law is stated in the divine saying, "My mercy takes precedence over My wrath." Wrath is real, but it is not final; mercy is the older and the higher word.

The Imam's own sayings press the same truth into the register of daily conduct. "The dispensing of mercy brings down divine mercy," he says — as if every act of compassion below draws a corresponding compassion from above. "As you grant mercy, so will you be granted mercy." And with a note of genuine astonishment: "I am astounded by the person who hopes for mercy from one above him, while he is not merciful to those beneath him." The man who begs God's forgiveness while withholding his own from those in his power has not understood the terms of the exchange he is asking to enter.

There is a kind of spiritual arithmetic in these sayings that is worth pausing over. Mercy is not simply commended as a virtue; it is described as a channel. To show mercy is to open a conduit through which mercy flows back — and to withhold it is to seal that conduit shut with one's own hand. This means the reality one lives in is, to a real degree, the reality one has chosen to make. A person who reflects the predominance of mercy in his conduct comes to stand more fully within the merciful nature of things; his soul takes on the character of the reality it imitates. To be merciful, on this view, is not only to do a good deed. It is to become more real — more aligned with the deepest grain of existence — and to be harsh is, correspondingly, to shrink oneself toward the thin and passing region of things that are subject to wrath.

But — and this is essential — mercy so understood does not dissolve rigour. The rigorous side of things, the wrathful aspect, is not denied; it is subordinated to the higher principle of mercy, not abolished by it. There remains a place for the firm application of penalty where it is genuinely unavoidable, and the Imam's letters are unflinching on exactly this point. In the same letter to Malik he orders corporal punishment for any officer found misappropriating public funds. His correspondence bristles with warnings of swift and severe retribution to governors who would enrich themselves at the people's expense — the severity of the warnings measuring the depth of his fidelity to honesty in handling public money. To Ziyad b. Abihi, a deputy governor in Basra, he swears a plain oath: "if news reaches me that you have misappropriated the revenue of the Muslims, whether small or large, I shall inflict a severe punishment upon you." The mercy is real; so is the iron.

And the iron came down hardest, tellingly, on those closest to him. In one sermon the Imam recalls how his own blind brother, Aqil b. Abi Talib, once asked for more from the public treasury than was his due — thinking, the Imam says, that he would sell his religion for the sake of family feeling. The Imam's answer was to bring a piece of red-hot iron close to his brother's body, telling him that by making such a request he was urging Ali (ع) himself into a far more terrible fire. When his cousin and long-standing ally Abd Allah b. al-Abbas was implicated in misusing funds, the Imam met him with a thundering remonstrance, ordering him to fear God and return the people's property, and warning that if he did not, the sword would answer for it. Then came the line that leaves no room for favouritism at all: "even if Hasan and Husayn had done the like of what you have done, they would not have been granted any leniency by me." That he names the Prophet's own grandsons — the most beloved figures imaginable — is the whole point. No closeness of blood, no exaltation of status, buys the smallest relaxation of justice.

This is exactly what the Qur'an commands, and the Imam is simply living it out: "O ye who believe, be staunch in justice, witnesses of God, even though it be against yourselves, your parents or your near of kin" (chapter 4, verse 135). Justice that bends for one's own people is not justice; it is nepotism wearing justice's clothes — the very corruption that had undermined the administration before him.

And yet the final word is not severity but a breathtaking statement of human unity. Returning to the theme of mercy, the Imam writes to Malik a passage that places compassion in the widest possible frame: "Infuse your heart with mercy for the subjects, love for them and kindness towards them. Be not like a ravenous beast of prey above them, seeking to devour them. For they are of two types: either your brother in religion or your like in creation." Read that last distinction carefully, because it dismantles every wall a ruler might hide behind. The people you govern are either your brothers in faith or your fellows in the sheer fact of being created — and there is no third category, no group of persons who fall outside the reach of your mercy. Shared religion binds some of them to you; shared humanity binds all the rest. Either way, the door of compassion stays open.

The Imam closes that passage with a reminder of why. The governor is above the people, but the one who appointed him is above the governor, and God is above them all — testing the ruler through the very subjects he is tempted to devour. Every man in a position of superiority must remember his own inferiority before the Absolute, and that memory both moves him to mercy toward those beneath him and draws down the mercy of the One above him.

There is one last incident that shows the whole teaching in a single scene. The Imam once came upon an old, blind beggar and asked about him, and was told the man was a Christian. "You have employed him until he is old and infirm," the Imam said to those around him, "and now you refuse to help him. Give him maintenance from the public funds" — the bayt al-mal, the treasury of the whole community. In one sentence he founds a principle of social welfare — in Arabic, maslaha — rooted in redistributive justice and a strict refusal to discriminate between Muslims and others. The beggar's religion was not the question; his humanity was. He was, in the Imam's own phrase, "your like in creation," and that was claim enough on the community's care. Social justice and religious equality flow, here, not from sentiment but from compassion understood as an intrinsic dimension of the Real — and so they become a religious duty binding on ruler and citizen alike.

## What this episode lands

Follow the thread back and it forms a single line. The tyrant's war against God is finally a war against his own conscience — against the divine discernment breathed into his soul and against the covenant his soul once made when it testified that God was its Lord. That is the seed of every outward oppression: a soul that has learned to override the voice it was given. And so justice cannot start with reforming other people; it starts with reforming oneself, and with the deliberate self-lowering that puts the ruler shoulder to shoulder with the weakest of those he governs, remembering that before God every one of us is poor.

From that lowered ground, compassion rises — not as a soft feeling set against the demands of justice, but as justice being faithful to the merciful nature of the Real, the God whose name is joined to mercy at the head of nearly every chapter of His book. That is a compassion with iron in it: impartial even toward the Prophet's own grandsons, unbending toward a governor's misused coin, yet open-handed to a blind Christian beggar grown old with no one to help him. Conscience, self-reform, self-lowering, mercy — they are not four separate counsels but one continuous movement of the soul that has remembered Whose it is.

What holds these together is a single conviction: that the outer world of governance is downstream of the inner world of the soul. A just society cannot be engineered from the outside by a soul that is itself disordered, because justice is not finally a technique of administration but a quality of being — the placing of each thing in its right place, beginning with the placing of oneself rightly before God. The mercy the Imam commands to Malik is real mercy only because it is grounded in the ruler's own remembered poverty and need. Strip out that inner ground, and the same words become either sentimentality or manipulation.

Having followed that movement into the ruler's own interior, the letter now turns outward, from the ruler's inner state to the ordered life of the society he serves — and above all to the claim that the poorest of its members have upon him.

## Closing

For the Letter to Malik does not end in the ruler's soul. Having established that justice begins within, it goes on to lay out an anatomy of the whole society the ruler holds in trust — the ranks and callings that make a community work, each with its proper place and its proper due. The army that defends it, the judges who weigh its disputes, the scribes who keep its records, the tillers who feed it, the merchants who supply it — and, standing at the sharpest edge of justice's practical demand, the destitute who have no one to speak for them but the ruler himself. It is one thing to teach that mercy is the nature of the Real; it is another to build a government in which that mercy reaches the man at the very bottom. The inner disciplines we have traced in this episode — the listening to conscience, the reform of the self, the deliberate self-lowering, the mercy that answers mercy — were never meant to stay locked inside the ruler's heart. They were meant to become a structure, a working arrangement of offices and duties through which a whole people is held in justice. That is where the letter goes next, and where we will follow it: from the inner discipline of the just ruler to the ordered classes of a just society — the soldiers, the judges, the scribes, the farmers, the traders — and, at the sharpest edge of the whole design, to the standing claim of the poor, the one measure that finally tells us whether any of the rest of it was ever real.
