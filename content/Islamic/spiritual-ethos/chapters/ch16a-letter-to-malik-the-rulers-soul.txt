This episode enters the longest letter Ali (ع) ever wrote — the mandate he sent with Malik al-Ashtar when he made him governor of Egypt, at a time when the province had slipped into unrest. Earlier episodes heard a scholar read this letter from the outside, weighing its vision of justice against Plato and against the secular idea of fairness. Here we hear Ali's own voice give the commands. We follow the letter's opening movement: the inner disposition it demands of a ruler before it will let him touch a single lever of power, the company he must keep and the company he must banish, and the first pillars of the state he is charged to turn — the soldiers, the judges, and the officers who handle its affairs.

## Where this episode picks up

What is the first thing you would hand a man to whom you were about to give a province — an army, a treasury, the seal of office? Ali (ع) hands Malik none of these. Before the letter names a single duty of state, before it mentions a soldier or a coin or a border, it spends its opening breath on the one territory a governor can lose without anyone noticing he has lost it: his own soul. The whole letter, longest and most complete of everything the Imam wrote, is built on a wager that everyone who has ever held power is tempted to forget — that the outward kingdom is only ever as sound as the inward one, and that a man who cannot govern the appetites of his own heart has no business governing the affairs of other people's.

That is the tension this episode turns on. We have already heard, in the sermon that opened Ali's own voice to us, how he pictures the cosmos as a single act of a God beyond number, and how every creature hangs upon that One. The letter now brings that vision down out of the heavens and lays it across the desk of a working administrator. It is one thing to know that all power belongs to God; it is another to be handed a province and to keep believing it while you sign the orders. The letter's method is to refuse to let Malik separate the two. Every practical instruction it gives — whom to promote, whom to tax, whom to forgive — is anchored back to a claim about God and about the self, so that administration never becomes a merely technical art. The Imam is teaching a man how to run Egypt. He is also, in the same sentences, teaching him how to save his own soul, and the letter's genius is that it never lets him treat these as two different jobs.

So it opens not with a grant of authority but with a warning about it. The mandate ('ahd) begins by naming what Malik is being sent to do — to gather the revenues of Egypt, to fight its enemies, to seek the welfare of its people, and to make its land prosper — and then, before the ink is dry on that commission, it turns and commands him to fear the God who gave it. The order of those two things is the whole point. Authority first, and immediately the fear of God as the frame around it. This is where we begin: with the ruler's soul, and with what a man is really holding when he holds a province.

## The ruler's soul

The letter's first commands are not about Egypt at all. Ali (ع) tells Malik to fear God, to give obedience to God precedence over every other claim, and to hold fast to what God has enjoined in His Book, "for no one attains virtue except by following them, and no one becomes wretched except by rejecting and neglecting them." Then comes the command that governs all the rest: that Malik curb the passions of his own soul and restrain it when whim and caprice run high — "for the soul incites to evil unless God shows mercy." That single clause carries an entire anthropology. The soul, left to itself, does not drift neutrally; it leans, and it leans downward, and the only counterweight is a mercy the man cannot manufacture from inside himself. A governor is therefore a man at war on two fronts, and the inner front is the one he must win first, because the enemy there wears his own face. Assist God, the letter says, with your heart, your hand, and your tongue — for God has undertaken to grant victory to whoever assists Him. The victory offered is not military. It is the victory of a man over the appetite that would otherwise turn his office into a weapon against the very people it was given to protect.

It is worth pausing on the four things the mandate actually commissions Malik to do, because their order is not innocent. He is charged to gather Egypt's revenues, to fight its enemies, to seek the welfare of its people, and to bring prosperity to its lands. Two of those charges — the taxes and the war — are what any ruler in history would list first, the hard currency of holding a province. But the letter brackets them and finally subordinates them to the other two: the welfare of the people and the flourishing of the land. By the time the letter reaches the question of taxation in its later pages it will say outright that the cultivation of the soil matters more than the collection of the revenue; the seed of that reversal is already planted here, in the way the commission refuses to let money and warfare stand as ends in themselves. A governor is sent to make a place thrive, and the gathering and the fighting are only instruments of that thriving. Read the four charges in the wrong order and you have licensed a predator. Read them in the Imam's order and you have described a gardener who happens to command an army.

Then the letter widens the frame with a piece of counsel that is really a mirror. "Know, Malik," it says, "that I am sending you to a land that has seen governments before yours — some just and some unjust — and the people will now weigh your conduct just as you once weighed the conduct of the rulers before you. They will speak of you just as you spoke of them." It is a devastating reminder, and it works by memory. Malik has stood where the governed stand; he has judged rulers; he knows exactly how a subject's eye tracks the small hypocrisies of the powerful. Now he is to be watched by that same unforgiving eye, and the letter tells him what will actually survive the watching: "the righteous are known only through what God causes to flow about them on the tongues of His servants. So let the treasure dearest to you be the treasure of good deeds." A ruler amasses many things. The only one that lasts is the one he cannot spend. And the first discipline of amassing it is fairness toward the self — mastering one's own desires and holding back from what is not lawful, which the Imam calls a kind of justice the soul does toward itself, keeping it balanced between what it likes and what it dislikes.

From the self the letter turns to the subjects, and the turn is abrupt and physical. "Habituate your heart to mercy, love, and kindness towards your subjects. Do not stand over them like a ravenous beast that considers them fair prey." The image is deliberately ugly, because the temptation it names is real: power invites a man to look at the people beneath him as things to be consumed. Against that, the Imam offers a classification that dissolves the predator's whole way of seeing. The people you govern, he says, "are of two kinds: either your brother in religion or your equal in creation." There is no third category. There is no class of persons who fall outside the circle of kinship and may therefore be treated as prey. Faults will slip from them, deliberately or by accident; extend them forgiveness and pardon, the letter says, "just as you would wish God to extend to you His forgiveness and pardon." And then the vertical order that keeps the whole thing honest: "for you are above them, and the one in authority over you is above you, and God is above the one who appointed you." Every rung of authority is answerable to a higher rung, and the ladder does not stop until it reaches the One who is above all appointing. Malik holds power over Egypt exactly as someone holds power over Malik, and God holds power over that someone. To forget the top of the ladder is to imagine oneself standing on the ground when one is in fact suspended in mid-air.

This is where the letter issues its sternest warning, and the earlier episodes on justice already unfolded its logic — that pride is not merely a vice but a kind of war declared against God, a rival claim to a greatness that belongs to Him alone. Here we hear the Imam say it to a specific man about to be tempted in a specific way. "Do not set your soul on a course of war with God," he tells Malik, "for you have no power before His retribution and cannot do without His forgiveness and mercy." And he names the exact sentence a new governor is most likely to say to himself: "Do not say, 'I have been given authority, so I command and am obeyed,' for that breeds corruption in the heart, weakens religion, and draws misfortune near." The corruption begins as a private thought — the small intoxication of being obeyed — and works outward from there. Against it the Imam prescribes not suppression but a change of gaze: "If the authority you hold breeds pride or vanity in you, look at the greatness of God's dominion above you and at His power over you — power the like of which you do not hold even over your own self." The cure for feeling large is to look at something genuinely large, and to notice that one cannot command even a single heartbeat of one's own body. "This will calm your ambition, restrain your vehemence, and restore the good sense that had strayed from you." Pride, in this diagnosis, is a lapse of good sense before it is a moral failure — a man doing bad arithmetic about his own size — and the correction is simply to see accurately again. "Beware of matching yourself with God in greatness or likening yourself to Him in might, for God humbles every tyrant and disgraces every braggart."

The movement closes by binding justice to the same divine reference. "Be just with God and be just with people," the Imam says, "giving them their due from yourself, from your close relatives, and from those of your subjects for whom you feel fondness — for if you do not, you are an oppressor." Justice, note, must be paid first out of the ruler's own pocket and his own family's before it is demanded of strangers; the fondness a man feels for his intimates is precisely where injustice hides. And the consequence of failing is stated with a terrible economy: "whoever oppresses the servants of God has God, rather than His servants, as his adversary; and whomever God opposes, He refutes his plea, and he remains at war with God until he desists and repents." Nothing, the letter warns, so surely hastens the withdrawal of God's grace as persistence in oppression, "for God hears the cry of the oppressed and lies in wait for the oppressors." That last image — God lying in wait — is the frame for everything the letter will now build. The oppressed man may have no court to appeal to on earth; he has a hearer in heaven who is patient and who does not forget.

## The company a ruler keeps

Having set the disposition, the letter turns to a question every ruler underestimates: who is allowed to stand close. A governor is shaped by the voices at his ear, and Ali (ع) is fierce about which voices Malik must draw near and which he must drive off. He begins, strikingly, by settling whose contentment the ruler should chase. There are the common folk — al-'amma, the ordinary mass of the people — and there are the elite, al-khassa, the few with wealth and influence. A ruler's instinct is to court the second, because they are near and articulate and useful. The Imam reverses it. "Let the dearest of your affairs," he says, "be those most balanced in truth, most comprehensive in justice, and most inclusive of your subjects' contentment — for the discontent of the common folk cancels out the contentment of the elite, while the discontent of the elite is absorbed within the contentment of the common folk." He then draws the portrait of the elite without flattery: no one is heavier on a ruler in easy times and less useful in hard ones, more resentful of fair treatment and more insistent on favours, slower to be grateful and quicker to abandon patience, than the privileged. "It is the common folk of the community who are the pillar of religion, the mass of the Muslims, and the defence against enemies — so let your inclination and your leaning be towards them." The centre of gravity of a just state is the many, not the few.

Then the letter turns to concealment, and here it teaches something that runs against every instinct of a security apparatus. The ruler will be surrounded by people eager to report the faults of others. Ali (ع) tells Malik to keep exactly those people furthest from him and most despised in his eyes, "for people do have faults, and the ruler is the one most bound to conceal them." His duty is not to expose what is hidden but only to correct what has become plain; the rest God will judge. And the reason he gives is a piece of pure spiritual reciprocity: "Veil people's failings as much as you can, and God will veil from your subjects the failings in yourself that you would wish kept concealed." The ruler who hunts sins will have his own sins hunted; the ruler who covers them buys himself a covering. So he is told to loosen from the people every knot of resentment and to cut from his own heart the cord of every enmity, to turn a blind eye to whatever is genuinely unclear, and never to be quick to trust a slanderer — "for a slanderer is a deceiver, however sincerely he may appear to advise." The most dangerous informant is the one who comes dressed as a well-wisher.

There is a hard realism in this that is easy to miss under its gentleness. The Imam is not naive about wrongdoing; he does not tell Malik to pretend that faults do not exist. He tells him to distinguish sharply between two things a ruler is constantly tempted to confuse — the faults that have become public, which he is bound to correct, and the faults that remain hidden, which are not his to drag into the light. A government that makes it its business to expose everyone's private failings does not become more just; it becomes a machine of suspicion in which no one is safe and the ruler himself is the least safe of all, since his own concealed faults now sit under the same lamp he has trained on everyone else. Concealment, in the Imam's economy, is not the covering-up of evil but the refusal to turn human weakness into a weapon. Loosen the knots of resentment, he says; cut the cords of enmity from your own heart. A ruler who carries private grudges will find a way to make them public policy, and the people will pay for a quarrel that was never theirs.

Now the letter names the counsellors a ruler must refuse outright, and the earlier episodes already traced the theology beneath the list — that miserliness, cowardice, and greed, for all their differences, are a single disease, "united by a single bad opinion of God," each of them a quiet suspicion that the divine is not generous enough, or protective enough, or providing enough. Here the Imam applies that diagnosis to hiring. Do not admit a miser to your counsel, he says, for he will turn you from generosity and frighten you with the spectre of poverty; nor a coward, for he will make you feel too weak for your own affairs; nor a greedy man, for he will dress up avidity until it looks like justice. And then a harder rule: "The worst of your ministers is the one who served evil rulers before you and shared in their sins — do not let such men into your inner circle, for they are helpers of sinners and brothers of tyrants." The competence of such men is not the point; their formation is. You can find substitutes of equal ability, the Imam says, who carry none of that inherited guilt, and those men will cost you less, support you better, and attach themselves to no one above you. Choose them, "in private and in public."

Among the men a ruler keeps close, the letter then singles out one for the very highest place — and the choice is astonishing. Give the highest rank, Ali (ع) says, to the man "who most readily speaks the bitter truth to you and gives you the least encouragement in whatever you do that God dislikes for His friends, however painfully that strikes at your own desires." The most valuable person in the room is the one who makes the ruler uncomfortable. Attach yourself to the pious and the truthful, and train them not to flatter you, not to gladden you with praise for something you never did — "for excessive praise breeds conceit and carries a person towards vanity." A ruler surrounded by applause slowly loses the ability to hear anything else. And he must never place the good and the wicked on the same footing, "for that would make the good think little of their goodness and the wicked settle deeper into their wickedness." Justice among his servants means that virtue and vice are seen to matter, each held to exactly what it has earned.

The letter closes this movement on the theme of continuity, and it is a quiet lesson in humility about time. Nothing, the Imam observes, so builds a ruler's confidence among his people as kindness, the easing of their burdens, and the refusal to press them past their strength; such conduct earns a trust that spares him long trouble. And a ruler should hold that trust in both directions — the person most deserving of his good opinion is the one he has treated well, and the person most deserving of his suspicion is the one he has treated badly, because men return the treatment they are given. Then the counsel about custom: "Do not break any good tradition established by the leaders of this community, by which harmony was knit together and the subjects set right; and do not introduce any new practice that harms those already in place." The reward for a good tradition goes to the one who founded it, the Imam warns, but the guilt for breaking it falls on the one who breaks it. And the earlier episodes have already heard him distinguish the scholars from the sages — those who know the letter of the law from those who know how to lay it against a living case — so that his final instruction here lands as a familiar note struck again: study long with the scholars, hold long discourse with the sages, "so as to strengthen what keeps your lands sound and to uphold what those before you had already set right." A ruler is not the first just man to hold his office, and he will not be the last; his task is to conserve what worked and add to it, not to treat the state as a blank page for his own signature.

## The pillars of rule

Only now, its foundations laid, does the letter turn to the machinery of the state itself. The Imam tells Malik that his subjects fall into several classes, none sound without the others — the soldiers of God, the scribes who serve high and low, the judges who administer justice, the officers who secure people's right of redress, those who pay the land-tax (kharaj, خراج) and the poll-tax (jizya, جزیۃ), the merchants and artisans, and the lowest class, the needy and the destitute. The earlier episodes have already turned this catalogue into its famous image, the wheel of society whose classes are spokes and whose governor is a hub that turns nothing without the unseen axle joining it to the Real. Here the fresh point is the word the Imam uses for how the shares are fixed: to each class God has assigned its due, "as a binding covenant ('ahd) from Him, kept safe with us." The distribution of rights across society is not a policy Malik invents; it is a trust handed down, and the same word names both this document and the thing it protects. The mandate is itself an 'ahd about a series of smaller 'uhud. Everything that follows is the keeping of a covenant.

The first pillar is the army, and the earlier episodes already drew out the paradox in the Imam's portrait of a commander — that nearly every quality he demands of a fighting man is a quality of gentleness, so that the best soldier turns out to be the mildest man. Here we hear the full instruction in his own words, and it goes further than the portrait. Appoint as commander, he says, the man you feel most sincere toward God, the Prophet, and your Imam, "the purest of heart and the most excellent in forbearance (hilm): one who is slow to anger, glad to pardon, kind to the weak, and firm with the strong." Choose men of established name and proven courage, for they are "a group formed by nobility and a party made of honour." And then the tenderness that the commentary passed over: "supervise their affairs as parents would supervise their child." Let no kindness to them seem too small to bother with, for a small kindness "is a summoner calling them to give you good counsel and to place their trust in you." The soldier is not a tool to be maintained but a child to be raised; the loyalty a ruler wants cannot be commanded, only earned by a care that stoops to the smallest thing.

The letter lingers on this care longer than on any other pillar, because an army is where a ruler's inner disposition becomes visible fastest — a hard man produces hard soldiers, and hard soldiers turn on the people they were raised to guard. Let the most preferred of your commanders, Ali (ع) says, be the one most benevolent to his men and most generous with his bounty, so that they and their dependants have ample means and want never drives them; and let their every concern be gathered into a single resolve — to face the enemy. Then he names what a just command actually produces. "The greatest source of joy for governors," he says, "is the establishment of justice in the land and the flowing of love from their subjects." That love, he warns, will not appear unless the people's hearts are secure and they no longer privately hope the ruler's term will end — a quiet, terrible test of a governor, that his subjects should stop wishing him gone. So raise their hopes, he tells Malik, praise them continually, and recount aloud the brave deeds of those who have been brave, "for abundant praise of noble acts rouses the courageous and spurs on the lazy." But praise must be exact, or it does the opposite of what it intends. Acknowledge the real deed of every man and never ascribe the deed of one to another; do not let a man's high birth inflate a small accomplishment into a great one, and do not let a man's low station shrink a great accomplishment into a small one. Justice, even in the handing-out of honour, means each thing weighed at its true weight and set in its true place — the same principle that ordered the ruler's soul, now ordering the ledger of who gets thanked.

And when disputes arise among these men or anywhere in the realm, the Imam gives Malik the final court of appeal, quoting the verse that fixes it: "O ye who believe, obey God and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. And if ye dispute with one another over anything, then refer it to God and the Messenger" (chapter 4, verse 59). To refer a thing to God, he explains, is to follow what is clear and unequivocal in His Book; to refer it to the Messenger is to follow the part of his practice that unites rather than the part that divides. Even the resolution of conflict is bent toward unity.

The second pillar is the judge, and here the letter slows down and paints a soul the world cannot bend. Choose for the settlement of disputes, Ali (ع) says, the most excellent of your subjects: a man "whom complex matters do not confound and whom disputants do not enrage; who does not persist in error, and does not shrink from returning to the truth once he perceives it; whose soul is not drawn toward greed; who is not content with a shallow understanding but probes to its depths." He must be the most cautious before ambiguities and the firmest in holding to the proofs, the least wearied by litigants who come back again and again, the most patient in uncovering the true state of affairs, the most resolute once the right judgement is plain, and untouched by praise or enticement alike. Then the Imam adds the rueful line that concedes how tall an order he has just given: "Such people are indeed rare." He knows. And knowing it, he does two practical things the commentary only gestured at. First, watch the judge's work: examine carefully and often how his verdicts are actually carried out. Second — and this is the reformer speaking — pay him so generously that want is removed and his need to lean on anyone else disappears, and give him "a rank of nearness to yourself that none of your other close companions might hope to attain, so that he is safe in your presence and shielded from the attacks of other influential men." A judge who is poor can be bought; a judge who is exposed can be frightened. Ali (ع) closes both doors at once, and he tells Malik exactly why the stakes are so high: "this religion had been held captive in the hands of evil people, abused by them for their own caprice and exploited by them for the sake of this world." The independent judge is how a religion is unchained from the men who would use it as a leash.

The third pillar is the corps of administrators and officials who handle the state's affairs and its money, and toward them the letter's tone turns vigilant. Employ them only after testing them, the Imam warns, "and do not appoint anyone out of partiality or favouritism, for these two are a wellspring of every kind of injustice and treachery." Seek out men of proven experience and modesty, from righteous households and families with a precedent of service in Islam, for such men are the least given to greed and the most mindful of how things end. Then provide them a generous livelihood — the same logic as the judge's salary — because a well-paid officer has the strength to keep himself in order, is freed from the temptation to reach into the funds in his charge, and stands without excuse should he betray his trust anyway. And then the machinery of watching: appoint over them supervisors known for sincerity and reliability, "for your quiet watching of their affairs will move them to keep faith with their trust and to be gentle with the subjects." Trust is extended, but with the eyes open. And where the watching turns up a genuine betrayal — where an officer stretches his hand toward something treacherous and the reports of the watchers agree upon it until the evidence is sufficient — the Imam is unsparing: lay a bodily penalty upon him, take him to account for the damage his act has caused, set him in a place of disgrace, "brand him with the charge of treachery, and gird him with the shame of the accusation." Mercy toward the subjects and severity toward the officer who preys on them are, in this letter, the same virtue seen from two sides.

It is worth noticing that the same two instruments recur across all three pillars, because the repetition is a doctrine in itself. For the commander, the judge, and the administrator alike, the Imam prescribes generous provision and close watching — pay them enough that need cannot tempt them, and watch them closely enough that opportunity cannot either. The pairing is neither cynical nor naive. It refuses the fantasy that good character alone will hold a man steady against poverty and unchecked power, and it equally refuses the fantasy that surveillance alone can manufacture honesty in a man kept hungry. A just administration, on this reading, does not choose between trusting people and watching them; it does both at once, and it removes the pressures — want, exposure, the ache of being underpaid and overexposed — under which even good men bend. The Imam is building a state that makes it easier to be honest than to be corrupt, and that is a very different project from a state that merely punishes corruption after the fact.

## What this episode lands

The listener now holds the shape of the letter's first half. It begins not with Egypt but with Malik — with the discovery that the hardest province a governor rules is the one behind his own eyes. The soul inclines to evil unless mercy intervenes; power tempts a man to say "I command and am obeyed" and to imagine himself large; the cure is to look at a genuinely large thing and remember he cannot command his own heartbeat. From that inward correction the whole outward order follows. The people beneath him are brothers in religion or equals in creation, never prey. The voices near him must be chosen against his own comfort — the common folk over the flattering elite, the concealer of faults over the hunter of them, the bitter-truth-teller over the applauder, the clean minister over the skilled accomplice of past tyrants.

And when the letter finally reaches the machinery of state, each pillar is set on a spiritual footing. The soldier is chosen for his gentleness and raised like a child; the judge is made rare, watched, paid, and shielded, so that a religion cannot be taken captive again; the officer is tested, paid, supervised, and, if he betrays, disgraced. Every practical instruction has carried a claim about God folded inside it. But the letter is not finished. It has ordered the ruler's soul and the great instruments of his power; it has not yet reached the ground those instruments stand on — the land itself and the people who till it, the poor from whom no ruler can profit, and the pledges a ruler gives to friend and enemy alike. That is where the next episode goes, down from the machinery of the state to its soil and its most forgotten faces, and out to the covenants on which, the Imam will say, everything finally rests.

## Closing

The first half of the letter has been, in a sense, about height — about a man placed above others and taught not to be undone by the placing. It has moved from the ruler's inmost desires up the ladder of authority to the God who stands above every appointment, and back down through the classes of counsellors and commanders and judges he must arrange beneath him. What it has not yet done is kneel. For all its talk of the destitute among the classes, the letter has so far kept the poor at the edge of the frame, one spoke among many. In the next movement Ali (ع) will do something the structure has been quietly promising: he will stop the whole procession, cry out "O God, O God," and turn the ruler's face toward the people who can never repay him — and from there to the ledger of covenants and blood on which a soul is finally weighed. The machinery is built. The next episode asks what it is all for, and finds the answer where power looks least and matters most.
