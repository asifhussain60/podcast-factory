This episode follows the remembrance of God to its vertiginous end — the point where the self that was doing the remembering is itself forgotten. We move from perfect remembrance, in which heart and tongue are brought into harmony, to the reality of remembrance, in which the individual is effaced altogether; through the mystical extinction the tradition calls fana; and finally to the astonishing reversal by which it turns out that God, not man, is the true one who invokes.

## Where this episode picks up

Why would the highest form of remembering God turn out to be a kind of forgetting? That is the question this episode has to answer, and it is the Imam himself who forces it. He has taught that remembrance polishes the heart until it sees, that the invocation of the Name is the remedy for the soul's forgetfulness, that the purified heart beholds God everywhere. And then he says something that seems to pull the ground out from under all of it: you will not remember God according to the true reality of the remembrance until you forget your own soul in your remembrance. The summit of remembrance is a self-forgetting. This is the strangest and most demanding of all his teachings on dhikr, and it opens onto what the mystics called realization, tahqiq — the process by which the Real, al-Haqq, is made real within a person. Following the Imam's characteristic way, we approach the fruit of remembrance first through its opposite: through heedlessness and distraction, the forgetting of God, before turning to that far stranger forgetting — the forgetting of the self — in which alone remembrance is consummated. There are, we will find, two forgettings that could hardly be more different. One is the disease the whole discipline exists to cure: the heedlessness that lets God slip from view. The other is the cure carried to its furthest point: a forgetting so complete that even the self who was doing the remembering drops out of sight, leaving only the Remembered. To move from the first forgetting to the second is the whole journey this episode describes.

## Perfect and imperfect remembrance

The Qur'an is severe with those who go through the outward motions of worship while their hearts are elsewhere. Woe, it says, to those who pray, who are heedless of their prayer, who make a show of it, and who withhold simple assistance (chapter 107, verses 4 to 7). It is possible to pray and to be, at the same time, absent from one's prayer — present in body and posture, gone in mind and heart. Worse still is the prayer offered for show, the worship whose real audience is other people rather than God; the Qur'an couples the two failings, the heedlessness and the showing, and calls those who combine them givers of the lie to religion. Both are forms of a single disease: a devotion in which the self, and not God, remains at the center — whether the self that has drifted off into its own distractions or the self that is performing its piety for an onlooker. In each case the tongue says one thing and the heart is somewhere else, and the gap between them is exactly the impurity that empties the act of its power. The Imam warns in the same key against remembering God heedlessly or distractedly, and in describing the opposite he gives one of the most concentrated sayings in his whole corpus. Do not remember God absent-mindedly, he says, nor forget Him in distraction; rather, remember Him with perfect remembrance, a remembrance in which your heart and tongue are in harmony, and what you conceal conforms with what you reveal. But you will not remember Him according to the true reality of the remembrance until you forget your own soul in your remembrance.

Read slowly, this saying contains a whole map of the spiritual path, and it moves in two stages that must be carefully distinguished. The first stage is what the Imam calls perfect remembrance. Its mark is harmony — the tongue and the heart brought into a single accord, so that what a person conceals within matches exactly what he reveals without. This is the elimination of every trace of hypocrisy, the achievement of total sincerity. The mystical tradition often describes its whole aim in just this word, sincerity, or truthfulness: to orient oneself and every desire purely toward God, with no impurity mixed in — where impurity means the presence, in one's worship or intention or act, of any wish for something other than God. And there is a reciprocal relation between this sincerity and the remembrance. On one side, the remembrance helps produce sincerity by polishing the heart; on the other, it is sincerity that confers perfection on the remembrance, that makes the invocation spiritually effective rather than a hollow verbal exercise. What matters at this stage is that the outward form of the invocation must harmonize with the heart's inmost intention, so that the divine Reality named by the tongue actually reverberates in the heart — the very source of the soul's dispositions and virtues.

It is worth seeing that this whole first stage is, in effect, a summary of the entire spiritual path as the tradition maps it — the long sequence of stations, the maqamat, through which total sincerity is gradually attained. Sincerity is not a single act but the slow work of a lifetime, the progressive purging of every motive that is not God from a soul that begins riddled with them. Each station won is a further stretch of the self brought into the harmony the Imam names, until the concealed and the revealed no longer diverge at any point. This is why the tradition can define its entire enterprise with the one word truthfulness, or sincerity: everything it asks of a person is contained in the single demand to become, through and through, someone whose inner life and outer life say the same thing, and who says that one thing to God alone. And the remembrance is both the means of that ascent and, at each stage, the measure of it — for the invocation is only as perfect as the sincerity of the heart from which it rises, and the sincerity of the heart is deepened, in turn, by the invocation that polishes it.

For the divine qualities held within the Name of God must, to some degree, come to be reflected in the heart and soul of the one who invokes, in the form of virtues — and these virtues are summed up in the purity, the sincerity, to which the whole discipline is directed. When the soul is truly pure, wholly at one with the highest realities the tongue and the intellect proclaim, it becomes transparent to the spiritual qualities embedded in its own deepest substance, the primordial nature with which it was made. Those qualities are brought to light — and brought to life — by the remembrance; they shine out from a heart the invocation has polished. In their absence, the merely technical act of invocation is heedless and devoid of all power. And this is where the effort comes in that the Imam never lets us forget. One who makes no effort to attain sincerity — who does not engage in that inner struggle followed earlier in this series as the greater striving, the war against one's own ego — is heedless of God even while his tongue is moving, neglecting the moral conditions on which the whole efficacy of remembrance depends. Perfect remembrance is not a trick of concentration; it is the fruit of a life given over to becoming sincere.

But then comes the second stage, and with it a break so sharp it amounts to a change of order. For all its perfection, the remembrance so far described still belongs to the individual as its agent. It is the person's own heart brought into harmony with the person's own tongue; the self that remembers remains intact, standing at the center of the act. And the Imam says that even this perfect remembrance is not yet the reality of remembrance. You will not remember Him according to the true reality of the remembrance, he says, until you forget your own soul in your remembrance. There is, then, a real disjuncture between perfect remembrance and the reality of remembrance — between human perfection, on the one hand, and divine Reality on the other. At the first level, where the aim is the removal of hypocrisy and the attainment of sincerity and wholeness, the individual as agent of the remembrance keeps his identity; the harmony of hidden intent and outward act is what earns the name perfect. At the second level the remembrance can no longer be defined in relation to the individual at all, because the individual has been forgotten — by himself. And that can only mean one thing: that the center of consciousness has shifted, moving from the one who invokes to the One who is invoked. The reality of remembrance is thus one with its very object, the Real itself. To pass from the perfection of remembrance to its reality is to cross from the human to the divine, and no amount of further polishing carries a person across that gap. It is a leap, and it costs the self.

## Extinction in remembrance

What the Imam is describing at this second level is what the whole mystical tradition means by extinction, fana — the perishing of egoistic self-awareness before the Absolute. There can be no true knowledge of God, the tradition holds, without self-effacement at the beginning and this extinction at the end; the veil of the self must be cast aside if the spiritual vision of the divine Reality is to be attained. But here an obvious and pressing difficulty arises, and the Imam's own words already contain its answer. If the self must be forgotten, can a person simply set out to forget it? The answer is no — and the reason is exact. Any effort to will oneself into unconsciousness of oneself is itself an act of the conscious will, and so it entrenches the very self-consciousness it means to dissolve. The harder one tries to forget oneself, the more firmly one is there, trying. This is why it matters that the Imam says the soul is to be forgotten in the remembrance. It is not a matter of forcing oneself to forget oneself while invoking God; it is a matter of total concentration on the Name — a concentration so complete that no room remains in consciousness, no space mentally or existentially, for anything but the Named, the reality being invoked. A first degree of self-effacement follows naturally from that total absorption; where there is room only for God, there is no room left for the self. But the ultimate consummation of this effacement, the full extinction, comes as a divine grace, something that surpasses all individual effort — even though the act of invocation is understood to attract that grace, or to remove from the heart the rust that hides from a person their own nothingness before the sole reality of God.

The Qur'an itself hints at this when it says that a person will not forget unless God wills it so (chapter 87, verses 6 and 7) — read here in an inner sense, of the forgetting of self that God alone can grant. One of the earliest masters of the Sufi tradition, al-Kalabadhi, opens his chapter on remembrance with words that echo the Imam almost exactly: the reality of remembrance, he writes, is that you forget everything other than the Invoked in the remembrance — for God says, remember your Lord when you have forgotten (chapter 18, verse 24), which means that when you have forgotten what is not God, then you have truly remembered God. Remembrance in its deepest sense is thus a double movement: to bring God to mind is, at the limit, to let everything else — the self above all — fall out of mind entirely.

There is a strict logic to how this self-forgetting can be, at once, the peak of remembrance rather than its collapse. The human invocation is a kind of reflection of the divine, and like any reflected image it both participates in what it reflects and inverts it. In one respect the human act of invocation leads to a greater fullness of consciousness, a fuller share in being; in the other respect it finds its consummation in what looks like the exact opposite — the effacement of the individual in the One invoked. Yet this is only apparently opposed to the heightening of consciousness. In truth it is that heightening carried to its end, where the final step requires a leap: the negation of the very limits that make up individuality as such. For to be an individual is to be limited, and a limit is itself a kind of negation, a not-being-everything-else. To negate that negation is not to fall into nothingness but to arrive at pure affirmation — the affirmation of unconditioned, supra-individual being. The dissolving of the self is not a loss of reality but the removal of the one thing that was keeping reality bounded. This is the sense of the Imam's enigmatic words, followed earlier in this series, when he calls himself the death of the dead; and it is the sense of the lines Rumi places in his mouth in the Mathnawi — I have removed the baggage of self out of the way, the Imam says there, I have deemed all that is other than God to be non-existence; I am a shadow, the Sun is my lord; I am the chamberlain, I am not the curtain that prevents approach to Him.

The mystics found the same reality dramatized in the story of Moses on the mountain. When Moses asked to see God, the Qur'an says, he fell down stunned (chapter 7, verse 143). Ibn al-'Arabi, recounting his own visionary ascent through the heavens, tells of meeting Moses in the sixth heaven and questioning him about that request, reminding him of the Prophet's saying that no one will see his Lord until he dies. And Moses answers: it was just so — when I asked for the vision, He answered me, so that I fell down stunned, and then I saw Him in my being stunned. Ibn al-'Arabi presses: while you were dead? And Moses replies, while I was dead; I did not see God until I had died. The vision of God, in other words, could not belong to Moses the living, self-standing individual; it came only when that individual had, in the decisive sense, ceased to be. Ibn al-'Arabi builds a whole treatise on this, whose very title — the Book of Extinction in Contemplation — points to the Imam's paradox of forgetting in the heart of remembrance. At its opening he declares the divine Reality too exalted to be witnessed by any creaturely eye, so long as the least trace of the creature's condition remains in the eye of the witness; and he resolves the impasse only at the end, by way of the Prophet's own definition of excellence in worship — to worship God as though you see Him, and if you see Him not, yet truly He sees you. The Arabic of that saying, Ibn al-'Arabi notes, can be read with a pause in the middle that transforms its sense entirely, so that it comes to say: if you are not, you see Him. The vision of God is possible precisely where the self is not.

This is no mere speculation, and the surest proof is the Imam's own life. Abu'l-Darda, an eminent companion of the Prophet, once found Ali (ع) lying in one of the palm orchards of Medina, his body seemingly lifeless, as rigid as a piece of wood. Thinking him dead, he went to condole with the family, only to be told by Fatima: my cousin has not died; rather, in fear of God he has fainted, and this condition overcomes him often. The fear in question is not terror but awe — the awe of one who feareth the station of his Lord and restrains his soul from caprice, to whom the Garden is promised (chapter 79, verses 40 and 41). In the spiritual rather than the moral sense, the fear of God does not so much frighten as beatify; it produces not merely a trembling before the transcendent but an intimate absorption in the divine presence. As the Sufis often say, when one fears creatures one flees from them, but when one fears God one flees to Him — for there is no refuge from God except to Him (chapter 9, verse 118). The Imam's recurring collapse in the orchard was the overflow of a soul so absorbed in God that its ordinary consciousness of itself gave way.

It is important not to mistake this extinction for a final annihilation, an ending in emptiness. In the Sufi tradition, the state of extinction, fana, is followed by a station of subsistence, baqa — a return of consciousness to a self now transfigured by the knowledge impressed upon it in the moment of extinction. For it is only in the state of fana that the ultimate vision of absolute oneness is attained, a vision in which there is no room for the duality that individuality always drags along with it; and from that vision one comes back, no longer the same. Al-Ghazali describes what happens at the extreme of this state in unforgettable terms. Those who verify the divine oneness through direct mystical taste, he writes, become drowned in oneness, so utterly dumbfounded that no capacity remains within them save to recall God — not even the capacity to recall their own selves. This condition, in relation to the one who undergoes it, is called extinction, or rather the extinction of extinction: for the soul has become extinct to itself, and extinct even to its own extinction; it is unconscious of itself, and unconscious of its own unconsciousness — since, were it conscious of its unconsciousness, it would still be conscious of itself. The self does not merely fall silent; it falls silent about its own silence. There is a hard saying preserved from the early master Abu 'Uthman that names the price of this and its reward together: whoever has not tasted the loneliness of unconsciousness, he says, will not find the food of the intimacy of remembrance. Losing consciousness of oneself is a kind of loneliness, a stripping-away of the familiar company one keeps with one's own self; and only through that loneliness does one arrive at the true intimacy with the divine Reality that the remembrance is for. One cannot have both the old self-satisfied intimacy — the habitual, comfortable self-regard that is the shadow cast by self-awareness — and the intimacy of God at once. The first must be surrendered for the second. This is exactly the self-awareness the Imam says must be forgotten, and the higher forgetting can rest on nothing less than the remembrance of God, since only the source of Being itself is strong enough to eclipse the relative existence and partial consciousness of the individual.

The tradition maps this ascent through graded degrees of the remembrance itself, each deeper than the last. There is the remembrance of the tongue; then the remembrance of the heart; then the remembrance of the innermost secret; then that of the spirit; then that of the hidden; and finally the remembrance of the Essence, which is essential vision, attained only through the removal of duality altogether. The whole movement is a progressive descent inward, from the lips toward the ground of the soul, until nothing stands between the rememberer and the Remembered — because the rememberer, as a separate self, is no longer there. And yet, precisely because this final degree cannot be forced, the master Ibn 'Ata'illah counsels the seeker never to give up the practice for failing to feel God's presence in it. Do not abandon the invocation, he says, because you do not feel the Presence of God in it — for your forgetfulness of the invocation is far worse than your forgetfulness in the invocation. Perhaps God will carry you from an invocation clouded with heedlessness to one with vigilance, and from vigilance to the Presence of God, and from the Presence of God to a state in which everything but the Invoked is absent. The seeker's task is only to keep invoking; the passage through the degrees is God's to give.

And this is why one further saying of the Imam is no mere flourish of rhetoric but an account of a real state. God, he says, has a drink for His friends. When they drink it, they are intoxicated; and when they are intoxicated, they are enraptured; and when they are enraptured, they are blessed; and when they are blessed, they dissolve; and when they dissolve, they are free; and when they are free, they devote themselves purely; and when they devote themselves purely, they seek; and when they seek, they find; and when they find, they arrive; and when they arrive, they are at one — and there is no difference between them and their Beloved. Read that final clause with care, for it is the destination of the whole path this episode has traced. The chain runs from the first sip of intoxication, through the dissolving of the self, to a state in which no difference remains between the friend of God and the Beloved. This is not the union of two things becoming one, which would still leave two; it is, as the tradition insists, the realization of oneness — the disclosure that there was never, at the deepest level, a genuine second at all. And it is the fruit of the remembrance, the most potent of all the drinks that intoxicate, if prayer may be called a drink. It leads one, inevitably, to ask the last and strangest question: who, in the end, is the one who truly invokes?

## God the true invoker

The Imam answers that question with a sentence that turns the whole practice of remembrance inside out. The invocation, he says, is not a formality of speech nor a way of thinking; rather, it comes forth firstly from the Invoked, and secondly from the invoker. Read that again. The remembrance originates first from the One who is remembered, and only second from the one who remembers. It is God Himself who performs the invocation, first and foremost; a person's own invocation is but a shadow of it, a reflection, a consequence. This resolves the paradox of self-forgetting from the far side. If, at the summit, it is not really the individual who invokes but God who invokes through him, then the extinction of the individual is not the failure of the remembrance but its truth coming to light — the passing-away of an agent who was never, at the deepest level, the real agent at all. What looked like a person's invocation of God is unmasked as God's invocation, in which the person was only ever a shadow cast by the light.

Weigh the force of the Imam's phrasing. The invocation, he says, is not a formality of speech nor a way of thinking. It is not, in its deepest reality, a human utterance or a human mental act at all — not something a person produces out of his own resources and then directs toward God. Its first source is the Invoked, and only its second source is the invoker. This inverts the whole natural picture of prayer, in which a creature, standing at a distance, reaches across a gap toward a God who may or may not answer. On the Imam's account the movement runs the other way first: God's own reality is the origin of the remembrance, and the human invocation is the reflection that reality throws back into the mirror of a heart. When at last the mirror is perfectly clear — the self forgotten, the rust of egoity rubbed away — nothing of the reflecting surface intrudes, and what is seen in it is simply the light itself. The invocation was God's from the beginning; the person only had to stop obstructing it.

Once remembrance is seen this way, its meaning opens far beyond anything the individual does with tongue or mind. It reaches all the way down into the act of creation itself. God's creative Word — Be — which calls the possibilities of being into existence, can be understood as a form of remembrance: God utters the name of the possible thing, calling it out of nothingness into being. And that very passage from nonexistence to existence is what makes the thing remembered. The Qur'an poses the question exactly in these terms: has there come upon man any moment in time when he was not a thing remembered (chapter 76, verse 1)? To be a thing remembered is to have been called into being; to be not remembered is to be nothing, not yet summoned from the hiddenness that precedes all existence. Before he exists, the human being is known, but known only by God. So God's remembrance of man precedes man's remembrance of God — not merely in time but in the order of being itself. And this gives yet another sense to the verse this whole book keeps returning to, that the remembrance of God is greater: God's remembrance of us is infinitely greater than our remembrance of Him, for His remembrance is what gives us our very being, while ours is only the shadow-answer of a creature to its Creator.

This throws a new and clarifying light back on the extinction the last movement described. There are, the mystics say, two movements that mirror each other. The first is the descent of creation, the movement from God to man: to bring a thing into being, God negates the relative nothingness that was pregnant with its possibility — He calls it forth, so that what was merely possible becomes actual. The second is the ascent of realization, the movement from man back to God: and here the very same nothingness that was breathed into being must be negated again, so that what was called forth returns to its source. This is why extinction is not a fall into the void but a homecoming. What is extinguished, in the words of Ibn al-'Arabi's treatise, is that which never truly was — the creature's illusory self-standing existence — while what subsists is that which never ceased to be, the divine reality that was always its ground. The Qur'an says it plainly: everything upon the earth perishes, and there subsists only the Face of your Lord, possessor of glory and bounty (chapter 55, verses 26 and 27). Al-Ghazali, commenting on the kindred verse that everything perishes except His Face (chapter 28, verse 88), insists that this perishing is not something that happens at some future moment but is true forever: every thing, considered in and by itself, apart from God, is pure nothingness even now, and has existence only from the face turned toward the One who gives it being. The divine Face is the sole reality there is. To be extinguished, then, is simply to stop mistaking one's own nothingness for a second reality beside God — and to find, on the far side of that letting-go, that only the Face remains, and that it was there all along.

Here the reciprocity at the heart of remembrance shows its true depth. Remember Me, the Qur'an says, and I will remember you (chapter 2, verse 152). On the surface this reads as a simple exchange: turn to God and He turns to you. But in the light of everything the Imam has said, the order of the two remembrances is not level. God's remembrance of the human being is the prior, engendering reality — the very act by which the person is held in existence — and the human being's remembrance of God is the answering movement it makes possible, the shadow that follows the light. When a person invokes God, the Imam says, God enlivens his heart and illuminates his inmost substance; the Qur'an describes how those who invoke much are blessed by God and by His angels, who send their blessing upon the believers so as to bring them out of darkness and into light. There is a genuine reciprocity here, but it is not a reciprocity between equals. The human being remembers, and God blesses; the human being invokes, and God illuminates. Yet the divine side of the exchange is of another order altogether from the human side — for God's blessing is what brings a person from darkness into light, and that light is not a reward added onto an act the person completed by himself but the very thing that made the act possible in the first place. The response, in a sense, comes before the call, since the capacity to call at all was itself the first gift. This is the mystery folded into the verse: remember Me, and I will remember you. It sounds like an even trade, but the two remembrances are as unequal as a source and its reflection, a sun and the light it throws. To remember God, then, is finally to be caught up into a remembrance that was already underway before one began: God remembering Himself, and holding the whole of creation in being, through the very creature who imagines he is the one doing the remembering. The self-forgetting the whole episode has traced is, seen from this height, only the moment when the creature stops imagining that and lets the true remembrance shine through unobstructed.

## What this episode lands

This episode has followed remembrance across the gap that divides the human from the divine. Perfect remembrance — the bringing of heart and tongue into harmony, the achievement of total sincerity through the long struggle against the ego — is real and necessary, but it is not the summit; in it the self still stands at the center, the agent of its own devotion. Beyond it lies the reality of remembrance, in which the self is forgotten and the center of consciousness passes from the one who invokes to the One invoked. That forgetting is the mystical extinction, fana, which no one can accomplish by willing it, since to will one's own unconsciousness is only to be more conscious; it comes, where it comes, as a grace attracted by total absorption in the Name — the state the Imam knew in his own fainting in the orchard, the state named in his saying about the drink of God's friends, in which at last no difference remains between the lover and the Beloved. And extinction is not the end of the road but a passage: through it comes the station of subsistence, a return to a self remade by the vision granted only where the self was not, so that the one who comes back is no longer the one who went. And beneath all of it lies the deepest truth of all: that the invocation comes first from the Invoked and only second from the invoker, that God's remembrance of the creature precedes and grounds the creature's remembrance of God, so that the self's extinction is not the ruin of remembrance but its reality laid bare. What began as a discipline a person takes up ends as a light a person stops blocking — the same remembrance throughout, seen first from the near side and now from the far.

## Closing

Yet to say that God is the true invoker, and that the Name is His remembrance of the thing He calls into being, presses a question that has hovered at the edge of this whole discussion and can no longer be deferred. What exactly is the relation between the Name a person invokes and the God who is named by it? Is the Name simply a word, a human label pointing at a reality beyond it — or is there some deeper identity between the Name and the Named, such that to invoke the Name is already to touch the reality? The mystics answered with a formula as bold as any in the tradition: the Name is the Named. And yet the Imams themselves are recorded denying exactly that, insisting that the Name of God is other than God. The next episode takes up that apparent contradiction, and follows it into the most astonishing claim of all — that the friends of God, effaced in Him, become themselves the most beautiful Names.
