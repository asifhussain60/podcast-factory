This episode turns from justice to its most hidden enemy: the inner attitude the Imam names as war with God. We follow Ali (ع)'s stern warning to Malik al-Ashtar against pride — the vice that usurps a greatness belonging to God alone, that corrupts the heart before it corrupts religion, and that carries within itself the seed of its own humiliation. We trace it from the cloak of divine majesty, through the disgrace of Iblis and the hidden idolatry of vanity, to the slow, quiet ruin of the arrogant soul.

## Where this episode picks up

How can an inner attitude — a mere feeling of superiority, a way of carrying oneself in front of others — be described, without any exaggeration, as war against God? It sounds like a category mistake. War is something you wage against an enemy you can see, with armies and weapons; God is neither an equal nor a rival to be fought. And yet this is exactly the language the Imam reaches for when he turns from the duties a ruler owes his people to the state of the ruler's own soul. "Do not set your soul up for war with God," he tells Malik — and the phrase is arresting precisely because it names something invisible, something interior, and treats it as an act of open hostility toward the Absolute.

Having watched, in the letter's earlier counsels, how justice is not merely a policy a ruler adopts but a reflection of God's own nature — how putting each thing in its proper place is a participation in the way reality itself is ordered — we are now led to look at the reverse. If justice aligns the soul with the real, there must be a disposition that sets the soul against the real, and the Imam locates it with great precision. It is not, first of all, the gross violations he catalogued at the letter's outset — the many breaches of religious obligation that are the obvious meaning of "fighting" against God. Those are the visible surface. The allusion here reaches deeper, into subtler modes of opposition to divine reality: attitudes that can accompany even outward acts of obedience and yet quietly contradict their very spirit.

Chief among them stands the cardinal vice against which the Imam warns again and again: pride. Not pride as a passing mood, but pride as a settled psychic state — the condition in which a person feels himself superior and behaves highhandedly toward those around him, its signs so legible in his conduct that they cannot be concealed. For a person invested with power, this is not a small moral blemish among others. It is, as we are about to see, a misappropriation of something that belongs to God alone, an arrogation of a greatness no creature may claim — and for that reason a covert declaration of war against the one from whom all greatness derives. What follows is an anatomy of that war: how pride steals a divine attribute, how it took its first and archetypal form in the refusal of Iblis, and how it works its slow ruin in the soul of anyone entrusted with authority over others.

## The cloak of pride

Begin with the warning itself, and with the strangeness of it. "Do not set your soul up for war with God." One might well ask what this means, and what kinds of actions or attitudes could possibly place a soul in a posture of combat against its own Creator. The most obvious answer is disobedience — the breaking of commands, the many violations of religious obligation that the Imam had already named. That is real, and it is the plainest sense in which a person "fights" against God. But the Imam's words seem to reach past it, toward something subtler and more dangerous, because it can hide inside a life that looks, on its surface, obedient and correct. There are attitudes that may accompany outward acts of piety and yet contradict their very spirit — and the first of these, the one the Imam returns to more often than any other, is pride.

The word he warns against is takabbur, sometimes rendered kibr: the state in which a person feels superior to others and carries himself highhandedly among them. It is worth pausing on how visible this vice is. Pride is not a secret of the heart that leaves no trace; its marks show in a man's bearing, his tone, the way he receives those beneath him and the way he answers those who question him. The proud man advertises himself whether he means to or not. But the Imam is not finally interested in pride as a social unpleasantness, an unattractive manner. He is interested in what pride is, beneath its symptoms — and what it is, when the one who is proud holds power, is theft. A ruler's pride is a misappropriation of a divine attribute, an arrogation to himself of a greatness that belongs exclusively to God.

To see why this is theft and not merely arrogance, listen to the divine word the tradition places at the center of this teaching: "Pride is My cloak and greatness is My robe; whoever contends with Me over either of them, I shall cast him into the Fire." The image is exact and worth dwelling on. A cloak and a robe are what a king wears to signify his majesty; they are not shared, not lent out, not worn by a subject in the king's own hall. Here the cloak of pride and the robe of greatness are said to be God's own regalia — the outward signs of a sovereignty that is His and His alone. To take up pride, then, is not simply to think too well of oneself. It is to reach for a garment that belongs to God and to drape it over one's own shoulders, and the warning attached to that act is not a mild rebuke but the Fire. The person who contends with God over pride is described as a rival contending for a throne.

This is why the tradition draws so sharp a line around the word itself. God alone has the right to takabbur, and God alone may be called, without any offence, al-Mutakabbir — the Proud, the one whose greatness is real because it is underived and absolute. When the name is spoken of God it is pure praise; there is nothing in Him that greatness could distort, because His greatness is simply the truth of what He is. But the very same disposition, transferred to a creature, becomes the worst of vices, because a creature has no greatness of its own to be proud of. Everything he has, he has received; every faculty by which he might exalt himself was given to him. For such a being to vaunt himself is to lay claim to a self-sufficiency he does not possess, to behave as though the borrowed were his own. The name that is perfection in God is corruption in man, and the difference is the whole difference between the Creator and the created.

It is worth staying with that difference a moment longer, because the whole warning depends on it. The proud man's error is not that greatness is a bad thing to possess; it is that he ascribes to himself a greatness he does not, and cannot, possess. Consider what a ruler actually holds. His power was entrusted to him and can be withdrawn; his strength was lent and will fail him; his very existence is held in being, from one instant to the next, by the one who fashioned him. Nothing in this inventory is his own. To be proud of any of it is to mistake a loan for a possession, a gift for an achievement — and to forget the giver in the act of admiring the gift. This is why pride, at its root, is a lie about reality before it is a fault of character. The proud man is telling himself, and displaying to others, a false account of where his greatness comes from, and it is the falseness, quite as much as the arrogance, that sets him against the truth of God.

From here the logic of "war" becomes clear. To vaunt oneself before others is tantamount to inwardly defying God's authority; it amounts to an implicit denial of His unique and incomparable majesty. Think of how a subject relates to a king. A loyal subject acknowledges, in his very bearing, that the greatness in the room is not his; he defers, he lowers himself, he keeps his proper place. But a subject who openly exalts himself, who behaves as though the majesty were his to display, is by that very posture refusing the king's supremacy — and a subject who refuses his king's supremacy is, in effect, in rebellion, waging war against him. Pride does exactly this to God. It is a denial, carried in the body and the manner, of a truth that ought to govern every creature: that there is only one who is truly great, and that the proud man is not He. Enmity toward transcendent reality is the invisible substance that pervades all pride, and it is present in every act of arrogance or oppression a ruler commits against those beneath him.

And here is the paradox that only a sense of spiritual values can illuminate, for it turns the ordinary picture of pride inside out. We tend to think of arrogance as something aimed downward — the strong man's contempt for the weak, the ruler's disdain for the ruled, a sin committed against those "below." But an attitude of superiority toward those below is, in fact, an act of rebellion against that which is above. The injustice a proud ruler does to the people beneath him is not merely a horizontal wrong, a crime against fellow creatures that will one day draw down upon him the justice of the Creator. It is that, but it is also more: in and of itself it manifests outright opposition to the Creator. The contempt that seems to point downward is really pointing upward, at God. What looks like a horizontal vice — belonging to this world, played out between a man and his neighbors — is in reality a spiritual disorder, a fracture in the soul's relation to the Absolute.

This is why the Imam can treat the outward immorality of corruption as a mere symptom. The oppression, the highhandedness, the abuse of the weak — these are the visible eruptions of an inner spiritual malaise that lies deeper than any of them: an implicit denial of the absoluteness, and hence the uniqueness, of the source of all power and authority. The corrupt ruler has forgotten something, and his corruption is the shape his forgetting takes. What he has forgotten is the truth voiced in the Qur'anic question and answer: "To whom belongeth the dominion this day? To God, the One, the All-Conquering" (chapter 40, verse 16). Dominion belongs to God — not tomorrow only, not at the reckoning only, but this day. And the tradition insists that "this day" is to be heard as every day, not merely the Day of Judgement. The verse is not a report about a future scene; it is a truth that should resound constantly in the ears of every ruler, in the very hour he is tempted to imagine that the dominion, for the moment, is his. The proud man's whole error is contained in that single lapse of memory. He behaves as though the sovereignty of this day were his to wield, when the sovereignty of this day, and of every day, belongs to God alone.

## Iblis and hidden idolatry

If pride is the misappropriation of a divine attribute, then the tradition has a name for the being who committed that theft first and most completely, and whose refusal became the archetype of every arrogance since. The Imam devotes to him one of his most searching sermons, known as al-Qasi'a — "the Abasement," so named because its whole purpose is to humiliate the proud, to bring the arrogant soul low by showing it the fate of the first arrogant one. That figure is Iblis, Satan, described as the first of those who competed with God in grandeur, and thus the very embodiment of the principle of pride. Every later act of arrogance is, in a sense, a repetition of his; he is the pattern, and the sermon holds him up so that the listener may recognize the same disease at work, in miniature, in himself.

The scene of his fall is recounted in the Qur'an, and it turns entirely on a refusal to bow. "When your Lord said to the angels: Truly I am going to create man from clay; and when I have fashioned him and breathed into him of My spirit, then fall down before him prostrate. So the angels prostrated themselves, all of them together; save Iblis: he was proud and was one of the disbelievers" (chapter 38, verses 71 to 74). Notice how the whole company of angels obeys as one — "all of them together" — and how a single exception breaks the harmony. The command was to prostrate before the newly fashioned man, into whom God had breathed of His own spirit; the prostration was, in truth, a recognition of what God had placed in Adam. And Iblis would not. The reason the tradition gives is that a vehement passion overcame him — that he deemed himself superior to Adam on a ground that seemed, to him, self-evident: he had been created from fire, and Adam merely from clay. Fire, he reasoned, is nobler than clay; therefore he was nobler than the one he was told to honor; therefore he would not bow.

This is the exact anatomy of pride, laid bare in a single refusal. Iblis took the fact of a difference — fire against clay — and converted it into a claim of superiority, and converted that claim into a defiance of God's own command. Taking pride in a presumed excellence, and rebelling against God on the strength of it, he became not only the first enemy of God but, as the Imam names him, the leader of the bigots and the forerunner of the proud. Every proud soul that has come after walks in a procession he leads. And because this is so, the Imam turns to his listeners with an urgent, practical warning: beware lest Iblis infect you with his disease. The disease spreads by insinuation, by whispering, by machination — it does not announce itself as pride but slips in disguised as self-regard, as wounded dignity, as the reasonable sense that one deserves better. The Imam's counsel against it is vividly physical. Be resolute, he says, in placing self-abasement over your heads, in casting self-glorification beneath your feet, in removing pride from your necks; and take up humility as the fortified watchtower between you and your enemy.

That last image repays attention, because it tells us what humility actually is in this contest. It is not a mood of self-deprecation, a habit of thinking poorly of oneself. It is a defensive position — a watchtower, a fortress — the one ground on which the assault of Iblis cannot land. For the whole aim of the enemy is, inversely, to inculcate pride, since pride is not merely the worst of vices but the poison that infects and destroys every other virtue. This is the point that makes humility indispensable rather than optional. A person may possess real virtues — courage, generosity, learning, devotion — and pride will convert each of them into a vice the moment it attaches itself to them, because a virtue one is proud of has already been turned into an occasion for self-exaltation, which is the very thing the virtue was meant to overcome. Hence the startling measure the tradition offers: "The sin that grieves you is better, in the sight of God, than the virtue that makes you proud." A sin that leaves you humbled has kept you in the right relation to God; a virtue that puffs you up has quietly reversed it. This is also why excessive praise is so dangerous, and the conceit it breeds so ruinous — such praise is among Satan's most reliable opportunities to efface the virtue of one who is genuinely virtuous.

Taken together, these teachings amount to a commentary on the disgrace of Iblis, and they open onto a danger the Prophet named with unforgettable precision: the danger of falling prey to a hidden form of idolatry. "The creeping of shirk in my community," he warned, "is more hidden than the creeping of a black ant over a hard rock on a dark night." Every detail of that image works to make the point. A black ant is already hard to see; a black ant on a dark rock, harder; a black ant on a dark rock on a moonless night, all but invisible — and the movement of shirk within the soul is more hidden still. This forces a deepening of what we usually mean by the word. The superficial notion of shirk is the worship of material idols — call it shirk al-jali, the open and manifest form, the bowing to statues that the tradition condemns without difficulty. But that is not the shirk the Prophet is warning his own community about, for they had already renounced idols. He means shirk al-khafi, the hidden form: false gods concealed not in temples but within one's own intentions, attitudes, and orientations — the whole apparatus of hidden pride, vanity, ostentation, and pretension.

The Imam makes the connection unmistakable: "Know that the slightest pretension is polytheism." Pretension here is riya — acting so as to be seen, performing one's virtue for the eyes of others. And the reason it counts as a form of polytheism, of associating partners with God, is exact rather than rhetorical. To act for the sake of earning others' praise is to act for others rather than for God; it is to set up, in the place that belongs to God alone, an audience whose approval one is really seeking. The deed may look devout, but its true object is not the divine; it is the admiring gaze of one's fellows, and that gaze has become a rival god. The consequence is stated with a terrible symmetry: "Whoso acts for other than God, God entrusts him to those for whose sake he acted." The man who worked for human praise is handed over to the humans he worked for — and left, in the end, with exactly what he sought and nothing more.

What makes this form of idolatry so treacherous is precisely its hiddenness. The worshipper of a stone idol at least knows what he is doing; the object of his devotion stands plainly before him. But the one who has slipped into riya may believe himself wholly devoted to God, may perform every outward act with scrupulous care, and never once suspect that the true recipient of his worship is his own reputation. This is the black ant on the dark rock in the moonless night — a shift of the heart's intention so slight, so silent, that it can pass a lifetime undetected beneath a surface of unbroken piety. It is for exactly this reason that the Prophet warned his own community, who had already turned from idols, and not some distant nation of idolaters. The danger he named was not out there in the temples; it was in here, in the folds of a believing heart, where a false god can hide behind the very acts meant to honor the true one.

Read in this light, the disobedience of Iblis discloses its full meaning. It was the outward moral consequence of an inner spiritual malaise, and the malaise was precisely the pride that amounts to hidden polytheism — the exalting of the self into a god beside God. And this is why the Imam presses one final point, the one the sermon of Abasement is named for. Iblis is disgraced because he disobeyed God, and he disobeyed God on account of pride; therefore pride is, in and of itself, a disgrace. It is a state of dis-grace in the literal sense — a fall out of grace, a perversion of the human condition that carries within it the seed of its own inevitable inversion. "Do you not see how God humiliated him through his pride?" the Imam asks. The proud one imagines he is exalting himself; in reality he is bringing himself low, for the very act of self-exaltation is what cast him down. Being proud is already, in truth, a form of humiliation, because self-aggrandizement is nothing other than self-destruction dressed up as glory.

## Arrogance and slow ruin

Return now from the archetype to the ordinary case: the ruler on the slope toward oppression, and the small sentence he tells himself as he begins to slide. The Imam reproduces it with uncanny accuracy, catching the very train of thought that sets a man in motion toward tyranny: "Do not say, 'I have been given authority, I order and am obeyed,' for this leads to corruption in the heart and the erosion of religion." It is a small, almost reasonable-sounding thought — I hold the office, I give the command, the command is carried out. But the Imam treats it as the first step onto the incline, because in it the ruler has begun to locate the source of his authority in himself, in the bare fact that his orders are obeyed, rather than in the God from whom all authority is on loan. The self-glorification is subtle, and that is exactly what makes it dangerous.

But the detail most worth pausing on is the order in which the Imam says the damage arrives. This self-glorification is seen as producing first an inner, spiritual corruption — the corruption of the heart — and only then the erosion of religion. The sequence is not incidental; it is the whole diagnosis. The rot begins on the inside, in the ruler's relation to God, and works outward to the practice of his faith, not the other way around. The outward prescriptions of religion, the Imam is telling us, require the right inward attitude if they are to be applied with integrity and held to with any stability. His idea might be put very simply: when power goes to the head, faith departs from the heart; and conversely, for faith to be manifested truly in the world, it must flow from a heart suffused with humility. This too is an application of the principle we have seen running through the whole letter — putting each thing in its proper place — for a heart in its proper place is a heart that knows it is not the source of its own greatness.

What, then, is the remedy the moment vanity begins to rise? The Imam prescribes a deliberate act of attention: "If the authority of your position engenders vanity and arrogance, then look at the grandeur of God's dominion above you — this will calm your ambition, restrain your vehemence, and restore to you what had strayed from your intellect." The last phrase is the key that unlocks the rest. Something in the intellect strays when vanity takes hold, and something is restored when the ruler lifts his eyes to the grandeur above him. What strays is precisely the part of the intellect that is conscious of God's absolute reality — and of the relative, derivative, ultimately illusory character of everything else, the ruler's own power included. Arrogance, in other words, is not merely a failure of humility; it is a failure of intelligence, a darkening of the very faculty by which the soul perceives what is truly real.

The tradition even preserves this in the shape of the words. The term the Imam uses for arrogance, makhila, springs from the same root as the word for imagination, khayal — and the kinship is not accidental but revealing. Arrogance belongs to the realm of the imaginary, the fanciful, the unreal: it is what happens when the imagination fastens upon something that is not truly there and swells with it. When imagination seizes on the unreal and inflates itself with pride, it becomes makhila, arrogance. So arrogant pride is at bottom an intense form of self-delusion rooted in an intellectual defect. It shows itself as a vice, a moral failing, but its cause runs deeper than the moral level, for it involves not merely an overestimation of oneself but, more fundamentally, an underestimation of the Absolute. The arrogant man overrates himself only because he has first underrated God; the swelling of the self is the exact measure of how small the Real has grown in his sight. His egotism is an intensification of the ordinary egocentricity that clings to anyone who has not yet realized the sole reality of God and the ultimately illusory nature of everything else.

This is why the sage — and the sage alone — is free of it, for the sage has genuinely assimilated the truths the tyrant has forgotten. "Everything is dying away except His countenance" (chapter 28, verse 88). "Everything that is thereon is perishing, and there subsisteth but the Face of thy Lord, possessor of Glory and Bounty" (chapter 55, verses 26 to 27). "The Truth hath come and falsehood hath passed away; indeed, falsehood is ever passing away" (chapter 17, verse 81). To take these words into oneself is to see the whole created order, and one's own self within it, as passing, contingent, on the way to dissolution — and to see that only one Face endures. From such a vision, pride simply cannot be drawn, because there is nothing in a perishing self to be proud of; only the humility that dissolves all individualism and egocentricity can be drawn from it. And humility, so grounded, is the antidote to the very roots of pride, vanity, and arrogance — which are, in their turn, the lifeblood of every tyranny.

With this the Imam's most direct warning falls into place: "Beware of comparing yourself with God in greatness and likening yourself to Him in might, for God abases every tyrant and disgraces every braggart." The tyrant sets himself up as a rival in majesty, and the outcome is fixed by the nature of things: God abases him, God disgraces him, because a creature who reaches for the incomparable can only be broken by the reach. And there is a further and graver consequence. The one who fails to act justly toward God and toward God's creatures finds that not only the creatures but God Himself becomes his adversary. When a man wrongs the servants of God, God stands as his opponent in place of those servants; and the one whom God opposes has his every argument struck down. Such a man, the Imam says, remains at war with God until he desists and repents. The war we began with is now revealed in its full duration: it is not a single act but a standing condition, a state of hostility that persists as long as the injustice persists, and that ends only when the tyrant lays down his pride. He may be compelled to that surrender only at the last, at the very edge of death and judgement — but come to it he must, and if he seems to have escaped the reckoning in this world, he will not escape it in the next.

Yet the deepest teaching in this section concerns how the war is waged before repentance or judgement overtakes the tyrant — how, that is, his punishment is already underway while he imagines himself untouched. The tradition names this the doctrine of istidraj, the drawing out of punishment by degrees. The word comes from a root meaning stages or steps, the same sense in which one speaks of proceeding step by step — and it describes a punishment that does not fall all at once but is administered gradually, stage upon stage, often without the one being punished perceiving it at all. The Qur'an sets it in stark contrast: "Of those We have created are people who guide with truth and dispense justice therewith. But those who reject Our signs, We will lead them on by degrees to punishment from whence they know not" (chapter 7, verses 181 to 182). Between the just, who guide by truth, and those who reject the signs, the difference of fate is drawn — and the fate of the rejecters is to be led onward, by imperceptible degrees, toward a ruin they never see approaching.

Here the teaching reaches its most unsettling point, the one that overturns every worldly measure of how things stand. The punishment meted out by degrees can take the form of apparent worldly success. "Let not their wealth nor their children please thee. God desireth only to punish them thereby in the world, that their souls shall pass away while they are disbelievers" (chapter 9, verse 85). The wealth that grows, the family that flourishes, the power that consolidates — these need not be signs of favor at all. They may be the very instrument of a punishment drawn out by stages, a prosperity that hardens the soul in its rejection until it passes away still turned from God. What looks like the tyrant's triumph may be the slow closing of a trap. This is why the tradition can say, without contradiction, that a tyrant or oppressor may be a Muslim in the formal sense — bearing the name, performing the outward acts — and yet be simultaneously guilty of a mode of disbelief. The word for it is kafir, taken in its strict, etymological sense: not merely one who denies a creed, but one who covers over the truth — covers it over both in his own being and in his actions, burying under his arrogance the reality he was made to acknowledge. And so we arrive back at the contrast the Qur'an draws between those who dispense justice in accordance with the truth and those who reject God's signs: the just on the one side, and on the other, precisely, the oppressors and the tyrants.

## What this episode lands

The listener now holds a single, unbroken thread. Pride is war with God, and not by metaphor: it usurps a divine attribute — the cloak of greatness that belongs to God alone — and in doing so it denies, in the very posture of the soul, the sole and incomparable reality of God. What looks like contempt aimed downward at the weak is really rebellion aimed upward at the Absolute; what looks like a social vice is a spiritual disorder; what looks like the tyrant's triumph may be his punishment drawn out by degrees.

We have watched this diagnosis move through three registers. In the divine word, pride is a garment stolen from God, so that only God may be called the Proud, and the creature who claims the name declares war. In the disgrace of Iblis, pride is shown to be its own humiliation — self-aggrandizement that is really self-destruction, a hidden idolatry in which the self, or the praise of others, is set up as a god beside God. And in the ruler on the slope toward oppression, pride is shown to corrupt the heart first and religion afterward, to darken the very intelligence that perceives the Real, and to work a slow ruin that can wear the mask of worldly success. The Imam's remedy through all of it is the same: humility, grounded in the sight of a grandeur above and a world that is passing away, held like a fortified watchtower against the enemy.

From here the letter follows the war inward, past the ruler's relation to God and his people, to something more intimate still — the war a man wages against his own conscience, and the inner voice the tyrant must silence before he can do any of this at all.

## Closing

For everything we have traced about pride points, in the end, toward a smaller and more private battlefield than the throne room or the field of oppression. Before a ruler can set his soul against God, and before he can wrong the people beneath him, something must first be overruled within — a voice that knows better, that recognizes the injustice for what it is even as the will proceeds to commit it. The tradition calls this the conscience, and it is the inner witness that pride most needs to quiet, because as long as it speaks, the soul cannot rest easy in its arrogance. The tyrant does not usually begin by disbelieving that he does wrong; he begins by silencing the part of himself that says so.

That silencing is the next movement of the letter, and it turns the whole subject of justice inward. We have heard the Imam insist that a ruler act with justice toward God and toward his people; we are about to hear him insist that justice begins closer to home still — with the reforming of oneself, with a man rendering to his own conscience the honesty he owes it before he can render anything true to anyone else. The war with God, it turns out, is first fought and either won or lost in that quiet interior court, where a person is alone with what he knows. That is where we turn next: from pride as war with the Most High to the war a man wages against the truth within his own breast.
