This episode opens the second half of the series and turns from the person of Ali (ع) to a single document he left behind: his letter to Malik al-Ashtar, written as he sent a trusted governor out to Egypt fourteen centuries ago. Landing here mid-series, you need carry only one thread forward — that in Ali (ع)'s vision the whole of the moral life flows from the nature of God — because this letter is where that lofty vision meets the hard, practical business of ruling other people, and either survives the collision or is exposed as mere sentiment.

## Where this episode opens

What if justice were not a rule we impose on the world from outside, but the very nature of God Himself? In ordinary speech the word has shrunk to something almost administrative — fairness, equality, fair play, the even splitting of a disputed thing, the impartial ruling of a court. It is a serviceable meaning, and an entirely formal one. The conception at work in Ali (ع)'s letter to Malik al-Ashtar is of an altogether different order. There, justice is aligned with the sacred, with the All-Holy, and its roots are located not in human convention but within the divine nature itself. To be just, on this reading, is not merely to behave well toward others; it is to conform, as far as a creature can, to the way things truly are in God — and therefore to become, in some measure, like God.

That is the provocation this episode asks you to sit with, and it changes everything downstream. If justice is only fairness, then a ruler who keeps the rules and balances the books has discharged his whole duty. But if justice is the nature of the Real, then keeping the rules is the least of it: the real work is interior, a matter of putting one's own soul in its right place before God so that right action can flow out of it. This is why the letter that occupies the rest of this episode is as much a work of spirituality as of politics — and why we cannot read a line of it well until the small, formal meaning of justice has been set aside.

The document itself is famous. It is the epistle Ali (ع) wrote when he appointed Malik al-Ashtar his governor of Egypt, and across the centuries it has been read as something close to an ideal constitution — a detailed account of the duties and rights of the ruler, the officers of the state, and the main classes of society. Yet its counsel reaches far beyond anything a political or legal text would attempt. Much of what it says pertains to universally applicable ethical principles, as relevant to the governed as to those who govern; it is a statement of ethics quite as much as of politics. Before we can enter it, though, we have to undo the shrunken meaning of justice that we all carry without thinking — and see why, for Ali (ع), fairness is only the outermost shell of something sacred.

## Justice beyond fairness

Begin with the word as we normally use it. In everyday speech, justice means little more than fairness, equality, and fair play — a serviceable but essentially formal account. It tells us how to divide a thing evenly, or how to treat like cases alike, but it says nothing about why we should care, or what the demand of justice is grounded in. It is, to borrow the essay's own image, a body without a spirit. And the moment we press it — the moment we ask what animates the demand, what makes it binding rather than merely conventional — the ordinary meaning falls silent. It can tell us the shape of a just act; it cannot tell us why justice has any claim on us at all.

This silence is not an accident. It is the symptom of a much larger drift, and to see that drift clearly the essay turns to one of the most serious critiques of modern and postmodern ethics, that of the philosopher Alasdair MacIntyre. Put simply, MacIntyre argues that for several centuries ethical discourse in Western society has drifted far from its moorings in absolute values. Debates about morality, and the philosophies built to support them, are no longer grounded in any conception of 'the good life' or of 'the Good' as such. Cut loose from that anchor, ethics is left to improvise — and MacIntyre names four shapes the improvisation keeps taking, four default positions that step in wherever the Good has been abandoned.

The first is relativism, which holds that knowledge, truth, and morality are not absolute but relative — existing only in relation to a particular culture, a society, or a moment in history, so that what is right here is not right there, and no standard ever rises above the local. The second is nihilism, which rejects all religious and moral principles outright and takes life to be essentially meaningless, so that in the end there is nothing left for an ethics even to be about. The third is utilitarianism, which counts an action right only insofar as it is useful or benefits a majority, so that good and bad reduce to a calculation of greater benefit or loss — a sum to be tallied rather than a truth to be honoured. And the fourth is opportunism, which simply seizes whatever benefit the circumstances happen to offer, regardless of any plan or principle: the way of the manipulator, who flatters, performs, or deceives in order to get what he wants.

Whatever occasional value these human-made theories yield, none of it is rooted in the divine; and so even where they happen to mirror the values of faith, they are, in the essay's stark image, baseless, without spirit — dead corpses. For virtue practised on an ad hoc basis is not really virtue at all. It is a series of pragmatic adaptations to shifting circumstance in the absence of any genuine principle of virtue — the outward appearance of goodness with nothing beneath it holding it up. A person may do the right thing a hundred times running and still not be virtuous, if each right thing was merely the convenient thing that a hundred separate situations happened to call for.

The example the essay reaches for is truthfulness, and it repays close attention, because it shows the whole argument in miniature. Suppose a person resolves always to speak the truth, but never grasps the underlying reason for it — never sees that truth is the very nature of our Creator, al-Haqq, the absolutely Real, and that our purpose in this world is to take on the virtues of that Creator. What becomes of such a resolution under pressure? It turns situational and self-serving. Truth gets spoken when it profits and abandoned when it costs; it is compromised the instant circumstances seem to warrant it, because nothing anchors it beyond the person's own convenience. But once truth is seen as the nature of the divine, everything changes. It becomes everlasting and eternal, indifferent to consequence, because one now speaks it in emulation of the immortal nature of God. The virtue itself becomes immortal, and the whole need to practise it opportunistically, case by case, simply falls away. The difference between the two truth-tellers is not in what they do — both tell the truth — but in what their truthfulness is anchored to, and therefore in what happens the day telling the truth becomes expensive.

What MacIntyre is searching for, and cannot find within the resources of a secularised ethics, is exactly the anchor that the truthfulness example points toward. 'We are looking,' he writes, 'for a conception of the good which will enable us to order other goods, for a conception of the good which will enable us to extend our understanding of the purpose and content of the virtues, for a conception of the good which will enable us to understand the place of integrity and consistency in life.' It is a remarkable admission from within modern philosophy — that without some conception of the Good above the particular goods, the virtues cannot be ordered, cannot be made consistent, cannot even be properly understood. The Good is the missing keystone, and everything the essay goes on to say about justice is an attempt to supply it from the resources of a living spiritual tradition rather than to construct it, as MacIntyre must, out of philosophy alone.

This is exactly where the insights of Ali (ع) become relevant, for the Islamic tradition supplies precisely the grounding MacIntyre finds missing. And that grounding has a definite structure. There are three components to the spiritual life, and together they form the soil out of which any real ethics can grow. The first is islam, submission, which supplies the outer aspects of morality — the actions, the observances, the rules of right conduct. The second is iman, faith, which supplies the framework of ethics — the convictions about reality within which those actions take on their meaning. And the third is the union of the two, serving as their underlying principle: it issues in ihsan, which the tradition renders as the good life, the beautiful life. Abstract questions of right and wrong come, in this scheme, to rest on a more fundamental question. The issue is no longer merely 'what is the rule?' but 'what is reality?' — and morality is set at last on a foundation deeper than itself.

The definitive statement of these three comes in a single tradition of central importance, known as the hadith of Gabriel. The Prophet was sitting among his Companions when a man approached whose appearance none of them recognised — and who was, unbeknown to them, the angel Gabriel in human form. He put three questions to the Prophet in turn. First: tell me about submission, al-islam. The Prophet answered that it is to testify that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is His messenger, to perform the prayers, to pay the poor-due, to fast in Ramadan, and to make the pilgrimage to the House for those able to do so. 'You have spoken truly,' the visitor said — and the Companions were astonished that a stranger should both question the Prophet and then confirm his answer. Then: tell me about faith, al-iman. The Prophet answered that it is to believe in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, and the Last Day, and to believe in the divine decree, both its good and its evil. 'You have spoken truly,' the visitor said again. And then the third question: tell me about virtue, al-ihsan. To this the Prophet gave the answer that governs everything to follow — 'It is to worship God as though you see Him, and if you see Him not, yet truly He sees you.'

Hold on to that definition, because it is the hinge on which the whole episode turns. As the earlier teaching on beauty as virtue already established, ihsan carries within it the sense of doing what is beautiful, virtue as the beauty of the soul; that need not be reopened here. What matters now is something the definition discloses about the very structure of ethics. The Prophet does not define virtue as a list of good deeds, nor as obedience to a code. He defines it as a mode of awareness — worshipping God as if one could see Him, and knowing that even when one cannot, one is nonetheless seen. Virtue, on this account, is what a certain quality of consciousness produces. It is the fruit of a transformed relationship to God, not a set of behaviours bolted on from the outside.

And this is the crucial point, the one that carries the argument of the whole episode. From the Prophet's definition of ihsan one sees with real clarity the relationship between worship and virtue — between spirituality and ethics, between devotion to the Creator and goodness toward His creatures. You cannot have the one without the other. Worship that does not issue in goodness is empty; goodness that is not rooted in the vision of God is baseless — one of MacIntyre's dead corpses, a right act with no living principle inside it. Ihsan is the living link between the two. This is why ihsan is not merely one virtue in a catalogue but the very ground on which ethics stands. Islam gives the acts, iman gives the framework, and their union in ihsan gives the life — a life in which right action is not imposed but flows, because the person acting has been reoriented, at the root, toward the Real. Set justice inside this frame, and its ordinary meaning is transfigured. Fairness was the shell; the sacred is the substance. To ask what justice truly is, we now have to ask what God truly is — and it is to that question the letter to Malik al-Ashtar carries us next.

## Justice as the nature of God

Return, then, to the Imam's most explicit definition of justice: justice puts everything in its right place. It sounds almost modest until you unfold it. To put everything in its right place is to give every being its rightful due — the haqq owed to each person, and indeed to each and every thing in existence. One is just precisely insofar as one renders that due; and the capacity to do so, in this demanding and universal sense, grows in proportion to one's attunement to al-Haqq, the Real. Justice, then, is not a procedure applied to disputes when they arise. It is a way of standing in true relation to everything that is — and it engages not only what one owes to others but everything that one is, for one has to be good before one can do good.

But that relation has an order, and the order is decisive. 'Be just with God,' the Imam instructs Malik, 'and be just with people, giving them their due from yourself.' Notice which comes first. Being just with God means, among other things, conforming as best one can to His nature — and everything else in the moral life derives from getting that relationship right before any other. You do not begin with fairness toward people and then reason your way up to God; you begin by putting your relationship with God in its proper place, and justice toward people flows down from there. The whole of the letter's ethics hangs on this order of precedence, and it is exactly the order the modern reduction of justice inverts, starting and ending with people alone.

And here the argument reaches its centre. What can it mean to conform to God's nature, when that nature is justice? For the Imam does not treat justice as one divine attribute among many — one item on a list that also happens to include mercy, and wrath, and love. He makes a far stronger claim. Justice is the very essence of God's nature, so that all His other qualities flow out of it rather than sitting alongside it. His mercy is an expression of His justice; and so too are His anger, His love, His creating, and His destroying. 'I bear witness,' the Imam says in one of his sermons, 'that He is Justice, and He acts justly.' Not that He is just, as one might praise a fair-minded judge — that He is Justice, the thing itself. Every other name we give Him turns out to be a facet of that one reality, so that to conform to God at all is to conform to justice, and there is no way to become like Him that is not also a way of becoming just.

An objection presents itself at once, and the essay meets it head on. Surely there is a radical gap between justice in God and justice in a human being. In God, justice means that everything already is in its right place — an immutable, metaphysical fact, settled and complete. In man, justice means the effort to put everything in its right place — a dynamic, volitional, often exhausting labour against resistance. How can a single word name both a finished perfection and a struggle that is never finished? The response is that man's moral effort to act justly, in a world where things are plainly not in their right place, is itself rooted in an innate spiritual predisposition toward justice. The human striving is not a different thing from the divine perfection; it is that same principle of justice at work under the conditions of a creature — the immutable pattern by which everything is in its right place, now expressed as the effort to realise it. Just action, on the human side, expresses not only moral and intellectual labour but a spiritual affinity with the ultimate nature of reality: with things as they truly are in God, and therefore as they ought to be here on earth.

It is with this understanding of justice that we can finally open the letter itself. The Imam's epistle appointing Malik al-Ashtar as his governor of Egypt appears as number fifty-two in most editions of the Nahj al-balagha, the great collection of his sermons, letters, and sayings. Across the centuries it has served as a source of inspiration far beyond its original occasion, read as an ideal constitution for just governance — a relatively detailed account of the duties and rights of the ruler, the various officers of the state, and the main classes of society. Yet, as will become clear, its counsel far exceeds anything a political or legal text would attempt. Though addressed in the first instance to a ruler, much of its advice in fact pertains to universally applicable ethical principles, as relevant to the governed as to the governor. It is as much a statement of ethics as of politics — not a system or a theory, but an inspiring expression of the spiritual ethos the Imam embodied and radiated, an ethos flowing directly from the sources of the revelation.

The man who received it deserves a word, because the choice of recipient tells us something about the letter's pitch. Malik al-Ashtar was a long-standing and devoted follower of the Imam, spoken of as one of his 'right-hand' men. That phrase is not a later embellishment; it comes from the mouth of the Imam's chief antagonist. Mu'awiya, who fought the civil war that dominated Ali (ع)'s caliphate, arranged to have Malik poisoned before he could take up his post in Egypt — and on hearing that the plot had succeeded, he is reported to have remarked: 'Ali ibn Abi Talib had two right hands. One was cut off at Siffin, and the other today.' The Imam's own response to the news measured the loss differently. 'Malik — and what was Malik!' he cried. 'By God, were he a mountain, he would be the solitary one soaring above all others, and were he a rock, he would have been impenetrably solid. No horse could ascend such a mountain, no bird could fly up to it.' This is the calibre of man to whom the letter's demands were addressed — which tells us that those demands were pitched not at the average official but at the very best a community could produce.

For the necessity of a just ruler is not incidental to this vision; it follows directly from it. If justice is the putting of things in their right place, and if a whole society is among the things to be so ordered, then the character of whoever holds power over it is decisive. The Imam draws the reciprocal bond tightly: 'The subjects will not be righteous except through the righteousness of the rulers, and the rulers will not be righteous except through the uprightness of the subjects.' Neither side can be just alone; each holds up the other. When the subjects render the ruler his due and he renders them theirs, then — in the Imam's own words — right is honoured among them, the ways of religion are upheld, and the banners of justice are raised. Good governance, on this account, is not a machine of laws but a living reciprocity of character, and it fails at whichever end character fails first. This insistence on the piety of the ruler runs through the whole of the Imam's message to his governor; but, as everything that follows will show, it involves far more than a simple exhortation to keep the formal rules of the law.

And the Imam lived this teaching at real cost, which is what lifts the letter out of theory. One moment from the very start of his caliphate stands for many. His cousin Ibn al-'Abbas advised him, as a matter of prudent statecraft, to keep the previous ruler's governors in their posts for a time — to consolidate his own position first and remove the corrupt ones later, once he was secure. It was shrewd counsel, and the Imam did not pretend otherwise. 'I do not doubt,' he replied, 'that this would be best for the sake of reconciliation in this world. But there is my obligation to the Truth, and my knowledge of these governors — so, by God, I shall never appoint one of them.' He knew the political price. He knew that dismissing them made the revolt against him all but inevitable. He dismissed them anyway, because to confirm men whose corruption was beyond both doubt and remedy would have been, for him, to renounce a part of his religion — and religion without justice is not religion. The letter to Malik is written by a man who had already paid, in the hardest currency there is, for the very conviction it teaches.

## Becoming like God

If justice means becoming like God, the letter refuses to let us forget for a moment what stands in the way. It opens on a note of sombre realism. From its very first lines the dominant tone is the potential for evil in a human being left to his own devices and deprived of divine grace — which is why the preamble presses so hard on the obligations binding every believer, and why Malik is instructed to place obedience to God above all else, to abide by what God has commanded in His Book. This is no perfunctory throat-clearing before the real business of governance begins. Obedience to the divine commands is presented not merely as legally binding but as spiritually liberating — the very thing that frees a person from a tyranny lodged within.

The tyranny has a name. Immediately after calling Malik to obedience, the Imam names what obedience delivers us from: the soul that incites to evil, al-nafs al-ammara. 'For truly,' he writes, 'the soul incites to evil, unless God has mercy.' The sentence is almost word for word the confession of the prophet Joseph in the Qur'an — 'I do not exonerate myself; truly, the soul incites to evil, unless my Lord has mercy' (chapter 12, verse 53) — and the echo is deliberate, meant to set the scene for every instruction that follows. The essential relationship determining the moral substance of a soul, the letter is saying, is the relationship between that soul and divine grace. Left to itself, the soul tends downward, toward evil. With God's assistance it is lifted, freed from its base inclinations, and guided back toward its true and original nature.

The choice of Joseph is not decorative, and the context repays recalling. Joseph offers his confession only after his innocence has been fully established before the Egyptian king — at the very moment, that is, when he might most easily have congratulated himself on his own virtue. Instead he refuses to exonerate himself. For on the two occasions when he was subjected to the seductive temptations of the woman Zulaykha, the Qur'an makes clear that his successful resistance was due not to his own moral resources but to the grace of God. This is precisely the lesson a man about to govern a vast and wealthy country needs, where temptation lies in wait on every side, gross and subtle alike: that the soul's susceptibility to allurement cannot be neutralised by the soul itself, but only by the mercy and grace of God. To open a letter of high office with the sentence of Joseph is to remind its recipient, before anything else whatever, of his absolute and unconditional need of God in every circumstance.

But if the soul left to itself tends toward evil, that is not the deepest truth about it. Beneath the fallen state lies an original one — the fitra, the primordial nature according to which God created the human being. The Qur'an names it directly: 'So set thy purpose for religion with unswerving devotion: the nature of God, according to which He hath created man. There is no altering God's creation. That is the right religion, but most men know not' (chapter 30, verse 30). The fallen soul, in other words, is called to give way to a nature that was always there beneath it — a nature that is at once the origin and the goal of the human condition, the true substance of every soul, always accessible in principle even when, for most people, it lies clouded over in practice. And from this integral nature, in harmony with the divine nature, all the essential virtues flow spontaneously and unhindered. The task is not to manufacture virtue out of nothing; it is to clear away what buries an original goodness that was there from the start. This is the God-given knowledge that the earlier teaching described revelation as unearthing — the buried treasures of the intellects — and it need not be reopened here beyond noting that virtue, on this account, is a recovery and not an invention.

How, then, does one recover it? Here the letter's deepest instruction comes into view, in a phrase drawn from the tradition of spiritual excellence: assume the character traits of God. Every virtue can be regarded as the reflection, on the human plane, of a divine quality — what the tradition calls a divine Name. One is generous for the sake of God, because God is al-Karim, the Generous; one is compassionate and merciful because He is al-Rahman al-Rahim, the Merciful, the Compassionate. But the manner matters quite as much as the fact, and this is the subtle heart of the whole teaching. One assumes these traits never out of any claim that 'I am generous' or 'I am merciful.' The aim is to emulate the qualities of God, not to claim to personify them. The individual must be effaced before God in order to be truly virtuous — and yet, at the very same time, he must actively embody the very qualities he hopes God will show toward him. Efface yourself, and reflect Him: both at once, and neither without the other.

This is the paradox the letter holds together, and it rests on a double truth about what a human being is. Man is at once khalifat Allah, God's vicegerent upon the earth, and abd Allah, His slave. The two are not in tension; each guards against the failure that the other, taken alone, would invite. The consciousness of servitude ensures that, while representing God, a person does not slip into competing with Him in grandeur — which is the very sin the letter most fears in a ruler. And the active commitment to representing God's qualities preserves him from the opposite failure, the spiritual paralysis of neglecting his duties toward the world in the name of humility. The Qur'an fixes the servitude in a single sentence: 'O mankind, you are the destitute before God, and God is the Self-sufficient, the Praiseworthy' (chapter 35, verse 15). The creature owns nothing of his own; whatever generosity or mercy passes through him is borrowed, on loan from its only real source, and to forget that is to forget everything.

The image the essay offers for this is a mirror, and it is exact. To assume the traits of God is like gazing into a mirror: what one sees is a real reflection, but an inverted one. The aim is to catch, on the human plane, the inverted reflection of divine transcendence — never the thing itself, always its reflection, and always reversed by the very fact of being cast onto a creature. What this demands is a synthesis: the active human virtues on the one side, and on the other a steady consciousness of one's own nothingness before the divine Reality. Drop either half and the whole thing collapses — active virtue without humility swells into the pride that competes with God, while humility without active virtue decays into passivity and neglect. And the two halves are joined, in the end, by a single faculty. The intellectual perception of the divine qualities is itself what produces humility: the more truly one apprehends what God is, the more one grasps that there is no proportion whatever between the Absolute and the relative, the Creator and the created — and that very grasp displaces attention from the self and turns it toward the transcendent as the source of all virtue and all truth. Intelligence, rightly used, does not inflate the self. It humbles it. And the humbled self is the just one, because it has at last put the most stubborn thing of all — its own ego — in its right place.

## What this episode lands

Draw the pieces together and a single line runs through them all. Justice, which ordinary speech shrinks to fairness and fair play, is in Ali (ع)'s vision nothing less than the nature of God — not one of His attributes among others but the very essence from which His mercy and His wrath and His love all flow. That is why justice is sacred, and why an ethics cut loose from the divine, in MacIntyre's diagnosis, drifts into relativism and opportunism and finally into dead forms without a spirit. The three components of the spiritual life name the remedy: submission supplies the acts, faith supplies the framework, and their union in ihsan — worshipping God as though you see Him — supplies the living ground out of which real virtue flows.

To be just, then, is to become like God: to assume His character traits, to be generous because He is the Generous and merciful because He is the Merciful, while never once claiming the qualities as one's own. The human being does this as both vicegerent and slave — reflecting the divine on the human plane, inverted as in a mirror, and remaining destitute before the only source of what he reflects. Everything the letter to Malik will go on to demand of a ruler descends from this one movement: put your own soul in its right place before God, efface the ego that wants to stand where God should stand, and just rule follows as the fruit rather than the goal.

But the letter has a darker edge that we have not yet touched. If the just ruler becomes like God, what becomes of the ruler consumed by pride? The next episode takes up the letter's sternest warning of all — that a certain inner attitude is not merely a private flaw or a failure of manners, but something the Imam is prepared to call, without softening the word, war against God Himself.

## Closing

For all its counsel about the offices of state and the ranks of society, the letter keeps circling back to a single interior danger, and it is not incompetence, nor even cruelty in the ordinary sense. It is pride — the ruler's engrossment in himself, his appetite for the flattery and the grandeur that power puts so easily within reach. We have already seen why this should be the gravest of all failings on the terms this episode has laid out. If to be just is to efface the self so that the divine qualities can be reflected through it, then the swelling of the self is the exact reversal of justice: it puts the ego where God alone belongs, and by that single displacement drives everything else out of its right place. What we have not yet seen is just how severely the Imam is willing to name that reversal.

The next episode follows him to the naming. There the language leaves the register of ordinary moral warning behind and becomes something startling — the claim that when a ruler exalts himself, contends in grandeur with his Lord, and disputes the majesty that belongs to God alone, he has entered into nothing less than a war with God. How an inner attitude, a mere movement of the heart, could amount to open war against the Real — and why Ali (ع) reached for the fiercest word he had to describe it — is where we turn next.
