# Effacement and Realization

*The book's final episode follows remembrance to its summit — the point where the one who remembers is himself effaced in the Reality he remembers. Reza Shah-Kazemi unfolds the Imam's most vertiginous saying, that a person will not truly remember God until he forgets his own soul in his remembrance, and shows why this extinction is a grace and not an achievement. It traces the mystics' witness to the state — Ibn al-'Arabi's extinction in contemplation, the Imam's own faintings in awe of God — and closes on the paradox that the deepest effacement of self is the most perfect flowering of a human being: the saints as spotless mirrors of the divine Names, and Ali (ع) himself dying with the Name of God still moist on his tongue.*

## Where this episode picks up

We come to the strangest turn in the whole doctrine of remembrance, and it is one that seems, at first hearing, to contradict everything that came before. All along, remembrance has been presented as a heightening of consciousness — a polishing of awareness, an awakening of the heart, a deepening of the soul's perception until it sees God everywhere. And now the Imam tells us that its summit is a kind of forgetting. "Do not remember God absent-mindedly," he says, "nor forget Him in distraction; rather, remember Him with perfect remembrance, a remembrance in which your heart and tongue are in harmony, and what you conceal conforms with what you reveal. But you will not remember Him according to the true reality of remembrance until you forget your own soul in your remembrance." Read that last clause again. The true reality of remembrance is reached only when you forget your own soul. The practice whose whole aim seemed to be a fuller awareness culminates in the disappearance of the one who is aware.

This is not a contradiction but the doctrine's deepest secret, and unfolding it is the work of this final episode. The Imam distinguishes two levels, and the distance between them is an abyss. There is perfect remembrance — dhikr made complete on the human plane, where heart and tongue are brought into harmony and the outward act conforms to the inward intention, so that all hypocrisy is purged and the soul attains total sincerity. This is the fruit of the sincerity the book has taught from the first: the whole soul integrated, oriented purely toward God, with no taint of desire for anything other than Him. And then there is the reality of remembrance — its haqiqa — which is something else entirely, opening not onto human perfection but onto divine Reality, and reached only through the forgetting of the self. Between the perfect and the real lies an ontological leap that no human effort can make, for the veil that must fall is the self, and the self cannot efface itself by an act of self. To that leap, and to what lies on its far side, this episode is given whole.

Three movements carry it, and the book, to their close. We begin with the Imam's teaching on forgetting the self in remembrance — the two levels, the leap between them, and why the summit is a grace and not an achievement. We turn then to the mystics' witness to the state the tradition calls extinction, and to the Imam's own immersion in it. And we close on the final paradox: that the deepest self-effacement is the most perfect flowering of the human being — the saints as mirrors of the divine Names, and the Imam's own life sealed, at its last breath, by the Name of God.

## Forgetting the self

Everything begins from the two levels the Imam sets side by side, for confusing them is the surest way to miss the whole teaching. The first level is perfect remembrance, and its mark is harmony — heart and tongue in accord, what a person conceals conforming to what he reveals. This is the remembrance that has become sincere, purged of the division in which the tongue says one thing and the heart intends another. And sincerity, as the book has taught in its treatment of the greater struggle against the soul, is the whole labor of integrating a person around a single orientation toward God, so that no corner of desire is reserved for anything but Him. On one side, remembrance itself works this sincerity by polishing the heart; on the other, it is sincerity that confers perfection upon remembrance, for the divine qualities carried in the Name must be reflected, at least in some measure, in the soul of the invoker, in the form of virtues. Without that reflected sincerity the purely technical act of invocation is heedless and empty of all effect. One who makes no effort toward sincerity — who shirks the inner struggle — is heedless of God even while his tongue moves, neglecting the moral preconditions of remembrance's efficacy. So the first level is already demanding: it is the whole moral and spiritual perfection of the human person, the soul made one and sincere.

But the Imam's most astonishing stroke is to present the ultimate stage not as a further perfection of this but as its apparent opposite. The reality of remembrance requires the forgetting of the individual: the veil of the self must be cast aside if the vision of divine Reality is to be attained. There can be no true knowledge of God without self-effacement at the outset and extinction in the end; the reality of remembering calls for the most radical forgetting. And here the two levels part decisively. At the first level, where the aim is the elimination of hypocrisy and the attainment of sincerity and wholeness — perfection on the plane of the human soul — the individual remains, as the agent of remembrance, in possession of his identity; the harmony of hidden intent and outward act is his harmony, a perfection that still pertains to him as its owner. At the second level the remembrance can no longer be defined in relation to the individual at all, because the individual has been forgotten — by himself. And this can only mean one thing: that the center of consciousness has shifted from the one who invokes to the One who is invoked, from the rememberer to the Remembered. The reality of remembrance is one with its object, the Real itself. Esoterically, then, the true agent of the invocation is God — which is exactly the truth the previous episode reached from the side of the Name, and which now returns from the side of the self: the apparent invocation by man is an outward appearance, extinguished in the very act of invocation.

If this is so, then the decisive question is how the leap from the first level to the second is made — and the answer is the hinge of the whole teaching: it is not made by the individual at all. It is impossible for a conscious person, concentrating on the act of invocation, to render himself unconscious of himself in the course of it. Any effort to will oneself into self-forgetting proceeds from a conscious will, and so entrenches the very self-consciousness it means to efface. Notice, the Imam says, that the soul is to be forgotten in the remembrance — not by a separate act of forgetting, but within the act of remembering itself. This is not a matter of forcing oneself to forget oneself while invoking God; it is a matter of total concentration on the Name — a concentration so complete that no room remains in consciousness, no space mentally or existentially, for anything but the Named. A first measure of self-effacement follows naturally from such total concentration; but the ultimate consummation, the extinction the tradition calls fana, comes as a divine grace, surpassing all individual effort — even though the act of invocation is understood to attract that grace, or to remove from the heart the rust that obscures the vision of its own nothingness before the sole Reality. This is the esoteric sense of the Qur'anic words, "thou wilt not forget, unless God willeth it so" (chapter 87, verses 6 to 7). One early master of the path put it with perfect economy: the reality of remembrance is that you forget everything other than the Invoked in the remembrance — so that, as the Qur'an says, "remember thy Lord when thou hast forgotten" (chapter 18, verse 24) comes to mean: when you have forgotten what is not God, then you have remembered God.

It is worth seeing why this extinction, far from being the opposite of the heightened consciousness remembrance was supposed to yield, is that heightening carried to its very end. The human invocation, the previous episode showed, is a reflection of the divine invocation — and a reflected image both participates in what it reflects and inverts it. In the first respect, the human act of invocation leads to a fuller consciousness and a richer participation in being for the one who invokes; this is the whole gain the practice promised. In the second respect, the same human invocation finds its consummation in what looks like the exact reverse: the effacement of the individual in the object he invokes. Yet the reverse is only apparent. To grasp it, consider what individuality is. To be an individual is to be limited, bounded, cut off — and limitation is itself a kind of negation, a not-being-everything. The extinction of the self is therefore the negation of a negation; and the negation of a negation is not a further loss but a pure affirmation, the affirmation of an unconditional, supra-individual being that the limits of selfhood had been holding back. The self is not so much destroyed as un-bounded — the wall that made it a separate thing is taken down, and what floods in is not nothingness but the fullness the wall had excluded. This is why the tradition can speak of extinction and realization in the same breath, and mean no paradox by it: to lose the limited self is to gain the unlimited reality the limited self was a cramped and partial version of. The heightening of consciousness the practice began by promising is completed, not contradicted, when the self that was its narrow vessel is finally broken open.

## Extinction in contemplation

The mystics who came after the Imam gave this extinction its fullest description, and they anchored it, remarkably, in the same paradox of forgetting that the Imam had voiced. Ibn al-'Arabi wrote a treatise whose very title names the state — the book of extinction in contemplation — and its argument turns on a piece of Qur'anic grammar that discloses the whole secret. He begins by insisting that the divine Reality is too exalted to be witnessed by any creaturely eye: the vision of God is impossible so long as any trace of the condition of the creature remains in the eye of the one who would witness. Only at the treatise's close does he remove the barrier, and he does so through the Prophet's definition of the highest excellence of faith — to worship God as though you see Him, and if you see Him not, yet truly He sees you. The Arabic of the phrase "if you see Him not" can be re-voiced, by placing a stop in its middle, to read: "if you are not, you see Him." The condition of the vision is not that you look harder but that you cease to be. The vision of God cannot belong to the individual, who has been forgotten, or is, in this idiom, non-existent; the vision of God can only be by God, not by man.

Ibn al-'Arabi drove the point home with a scene from his own visionary ascent, a dialogue he recounts with Moses in the sixth heaven. He reminds Moses that the Prophet had said not one of you will see his Lord until he dies. "And it was just like that," Moses answers: "when I asked Him for the vision, He answered me, so that I fell down stunned" — the word is the Qur'an's own, of Moses' state when God revealed Himself to the mountain — "then I saw Him in my state of being stunned." "While you were dead?" Ibn al-'Arabi asks. "While I was dead," Moses replies; "I did not see God until I had died." This supra-personal vision is entirely of a piece with the Imam's unselfconscious remembrance: the true vision of God cannot take place so long as anything creaturely subsists in the eye of the beholder. And the Imam had folded the same principle into a supplication, pleading with God to make him one of those whom He has called and who has answered, upon whom He has glanced and who falls unconscious before His majesty — the very word for that fainting being the word the Qur'an uses of Moses at the mountain. A single glance from the Real is enough to precipitate this undoing of self-awareness, the shadow cast, on the plane of the individual, by the overwhelming light of God's self-disclosure.

That this is no mere speculation the tradition attests from the Imam's own life. Abu'l-Darda, an eminent companion of the Prophet, reported that he once found Ali (ع) lying in one of the palm orchards of Medina, his body stiff and seemingly lifeless. He went to condole with the family, but Fatima told him: my cousin has not died; rather, in fear of God he has fainted, and this condition overcomes him often. The fear in question is awe — the heavenly fear the Qur'an names when it says that whoever fears the station of his Lord and restrains his soul from caprice shall have the Garden for his abode (chapter 79, verses 40 to 41). Such fear does not terrify so much as beatify; it produces not merely a quaking before the transcendent but an intimate absorption in the immanent presence. When one fears creatures, the Sufis say, one flees from them; but when one fears God, one flees to Him — for, as the Qur'an puts it, "there is no refuge from God except fleeing to Him" (chapter 9, verse 118). And this recurring faint of the Imam gives the ground of another of his sayings, which is no rhetorical extravagance but a description of an authentic state: "Truly, God has a drink for His friends. When they drink it, they are intoxicated; and when they are intoxicated, they are enraptured; and when they are enraptured, they are blessed; and when they are blessed, they dissolve; and when they dissolve, they are free; and when they are free, they devote themselves purely; and when they devote themselves purely, they seek; and when they seek, they find; and when they find, they arrive; and when they arrive, they are at one — there is no difference between them and their Beloved." The remembrance that began as a word on the tongue ends here, where the last difference between the lover and the Beloved is gone.

Al-Ghazali, reaching for language equal to this dissolution, pressed it further than any single word could go. The Sufis who verify the divine oneness by mystical taste, he wrote, are drowned in oneness until they become dumbfounded: no capacity remains within them save to recall God — not so much as the capacity to recall their own selves. And this state, he says, is called extinction, nay the extinction of extinction, for the soul has become extinct to itself and extinct even to its own extinction; it is unconscious of itself and unconscious of its own unconsciousness — since, were it conscious that it was unconscious, it would still be conscious of itself. The self is not merely set aside but has lost even the awareness of having been set aside; the last trace of the observing "I," which could have watched its own disappearance and so survived it, is itself gone. Another master caught the human cost and sweetness of this in a single sentence: whoever has not tasted the loneliness of unconsciousness will not find the food of the intimacy of remembrance. To lose consciousness of oneself is a kind of loneliness — the emptying-out of that self-satisfied, habitual self-regard that is the shadow cast by self-awareness — and only through that loneliness is the true intimacy with God found, for one cannot find intimacy with the Real except by depriving the self of its false intimacy with itself.

Nor is this extinction a single door through which one either passes or does not; the tradition maps it as a graded ascent. Al-Kashani traces the degrees of remembrance as a ladder climbed rung by rung: first the remembrance of the tongue, which corresponds to the lower soul; then the remembrance of the heart, answering to the divine acts that manifest God's graces; then the remembrance of the secret, the innermost point, which unveils the sciences of the divine qualities; then the remembrance of the spirit, which contemplates the lights of those qualities and apprehends the light of the Essence; then the remembrance of the hidden, which contemplates the beauty of the Essence while a last duality yet subsists; and finally the remembrance of the Essence itself — essential vision, attained only through the removal of that last duality. The path does not leap from speech to extinction in one bound; it ascends through a whole hierarchy of interiorization, each level a further inward turn, until at the summit even the subtle duality of a seer and a Seen is dissolved. And because the ascent is long and its higher reaches are not in the traveler's power to seize, the counsel of the masters is never to abandon the practice for want of feeling God's presence in it. Do not leave off the invocation, Ibn 'Ata'illah warns, merely because you do not sense the presence of God within it — for your forgetfulness of the invocation is worse than your forgetfulness in the invocation. Perhaps God will carry you from an invocation clouded by heedlessness to one marked by vigilance, and from vigilance to His presence, and from His presence to a state in which everything but the Invoked is absent — and that, he ends, is not difficult for God.

## The mirror of the Names

Now the whole doctrine turns on a final paradox, and it is the one that redeems everything harsh-sounding in the language of extinction. One might fear that the effacement of the self would end in a blank — that to lose oneself in God would be to lose everything, to become nothing and nobody. The tradition teaches the reverse: that the deepest effacement of individuality goes hand in hand with the most perfect flowering of the human being. Al-Ghazali, commenting on the Qur'anic word that "everything perisheth except His Face" (chapter 28, verse 88), explains that each thing, considered in and by itself, is pure nothingness, and is real only from the face turned toward the One who gives it existence — so that the divine Face is the sole reality, and the perishing of the creature is not its annihilation into a void but its return to the only being it ever truly had. The soul extinguished by the Name attains, at one and the same moment, its total nullification and its ultimate realization — no longer as a separate self, but as the pure locus in which the one Reality knows itself. This is why the tradition can speak, after the state of extinction, of a subsistence that follows it — a return of consciousness to a self now transfigured by the knowledge of reality impressed upon it in the extinction, a self that has passed through its own nothingness and come back bearing the vision it found there.

It is in this light that the astonishing self-descriptions of the saints and of the Imams must be read, for taken otherwise they look like the very sin the whole book has warned against. When Ali (ع) is reported, in one much-disputed sermon, to say "I am the gnosis of the mysteries," and even "I am the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden," the exoteric ear hears blasphemy, a creature claiming the attributes of God. But the key the Imam himself supplies unlocks the rest: "I am the gnosis of the mysteries." He is not identifying himself with God, but with the knowledge of God — and not with his own knowledge of God, which would still be a relative knowledge belonging to a relative being, but with knowledge itself, the knowledge God has of Himself, which alone is fully identical with its object. Only where the knower is the known, because the knowledge in question is the divine Reality's consciousness of itself, do the distinctions of knower and known dissolve; and an individual consciousness reaches that knowledge only on condition of the utter extinction of every trace of creaturely selfhood. When the Imam appears to speak of his own greatness, then, it is not his own: it is the greatness of God expressed through him as a locus of theophany, precisely in the measure of his effacement in that greatness. The point had been made of the divesting of the divine attributes that the book examined in its treatment of the Imam's way of affirmation and denial — that to strip God of all attributes, rightly understood, is not to deny His qualities but to refuse to confine them within human conception, and is thus the deepest tawhid rather than any diminishment. The same logic governs the saint. Stripped of every claim of his own, annihilated in the divine Essence like an attribute that is one with what it qualifies, he can reflect the divine qualities without appropriating them, so that there is no question of a creature seizing the glory of God.

Rumi caught this same self-effaced greatness in the lines he places in the Imam's own mouth, on the field of battle. "I am the Lion of God," the Imam says there, "I am not the lion of my passion; my deed bears witness to my religion." In war, he goes on, he is but the sword, and the wielder is the divine Sun; he has removed the baggage of self out of the way and deemed all that is other than God to be non-existence. "I am a shadow," the lines conclude, "the Sun is my lord; I am the chamberlain, I am not the curtain that prevents approach to Him." The whole doctrine of the mirror is in that last image. A chamberlain admits the visitor to the king; a curtain shuts him out. The effaced saint is the one who, having removed the baggage of self, no longer stands between the seeker and God as an obstruction to be got past, but has become the very opening through which God is approached — a shadow cast by the Sun that points back, unfailingly, to the Sun.

Ibn al-'Arabi gave this its exact formula when he described the climax of his own ascent, in which God removed from him his contingent dimension and he attained the inner realities of all the Names, seeing them return to a single Subject and a single Entity — "that Subject was what I witnessed and that Entity was my Being; for my voyage was only in myself and pointed to myself, and through this I came to know that I was a pure servant, without a trace of lordship in me at all." The servitude is not denied but perfected: the saint's true identity as a mirror of the Names is attained only upon the effacement of his individuality, so that the more he is emptied of self, the more brilliantly the divine qualities shine through him. This is the doctrine of the mirror the Imam pressed to its finest point. A mirror loses its own identity as a mirror when attention fixes wholly on the image within it — and when the image is fixed upon, the mirror ceases to be a veil and instead reveals the object. So too the friends of God, who know themselves to be nothing before the Absolute, are precisely the ones who most faithfully reflect the divine Names, which belong not to them but to God; their creativity is raised to its highest pitch because the mirrors of their hearts have been polished by remembrance until the most stubborn stains, those of egotism, are gone. Their hearts are already in the Gardens while their bodies are at work — for, as the book taught in speaking of detachment and the Hereafter, such souls live already more in the transcendent world than in this one, and though they labor here with their bodies, they are not bound by the fruits of their action.

A story told of Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq puts the whole paradox into a single exchange. Accused once of pride, he answered: I am not proud, for pride pertains to the divine greatness. Since I have gone beyond the pride of self, His pride has come and taken the place of my own; with pride of self present there can be no divine greatness, but through the divine greatness, pride will be present. The greatness that shows through such a soul is not the soul's own swelling but God's, shining the more brilliantly for the clearing-away of every trace of egotism, as light passes more purely through a glass from which the last film has been wiped. The Imam gave this its most vivid image: "Down from me torrents do pour; up to me no bird can soar." The torrents flow down from him because — and exactly in the measure that — he is extinguished in the source of the torrent; what pours through him is not his but the flood he has ceased to obstruct. The Qur'an itself, describing the friends of God who drink from the celestial spring, says they make it gush forth abundantly (chapter 76, verse 6); and the commentators explain that they cause the spring to flow more freely because they are themselves the sources of it, there being in them no duality, no otherness, no dark veil of self-regard to dam the flow. This is the final overturning of every fear that effacement means diminishment. The self that is lost was the one obstruction between the soul and the infinite reality it was made to transmit; and when it is gone, what remains is not a lesser thing but a clearer channel — a human being become so transparent to God that the divine qualities pour through him into the world without impediment.

One consequence of this transparency deserves a final word, for it completes the arc the whole book has traced from its treatment of the intellect onward. When the self is effaced, what knows God is no longer the creature but God knowing Himself through the creature — which is why the tradition calls such a one not simply the knower of God but the knower through God, and why the Imam could compress the whole matter into the phrase that one must know God through God. Knowledge of God at this deepest level is the Real knowing itself through a creature made perfectly poor, perfectly empty, a passage offering no resistance. And the heart so emptied does not lose the world in gaining God; it gains the world back transfigured. Remembrance, in the end, means not only forgetting everything but God but discovering that there was never anything in being but God — so that the heart, once blind and deaf and now made to see and hear, sees the Face of God wherever it turns, as the Qur'an says, "wherever ye turn, there is the Face of God" (chapter 2, verse 115), and hears in all creation the hymn of praise that everything sings, for "do you not see that God is glorified by everything in the heavens and the earth, and the birds with wings outstretched, each knowing its prayer and its glorification?" (chapter 24, verse 41). The extinction that seemed to empty the world of the self fills it, instead, with God — the same world the unremembering eye saw as a wall of opaque things now heard, by the cleared heart, to be singing.

And the whole of it is sealed, at last, in the manner of the Imam's own death. When Ali (ع) was struck by the poisoned sword of his assassin, the cry that broke from him was not one of pain but of triumph: "I have triumphed, by the Lord of the Ka'ba!" At the very moment of death there was in him nothing but the remembrance of God, and he passed away, it is said, with his tongue still repeating the words of the testimony that there is no god but God. His death was consummated exactly as his life had been lived — in accordance with the wisdom of the Prophet, who, asked which of all acts is the most meritorious, answered: "That you die while your tongue is moistened with the invocation of God." The whole journey of this book ends there, on the palm-orchard floor and at the sword's edge alike: a man so given to the remembrance of God that awe of Him could still the body like death in the midst of life, and the Name of God could be the last word on the tongue at the coming of death itself.

## What this episode lands

This final episode has followed remembrance to the summit the whole book was climbing toward. We have unfolded the Imam's most vertiginous saying — that a person will not remember God according to the true reality of remembrance until he forgets his own soul in the remembering — and seen that its two levels are separated by an abyss: perfect remembrance, the soul made whole and sincere on the human plane, and the reality of remembrance, reached only when the self is effaced, and reached not by any act of the self, which cannot efface itself, but by a grace that total concentration on the Name attracts. We have taken the mystics' witness to that extinction — the vision that belongs not to the seer but to God, the "if you are not, you see Him," Moses stunned upon the mountain — and set beside it the Imam's own faintings in the awe of God and his saying of the drink that ends where there is no difference between the friend and the Beloved. And we have reached the redeeming paradox: that the deepest effacement of self is the fullest flowering of the human being, the saint a spotless mirror of the divine Names precisely because nothing of his own remains to cloud them — with the Imam's own death, the Name of God on his dying tongue, as the seal of the whole teaching.

With this the book closes, and its argument stands complete. To speak of Ali (ع), it said at the outset, is to speak of the quintessential spirituality of Islam; and the long road through intellect and justice and remembrance has borne that claim out. The remembrance of God has proved to be the root of which the intellect's vision and the just soul's disposition were branches: the polish that clears the heart, the practice that opens onto the whole of a life, the reciprocity in which God's remembrance holds man in being, and finally the extinction in which the one who remembers is lost and found in the Reality he remembers. It is fitting that a figure who stands at the head of nearly every chain of the mystical tradition, Sunni and Shi'i alike, and who is traced as the source of the chivalric virtues that ran through the guilds and the arts of the whole Muslim world, should be the one in whom this teaching is given and in whose death it is sealed. The book began by naming him the gate to the city of prophetic wisdom; it ends by showing what lies within the city — a remembrance that determines, in the last analysis, the whole quality of a human life, and beyond which there is only the Face that alone remains when everything else has passed away. That is where the long argument has been leading from its first page: not to a doctrine to be held, but to a Face to be seen, by a heart cleared enough to see it and emptied enough to let God see through it. The gate opened onto a city, and the city, in the end, opened onto God — and the whole of that road was walked by the single, quiet act of remembering His Name.
