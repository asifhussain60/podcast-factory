# Remembrance as Polish for the Heart

*This episode opens the book's final movement, on the remembrance of God — dhikru'Llah — which the letter to Malik had named as the living essence of prayer and then deferred for a fuller treatment. Reza Shah-Kazemi reads the sayings of Ali (ع) as a running commentary on the Qur'anic notion of remembrance, and shows why the Sufi tradition, across every land and both branches of Islam, traces its spiritual lineage back to him. The episode establishes what dhikr means, why the Imam calls it a polish for the hearts that lets them hear after deafness and see after blindness, and what kind of organ the spiritual "heart" is — an organ of vision that, once cleared, sees not by its own power but by the light of the One it remembers.*

## Where this episode opens

What if the most exalted act of worship in all of Islam were also the easiest — a single Name, repeated, that a person could carry through the marketplace and the sickbed and the last breath, needing no ablution, no set time, no fixed posture at all? That is the claim the Imam and the whole mystical tradition of Islam press upon us, and it is a claim that ought to unsettle us before it consoles. We are accustomed to measuring worship by its cost — the fast that empties the stomach, the pilgrimage that empties the purse, the vigil that robs the body of sleep. Yet the Qur'an says plainly of the remembrance of God that it is greater than the prayer which keeps a person from lewdness and iniquity (chapter 29, verse 45), and the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, told his companions that the perpetual invocation of God is better than giving silver and gold, and better than meeting the enemy on the field of battle. How can the lightest practice be the weightiest?

There is a hint in the very word the Qur'an chooses. It does not say the remembrance of God is more difficult, or more meritorious, or more rewarded; it says it is greater — akbar — and greatness is not a matter of effort at all. A mountain is not great because it labored to become large. The weight of remembrance, the tradition insists, has nothing to do with the exertion it costs and everything to do with what it touches: not one region of the religious life but its living center, the thing all the other rites exist to serve. A rite that empties the stomach or the purse acts upon the body and its possessions; remembrance acts upon the heart, and the heart is where a person is finally either turned toward God or turned away. That is why the lightest practice can be the weightiest — because weight here is measured by depth of reach, and nothing reaches deeper, or costs the body less to reach with, than the Name of God carried on a quiet tongue.

The answer, this book argues, lies in what remembrance actually does to the one who practices it — and that is the subject the whole of this closing essay sets out to unfold. In the letter to Malik al-Ashtar the Imam had called prayer the lynchpin of the just life and named remembrance as prayer's inner core, the polish that clears the heart until it can see; but he treated it there only as the tool by which justice is forged, and deferred its full account. Now that account arrives. Where the earlier discussion asked what remembrance does for one particular virtue, this one asks what remembrance is in itself — the very heart of the spiritual life, pursued all the way to its root, where the one who remembers is at last effaced in the Reality he had set out only to recall.

Three movements carry this first episode. We begin with the word itself — what dhikr means, why the tradition accords it such supremacy, and why the figure of Ali (ع) stands at the head of nearly every chain of Sufi transmission, the one point on which Sunni and Shi'i mysticism converge. We turn then to the image the Imam gives us in one of his sermons: remembrance as a polish for the hearts, by which they hear after being deaf and see after being blind. And we close on the nature of the organ so polished — the spiritual heart, which is neither the reasoning mind nor the bodily senses, and which, once cleared, sees God not by any power of its own but by the very light it was made to reflect.

## The word and its weight

To begin, one must ask what is actually meant by this term dhikr, and why Islamic devotion accords it so central a place. The word is extraordinarily rich, and no single English word can carry its range. It means remembrance, recollection, invocation, repetition, recitation; but also memory, mention, telling, and relating, as of a story; and further, praise, glorification, celebration, fame, eminence, and honor; and it can denote the Qur'an itself, or any book of revelation. The definition that comes closest to the spiritual principle at its core is the one given by the lexicographer al-Raghib al-Isfahani, who wrote that dhikr is that by which a person preserves the knowledge he has attained. It is akin to memory, he explained, except that memory is spoken of in respect of its function of acquiring, of laying something up, whereas dhikr is spoken of in respect of its function of rendering present — making a thing present once more to the consciousness. It refers, he added, to the presence of a thing either in one's heart or on one's tongue; hence there is a dhikr of the heart and a dhikr of the tongue, and each is of two kinds — a remembrance that follows upon forgetting, and a remembrance that follows upon no forgetting at all but expresses a continuous, unbroken presence.

Hold that distinction in view, because it governs everything that follows. When the thing rendered present to consciousness is God, the whole importance of remembrance declares itself at once. Dhikru'Llah is, first, a principle of awareness — the in-depth assimilation of the divine presence, God made present to the heart. But it is also an action, a practice, a discipline: a methodic thing one does. It is, in other words, both the end and the means to that end — both the spiritual awareness and the deliberate practice by which that awareness is cultivated. The Sufi tradition is where this double character is most fully worked out, for there the remembrance is presented at once as a contemplative discipline centered on the methodic invocation of a Name of God, and as the mystical fruit of that very discipline. It is the spiritual method above all others, the practice that assimilates within the heart what is merely present to the mind, turning a notional grasp of ideas into a concrete perception of realities. And insofar as it is the permanent awareness of the divine, it is nothing less than the quintessence of religion as such — that which binds the human being back to God, and so the reason every rite exists. If prayer is the core of religious practice, remembrance is, as the Qur'an says with a single word, greater.

It is worth pausing on how the tradition itself insists on this supremacy, because the witnesses are unanimous across every school. Ali (ع) himself affirms, "Perpetuate the remembrance, for truly it illumines the heart, and it is the most excellent form of worship." Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq described his own followers precisely in terms of it: "Our people are those who, when they are alone, invoke and remember God much" — the aloneness here being the khalwa, the spiritual retreat in which one seeks to be alone with God, which the masters of the path hold to be essential. From the Sufi side, Shaykh Ahmad al-'Alawi, who died in the last century, could be taken as speaking for the whole tradition: remembrance, he said, is the mightiest rule of the religion; the law was not enjoined upon us, nor were the rites of worship ordained, except for the sake of establishing the remembrance of God, and our performance of every rite is strong or weak exactly according to the degree of our remembrance while performing it. Al-Ghazali raised the obvious objection — why should the simplest practice be the most meritorious, when every other act of worship demands so much more effort? — and answered that the full reason can only be reached through the science of unveiling; but that from the standpoint of the practical religious sciences, what can be said is that the most efficacious remembrance is the one performed continuously with the presence of the heart, and that it is through this presence that all the other acts of worship are dignified. Remembrance is the ultimate fruit of the practical acts of worship, the thing they were all for.

It is worth dwelling a moment on the two kinds of remembrance the lexicographer distinguished, because the distinction is not a pedant's nicety but a map of the whole path. There is a remembrance that follows upon forgetting — the sudden recovery of something that had slipped from mind, the jolt of recollection by which what was lost is found again. And there is a remembrance that follows upon no forgetting at all, a continuous and unbroken presence in which the thing remembered is never let go. The spiritual life moves from the first toward the second. In the beginning, remembrance is intermittent and effortful, a repeated return from distraction, a rememberer forever catching himself in the act of having forgotten. Its aim is a state in which the return is no longer needed because the departure never happens — a remembrance woven so deeply into the texture of a soul that it continues, as the tradition will say of one of the Imams, even while its owner eats and walks. To render God present to the heart once, with effort, is the discipline; to have God present to the heart always, without effort, is the fruit. Between them lies the whole labor of the path.

And here the figure of Ali (ع) enters, for the convergence of the Imam and remembrance within the mystical tradition is anything but coincidental. To speak of Ali (ع), this book said at its outset, is to speak of the quintessential spirituality of the Islamic faith; his influence is felt wherever spirituality has flourished, whether in the disciplines of Sufism and Shi'i gnosis, or in the spiritual chivalry of the craft guilds, or in music, poetry, and the arts. As the gate to the city of prophetic wisdom, he leaves his mark on all the noblest expressions of the spirit of Islam. With the Sufi orders the connection is uniquely organic. Whether they are Sunni or Shi'i, Arab or Persian, Turk or Malay, they are united in the person of Ali ibn Abi Talib, who stands at the summit of nearly all the chains of affiliation — the silsilas — by which the masters trace their lineage back to the Prophet. Seyyed Hossein Nasr observed that Islamic esotericism crystallized into Sufism in the Sunni world while it poured into the whole structure of Shi'ism, so that the same esoteric core expresses itself in Sunni Sufism and Shi'i gnosis with different accents but without any change in its attunement to the message of divine oneness. Sayyid Haydar Amuli, one of the great figures in whom the two streams converge, went so far as to say that the true Shi'i cannot but be a Sufi if he plumbs the mystical depths of the Imams' teaching, and the Sufi is a Shi'i if he recognizes that the roots of Sufism lie in the Imams of the Prophet's household, the first poles of the spiritual tradition. For both, the differences of outward form dissolve in the fire of sanctity, and for both, Ali (ع) is the friend of God — the wali Allah — par excellence. The first manual of Sufism written in Persian could describe him as the leader of the saints and the pure ones, of tremendous honor in this path, of whom Junayd said, "Ali is our master as regards principles and affliction." His elucidation of remembrance, and his initiation of disciples into its practice, is one face of his role as spiritual guide — the pole of his age — and it is this that makes him, for the tradition, the first spiritual pole of Sufism after the Prophet himself.

It must be admitted at once that we have very little historically firm information about this initiatic role — his actual bestowal of the invocation upon disciples, which the tradition calls talqin, just as we have almost nothing about his own initiation into the esoteric practice by the Prophet. This should not surprise us, for the practice is by its nature esoteric; it is not enjoined upon all as an obligatory rite, but bestowed within both Sufism and Shi'i gnosis upon those with a particular vocation, a calling to something beyond the rites that bind everyone. Yet the earliest sources describe those devoted to remembrance as a particular discipline as close associates, if not disciples, of the Imam, and both the oral and the written sources of the path preserve reports of the transmission of the invocation from the Prophet to him and from him onward to those who initiated others into what came to be called the Sufi way. And where the historical record is thin, the literary record is extraordinarily rich. In the sayings gathered in the Nahj al-balagha and the Ghurar al-hikam, and in the supplications attributed to the Imam, we possess a homogeneous corpus — a distinct body of doctrine — that furnishes the researcher with a wealth of philosophical reflection and metaphysical insight into the nature of this practice. These sayings shed light not only on the meaning of remembrance but on the philosophy and epistemology underlying it, and they offer precious allusions to its fruit in spiritual realization. They stand as a bridge: on one side they comment on the Qur'anic verses that speak of remembrance, and on the other they foreshadow the elaborate doctrines the later mystics would build. The mystics, one might say, made explicit what the Imam had conveyed in sayings that were concise, often paradoxical, always intellectually demanding — the doctrinal reverberations of his dense and elliptical pronouncements.

## A polish for the hearts

We come now to the single image around which the whole doctrine gathers, and though it was glimpsed once before — where the letter to Malik used it to explain how a ruler's heart is fitted for justice — it was introduced there only as a tool and set aside for later. Here it becomes the theme itself. The most fitting place to begin is the Imam's comment on remembrance in the two hundred and thirteenth sermon of the Nahj al-balagha. He first recites a fragment of a Qur'anic verse — "men whom neither trade nor merchandise diverts from the remembrance of God" — and then comments on it. What gives the moment its depth is that this fragment is organically joined to the great verse of light that stands just before it, so rich in mystical symbolism, which the Imam knew his recitation would call to his listeners' minds even though he did not quote it in full. That verse runs: "God is the light of the heavens and the earth. A similitude of His light is a niche wherein is a lamp; the lamp is enclosed in a glass; the glass is as it were a shining star, lit by the oil of a blessed olive tree, neither of the East nor of the West, whose oil well-nigh shineth forth though fire touch it not. Light upon light! God guideth to His light whom He will" (chapter 24, verse 35). It is in the light of this light, then, that the Imam speaks.

"Truly," he begins, "God has made the remembrance a polish for the hearts, by which they hear after being deaf, and see after being blind, and yield after being resistant. There have always been slaves of God with whom He held intimate discourse through their thoughts and spoke with them through the essence of their intellects. They diffused illumination through the awakened light in their hearing and their seeing and their hearts, calling unto the remembrance of the days of God. Indeed, there is a special group who belong to the remembrance; they have adopted it in place of the world, such that neither trade nor merchandise distracts them from it. They spend the days of their life in it. It is as though they had left this world for the Hereafter, and they are there, witnessing what is beyond this world." The decisive words are the description of remembrance as a polish for the hearts, and the reference to the awakened light in the hearing and sight and hearts of those who have applied that polish. The connection with the verse of light could not be clearer. The latent light of the heart is disclosed by the remembrance of God; the light within shines together with the light from on high — light upon light. Once the heart is illumined, every other faculty shares in the luminosity the invocation generates, and so there is, again, further light upon light.

The very word for polish carries the doctrine. In Arabic it derives from a verb whose primary meaning is to become clear, to be exposed to view, to be brought to light — the same root the Qur'an uses of the day when it brings the sun to light, and of the moment when the light shines forth in brightness. The consciousness lying hidden within the heart is brought to light by the polishing that remembrance works upon it. And the image repays reflection precisely because it corrects a misunderstanding we are prone to. We tend to imagine that remembering God is a matter of acquiring something — adding a devotion, learning a formula, taking on new information. The image of the polish says the opposite. A mirror does not manufacture the images it shows; it reflects faithfully whatever is set before it, but only when its surface is clear. The heart is such a mirror, made to reflect the Real, and remembrance is not the writing of something new upon it but the removal of the film that has dulled it — the rust of forgetfulness scraped away until the surface once more receives what it was always meant to receive. The knowledge thus recovered was never absent; it was only occluded. This is why the Imam can speak of slaves of God with whom their Lord held discourse through their very thoughts and spoke through the essence of their intellects: once the mirror is clear, the divine converse that was always addressed to it can at last be received. The Imam's words echo a saying of the Prophet that the Sufis often quote: "For everything there is a polish, and the polish of the hearts is the remembrance of God."

The mirror-image conceals a second movement worth drawing out, for the polished heart does two things at once, not one. On the one hand it functions as a mirror that reflects truths from above — receiving, like any true mirror, the image set before it, the realities that descend upon it from on high. But on the other hand it reveals its own hidden depths, disclosing truths from within — those concealed contents of consciousness the Imam elsewhere calls the buried treasures of the intellect. The polishing, in other words, clears the surface in both directions: it lets the light from above fall in and be reflected, and it brings to the surface a light that was buried in the heart's own depths. This is why the Imam's teaching cannot be reduced to a doctrine of reception alone, as though the heart were merely a passive plate awaiting an impression. What remembrance uncovers is partly a knowledge the heart already held and had forgotten it held — a treasure not brought from outside and deposited but unearthed from a ground that was always the heart's own. The two lights meet at the cleared surface, the descending and the ascending, and their meeting is once more that phrase the verse of light supplies: light upon light. To purify the organ of perception is thus at the same time to enable it to reflect what is above it and to disclose what is within it, and the tradition holds these two together because in the end they are one — the reality reflected from above and the reality buried within being, at their root, the same reality.

That the polishing is a real discipline, and not merely a mood, deserves emphasis — though naming it a discipline in no way excludes its meaning as awareness, as a state of recollectedness in the divine presence, the disposition of a soul shaped by the knowledge that if you cannot see God, He sees you. The polishing can be understood at once as the work of purifying thought through reflection and meditation, and as a regular methodic practice, whether that takes the form of glorification, or magnification, or praise, or the declaration of divine unity, or the invocation of God's Names in the strict sense — by all of which the rust of forgetfulness is removed. And the Imam speaks, as the Prophet did, of a particular group who belong to the remembrance, whom he calls an ahl, a people or a family of it. The Prophet had spoken of the same: "God has angels who rove the pathways, seeking out the people of remembrance, and when they find a group of people invoking God, they call out to one another, Come to that which you desire." Moving forward in time to one of the greatest of what the Sufis call the folk, Rumi gave the image of the polished heart its most memorable poetic form. It is the custom of kings, he tells us in the Mathnawi, to seat the Sufis directly before them — for they are a mirror for the soul, and better than any mirror: breasts polished by invocation and meditation, such that the mirror of the heart receives the pure, virginal image. What every one of these witnesses is describing is the same single operation, seen from different sides: the heart made clear until it can once again hold the light it was fashioned to hold.

## The heart that sees

Everything turns, then, on what this "heart" is, and it is not what our ordinary speech means by the word. In the Imam's perspective, and the Qur'an's, the heart is the organ of consciousness and of vision — an insight that penetrates beneath the surface of things, which the outward eye can never reach — and it cannot be simply identified with the reasoning faculty, still less with the organs of sense. The Qur'an points to this distinctive mode of seeing with striking force: "Have they not travelled in the land, that they might have hearts with which to comprehend, and ears with which to hear? Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but blind are the hearts that are in the breasts" (chapter 22, verse 46). The outward sight of the eye reaches only the surface of things; the vision proper to the heart is an insight that reaches the reality concealed beneath. And the Qur'an sets the two conditions of the heart side by side. Of the heedless it says, "They have hearts with which they understand not, eyes with which they see not, ears with which they hear not; they are like cattle — nay, even further astray; they are the heedless" (chapter 7, verse 179) — and the word for the heedless, the ghafilun, is the exact opposite of the dhakir, the rememberer. Of the awakened it says, "Those who believe and whose hearts are at peace in the remembrance of God — is it not in the remembrance of God that hearts are at peace?" (chapter 13, verse 28); and, "Truly, therein is a reminder for him who hath a heart" (chapter 50, verse 37); and it warns of "those whose hearts are hardened against the remembrance of God," even as it promises that the skins and hearts of those who fear their Lord "soften to the remembrance of God" (chapter 39, verse 23). The whole relation is there in a sentence: the heart is hardened by forgetting and softened, cleared, made able to see, by remembering.

What sort of vision does the cleared heart attain? In the Imam's perspective it is nothing less than the spiritual vision of God — and here we must tread carefully, for the book has already taught, in its very first movement, that this vision does not belong to the outward eye. Asked once how he had seen the Lord he worshipped, the Imam answered that eyes do not behold God through the observation of sight, but hearts behold Him through the verities of faith — a saying that has served this whole book as a refrain, and needs no fresh unfolding here. What this chapter adds is the metaphysics of that seeing, and it is a startling addition. The Imam says elsewhere that "pure hearts" are the very "places" from which God can be seen, so that "whoever purifies his heart sees God"; and he asks, "Where are they whose actions are accomplished purely for God, and who purify their hearts so that they become places for the remembrance of God?" The purification of the heart and the seeing of God are, in these sayings, one and the same event.

Read together, these sayings function as a commentary on a divine word — a hadith qudsi — that the tradition treasures, in which God says of the servant who never ceases to draw near through the supererogatory acts, "when I love him, I am his hearing by which he hears, his sight by which he sees, his hand by which he grasps, and his foot by which he walks." The Sufis take this utterance as a foundational principle of their metaphysics and as the goal of their practice at once, and its bearing on drawing near through worship has been drawn out before. The point to seize here is the one the Imam's sayings force upon us. If God becomes the hearing by which His loved servant hears and the sight by which he sees, then the heart at the center of that servant can no longer be regarded only in its human, relative dimension. Its inmost essence — its secret, its sirr — pertains in truth to the divine, and it is this alone that can see as God sees, and ultimately can "see" God at all. As one Sufi master put it, none sees Him to whom nothing is similar but Him to whom nothing is similar. If God enters even into the human acts of outward perception, then the supreme act of spiritual awareness — the vision of God — is, in the final reckoning, not a vision of God by man but a vision of God by God, effected through the servant's heart rather than by it. This is the sense the Imam compressed into three words: "Know God through God." In the last analysis it is God who both sees and is seen; and one esoteric reading of the Qur'anic phrase "by a witnesser and that which is witnessed" (chapter 85, verse 3) takes both the witnesser and the witnessed to be God Himself, the human witness effaced in his very witnessing, so that there is no difference between the two except in the way we happen to speak of it.

There is a further consequence, latent in the sermon we began from, that belongs here. When the Imam described the people who belong to the remembrance, he did not stop at saying they are undistracted by trade and merchandise; he said that they spend the days of their life in it, and that it is as though they had left this world for the Hereafter, and are there, witnessing what is beyond this world. The seeing of the cleared heart is thus not an occasional flash granted in a moment of devotion and then withdrawn; in those who have made remembrance the whole occupation of their days, it becomes a settled condition, a standing residence of consciousness in a reality other than the one their bodies inhabit. They are, in the sermon's astonishing phrase, already witnessing what is beyond — living more in that world than in this one, though they walk about in this one. And this is only the reasoning of the polish carried to its conclusion: if the film of forgetfulness is what confines a person to the surface of things, then its removal does not merely let him glimpse the depth on occasion but lets him dwell in the depth continuously, so that the surface itself becomes transparent to what lies behind it. The vision the heart attains, in other words, is meant to become the medium a person lives in and not a spectacle he now and then observes — which is precisely why the tradition can speak of it as a mode of being and not only a mode of knowing.

That last thought reaches ahead of us — to the effacement of the self that is the summit of this whole path, and that we shall reach only at the end. For now it is enough to have established the ground: that the heart is an organ of vision, that remembrance is the polish that clears it, and that what the cleared heart finally sees, it sees by a light that is not its own. The chapters to come will follow this outward, into the world the cleared heart beholds, and then inward, to the fate of the self that beholds it.

## What this episode lands

This opening episode has laid the foundation on which the rest of the book's argument stands. We have seen what remembrance means — the rendering-present to consciousness of what was known and forgotten, at once an awareness and a discipline, at once the end and the means. We have seen why the whole tradition, in a rare unanimity, sets it above every other act of worship, calling it greater than the prayer that guards against sin; and why that same tradition, Sunni and Shi'i alike, gathers around the figure of Ali (ع) as the first pole of the mystical path and the friend of God par excellence, the one point on which its many chains converge. We have taken up the image that carries the doctrine — remembrance as a polish for the hearts, by which they hear after deafness and see after blindness — and seen that it names not the acquisition of something new but the clearing of a mirror already made to reflect the Real. And we have looked hard at the organ so cleared: the spiritual heart, which is neither reason nor sense but a mode of vision, and which, once purified, sees God by a light that is finally God's own.

What remains open is everything the cleared heart now beholds, and everything that becomes of the one who beholds. If the heart purified by remembrance can see God, the immediate question is where it sees Him — and the answer, startling on its face, is: everywhere, in all things, and yet without the least reduction of God to the things in which He is seen. That is the paradox the next episode takes up: the divine immanence that fills the whole of creation, and the veil that creation seems to be, which the Imam says in the end does not veil God at all. And it is worth carrying one caution across the threshold. The heart's vision of God everywhere will never mean, in this teaching, that God is reduced to the things in which He is found; the same tradition that says the cleared heart sees God in all things says with equal force that nothing whatever is comparable to Him. Holding those two together — immanence without diminishment, presence without confinement — is the whole difficulty and the whole beauty of what comes next. It is a difficulty the reasoning mind cannot resolve and the polished heart does not so much resolve as simply see, and that difference between resolving and seeing will turn out to be the difference on which everything in the coming episodes depends. The polish has cleared the mirror; now we turn to what the mirror shows.
