This episode closes the letter to Malik al-Ashtar by turning to the one practice on which the Imam rests the whole edifice of just rule: prayer. We follow how drawing near to God in devotion becomes the source from which justice overflows into action, how the remembrance of God polishes the heart until it can see, and how this spiritual method supplies precisely what Plato left unspoken when he spoke of gazing upon the Good.

## Where this episode picks up

What if the entire architecture of just governance — every measured judgment, every fair appointment, every restraint of a ruler's own hand — rested in the end on something done not in the council chamber or the courtroom but alone, in the dead of night, when no subject is watching and no ledger records it? That is the claim the Imam builds toward across the whole of his letter, and it is where he now arrives: the source of justice is prayer.

Across the earlier stretches of this letter we watched the Imam counsel Malik on the concrete machinery of rule — how to weigh the classes of society against one another, how to choose officials and judges, how to hold merchants and soldiers and the common people each in their proper place, how to keep a ruler's own appetites from corrupting the trust placed in him. All of that was the outward face of justice, the placing of each thing in its right order in the visible world. But the Imam has never let that outward face stand on its own. He has kept returning, beneath every practical instruction, to a single interior conviction: that the capacity to place things rightly in the world depends on a prior rightness in the soul, and that this rightness is not manufactured by good intentions or clever policy but received, drawn down from its source.

Now, having stressed that every administrative task must be performed at its proper time, the Imam does something that would surprise a purely political reader. He tells Malik to set apart not the leftover scraps of his time but the most excellent portion of it for what he calls "what is between you and God" — for acts of worship and devotion. He singles out the obligatory prayers as the means by which the ruler purifies his religion for God, renders his devotion sincere. And this, the essay proposes, is arguably the lynchpin of the entire epistle: the single most important means by which the sacred substance of all the virtues is actually assimilated by the one who must govern. Everything the letter has said about justice converges here, on a practice that looks, from the outside, like the least political thing a ruler could do.

## Drawing near in prayer

To understand why prayer occupies this pivotal place, we have to take the Imam's language about "purifying" one's religion in its deep sense, not its surface one. On the surface, purifying one's religion for God means performing the prescribed prayers sincerely rather than for show. But the Imam means something that reaches far beneath the ritual form. To purify one's religion for God is to purify the very substance of the relationship between the soul and God — because it is the depth of that relationship, and nothing else, which determines the quality of all human virtue. Justice, generosity, patience, mercy: none of these can rise higher than the soul's actual nearness to their source. A virtue is only as real as the relationship that feeds it. And so the Imam gives Malik a sentence that turns the whole of practical ethics on a devotional hinge: "Know that your every act is dependent upon your prayer." Your every action, in other words, depends upon the way you gaze upon God, who is the source of all good. Right action does not generate itself; it flows from a right vision, and that vision is cultivated in prayer or nowhere.

It is worth noticing that this is not the first time prayer has appeared in the letter. The Imam opened the whole epistle with a counsel to observe the obligatory prayers, and now, near its end, he returns to them with fresh weight — as though the entire body of practical instruction lying between the two mentions were deliberately framed by devotion at either end. Prayer is the letter's first word of counsel and one of its last, and everything about governance is set within that frame. The obligatory prayers are singled out because they are the fixed, recurring points at which the ruler is compelled to leave the business of rule and stand, unwatched and defenseless, before the one to whom he himself must answer. In a life crowded with the demands of power, they are the appointed interruptions that keep the source in view — and a ruler who let them slip would be, in the Imam's terms, cutting himself off from the wellspring of the very justice he is charged with administering.

This is why the Imam is emphatic that what matters is not merely the formal, physical, and verbal performance of the prayer. The bodily motions and the recited words are indispensable, but they are the vessel, not the wine. What gives prayer its power is the inward quality that accompanies it and enlivens it from within — the sincerity, the intensity, and the frequency with which it is offered. He presses Malik toward this inward fullness in words of striking physicality: "Give unto God of your vital energy in your nights and your days, and perform fully that by which you draw near to God, doing so perfectly, without becoming dull or deficient, taking your body to its limits." Notice how far this is from a request for a few dutiful minutes. It asks for one's vital energy, spent across nights and days, carried to the very limits of the body — a total giving, not a scheduled observance.

All the emphasis in this passage falls on one word: drawing near, taqarrub. This is the heart of the matter. One attains divine nearness most directly through deep devotion, offered up alone, in the dead of night, with the whole of one's heart. And here is the turn on which everything depends: once that nearness is realized, all of one's other actions in the world become imbued with something of the spiritual quality gained through that proximity. Nearness is not a private consolation sealed off from public life. It overflows. If a person's inner life comes to be dominated by prayer, prayer begins to penetrate the outer life as well, until every ordinary action is quietly governed by the intention to give oneself to God. This is why the Imam can say, immediately after telling Malik to reserve the best of his time for God, that "all times and the actions performed therein are for God if the intention underlying them is good and if your subjects derive security as a consequence." A ruler's every act partakes of the sacred exactly to the degree that his devotion truly reaches into the source of the sacred. One can picture it as an overflow, a spilling-out, from the fountain of prayer into the whole domain of action.

There is a precise logic to this overflow, and it repays a moment's attention, because it is easy to hear the Imam's counsel as a demand that a busy ruler simply pile more hours of ritual onto an already impossible schedule. That is not what he is describing. He is describing a change in the center of gravity of a whole life. When prayer genuinely dominates a person's inner life, it does not stay sealed inside the hours set apart for it; it seeps outward, coloring the intention behind ordinary acts until the boundary between devotion and work begins to dissolve. The ruler who has given God the most excellent portion of his time does not thereby have less of himself left for his subjects — he has more, because what he now brings to them carries something of the quality gathered in those hours of nearness. Their security, the Imam says, is itself the sign that the intention beneath his actions is sound. The private practice and the public good are not competitors for a fixed store of hours; the first is the hidden spring of the second, and a governance drawn from that spring is qualitatively different from one that is not.

The Imam grounds this vision of overflow in one of the most luminous of the Prophet's sayings, a saying in which God speaks in the first person — often called the hadith of drawing near. In it God declares: "My slave draws near to Me through nothing I love more than that which I have made obligatory for him. My slave never ceases to draw near to Me through supererogatory acts until I love him. And when I love him, I am his hearing by which he hears, his sight by which he sees, his hand by which he grasps, and his foot by which he walks." Sit with the astonishing claim at the end. The one who persists in drawing near is finally so transformed that God becomes the very faculty of his perception and action — the hearing by which he hears, the sight by which he sees, the hand and the foot by which he acts in the world. To see and act through God in this way means, at one level at least, to see things as they truly are and then to act upon that vision. And to place each thing according to what it truly is — that is nothing other than perfect justice. This is why the Imam so persistently draws the reader of his sermons back to the vision of God and sets that vision as the foundation of a life of virtue. In prayer the ego is effaced through prostration, and that very submission, that self-effacement, is what allows all things to fall into their right places — the ego first among them.

Two features of the saying deserve to be weighed. The first is its distinction between the obligatory and the supererogatory acts of devotion. Both are means of drawing near — God loves nothing more, He says, than what He has made obligatory. Yet the saying goes on to describe a further and continuous drawing-near through the voluntary, supererogatory acts, and it is chiefly through these that the slave comes at last to perceive and to act through the divine. The obligatory establishes the relationship; the freely offered devotion, undertaken beyond all requirement, is what deepens it toward that intimacy in which God becomes the worshipper's own sight and hand. The second feature is the Imam's insistence on effort — "taking your body to its limits." Strenuous exertion is called for. It is nowhere near enough to perform the prayers mechanically, absent-mindedly, as a box to be checked. "He who prays without making an effort," the Imam says, "is like one who shoots arrows without a bow." The image is exact and unforgiving: motion without the instrument that gives it force, gesture without the tension that sends anything toward its mark. The sincerity that the Imam's whole conception of God's oneness demands requires the integration of the soul, and it is precisely in prayer that the deepest dimensions of the soul are expressed, cultivated, and drawn together into one. The prayer must therefore be accomplished not with the tongue alone, nor with the body alone, but with all that one is.

This is also why the Imam speaks of sincerity as the demand that his whole understanding of God's oneness places upon the worshipper. To affirm that God is one is not merely to hold a doctrine; it is to be summoned toward a corresponding unity in oneself — to gather the scattered energies and divided attentions of the soul into a single orientation. A fragmented soul cannot offer a sincere prayer, because sincerity is precisely the undividedness of the one who prays. And so prayer is not only the expression of an integration already achieved but the very workshop in which that integration is forged. Each time the whole of a person is brought to bear on the act of drawing near, the soul is knit a little more tightly into one. The strenuous effort the Imam demands — the arrows loosed from a real bow, the body carried to its limits — is the labor of that knitting, without which the affirmation of oneness stays a word on the tongue rather than a reality in the heart. Prayer, in this light, is where the ruler becomes a whole enough person to be trusted with the ordering of others.

## The polish of remembrance

If prayer in general is the fountain, the Imam identifies a particular practice at its heart — the supererogatory devotion by which the station of nearness is most fully attained. He does so in a commentary on a phrase from the Qur'an describing certain men "whom neither commerce nor trade diverts from the remembrance of God" (chapter 24, verse 36). Of this remembrance the Imam says something extraordinary: "Truly God has made the remembrance a polish for the hearts, by which they hear after suffering from deafness, and see after being blind. There have always been slaves of God with whom He held intimate discourse through their thoughts and spoke with them through the essence of their intellects." The governing image is polishing. The heart is like a mirror or a blade dulled and clouded by neglect; remembrance burnishes it until it recovers senses it had lost — hearing after deafness, sight after blindness. And to the polished heart God speaks, not from outside through the ear, but inwardly, through the person's own thoughts, through the very essence of the intellect.

The remainder of the Imam's description deepens this picture of inward speech. He tells of slaves of God through whom illumination was diffused — an awakened light kindled in their hearing, their seeing, and their hearts — who then called others to the remembrance of the days of God. The remembrance, then, is not a private glow sealed inside a solitary worshipper; it radiates. The heart that has been polished until it hears and sees becomes itself a source of light for others, so that the recovered senses of one soul awaken the senses of many. There is a transmissive dimension to remembrance: those who remember become, in turn, those who call others to remember. And what God grants such souls is described in the most intimate terms the Imam could choose — He holds discourse with them through their thoughts and speaks to them through the essence of their intellects. The distance between Creator and creature, without ever being abolished, becomes a place of conversation. This is already a portrait of the kind of soul the letter to Malik has been reaching toward all along: not merely a competent administrator but a person through whom something of the divine light passes into the world he governs.

This remembrance, dhikr, carries a double meaning in the Imam's usage, and both are essential. On one side it names a methodic practice — the deliberate, repeated invocation of a divine Name, an intense and prolonged prayerful meditation on ultimate reality, focused through one or more of the Names of God. On the other side it names a principle of permanent awareness of God that embraces every mode of worship, meditation, and reflection. Dhikr is thus both the concentrated act and the abiding condition — both the flame and the warmth that fills the room. What must not be missed is the peculiar character of what this remembrance recovers. It is not the acquisition of new information, not the learning of something from scratch. It is recollection — the drawing back into awareness of ultimate reality, of the immutable, unchanging principles and divine attributes that already lie inherent in the very substance of our intellects, by which everything else can be understood, and which are ultimately rooted in the divine nature of our Creator. Earlier in this series we opened up those buried principles of the intellect, and the difference between the intellect as a spiritual organ and reason as a lesser instrument; here it is enough to recall that remembrance works upon what is already there, uncovering rather than importing.

Because remembrance uncovers rather than imports, it stands in a peculiar relation to time. Ordinary learning moves forward, accumulating what it did not have before; remembrance moves, in a sense, downward and backward, toward a reality that was never absent, only forgotten or obscured. The polish does not lay a new surface upon the mirror; it strips away what had dulled the surface the mirror always had. This is why the Imam can describe the recovery of hearing and sight as the return of faculties suffered to lapse into deafness and blindness, rather than the gift of senses never possessed. The immutable principles and divine attributes are already inherent in the substance of the intellect; the whole labor of remembrance is to clear away what keeps them from being seen. And this is precisely what makes remembrance available to a ruler in the thick of public life as much as to a recluse in his cell: what it recovers is not stored in some distant place one must journey to, but lodged already in the depths of the one who remembers. The busiest governor and the most secluded contemplative are digging in the same ground — their own hearts.

Now comes the point that binds this whole essay together — the tie between remembrance and justice. The Imam describes those who have been illuminated by the dhikr in words that make the connection unmistakable: "Truly there are people who belong to the dhikr; they have adopted it in place of the world, such that neither commerce nor trade distracts them from it; they spend the days of their life in it; they instruct people about justice and themselves are steadfast therein." Read that closing clause slowly, because it is the hinge of the argument. It is those souls who truly belong to the remembrance who are both steadfast in justice themselves and able to instruct others in it. The two are not accidentally paired. Putting first things first — placing God at the center through remembrance — is precisely what equips a person to place everything else in its proper order, which is what justice is. The one who has learned to put the highest thing first can teach others how to order all the rest.

There is a quiet but important claim buried in that pairing of steadfastness and instruction. It would be possible to imagine a just person who could not teach justice — someone who acted rightly by instinct or habit but could give no account of it, and could pass nothing on. The Imam does not describe such a person. The souls who belong to the remembrance are steadfast in justice and able to instruct others in it, and the two capacities share a single root. Because their justice is not a private knack but a participation in the order of the Real itself, they can articulate it, embody it, and reproduce it in others. Remembrance does not merely make a person good; it makes goodness communicable, because it grounds that goodness in a reality that is the same for everyone and can therefore be shown. This is exactly the ruler the letter has been trying to form — not one who happens to govern well, but one who can hand on the principle of good governance because he has touched its source.

This overturns a suspicion that runs deep in the modern mind: that fervent, inward spirituality must come at the expense of effective action in the world, that the contemplative and the just administrator pull in opposite directions. The Imam's portrait says the reverse. Lived spirituality is not opposed to dedicated activity in the world; it infuses that activity with an ethical depth that springs from direct contact with the divine — a contact articulated by prayer in general and by remembrance in particular. And just as the sincerity of a good intention is proven only by the sustained effort to realize it, so the sincerity of prayer is proven, and made effective, only through the sustained effort to prolong and intensify it — again, "taking your body to its limits." The steadfastness in justice and the steadfastness in remembrance are one discipline seen from two sides.

Why should nearness to God draw a person toward justice in particular, rather than leaving devotion and fairness as separate goods that happen to coexist in a pious life? The answer lies in what justice ultimately is. If justice is grasped as a quality rooted in the Real, in al-Haqq, in God as the ground of all that is true and right, then nearness to the Real cannot but draw one nearer to justice — and, with it, nearer to every positive quality, spiritual, intellectual, and moral. Justice is not a rule external to God that a devout person also happens to keep; it is a radiance of God's own nature, and to approach that nature is to be drawn into its light.

This is worth stating carefully, because it changes what we should expect a just person to look like. If justice were merely a set of correct rules, then a just ruler would be, above all, someone who knew the rules and applied them without fear or favor — a skilled and impartial technician of the law. The Imam's vision does not deny that competence, but it locates the deep source of justice elsewhere. The truly just ruler is the one whose soul has been brought near enough to the Real that its own ordering has begun to mirror the order of the Real, so that justice issues from him less as a policy applied than as a quality expressed. He places things rightly in the world because he has first been rightly placed himself — effaced before God in prostration, his own ego set in its proper and subordinate order. Outward justice is the overflow of an inward rectitude, and inward rectitude is the fruit of nearness. Take away the nearness, and what remains is at best a competent mimicry of justice, liable to bend the moment self-interest makes bending attractive. This is what allows the Imam's conception of justice to be called sacred in the fullest sense, and it means the relationship between worship and justice must be firmly established rather than left to chance. From this vantage, justice turns out to be at once the cause and the consequence of worship. It is a cause, because justice requires that everything be given its due, and God above all must be given His due — which, sacramentally, means worship and prayer. And it is a consequence, because justice emerges ethically out of that worship, as do all the virtues, in exact proportion to the nearness God grants His worshipper and to the radiant repercussions that nearness sends out into the world. Worship generates justice; justice demands worship; and the circle turns without end.

## Gazing on the Good

Throughout this essay the Imam's teaching on justice has been set now and then beside a very different source — Plato's Republic — and it is by returning to that comparison that the whole discussion can be brought to its close. The proposal is a bold one: that what the Imam offers in his stress on prayer, and on the remembrance of God at its core, is a devotional principle that illuminates and even sublimates Plato's philosophical position — that it renders explicit, in spiritual terms, precisely what remains implicit and unspoken in Plato's pedagogy. To see the force of this, we have to watch Plato arrive at the threshold of the very thing the Imam supplies, and then stop short of it.

After examining justice at enormous length, Socrates comes at last to the crux of the whole dialogue: the nature of the Sovereign Good, the supreme concern of the ideal ruler, the guardian whose moral and intellectual formation is the practical heart of the Republic. Socrates puts it in the highest possible terms — that "the highest object of knowledge is the essential nature of the Good, from which everything that is good and right derives its value for us," and that without this knowledge, to know everything else, however well, would be of no value at all. Everything, then, hangs on knowing the Good. And yet, at the decisive moment, Socrates declines to say what the Good actually is. Pressed by Glaucon to describe it, he offers instead to speak only of its offspring, and draws his famous analogy between the Good and the Sun: what the Sun is to visibility — the light that lets the eye see and the seen thing be seen — the Good is to intelligibility and to being itself. He follows this with the great allegory of the cave, the prisoners turning from the shadows on the wall toward the real objects and finally toward the sun outside. And he lays out the long educational program that is meant to turn the soul from the world of shadows to the world of real things: mathematics, arithmetic, geometry, solid geometry, astronomy, harmonics, and at the summit, dialectic. Only those who pass through all of it, and prove the best at every stage in both action and study, are brought at fifty to the goal. In Plato's own words, they "must lift up the eye of the soul to gaze on that which sheds light on all things; and when they have seen the Good itself, take it as a pattern for the right ordering of the state and of the individual, themselves included."

Here we stand at the very edge of the Imam's territory — and here Plato falls silent. For nowhere does he say what this gazing actually is, nor by what means it is to be achieved: whether it is intellective, contemplative, or methodic, what a soul must do to lift up its eye and hold that light. The whole apparatus of the Republic ascends to this summit and then names it without disclosing how it is climbed. The gap becomes explicit at another point, when Glaucon presses Socrates directly: "But, Socrates, what is your account of the Good? Is it knowledge, or pleasure, or something else?" Socrates refuses a direct answer, and after some discussion confesses: "I am afraid it is beyond my powers; with the best will in the world I should only disgrace myself and be laughed at." The highest object of knowledge, the thing on which all value depends, is left undefined by the one who has insisted on its supremacy.

Set beside this exchange a scene from the life of the Imam, and the contrast becomes luminous. A man named Dhi'lib al-Yamani, very much in the provocative manner of Glaucon, asked Ali (ع) whether he had actually seen the object of his devotion. The Imam replied, "I would not be worshipping a lord whom I have not seen." Dhi'lib, startled, pressed exactly as Glaucon had pressed Socrates: "O Commander of the Faithful! How did you see Him?" And where Socrates had said the matter was beyond his powers, the Imam answered — with a sentence that serves as one of the leitmotifs of this whole book: "O Dhi'lib! Eyes see Him not through sight's observation, but hearts see Him through the verities of faith." Where the philosopher demurred, the Imam gave a direct account of a real seeing — not the seeing of the physical eye, which cannot reach God, but the seeing of the heart through the realities of faith. That the heart and not the eye is the organ by which God is known has run through this series as a touchstone, and it returns here as the exact answer to the question Plato could not bring himself to answer.

The parallel between the two scenes is almost uncanny, and the essay does not shrink from pressing it. Glaucon and Dhi'lib ask, in effect, the same question — show me the highest thing, the reality on which everything else depends. Socrates, the master of dialectic, the philosopher who has led his companions up the entire ladder of the sciences, confesses that the summit is beyond his powers to describe. The Imam, put to the same question by a similarly provocative questioner, answers without hesitation, because for him the highest reality is not the terminus of an argument that finally outruns the arguer, but the living object of a devotion that has actually reached its goal. The difference is not that the Imam is quicker or cleverer than Socrates. It is that he stands on the far side of a threshold Socrates could only point toward — the threshold of a seeing that belongs to the heart, reached by a method the philosopher never named. Socrates knew there was a summit; the Imam had climbed it.

The convergence goes deeper still, and turns precisely on the image of polishing. The remembrance that polishes the heart until it sees — this evokes Plato's own account of knowledge as recollection, as anamnesis, the drawing back into awareness of what the soul already, at its depth, possesses. For remembrance in the Imam's sense is not the learning of anything new from scratch; it is the recollection of ultimate reality, of the immutable divine principles and attributes inherent in the very substance of our intellects, by which everything else can be understood. Plato and the Imam meet on this ground: that the highest knowing is a recovering rather than an acquiring. But Plato, having grasped that knowledge is recollection, never named the devotional, methodic, or contemplative counterpart to his great educational and intellectual endeavor. He described the ascent through the sciences to the threshold of the Good, and he described knowledge as recollection — but he never joined the two, never said by what inward practice the soul comes not merely to remember the forms but to know the Bestower of forms, the source that gives all forms their reality. In the language of Islam that source bears a divine Name — al-Musawwir, the Fashioner, the one who bestows form upon all things — and it is one thing to remember the forms, quite another to come to know the One who fashions them. Plato reached the first and gestured toward the second, but he left the passage between them dark.

It has long been asked whether Plato in fact possessed some spiritual method that he deliberately withheld, and, if he did, why he chose to keep it unspoken. There is even a hint, from Plato's own hand, of why he stopped where he did. In his Seventh Letter he denies that the true reality of philosophy can be set out in any exposition at all, and points instead to a disciplined way of life, a rigorous daily routine, without which philosophy in its deepest sense cannot be realized. "No treatise by me concerning it exists or ever will exist," he writes. "It is not something that can be put into words like other branches of learning; only after long partnership in a common life devoted to this very thing does truth flash upon the soul, like a flame kindled by a leaping spark, and once it is born there, it nourishes itself thereafter." That flame kindled by a leaping spark, that truth flashing on the soul after long devotion to a shared way of life — Plato senses the reality but leaves it nameless, unmethodical, a gesture toward something he will not or cannot specify. And this is exactly the emptiness the Imam fills. If the Real, indefinable in its essence, is the foundation of all good, then the means of assimilating the Real existentially — not merely comprehending it mentally — cannot be set aside as optional. Plato's philosophical approach to the Good can be taken up, augmented, and deepened by the Imam's method of drawing near to the Real, in which intellectual comprehension and spiritual devotion are not two enterprises but one, inseparable and complementary. And integral, deep-rooted justice emerges as one of the fruits of that drawing-near — just as, for Plato, the gazing on that which sheds light on all things was to yield the capacity to take the Good as the pattern for the right ordering of the state and the soul. The Imam gives the ascent the one thing Plato left out: the way up.

## What this episode lands

The whole arc of the letter to Malik now stands revealed in a single line of vision. Justice is sacred because it is not a human convention laid over the world but a radiance of God's own nature — a quality rooted in the Real. And because it is God's nature, it cannot be manufactured chiefly by rules, procedures, or good policy, however necessary those remain. It is assimilated, drawn down into a soul, through prayer and through the remembrance at prayer's heart — the drawing-near in which the ego is effaced and the heart is polished until, in the words of the Prophet's saying, God becomes the very sight by which the worshipper sees and the hand by which he acts. From that nearness justice overflows into the world, so that a ruler's every act partakes of the sacred to the exact degree that his devotion reaches into the source of the sacred. This is why justice is at once the cause of worship and its consequence: it demands that God be given His due, and it is born, in turn, as the fruit of that giving. The person steadfast in remembrance is the person steadfast in justice, and able to teach it. With this the essay on the Imam's letter to Malik al-Ashtar comes to its close — not on a maxim of statecraft but on a fountain of devotion, from which alone, in the Imam's vision, a truly just order can flow.

## Closing

Yet the letter's final word is not really an ending but a door left open. Everything it has said about prayer and remembrance points beyond the practical question of how to govern toward a deeper question it has only begun to raise: what, exactly, is this remembrance meant to accomplish in the depths of a human soul? We have seen that dhikr polishes the heart, that it recovers what already lies buried in the substance of the intellect, that God speaks to His servants through their own thoughts and the essence of their intellects. But we have watched it here chiefly through its fruit in the world — through justice, through the right ordering of a ruler's actions. What remains is to turn from the fruit to the root, from remembrance as the source of just governance to remembrance as spiritual realization in itself: the inner transformation it intends and cultivates, the knowing of the Real that it exists to bring about. That is the ground the next chapter takes up, following the remembrance inward, past its outward consequences, to the realization it was always reaching toward.
