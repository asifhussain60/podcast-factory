# Letter to Malik: The Classes

*This episode completes the reading of Ali (ع)'s mandate to Malik al-Ashtar begun in the previous one. Where the first half formed the ruler's soul and named the instruments of his authority, the second half descends to the ground those instruments are meant to serve — the cultivators whose land feeds the state, the scribes and the merchants, and, in the passage the Imam most wants heard, the destitute who can never repay a ruler's care — and then turns back to the governor's own conduct: how he keeps his time and his prayer, how he holds a covenant once given, how he guards his hands against unlawful blood and his heart against the flattery that effaces a good man's works. It is heard whole, in refined English, and it ends where the letter itself ends: not with an instruction but with a prayer the Imam offers for them both.*

## Where this episode picks up

Where in a book about the soul of a just ruler should the beggar belong — the man who holds no office, commands no soldiers, pays no tax, and can do nothing whatever for the governor in return? The letter's answer is one of the most telling things about it. In its survey of the classes, the destitute come last, after the army and the scribes, the judges and the merchants; and yet, when the Imam reaches them, his measured administrative prose breaks open into a doubled cry of the divine name, as though the whole letter had been travelling toward this class all along. Last in the order, first in the concern — the placement is deliberate, and it is the hinge on which the second half of the letter turns.

The first half of the letter had done its work on the ruler from the inside, forming his fear of God and his mercy and his humility, and had set around him the men who wield authority in his name — the commander, the judge, the administrator. What remains is everything that authority exists for. The letter now goes down to the productive base of the whole society and to the people at its very bottom, and then, having reached the lowest, it climbs back up through the ruler's own daily life — his hours, his prayers, his favourites, his enemies, his hands — to end on the two things the Imam most fears a man in power will betray: a covenant given, and innocent blood. As before, we hear the document itself rather than a lesson drawn from it, and much of what it teaches the book has already unfolded; the recitation lets it be heard in its own order and voice. But the second half also carries counsel the commentary passed over, and it is worth listening for it as it comes.

Three movements carry the episode. We begin by drawing out what is fresh and unmistakable in this half of the letter — its radical priority of cultivation over revenue, its change of register at the poor, and the counsel on covenants and bloodshed and self-admiration that the book's teaching did not stop to expound. We then hear the second half of the letter itself, whole and unbroken, from the care of the land to the closing prayer. And we close by listening to how the letter ends — with the ruler turned back upon his own soul, and with a supplication that folds both men, the one who wrote it and the one who received it, into a single hope.

## The document's turn to the poor

Three things stand out in this half of the letter as genuinely its own, unremarked in the book's earlier teaching, and it helps to have them in view before the recitation carries them past. The first is a maxim about taxation so plain it is easy to underestimate: let your concern with the cultivation of the land outweigh your concern with the collection of the tax, for no tax is gathered where there is no cultivation, and whoever exacts the tax without nurturing the land ruins both the land and the people. It inverts the ordinary instinct of every treasury. Revenue is a fruit and cultivation is the root, and a ruler who devours the root to feed on the fruit will soon have neither. The Imam presses further into the spiritual diagnosis behind ruinous taxation — the desire to amass, the doubt about what endures — but the practical instruction stands on its own as one of the quietly radical sentences in the whole document.

The second is the change of register at the poor, and it must be heard rather than described. Through the survey of the classes the Imam keeps an even, administrative tone — a class named, a duty stated, the duty sealed with its spiritual point. Then he reaches those with no resources at all, the destitute, the crippled, the orphaned, the elderly, and the prose breaks: "O God, O God," he cries, before charging Malik with the rights of the ones "most in need of justice from you." He would have the governor set aside a share of the treasury and of the common wealth for them, appoint a trusted, God-fearing officer to seek out the needs of those too proud to beg, and treat them so that God may excuse him on the day they meet. The care of people from whom no political benefit can ever be drawn is, by any worldly measure, a losing venture — which is exactly why the Imam grounds it not in calculation but in the aspiration for the Hereafter, and why the book heard in this passage the sharpest edge of its sacred conception of justice. Here that passage is heard in its own setting, with the cry still in it.

The third is a cluster of counsel the book's teaching left almost untouched, and it belongs to the ruler's own conduct rather than to the classes. There is the astonishing weight the Imam places on keeping a pledge: honour the covenant of protection with complete faithfulness, he tells Malik, make your very life a shield for what you have promised, for among all God's obligations there is none on which people more firmly unite than the honouring of pledges — even the idolaters kept faith among themselves, so greatly did they dread the consequences of treachery. The point is more radical than it first sounds. The Imam is telling a governor that a promise once given binds him even when circumstance turns hard and breaking it would be convenient; that patience under a difficulty whose relief he still awaits is better than a treachery whose reckoning he will dread; and that the sanctity of a pledge is one of the few things on which all peoples, believing and unbelieving alike, have always agreed, so that to betray it is to fall below even those who never knew the faith. There is the grave warning against unlawful bloodshed, of which the Imam says that nothing so swiftly invites retribution or so surely ends a blessing, and with which God will begin His very judgement among His servants on the Day of Resurrection — a governor who imagines that spilled blood secures his rule is told, in the plainest terms, that it does the opposite, undermining and finally toppling the authority it was meant to protect.

There is the counsel on peace — never turn away from a call to peace in which God's pleasure lies, for in it lies rest for the soldiers and security for the land, yet stay fully on guard once you have made it, holding even your own good opinion of the enemy under suspicion. And there is the closing discipline of the self, in which the letter turns its whole apparatus of government back upon the single soul that runs it: beware the self-admiration and the love of flattery through which Satan finds his surest opening to efface the works of the virtuous; beware laying people under obligation for the good you do them, and inflating your own deeds, and promising what you fail to fulfil; put every matter in its proper place and every task at its proper time; and master the heat of your pride and the edge of your tongue by holding back the blow until your anger has passed — which, the Imam says, you will never do unless you increase your remembrance of your return to your Lord. What follows is the second half of the letter, in refined English, as the Imam set it down.

## The classes and the conduct

What follows is the second half of the letter, in refined English, as Ali (ع) set it down for Malik.

Look into the matter of the land-tax in such a way that those subject to it are kept in a proper state of well-being, for it is from their welfare, and from the sound collection of the tax itself, that the good of all others is drawn. There can be no welfare of the rest except through them, since all without exception depend upon the revenues of the land and upon those who pay them. Let your concern with the cultivation of the land outweigh your concern with the collection of the tax, for no tax will be gathered where there is no cultivation. Whoever exacts the tax without cultivating the land ruins the land and destroys the people, and his rule will not endure for long. So if the people complain of a heavy burden, or of a blight, or of the cutting off of irrigation, or of a lack of rain, or of a change in the condition of the soil from flooding or drought, then lighten their burden in the measure you hope will set their situation right. Do not let any relief you grant them weigh heavily upon you, for it is an investment that will return to you in the prosperity of your land, the adornment of your rule, and the finest praise you reap from them, and in the pride you take in dispensing justice among them. You may then rely upon an increase in their capacity as a result of what you invested in them when you gave them respite, and you may trust them, having accustomed them to your justice and your kindness. The devastation of the land comes about only through the destitution of its inhabitants, and the destitution of its inhabitants comes about only when the desire to amass wealth rules the souls of the governors, when they have doubts about what endures, and when they profit little from exemplary teachings.

Now consider with care the situation of your scribes. Appoint the best of them to manage your affairs. Assign those letters containing the most sensitive strategies and secrets to the scribe in whom you find the most comprehensive moral qualities: one who will not be emboldened by his elevated status to contradict you in public; who is not negligent in delivering to you the correspondence of your officials and properly despatching your replies; who takes and gives on your behalf with care. He must not weaken any agreement contracted for you, nor be incapable of repudiating what has been contracted to your detriment. He must not be ignorant of the value of his own capabilities, for one who is unaware of his own worth will be even more ignorant of the worth of others. Do not make your selection solely on the basis of your own instinct, confidence, and trust, for men know how to present themselves to rulers through affectation and good service. Rather, test them by what they did in the service of the good people who came before you, and appoint the one whose virtue left the deepest impression upon the common people and whose reputation for integrity is most widely known. This will be proof of your sincerity toward God and toward the one on whose behalf you have been appointed to this office, for you will be putting the right man in the right place. Appoint for each of your affairs a head officer among your officials, one who will not be daunted by the magnitude of affairs nor scattered by their multiplicity. Any fault of your scribes which you have overlooked will be ascribed to you.

Then attend to the merchants and the artisans, treating them well and urging the same on others — those of fixed abode, those who travel with their goods, and those who earn their livelihood with their hands. They are sources of benefit and the means by which conveniences are obtained. Through them, goods are procured from distant and remote places, brought by land and sea, from plains and mountains, and from places where men neither settle nor even dare to venture. They live in a state of reconciliation, so no calamity need be feared from them, and in a state of peace, so no disturbance need be expected. Examine their affairs attentively, both those close to you and those in every corner of your land. Yet be aware that, despite all this, many of them harbour a despicable stinginess, a repugnant greed, a tendency to hoard goods and to fix prices arbitrarily. All of this brings loss to the people and is a source of shame for governors. So prohibit hoarding, for the Messenger of God — God bless him and his family — prohibited it. Let trading be carried on with propriety and fairness, at prices that harm neither party, buyer nor seller. If any one of them succumbs to the temptation to hoard after you have prohibited it, inflict upon him an exemplary — but not excessive — punishment.

Then — O God, O God! — pay particular attention to the lowest class: those who have no wherewithal, the destitute, the needy, the afflicted, the disabled. Within this class are those who beg and those whose wretchedness calls out to be relieved though they do not beg. Be mindful of God in regard to their rights, for He has entrusted those rights to your care. Assign to them a portion from your public treasury and a portion of the produce of what is taken as booty by the Muslims in every region, for those who are furthest away have the same rights as those nearest, and upholding the right of each of them is incumbent upon you. Do not let any haughtiness on your part cause you to neglect them, for you will not be pardoned even the slightest shortcoming toward them on account of attending to some seemingly more important matter. Do not turn your concern away from them or assume a contemptuous attitude toward them. Keep a watchful eye over the affairs of those who cannot gain access to you and who are disdained by men of high standing. Appoint from among those you trust a God-fearing and humble person to bring their affairs to your attention. Treat these people in a manner such that God may excuse you on the day you meet Him, for they are more in need of justice from you than any others among your subjects. Offer your excuse to God in respect of fulfilling each one's right. Assume responsibility for the orphans and the elderly who have no resources and yet cannot bring themselves to beg. This is onerous for governors, and the fulfilment of all rights is onerous — but God makes it light for those who aspire to the Hereafter, who restrain their souls in patience, and trust in the truth of what God has promised them.

Apportion a part of your time to those who have special needs, making yourself free to attend to them personally, sitting with them in a public assembly in all due humility before God, your Creator. Keep your soldiers, guards, and officers away from them so that they may speak to you without inhibition, for I heard the Messenger of God say — God bless him and his family — on more than one occasion: "A nation in which the rights of the weak are not wrested in an uninhibited manner from the strong will never be blessed." Bear patiently any coarseness or inarticulate expression they might show; do not display irritation or disdain toward them — God will thereby extend His compassion to you in its fullest measure and make the reward for your obedience to Him binding. Give whatever you give with beneficence, and withhold, if this be unavoidable, with grace and apology. There are certain affairs which you must handle personally — among them, replying to your officers when your scribes are incapable of doing so, and issuing responses to the requests of people when they are brought to you in person because your aides find themselves at a loss on their account.

Perform each day the task proper to it, for to each day belongs a particular task. Set apart the finest portion of your available time — and the greatest share of it — for your soul and for what is between you and God, even though all your times and all your actions are for God, provided the intention underlying them is good and your subjects derive security as a consequence. Let your observance of those duties that relate exclusively to God be the special means by which you purify your religion for Him. Give unto God of your vital energy in your nights and your days, and perform fully that by which you draw near to God — doing so perfectly, without becoming dull or deficient, taking your body to its limit. When you lead the people in prayer, do so without repelling them or squandering it, for among them are people with infirmities and people with special needs. Indeed, I asked the Messenger of God — God bless him and his family — when he sent me as his representative to Yemen, "How should I lead them in prayer?" He replied: "Lead them according to the prayer performed by the weakest among them, and be merciful to the believers."

In addition to all of this, do not prolong any period of absence from your subjects, for the isolation of governors from those they govern creates a kind of constriction and breeds deficiency in awareness of their affairs. Such isolation cuts rulers off from acquiring knowledge of what is hidden from them, so that what is great comes to appear small and what is small, great; the beautiful appears ugly and the ugly, beautiful; the true becomes mixed with the false. The governor is but a human being: he cannot know what people conceal from him, and truth bears no visible mark by which sincere expressions may be distinguished from false ones. You can only be one of two kinds of men: either a man who gives himself generously for the sake of the truth — in which case, why seclude yourself, and so prevent yourself from bestowing a necessary right or performing an honourable deed? Or else you are a man afflicted with an unwillingness to give of himself — in which case, how quickly people will refrain from making requests of you, despairing of your generosity! And this, despite the fact that fulfilling most of the needs people bring to you is not burdensome, whether they be complaints against injustice or demands for fairness in transactions.

The governor has favourites and intimate companions, and among them some are prone to presumptuousness, arrogance, and unfairness in their dealings. Cut this out at the root by eliminating the causes of these vices, and by granting no landed estates to anyone in your entourage or circle of friends. Give them no reason to hope for such a grant; should they receive one, it would harm those living nearby in their access to water and in any shared undertaking, the burden of which would fall on the neighbours while the profit went to those holding the estate — not to you. What would fall to you is only the blame for it, in this world and the next. Impose what is right upon all to whom it applies, whether they are close to you or distant. Be steadfast and vigilant in this, however it may weigh upon your intimates and favourites, and always seek the end that this principle leads to, however heavily the path may lie upon you, for its outcome is praiseworthy. If any of your subjects suspect you of an injustice, present your case openly, so that the suspicion is cleared away from you by plain explanation. Such conduct is a discipline for yourself, a kindness to your subjects, and a means of pleading your cause that will help you keep them upright in adherence to the truth.

Never turn away from a call to peace with your enemy when God's pleasure lies in it, for in peace there is rest for your soldiers, relief from your cares, and security for your lands. Yet once you have made peace, remain fully on your guard against your enemy, for he may have drawn close only in order to catch you off guard; so take every precaution, and hold even your own favourable opinion of him under suspicion. Yet if you and your enemy enter into a solemn agreement, or he receives from you a covenant of protection, then keep your agreement faithfully and honour your obligation of protection with complete trustworthiness. Make your very life a shield for what you have promised, for among all the obligations God has laid down there is none on which people stand more firmly united — despite all the differences of their inclinations and the variety of their opinions — than the honouring of pledges. Even the idolaters observed it among themselves, quite apart from their dealings with the Muslims, so greatly did they fear the consequences of treachery. So do not betray your covenant of protection, do not break your promise, and do not deal treacherously with your enemy, for none dares defy God but the ignorant and the wretched. God has made His pledge and His protection a sanctuary of security, which by His mercy He has spread over His servants — a refuge in whose inviolable strength they find rest, and toward whose sheltering power they hasten. Let there be no corruption within it, no treachery, and no deception. Do not enter into any agreement that leaves room for evasion, and do not resort to twisting words once an agreement has been confirmed and made binding. Do not let any hardship that befalls you drive you to break, without right, an agreement God has made binding upon you; for patience under a difficulty whose relief and good outcome you await is far better than a treachery whose consequence you dread — that a reckoning from God should close in upon you from which you find no release in this world or the next.

Beware of shedding blood unlawfully, for nothing so swiftly invites retribution, weighs more heavily in its consequence, or is more apt to end a blessing and cut short a span of life, than bloodshed without right. On the Day of Resurrection, God — glorified be He — will begin His judgement among His servants with the blood they have shed. So do not seek to strengthen your authority through unlawful bloodshed, for that in truth weakens and undermines it — indeed, it brings it down and takes it away entirely. You have no excuse before God, nor before me, if you kill anyone deliberately, for that demands capital punishment. And if you are overtaken by an error, and your whip, your sword, or your hand goes too far in inflicting a penalty — for even a single blow, and whatever exceeds it, can bring about death — do not let the pride of your authority hold you back from paying the relatives of the slain what is rightfully theirs.

Beware of self-admiration, of leaning on whatever pleases you in yourself, and of loving to be flattered, for these are among the surest openings Satan finds to efface the good works of the virtuous. Beware of making your subjects feel indebted to you for the good you do them, of inflating your own deeds, and of promising what you then fail to fulfil; for laying people under obligation destroys the good itself, self-praise extinguishes the light of truth, and breaking a promise earns you the hatred of God and of men. God the Exalted has said: "It is indeed hateful to God that you say that which you do not do" (chapter 61, verse 3). Beware of rushing into matters before their time has come, and of letting them slip when the moment to act has arrived; of stubbornness when they grow tangled, and of slackness once they have become clear. Put every matter in its proper place, and attend to every task at its proper time. Beware of taking for yourself what belongs equally to all, and of feigning blindness to what is urgent and plain to every eye, for it will be taken from you for the benefit of others. Before long the coverings will be lifted from all affairs, and justice will be sought from you on behalf of the wronged. So master the heat of your pride, the sharpness of your temper, the force of your hand, and the edge of your tongue; guard against all of this by holding back the sudden impulse and delaying the blow until your anger has subsided and you have regained your self-command. You will never achieve this mastery over yourself, however, unless you increase your remembrance of your return to your Lord.

It is incumbent upon you to remember the just governance and noble custom that have come before you, the practice of the Prophet — God bless him and his family — and the obligations set forth in the Book of God. Follow faithfully what you have seen us do in these matters, and strive with all your soul to act upon what I have enjoined upon you in this mandate, by which I have made my plea complete against you, so that you will have no excuse when your soul races toward its caprice. I ask God, by the vastness of His mercy and the greatness of His power to grant every desire, that He bestow upon me and upon you the success that wins His pleasure — that we may present a clear justification before Him and before His creatures, earn the fairest praise among His servants, and leave behind the most beautiful traces in the land, with blessing made complete and honour ever increased; and that He seal my life and yours with felicity and with martyrdom. Truly unto Him we are returning (chapter 2, verse 156). And peace be upon the Messenger of God — God bless him and his good and pure progeny.

## The letter turned back on the soul

Heard whole, the second half of the letter makes a movement the fragments never showed: it goes all the way down to the man who owns nothing, and then it climbs all the way back up to the soul of the man who owns the province. The descent to the poor is the letter's lowest point and its most urgent, and everything after it — the hours set aside for those with special needs, the assembly cleared of guards so the weak can speak without fear, the prayer led at the pace of the frailest worshipper — keeps the ruler bent toward the ground even as the letter turns to his own conduct. Then the climb: out from among the subjects, whom he must never seclude himself from, past the favourites who must be granted no estates and the enemies with whom he may make an honourable peace, until the letter arrives at the two betrayals the Imam most dreads in a man given power — a covenant broken and innocent blood shed — and treats them not as policy failures but as ruptures in the fabric of trust that God Himself has spread over His servants.

What gives this climb its force is that the letter refuses to let any of it become merely technical. The instruction to keep no favourite in a landed estate is not a rule of property law but a guard against the slow capture of a ruler by the people closest to him; the demand that he clear a suspicion of injustice by open explanation rather than by the silence power permits is a discipline of transparency offered as a kindness to the very subjects it exposes him to; even the counsel on his daily hours — a task proper to each day, the finest portion of his time reserved for what is between him and God — treats the governor's calendar as a spiritual document, an arrangement of the soul's priorities disguised as an arrangement of the working day. The letter will not allow a seam to open between the outer conduct of office and the inner state of the man conducting it. Every practical instruction in this half, as in the first, is fastened to something that outlasts the office, so that by the time the reader reaches the end he has been given not a set of procedures to execute but a way of being that the procedures merely express.

And then, in its final paragraphs, the letter does something a manual of governance would never do. Having warned Malik against self-admiration and flattery and the deeds inflated by their doer, the Imam names the one discipline that holds all the rest together — that a ruler will never master the heat of his pride or the edge of his tongue unless he increases his remembrance of his return to his Lord. The whole ethics of power is folded back, at the last, into the remembrance the book has taught to be the polish of the heart, so that the just administration of a province turns out to rest on the same interior act as the private worship of a solitary soul. The letter closes not by commanding but by praying — asking God to grant both the writer and the reader the success that wins His pleasure, and to seal both their lives with felicity and martyrdom. It is an extraordinary way for a mandate of appointment to end: the ruler who has just laid a plea complete against his governor turns, in the same breath, to plead with God on that governor's behalf, and binds the two of them together in a single hope.

## What this episode lands

This episode has completed the reading of the letter to Malik al-Ashtar and, with it, the book's presentation of its two great primary documents — the opening sermon on the oneness of God and the origin of all things, and this mandate on the just ordering of a people. We have heard the second half descend to the productive base of society and to the destitute at its very bottom, where the Imam's even prose breaks into a cry of the divine name and the letter reaches the sharpest edge of its justice: the care of those who can never repay it, possible only for a ruler who aspires beyond this world. We have heard it climb back through the ruler's own conduct — his time, his prayer at the weakest man's pace, his covenants that his very life must shield, his hands kept clear of unlawful blood — and end by folding the whole art of governing back into the remembrance of the return to God.

Set beside the sermon, the letter shows the book's single claim from its other side. The sermon spoke of the God beyond every word who bends all the way down into guidance; the letter shows what that guidance asks of a man handed power over others — that he form his soul first, lean toward the weak, keep his word, guard innocent life, and never once let the machinery of rule cut loose from the vertical relation that gives it its purpose. Heard in the Imam's own voice, spoken to one governor on his way to one province, the counsel is the spiritual ethos of Ali (ع) made wholly concrete: not a doctrine to admire but a way to govern, sealed with a prayer that the man who receives it, and the man who wrote it, might both leave the most beautiful traces in the land. With the letter and the sermon both now heard entire, the book has let its central figure speak its whole argument in his own words — the transcendence and the mercy, the justice and the remembrance — and the two documents stand together as the plainest evidence the study could offer for the claim it set out to make.
