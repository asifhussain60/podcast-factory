# Letter to Malik: The Principles

*Alongside the opening sermon of Nahj al-Balagha, the tradition preserves a second great primary document from Ali (ع): the mandate he wrote for Malik al-Ashtar when he sent him to govern Egypt. It is the longest letter he left and the one the tradition honours as the most comprehensive of all — a whole ethics of power set down as working instructions for a man about to hold it. This episode presents the first half of that letter, heard whole in refined English: the forming of the ruler's own soul before any mention of his powers, his mercy toward the governed, his refusal to make his office into a war with God, his leaning toward the common people over the elite, his concealment of others' faults, his choice of counsellors and commanders and judges. The book has already drawn on this letter for its teaching on justice; here the document itself is set before the listener, so that the counsel can be heard in the Imam's own voice, in the order he gave it, from the first line of the mandate to the naming of the men who will serve under it.*

## Where this episode opens

What is the first thing a man ought to be handed on the day he is given power over a province — a catalogue of what he may now command, or a warning about the one person his authority will never reach, which is himself? Ali (ع) answers that question in the very structure of the letter this episode is given to. He is appointing Malik al-Ashtar governor of Egypt, and he does name the powers: collect the revenues, fight the enemies, seek the welfare of the people, bring prosperity to the land. But those four charges take a single sentence, and then the letter turns at once away from the province and toward the governor's own heart — to the fear of God, the curbing of the soul, the mastery of desire — and it stays there, working on the man himself, before it will let him lay a finger on the machinery of the state. Power, in the Imam's hands, begins as a problem of the self before it is ever a problem of administration.

We arrive at this letter having heard a great deal about it. The book's whole treatment of justice — the sacred conception of it, the mercy that gives it its edge, the interdependence of the classes, the care of the poor, the intention that acts for justice's own sake and not for reward — was drawn out of this document, read and expounded in the study's own voice. What this source-episode does is different, and it is the same move the book made with the opening sermon at its close: it stops quarrying the letter for doctrine and sets the whole thing down to be heard. The counsel we met as teaching is now heard as command, spoken by the man to the man, in the plain and pressing register of one ruler handing another the weight he himself carried. Nothing here needs to be taught again. It needs only to be heard in its own order and its own voice.

Three movements carry the episode. We begin by standing back from the letter to see what kind of document it is — its occasion, its unmatched reach, and the single method that runs through every line of it. We then hear the first half of the letter itself, whole and unbroken, from the words of the mandate through the forming of the ruler's soul to the choosing of his commanders, his judges, and his administrators. And we close by walking back through what that first half does when it is heard entire — the deliberate placing of the soul before the state, and the fresh, concrete counsel the commentary passed over: the leaning toward the common folk, the veiling of faults, the counsellors to keep near and the ministers to keep out, and the refusal to break what earlier hands had rightly built.

## The longest letter

The occasion is a hard one. Ali (ع) is sending Malik al-Ashtar — his most trusted commander, a man of fierce loyalty and long service — to take up the government of Egypt at a moment when the province is slipping. The previous governor, Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr, had found his authority weakening, and Egypt was among the most contested and strategically vital lands of the young community. Into that unstable inheritance the Imam sends not merely an administrator but a whole vision of what it means to rule justly, written out at a length he permitted himself nowhere else. The tradition has never forgotten the result. Of all the letters gathered in Nahj al-Balagha, this mandate to Malik is the longest, and it has been received for centuries as the most comprehensive in beauty of form and excellence of meaning — a complete ethics of governance compressed into the form of a working brief for one man on his way to one post.

What makes the letter more than a manual is a habit it never once drops, and it is worth naming before hearing it, because the habit is the argument. The Imam describes a duty, states it concretely — appoint this kind of commander, pay the judge this generously, treat this class in this way — and then, almost every time, he anchors that concrete duty in something that transcends administration altogether: the nature of God, the destiny of the soul, the covenant that will be asked about on the day of meeting. A lesser brief would stop at the instruction and hand the ruler a set of rules. This letter never stops there, because its author does not believe that rules can be applied justly by a soul that has not been formed to see why they matter. The book's own image for this was a wheel and its axle — the visible spokes of the administration turning on an invisible connection to the Real — and reading the letter whole, one watches that axle run through every paragraph, coupling the tax office and the barracks and the courts to a single vertical relation without which none of them turns to any purpose.

That is why the letter opens where it does. Before Malik is told anything about Egypt, he is told to fear God, to give obedience to God precedence over everything else, to assist God with heart and hand and tongue, and to curb the passions of a soul that, left to itself, incites to evil. The Imam is not clearing his throat with piety before getting down to business. The forming of the soul is the business — or rather, it is the ground on which all the later business will either stand or collapse. A ruler who has not first been made just within himself cannot be trusted to render justice without; a ruler whose desires are not mastered will bend every office of the state toward feeding them. So the letter equips the inner man first, and only then, with that man in place, does it begin to describe the classes he governs and the servants he must choose. As we listen, it helps to hear the whole first half as a single descent — from the soul, to the general disposition toward the governed, to the covenant that orders society under God, and finally to the concrete choosing of the men through whom the ruler's justice will actually reach the people. What follows is that first half, in refined English, as the Imam wrote it to Malik.

## The mandate and its principles

What follows is the first half of the letter, in refined English, as Ali (ع) set it down for Malik.

In the Name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful.

This is what the servant of God, Ali (ع), Commander of the Faithful, commands Malik ibn al-Harith al-Ashtar in his mandate of appointment, upon making him governor of Egypt: to collect its revenues, to fight its enemies, to seek the welfare of its people, and to bring prosperity to its lands.

He commands him to fear God, to give obedience to God precedence over all else, and to abide by what He has commanded in His Book — both the obligatory and the recommended — for no one attains virtue except by following them, and no one becomes wretched except by rejecting and neglecting them. He commands him to assist God with his heart, his hand, and his tongue, for God, majestic is His name, has undertaken to grant victory to whoever assists Him and to exalt whoever glorifies Him. And he commands him to curb the passions of his soul and to restrain it when whim and caprice run high, for the soul incites to evil unless God shows mercy.

Know, Malik, that I am sending you to a land that has seen governments before yours — some just and some unjust — and the people will now weigh your conduct just as you once weighed the conduct of the rulers before you. They will speak of you just as you spoke of them; and the righteous are known only through what God causes to flow about them on the tongues of His servants. So let the treasure dearest to you be the treasure of good deeds. Master your desires and hold yourself back from what is not lawful to you, for such restraint is fairness by the soul toward itself, keeping it balanced between what it likes and what it dislikes.

Habituate your heart to mercy, love, and kindness toward your subjects. Do not stand over them like a ravenous beast that counts them fair prey, for they are of two kinds: either your brother in religion or your equal in creation. Mistakes slip from them and faults befall them, whether by intention or by accident. So extend to them your forgiveness and your pardon, just as you would wish God to extend to you His forgiveness and pardon — for you are above them, and the one in authority over you is above you, and God is above the one who appointed you. He has asked you to manage their affairs, and He is testing you through them.

Do not set your soul on a course of war with God, for you have no power before His retribution and cannot do without His forgiveness and mercy. Never regret a pardon, and never rejoice in a punishment. Do not act rashly on any impulse when you can find a way around it. Do not say, "I have been given authority, so I command and am obeyed," for that breeds corruption in the heart, weakens religion, and draws misfortune near. If the authority you hold breeds pride or vanity in you, look at the greatness of God's dominion above you and at His power over you — power the like of which you do not hold even over your own self. This will calm your ambition, restrain your vehemence, and restore to you the good sense that had strayed. Beware of matching yourself with God in greatness or likening yourself to Him in might, for God humbles every tyrant and disgraces every braggart.

Be just with God and be just with people, giving them their due from yourself, from your close relatives, and from those of your subjects for whom you feel fondness — for if you do not, you are an oppressor. And whoever oppresses the servants of God has God, rather than His servants, as his adversary; and whomever God opposes, He refutes his plea, and he remains at war with God until he desists and repents. Nothing so surely hastens the withdrawal of God's grace and the coming of His retribution as persistence in oppression, for God hears the cry of the oppressed and lies in wait for the oppressors.

Let the dearest of your affairs be those most balanced in truth, most comprehensive in justice, and most inclusive of your subjects' contentment — for the discontent of the common folk cancels out the contentment of the elite, while the discontent of the elite is absorbed within the contentment of the common folk. No one is more of a burden to a ruler in times of ease, less helpful in times of hardship, more averse to fair treatment, more insistent in asking for favours, less grateful when given them, slower to accept an excuse when refused, and weaker in patience under the blows of fortune, than the elite. It is the common folk of the community who are the pillar of religion, the mass of the Muslims, and the defence against the enemy — so let your inclination and your leaning be toward them.

Let those among your subjects who most eagerly hunt out the faults of others be the furthest from you and the most despised in your eyes, for people do have faults, and the ruler is the one most bound to conceal them. Do not lay bare what is hidden from you; your task is only to correct what has become plain, and God will judge whatever remains hidden. Veil people's failings as much as you can, and God will veil from your subjects the failings in yourself that you would wish kept concealed. Loosen from the people every knot of resentment, and cut away from your own heart the cord of every enmity. Turn a blind eye to whatever is unclear to you, and do not be quick to believe a slanderer, for a slanderer is a deceiver, however sincerely he may appear to advise.

Do not admit into your counsel a miser, for he will turn you away from generosity and frighten you with poverty; nor a coward, for he will make you feel too weak for your affairs; nor a greedy man, for he will dress up avidity as though it were justice. Miserliness, cowardice, and greed are distinct traits, united by a single ill opinion of God. The worst of your ministers is the one who served evil rulers before you and shared in their sins — do not let such men into your inner circle, for they are helpers of sinners and brothers of tyrants. You can find good substitutes for them among men of equal intelligence and capability who carry none of that burden of sin, who never helped a tyrant in his tyranny or a sinner in his sin. Such men will cost you the least, support you the best, incline most warmly toward you, and attach themselves least to anyone but you — so choose them as your intimates, in private and in public.

Among these, give the highest place to the one who most readily speaks the bitter truth to you and gives you the least encouragement in whatever you do that God dislikes for His friends, however painfully that strikes at your own desires. Attach yourself to people of piety and truthfulness, and school them not to flatter you or to gladden you with praise for something you never did, for excessive praise breeds conceit and carries a person toward vanity. Never place the good and the wicked on the same footing before you, for that would make the good think little of their goodness and the wicked settle deeper into their wickedness; hold each of them to what he has bound himself to. Know that nothing so builds a ruler's confidence in his subjects as his kindness toward them, his easing of their burdens, and his refusal to press them beyond what they can bear. So conduct yourself in a way that earns their trust, for that trust will spare you long trouble; and the one most deserving of your good opinion is the one you have treated well, while the one most deserving of your distrust is the one you have treated badly.

Do not break any good tradition established by the leaders of this community, by which harmony was knit together and the subjects set right; and do not bring in any new practice that harms what those traditions already secured — for then the reward for the old traditions will go to the one who founded them, while the burden of breaking them will fall upon you. Study much with the scholars and hold much discourse with the sages, so as to strengthen what keeps your lands sound and to uphold what those before you had already set right.

Know that the subjects are made up of several classes, none of which can be sound without the others, and none of which can do without the rest. Among them are the soldiers of God; the scribes who serve the common people and the elite; the judges who administer justice; the officials who uphold fairness and secure the right of redress; those who pay the poll-tax and the land-tax from among the protected people and the Muslims; the merchants and the artisans; and the lowest class, the needy and the destitute. To each of these God has assigned its share, and He has fixed its bounds and its duties in His Book or in the practice of His Prophet — God bless him and his family — as a binding covenant from Him, kept safe with us.

As for the soldiers, they are, by the grace of God, the fortresses of the subjects, the adornment of the governors, the strength of religion, and the pathways to security. The subjects cannot maintain themselves except through the soldiers, and the soldiers in their turn cannot be maintained except through the land-tax that God brings forth for them — by which they gain the strength to wage war on their enemies, on which they depend for their own welfare, and by which they meet their needs. Neither of these two classes can be maintained without the third: the judges, the administrators, and the scribes, who uphold every contract, gather and order all interests and benefits, and are entrusted with the particular and the general affairs of state alike. None of these can function without the merchants and the artisans, who gather the goods that are needed, establish the markets, and by their own labour supply what the work of others could not procure. Last comes the lowest class, the needy and the destitute, whose relief and support is an obligation owed to them. For every one of these classes there is plenitude with God, and every one has upon the governor a right measured by what its welfare requires. And the governor cannot fully discharge the duties God has laid upon him except through earnest striving and seeking God's help, training himself to hold fast to what is right, and bearing patiently whatever comes his way, be it easy or hard.

Appoint as commander of your soldiers the man you feel most deeply to be the most sincere toward God, the Prophet, and your Imam — the purest of heart and the most excellent in forbearance: one who is slow to anger, glad to pardon, kind to the weak and firm with the strong; neither driven by violence nor held back by weakness. Hold fast to men of noble descent, from pious families of established name and repute, and to those known for their bravery, their courage, their generosity, and their tolerance — for they are a group formed by nobility and a party made of honour. Then oversee their affairs as parents would oversee their child. Let no act by which you strengthen them appear too great in your eyes, and do not belittle any kindness, however slight, that you have promised them, for such kindness summons them to give you good counsel and to place their trust in you. Do not neglect the close inspection of their affairs in favour of matters that seem weightier, for there are cases in which a small act of kindness benefits them greatly, and cases in which the weightier matters cannot be set aside.

Let the most preferred of your commanders be the one most benevolent to the people and most generous with his bounty, so that they and their dependants have ample means. Let their concern be gathered into a single resolve: to fight the enemy. Your kindness toward them will cause their hearts to incline toward you.

The greatest source of joy for governors is the establishment of justice in the land and the flowing of love from their subjects. Such love will not appear unless the subjects' hearts are sound, and their counsel will not be sincere unless they gather to protect their governors and find the burden of rule light and easy to bear — so that they no longer wait anxiously for that rule to end. So raise their hopes, praise them continually, and recount the brave deeds of those who have been brave, for abundant praise of noble acts rouses the courageous and spurs on the laggard, if God wills. Acknowledge the worth of each man's deed; do not ascribe the deed of one to another, and do not fall short in granting anyone his due. Do not let a man's nobility lead you to reckon a small accomplishment great, nor a man's lowliness lead you to reckon a great one small. Refer to God and His Messenger any matter that weighs heavily upon you or is unclear to you, for God the Exalted has said to a people whom He loved to guide: "O you who believe, obey God and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. And if you dispute with one another over anything, then refer it to God and the Messenger" (chapter 4, verse 59). To refer to God is to follow what is clear and unequivocal in His Book; to refer to the Messenger is to follow that part of his practice which unites rather than that which divides.

For the settlement of disputes among the people, choose the one you hold to be the most excellent of your subjects: one whom complex matters do not confound and whom disputants do not enrage; who does not persist in error, and does not shrink from returning to the truth once he perceives it; whose soul is not drawn toward greed; who is not content with a shallow understanding but probes to the depths; who is most circumspect in the face of ambiguities, most firm in holding to the proofs, least wearied by litigants returning again and again, most patient in uncovering the true state of affairs, and most resolute once the right judgement is plain; whom praise does not make vain and whom enticement does not sway. Such people are indeed rare. Then examine carefully and often the carrying-out of his verdicts, and be generous in what you pay him, so that his want is removed and his need to seek help from others is lessened. Give him a rank of nearness to yourself that none of your other close companions might hope to attain, so that he is safe in your presence and shielded from the attacks of powerful men. Look into all of this with a searching eye, for this religion had been held captive in the hands of evil people, worked for their own caprice and exploited for the sake of this world.

Then look into the affairs of your administrators. Employ them only after testing them, and do not appoint anyone out of partiality or favouritism, for these two are a wellspring of every kind of injustice and treachery. Seek out men of proven experience and modesty, from righteous households and families of precedence in Islam, for they are the most noble in character, the most rooted in dignity, the least inclined toward greed, and the most mindful of how all things end. Then provide them with a generous livelihood, for this gives them the strength to keep themselves in good order, frees them from reaching into the funds in their charge, and stands as an argument against them should they disobey your orders or betray your trust. Examine their conduct carefully by setting over them supervisors known for their sincerity and reliability, for your quiet watching of their affairs will move them to keep faith with their trust and to be gentle with the subjects. And keep watch over your assistants: if any one of them stretches out his hand toward a treacherous deed, and the reports of your watchers agree upon it so that you hold it sufficient, then lay corporal punishment upon him, take him to account for what his act has brought about, set him in a place of disgrace, brand him with the charge of treachery, and gird him with the shame of the accusation.

## Hearing the principles whole

Heard entire rather than quarried for a lesson, the first half of the letter makes an argument by its very sequence, and the sequence is the first thing to notice. The Imam spends his opening paragraphs entirely on Malik's interior — the fear of God, the mastery of desire, the refusal to let authority breed the sentence "I command and am obeyed" — and only after the man has been formed does the letter turn outward to the governed, and only after the disposition toward the governed is set does it descend to the concrete choosing of commanders and judges and administrators. As the book taught in its own voice, this is power built from the inside out: the equipped soul first, then the machinery it will run. What the recitation adds is the felt weight of that order. One hears how long the Imam dwells on the self before he will say a word about the state, and the proportion itself is the counsel.

One more feature of the first half becomes audible only when it is heard whole: its address. This is not a decree issued to a province but a letter written to a man, and the Imam never loses sight of the single reader. He addresses Malik by name, warns him of what his own soul will do under the pressure of power, anticipates the pride that authority breeds and hands him the exact remedy for it, and speaks throughout in the second person of one who has carried this weight to one about to take it up. The counsel is universal in reach — any ruler of any age could receive it — yet it is delivered as intimate, personal instruction, and that doubleness is part of why the letter has lasted. A treatise on justice would argue; this letter simply tells one trusted man what he must become and what he must do, in the confidence that a soul rightly formed will find its own way through the particular cases the letter cannot foresee. The personal register is not incidental to the teaching. It is the teaching's proof that the ethics of power is finally borne by persons, not by procedures.

Much of what the first half teaches the book has already unfolded, and those threads need only a word of recognition as they pass: the mercy the ruler must habituate his heart to, and the two kinds of people — a brother in religion or an equal in creation — who are never fair prey; the refusal to make office into a war with God, and the antidote to swelling pride, which is to look up at a dominion beside which the ruler holds no power even over himself; the ill opinion of God that secretly unites the miser, the coward, and the greedy man. Each of these the book expounded at length from this very letter, and the recitation lets them be heard now in their original setting rather than taught afresh.

But the first half also carries counsel the commentary passed lightly over, and it is here that the fresh work of the episode lies. There is the startling political preference the Imam presses on Malik — to lean not toward the elite but toward the common folk, whose discontent, he says, cancels the contentment of the privileged few, and who are the real pillar of religion and defence of the community, while the elite are the heaviest burden in ease and the least help in hardship. The reasoning is worth pausing on, because it cuts against the grain of how power ordinarily distributes its favour. A ruler is naturally drawn to the influential few, whose goodwill seems to steady his position and whose displeasure he learns to fear; the Imam reverses the instinct entirely, telling Malik that the elite are precisely the group whose favour costs the most and returns the least — quick to demand, slow to thank, thin in patience — while the mass of ordinary people, who ask little and bear much, are the ground the whole order actually rests on. To lean toward them is not sentimentality but sober arithmetic: their contentment can absorb the grumbling of the few, whereas no amount of courting the powerful will survive the anger of the many.

There is, alongside this, the whole discipline of concealment, which the letter treats as a positive duty rather than a mere restraint. Keep the fault-hunters furthest from you and most despised in your eyes; do not lay bare what is hidden, for correcting what has become plain is task enough and God will judge the rest; veil people's failings as much as you can, and trust that God will veil your own in turn. Above all, do not be quick to believe a slanderer, however sincerely he wears the mask of an adviser — for the man who comes bearing another's secret fault has already shown you the kind of man he is. There is the fine anatomy of counsel that follows — the ministers to keep out, chief among them any man who served evil rulers before and shared their sins, and the counsellor to prize above every other, the one who will speak the bitter truth and refuse to gladden you with praise for a thing you never did. And there is the quiet conservatism of the just ruler, who does not break a good tradition merely because it is not his own, who studies long with the scholars who know the law and converses long with the sages who know how to apply it. These are not doctrines the book stopped to teach; they are the working texture of just rule, and hearing them in the Imam's own order restores the letter's balance between the height of its principles and the granularity of its practice — a document that can move without a seam from the war a proud soul wages against God to the precise question of whom a governor should let near his ear.

## What this episode lands

This episode has set down the first half of Ali (ع)'s mandate to Malik al-Ashtar and let it be heard whole — the second of the two great primary documents, standing beside the opening sermon, in which the book's argument is spoken by the man himself rather than about him. We have seen why the letter opens on the soul and not the province: a ruler unjust within himself cannot render justice without, so the interior man is formed before the machinery is touched. We have heard the principles of that formation and the disposition that follows from it — mercy, humility before God's dominion, justice toward all, a leaning toward the common people, the veiling of faults, the guarding against slanderers and self-serving ministers — and we have watched the letter begin its descent into the choosing of the men through whom a ruler's justice actually reaches the ground: the forbearing commander, the rare incorruptible judge, the tested administrator.

But the letter is only half spoken. It has formed the ruler and named the instruments of his authority; it has not yet reached the ground those instruments are meant to serve — the cultivators whose land feeds the state, the scribes and the merchants, and, most urgently of all, the destitute who can do nothing for the ruler in return. Nor has it turned back to the ruler's own conduct: how he keeps his time and his prayer, how he holds a covenant once he has given it, how he guards his hands against unlawful blood and his heart against the flattery that Satan uses to efface a good man's works. The second half of the letter carries all of that, and it ends not with an instruction but with a prayer the Imam offers for them both. Having formed the ruler and staffed his court, the letter has still to reach the people for whose sake the whole apparatus exists, and to turn, at the last, back upon the soul that must answer for it. That is where the next episode goes.
