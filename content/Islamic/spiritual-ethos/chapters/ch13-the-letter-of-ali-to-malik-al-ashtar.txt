This chapter carries the longest letter Ali (ع) ever wrote, from its first word to its last — the mandate he sent with Malik al-Ashtar when he made him governor of Egypt, at a time when the province had slipped into unrest. Earlier chapters heard a scholar read this letter from the outside, weighing its vision of justice against Plato and against the secular idea of fairness. Here we hear Ali's own voice give the commands entire: the inner discipline demanded of a ruler before he may touch a lever of power, the company he must keep and banish, the pillars of the state he is charged to turn, and then the letter's own descent — down to the land and the people who work it, the destitute who can never repay a ruler's care, and the covenants and the blood on which his whole account with God is finally settled. This is the final chapter of the book.

## Where this episode picks up

What is the first thing you would hand a man to whom you were about to give a province — an army, a treasury, the seal of office? Ali (ع) hands Malik none of these. Before the letter names a single duty of state, before it mentions a soldier or a coin or a border, it spends its opening breath on the one territory a governor can lose without anyone noticing he has lost it: his own soul. The whole letter, longest and most complete of everything the Imam wrote, is built on a wager that everyone who has ever held power is tempted to forget — that the outward kingdom is only ever as sound as the inward one, and that a man who cannot govern the appetites of his own heart has no business governing the affairs of other people's.

That is the tension this episode turns on. We have already heard, in the sermon that opened Ali's own voice to us, how he pictures the cosmos as a single act of a God beyond number, and how every creature hangs upon that One. The letter now brings that vision down out of the heavens and lays it across the desk of a working administrator. It is one thing to know that all power belongs to God; it is another to be handed a province and to keep believing it while you sign the orders. Every practical instruction the letter gives — whom to promote, whom to tax, whom to forgive — is anchored back to a claim about God and about the self, so that administration never becomes a merely technical art. The Imam is teaching a man how to run Egypt. He is also teaching him how to save his own soul, and the letter's genius is that it never lets him treat these as two different jobs.

So it opens not with a grant of authority but with a warning about it. The mandate ('ahd) begins by naming what Malik is being sent to do — to gather the revenues of Egypt, to fight its enemies, to seek the welfare of its people, and to make its land prosper — and then, before the ink is dry on that commission, it turns and commands him to fear the God who gave it. The order of those two things is the whole point. Authority first, and immediately the fear of God as the frame around it. This is where we begin: with the ruler's soul, and with what a man is really holding when he holds a province.

## The ruler's soul

The letter's first commands are not about Egypt at all. Ali (ع) tells Malik to fear God, to give obedience to God precedence over every other claim, and to hold fast to what God has enjoined in His Book, "for no one attains virtue except by following them, and no one becomes wretched except by rejecting and neglecting them." Then comes the command that governs all the rest: that Malik curb the passions of his own soul and restrain it when whim and caprice run high — "for the soul incites to evil unless God shows mercy." The Imam is not coining a private doctrine here; he is leaning on the Qur'an's own verdict on the self, the confession Joseph makes in the hour of his vindication: "Indeed the soul is ever an inciter to evil, save where my Lord bestows His mercy" (chapter 12, verse 53). That single clause carries an entire anthropology. The soul, left to itself, does not drift neutrally; it leans downward, and the only counterweight is a mercy the man cannot manufacture from inside himself. A governor is a man at war on two fronts, and the inner front is the one he must win first, because the enemy there wears his own face. Assist God, the letter says, with your heart, your hand, and your tongue — for God has undertaken to grant victory to whoever assists Him. The victory offered is not military. It is the victory of a man over the appetite that would otherwise turn his office into a weapon against the very people it was given to protect.

It is worth pausing on the four things the mandate actually commissions Malik to do, because their order is not innocent. He is charged to gather Egypt's revenues, to fight its enemies, to seek the welfare of its people, and to bring prosperity to its lands. The taxes and the war are what any ruler in history would list first; but the letter brackets them and subordinates them to the other two — the welfare of the people and the flourishing of the land. The seed of that reversal is already planted here: a governor is sent to make a place thrive, and the gathering and the fighting are only instruments of that thriving. Read the four charges in the wrong order and you have licensed a predator. Read them in the Imam's order and you have described a gardener who happens to command an army.

Then the letter widens the frame with a piece of counsel that is really a mirror. "Know, Malik," it says, "that I am sending you to a land that has seen governments before yours — some just and some unjust — and the people will now weigh your conduct just as you once weighed the conduct of the rulers before you. They will speak of you just as you spoke of them." It is a devastating reminder, and it works by memory. Malik has stood where the governed stand; he knows exactly how a subject's eye tracks the small hypocrisies of the powerful. Now he is to be watched by that same unforgiving eye, and the letter tells him what will actually survive the watching: "the righteous are known only through what God causes to flow about them on the tongues of His servants. So let the treasure dearest to you be the treasure of good deeds." A ruler amasses many things. The only one that lasts is the one he cannot spend. And the first discipline of amassing it is fairness toward the self — mastering one's own desires and holding back from what is not lawful, which the Imam calls a kind of justice the soul does toward itself, keeping it balanced between what it likes and what it dislikes.

From the self the letter turns to the subjects, and the turn is abrupt and physical. "Habituate your heart to mercy, love, and kindness towards your subjects. Do not stand over them like a ravenous beast that considers them fair prey." The image is deliberately ugly, because the temptation it names is real: power invites a man to look at the people beneath him as things to be consumed. Against that, the Imam offers a classification that dissolves the predator's whole way of seeing. The people you govern, he says, "are of two kinds: either your brother in religion or your equal in creation." There is no third category, no class of persons who fall outside the circle of kinship and may therefore be treated as prey. Faults will slip from them, deliberately or by accident; extend them forgiveness and pardon, the letter says, "just as you would wish God to extend to you His forgiveness and pardon." And then the vertical order that keeps the whole thing honest: "for you are above them, and the one in authority over you is above you, and God is above the one who appointed you." Every rung of authority is answerable to a higher rung, and the ladder does not stop until it reaches the One who is above all appointing. To forget the top of the ladder is to imagine oneself standing on the ground when one is in fact suspended in mid-air. The responsibility the Imam is pressing on Malik is one the Prophet had already made universal, binding on everyone who holds any charge over anyone at all:

> Each of you is a shepherd, and each of you is responsible for his flock. — the Prophet.

A governor is only a shepherd whose flock happens to be a province, and the reckoning he owes for it is the same in kind, though not in scale, as the reckoning owed by a parent for a child or by anyone for whatever has been placed in his hands.

This is where the letter issues its sternest warning, and an earlier chapter on justice already unfolded its logic — that pride is not merely a vice but a kind of war declared against God, a rival claim to a greatness that belongs to Him alone. Here we hear the Imam say it to a specific man about to be tempted in a specific way. "Do not set your soul on a course of war with God," he tells Malik, "for you have no power before His retribution and cannot do without His forgiveness and mercy." And he names the exact sentence a new governor is most likely to say to himself: "Do not say, 'I have been given authority, so I command and am obeyed,' for that breeds corruption in the heart, weakens religion, and draws misfortune near." The corruption begins as a private thought — the small intoxication of being obeyed — and works outward from there. Against it the Imam prescribes not suppression but a change of gaze: "If the authority you hold breeds pride or vanity in you, look at the greatness of God's dominion above you and at His power over you — power the like of which you do not hold even over your own self." The cure for feeling large is to look at something genuinely large, and notice that one cannot command even a single heartbeat of one's own body. "This will calm your ambition, restrain your vehemence, and restore the good sense that had strayed from you." Pride, in this diagnosis, is a lapse of good sense before it is a moral failure, and the correction is simply to see accurately again. "Beware of matching yourself with God in greatness or likening yourself to Him in might, for God humbles every tyrant and disgraces every braggart."

The movement closes by binding justice to the same divine reference. "Be just with God and be just with people," the Imam says, "giving them their due from yourself, from your close relatives, and from those of your subjects for whom you feel fondness — for if you do not, you are an oppressor." Justice, note, must be paid first out of the ruler's own pocket and his own family's before it is demanded of strangers; the fondness a man feels for his intimates is precisely where injustice hides. And the consequence of failing is stated with a terrible economy: "whoever oppresses the servants of God has God, rather than His servants, as his adversary; and whomever God opposes, He refutes his plea, and he remains at war with God until he desists and repents." Nothing, the letter warns, so surely hastens the withdrawal of God's grace as persistence in oppression, "for God hears the cry of the oppressed and lies in wait for the oppressors." That last image — God lying in wait — is the frame for everything the letter will now build. The oppressed man may have no court to appeal to on earth; he has a hearer in heaven who is patient and who does not forget.

## The company a ruler keeps

Having set the disposition, the letter turns to a question every ruler underestimates: who is allowed to stand close. A governor is shaped by the voices at his ear, and Ali (ع) is fierce about which voices Malik must draw near and which he must drive off. He begins by settling whose contentment the ruler should chase. There are the common folk — al-'amma, the ordinary mass of the people — and there are the elite, al-khassa, the few with wealth and influence. A ruler's instinct is to court the second, because they are near and useful. The Imam reverses it. "Let the dearest of your affairs," he says, "be those most balanced in truth, most comprehensive in justice, and most inclusive of your subjects' contentment — for the discontent of the common folk cancels out the contentment of the elite, while the discontent of the elite is absorbed within the contentment of the common folk." He then draws the portrait of the elite without flattery: no one is heavier on a ruler in easy times and less useful in hard ones, more resentful of fair treatment, slower to be grateful and quicker to abandon patience, than the privileged. "It is the common folk of the community who are the pillar of religion, the mass of the Muslims, and the defence against enemies — so let your inclination and your leaning be towards them." The centre of gravity of a just state is the many, not the few.

Then the letter turns to concealment, and here it teaches something that runs against every instinct of a security apparatus. The ruler will be surrounded by people eager to report the faults of others. Ali (ع) tells Malik to keep exactly those people furthest from him and most despised in his eyes, "for people do have faults, and the ruler is the one most bound to conceal them." His duty is not to expose what is hidden but only to correct what has become plain; the rest God will judge. And the reason he gives is a piece of pure spiritual reciprocity: "Veil people's failings as much as you can, and God will veil from your subjects the failings in yourself that you would wish kept concealed." The ruler who hunts sins will have his own sins hunted. What the Imam presses on a governor here, the Prophet had already promised to every believer — that the concealment a man extends to others is the very measure God extends back to him:

> Whoever shields another in this world, God will shield him on the Day of Resurrection. — the Prophet.

A hidden fault, in that economy, is not overlooked but banked: the governor who folds away what he could have exposed finds his own failings folded away in turn, by the only Judge whose concealment finally matters. So he is told to loosen from the people every knot of resentment and cut from his own heart the cord of every enmity, to turn a blind eye to whatever is genuinely unclear, and never to be quick to trust a slanderer — "for a slanderer is a deceiver, however sincerely he may appear to advise." The most dangerous informant is the one who comes dressed as a well-wisher.

There is a hard realism in this that is easy to miss under its gentleness. The Imam distinguishes sharply between faults that have become public, which he is bound to correct, and faults that remain hidden, which are not his to drag into the light. A government that makes it its business to expose everyone's private failings does not become more just; it becomes a machine of suspicion in which the ruler's own concealed faults sit under the same lamp he has trained on everyone else. A ruler who carries private grudges will find a way to make them public policy, and the people will pay for a quarrel that was never theirs.

Now the letter names the counsellors a ruler must refuse outright, and an earlier chapter already traced the theology beneath the list — that miserliness, cowardice, and greed, for all their differences, are a single disease, "united by a single bad opinion of God," each of them a quiet suspicion that the divine is not generous enough, or protective enough, or providing enough. Here the Imam applies that diagnosis to hiring. Do not admit a miser to your counsel, for he will turn you from generosity and frighten you with the spectre of poverty; nor a coward, for he will make you feel too weak for your own affairs; nor a greedy man, for he will dress up avidity until it looks like justice. And then a harder rule: "The worst of your ministers is the one who served evil rulers before you and shared in their sins — do not let such men into your inner circle, for they are helpers of sinners and brothers of tyrants." The competence of such men is not the point; their formation is. You can find substitutes of equal ability, the Imam says, who carry none of that inherited guilt, and those men will cost you less, support you better, and attach themselves to no one above you. Choose them, "in private and in public."

Among the men a ruler keeps close, the letter then singles out one for the very highest place — and the choice is astonishing. Give the highest rank, Ali (ع) says, to the man "who most readily speaks the bitter truth to you and gives you the least encouragement in whatever you do that God dislikes for His friends, however painfully that strikes at your own desires." The most valuable person in the room is the one who makes the ruler uncomfortable. Attach yourself to the pious and the truthful, and train them not to flatter you, not to gladden you with praise for something you never did — "for excessive praise breeds conceit and carries a person towards vanity." A ruler surrounded by applause slowly loses the ability to hear anything else. And he must never place the good and the wicked on the same footing, "for that would make the good think little of their goodness and the wicked settle deeper into their wickedness." Justice among his servants means that virtue and vice are seen to matter, each held to exactly what it has earned.

The letter closes this movement on the theme of continuity. Nothing, the Imam observes, so builds a ruler's confidence among his people as kindness, the easing of their burdens, and the refusal to press them past their strength; such conduct earns a trust that spares him long trouble. A ruler should hold that trust in both directions — the person most deserving of his good opinion is the one he has treated well, and the person most deserving of his suspicion is the one he has treated badly, because men return the treatment they are given. Then the counsel about custom: "Do not break any good tradition established by the leaders of this community, by which harmony was knit together and the subjects set right; and do not introduce any new practice that harms those already in place." The reward for a good tradition goes to the one who founded it, the Imam warns, but the guilt for breaking it falls on the one who breaks it. And an earlier chapter has already heard him distinguish the scholars from the sages, so that his final instruction here lands as a familiar note struck again: study long with the scholars, hold long discourse with the sages, "so as to strengthen what keeps your lands sound and to uphold what those before you had already set right." A ruler is not the first just man to hold his office, and he will not be the last; his task is to conserve what worked, not to treat the state as a blank page for his own signature.

## The pillars of rule

Only now, its foundations laid, does the letter turn to the machinery of the state itself. The Imam tells Malik that his subjects fall into several classes, none sound without the others — the soldiers of God, the scribes, the judges, the officers who secure people's right of redress, those who pay the land-tax (kharaj) and the poll-tax (jizya, جزیۃ), the merchants and artisans, and the lowest class, the needy and the destitute. An earlier chapter has already turned this catalogue into its famous image, the wheel of society whose classes are spokes and whose governor is a hub that turns nothing without the unseen axle joining it to the Real. Here the fresh point is the word the Imam uses for how the shares are fixed: to each class God has assigned its due, "as a binding covenant ('ahd) from Him, kept safe with us." The distribution of rights across society is not a policy Malik invents; it is a trust handed down, and the same word names both this document and the thing it protects. Everything that follows is the keeping of a covenant.

The first pillar is the army, and an earlier chapter already drew out the paradox in the Imam's portrait of a commander — that nearly every quality he demands of a fighting man is a quality of gentleness, so that the best soldier turns out to be the mildest man. Here we hear the full instruction in his own words, and it goes further than the portrait. Appoint as commander, he says, the man you feel most sincere toward God, the Prophet, and your Imam, "the purest of heart and the most excellent in forbearance (hilm): one who is slow to anger, glad to pardon, kind to the weak, and firm with the strong." Choose men of established name and proven courage, for they are "a group formed by nobility and a party made of honour." And then the tenderness that the commentary passed over: "supervise their affairs as parents would supervise their child." Let no kindness to them seem too small to bother with, for a small kindness "is a summoner calling them to give you good counsel and to place their trust in you." The soldier is not a tool to be maintained but a child to be raised; the loyalty a ruler wants cannot be commanded, only earned by a care that stoops to the smallest thing.

The letter lingers on this care longer than on any other pillar, because an army is where a ruler's inner disposition becomes visible fastest — a hard man produces hard soldiers, and hard soldiers turn on the people they were raised to guard. Let the most preferred of your commanders, Ali (ع) says, be the one most benevolent to his men and most generous with his bounty, so that want never drives them; and let their every concern be gathered into a single resolve — to face the enemy. Then he names what a just command actually produces. "The greatest source of joy for governors," he says, "is the establishment of justice in the land and the flowing of love from their subjects." That love, he warns, will not appear unless the people's hearts are secure and they no longer privately hope the ruler's term will end — a quiet, terrible test of a governor. So raise their hopes, he tells Malik, praise them continually, and recount aloud the brave deeds of those who have been brave, "for abundant praise of noble acts rouses the courageous and spurs on the lazy." But praise must be exact: acknowledge the real deed of every man and never ascribe the deed of one to another; do not let a man's high birth inflate a small accomplishment into a great one, nor a man's low station shrink a great one into small. Justice, even in the handing-out of honour, means each thing weighed at its true weight and set in its true place.

And when disputes arise among these men or anywhere in the realm, the Imam gives Malik the final court of appeal, quoting the verse that fixes it: "O ye who believe, obey God and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. And if ye dispute with one another over anything, then refer it to God and the Messenger" (chapter 4, verse 59). It is worth noticing that the verse standing immediately before the one the Imam reaches for had already named the whole of what a governor is for — "God commands you to render trusts back to their owners, and, when you judge between people, to judge with justice" (chapter 4, verse 58) — so that the obedience commanded in the verse he cites is obedience to an authority already defined, one line earlier, as the keeping of trusts and the doing of justice. To refer a thing to God, he explains, is to follow what is clear and unequivocal in His Book; to refer it to the Messenger is to follow the part of his practice that unites rather than the part that divides. Even the resolution of conflict is bent toward unity.

The second pillar is the judge, and here the letter slows down and paints a soul the world cannot bend. Choose for the settlement of disputes, Ali (ع) says, the most excellent of your subjects: a man "whom complex matters do not confound and whom disputants do not enrage; who does not persist in error, and does not shrink from returning to the truth once he perceives it; whose soul is not drawn toward greed; who is not content with a shallow understanding but probes to its depths." He must be the most cautious before ambiguities and the firmest in holding to the proofs, the least wearied by litigants who come back again and again, the most patient in uncovering the true state of affairs, and untouched by praise or enticement alike. Then the Imam adds the rueful line that concedes how tall an order he has just given: "Such people are indeed rare." And knowing it, he does two practical things: watch the judge's work, examining carefully and often how his verdicts are carried out; and pay him so generously that his need to lean on anyone else disappears, giving him "a rank of nearness to yourself that none of your other close companions might hope to attain, so that he is safe in your presence and shielded from the attacks of other influential men." A judge who is poor can be bought; a judge who is exposed can be frightened. Ali (ع) closes both doors at once, and tells Malik exactly why the stakes are so high: "this religion had been held captive in the hands of evil people, abused by them for their own caprice and exploited by them for the sake of this world." The independent judge is how a religion is unchained from the men who would use it as a leash.

The third pillar is the corps of administrators and officials who handle the state's affairs and its money, and toward them the letter's tone turns vigilant. Employ them only after testing them, the Imam warns, "and do not appoint anyone out of partiality or favouritism, for these two are a wellspring of every kind of injustice and treachery." Seek out men of proven experience and modesty, from righteous households with a precedent of service in Islam, for such men are the least given to greed and the most mindful of how things end. Then provide them a generous livelihood — the same logic as the judge's salary — because a well-paid officer is freed from the temptation to reach into the funds in his charge, and stands without excuse should he betray his trust anyway. And then the machinery of watching: appoint over them supervisors known for sincerity and reliability, "for your quiet watching of their affairs will move them to keep faith with their trust and to be gentle with the subjects." And where the watching turns up a genuine betrayal — where an officer stretches his hand toward something treacherous and the evidence is sufficient — the Imam is unsparing: lay a bodily penalty upon him, take him to account for the damage his act has caused, "brand him with the charge of treachery, and gird him with the shame of the accusation." Mercy toward the subjects and severity toward the officer who preys on them are, in this letter, the same virtue seen from two sides.

It is worth noticing that the same two instruments recur across all three pillars, because the repetition is a doctrine in itself: generous provision and close watching, so that need cannot tempt a man and opportunity cannot either. The pairing is neither cynical nor naive — it refuses the fantasy that good character alone will hold a man steady against poverty and unchecked power, and equally refuses the fantasy that surveillance alone can manufacture honesty in a man kept hungry. The Imam is building a state that makes it easier to be honest than to be corrupt, a very different project from one that merely punishes corruption after the fact.

The pillars of the state are now in place — the gentle commander, the incorruptible judge, the tested and watched officer. But a machine turns on something, and the letter now kneels to touch the ground it actually rests on. Having spent its length among the men closest to power, Ali (ع) now turns to the people furthest from it — the tiller of the soil, the traveling merchant, and, at the very bottom, the beggar too proud to beg. Everything just assembled exists, he now insists, to serve exactly these people; a ruler who treats the powerful as the point and the powerless as the cost has understood nothing.

## Land, scribes, and merchants

The letter reaches the land, and with it one of the most quietly radical sentences in the whole document — a line an earlier chapter already marked but which lands differently in the Imam's own voice, addressed to a man who will actually set the tax rates of Egypt. "Let your concern for the cultivation of the land outweigh your concern for collecting the tax," Ali (ع) tells Malik, "for no tax will be gathered where there is no cultivation. Whoever exacts the tax without cultivating the land ruins the land and destroys the people, and his rule will not endure for long." The entire logic of extractive rule is turned upside down. Revenue is not the goal; the flourishing of the land and its people is the goal, and revenue is only what flows from that flourishing when it is healthy. A ruler who squeezes the source dries up the stream and then wonders where the water went.

So the Imam makes relief a matter of policy, not of mood. If the people complain of a heavy burden, or of blight, or of the cutting of their water, or of failed rain, or of soil ruined by flood or drought, then lighten what they owe "in the measure that you hope will set their situation right." And he anticipates the governor's flinch at the lost revenue with a piece of pure statecraft dressed as spiritual counsel: do not let any relief you grant them weigh on you, "for it is an investment that will return to you in the prosperity of your land, the adornment of your rule, the finest praise you reap from them, and the pride you take in dispensing justice among them." The remission is not charity subtracted from the treasury; it is capital placed into the only thing that generates treasure — a people healthy enough to produce. A prosperous land, the Imam observes, can bear whatever burden you later place on it; it is the impoverished land that collapses under the lightest touch.

There is a whole philosophy of wealth folded into this counsel. A ruler's treasury and his people are not two competing interests, as if every coin left in a farmer's hand were a coin lost to the state; they are one interest seen at two moments in time — the wealth of a country simply is the wealth of its people, held in a slower form. To drain the people in order to fill the treasury is to bail water from one end of a boat into the other and call it progress. And the ruin is never only economic: a people bled white lose any reason to believe their government is more than a larger, better-armed bandit. Centuries later a poet of the same tradition would set the whole of this into two lines a king could not easily forget:

> Cherish the poor and the needy at your gate — for the king wears his crown by the grace of the people. — Sa'di.

The crown never sits on the head of a man who has emptied the fields beneath him; it rests, always, on the flourishing of the governed, and a ruler who squeezes them for revenue is sawing through the very branch he is seated on.

Then he names the disease that lays such land waste, and an earlier chapter has already unfolded its inner meaning — that the ruin of a country begins in the souls of its governors, in a greed that is really a doubt about what endures, a forgetting that one will die and be judged. Here we hear the chain in his own words: "The devastation of the land comes about only through the destitution of its inhabitants, and the destitution of its inhabitants comes about only when the desire to amass wealth rules the souls of the governors, when they have doubts about what endures, and when they profit little from exemplary teachings." Greed is a form of spiritual short-sightedness; a man grabs at the material because he has lost sight of the enduring, and the damage runs from his blind soul outward through the exploited people to the wasted fields. Injustice, in this letter, is never a single act. It is the last visible link in a chain whose first link is a soul that has stopped believing in anything that lasts.

From the soil the letter turns to the scribes, because a state runs on its correspondence and its records, and a treacherous or careless clerk can undo in a sentence what an army secured in a campaign. Appoint the best of them to your affairs, he says, and entrust your most sensitive strategies and secrets only to the scribe of the most complete moral character: one whom high office will not embolden to contradict you in public, who is never negligent in bringing you your officials' correspondence or dispatching your replies, who will not weaken any agreement made on your behalf nor fail to repudiate one made to your harm, and who "is not ignorant of the value of his own capabilities — for one who is unaware of his own worth will be even more ignorant of the worth of others." The Imam warns Malik against the trap that catches most rulers in their appointments: do not choose by instinct and personal impression alone, "for men know how to present themselves to rulers through affectation and good service" that hides neither sincerity nor fidelity. Test them instead by what they actually did in the service of good rulers before you, and appoint the one whose integrity left the deepest mark on ordinary people. To overlook a scribe's fault is to own it: "Any fault of your scribes which you have overlooked will be ascribed to you." And because no single clerk can carry an entire administration, set over each department a head officer "who will not be daunted by the magnitude of affairs nor scattered by their multiplicity." The principle beneath every appointment is the one the letter never abandons: put the right man in the right place and you have done justice; appoint by favouritism or by first impression and you have planted the seed of every injustice that will later flower from it. This is nothing but the Imam's own definition of justice made practical, the same one he compressed elsewhere into a single line:

> Justice sets each thing in its place. — Ali (ع).

An appointment is only a thing put in a place; make it by merit and you have done justice in miniature, make it by favour and you have committed an injustice at the root of everything that office will later touch.

Last among the working classes come the merchants and the artisans, and here the Imam's balance is exact. Attend to them, he says, and treat them well — those of fixed shop, those who travel with their goods, and those who earn their bread with their hands — for they are the veins through which a society's necessities flow, bringing goods "from distant and remote places, by land and sea, from plains and mountains, and from places where men neither settle nor even dare to venture." They live, he notes, in a state of reconciliation and peace, so no calamity need be feared from them. And yet — and the letter does not soften this — many of them harbour "a despicable stinginess, a repugnant greed, a propensity to hoard goods, and a tendency to fix prices arbitrarily," all of which brings loss to the people and shame to the governor who permits it. So the Imam gives a concrete command grounded in prophetic authority: prohibit hoarding, "for the Messenger of God — God bless him and his family — prohibited it." Let trade be conducted with fairness, at prices that harm neither buyer nor seller. And if a merchant hoards after the prohibition, "inflict upon him an exemplary — but not excessive — punishment." Even the penalty is measured; justice does not become vengeance because the offender is a profiteer. The market is a good servant and a bad master, and the Imam's task is to keep it in the servant's place. He does not moralize against commerce; he honours the merchants as a genuine good, the arteries through which a society's needs are met. What he refuses is the surrender of the market to its own worst appetites — the hoarding that manufactures scarcity, the price fixed to wound. A just ruler neither strangles trade nor abandons it, and the difference is a governor paying attention.

## The ruler's open door

Now the letter does something its structure has been quietly promising, and it does it with a cry. "Then — O God, O God!" Ali (ع) writes, and the exclamation is not decoration; it is the Imam seizing Malik by the collar. Pay particular attention, he says, to the lowest class of all: those with no resources, the destitute, the needy, the afflicted, the disabled — and among them both those who beg and those "whose wretchedness calls out to be alleviated but who do not beg," whose need is invisible precisely because their dignity hides it. This class is the sharpest test of justice, because a ruler can win nothing from them. Assign the poor a fixed share from the public treasury (bayt al-mal) and a share of what is gathered as the community's common wealth in every region, "for those who are furthest away have the same right as those nearest." Do not let any haughtiness turn your face from them; do not neglect them for some matter that merely looks more important, "for you will not be pardoned even the slightest shortcoming toward them." And because such people often cannot reach a governor at all, Malik is to appoint, from among those he most trusts, a God-fearing and humble officer whose whole charge is to seek these people out and bring their affairs to him — including the orphans and the aged who cannot bring themselves to beg. "This is onerous for governors," the Imam admits, "and the fulfilment of all rights is onerous; but God makes it light for those who aspire to the Hereafter, who restrain their souls in patience, and trust in the truth of what God has promised them."

Notice how the Imam frames the difficulty. He does not pretend the duty is light — he says plainly that it is heavy — but he denies that the heaviness is the last word. For a ruler whose horizon stops at this life, the poor are pure cost, a claim that returns nothing the ledger can measure, and such a ruler will, sooner or later, quietly let them slip. What makes the load bearable is a change of horizon: the man who aspires to the Hereafter finds that the very duty which crushes the worldly ruler has become, for him, the natural expression of what he already believes. The weight has not changed. The man carrying it has.

The passage on the poor flows straight into the letter's teaching on presence, and the two belong together, because the deepest failure a ruler can commit against the weak is simply to become unreachable. Apportion a part of your time, the Imam says, to those with special needs, "making yourself free to attend to them personally, sitting with them in a public assembly in all due humility before God, your Creator." And then a detail that costs a ruler his dignity on purpose: keep your soldiers and guards and officers away from that assembly, "so that they may speak to you without inhibition." The apparatus of protection is also an apparatus of insulation, and the Imam orders it stood down when the weak come to speak. He grounds the command in something he heard from the Prophet himself on more than one occasion: "A nation in which the rights of the weak are not wrested in an uninhibited manner from the strong will never be blessed." Bear patiently, he adds, any roughness or clumsiness of speech from such people; show them no irritation or disdain, and God will spread His compassion over you in the measure you spread yours over them. Give what you give graciously, and when you must refuse, refuse "with grace and apology."

Against all this outward availability the letter sets one seclusion the ruler must never surrender — the one door he closes, and it opens toward God. "Set apart the finest portion of your available time, and the greatest share of it," the Imam says, "for your soul and for what is between you and God." Perform each day the task that belongs to that day, and reserve the best of your hours for the duties owed to God alone, "the special means by which you purify your religion for Him." The busiest man in Egypt is commanded to guard, above every audience and dispatch, the private appointment with his Lord — because the ruler's justice in the daylight is drawn secretly from his standing before God in these hours, and a hub that loses its connection to the axle turns nothing at all. Even here the Imam bends the devotion toward the weak. When you lead the people in prayer, he says, do it "without repelling them or squandering it, for among them are people with infirmities or special needs." And he tells Malik how he learned this, in a memory that reaches back to his own youth: when the Prophet sent him to Yemen as his representative, he asked, "How should I lead them in prayer?" and the Prophet answered, "Lead them according to the prayer performed by the weakest among them, and be merciful to the believers." Even the pace of communal worship is set by the frailest person in the row. The whole letter is in that instruction.

It is worth dwelling on how strange this emphasis is in a manual of statecraft. Most advice to rulers has run the other way — toward distance and mystique, so that to reach the ruler becomes a privilege he can grant or withhold. The Imam's letter treats access as a duty and distance as a danger, because it measures a ruler by a standard the other manuals never invoke: not the security of his throne but the answer he will give on the day he meets God. Treat these people, he says of the weak who come to the assembly, in a manner "such that God may excuse you on the day you meet Him." The audience the ruler is really playing to is not the crowd in the hall. It is the One who will ask him, afterward, what he did with the hour. And to exactly such a ruler the Prophet had promised the one shade that will matter on that day:

> Seven will God shelter in His shade on the day when there is no shade but His — and the first of them, a just leader. — the Prophet.

The unguarded assembly, the officer sent out to find the destitute, the pace of prayer set to the weakest worshipper in the row — each is a small down payment on that shade.

The Imam then names the corrosion that accessibility exists to prevent — isolation. Do not prolong your absence from your subjects, he warns, "for the isolation of governors from those they govern creates a kind of constriction and breeds deficiency in awareness of their affairs." A sequestered ruler loses the very knowledge he needs; cut off from his people, "what is great comes to appear small and what is small, great; the beautiful appears ugly and the ugly, beautiful; the true becomes mixed with the false." He is only a human being, the Imam reminds him, who cannot see what people hide, and truth carries no visible mark to distinguish the honest report from the false one. So a ruler who walls himself off does not gain mystery or majesty; he goes blind. And there is no innocent version of the withdrawal, the Imam argues: either you are a man generous with yourself for the sake of truth — in which case, why hide, and cut yourself off from bestowing a right or doing an honourable deed? — or you are a man unwilling to give of yourself, in which case people will soon stop asking, despairing of your generosity. Either way seclusion accuses him. And most of what people actually bring, the Imam notes drily, is not even burdensome: a complaint of injustice, a plea for fairness in some dealing. The door is not heavy. A ruler only imagines it is.

Two last dangers to a ruler's openness close this movement, and both concern the people nearest him. A governor has favourites and intimates, the Imam says, and some of them are prone to presumption and self-dealing. Cut the root of that, "by granting no landed estates to anyone in your entourage or circle of friends" and giving them no reason to hope for such a grant — because an estate handed to an insider harms the neighbours in their water and their shared work, loading the burden on those nearby while the profit runs to the favourite, and leaving the governor with nothing for himself but the blame, "in this world and the next." Impose what is right on everyone it touches, the Imam says, near to you or far, "however heavily the path may lie upon you, for its outcome is praiseworthy." And when suspicion falls on the ruler himself — when his own subjects think he has done them an injustice — he is not to hide behind his office but to open it: "present your case openly so that the suspicion is cleared away from you by plain explanation." Such candour, the Imam says, is a discipline for the ruler's own soul, a kindness to his subjects, and the very thing that keeps them upright in their attachment to the truth. The just ruler, in the end, keeps no door permanently shut except the one that opens toward God.

## Covenants, blood, and last counsel

The letter turns now to a ruler's dealings with those outside his trust — his enemies — and here Ali (ع) reaches one of the moral summits of the whole document. Never turn away from a call to peace when God's pleasure lies in it, he tells Malik, "for in peace there is rest for your soldiers, relief from your cares, and security for your lands." But make peace with open eyes: once you have made it, stay fully on your guard, "for he may have drawn close only in order to catch you off guard," so take every precaution and hold even your own favourable opinion of him under suspicion. And then the thing itself — the pledge. If you and your enemy enter a solemn agreement, or he receives from you a covenant of protection, then keep it "with complete trustworthiness. Make your very life a shield for what you have promised." Of all the obligations God has laid down, the Imam says, there is none on which people stand more united than the honouring of pledges; even the pagan Arabs kept faith among themselves, "so greatly did they fear the consequences of treachery." So do not betray a covenant of protection, do not break a promise, do not deal treacherously even with an enemy — "for none dares defy God but the ignorant and wretched." God has made His own pledge and protection a sanctuary of security spread by His mercy over His servants; let no ruler introduce corruption or treachery into so sacred a thing. And should some hardship tempt Malik to break a binding agreement, the Imam sets the account plainly: patience under a difficulty whose good end you await is far better than a treachery whose consequence you dread — the fear that a reckoning from God should close in on you "from which you will find no release in this world or the next."

What is most striking in this passage is where the Imam locates the sanctity of a promise. He does not ground it in the trustworthiness of the other party — Malik is told in the same breath to keep his guard up and to suspect even his own good opinion of the enemy. He grounds it in God: a covenant of protection is sacred not because the one protected has earned it, but because God has made His own pledge a sanctuary spread by His mercy, and a ruler who breaks faith is desecrating something that belongs to God. That is why even the treachery that would plainly benefit the state is forbidden — the advantage is real, but it is set against a reckoning no advantage can survive.

From the pledge the letter moves to blood, and its warning is absolute. "Beware of shedding blood unlawfully," the Imam says, "for nothing so swiftly invites retribution, weighs more heavily in its consequence, or is more apt to end a blessing and cut short a span of life, than bloodshed without right." He reaches for the scene of final judgment to fix its gravity: on the Day of Resurrection, God "will begin His judgement among His servants with the blood they have shed." And he shuts down in advance the tyrant's favourite justification — that a little killing secures a throne: "Do not seek to strengthen your authority through unlawful bloodshed, for that in truth weakens and undermines it — indeed, it brings it down and takes it away entirely." Power that rests on spilled blood is not power; it is a countdown. The Imam then binds Malik personally, and binds himself as the one who will hold him to account: "You have no excuse before God, nor before me, if you kill anyone deliberately." And with a realism that never leaves this letter, he legislates even for the accident — the whip, the sword, or the hand that goes too far in a lawful penalty, since even a single blow can kill: do not let the pride of your authority hold you back from paying the family of the slain what is rightfully theirs. There is no rank high enough to stand above the blood-price owed to a grieving household. The scale the Imam is weighing on is the Qur'an's own scale for a single life: "whoever slays a soul — unless it be for another soul or for corruption spread in the land — it is as though he had slain all mankind; and whoever gives a soul life, it is as though he had given life to all mankind" (chapter 5, verse 32). One killing set against the whole of humanity is the measure that makes any throne bought with unlawful blood a bad bargain the moment it is struck.

Then the letter gathers itself for its final counsel, and it returns, at the end, to the same inner ground on which it opened. Beware of self-admiration, the Imam warns, of leaning on whatever pleases you in yourself, of loving to be flattered — "for these are among the surest openings Satan finds to efface the good works of the virtuous." Beware of making your subjects feel indebted to you for the good you do them, of inflating your own deeds, and of promising what you then fail to perform — for laying people under obligation destroys the good itself, self-praise puts out the light of truth, and a broken promise earns the hatred of God and of men. He seals the warning with the Book: God has said, "It is indeed hateful to God that you say that which you do not do" (chapter 61, verse 3). And he ends where the whole letter began, with the mastery of the self: "So master the heat of your pride, the sharpness of your temper, the force of your hand, and the edge of your tongue," guarding against all of it by holding back the sudden impulse and delaying the blow until the anger has passed and self-command returns. Then the single sentence that names the source of every discipline the letter has demanded: "You will never achieve this mastery over yourself unless you increase your remembrance of your return to your Lord." Everything — the mercy, the patience, the open door, the kept pledge, the unspilled blood — flows from a man keeping alive in himself the memory that he is going back to God.

It is a striking place for the letter to arrive. It has spent its length on the most external things imaginable — troop commands and tax schedules, the salaries of clerks, the punishment of hoarders — and it comes to rest on the most internal thing of all: a man's private remembrance of his own death and return. The Imam has closed a circle: he opened by refusing to let Malik govern Egypt before he had learned to govern himself, and he closes by naming the single practice that makes self-government possible — what happens in the ruler's soul in the hours when no one is watching him at all.

The Imam signs off as a man who has now made his case complete. Remember, he tells Malik, the just governance and noble custom that came before you, the practice of the Prophet, and the obligations set down in the Book of God; follow faithfully what you have seen us do; and strive with all your soul to act on what I have laid upon you in this mandate, "by which I have made my plea complete against you, so that you will have no excuse when your soul races toward its caprice." And then the letter, which has spent thousands of words instructing another man, turns at the last into a prayer for them both. "I ask God, by the vastness of His mercy and the greatness of His power to grant every desire, that He bestow upon me and upon you the success that wins His pleasure" — that they might present a clear justification before Him and before His creatures, earn the fairest praise among His servants, and leave behind the most beautiful traces in the land, "and that He seal my life and yours with felicity (al-sa'ada) and martyrdom (al-shahada). Truly unto Him we are returning" (chapter 2, verse 156). The letter that taught a governor how to hold a province ends by asking for a martyr's death — and the man who wrote it would receive exactly that. "And peace be upon the Messenger of God — God bless him and his good and pure progeny."

## What the letter leaves

From the summit of the state the letter has carried us down to its foundation and out to its edges, and the descent has been its whole meaning. The land matters more than the tax drawn from it; the scribe's hidden fault becomes the ruler's own; the merchant's freedom is watched but not crushed. Then the letter cries out over the poor and commands a ruler to appoint an officer to hunt out their need, to sit with them in an assembly stripped of his guards, to set the pace of his own prayer by the weakest body in the room, and never to wall himself off, because a secluded ruler goes blind and every seclusion accuses him. Only one door does he keep shut, and it opens toward God.

And at the last the letter weighs a ruler on two things a lesser vision would have left to expediency: the pledges he keeps and the blood he refuses to spill. Make your life a shield for your promise, even to an enemy; and beware the blood shed without right, for God begins the judgment of the worlds with it. It comes back, in the final sentences, to a single discipline — that a man will never master his pride, his temper, his hand, or his tongue until he keeps alive the memory of his return to God. The letter that opened by refusing to hand Malik an army before it had ordered his soul closes by handing him nothing at all except that memory, and a prayer that they might both die well.

## Closing

And here the book itself comes to rest. It began by drawing near to a man the tradition places closest of all to the Prophet, and it has moved through his metaphysics of the One beyond number, his epistemology of the intellect that is a light and not a calculation, his mysticism of the remembrance in which the self is polished away, and now his politics — the same teaching worn as a working garment. Everything this last letter demands of a governor is only what earlier chapters heard Ali (ع) demand of any soul that would know God: the curbing of the self that incites to evil, the good opinion of a generous God, the justice that is not fairness but the very nature of the Real, the remembrance that keeps the vertical axis of a life open and alive. The letter to Malik is the proof that this spiritual ethos was never meant to stay in the cell of the contemplative. It was meant to set tax rates, to appoint judges, to sit in an unguarded assembly with the poor, and to keep a promise to an enemy at the cost of a night's ease. A whole life stands behind it — a man who ruled under near-constant conflict, who was prevented outwardly from enacting much of the justice his soul demanded, and who never once slackened his effort or lost his hope. The book ends, as the letter ends, with a return: "Truly unto Him we are returning." Everything the series has traced — the closeness, the light, the remembrance, the justice — was, in the end, the shape of a soul making its way home.
