This episode covers the opening of the author's exploration of the spirit of the intellect, and asks the deceptively simple question of which faculty allowed Ali (ع) to know what he knew. It follows the author's decision to render the Arabic word 'aql as "intellect" rather than "reason," and traces where that single choice leads: to a battlefield conversation about whether God is one, to a oneness that lies beyond all counting, and to the buried treasures that revelation comes to awaken within us.

## Where this episode picks up

What if the very word we reach for first — "reason" — is precisely the wrong word for the way Ali (ع) knew what he knew? We are used to praising a great mind by calling it rational, logical, sharp in argument, quick to weigh one claim against another. And yet the moment we try to describe how the Imam saw into the heart of things, that vocabulary begins to feel too small, as though we were trying to measure the depth of an ocean with a ruler meant for a desk.

The previous episode was concerned with who Ali (ع) was: the one through whom others reach what they seek, the friend and master to whom loyalty is owed, the figure who did not merely receive the Qur'an and the pattern of prophetic life from outside but took them inward, until the revealed word and the prophetic nature lived in him as his own substance. That is a claim about his rank and his role — about what he was to the community and to the tradition that grew from him. But it leaves a further question standing, and it is a question about the inner life rather than the outer standing: by what power, by what organ of knowing, did he reach the realities he reached? A person can be honored, can be central, can be a gate through which others pass, and we may still not have said anything about the faculty by which that person actually perceives.

This episode turns to exactly that. It is the beginning of the author's attempt to sketch the intellectual dimensions of the Imam's spiritual ethos — to show that the intellect, far from being a cold instrument set apart from the life of the spirit, is in fact the pivot on which his whole teaching turns. The author even coins a phrase for the bond he wants us to feel: the spirit of the intellect, a spirit that surpasses while it contains the ordinary work of the thinking mind, and that reaches into regions we no longer file under the heading of intelligence at all — the whole of a person's moral bearing, the whole of their sense of what is beautiful and fitting. To follow him there, we have to begin with a matter that looks at first like a translator's footnote and turns out to be the doorway to everything else: why he insists on the word "intellect," and refuses the word "reason," for the single Arabic term that stands at the center of it all.

## Intellect, not reason

Begin, then, with the word itself. The Arabic is 'aql, and it is habitually translated "reason." The author asks us to set that translation aside and to hear instead the word "intellect" — but not "intellect" in the loose, modern, everyday sense in which it is merely a synonym for cleverness or book-learning. He means something older and more precise. He is reaching back to the meaning that the Latin word intellectus carried in Christendom, a meaning that was in turn almost identical to what the Greek word nous carried in the early Christian and Patristic writers. That is the register in which the word has to be heard if the argument is to land at all.

What did intellectus mean? At its root it meant understanding — and the author asks us to take the word "understanding" apart and to feel the picture hidden inside it. To understand a thing is, quite literally, to stand under it. When you understand something truly, you place yourself beneath a perception of reality and let that perception fall across you like a shadow, governing what you do. This is not an accident of English. The author's point is that our actions are always carried out in the shadow of how we see things — we behave in the light, or the darkness, of our perception, whether we notice it or not. So to "stand under" a reality is to let a true vision of it overshadow and direct the whole of one's conduct. Understanding, in this sense, is not a fact filed away in the mind; it is a seeing that reorganizes a life.

The intellect, then — intellectus, nous — is the faculty capable of a direct, contemplative vision of transcendent realities. Notice each of those words. Direct: not mediated, not arrived at by a chain of inferences, but a seeing. Contemplative: a looking-upon, a beholding, rather than a manipulating. And its objects are the transcendent realities — the very realities of which the Qur'an speaks, the realities hidden from the senses, the things that no eye reports and no ear delivers. The intellect is the organ by which such realities can be, in some real sense, seen.

Set beside this the faculty the author wants to distinguish from it, the faculty that properly answers to the word "reason." Its ancestors are the Latin ratio and the Greek dianoia, and both are, by their nature, indirect and discursive. Reason does not see; it works. It proceeds step by step, through logic, through a chain of moves, and it arrives not at the reality itself but at a mental concept of that reality — a picture, a definition, a formula standing in for the thing. The author points to something buried in the very root of the word ratio: it denotes a ratio in the mathematical sense, a relation or comparison between two numbers. And that etymology is not a curiosity; it is a portrait of how reason operates. Reason moves by comparison. It sets one thing beside another and measures the difference. It runs on a binary logic of affirmation and denial — this, not that; yes, not no — weighing and contrasting until it has fenced off and defined whatever it is examining. Reason is, in a word, a way of relating things to each other in order to pin them down.

From this the author draws the distinction that governs the whole essay. Reason is the instrument the intellect employs. It is not the enemy of the intellect and not a rival to it; it is a tool in its hand. But an instrument has a reach, and reason's reach has a limit. Reason can carry us as far as the outward form of a thing — as far as the mental concept, the image, the definition of a reality — and no further. It can bring us up to the door. What it cannot do is take us through it. And so the author fixes the difference in a single, memorable contrast: with the intellect one is able to contemplate, to "see," the Absolute; with reason one can only think about it. To know about a reality is one thing. To contemplate it, to experience it directly, is quite another. The first is reason's business; the second belongs to the intellect alone.

This is worth pausing on, because it is easy to mistake it for a dismissal of reason, and it is not. The author is careful. The intellect, he says, is often brought to its contemplation through reason — reason is the path by which one climbs, even if the summit is a seeing that reason itself could never have performed. The relationship is not opposition but subordination: reason serves, the intellect beholds; reason is the mode, the intellect is the principle. A person can reason their way to the edge of a truth and still never taste it; and a person can be given to taste a truth directly, and only afterward find the words and the arguments — the work of reason — by which to lay it out for others. The two are not the same power, and the confusion of the two, the quiet substitution of "reason" for "intellect," is exactly what the author is trying to undo before the essay can even begin.

This is why the author coins his phrase for the whole relationship: the spirit of the intellect. He wants a name for a spirit that surpasses the activities of the rational mind while still containing them — that does not throw reasoning away but exceeds it — and that reaches into territories we no longer file under the heading of intelligence at all. In our ordinary way of speaking, a person's intellect is one thing and their moral character is another, and their sense of beauty is a third; we keep the three in separate rooms. The author's point is that in the Imam's worldview these are not separate rooms. The spirit of the intellect encompasses a person's whole moral comportment and their aesthetic sensibility — how they bear themselves, what they find fitting, what they find beautiful — because all of these are forms of a single perceiving. And this spiritual understanding of the intellect is bound up, he tells us, with the Qur'anic conception of the spirit, al-ruh: the intellect at its root is not a faculty of the head but a reach of the spirit that God breathes into the human being. That is a large claim, and it is set here only as a horizon. The author says he will approach it through a short discourse of the utmost importance — the discourse the Imam gave to his close disciple Kumayl ibn Ziyad — reading it with the same method he uses throughout: an interpretive commentary that lets the Imam's words be illuminated by his other sayings, by concordant verses of the Qur'an, and by traditions of the Prophet. But before any of that, he wants three things fixed in our minds.

Having settled the vocabulary, the author flags three factors that he wants us to hold in mind throughout, because they are the load-bearing beams of the whole intellectual perspective he is describing, and everything that follows rests on them. The first is the centrality of revelation. In this vision the intellect is not a self-sufficient faculty spinning truths out of itself; it needs a center to revolve around, principles and definitions it can hold fast to, and that center is the revealed word. The intellect, left to orbit nothing, wanders. Given the Qur'an as its fixed center, it finds its proper motion. So thoroughly is this the case that the author will describe the Imam's whole life and thought as itself a kind of commentary on the sacred text — a ta'wil, a drawing-out of its inner meaning, enacted not only in words but in a life lived from the shrine of his birth to the mosque of his death.

The second factor is the harmony between revelation and the intellect. This is the claim that will do the most work later, and it is stated here only as a promise: that revelation and the intellect are not two competing sources of truth, one human and one divine, forever at odds, but partners in a single act of understanding. The rationalist assumes they must exclude each other — that to lean on revelation is to abdicate the intellect, and to trust the intellect is to dispense with revelation. The author's whole perspective denies this, and holds instead that the two subsist in a creative reciprocity, each completing the other.

The third factor is the very distinction we have just drawn, now named as a matter of principle: the difference between the intellect, conceived as the principle by which consciousness itself is articulated, and reason, understood as one expression, one mode, of that principle. The intellect is the wellspring; reason is one of the streams. Keep those three in view — revelation at the center, revelation and intellect in harmony, and the intellect as principle over reason as mode — and the strange scene the author turns to next, of a plain question asked on the edge of a battle, stops being strange and becomes the whole teaching in miniature.

## A oneness beyond number

Picture the moment. An army is drawn up; the battle of the Camel is about to begin; in a few breaths men will die. And out of the ranks a bedouin steps forward, approaches the Commander of the Faithful, and asks him a question that sounds, in that setting, almost absurd in its simplicity: "Do you say that God is one?" Those standing near rebuke the man at once. This is neither the time nor the place, they tell him — as though the question were a child's, to be indulged later, when the swords are back in their sheaths. But the Imam stops them. "Leave him," he says, "for surely what the bedouin wishes is what we wish for the people."

The author lingers here because the reply is a key to the entire spiritual ethos. Notice first what it reveals about the order of priorities. Had the Imam been guided by reason alone — by the sensible, calculating faculty that weighs time and place and fitness — he would have sided with those who scolded the bedouin. Of course this is not the moment for theology; of course there is a battle to fight; of course the question can wait. That is the reasonable view, and it is wrong. The Imam saw with the faculty that stands above reason, the intellect, and from that higher vantage the question was not a distraction from the battle but the very reason the battle was being fought. For him, the most fundamental intention behind any action whatsoever — whether an act of worship in the ordinary religious sense or an act of war as here — is knowledge of the true nature of ultimate reality. That is what he "wishes for the people": this knowledge, which must sit at the very heart of every concern, every motive, every deed, on the battlefield exactly as on the prayer-rug.

And so there is, the author insists, no time and no place where the question "What do you mean when you say God is one?" is out of place. There cannot be, because it is for the sake of the right knowledge of the divine — and all that such knowledge implies for how a society orders itself — that the Imam is fighting at all. The wars of his caliphate, on this reading, were not power struggles dressed up in religious language; they were battles to clarify and to enforce the oneness of God according to the principles of the Qur'an, in thought, in speech, and in action. The bedouin's question, far from interrupting that purpose, states it exactly. And so, on the very field of battle, the Imam delivers a discourse on the meaning of divine oneness.

The first move of that discourse is the one the author wants us to catch, because it overturns something we may not have noticed we assumed. The Imam refuses to let the word "one" be heard as a number. It is not permissible, he says, to say "one" of God while holding any numerical conception in the mind, for — and this is the phrase to carry away — that which has no second does not enter into the category of number. Feel the logic of it. The instant a thing can be counted as "one," it has admitted the possibility of "two," and "three," and "four"; the number one is only the first in a series and calls the rest into being by implication. A oneness of that kind is fragile, provisional, undone by the very counting that would affirm it. To say God is "one" in that sense is already to have set Him within a series, to have made Him one item, however exalted, among possible others. And that is precisely what the Imam denies. God's oneness is not the oneness of a single item; it is the oneness of that which has no second at all, and therefore never enters the counting in the first place.

The author then draws out what this really means, and it is more radical than it first appears. "That which has no second" is not merely a God to whom no partner happens to be ascribed — the way a theologian might put it, imagining rival gods that the believer simply declines to worship. It is the reality that has no otherness whatsoever. It is the Real, al-Haqq: at once transcendent and immanent, beyond all things and yet manifested by all things, at the same moment inaccessible and inalienable. To say God has no otherness is not to say that there exist potential partners which the faithful politely refuse to associate with Him. It is to say that nothing in all of existence bears His likeness — nothing that could conceivably be set beside Him, nothing of the same order that He might be counted against. The competitors are not declined; they are impossible.

In these few words, the author says, the Imam works a shift of consciousness for those who have "ears to hear." He moves the listener off the familiar ground of dogma — one God as against many gods, the true deity as against the false ones — and onto the ground of mystical apprehension: the one and only Reality, in the face of which everything else is mere appearance. This is a change in the kind of oneness being affirmed. It is no longer a count that comes out at one instead of many; it is the recognition that there is, finally, only one Reality there to be reckoned with, and that all else is its showing-forth.

And notice the strange double character the author gives to that Reality, because it is easy to hear "transcendent" and stop there, as though the point were simply that God is remote and unreachable. The Real is transcendent, yes — beyond all things, inaccessible, bearing no likeness to anything that exists. But in the same breath the author calls it immanent: manifested by all things, inalienable, nearer than the near. It is at once beyond everything and shown forth in everything, at once utterly out of reach and impossible to lose. This is not a contradiction the author has fallen into; it is the very shape of what "that which has no second" implies. A reality that had a second, that stood as one item among others, could be located — here and not there, present in this and absent from that. But a reality with no otherness cannot be fenced off in that way. There is no "outside" it for it to be absent from, and no "inside" it fills to the exclusion of the rest. So it can be described only in these paired opposites, transcendent and immanent together, which is exactly why the multiplicity of the world does not compete with the divine oneness but declares it.

And once the shift is made, the whole visible world changes character. If there is one Reality and all else is its appearance, then things are no longer opaque objects sitting there in their own right. They become signs — ayat — outward phenomena that call out to be deciphered, tokens that point beyond themselves to That of which they are the manifestations. That is what an aya is: a meaningful sign that does not rest in itself but gestures past itself to another. The world, seen this way, is not a collection of self-standing facts but a text written in things, every item of which points home. And retracing those appearances back to their source and origin is exactly what the word ta'wil literally means — a going-back to the awwal, "the First." The interpreter's task and the seeker's task turn out to be one: to lead the many appearances back to the one Reality from which they came.

The author anchors this in the revealed word from both directions. On one side, the Real is described as the beginning and the end of all things, the outward and the inward, so that there is nothing in being but this one Reality: "He is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward" (chapter 57, verse 3). Nothing stands outside that description; it closes over all of existence. On the other side, the multiplicity we see is to be carried back to the oneness that underlies it, and the promise is that this return can be made clear to us — that the signs themselves will do the leading. "We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and in their own souls, so that it be clear to them that He is the Real" (chapter 41, verse 53). Two verses, two movements: the Real named as the sole reality that is First and Last and Outward and Inward, and the many signs — those on the far horizons and those within the soul — shown to us until we read them back to Him. The bedouin asked whether God is one. The answer, unfolded, is that "one" is too small a word, and that everything that exists is a sign pointing to the one Reality that no number can hold.

## Revelation and the buried treasures

The word ta'wil has already appeared, and now it carries the weight of the third movement. Its literal sense, the author reminds us, is a return to the awwal, "the First" — the retracing of appearances back to their origin, the leading of the many home to the one. But that return does not happen by itself, and this is where the second of the three factors, the harmony between revelation and the intellect, finally does its work. For the revealed text, left to itself, cannot make the return for us. The Imam says so in words the author treats as fundamental: the Qur'an is a book inscribed between two covers; it does not speak with a tongue; it cannot do without an interpreter. The text is silent. It lies there, complete and closed, and it waits. It speaks only through the one who interprets it — and that interpreter is the intellect.

This is the reciprocity the author has been promising. The revealed word needs the intellect to be spoken; and the intellect, we will see, needs the revealed word to be awakened. The Imam draws the bond even tighter in another saying: the prophet of a man is the interpreter of his intellect. And elsewhere the correspondence is made explicit — the intellect is called "the messenger of the Real." Hold those together. There is an outer messenger, the prophet who brings the revelation from beyond; and there is an inner messenger, the intellect, which receives that revelation and renders it into understanding. The one who brings the word and the faculty that construes it are described in the same language, as though they were two reaches of a single act of communication between the Real and the human being.

This helps explain why the Imam stands so high in the discipline of interpretation. The science of ta'wil, the author notes, was taught first to the Prophet, who taught it in turn to the Imam — and the tradition remembers the depth of that inheritance in vivid form. In one saying the Imam claims that he could load seventy camels with the pages of commentary he could offer on the opening chapter of the Qur'an alone. His cousin Ibn Abbas, often called the very first of the commentators, is himself said to have learned the science of interpretation from him. These are not boasts about cleverness; they are testimony to the inexhaustibility of the inner meaning once the intellect is truly brought to the silent text. And it is why the author says the Imam's teachings should be read, at bottom, as creative interpretations of the Qur'anic revelation — a movement that runs in stages, from the letter of the revelation to its spirit, and from the spirit of the revelation to the spirit of the Revealer. The interpreter follows the text back through itself toward its source, and how far he can go depends on how fully the spirit of the intellect has been enlivened within him.

Yet there is a limit built into that ascent, and the author is careful to mark it, because it will matter later. The Revealer remains concealed even as He is revealed. The essence of what is disclosed — the divine Reality in itself — is ineffable; only the forms of its manifestation can be put into revelation at all. So the interpreter is always tracing the shown-forth back toward a source that shows itself precisely by remaining hidden. This interplay of the evident and the hidden, of form and the formless, is the root of the paradoxical way of speaking for which the Imam is famous, and which the essays will keep meeting. It is also why the author refuses to pit reflection against faith. Far from being a decree imposed from outside that shuts down thinking, revelation, he insists, presupposes a creative engagement with every resource of the intelligence — and above all with fikr, the deep and sustained reflection the Qur'an itself repeatedly demands. Such reflection does not seal meaning inside the literal surface of the words; it opens the inner horizons folded within the letter. The text carries several levels of meaning at once, and how deeply they are plumbed depends on the depth of the interpreter's own intellect. At its most profound, this "prophetic" intellect — the inner prophet, the inmost degree of a person's consciousness — rejoins the very source of the revelation, so that a person no longer merely affirms the outer verses as a matter of dogma but confirms them through lived experience, validating from within what the revelation declares from without.

Against this stands the rationalist's picture, and the author names the opposition plainly. The rationalist, in the strict sense, holds that intellect and revelation are mutually exclusive — that to accept the one is to surrender the other, that a mind is either free and rational or bound and revealed, and cannot be both. In the Imam's perspective the very reverse is true. Intellect and revelation are perfectly harmonious, subsisting in a state of creative reciprocity, each requiring the other for its completeness, its integrity — for the "making one" that is the very essence of tawhid. And the author notes how this exposes as hollow a whole crowd of forced dichotomies that later hardened into Islamic thought or were imposed upon it: reason against faith, convention against nature, blind imitation against real verification, piety against inquiry. In the Imam's ethos these are not the unbridgeable oppositions they were made to seem. Intellect and revelation are complementary, and at the highest level inseparable, because truth is one.

But why should the human intellect be able to recognize revelation as divine at all? Here the author reaches the heart of the matter, and it is a beautiful turn. Revelation, he says, is a reminder — a dhikr. It does not, in the deepest sense, bring us information we never had. A reminder never does. You cannot be reminded of what you never knew; to be reminded is precisely to have back a knowledge you already possessed and had forgotten. And this is exactly the relationship between revelation and the human spirit. For the spirit of one's intellect already carries the initial imprint of its Creator. In the world of the spirit, before this world, it witnessed its Lord and testified to His lordship — and revelation does no more than remind it of that primordial testimony. We were created into this world in a kind of amnesia, having forgotten what we once saw. And so, the author offers a homely and precise image: just as a physician tells the family of a man who has lost his memory to surround him with familiar things, with tokens out of his past, to jog what has gone dormant, so God has set His reminders in the horizons and in the souls — the very signs of that verse — to wake in us the knowledge we came in already carrying.

This is why the intellect can recognize revelation at all. The spirit of one's intellect cannot be alien to what descends as revelation from on high; on the contrary, it must be fundamentally akin to it, and it is precisely because of that kinship that a person can be certain the revelation truly comes from the Absolute. The two are recognized as belonging together because they issue from the same source — we came from a spiritual realm, and so did the revelation sent to remind us of it. The upshot is startling when the author states it flatly: the point is not to set up a dichotomy between intellect and revelation at all, but to see the intellect as revelation, or at least as one mode of revelation — the God-given means by which a person discerns and confirms the divine signs outside themselves, both the signs of scripture and the signs strewn through the natural world. The silent text and the inner light are two forms of the one thing.

All of this the author gathers into a single phrase from the first sermon of the Nahj, a phrase he says he will return to again and again: one of the reasons God sent the prophets to mankind was to unearth for them the buried treasures of the intellects — the dafa'in al-'uqul. Feel the whole teaching folded into that image. The treasures are already there, buried in us; they were not manufactured by the prophets and are not imported from outside. What the prophets do is unearth them. Revelation does not stifle the intellect or override it or replace it; it brings it to life. It is a clarion call to the spiritual depths of the intellect, cultivating the seeds of consciousness — the seeds of tawhid — that God has already planted in us and that lie buried, waiting, beneath the surface of our forgetting.

The author closes the movement with three images that make the point unmistakable, and they are all images of drawing out what is already hidden. The messengers do for these buried seeds what the farmer does when he waters what lies concealed beneath the soil, coaxing into growth a life that was already there in the seed. They do what the miner does when he digs into the mountain for the gold and silver locked inside it — not creating the ore but bringing it up into the light. They do what the diver does when he descends to the sea-bed to bring up the treasures resting there, out of sight, until someone goes down for them. Farmer, miner, diver: in each case the wealth is already present, and the work is the work of retrieval, not of manufacture. That is what revelation does to the intellect. And so the revealed text is not some foreign authority set against our intelligence; it is the outward, objective expression of the very principles the intellect already yields up from within — the outer messenger awakening and nourishing the inner one. Where, the author is careful to add, by "intellect" he means throughout not the mere faculty of reasoning but the source of consciousness itself, the deep spring that articulates the human spirit.

## What this episode lands

Gather up what the episode has established. First, the intellect is a spiritual faculty and not merely a rational one — 'aql is best heard as intellectus and nous, the power of a direct contemplative vision of transcendent realities, with reason (ratio, dianoia) as the discursive instrument it employs but never as its equal. To understand is to stand under a reality and let it govern one's life; reason can think about the Absolute, but only the intellect can see it. Second, revelation and the intellect are not rivals but partners in creative reciprocity: the revealed text is silent and cannot do without its interpreter, the intellect, while the intellect is itself awakened by the revelation it construes — the outer messenger and the inner messenger of the one Real. Third, the oneness of God lies beyond number: "that which has no second" never enters the category of counting, and names not a single deity among possible others but the one and only Reality, al-Haqq, beyond all things and manifested by all of them, so that everything else stands revealed as a sign pointing home. Fourth, ta'wil is the return of all appearances to the First, al-awwal — the retracing of the many signs, on the horizons and in the souls, back to the Real they declare. And finally, the intellect holds buried treasures, the dafa'in al-'uqul, seeds of consciousness planted before birth and forgotten in this world's amnesia; revelation is the dhikr, the reminder, that comes to unearth them as the farmer, the miner, and the diver draw up what already lay hidden.

## Closing

If all of this is so — if the intellect is a faculty of vision and not merely of calculation, and if reason is only the instrument it holds — then a question presses in that the next stage of the exploration must face directly. What happens when reason, that comparing and measuring faculty, is turned toward the very Reality that has no second, no likeness, nothing to compare it with? A power that works by relating one thing to another must falter before that which stands in relation to nothing. There is a limit here, and it is not a flaw in reason but the honest edge of what reason is for — and at that edge something breaks open that reason alone could never have reached. That is where the path leads next: to the point where the discursive mind exhausts itself, and to the paradox that begins exactly where its measuring ends. And it leads, too, back toward the spirit — the breath that was breathed into the human being at the first — for it is that spirit, animating the intellect from within, that recognizes the divine in revelation because it is not, in the final reckoning, bound by human limits at all. The buried treasure and the breath that quickens it are the same theme, approached from two sides, and the essays return to it again and again.
