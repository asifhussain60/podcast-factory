This episode follows the Letter to Malik al-Ashtar into its most demanding stretch: the passage where Ali (ع) lays out the whole anatomy of a society and assigns to each of its classes what is due. We trace power understood as a trial rather than a prize, the wheel whose classes turn only on a hidden axis, the giving of each class its rightful share, and the sharpest test of justice of all — the claim of the poor, and the pure intention that alone makes it possible to honour.

## Where this episode picks up

What good is the hub of a wheel if the axle it turns on is one it can never see? The letter had left the governor at exactly that point — placed at the centre of everything, the fixed axis around which the soldiers and the scribes and the judges and the tax-collectors all revolve, the one man to whom every thread of the state runs back. And yet, for all that centrality, powerless in himself. A hub is the most important part of a wheel and also the most helpless: everything depends on it, and it depends, in turn, on something it cannot supply from within its own circle.

That is the tension this episode turns on, and it grows directly out of where the letter's preceding counsel ended. There we watched the Imam build the governor from the inside — the disposition of his heart, the mastery of his own anger, the mercy he owes those beneath him, the counsellors he must and must not keep near. All of that was preparation for the moment the letter now reaches, when the inward man is turned outward and asked to govern an entire society made of competing parts. It is one thing to describe the ideal ruler's soul; it is another to describe the machine of state he must actually turn, and to keep the two descriptions from ever coming apart. The letter's wager is that they need not — that the same conviction which orders a man's inner life can order a whole kingdom, provided he never once forgets on whose behalf he holds the office, and never mistakes the centre he occupies for a strength that originates in him.

So the letter does something remarkable here. It stops speaking of the governor alone and begins to enumerate the classes of people beneath him — the army, the scribes, the officers, the judges, the collectors, the merchants, the artisans, and, last and most easily forgotten, the destitute. Each is given its rights and its duties. But before a single class is named, the Imam plants the principle that governs all of them: that power is not a possession but a trust, held on loan from God and answerable to Him. Get that wrong, and the whole wheel spins loose from its axle. Get it right, and the anatomy of society becomes an anatomy of worship. This is where we begin — with power itself, and what a man is really holding when he holds it.

## The wheel of society

The letter has already warned Malik that his authority over his subjects is a trial from God and not a privilege over them, and that single sentence carries the Imam's own attitude to rulership entire. We are not left to infer it. In a famous sermon of his own, he says plainly that he accepted power only as an unavoidable duty, and the image he chooses to say it with is deliberately undignified. Had God not taken from the learned a promise that they would not acquiesce in the rapacity of the tyrant nor in the hunger of the oppressed, he declares, "I would truly have flung its reins back upon its withers, and you would indeed have discovered that this world of yours is as insignificant to me as that which drips from the nose of a goat." The reins are the reins of the caliphate; the withers are the back of the beast he would let it run off on; and the thing that keeps him in the saddle is not appetite for the office but a covenant that forbids the knowing man to stand by while the strong devour the weak. Power, to him, weighs like a debt.

There is a striking meeting here between the Imam and the ideal Plato drew of the true philosopher who consents to rule. For Plato, the person fit to govern is precisely the one who does not want to, who takes office "not regarding it as a distinction, but as an unavoidable task," caring only for justice as the one indispensable thing his state exists to serve. Both pictures reverse the ordinary logic of ambition. The office is not a reward the ruler collects; it is a burden the community lays on the shoulders best able to bear it, and the best shoulders are the ones that feel its full weight rather than its glamour.

Underneath this lies a principle the Imam states with memorable economy. Rebuked once into modesty by a supporter's praise, he answered that "the blessings of God upon a person do not become immense without the right of God over him becoming immense." This is the ancient truth that privilege obligates: to be granted knowledge, or justice, or power, is not to be handed something to spend but to be handed a claim against oneself. The more one has received, the more is asked. A governor is simply a man to whom a very great deal has been given, and therefore a man against whom God's claim runs very deep.

From this the letter draws a warning about the company a ruler keeps, and it is sharper than it first appears. Malik is told to exclude from his counsel the miser who will frighten him from generosity, the coward who will make him doubt his own strength, and the greedy man who will dress up avarice as good sense — three different faces of one fault, since, in the letter's own summary, "miserliness, cowardice and avarice are diverse inclinations, all united by a bad opinion of God." That last phrase is the whole point, and it is easy to read past. A bad opinion of God, su' al-zann, is not a mood; it is a mistaken theology, a misreading of the divine nature that quietly produces the vices as its symptoms. The miser is a man who secretly believes God may not provide for him tomorrow. The coward secretly believes God may not protect him. The greedy man secretly believes God has given him less than he needs. Trace each vice to its root and you find the same false idea of who God is. All these vices, on this reading, trace back to a kind of intellectual dysfunction — a fault not first in the will but in the understanding — and that dysfunction is itself the fruit of weak faith, or in the limit of outright disbelief, since to deny God's reality is only the most extreme form of holding a bad opinion of Him.

The converse follows just as tightly, and it is the constructive half the letter leaves implicit. A strong and living faith generates the opposite — a good opinion of God, husn al-zann — a truer perception of the divine reality, and out of that perception the corresponding virtues grow of their own accord. The man who knows God as generous can afford to be generous; the man who trusts God's protection can afford courage; the man certain of God's provision has no need to hoard. Character, on this reading, is downstream of belief about God. Advisers who counsel meanness and fear are not merely unpleasant; they are theologians of a small God, and they will make the ruler's God small too.

There is a further turn to this that the letter leaves for the attentive listener to complete. If the virtues grow from a true perception of God, then the way to acquire them is to contemplate the divine qualities and let the soul be reshaped by them — to be generous because God is the Generous, to be merciful and compassionate because He is the Merciful and the Compassionate, and never because one imagines these things to originate in oneself. Yet the emulation runs alongside its exact opposite. The same soul must also be lowly and self-effacing, because no height of virtue can close the infinite distance between the Creator and the created. One is at once the servant of God and His representative on earth, and the two must be held together: servanthood keeps the representative from sliding into mistaking himself for what he represents, while the duty of representing God keeps the servant from collapsing into a paralysed forgetfulness of the dignity his station carries. That balance is itself a form of justice — the placing of each thing in its right order — and it is the inner disposition every line about the classes presupposes.

Having set the disposition, the letter turns to the structure, and it does so by invoking a covenant: God has ordained the share of every class and set down its limits in His Book and the Sunna of His Prophet ﷺ. The share of each class and the limit of each is not a policy the governor devises but a trust, an 'ahd, handed down and preserved. That word carries the weight of the whole Qur'an behind it, and the Imam is drawing on that weight deliberately, on verses that make the covenant a mark of the discerning soul: "those who abide by the covenant of God, and break not the tryst" (chapter 13, verses 19 to 20), and the blunt reminder that "the covenant will be asked about" (chapter 17, verse 34). To govern each class justly is therefore not administration; it is covenant-keeping, and a covenant, the verse warns, is a thing one will be questioned on. The Prophet had pressed the same charge into an image every listener could carry: each of us is a shepherd, he taught, and each is answerable for his flock — the ruler most of all, a shepherd answerable for those in his keeping. A shepherd owns nothing of the flock he tends; he simply answers for it. To hold a class of people in trust is to be handed sheep that were never one's own, and to be asked, at the last, how one kept them.

Just before laying out the classes, the Imam gives Malik a piece of advice that quietly names the faculty all of this requires. Study long with the scholars, he says — al-ulama, those who know the formal rules, the letter of the law as fixed in the Book and the Sunna. But converse long, too, with the sages — al-hukama, those who know how to apply them, which is the spirit of wisdom. The distinction matters enormously. Knowing the rule and knowing how to lay the rule against a living situation are two different competences, and a ruler armed with only the first is dangerous. Wisdom is knowledge put into practice, the sensitivity without which even correct rules are misapplied. A just society needs both the letter and the spirit, and the spirit is the harder of the two to acquire. The purpose the Imam names for all this study is not learning for its own sake but a practical one — to consolidate whatever brings well-being to the lands under Malik's charge, and to keep standing whatever his predecessors had already set on a sound footing.

Then comes the image that gathers the passage. The Imam describes the interdependence of the classes as a wheel. The governor is the hub, operating on the horizontal plane among his fellow creatures; the classes — the army, the scribes, the executive officers, the judges, the tax-collectors, the merchants, the artisans, and the needy — are the spokes radiating out from him to the rim. Yet the hub itself is nothing without the axle that turns it. This horizontal centre is useless without the vertical axis that connects it, and through it the whole of society, to the Real. Just as a hub is dead weight without its connection to the axle, all the governor's efforts are dead weight without his connection to the Absolute. He may sit at the centre of every human relationship in the state and still, if that vertical line is severed, turn nothing at all. The classes are the visible wheel; the axle is invisible; and only the axle makes the wheel a wheel.

The Imam fills the image out with a telling detail before he leaves it. The soldiers, he says, are the fortresses of the subjects — the walls behind which ordinary life can go on unafraid. And he closes the passage by telling the governor plainly that he cannot accomplish what God requires of him without resolute determination and recourse to God — without leaning, at the decisive moment, on the very axle the image has been describing. It is a point he will drive home again when the letter reaches the poor, and it is worth holding onto now: the wheel is turned not by the exertion of the hub but by the hub's dependence. The one who has grasped the axle knows that his strength is borrowed, and that the borrowing is the whole secret of the thing.

## Each class its due

The passage that follows is a small masterpiece of compression, and its ordering is deliberate. It opens with the army as the foundation on which the state rests and closes with the governor himself as the centre around which every other element revolves, so that simply reading through it is a journey from the outermost defence of society inward to its axis. Between those two poles the Imam threads the scribes, the officers, the judges, the collectors, the merchants and the artisans, and at each step he shows how one class leans upon another — how the soldier cannot stand without the revenue the collector gathers, how the collector gathers nothing without the land the tiller works, how none of it holds together without the justice the judge dispenses. It is an anatomy in which no organ is self-sufficient: interdependence all the way down, and dependence, at the last, on God. The letter's own words will walk this same ground again, in full, when we reach it directly later in the book; from so rich a passage here, it is enough to draw out the most telling points.

With the axle in place, then, the Imam walks the wheel spoke by spoke, and the first spoke is the army. For the command of his troops Malik is told to choose a man marked above all by hilm — that steady, unhurried forbearance — gentle with the weak and firm with the strong, quick to pardon and slow to anger, sincere in his devotion and pure of heart. Read that description again and notice how strange it is as a portrait of a soldier. Nearly every quality named is a quality of gentleness. The martial virtues are almost taken for granted; what the Imam labours to specify are the softer ones — mildness, mercy, the readiness to forgive — precisely the qualities one does not expect from a man whose trade is force. One discerns here a spiritually refined approach to an activity which, left to its own devices, easily runs roughshod over the ordinary norms of decency and propriety. He is refining that activity from within, and he does it by insisting that the best fighter is the gentlest man — that gentleness is not a weakness bolted onto the warrior as an afterthought but the very thing that makes his strength safe to unleash.

The resemblance to Plato surfaces again here, and it is exact. Plato worried over the central paradox of the guardian, the protector of the ideal city: how does one raise men fierce enough to defend the state without their fierceness turning on the very people they guard? They must, he concluded, "be gentle to their own people and dangerous only to their enemies." That is the same double demand the Imam makes, and it is the same double demand the Qur'an makes of those with the Prophet, describing them as "severe against the disbelievers, compassionate amongst themselves" (chapter 48, verse 29). The ideal warrior is not a tempered version of a brute; he is a man in whom ferocity and tenderness are both fully present and correctly aimed. The Imam, of course, was describing himself.

The judges come next, and the portrait is one of a soul the world cannot bend. Two temptations threaten a judge above all others, and the Imam names them first: the complexity of a hard case, which tempts him to reach for the nearest easy answer rather than the true one, and the pressure of the litigants, who between them will try every lever of provocation and appeal to move him off the truth. Against both he must be unflinching before the truth, free from greed, unsatisfied with easy answers, and immune equally to flattery and to pressure. Then the Imam adds a rueful line that undercuts nothing and confirms everything: "Such people are indeed rare." He knows the specification is severe. His response is characteristically practical — pay them generously, so that any lack is removed and their need for help from others is diminished. A judge who wants for nothing owes nothing to anyone, and only such a judge is safe to trust with a verdict. Incorruptibility is partly a matter of character and partly a matter of salary, and the Imam refuses to pretend otherwise.

For the administrators who handle the state's affairs and its funds, the counsel turns vigilant. Test their honesty before you employ them, the Imam tells Malik; let no partiality or favouritism decide your appointments; and once they are in place, keep watching, so that any abuse of privilege or misappropriated money is caught and punished firmly. There is no naivety here about the corrupting pull of proximity to wealth and power. Trust is extended, but it is extended with the eyes open, on evidence, and it is never allowed to harden into the assumption that a man once found honest stays honest unwatched.

Then the letter reaches the tillers of the soil, and the maxim the Imam offers them is one of the most quietly radical lines in the whole document: "Let your concern with the cultivation of the land outweigh your concern with the collection of the tax." The letter's own words will carry the rest of that instruction in full when it reaches us directly, but its logic can be stated plainly here. Tax without cultivation starves the very ground it draws from; a ruler fixated on the yield rather than the harvest is eating his own kingdom, and the true, lasting wealth of a land is the flourishing of the people who work it, not the sum collected from them. The Imam does not stop at the economics of it, either; he presses Malik to attend to the needs of those who work the land and to be scrupulously fair to everyone seeking redress of a wrong, so that the humblest cultivator has as real a claim on the governor's ear as the powerful.

The Imam then names the deeper disease behind such ruin: land falls to ruin through the destitution of its people, and that destitution takes root when a governor's soul is given over to amassing wealth, "when they have doubts about what endures." That last phrase — su' zann bi'l-baqa — is the heart of it. The commentators read it as a governor's forgetting that he will die and be judged, a doubt about what truly lasts. Greed, on this reading, is not first an appetite but a blindness — a man reaches for the material only because the enduring has slipped from his sight. The Imam even uses the same verb for amassing that the Qur'an uses in its own scorching warning: "Woe be to every slandering backbiter, who amasseth wealth and counteth it. He thinketh that his wealth will render him immortal. Nay, but he will be flung to the all-consuming Fire" (chapter 104, verses 1 to 4). The hoarder counts his coins as though they could buy him permanence. They cannot. What looks like a single unjust act, in the Imam's vision, is really the tail end of a much longer chain: a soul that has stopped perceiving what lasts, giving way to greed, giving way to exploitation, giving way at last to a wasted, exhausted land. Greed is not first a moral failure but a perceptual one, and the oppression it breeds is only its outward fruit.

The scribes receive a short but pointed instruction, and it too is sealed with a spiritual message. Malik must resist the easy temptation to appoint the men who have shown him their most polished face. He is to examine each candidate's actual record and the impression he has left on ordinary people, and appoint only those known and proven trustworthy where it counts. To do this rather than be flattered into a poor choice is described, strikingly, as proof of the governor's own sincerity toward God. Even a clerical appointment, made rightly, is an act of worship; made lazily, a small betrayal.

Last among the working classes come the merchants and the craftsmen, and the Imam's attitude to them is balanced. They are the veins through which a society's goods and needs travel, and on the whole they are peaceable people who should be left to pursue their trades undisturbed. But peace is not the same as neglect: the vices to which commerce is prone — hoarding, miserliness, the cheating of customers — must be kept in check, for a trade left entirely unwatched curdles into exploitation. Leave them free, the Imam is saying, but not unwatched; the market is a good servant and a bad master.

## The poor and pure intention

Now the letter arrives at the passage the Imam most wants Malik to take to heart, and he marks its urgency with a cry to God before he says anything else — a cry the letter's own words will voice in full when they reach us directly. The subject is the poor, those with no resources whatsoever: the destitute, the crippled, the orphans, the elderly, and those too proud to beg, whose need is invisible precisely because they will not display it. These, the Imam insists, are the people most in need of justice from their ruler, owed a treatment for which God alone can excuse him on the day they meet. He tells Malik to appoint an officer with the specific charge of uncovering their needs and attending to them. The lowest spoke of the wheel, the one furthest from power and easiest to forget, is the one the Imam circles back to and underlines twice. That charge — to go looking for the need that hides itself rather than waiting for it to announce itself — still runs through the Ismaili tradition that looks to the Imam. Across his own decades of guidance the Aga Khan pressed the same instinct into the language of institutions: that a community is measured by how it treats those with the least standing to demand anything, and that real care goes out searching for the overlooked rather than waiting at a counter for them to present themselves. The officer the Imam appoints to uncover invisible need is the seed from which that whole ethic grows.

He knows what he is asking. "This is onerous for the governors," he admits, "and the fulfilment of all rights is onerous" — but, he adds, God lightens that load for the soul that aspires to the Hereafter, that has schooled itself to patience, and that trusts what God has promised. That sentence is the hinge of the entire section, and it explains something the rest of the letter has been quietly building toward. The demand to care for those from whom no political advantage can ever be won is, by any worldly calculus, crushing. It costs, and it returns nothing the ledger can measure. What makes it bearable — what makes it possible at all — is the spiritual dimension. It is only when this world is seen through to the next that a fully just attitude toward this world becomes possible. A ruler whose horizon stops at this life will, sooner or later, take the easy road and let the poor fall away, because in the horizontal accounting of power the poor are pure cost. And yet even that ledger is in error, for the Prophet had said, in the bluntest possible terms, that a community is granted its victory and its provision only through its weak ones — that the sustenance of the strong reaches them by way of the very people the strong are tempted to overlook. The destitute are not the dead weight of the state; unseen, they are a channel through which its life is supplied. Charge the duty with real faith in God's justice and real longing for the Hereafter, and the same duty becomes not a burden to be shed but an inescapable consequence of what one believes.

Notice, too, the order in which the Imam lists the qualities that make the load light, because it is not accidental. First comes aspiration for the Hereafter; then patience; then trust in the truth of God's promise. Patience is placed second for a reason: once a person genuinely sees the absolute values at stake — not as an idea entertained but as something almost tasted — patience is no longer a matter of gritting the teeth and enduring by force of will. It can be extended indefinitely, because it is now sustained by the grace that flows from living faith rather than drawn from a finite reserve of personal resolve. And this is why the attitude the Imam describes goes far beyond conventional charity. A person who has truly realized it cannot be talked out of it by any appeal to self-interest or pragmatism, because his fidelity to the destitute no longer rests on a calculation that a cleverer calculation could overturn. It rests on something the calculators cannot see. That blindness is the mark of the oppressor: the zalim, among al-zalimun, is not merely one who breaks the rules of justice but one who violates the compassion that lies at the very heart of the divine nature — and who is, for that reason, blind at once to the promise of felicity and to the warning of retribution.

This is where the letter's deepest theme comes to the surface: the question of intention. What ultimately sustains a man in a task that yields him nothing visible is that he is no longer acting for the visible yield. And here the Imam draws his famous threefold distinction. "There is a group who worship God out of desire," he says, "and this is the worship of the merchants; and a group who worship out of fear, and this is the worship of the slaves; and a group who worship out of gratitude, and this is the worship of the free." The merchant serves for a reward not yet in hand; the slave serves to avoid a punishment; but the free man serves out of thankfulness for what he has already been given. The joy of that already-given certainty is a theme in its own right; here the fresh point is the three postures themselves, and above all the highest of them — the man who acts not from hope of gain nor fear of loss but from sheer gratitude, and who is therefore beyond the reach of both discouragement and bribery.

The logic of gratitude turns on a striking inversion of time. For the one whose certainty of the reward to come is absolute, the future has in a sense already arrived; the beatitude is not merely awaited but, in one respect, already possessed. The Qur'an speaks in this register when it tells the Prophet, "We have given thee the paradisal fountain of abundance; so pray to thy Lord and sacrifice" (chapter 108, verses 1 to 2) — the gift in the past tense, the worship following as its consequence rather than its price. This is worship as thanksgiving for something held, not petition for something owed, and it is the posture of the free — and a man who serves out of gratitude for a good already received cannot be discouraged by the failure of his efforts, because the ground of his service was never their success in the first place.

The Qur'an gives this posture a living face, and it is the face of Ali (ع) himself. The commentators agree that a certain passage was revealed on the occasion of an episode in his own household: for three consecutive days he and his family went without food, because at each evening's breaking of the fast someone in greater need came to the door, and each night they gave away the meal they were about to eat. The Qur'an records what such people say as they give: "They feed, out of love for God, the needy, the orphan and the prisoner, saying, 'We feed you only for the sake of God; we desire from you neither reward nor thanks'" (chapter 76, verses 8 to 9). Neither reward nor thanks — not even the small return of being thanked. The same purity is named again in the description of the truly pious man who gives of his wealth "seeking only the Countenance of his transcendent Lord, and he will be content" (chapter 92, verses 17 to 21). The gift is aimed past every recipient, straight at God.

This opens onto the crucial principle: that actions are weighed by their intentions and not by their outcomes. The Sufi masters distilled the same law into a single line about what gives a deed its life:

> Deeds are but lifeless forms; their soul is the secret of sincerity hidden within them. — Ibn Ata Allah al-Iskandari

A form without a soul is a corpse, however correct its shape; and the soul of any just act is the intention that no onlooker can inspect. The just man intends justice for its own sake, not for any reward it might fetch, because justice is not one attribute among God's others but is one with the divine nature itself. And this changes everything about how he meets failure. If he acted for results, then a defeated result would defeat him. But because he acts to conform to the nature of the Real, the outward frustration of his effort — by circumstance, by enemies, by the sheer unpredictability of political life — cannot touch the core of what he was doing. He never despairs, because achieving justice in this world was never the whole of his aim. The sincere intention is already, in a way invisible but spiritually palpable, its own fulfilment; the Imam elsewhere describes such people as having their hearts in the Gardens while their bodies are at work. A secular conception of justice stands or falls by its visible success. A sacred conception already finds its ideal fulfilled in the sincerity of the intention to be just.

That is not, however, a licence for passivity, and the Imam guards the point carefully. The sincerity of an intention is proven only by ceaseless striving toward its realization; without concordant, unyielding action, the intention is shown to be shallow and merely theoretical. The just man does not rest content in a good intention while injustice stands; his effort is as tireless as the reward he believes in is inexhaustible, and a slackening of that effort would expose the intention itself as insincere. What frees him from despair is not indifference to the outcome but the knowledge that the outcome, ultimately, rests with God, while the striving rests with him — which is the reverse of the suspicion one might have of the whole doctrine, that it hands the failed or lazy ruler an excuse to legitimate real injustice by pointing to good but thwarted intentions. The doctrine does not lower the demand for action; it removes only the despair that would otherwise follow when action, honestly given, does not succeed. That Ali (ع) himself lived under exactly this pressure the letter never lets us forget: his own years of rule were a near-continuous succession of conflicts that prevented him outwardly from enacting much of what his sense of justice demanded, and yet none of it produced in him dejection, cynicism, or the smallest slackening of effort. Such despair, in fact, is almost inconceivable for a soul shaped by the conviction that no act is negligible if it is accompanied by piety — that the smallest just deed, done in the awareness of God, is never lost. And so the passage closes on the note that is the way out of the whole dilemma — piety, in the deep sense of the permanent awareness of God, as the very thing that opens an exit from despair. "He who has pious awe towards God," the Imam says, "for him God establishes a way out of tribulation, and a light out of darkness." That way out is the makhraj: the door in the wall of every earthly injustice, the light that no darkness of circumstance can finally close over the soul that keeps its face turned toward the Real.

## What this chapter lands

The listener now holds the whole design in a single image. Society is a wheel, and its many classes — the army, the scribes, the officers, the judges, the collectors, the merchants, the artisans, and the poor — are the spokes that radiate from the governor at the hub. But the hub turns nothing on its own. It turns only on the vertical axle that joins it, and through it the entire wheel, to the Real. Sever that connection and the most powerful man in the state moves nothing; keep it, and the ordinary business of governing becomes an extension of worship.

Within that turning wheel each class receives its due — the fighting man his required gentleness, the judge his incorruptible independence, the tiller his flourishing land valued above the tax it yields, the clerk his proven honesty, the merchant his watched freedom. And the sharpest test of the whole system, the Imam insists, is the lowest spoke of all: the claim of the destitute, from whom no ruler can ever profit. That claim is a burden only a soul aspiring beyond this world can carry, and it is carried not by calculation but by gratitude and pure intention — by acting justly because justice is the nature of God, and leaving the outcome in His hands. But where does a ruler find the strength to keep that vertical line intact under the daily pressure of power? The letter has one more thing to say, and it locates the hidden spring from which all of this actually flows. That spring is prayer, and it is where the letter turns last.

## Closing

For everything we have traced in this episode — the wheel and its axle, the giving of each class its due, the unyielding claim of the poor — points back to a single source, and the letter names it last because it comes first. All the governor's justice, all his gentleness and vigilance and self-forgetting care for those who can give him nothing, has to be drawn from somewhere; it cannot be manufactured by effort alone, and it drains away under strain unless it is continually replenished. The Imam's answer is the one the whole tradition returns to: the ruler's justice flows from his prayer, and from the remembrance of God that keeps the vertical axis of his soul open and alive. The letter follows on to that hidden wellspring — to the moments of standing before God from which every just act in the daylight of governance secretly draws its water. We have watched the wheel turn. What remains is to look down the axle, past the hub, to the still point where the ruler meets his Lord, and to see that this, and not the machinery of state, is where the whole of his justice is really born.
