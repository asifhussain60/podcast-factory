# Justice Versus Tyranny

*Having grounded justice in the nature of God, Reza Shah-Kazemi's study turns to justice's clearest opposite — tyranny — and finds it, surprisingly, to be a spiritual disorder before it is a political one. Following the sombre opening of Ali (ع)'s letter to Malik al-Ashtar, this episode examines the soul that incites to evil, the pride that a ruler misappropriates from God, and the strange doctrine that the oppressor is, quite literally, at war with God. The argument is that outward corruption is only the symptom; its root is an inner denial of the sole reality of God — a denial that turns first against the tyrant's own conscience and his own deepest nature.*

## Where this episode picks up

The most dangerous moment in a ruler's life may not be when he breaks a law, but when he keeps one and lets it make him feel superior. That is the counterintuitive place the letter to Malik al-Ashtar begins, and it is where this episode picks up the thread. Having established that justice is the placing of everything in its right place, an attunement to the nature of God Himself, Shah-Kazemi now turns the argument over to look at its shadow. If justice is putting things in their right place, tyranny is a specific and terrible misplacement: the soul putting itself where only God belongs. And because the letter is addressed to one of the Imam's closest and most trusted companions — a man of proven integrity, not a known villain — its warnings about injustice carry a precise force. They are not aimed at monsters. They are aimed at the good man who has just been handed power.

That is the sober realism sounding from the letter's first lines. The Imam's understanding of injustice transcends both its historical moment and the formal boundaries of the faith. The letter speaks not only to those who wish to govern justly but to all those who, though not corrupt at the outset, are corruptible — and that category, Shah-Kazemi notes, embraces the overwhelming majority of humanity. The maxim that power corrupts finds a sharper form in the Imam's own words: "he who rules, appropriates." Something in the very fact of command tempts the one who holds it to seize for himself what is not his. The letter's whole opening movement is an attempt to inoculate a good man against that temptation before it takes hold, and it does so not by tightening the rules but by going underneath them, to the condition of the soul from which either justice or tyranny will flow. This is the feature that lifts the letter above the ordinary literature of governance. Political and legal texts try to constrain a ruler from the outside, with rules and penalties; the Imam works from the inside, on the disposition that decides whether the rules will be honored at all. He knew that a corrupt heart finds its way around any rule, and an upright one hardly needs them — so his first concern is not the statute but the soul that will administer it.

Three movements carry the episode. First, the soul that incites to evil — the Qur'anic teaching, drawn from the story of Joseph, that the human soul left to itself tends downward, and that only grace, actualized through effort, redirects it. Second, pride — the vice the Imam warns against most relentlessly, understood not as a mere character flaw but as the arrogation of a divine attribute, an implicit denial of God's uniqueness that amounts to a kind of war against Him. And third, the interior geography of that war: how tyranny turns against the tyrant's own conscience, violates his own deepest nature, and reveals that the oppressor is, before he is anything else, one who oppresses his own soul.

## Power and the commanding soul

The letter opens with a command that sounds conventional and is not: Malik is to place obedience to God above everything, to abide by what God has commanded in His Book, "for no one prospers except through abiding by them, and no one is wretched except through repudiating and neglecting them." What makes this more than boilerplate piety is the reason the Imam immediately gives for it. Obedience to the divine commands is not only legally binding; it is spiritually liberating — because it frees a person from the tyranny of what the Qur'an calls "the soul which incites to evil." Right after calling Malik to obedience, the Imam quotes it: "For truly, the soul incites to evil, unless God has mercy." The sentence is almost word for word the confession of the prophet Joseph, and its placement at the head of a letter on governance is deliberate and weighty.

To feel its force, Shah-Kazemi returns to the scene in the Qur'an where Joseph speaks these words. He says them after his innocence has been fully established before the Egyptian king — at the very moment he might have congratulated himself. Instead he says: "I do not exonerate myself. Truly, the soul incites to evil, unless my Lord has mercy." On the two occasions he had been subjected to seduction, the Qur'an is careful to attribute his successful resistance not to his own moral strength but to the grace of God. This is the theme the Imam wants planted in Malik's mind and in the mind of anyone reading a letter on power: human helplessness in the face of attractive but immoral gratification. It is a susceptibility to allurement on every plane, gross and subtle alike — a susceptibility that cannot be neutralized by the soul itself but only by the mercy and grace of God. A governor is exposed to it in a heightened form, since the temptations that lie in wait around wealth and authority are both more numerous and more refined than those an ordinary person meets; and the very reminder of one's helplessness is what keeps the door open to the grace that is the only real defense. Here are the two poles of attraction for the ordinary soul, the soul not yet at peace — its own base inclinations, pulling it toward the fleeting satisfactions of the world of appearances, and the higher orientation toward the grace of God, drawing it back toward the pure positivity of ultimate reality. The very sharpness of the contrast in that one Qur'anic sentence galvanizes an awareness of the soul's absolute and unconditional need of God in every circumstance. It could hardly be a more fitting way to open a letter to a man about to be vested with authority over a vast and wealthy country where temptation lies in wait on every side.

The essential relationship determining the spiritual substance of the soul, then — and therefore its moral conduct — is the relationship between the human soul and divine grace. Left without grace, the soul tends toward evil; assisted by God, it is liberated from its base inclinations and guided back toward its true, primordial nature, its fitra. The Qur'an names that nature directly: "So set thy purpose for religion with unswerving devotion: the nature of God, according to which He created man. There is no altering God's creation. That is the right religion, but most men know not" (chapter 30, verse 30). The actual state of the fallen soul is called to give way to the ideal state of this original nature — a nature that is at once the origin and the end of the human condition, and the true substance of every human soul, always accessible in principle even when, for most people, it stays clouded in practice. This is the same territory the opening chapter mapped in its teaching on the greater struggle against the soul; here the Imam applies it not to the seeker in his cell but to the governor in his palace, where the stakes of the inner war are multiplied by the power at his disposal.

But grace and effort are not opposites in this account, and the point matters because it guards against a fatalism the letter could otherwise seem to invite. When Joseph says the soul inclines to evil "unless my Lord has mercy," he is not excusing passivity. His own vision of the evidence of God implied active discernment and, as its moral consequence, resolute self-control. The human effort to avoid sin does not contradict the necessity of grace; it expresses a grace already bestowed, already present within the conscientious soul. The very capacity of the will to strive for virtue is itself a pre-existent grace embedded in the spiritual constitution — a grace, however, that must be rendered present, actualized, through sincere effort to draw upon this God-given power. This is why the early part of the letter takes on such a markedly negative, restraining character: the commands to "break the soul in the face of passionate desires," to "dominate your inclination," to "withhold yourself from that which is not lawful for you." Shah-Kazemi draws out why the negative register dominates here. The question of how to refrain from injustice, he argues, is far more difficult and subtle than its positive counterpart, how to act with justice. The Imam sets out the positive rules of governance in a direct and straightforward manner; what is far less straightforward is any office-holder's actual capacity to keep faith with those rules when confronted with the temptations power offers. One might frame laws to punish corruption, but what cannot be legislated is the will or the capacity to remain incorruptible in the face of political authority's blandishments. The Imam knew that the positive rules of governance are the easy part; the hard part is the ruler's actual power to keep faith with them under temptation. And that power, Shah-Kazemi argues, is exactly what a lived spirituality supplies and a merely formal obedience cannot. Without the spiritual grounding, injunctions to be fair and honest remain abstract postulates rather than unquestionable realities, and obedience stays external — compliance with something imposed from without rather than the moral concomitant of one's own inmost being. With it, living justly ceases to be a matter of obeying an imposition and becomes an identification with what is right, with al-Haqq as such — a shift, as the opening chapter put it, from doing to being, in which action is not weakened but made more real.

That last phrase is precise, and it names the mechanism by which spirituality strengthens rather than dilutes the effort to be just. An integral approach to justice leads to an identification with what is right rather than merely to the doing of what is right — a shift of consciousness from doing to being, from action to contemplation, without action being weakened in the slightest. On the contrary, action comes to reflect, on its own plane, the divine object of contemplation, and so becomes more, not less, effective. An orientation toward the Real imparts a deeper degree of reality to what one does and to one's own mode of being: one does what is right insofar as one is real. The Islamic spiritual tradition has a word for this process of realization — the making-real of the soul, in which one's ethical comportment is rendered real through spirituality, and spirituality in turn is expressed and made concrete through the practice of virtue. This is the exact opposite of the tyrant's condition. Where the just man grows more real by conforming to the Real, the oppressor grows more illusory by seceding from it — which is why the letter can treat corruption and unreality as two names for the same descent.

## Pride as war with God

Then comes a warning that stops the reader short. "Do not set your soul up for war with God," the Imam tells Malik. What could that possibly mean? What kind of action places a soul at war with God? The obvious answer — outright disobedience, the violation of religious obligation — is the least of it. Shah-Kazemi argues the allusion reaches deeper, to subtler modes of opposition that can accompany even outward acts of obedience and yet contradict their very spirit. The cardinal instance is pride. A ruler's pride, the Imam teaches, is not merely an unattractive trait; it is a misappropriation of a divine attribute, an arrogation of a greatness that belongs to God alone, who declares: "Pride is My cloak and greatness is My robe; whoever contends with Me over either of them, I shall cast him into the Fire." God alone has the right to greatness; God alone may be called, without offense, the Proud. To vaunt oneself before others is therefore to defy God inwardly, an implicit denial of His unique and incomparable majesty — and a subject who openly defies his king is, in effect, at war with him. Enmity toward transcendent reality is the invisible substance pervading every act of arrogance a ruler commits against those beneath him.

Here Shah-Kazemi draws out the paradox that only a sense of spiritual values can illuminate: an attitude of superiority toward those below is in fact an act of rebellion against that which is above. An injustice toward creatures not only draws down the justice of the Creator; it manifests, in and of itself, opposition to the Creator. So what looks like a horizontal vice — belonging to this world, a matter between a strong man and a weak one — is in reality a vertical, spiritual disorder. The outward immorality of corruption is only the symptom of an inner malaise: an implicit denial of the absoluteness, and therefore the uniqueness, of the source of all power, a forgetting of the truth the Qur'an puts as a question and answers in the same breath — "To whom belongs the dominion this day? To God, the One, the All-Conquering" (chapter 40, verse 16). That "this day," the Imam insists, means every day, not only the Day of Judgment; the truth should resound constantly in the ears of every ruler.

The archetype of this pride is Iblis, and the Imam devotes to him one of his most searing sermons, known as the sermon of abasement, so named for the way it humiliates the proud. Iblis is described as the first of those who competed with God in grandeur — the very embodiment of the principle of pride. Commanded to prostrate before Adam, he refused, as the Qur'an recounts: when God told the angels He was creating man from clay and breathing His spirit into him, all the angels prostrated "save Iblis; he was proud and was one of the disbelievers" (chapter 38, verses 71–74). He refused because he judged himself superior — he was made of fire, he reasoned, and Adam of clay. Taking pride in that presumed superiority and rebelling against the command, he became not only the first enemy of God but, as the Imam names him, the leader of the bigots and the forerunner of the proud. And the Imam turns the account into a direct warning to his listeners: beware lest Iblis infect you with his disease through his insinuations and whisperings. His counsel is a posture of the whole self: "Be resolute in placing self-abasement over your heads, casting self-glorification beneath your feet, and removing pride from your necks. And take up humility as the fortified watchtower between you and your enemy."

Read this way, the meaning of Iblis's disobedience opens up. It was the outward moral consequence of an inner spiritual malaise rooted in a pride that is tantamount to hidden idolatry — and it carried within it the seed of its own disgrace. Iblis is disgraced because he disobeyed, and he disobeyed because of pride; so pride is, in and of itself, a disgrace, a state of dis-grace, a perversion of the human condition that carries the seed of its own inexorable inversion. "Do you not see," the Imam asks, "how God humiliated him through his pride?" Being proud is already, in reality, a form of humiliation, for self-aggrandizement is tantamount to self-destruction. The tyrant does not escape this logic and then meet punishment later; his exaltation is his abasement, working itself out from within.

Humility, then, is the principal weapon in the war against Iblis, whose entire aim, inversely, is to inculcate pride — for pride is not merely the worst of vices but the poison that infects and destroys every other virtue. Virtue itself is converted into vice the moment pride accompanies it; hence the Imam's arresting inversion, "The sin that grieves you is better, in the sight of God, than the virtue that makes you proud." As the opening chapter already established in the teaching on sincerity, the subtlest idolatry is riya, the doing of good to be seen — "the slightest pretension is polytheism." Shah-Kazemi's point here is what that hidden idolatry looks like in a ruler. The Prophet warned that the creeping of this concealed polytheism in the community is more hidden than a black ant crawling over a hard rock on a dark night. The crude notion of idolatry as the worship of material idols must be understood more subtly as the worship of false gods concealed within one's own intentions and attitudes — the hidden pride, vanity, and ostentation that a person in power is peculiarly exposed to. To act for the sake of others' praise is to act for others rather than for God, an implicit attribution of partners to Him; and, as the Imam warns, whoever acts for other than God, God entrusts him to those for whose sake he acted.

Before that lexical turn, the Imam names the exact sentence a ruler tells himself as he steps onto the slope toward oppression, and diagnoses the order in which the damage is done: "Do not say, 'I have been given authority; I order and am obeyed,' for this leads to corruption in the heart and the erosion of religion." The sequence is worth pausing on. Self-glorification is seen to produce first an inner, spiritual corruption — that of the heart — and only then the destruction of religion. The priority underscores the whole letter's logic: the outward prescriptions of religion require the right inward attitude if they are to be applied with integrity and kept with stability. One might restate the Imam's thought this way: when power goes to the head, faith departs from the heart; and conversely, for faith to be properly manifested in the world, it must flow from a heart suffused with humility. This too is an aspect of putting each thing in its proper place — beginning with the placing of the ego beneath God rather than beside Him.

There is a further, almost lexical turn in the Imam's diagnosis, and it deepens the whole account of pride. When the authority of his position begins to engender vanity, Malik is told, he should look at the grandeur of God's dominion above him — "this will calm your ambition, restrain you from your own vehemence, and restore to you what had strayed from your intellect." The part of the intellect that strays when arrogance takes hold is precisely the part that is conscious of God's absolute reality and of the relative, derivative, ultimately illusory character of everything else. And the Arabic word for arrogance derives from the same root as the word for imagination — so that arrogance is intimately bound up with the realm of the imaginary, the fanciful, the unreal. When imagination fastens upon the unreal, it becomes infused with pride and turns into arrogance. Arrogant pride is thus an intense form of self-delusion rooted in an intellectual defect; it shows up as a moral vice, but its cause runs deeper than the moral level, for it involves not merely an overestimation of oneself but, more fundamentally, an underestimation of the Absolute. The overt egotism of the tyrant is only an intensification of the ordinary egocentricity clinging to anyone who has not realized the truth of God's sole reality — the truth the Qur'an states again and again: "Everything is perishing except His countenance" (chapter 28, verse 88); "everything that is thereon is passing away, and there remains the Face of thy Lord, possessor of Glory and Bounty" (chapter 55, verses 26–27). Only the sage draws from this the humility that dissolves the individualism and egocentricity which are the very lifeblood of tyranny. Hence the Imam's blunt warning: "Beware of comparing yourself with God in greatness and likening yourself to Him in might, for God abases every tyrant and disgraces every braggart." One who fails to act with justice toward God and His creatures finds that not only His creatures but God Himself becomes his adversary — and whoever God opposes has his argument defeated and remains at war with God until he desists and repents.

## The war against conscience

If the tyrant is at war with God, where exactly is the battle fought? The letter has already said what that war consists of before repentance or judgment overtakes the oppressor: it is his implicit opposition to ultimate reality, his violation of the deepest nature of things and of the true nature of his own being. Shah-Kazemi's answer to where it is fought is that it is fought, first of all, inside the tyrant himself — and this is where the doctrine becomes most searching. The war takes several forms. One is the sheer persistence of tyranny, for the longer it continues, the more wretched the tyrant's soul becomes and the more firmly the seeds of misery are planted in his being. This is what the Qur'an means by the drawing out of punishment by degrees — a phrase built on the word for stages, step by step. "Those who reject Our signs, We will lead them on by degrees, from whence they know not" (chapter 7, verses 181–182), the verse says, setting them opposite "people who guide with truth and dispense justice therewith." The terrible subtlety is that this graded punishment can take the form of apparent worldly success: "Let not their wealth nor their children please thee; God desires only to punish them thereby in the world, that their souls shall pass away while they are disbelievers" (chapter 9, verse 85). A tyrant may be a Muslim in the formal sense and yet be guilty of a mode of disbelief, taking the word in its root sense as one who covers over the truth — covers it in his very being and in his actions. The oppressor is precisely the one who rejects the signs while the just are the ones who dispense justice according to the truth.

There is a hard edge to this doctrine that Shah-Kazemi does not soften. The tyrant will eventually be compelled to repent — if only at the moment of death, when judgment is imminent and evasion is no longer possible. Even if he appears to have escaped the consequences of his tyranny in this life, he cannot escape them in the Hereafter. And in the meantime, the very worldly success that seems to vindicate him is, on the Qur'anic reading, the instrument of his undoing, the punishment administered by degrees under the disguise of prosperity. Wealth and power that swell while faith drains away are not evidence that God is indifferent; they are the slow closing of a trap. This is the sober background against which every warning in the letter should be heard: the Imam is not merely counselling prudence, he is describing a law as fixed as gravity, by which self-exaltation bends inexorably toward abasement.

The second and deeper form of this war is the tyrant's war against his own conscience. The voice of conscience — the very essence and sum of secular ethics, and often treated as the self-sufficient foundation of individual morality — can here be grasped in its fuller context. That context is the supra-individual, metaphysical source from which conscience springs: the divine infusion of spiritual discernment within the soul, the inspiration that underlies all ethical discrimination. The divine quality of justice is translated, at the level of the responsible soul, into the voice of inner conscience, exactly as the Qur'an describes: "And the soul and that which perfected it, inspiring it with consciousness of its wickedness and its righteousness" (chapter 91, verses 7–8). To war against one's conscience, even barely acknowledging it, is therefore — and all the more so — to war against God. For the soul does not merely contain a moral conscience, an inalienable awareness of the difference between good and evil; it also contains an innate consciousness of the reality of divine lordship and of its own creaturely servitude. Spiritual consciousness is the source of moral conscience. The Qur'an locates this consciousness at the very dawn of creation, in a moment that precedes the human condition yet enters into the making of every soul: God brought forth from the children of Adam their descendants and made them testify against themselves — "Am I not your Lord?" — and they answered, "Yes, verily, we testify" (chapter 7, verse 172). The testimony was taken, the verse explains, so that no one could plead ignorance on the Day of Resurrection. The source of morality thus transcends the human level even while forming part of the very definition of humanity: what makes the soul fully human is precisely that element of transcendence which infinitely surpasses it and yet, mysteriously, furnishes its own deepest being.

At this depth the tyrant's war against God stands revealed as something stranger still — a war against the very reality that defines the soul as such, against his own nature, his fitra. When he silences his conscience he is not merely ignoring an external rule; he is negating the innate consciousness of divine lordship and creaturely servitude that is written into the making of his own being. He is at war, in other words, with himself — with the deepest and truest part of himself, the part that testified "Yes, verily" before the world began. This is why the same vices that seem to belong only to those who wield political power are, on the Imam's reading, brought much closer to the lived reality of every soul. Tyranny is not confined to those with armies; it is a structure of the soul, present wherever the commanding self usurps the place of the God-given nature. The man in the palace and the man in the marketplace are running the same risk on different scales, and the letter, addressed in the first instance to a governor, is in truth addressed to anyone who has ever been obeyed by his own appetites.

Seen this way, tyranny takes on a far more intimate meaning, and it comes much closer to home than the abuses of the powerful. The tyrant is, first and foremost, one who violates his own soul — one who lets it be dominated by the soul that commands to evil. The statement the Imam offers as the epitome of the man on his way to corruption — "I have been given authority; I order and am obeyed" — is not only to be read socially but microcosmically. It becomes the very statement of the evil element within the soul, the commanding self that "orders" and is obeyed by every other faculty: intelligence, imagination, will, sentiment, sensibility, all of it. This is why the combat against one's own soul is a theme the Imam returns to again and again, and why he ties it directly to justice. Placing each thing in its proper place begins with rectifying oneself before presuming to reform or govern others; it means restoring the divine nature within oneself first. "If your aspiration is to reform the people," he says, "begin with yourself, for your pursuit of the reform of others while your own soul is corrupt is the greatest of faults." In his letter to Malik's predecessor as governor of Egypt, Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr, the Imam draws the connection between power and self-mastery as tightly as it can be drawn: "I have appointed you as governor over the most immense of the forces at my disposal, the people of Egypt, so you are obliged to oppose your soul and protect your religion." The greater the power available, the greater the obligation to restrain oneself — for if the soul yields to its desires through the power at its disposal, the result is the corruption not only of the man but of the very religion he was charged to uphold.

Beneath all of this lies the tyrant's founding error, which the Qur'an names with lapidary precision. "Truly man is rebellious, in that he deems himself independent" (chapter 96, verses 6–7). Insofar as the soul pretends to self-sufficiency and tries to sever itself from its total dependence on God, it sets itself up as a god in its own right — the person actually comes to believe that he himself is God. Here is the true portrait of the tyrant: the one who, as the Qur'an says, "takes his own desire as his god" (chapter 45, verse 23). And the antidote is exactly the knowledge the proud man lacks — knowledge of one's own nothingness before God and consequently of one's unconditional need of Him. That knowledge is the principal means of loosening the grip of the soul that commands to evil. For only the soul that already commands itself to evil will command others to evil; and only the soul that is lowly before God will be humble, compassionate, and just in its dealings with people. The Imam builds this insight directly into his counsel on governance. Let the most beloved of your affairs, he tells Malik, be those most centred upon the right, the most comprehensive in justice, and the most inclusive of the approval of the common people — for the disapproval of the common folk undermines the approval of the elite. This is sound practical advice, but Shah-Kazemi reads it also as a spiritual instruction: it urges the ruler to identify with the poor rather than the rich, and so to remember, in the very structure of his sympathies, man's actual lowliness before God. The recognition of one's own poverty before the Absolute is not a private devotional feeling that sits apart from the work of ruling; it is meant to reshape whose approval a governor seeks and whose interests he serves. The whole of the letter's severe opening, then, is not an exercise in gloom. It is the clearing of the one obstacle — the swollen ego, mistaking itself for God — that stands between a ruler and the justice he was appointed to serve.

## What this episode lands

This episode has turned the sacred conception of justice over to look at its underside, and found something the surface of political language conceals. Tyranny is not first of all a matter of bad policy or cruel acts; those are symptoms. Its root is a spiritual disorder — the pride by which a soul arrogates to itself a greatness belonging only to God, and so sets itself, however quietly, at war with Him. We have seen why the letter opens on such a sombre note, with the soul that incites to evil and the confession of Joseph: because a good man handed power needs, above all, to be reminded of his own helplessness and his unconditional need of grace. We have seen why pride is the vice the Imam fears most — because it is the misappropriation of a divine attribute, and because it poisons every virtue it touches. And we have seen where the tyrant's war is really waged: against his own conscience, against the innate testimony of his own soul, against his own deepest nature, so that the oppressor of others is first the oppressor of himself.

The chapter has also uncovered a principle it will now build on. If the whole disorder of tyranny is self-aggrandizement — the ego swelling until it eclipses God — then the whole cure must be its opposite: a self-lowering that reflects the soul's true poverty before the Absolute. We saw the first hint of that cure in the counsel to prefer the approval of the common people and to identify with the poor — a reorientation of the ruler's sympathies that follows directly from remembering his own lowliness before God. The next movement takes that hint and unfolds it into a full teaching. That is the hinge into the next movement. The letter turns from what a ruler must not become to what he must be, and the first quality it asks of him is the one most opposed to pride: mercy. The next episode follows the letter into its great teaching on compassion — why mercy, rather than blunting the demands of justice, turns out to be one of its own edges, why the powerful are commanded to identify with the powerless, and how a right opinion of God turns out to be the hidden root of every generous act.
