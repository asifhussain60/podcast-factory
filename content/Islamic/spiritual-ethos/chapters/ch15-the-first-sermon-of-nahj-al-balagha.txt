# The First Sermon of Nahj al-Balagha

*After three long movements through intellect, justice, and remembrance, the book turns at its close to a single primary document and lets it stand whole. This episode presents the opening sermon of Nahj al-Balagha — the compilation of Ali's (ع) discourses long honoured as the peak of Arabic eloquence — the great oration on the oneness of God and the origin of all things. It hears the sermon entire, in refined English, from its first paradox of praise through the making of the cosmos, the ranks of the angels, the kneading of Adam, the refusal of Iblis, and the long succession of prophecy that ends in Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him) and the Book he left behind. Where earlier episodes drew fragments of this sermon to teach a doctrine, here the whole is set before the listener, and its own architecture — from the God beyond every word to the Book placed in human hands — is allowed to make its argument.*

## Where this episode opens

How does a man set out to praise a God of whom he says, in the very same breath, that no praise can ever reach Him? That is the knot tied in the first line of the sermon this episode is given to, and it is tied on purpose. Praise be to God, the Imam begins — and then at once: whose laudation those who speak cannot deliver, whose graces those who count cannot number, whose rightful due those who strive cannot render. The office of speech is to carry a meaning across to a listener; here speech is turned against its own office, made to demonstrate that of this one subject it can carry nothing at all. The praise is real and the praise is confessedly empty, and the two are not a muddle but a method. The loftier the truth to be shown, the deeper the paradox built to show it — and the whole sermon is built on that principle, so that the eye cannot see Him yet the mind cannot deny Him, and He is farther than the reach of any flight of imagination yet nearer to us than our own jugular vein.

We have met pieces of this sermon before. Its opening lines on divesting God of every attribute stood at the head of the book's treatment of the Imam's way of affirmation and denial; its insistence that God is one but not by number ran under the whole account of a oneness that cannot fall into the category of numbers. What this final source-episode does is different. It does not quarry the sermon for a doctrine and move on. It sets the whole document down and lets it be heard from its first word to its last, so that the listener can feel the single great arc the fragments were cut from — an arc that begins at the summit of transcendence, where no word or concept can bind God, and descends, without ever letting go of that transcendence, all the way to a handful of clay breathed into life and a Book laid open in human hands.

Three movements carry the episode. We begin by standing back from the sermon to see what kind of speech it is and how its method of paradox works — the eloquence, the negation that is really an affirmation, the God who is beyond every attribute and yet the Originator of everything that bears one. We then hear the sermon itself, whole and unbroken, the way it was meant to be received. And we close by walking back through its great descent — the cosmos raised from water and wind, the angels in their tireless ranks, Adam kneaded and quickened, the covenant taken from the prophets, and the guidance that never once failed until it was sealed. The point of the episode is not to add a doctrine to the book but to let the book's whole spirit be heard in a single voice: the quintessential spirituality of Islam, spoken by the man the tradition places at the gate of prophetic wisdom.

## The peak of eloquence

Nahj al-Balagha — the name means, near enough, the way or the peak of eloquence — is the great gathering of the sermons, letters, and sayings attributed to Ali (ع), assembled generations after him and prized ever since across the Muslim world as one of the summits of the Arabic tongue, a book studied for its language by those who did not share its author's theology and revered for its wisdom by those who did. Its very first sermon is the one before us, and it is fitting that a compilation famed for eloquence should open on a meditation about the limits of speech. For the sermon's subject is the oneness of God, and its governing insight is that this subject is precisely the one thing eloquence cannot capture — so that the most eloquent thing that can be said of God is a confession, made as beautifully as language allows, that language delivers nothing of Him.

Watch how the method works in the opening cascade. God is the one whom far-reaching aspirations cannot reach and profound intuitions cannot fathom; whose attribute has no binding limit, no existing description, no appointed time, no extended term. Each clause is a negation — no limit, no description, no time, no term — and yet the cumulative effect is not to leave the mind with a blank. It is to lift the mind past every boundary it habitually draws. Reason works by defining, and to define is to draw a line, a hadd, a fence around a thing that marks it off from what it is not; that is how we grasp anything at all. The sermon's rush of negations breaks fence after fence, and in the breaking opens an awareness that reaches beyond the whole business of fencing — an awareness of the one Reality that no fence can enclose because it is not one object among others to be marked off from the rest. The negation, in other words, is in the service of an affirmation. To say what God is not, over and over, is the only honest way the tongue has of pointing at what He is.

This is the very movement the book earlier traced in the two halves of the testimony of faith, and the sermon is its deepest source: there is no god — the sweep that clears away every false object of the heart's regard — but God, the affirmation that alone remains. The book has already unfolded that logic at length, and this episode does not re-open it; it simply lets the listener hear it in the words that first gave it. The names revelation itself gives — that He is the Compassionate, the Merciful, the Mighty — are not thereby denied. They are affirmed as real and refused as definitions: He is merciful, but not with any mercy we can picture from our own; powerful, but not as any force or flood we have known. Each name is emptied of the limit our minds would smuggle into it and kept as a true word pointing past that limit to God alone. And so the sermon can hold, without strain, what looks like a contradiction: that God has no describable attribute, and that He is truly named by the words of His own revelation. The first guards Him from our idols; the second lets Him be praised.

It is worth pausing on why the sheer eloquence of the thing is not incidental to its meaning but part of it. A compilation could have been named for its wisdom or its doctrine; this one is named for its eloquence, its balagha, and the choice is exact. Beauty of speech, at its height, does what the sermon's whole method does: it points past itself. A merely correct sentence delivers its content and is used up in the delivery; a beautiful one carries its content and, at the same time, opens a surplus that the content alone cannot account for, a sense that there is more here than the words have said. That surplus is precisely what a discourse on the incomprehensible God requires. The sermon must make the listener feel the inadequacy of language at the very moment language is working at its finest — and eloquence is the only tool that can do both at once, because only the most beautiful speech makes its own falling-short audible. The plainer the sentence, the more it seems to have finished its job; the more magnificent the sentence, the more it seems to gesture beyond its own reach. So the reputation of Nahj al-Balagha for eloquence and its subject of the unnameable God are not two facts about it but one: the beauty is the theology, a language stretched to its utmost so that its utmost may be felt as a threshold rather than a wall.

To hear the sermon whole, and not in the fragments the book has quoted before, is to feel this surplus build across its length. Each clause is complete in itself, and yet none of them closes; the negations pile up, the images of creation surge one into the next, the ranks of angels multiply, and the cumulative pressure is of a speech straining upward under a weight it gladly cannot lift. That is why this final source-episode sets the document down entire. A fragment can teach a doctrine, but only the whole can produce the experience the sermon was built to produce — the sustained sense of a mind reaching, with everything language has, toward a Reality it confesses at every step it cannot contain.

What makes the sermon more than a lesson in negative theology is that it does not stay at the summit. Having lifted the mind clear of every attribute, the same opening breath turns downward and outward: through His power He originated all created things, through His mercy He set the winds moving, and He steadied with rocky pillars the trembling of His earth. In a single sentence the God who is beyond all reach becomes the Origin from which everything reachable pours. This is the sermon's boldest stroke, and it is why the recitation to come matters so much. The transcendence is not a withdrawal. The very God whom no word can bind is the one whose word calls water out of nothing, ranks the angels, kneads the clay, and sends the prophets in unbroken succession. The sermon means to hold both at once — the God farther than imagination and the God nearer than the jugular vein — and it holds them by descending, deliberately, from the heights of denial into the fullest possible telling of what this unnameable One has made. To hear the sermon whole is to feel that descent as one continuous motion. So let it now be heard.

## The First Sermon

What follows is the sermon entire, in refined English, as Ali (ع) delivered it.

Praise be to God, whose laudation those who speak cannot deliver, whose graces those who count cannot number, and whose rightful due those who strive cannot render. He is the one whom no far-reaching aspiration can reach and no profound intuition can fathom; whose attribute has no binding limit, no existing description, no appointed hour, no extended term. Through His power He originated all created things; through His mercy He set the winds moving; and He steadied with rocky pillars the trembling of His earth.

The foremost thing in religion is the knowledge of Him; the perfection of the knowledge of Him is to affirm Him; the perfection of affirming Him is to profess His oneness; the perfection of professing His oneness is sincerity toward Him; and the perfection of sincerity toward Him is to divest Him of attributes — for every attribute bears witness that it is other than the object it qualifies, and every object so qualified bears witness that it is other than the attribute. So whoever ascribes an attribute to God has coupled Him with another; whoever couples Him has made Him two; whoever makes Him two has parted Him into pieces; whoever parts Him into pieces is ignorant of Him. Whoever points to Him has confined Him; whoever confines Him has counted Him; whoever asks "in what is He?" has enclosed Him; and whoever asks "upon what is He?" has left Him out.

He is a Being, but not through any coming-to-be; existent, but not out of nonexistence; with every thing, but not by nearness of place; other than every thing, but not by distance of place; acting, but not by the movements of instruments; seeing, even when there was nothing of His creation to be seen; alone, when there was none whose company He might seek nor whose absence He might feel.

He brought creation into being and began it from nothing, without pondering a plan, without drawing on any prior experience, without setting in motion any restlessness, and without the disquiet of exertion. He gave all things their due season and harmonized their variety; He fixed their natures and fastened those natures upon them, knowing them before He brought them forth, encompassing their limits and their ends, aware of their bonds and their forms.

Then He — glorified be He — opened a cleft in the airs and split the surrounding regions and the high reaches of the sky. Then He set flowing within it a water whose waves surged, tossing one upon another; He loaded it upon a violent wind, a breaking gale, and commanded the wind to hold the water back, and gave the wind mastery over the water's force, and set it as a boundary about it — the air spread open beneath it, the water pouring in fullness above it. Then He raised up a wind and made it barren, held its course fixed, made its blowing constant, set its source far off, and commanded it to stir the deep waters and rouse the waves of the seas. It churned them as a churn beats milk and drove them hard into the open air, sending the first of them back upon the last and the still upon the moving, until the level rose and the surface threw up foam. Then He lifted that foam into a wide-cleft air and an open sky and made of it the seven heavens: the lowest a wave held in check, the highest a roof raised and guarded, without a pillar to hold them up or a nail to bind them fast. Then He adorned them with the ornament of the stars and the light of piercing rays, and He set running among them a shining lamp and a lit moon, in a turning sphere, beneath a moving roof and a spinning firmament.

Then He parted the high heavens and filled them with kinds of His angels. Among them are some who prostrate and never bow, and some who bow and never stand upright, and some ranged in ranks who never break their order, and some who glorify Him and never tire — no sleep of the eye overtakes them, no wandering of the mind, no weariness of the body, no forgetfulness's neglect. Among them are the trusted keepers of His revelation, and tongues that carry His word to His messengers, and those who come and go with His decree and His command. Among them are the guardians of His servants and the wardens of the gates of His gardens. And among them are those whose feet are fixed in the lowest reaches of the earth while their necks rise past the highest heaven, whose limbs spread beyond the ends of the world and whose shoulders are matched to the pillars of the Throne. Their eyes are cast down beneath it; they wrap themselves under it in their wings. Between them and all who are below them is a barrier of the veils of glory and the screens of might. They do not shape their Lord in imagination, nor lay upon Him the attributes of the made, nor confine Him within places, nor point to Him by likenesses.

Then He — glorified be He — gathered a handful from the earth, of the hard and the soft, the sweet and the salt. He poured water over it until it was cleansed, then kneaded it with moisture until it clung together, and shaped from it a form with hollows and curves, joints and limbs and parts. He held it firm until it dried and set it hard until it rang, for an appointed span and a known term. Then He breathed into it of His spirit, and it rose up a human being — with a mind to direct him, thoughts to move him, limbs to serve him, and instruments to turn to use, and a discernment by which to tell the true from the false, and tastes and scents, and colours and kinds; a creature kneaded of clashing natures and paired opposites, of heat and cold, of moisture and dryness.

Then God asked of the angels the trust He had laid upon them and their keeping of the charge He had given them, that they bow down before Adam in acknowledgment and humble themselves before his honour. So He said, "Prostrate yourselves before Adam," and they prostrated, all of them, save Iblis. Pride seized him and wretchedness overcame him. He gloried in his own making of fire and scorned the creature of clay. So God granted him respite, that His wrath might be fully earned and the trial completed and the promise fulfilled. He said, "You are among those reprieved until the day of the appointed time."

Then He settled Adam in a dwelling made pleasant for him and a place made safe, and He warned him of Iblis and his enmity. But his enemy deceived him, out of envy for his abode among the abiding and his company with the righteous. So Adam sold certainty for doubt and resolve for weakness, and took dread in the place of gladness and remorse in the place of pride. Then God turned to him in mercy, taught him the word of His forgiveness, and promised him a return to His garden — and He sent him down to the abode of trial and the begetting of offspring.

From Adam's seed God chose prophets and took their pledge upon His revelation and the delivery of His trust, at a time when the greater part of His creatures had broken the covenant of God, were ignorant of His right, and set up rivals beside Him, and the devils had turned them from the knowledge of Him and cut them off from His worship. So He sent among them His messengers and raised His prophets in succession, to ask of them the keeping of the covenant of their own creation, to recall to them His forgotten grace, to plead with them by the delivery of the message, to unearth for them the buried treasures of their intellects, and to show them the signs of His measured power: the sky spread over them, the earth laid low beneath them, the provisions that keep them alive, the ends that put them to death, the ailments that wear them down, and the run of events that passes over them.

God never left His creation without a prophet sent, a Book revealed, a binding proof, or a clear path — messengers whom the fewness of their number and the multitude of their deniers never weakened. Each earlier one was told the name of the one to come, and each later one was made known by the one who had gone before. So the generations passed and the ages turned, the fathers going and the sons coming after them, until God raised up Muhammad, His Messenger — peace and blessings be upon him — in fulfillment of His promise and completion of His prophecy: the one whose pledge had been taken from the prophets, whose signs were famed, and whose birth was noble.

The people of the earth in that day were sects at variance, their passions scattered and their ways divided — some likening God to His creation, some twisting His name, some pointing away from Him. So God guided them by His Prophet out of their error and, by his place among them, delivered them from their ignorance. Then God chose for Muhammad the meeting with Himself, and was well pleased to grant him what is with Him. He raised him in honour above the abode of this lower world and drew him from the seat of trial, taking him to Himself in dignity — and He left among you what the other prophets left among their peoples, for the prophets do not leave their peoples untended, without a plain road and a standing sign: the Book of your Lord among you, making clear its lawful and its forbidden, its obligatory and its recommended, its abrogating and its abrogated, its permitted and its enjoined, its particular and its general, its lessons and its parables, its unbounded and its bounded, its plain and its ambiguous — expounding what was brief in it and clarifying what was obscure.

In it are matters whose knowledge is made binding upon the servants and matters whose not-knowing is excused; matters affirmed as obligatory in the Book that the Sunna is known to have lifted, and matters made obligatory by the Sunna that the Book has left free to set aside; duties binding upon a person at their appointed hour that fall away once the hour is past. It marks off the forbidden one from another — the great sins, against which He has threatened His fire, and the small, for which He has readied His forgiveness — and it names acts acceptable when done in small measure and no less acceptable when done in greater, without either being made a duty.

## Creation and the covenant

Now walk back through what has just been heard, for the descent from the God beyond words to the Book in human hands is an argument, not a mere sequence, and its steps deserve to be told. The sermon does not offer its cosmogony as physics. When it raises the seven heavens from a foam churned out of water by a barren wind and hangs them with no pillar to hold them and no nail to bind them, the picture it draws is of a whole order held up by nothing whatever except the command that made it. That is the point of the strange, surging imagery — the water tossed upon the wind, the first of the waves driven back upon the last, the foam lifted and shaped and set as a roof: everything leans on everything, and the entire leaning tower of it leans, in the end, on the sheer word of the Originator. The listener who came through the sermon's opening knowing God as the one beyond every attribute is now shown that same God as the one on whom, at every instant, the whole of the reachable world depends. The transcendence and the sustaining are one act seen from two sides.

The angels extend the same lesson into the highest reaches of the created order. The sermon's ranks of them are unforgettable — some forever prostrate and never bowing, some forever bowing and never rising, some fixed in ranks that never break, some glorifying without ever tiring, untouched by sleep or distraction or fatigue or the lapse of forgetting — and among them the trusted keepers of revelation, the tongues that carry the word to the messengers, the wardens of the gates of the gardens, and those vast ones whose feet are set in the lowest earth while their necks pass the highest heaven. It would be easy to hear all this as decoration. But the sermon plants at the end of the passage a line that turns it back to its theme: even these, the mightiest of creatures, standing nearest the Throne, do not shape their Lord in imagination, nor lay upon Him the attributes of things made, nor confine Him within a place, nor point to Him by any likeness. The very beings closest to God are the ones most scrupulous never to reduce Him — a rebuke, gently placed, to the human habit of doing exactly that, and a confirmation from the summit of creation of the negative theology the sermon opened with.

Then the descent reaches us. God gathers a handful of earth — hard and soft, sweet and salt, the whole contrariety of the ground — cleanses it with water, kneads it, shapes it, dries it until it rings like fired clay, and breathes into it of His spirit, and it stands up a human being. The sermon lingers on what the creature is given: a mind to direct him, thoughts to move him, limbs and instruments to use, and, above all, a discernment by which to tell the true from the false. This is the faculty the book has honoured from its opening pages under the name of the intellect — not mere calculation but the light that distinguishes the real from the counterfeit — and here it is named as the very mark of the human, the thing breathed into the clay along with the spirit. Man is the creature kneaded of clashing natures, heat against cold and moisture against dryness, and set the task of discernment in the midst of that clash. The dignity and the difficulty of being human are given in a single image.

Against that dignity the sermon at once sets its refusal. Commanded to bow before Adam, the angels prostrate — all save Iblis, whom pride seizes, who glories in his own making of fire and scorns the creature of clay. The book has already named pride as the inward war against God, the self setting its own greatness against His; here is that war's first instance, and the sermon needs no commentary to make the point, only the bare contrast between the ranks who bow and the one who will not. Adam himself then falls, sells certainty for doubt at the whisper of his envious enemy, and tastes remorse — and is met not with abandonment but with a word of forgiveness and a promise of return, before being sent down to the abode of trial where the human story, the begetting of generations, begins. Even the fall is folded, in the sermon's telling, inside a mercy that precedes and outlasts it.

One phrase in the sending of the prophets deserves to be lifted out, because it carries a whole doctrine in four words. God sends the messengers, the sermon says, to ask of people the keeping of the covenant of their own creation. The covenant in question is not one struck at some later date, in the giving of a law; it is the pledge lodged in the very making of a creature — the debt of acknowledgment that a made thing owes, by the fact of being made, to the one who made it. To be created is already to have been taken into a covenant, whether one remembers it or not; and the whole work of prophecy, on this telling, is not to impose a new bond but to press for the honouring of a bond that creation itself contracted. This is why the same passage can describe the prophets' task as unearthing the buried treasures of the intellect: the knowledge of God is not brought from outside and installed in a blank mind, but recovered from within a nature that already held it and had merely let heedlessness bury it over. Guidance restores rather than invents, and the covenant it restores was signed in the act of being born.

The sermon grounds this appeal not in argument alone but in the plain furniture of a life. Among the signs it says the prophets were sent to display are the sky spread overhead and the earth laid low underfoot, the provisions that keep a person alive and the ends that put him to death, the ailments that wear the body down and the run of events that passes over everyone. These are not exotic proofs reserved for the learned; they are the most ordinary facts of being alive — that we are fed, that we sicken, that we die, that time carries us — and the sermon treats each of them as a sign already legible to anyone who has not deliberately blinded himself. The natural theology here is democratic and unsparing. The evidence of God is not hidden in the heavens the sermon has just described in such splendour; it is lying in the food on the table and the illness in the bed, waiting to be read by the discernment that was breathed into the clay for exactly this purpose.

From that beginning the sermon draws its longest line: the covenant and the prophets. God chooses prophets from Adam's seed and takes their pledge, and He sends them in unbroken succession into a world that has forgotten Him — to recall His forgotten grace, to plead by the message, and, in a phrase the whole book might take as its motto, to unearth for people the buried treasures of their own intellects. Guidance, in this vision, does not implant something foreign; it digs out what heedlessness had buried. And the succession never lapses: God never left His creation without a prophet sent, a Book revealed, a binding proof, or a clear path — each earlier prophet naming the one to come, each later one made known by the one before — until the whole chain is fulfilled and completed in Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him), and the guidance that had run through the ages is sealed. The book has taught elsewhere that the Qur'an stands at the centre of this faith; the sermon shows where that Book comes from and what it is for. When the Prophet is taken to his Lord in honour, what he leaves behind among his people is precisely the Book of God, and the sermon closes by cataloguing it almost lovingly — its lawful and forbidden, its obligatory and recommended, its abrogating and abrogated, its plain and its ambiguous, what is binding to know and what may be excused, the great sins under threat of fire and the small ones under the shadow of forgiveness. The oration that began by insisting no word can reach God ends with the words He has given, laid open in human hands as the plain road and the standing sign, so that the people are never left to wander without a beacon. That is the sermon's whole descent in one arc: from the God beyond every name, down through the raising of the heavens and the ranking of the angels and the kneading of Adam, to a Book a person can hold and read — transcendence bending all the way down into guidance, and never once ceasing to be transcendence.

## What this episode lands

This episode has done something the earlier ones did not: it has stood back and let a single primary document speak whole. Where the book elsewhere drew lines from this sermon to build its doctrines — the divesting of attributes, the oneness beyond number, the affirmation hidden inside every denial — here the First Sermon of Nahj al-Balagha was heard entire, and its own architecture was allowed to carry the argument. That architecture is a descent. It begins at the sheerest height of transcendence, where the office of speech is turned against itself to show that no word delivers anything of God, and it descends without a break — through the cosmos raised from water and wind and held up by nothing but command, through the tireless ranks of the angels who are nearest God and most careful never to reduce Him, through the clay kneaded and quickened and given the intellect's power to tell true from false, through the refusal of Iblis and the fall and forgiveness of Adam, through the long unbroken succession of prophets sent to unearth the buried treasures of the intellect — all the way to the Book left open in human hands. The God whom no aspiration can reach is the same God who set the winds moving and steadied the earth and gave us, at the last, a plain road to walk.

It is a fitting close to a book about the spiritual ethos of Ali (ع). The book opened by naming him the gate to the city of prophetic wisdom and by claiming that to speak of him is to speak of the quintessential spirituality of Islam. Its long road through the intellect, through justice, and through remembrance has borne that claim out; and now, at the end, the book lets him speak for himself, in the discourse the whole tradition has honoured as the peak of eloquence. Everything the book taught in its own voice is here in his — the transcendence that guards God from every idol, the creation that shows Him near in all things, the intellect that discerns, the covenant that never fails. The gate the book named at the outset opens here onto the words of the one who stands within it, and those words lead, as the whole book has led, from the God beyond all reach to the guidance placed within it — the same single motion, spoken this time not about Ali (ع) but by him.
