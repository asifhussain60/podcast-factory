This episode follows the Letter to Malik al-Ashtar into its second half and its close — and with it, the whole book reaches its end. Having ordered the ruler's soul and the great pillars of his power, Ali (ع) now turns downward and outward: to the land itself and the people who work it, to the scribes and merchants who move a society's goods and words, to the destitute from whom no ruler can ever profit, and finally to the pledges a ruler gives and the blood he must never spill. The letter ends where the whole book has been pointing all along — at a soul that acts justly because justice is the nature of God, and leaves the outcome in God's hands.

## Where this episode picks up

Who is the true foundation of a state — the soldiers who defend it, the judges who order it, or the barefoot farmer bent over a field no minister has ever visited? The previous episode built the ruler from the inside out and then set the great instruments of his power in place: the gentle commander, the incorruptible judge, the watched and well-paid officer. It left the machinery of the state fully assembled and turning. But a machine turns on something, and the letter now kneels to touch the ground that machine actually rests on. It is a startling descent. Having spent its first half among the men closest to power, the letter spends its second half among the people furthest from it — the tiller of the soil, the traveling merchant, and, at the very bottom, the beggar and the man too proud to beg.

This descent is the letter's argument, not merely its order. Everything the last episode assembled — the army, the courts, the treasury — exists, the Imam will now insist, to serve the people who appear in this half, and a ruler who inverts that order, who treats the powerful as the point and the powerless as the cost, has understood nothing. The earlier episodes on this same letter already drew out its deepest claim: that a truly just governance is possible only for a soul that sees this world through to the next, because the demand to care for those who can never repay you is, by any worldly calculus, simply irrational. Here we watch that claim take concrete shape, instruction by instruction, until the letter turns at the last to the covenants and the bloodshed on which, the Imam says, a ruler's whole account with God is finally settled. This is where we pick up — at the soil, and the hands that work it.

## Land, scribes, and merchants

The letter reaches the land, and with it one of the most quietly radical sentences in the whole document — a line the earlier episodes already marked but which lands differently in the Imam's own voice, addressed to a man who will actually set the tax rates of Egypt. "Let your concern for the cultivation of the land outweigh your concern for collecting the tax," Ali (ع) tells Malik, "for no tax will be gathered where there is no cultivation. Whoever exacts the tax without cultivating the land ruins the land and destroys the people, and his rule will not endure for long." The entire logic of extractive rule is turned upside down. Revenue is not the goal; the flourishing of the land and its people is the goal, and revenue is only what flows from that flourishing when it is healthy. A ruler who squeezes the source dries up the stream and then wonders where the water went.

So the Imam makes relief a matter of policy, not of mood. If the people complain of a heavy burden, or of blight, or of the cutting of their water, or of failed rain, or of soil ruined by flood or drought, then lighten what they owe "in the measure that you hope will set their situation right." And he anticipates the governor's flinch at the lost revenue with a piece of pure statecraft dressed as spiritual counsel: do not let any relief you grant them weigh on you, "for it is an investment that will return to you in the prosperity of your land, the adornment of your rule, the finest praise you reap from them, and the pride you take in dispensing justice among them." The remission is not charity subtracted from the treasury; it is capital placed into the only thing that generates treasure — a people healthy enough to produce. A prosperous land, the Imam observes, can bear whatever burden you later place on it; it is the impoverished land that collapses under the lightest touch.

There is a whole philosophy of wealth folded into this counsel. The Imam is telling Malik that a ruler's treasury and his people are not two competing interests to be traded off against each other, as if every coin left in a farmer's hand were a coin lost to the state. They are one interest seen at two moments in time — the wealth of a country simply is the wealth of its people, held in a slower form. To drain the people in order to fill the treasury is to bail water from one end of a boat into the other and call it progress. And the ruin, when it comes, is never only economic. A people bled white by their own government lose more than their crops; they lose any reason to believe the government is something other than a larger and better-armed bandit, and a state that has forfeited that belief is already, whatever the size of its armies, on its way down.

Then he names the disease that lays such land waste, and the earlier episodes have already unfolded its inner meaning — that the ruin of a country begins in the souls of its governors, in a greed that is really a doubt about what endures, a forgetting that one will die and be judged. Here we hear the chain in his own words: "The devastation of the land comes about only through the destitution of its inhabitants, and the destitution of its inhabitants comes about only when the desire to amass wealth rules the souls of the governors, when they have doubts about what endures, and when they profit little from exemplary teachings." Greed is a form of spiritual short-sightedness; a man grabs at the material because he has lost sight of the enduring, and the damage runs from his blind soul outward through the exploited people to the wasted fields. Injustice, in this letter, is never a single act. It is the last visible link in a chain whose first link is a soul that has stopped believing in anything that lasts.

From the soil the letter turns to the scribes — the class the commentary passed over quickly but the Imam treats with real care, because a state runs on its correspondence and its records, and a treacherous or careless clerk can undo in a sentence what an army secured in a campaign. Appoint the best of them to your affairs, he says, and entrust the letters carrying your most sensitive strategies and secrets only to the scribe of the most complete moral character: one whom high office will not embolden to contradict you in public, who is never negligent in bringing you your officials' correspondence or dispatching your replies, who will not weaken any agreement made on your behalf nor fail to repudiate one made to your harm, and who "is not ignorant of the value of his own capabilities — for one who is unaware of his own worth will be even more ignorant of the worth of others." And the Imam warns Malik against the very trap that catches most rulers in their appointments: do not choose by instinct and personal impression alone, "for men know how to present themselves to rulers through affectation and good service" that hides neither sincerity nor fidelity. Test them instead by what they actually did in the service of good rulers before you, and appoint the one whose integrity left the deepest mark on ordinary people. To put the right man in the right place, he says, is itself an enactment of justice — and to overlook a scribe's fault is to own it: "Any fault of your scribes which you have overlooked will be ascribed to you." And because no single clerk can carry an entire administration, the Imam tells Malik to set over each department a head officer "who will not be daunted by the magnitude of affairs nor scattered by their multiplicity" — a man whose composure does not shatter under volume, since one of the surest ways to lose a state is to staff its offices with people who panic. The principle beneath every one of these appointments is the one the letter never abandons: put the right man in the right place and you have done justice; appoint by favouritism or by first impression and you have planted, in the very machinery of the state, the seed of every injustice that will later flower from it.

Last among the working classes come the merchants and the artisans, and here the Imam's balance is exact. Attend to them, he says, and treat them well — those of fixed shop, those who travel with their goods, and those who earn their bread with their hands — for they are the veins through which a society's necessities flow, bringing goods "from distant and remote places, by land and sea, from plains and mountains, and from places where men neither settle nor even dare to venture." They live, he notes, in a state of reconciliation and peace, so no calamity need be feared from them. And yet — and the letter does not soften this — many of them harbour "a despicable stinginess, a repugnant greed, a propensity to hoard goods, and a tendency to fix prices arbitrarily," all of which brings loss to the people and shame to the governor who permits it. So the Imam gives a concrete command grounded in prophetic authority: prohibit hoarding, "for the Messenger of God — God bless him and his family — prohibited it." Let trade be conducted with fairness, at prices that harm neither buyer nor seller. And if a merchant hoards after the prohibition, "inflict upon him an exemplary — but not excessive — punishment." Even the penalty is measured; justice does not become vengeance because the offender is a profiteer. The market is a good servant and a bad master, and the Imam's task is to keep it in the servant's place. Note that he does not moralize against commerce itself, as if buying and selling were beneath a serious soul; he honours the merchants as a genuine good, the arteries through which a society's needs are met. What he refuses is the surrender of the market to its own worst appetites — the hoarding that manufactures scarcity, the price fixed to wound. A just ruler neither strangles trade nor abandons it, and the difference between the two is a governor paying attention.

## The ruler's open door

Now the letter does something its structure has been quietly promising, and it does it with a cry. "Then — O God, O God!" Ali (ع) writes, and the exclamation is not decoration; it is the Imam seizing Malik by the collar. Pay particular attention, he says, to the lowest class of all: those with no resources, the destitute, the needy, the afflicted, the disabled — and among them both those who beg and those "whose wretchedness calls out to be alleviated but who do not beg," whose need is invisible precisely because their dignity hides it. The earlier episodes already unfolded why this class is the sharpest test of justice — because a ruler can win nothing from them, and so his treatment of them measures exactly how much of his justice is calculation and how much is real. Here the fresh thing is how practical the Imam's compassion becomes. Assign the poor a fixed share from the public treasury (bayt al-mal, بیت المال) and a share of what is gathered as the community's common wealth in every region, "for those who are furthest away have the same right as those nearest." Do not let any haughtiness turn your face from them; do not neglect them for some matter that merely looks more important, "for you will not be pardoned even the slightest shortcoming toward them." And because such people often cannot reach a governor at all — they are the ones the important men wave away in the corridor — Malik is to appoint, from among those he most trusts, a God-fearing and humble officer whose whole charge is to seek these people out and bring their affairs to him. He must take responsibility for the orphans and the aged who have nothing and yet cannot bring themselves to beg. "This is onerous for governors," the Imam admits — as the earlier episodes heard him admit — "and the fulfilment of all rights is onerous; but God makes it light for those who aspire to the Hereafter, who restrain their souls in patience, and trust in the truth of what God has promised them."

Notice how the Imam frames the difficulty. He does not pretend the duty is light; he says plainly that it is heavy, that the fulfilment of all rights is heavy. What he denies is that the heaviness is the last word on the matter. For a ruler whose horizon stops at this life, the poor are pure cost — a claim that consumes resources and returns nothing the ledger can measure — and such a ruler will, sooner or later, quietly let them slip. What makes the load bearable is not a trick of administration but a change of horizon: the man who aspires to the Hereafter, who has schooled his soul to patience and genuinely trusts God's promise, finds that the very duty which crushes the worldly ruler has become, for him, simply the natural expression of what he already believes. The weight has not changed. The man carrying it has.

The passage on the poor flows straight into the letter's teaching on presence, and the two belong together, because the deepest failure a ruler can commit against the weak is simply to become unreachable. Apportion a part of your time, the Imam says, to those with special needs, "making yourself free to attend to them personally, sitting with them in a public assembly in all due humility before God, your Creator." And then a detail that costs a ruler his dignity on purpose: keep your soldiers and guards and officers away from that assembly, "so that they may speak to you without inhibition." The apparatus of protection is also an apparatus of insulation, and the Imam orders it stood down when the weak come to speak. He grounds the command in something he heard from the Prophet himself — "God bless him and his family" — on more than one occasion: "A nation in which the rights of the weak are not wrested in an uninhibited manner from the strong will never be blessed." Bear patiently, he adds, any roughness or clumsiness of speech from such people; show them no irritation or disdain, and God will spread His compassion over you in the measure you spread yours over them. Give what you give graciously, and when you must refuse, refuse "with grace and apology."

Against all this outward availability the letter sets one seclusion the ruler must never surrender — the one door he closes, and it opens toward God. "Set apart the finest portion of your available time, and the greatest share of it," the Imam says, "for your soul and for what is between you and God." Perform each day the task that belongs to that day, and reserve the best of your hours for the duties owed to God alone, "the special means by which you purify your religion for Him." The busiest man in Egypt is commanded to guard, above every audience and dispatch, the private appointment with his Lord — because, as the earlier episodes on this letter argued at length, the ruler's justice in the daylight is drawn secretly from his standing before God in these hours, and a hub that loses its connection to the axle turns nothing at all. Even here the Imam bends the devotion toward the weak. When you lead the people in prayer, he says, do it "without repelling them or squandering it, for among them are people with infirmities or special needs." And he tells Malik how he learned this, in a memory that reaches back to his own youth: when the Prophet sent him to Yemen as his representative, he asked, "How should I lead them in prayer?" and the Prophet answered, "Lead them according to the prayer performed by the weakest among them, and be merciful to the believers." Even the pace of communal worship is set by the frailest person in the row. The whole letter is in that instruction.

It is worth dwelling on how strange this emphasis is in a manual of statecraft. Most advice to rulers across history has run the other way — toward distance, toward mystique, toward the careful management of access as a source of power, so that to reach the ruler at all becomes a privilege he can grant or withhold. The Imam's letter treats access as a duty and distance as a danger, and it does so because it measures a ruler by a standard the other manuals never invoke: not the security of his throne but the answer he will give on the day he meets God. Treat these people, he says of the weak who come to the assembly, in a manner "such that God may excuse you on the day you meet Him." The audience the ruler is really playing to is not the crowd in the hall. It is the One who will ask him, afterward, what he did with the hour.

The Imam then names the corrosion that accessibility exists to prevent — isolation. Do not prolong your absence from your subjects, he warns, "for the isolation of governors from those they govern creates a kind of constriction and breeds deficiency in awareness of their affairs." A sequestered ruler loses the very knowledge he needs; cut off from his people, "what is great comes to appear small and what is small, great; the beautiful appears ugly and the ugly, beautiful; the true becomes mixed with the false." He is only a human being, the Imam reminds him, who cannot see what people hide, and truth carries no visible mark to distinguish the honest report from the false one. So a ruler who walls himself off does not gain mystery or majesty; he goes blind. And there is no innocent version of the withdrawal, the Imam argues: either you are a man generous with yourself for the sake of truth — in which case, why hide, and cut yourself off from bestowing a right or doing an honourable deed? — or you are a man unwilling to give of yourself, in which case people will soon stop asking, despairing of your generosity. Either way seclusion accuses him. And most of what people actually bring, the Imam notes drily, is not even burdensome: a complaint of injustice, a plea for fairness in some dealing. The door is not heavy. A ruler only imagines it is.

Two last dangers to a ruler's openness close this movement, and both concern the people nearest him. A governor has favourites and intimates, the Imam says, and some of them are prone to presumption and self-dealing. Cut the root of that, "by granting no landed estates to anyone in your entourage or circle of friends" and giving them no reason to hope for such a grant — because an estate handed to an insider harms the neighbours in their water and their shared work, loading the burden on those nearby while the profit runs to the favourite, and leaving the governor with nothing for himself but the blame, "in this world and the next." Impose what is right on everyone it touches, the Imam says, near to you or far, "however heavily the path may lie upon you, for its outcome is praiseworthy." And when suspicion falls on the ruler himself — when his own subjects think he has done them an injustice — he is not to hide behind his office but to open it: "present your case openly so that the suspicion is cleared away from you by plain explanation." Such candour, the Imam says, is a discipline for the ruler's own soul, a kindness to his subjects, and the very thing that keeps them upright in their attachment to the truth. The just ruler, in the end, keeps no door permanently shut except the one that opens toward God.

## Covenants, blood, and last counsel

The letter turns now to a ruler's dealings with those outside his trust — his enemies — and here Ali (ع) reaches one of the moral summits of the whole document. Never turn away from a call to peace when God's pleasure lies in it, he tells Malik, "for in peace there is rest for your soldiers, relief from your cares, and security for your lands." But make peace with open eyes: once you have made it, stay fully on your guard, "for he may have drawn close only in order to catch you off guard," so take every precaution and hold even your own favourable opinion of him under suspicion. And then the thing itself — the pledge. If you and your enemy enter a solemn agreement, or he receives from you a covenant of protection, then keep it "with complete trustworthiness. Make your very life a shield for what you have promised." Of all the obligations God has laid down, the Imam says, there is none on which people stand more united, across every difference of inclination and opinion, than the honouring of pledges; even the pagan Arabs kept faith among themselves, "so greatly did they fear the consequences of treachery." So do not betray a covenant of protection, do not break a promise, do not deal treacherously even with an enemy — "for none dares defy God but the ignorant and wretched." God has made His own pledge and protection a sanctuary of security spread by His mercy over His servants; let no ruler introduce corruption or treachery into so sacred a thing. And should some hardship tempt Malik to break a binding agreement, the Imam sets the account plainly: patience under a difficulty whose good end you await is far better than a treachery whose consequence you dread — the fear that a reckoning from God should close in on you "from which you will find no release in this world or the next."

What is most striking in this passage is where the Imam locates the sanctity of a promise. He does not ground it in the trustworthiness of the other party — the enemy may well be plotting, and Malik is told in the same breath to keep his guard up and to suspect even his own good opinion of him. He grounds it in God. A covenant of protection is sacred not because the one protected has earned it but because God has made His own pledge a sanctuary spread by His mercy over His servants, and a ruler who breaks faith is not merely wronging a man; he is desecrating something that belongs to God. That is why even the treachery that would plainly benefit the state is forbidden. The advantage is real, and the Imam does not pretend otherwise. He simply sets it against a reckoning that no advantage can survive, and finds it worthless.

From the pledge the letter moves to blood, and its warning is absolute. "Beware of shedding blood unlawfully," the Imam says, "for nothing so swiftly invites retribution, weighs more heavily in its consequence, or is more apt to end a blessing and cut short a span of life, than bloodshed without right." He reaches for the scene of final judgment to fix its gravity: on the Day of Resurrection, God "will begin His judgement among His servants with the blood they have shed." And he shuts down in advance the tyrant's favourite justification — that a little killing secures a throne: "Do not seek to strengthen your authority through unlawful bloodshed, for that in truth weakens and undermines it — indeed, it brings it down and takes it away entirely." Power that rests on spilled blood is not power; it is a countdown. The Imam then binds Malik personally, and binds himself as the one who will hold him to account: "You have no excuse before God, nor before me, if you kill anyone deliberately." And with a realism that never leaves this letter, he legislates even for the accident — the whip, the sword, or the hand that goes too far in a lawful penalty, since even a single blow can kill: do not let the pride of your authority hold you back from paying the family of the slain what is rightfully theirs. There is no rank high enough to stand above the blood-price owed to a grieving household.

Then the letter gathers itself for its final counsel, and it returns, at the end, to the same inner ground on which it opened. Beware of self-admiration, the Imam warns, of leaning on whatever pleases you in yourself, of loving to be flattered — "for these are among the surest openings Satan finds to efface the good works of the virtuous." Beware of making your subjects feel indebted to you for the good you do them, of inflating your own deeds, and of promising what you then fail to perform — for laying people under obligation destroys the good itself, self-praise puts out the light of truth, and a broken promise earns the hatred of God and of men. He seals the warning with the Book: God has said, "It is indeed hateful to God that you say that which you do not do" (chapter 61, verse 3). And he ends where the whole letter began, with the mastery of the self: "So master the heat of your pride, the sharpness of your temper, the force of your hand, and the edge of your tongue," guarding against all of it by holding back the sudden impulse and delaying the blow until the anger has passed and self-command returns. Then the single sentence that names the source of every discipline the letter has demanded, the sentence the earlier episodes heard him make the wellspring of a ruler's justice: "You will never achieve this mastery over yourself unless you increase your remembrance of your return to your Lord." Everything — the mercy, the patience, the open door, the kept pledge, the unspilled blood — flows from a man keeping alive in himself the memory that he is going back to God.

It is a striking place for the letter to arrive. It has spent its length on the most external things imaginable — troop commands and tax schedules, the salaries of clerks and the punishment of hoarders — and it comes to rest on the most internal thing of all: a man's private remembrance of his own death and return. The Imam has, in effect, closed a circle. He opened by refusing to let Malik govern Egypt before he had learned to govern himself, and he closes by naming the single practice that makes self-government possible at all. Between those two points lies an entire kingdom, and the letter's final wager is that the kingdom stands or falls on something no census can count and no army can defend — what happens in the ruler's soul in the hours when no one is watching him at all.

The Imam signs off as a man who has now made his case complete. Remember, he tells Malik, the just governance and noble custom that came before you, the practice of the Prophet, and the obligations set down in the Book of God; follow faithfully what you have seen us do; and strive with all your soul to act on what I have laid upon you in this mandate, "by which I have made my plea complete against you, so that you will have no excuse when your soul races toward its caprice." And then the letter, which has spent thousands of words instructing another man, turns at the last into a prayer for them both. "I ask God, by the vastness of His mercy and the greatness of His power to grant every desire, that He bestow upon me and upon you the success that wins His pleasure" — that they might present a clear justification before Him and before His creatures, earn the fairest praise among His servants, and leave behind the most beautiful traces in the land, "and that He seal my life and yours with felicity (al-sa'ada) and martyrdom (al-shahada). Truly unto Him we are returning" (chapter 2, verse 156). The letter that taught a governor how to hold a province ends by asking for a martyr's death — and the man who wrote it would receive exactly that. "And peace be upon the Messenger of God — God bless him and his good and pure progeny."

## What this episode lands

The second half of the letter has carried the listener from the summit of the state down to its foundation and out to its edges, and the descent has been its whole meaning. The land matters more than the tax drawn from it; the scribe's hidden fault becomes the ruler's own; the merchant's freedom is watched but not crushed. Then the letter cries out over the poor — the destitute, the orphan, the aged, the ones too proud to beg — and commands a ruler to appoint an officer to hunt out their need, to sit with them in an assembly stripped of his guards, to set the pace of his own prayer by the weakest body in the room, and never to wall himself off, because a secluded ruler goes blind and every seclusion accuses him. Only one door does he keep shut, and it opens toward God, in the finest hours of his day.

And at the last the letter weighs a ruler on two things a lesser vision would have left to expediency: the pledges he keeps and the blood he refuses to spill. Make your life a shield for your promise, the Imam says, even to an enemy; and beware the blood shed without right, for God begins the judgment of the worlds with it. The whole of it comes back, in the final sentences, to a single discipline — that a man will never master his pride, his temper, his hand, or his tongue until he keeps alive the memory of his return to God. The letter that opened by refusing to hand Malik an army before it had ordered his soul closes by handing him nothing at all except that memory, and a prayer that they might both die well.

## Closing

And here the book itself comes to rest. It began by drawing near to a man the tradition places closest of all to the Prophet, and it has moved through his metaphysics of the One beyond number, his epistemology of the intellect that is a light and not a calculation, his mysticism of the remembrance in which the self is polished away, and now his politics — which turns out to be the same teaching worn as a working garment. For everything this last letter demands of a governor is only what the earlier episodes heard Ali (ع) demand of any soul that would know God: the curbing of the self that incites to evil, the good opinion of a generous God, the justice that is not fairness but the very nature of the Real, the remembrance that keeps the vertical axis of a life open and alive. The letter to Malik is the proof that this spiritual ethos was never meant to stay in the cell of the contemplative. It was meant to set tax rates, to appoint judges, to sit in an unguarded assembly with the poor, and to keep a promise to an enemy at the cost of a night's ease. A whole life stands behind it — a man who ruled under near-constant conflict, who was prevented outwardly from enacting much of the justice his soul demanded, and who never once, on the testimony of everything he left behind, slackened his effort or lost his hope. The book ends, as the letter ends, with a return: "Truly unto Him we are returning." Everything the series has traced — the closeness, the light, the remembrance, the justice — was, in the end, the shape of a soul making its way home.
