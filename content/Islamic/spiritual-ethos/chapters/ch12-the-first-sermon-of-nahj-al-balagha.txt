This episode sets down the book's commentary and lets Ali (ع) speak in his own voice, taking up the sermon that stands first in Nahj al-Balagha — his great proclamation of the oneness of God, followed by the making of the cosmos and the angels, the fashioning of Adam, and the long succession of prophets that ends in Muhammad and the Book he left behind. We hear why the collection begins precisely here, we listen to the sermon whole, and we follow the single arc by which a doctrine of divine oneness pours itself out into a world and a history.

## Where this episode opens

What would it sound like to praise a God of whom, by his own testimony, no praise can say anything true? Every episode until now has been an exposition — a modern reader's careful walk through the sayings of Ali (ع), gathering fragments of his words to illuminate the intellect, the soul, remembrance, and the sacred root of justice. This episode does something different. It sets the commentary aside and lets one of those primary texts sound in full. The last episode closed on Ali (ع) as the source from which Islam's entire mystical current is said to flow, his long life sealed at its very end in one act of remembrance — the invocation still moving on his tongue as he died. Now we go back to the beginning of his recorded speech — to the sermon that the compiler chose to place first of all, before every letter and every aphorism, as the doorway into the entire collection.

And it is a strange doorway. One might expect the opening address of a book famous for its counsel on justice and government to begin with law, or with the duties of rulers, or with the ordering of a community. It begins instead with a plunge straight into the divine oneness — and with a paradox sharp enough to stop the tongue. The very first thing the sermon does is use speech to announce that speech can deliver nothing of God: praise be to God, it opens, whose laudation those who speak cannot deliver. The office of words is to carry a meaning across to a listener; here that office is turned against itself, made to demonstrate that the One it reaches for is too lofty for any word to carry. The sermon praises by confessing that praise fails.

That paradox is not a flourish. It is the seed of everything the sermon grows. From the unreachable Essence it will descend, in one unbroken motion, through the origination of the heavens and the churning of the primordial sea, through the ranks of angels who never tire of glorifying, down to a handful of dust kneaded into a human form and quickened by a breath from God — and then up again through the long line of prophets, generation after generation, until the coming of Muhammad and the Book he leaves behind. In this episode we do three things: we ask why the greatest collection of Ali's words should open on negation rather than on law; we listen to the sermon entire, in refined English, as it was meant to be heard; and we follow its single arc from the God no concept can bound down into a created world that is pure gift and back up toward the knowledge of Him that the sermon calls the very foundation of religion.

## The overture of negation

Why should this be the first word? The collection that carries it gathers the sermons, letters, and short sayings of Ali (ع) into one book, and whoever arranged it had to choose what would stand at the threshold. The choice fell on this cosmic address, and the placement is itself a claim: everything that follows — every judgment on justice, every counsel to a governor, every diagnosis of the soul — rests on the ground this sermon lays. Before a person can rule rightly, or know himself truly, or act justly toward another, he must first stand rightly before God. And to stand rightly before God begins, the sermon insists, in knowing how little the mind can hold of Him.

Listen first to how it actually begins, because the craft is in the very shape of the sentences. Three parallel clauses open the sermon, and each names one of the ordinary works of speech only to declare it beaten. Praise be to God, whose laudation those who speak cannot deliver — there speech is defeated in its office of praising. Whose graces those who count cannot number — there counting is defeated in its office of reckoning. And whose rightful due those who strive cannot render — there striving itself is defeated in its office of requital. The three cover the whole of what a creature can offer its Lord: words, measure, and effort. And the sermon opens by confessing that all three fall short at once — that no tongue can say enough, no mind can count enough, no exertion can repay enough. This is not modesty. It is a precise map of the creature's poverty before the Real, drawn in the first breath, so that everything the sermon goes on to affirm is heard from inside that poverty rather than in forgetfulness of it.

So the sermon opens with a ladder. Foremost in religion, it says, is the knowledge of Him; the perfection of that knowledge is to believe in Him; the perfection of belief is to affirm His oneness; the perfection of affirming His oneness is to purify one's devotion to Him; and the perfection of that purity is to divest Him of every attribute. Each rung is the completion of the one below it, and the climb ends not in a fuller description of God but in the stripping away of description altogether. We have met this apophatic doctrine before, in the earlier teaching on paradox and again in the meditation on the Name and the Named; what is new here is to see it in its native form — not as a scholar's reconstruction but as Ali's own ascending ladder, and to see that he places it at the very head of his speech, as the first thing a listener must climb.

The method by which he climbs is negation that turns into affirmation. Watch how the reasoning works. To ascribe an attribute to God, the sermon argues, is to set beside Him something that is not Him — for every attribute testifies that it is other than the thing it qualifies, and every thing so qualified testifies that it is other than its attribute. From that single observation a chain unrolls, each link tightening on the last: whoever ascribes an attribute to God has joined another to Him; whoever joins another to Him has made Him two; whoever makes Him two has divided Him into parts; and whoever divides Him is ignorant of Him. A second chain follows the first: whoever points to Him has set a limit around Him; whoever limits Him has counted Him as one thing among things; whoever asks in what He is has enclosed Him; whoever asks upon what He rests has set Him apart. This is not abuse heaped on the careless believer. It is a piece of exact reasoning, a machine built to show that every determination the mind adds to God is a boundary, and that a boundary drawn around the boundless is simply false. The loftier the truth, the deeper the paradox needed to reveal it — as the earlier teaching on paradox already established — and here the paradox is worked out link by link until the mind has nowhere left to stand except in silence.

Yet the silence is not empty, and this is the crucial turn. The negation is the near side of an affirmation. The sermon does not deny that God is merciful, powerful, living; it denies only the boundary the mind draws when it pictures those qualities as separate, finite things added to Him. The pattern is the one carried in the testimony of faith itself: there is no god — that is the negation, the sweeping away of every idol the imagination sets up — except God — that is the affirmation, in which the sole reality is confessed. Applied to the divine names, the rhythm runs: there is no All-Compassionate but God, no All-Merciful but God. The mercy is not denied; the merely pictured mercy, the mercy shrunk to a bounded thing, is denied, so that what remains is a mercy that is nothing other than God Himself. This is why the sermon can lean on the Qur'anic word that seals the whole movement — glorified is God above all that they attribute to Him (chapter 23, verse 91). The point is not that God has no qualities but that no quality the mind can frame is large enough to be His.

There is a distinctive grammar to the way the sermon holds negation and affirmation together, and it is worth catching, because it will govern everything that follows. God is Being, the sermon says, but not through any coming-to-be; existent, but not out of former nothingness; with every thing, but not by association; other than every thing, but not by separation; acting, but not through movements or instruments. Each clause makes a positive claim and then, in the same breath, files away the limit that the claim would carry if it were said of a creature. He is with all things — but not as one thing sits beside another; He is other than all things — but not as one thing is fenced off from another. The mind is given something to hold and, immediately, forbidden to shrink it to the measure of the holding. This is neither a bare denial nor a naive assertion; it is a third way of speaking, in which every affirmation is rescued from becoming an idol by the negation stitched to its side.

Seen this way, the overture is not a decorative prelude to the practical wisdom the collection is famous for. It is the grammar of that wisdom. A ruler who has not first let go of every image his mind makes of God will make an idol of something — of his own power, his own certainty, his own tribe — and justice will die at that idol's altar. The whole later teaching of the collection, that justice is not merely fairness but a participation in the very nature of God, depends on this: that God has been cleared, at the outset, of every finite likeness, so that to serve Him is not to serve a bigger version of oneself but to be emptied for a reality beyond all self-interest. The sermon opens on negation because negation is the first act of a soul that means to see truly. Only a mind emptied of its idols is free enough to serve. That is the claim the compiler makes by placing this sermon first, and it is the ground on which everything else in the collection is built.

## The First Sermon

Praise be to God, whose laudation those who speak cannot deliver, whose graces those who count cannot number, and whose rightful due those who strive cannot render. He whom no far-reaching aspiration can attain, and whom no diving depth of understanding can fathom; He whose attribute has no bounding limit, no existing description, no appointed time, and no extended term. He originated all created things through His power, scattered the winds through His mercy, and steadied the trembling of His earth with mighty mountains.

The foremost of religion is the knowledge of Him. The perfection of knowing Him is to believe in Him. The perfection of believing in Him is to affirm His oneness. The perfection of affirming His oneness is to be sincere toward Him. And the perfection of sincerity toward Him is to divest Him of all attributes — for every attribute bears witness that it is other than the object it qualifies, and every such object bears witness that it is other than the attribute. So whoever ascribes an attribute to God, glorified be He, has joined another to Him; and whoever joins another to Him has made Him two; and whoever makes Him two has divided Him into parts; and whoever divides Him into parts is ignorant of Him. Whoever points to Him has confined Him; whoever confines Him has counted Him; and whoever asks "in what is He?" has enclosed Him, while whoever asks "upon what is He?" has set Him apart.

He is Being, but not through any coming-to-be; existent, but not out of former nothingness. He is with every thing, but not by association; and other than every thing, but not by separation. He acts, but not through movements or instruments. He sees, even when there was nothing of His creation to be seen. He is alone, for there was none whose company He might seek or whose absence He might feel.

He brought forth creation and originated it from the beginning, without pondering a plan, without profiting from experience, without setting any motion in train, and without the disquiet of any exertion. He gave each thing its appointed span, harmonized their differences, fixed their natures, and impressed upon them their forms — knowing them before He made them, encompassing their bounds and their ends, and aware of what joins them and what parts them.

Then He, glorified be He, cleft the atmospheres and split the surrounding regions and the layers of air. Into them He made water flow, its waves surging and swelling, heaped upon the back of a violent, thunderous wind. He commanded the wind to hold the water back and gave it power over the fury of its currents, setting it against the water's bounds — air beneath it spread wide, water above it pouring in flood.

Then He raised up another wind and made it barren, holding it long in its place, driving hard its course, and reaching it out far and wide. He ordered it to stir the deep waters and to churn the waves of the sea, until it beat them as a milk-skin is beaten, and drove them on in the open air as it had driven them in the void — turning their first back upon their last, their still upon their moving — until the water rose in a heaped mass and lifted up a froth. This froth He raised into the open and empty air and made of it the seven heavens: the lowest a stayed wave held back, the highest a roof upheld and a vault raised high, with no pillar to prop them and no nail to bind them. Then He adorned them with the ornament of stars and the light of shooting meteors, and hung within them a shining lamp and a luminous moon, in a turning orbit, a moving vault, and a wheeling firmament.

Then He parted the heights of the heavens and filled them with kinds of His angels. Among them are those who prostrate and do not bow, and those who bow and do not stand, and those who stand in ranks and never break them, and those who glorify Him and never weary. No slumber of the eye overtakes them, no wandering of the mind, no weakness of the body, no forgetful heedlessness. Among them are the trustees of His revelation and the tongues that carry His word to His messengers, and those who come and go with His decree and His command. Among them are the guardians of His servants and the keepers of the gates of His gardens. Among them are those whose feet are fixed in the lowest depths of the earth while their necks pass beyond the highest heaven, whose limbs reach past the ends of the world, and whose shoulders are matched to the pillars of the Throne. Their eyes are lowered beneath it, and they have wrapped themselves in their wings beneath it, and between them and all below them are veils of majesty and curtains of might. They do not picture their Lord in any form, nor ascribe to Him the marks of things made, nor confine Him within places, nor point to Him by any likeness.

Then He, glorified be He, gathered a handful of the earth — of the hard and the soft, the sweet and the salt — and poured water over it until it was cleansed, and kneaded it with moisture until it held. From it He shaped a figure with curves and joints, limbs and parts. He set it until it dried, and hardened it until it rang, for a known term and an appointed end. Then He breathed into it of His spirit, and it rose up a human being, endowed with minds to govern its thought, with limbs to serve it, with instruments to wield, and with a knowing by which to tell the true from the false, taste from taste, and color from color — a being kneaded of clashing natures and gathered opposites, of heat and cold, of moisture and dryness.

Then God asked of the angels the discharge of the trust He had laid on them and the keeping of the covenant of His command: that they bow down before Adam in submission and lower themselves before his honor. So He said, "Prostrate yourselves before Adam," and they prostrated — all save Iblis. Pride seized him, and wretchedness overcame him. He gloried in his own making of fire and scorned the creature of clay. So God granted him respite, that His wrath might be earned in full and the trial completed and the promise fulfilled; and He said, "You are among those reprieved until the day of the appointed time."

Then He settled Adam in a dwelling where his life was made pleasant and his abode secure, and He warned him of Iblis and his enmity. But the enemy deceived him, begrudging him his lasting home and his fellowship with the righteous; and Adam bartered certainty for doubt and resolve for weakness, and took dread in place of gladness and remorse in place of pride. Then God spread His repentance before him, taught him the word of His mercy, and promised him return to His garden — and sent him down to the abode of trial and the begetting of children.

From among Adam's children God chose prophets and took their pledge upon His revelation and the delivering of His message — at a time when most of His creatures had broken their covenant with Him, ignored His right, and set up rivals beside Him, and the devils had turned them from the knowledge of Him and cut them off from His worship. So He sent among them His messengers and raised up His prophets one after another, to call them to fulfill the covenant of their creation, to remind them of His forgotten gift, to argue with them by the delivering of the message, to stir up for them the buried treasures of the intellect, and to show them the signs of His power: the sky spread above them, the earth laid beneath them, the means of their living, the deaths that carry them off, the ills that age them, and the events that follow upon them.

God did not leave His creation without a sent prophet, a revealed book, a binding proof, or a clear road. His messengers were never made helpless by their small number or by the throng of those who called them liars. To each earlier one the name of the one to come was given, and by each later one the one before was made known. So the generations passed and the ages ran on; fathers went and sons came after them — until God sent Muhammad, His Messenger, to fulfill His promise and complete His prophethood, his pledge already taken from the prophets, his signs well known, his birth noble.

The people of the earth in his day were of scattered creeds, divided desires, and warring ways: some likening God to His creation, some twisting His names, some pointing to another than Him. By him God guided them out of error, and by his standing among them delivered them from ignorance. Then God chose for Muhammad the meeting with Himself, and was pleased to bring him near, and honored him by taking him away from the abode of this world and the place of trial. He drew him to Himself in honor, and left among you what the prophets left in their communities — for they do not abandon them untended, without a plain road and a standing beacon.

He left the Book of your Lord among you, setting forth its lawful and its forbidden, its obligatory and its recommended, its abrogating and abrogated, its permitted and its enjoined, its particular and its general, its lessons and its parables, its unbound and its bound, its clear and its obscure — unfolding what it packed close and clarifying what it left dark. In it are matters whose knowledge is bound upon the servants and matters whose ignorance is forgiven them; things it sets down as obligatory that the practice of the Prophet is known to have lifted, and things the practice makes obligatory that the Book has left free to leave. There are duties fixed to their appointed times that fall away when their time is past. It marks off the forbidden acts, one from another: the great sins, against which it threatens the fire, and the small ones, for which it holds out hope of forgiveness; and it names acts acceptable in their lesser measure and left open in their greater, binding in neither.

## Creation and the prophets

The sermon does not remain in the heights of negation. Having emptied the mind of every concept of God, it descends — and the descent is the second half of the teaching. It is a striking thing to notice: the same movement that just now denied that God could be bounded, timed, or divided turns immediately to describe Him as sheer origination. He is Being, the sermon says, but not through any coming-to-be; existent, but not out of a former nothingness; acting, but not through movement or instrument. Every phrase affirms an act and in the same breath strips away the limit that would make it a creature's act. And this is exactly what lets the cosmology be what it is. Because God needs nothing, gains nothing, and undergoes no change in creating, the world that follows from Him is not a repair of some lack in God but a pure and unnecessary gift. The negation of the opening is what makes the generosity of the creation intelligible: only a God bound by nothing could give being freely.

Then the making unfolds, and Ali (ع) tells it as a vast physical drama, and the drama has a direction: it moves from turbulence to order. God cleaves the atmospheres and sends water surging through them, a formless flood heaped upon a violent wind; He sets a first wind to hold the flood in check, giving it mastery over the fury of the currents; then He raises a second, barren wind and drives it hard, and this wind beats the sea as a milk-skin is beaten, turning its first upon its last and its stillness into motion until the churned water throws up a froth. From that froth — the very foam of a chaos being worked — He lifts the seven heavens, staying the lowest as a held-back wave and raising the highest as a vault, roofing them without pillar to prop them or nail to bind them, and hangs in them the lamp of the sun, the luminous moon, and the ornament of the stars in their wheeling orbits. The image is deliberately violent at the start and serene at the end: what begins as beaten water and thunderous wind is brought, by nothing but the ordering command, to the stillness of a lamp turning in a settled sky. Creation is shown not as a calm act of decoration but as the mastering of a tumult, and the mastery is total precisely because the One who orders it is bound by nothing within it. It is worth dwelling on what happens next, because it is the hinge that ties the cosmology back to the theology. God fills the parted heavens with ranks of angels, and the sermon lingers over their orders as if describing a great hierarchy of nearness. Some are forever prostrate and never bow; some bow and never stand; some stand in unbroken ranks that never part; some glorify without pause, and no slumber of the eye overtakes them, no wandering of the mind, no weariness of the body, no forgetful heedlessness. Others have office: they are the trustees of His revelation and the tongues that carry His word to His messengers, or the guardians of His servants and the keepers of the gates of His gardens. And at the summit are beings of a scale the mind can hardly frame — their feet fixed in the depths of the earth while their necks pass beyond the highest heaven, their shoulders matched to the pillars of the Throne, their eyes lowered and their wings wrapped about them beneath it, with veils of majesty and curtains of might set between them and everything below. Yet for all their grandeur, the decisive word the sermon says of them is a negative one: they do not picture their Lord in any form, nor confine Him within places, nor point to Him by any likeness. The apophasis of the opening has become a form of worship. The very creatures nearest the Throne adore by refusing to picture the One they adore — keeping, in their mode of praise, the same divesting of every likeness that the sermon demanded of the human mind at the start. Heaven itself is built on negation; nearness to God is measured not by how vividly one imagines Him but by how completely one has ceased to.

Then the drama narrows to a handful of dust. God gathers earth hard and soft, sweet and salt, kneads it with water, shapes it, dries it until it rings like fired clay — and breathes into it of His spirit, so that it rises up a human being, given limbs to use, instruments to wield, and a knowing by which to tell the true from the false. As the earlier teaching on the in-breathed spirit already unfolded, the human is precisely this: clay quickened by a breath from God and awakened to discernment. What the sermon adds is the setting — that this quickened creature is at once the object of a cosmic test. God commands the angels to bow before Adam, and all bow save Iblis, who glories in his making of fire and scorns the creature of clay. Here again the earlier teaching on Iblis and hidden idolatry has already named the lesson: the first refusal is pride, the self exalting its own substance over the command of God. The sermon simply lets us watch it happen at the origin of the human story — Adam settled in his garden, warned of the enemy, beguiled, brought to remorse, granted repentance, and sent down into the abode of trial to begin the long line of his children.

And with that line the sermon opens its last movement: the succession of the prophets. From Adam's offspring God chooses messengers and takes their pledge, sending them one after another into a creation that has largely forgotten its covenant — to remind them of a forgotten gift, to argue with them by revelation, and, in a phrase the earlier teaching on revelation and the buried treasures has already opened, to stir up for them the buried treasures of the intellect. God never leaves His creation without a prophet sent, a book revealed, a binding proof, or a plain road — and the messengers are never made helpless, the sermon insists, by the smallness of their number or the throng of those who call them liars. There is a single unbroken chain here: each earlier messenger is given the name of the one to come, and each later one confirms the one who went before, so that the generations pass and the ages run on, fathers going and sons succeeding, with the promise of the final prophet carried down the whole line. The succession arrives at last at Muhammad, sent to fulfill the promise and complete the prophethood, his pledge already taken from the prophets before him. He finds the people of the earth scattered into warring creeds — some likening God to His creation, some twisting His names, some pointing to another than Him — and by him God guides them out of that error, and then, choosing for him the meeting with Himself, draws him away into honor.

And here the sermon makes its last turn, for a prophet's departure would leave his community untended were it not for what he leaves behind. Muhammad leaves the Book. The sermon closes by unfolding it — its lawful and its forbidden, its obligatory and its recommended, its abrogating and its abrogated, its particular and its general, its clear and its obscure — a scripture that at once binds and frees, threatening the fire for the great sins and holding out forgiveness for the small, fixing some duties to their appointed times and lifting others when their time is past. The point of ending here is unmistakable. The arc that began in an Essence no word can reach has come to rest in a book placed in human hands — the knowledge of God, which the sermon named in its first breath as the foremost thing in religion, now set down in a form a community can hold, read, and be guided by until the end.

So the sermon is a single motion, and its two halves are one teaching. The negation at the top and the cosmology and history below it are not two subjects but one: a God bound by nothing pours out a world that is pure gift, sets in the midst of it a creature carrying a breath of His own spirit and an intellect made to know, and then labors across all of time, through prophet after prophet, to wake that creature back to the knowledge of the One. The overture said that the foremost of religion is the knowledge of God. The whole descent that follows is the story of how that knowledge is planted, lost, and patiently restored — until the sermon's first sentence and its last are seen to be the same sentence, spoken once at the beginning of the world and again at the end of the sermon.

## What this episode lands

This episode let the source speak. Behind every earlier meditation on the intellect, the soul, remembrance, and justice stands a body of primary texts, and this is the first and greatest of them — the sermon the compiler set at the threshold of the whole collection. We saw why it opens not on law but on negation: because a soul that has not first let go of every idol the mind makes of God cannot see truly, and cannot rule, or know itself, or act justly, until it has. We heard the sermon whole — the ladder from the knowledge of God to the divesting of every attribute, the descent through the churning of the primordial sea and the ranks of the tireless angels, the kneading of Adam from clay and the breath of the spirit, the pride of Iblis, and the long succession of prophets that ends in Muhammad and the Book. And we followed the single arc that binds it all: the negation and the creation are one teaching, for only a God bound by nothing could give being as sheer gift, and the whole of that gift is ordered toward the return of the creature to the knowledge of the One — the very knowledge the sermon names, in its first breath, as the foremost thing in religion.

## Closing

There is a reason this sermon stands first. Before the counsel to governors, before the judgments on justice, before the diagnosis of the soul, comes the knowledge of God — and Ali (ع) sets it at the head of his speech not as a theologian arranging concepts in order but as one who has looked, and found every word too small. The paradox with which the sermon opens, that speech can deliver nothing of God, is the confession of a man who knows the difference between talking about the Real and standing before it. Everything the rest of the collection will say about how to live rests on that standing. To empty the mind of its idols, to praise by admitting that praise fails, to let a doctrine of oneness become a whole cosmos and a whole history and still point beyond itself to the One no concept can hold — this is the spiritual ethos the entire book has been tracing, and here it is heard at its source, in the first words the tradition chose to remember.
