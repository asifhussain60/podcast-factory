# Foundations of Faith and Guardianship

## Where this episode opens

Annotated structure: the chapter as a whole, dissected into its parts, with one callout per part.

Whole: an opening chapter that lays foundations rather than settling a single argument.

Parts, in order of appearance:
- Outer wrapper: the compressed authorial preface. Position: before the teaching begins.
- First movement: levels of faith, the seven duties, the twelve obligations. Position: the base course of stones.
- Second movement: the walaya of the Father of Imams, and scenes in which questions expose who carries knowledge. Position: the load-bearing column.
- Third movement: salām, prayers upon Muhammad and his family, reverence for the borders of religion. Position: the doctrine brought down to the doorway.

Annotations:
- The preface names the author, Faidh Ali bin Sayyidna Sulaiman, the work, Iṣāf al-Ṭālib fī Jamīʿ al-Maṭālib, the method, scattered rulings gathered from the books of the saints of God and their learned preachers, and the purpose, that seekers understand the doctrine of the Ahl al-Bayt. Orientation, not teaching.
- First movement: an inward claim becomes a map of obligation.
- Second movement: a claim of authority becomes a demonstration of knowledge.
- Third movement: doctrine becomes speech a person uses every day.

Contrast pair: two models of how a chapter can argue, and which one this chapter uses.

Column A: The courtroom model, rejected here
- Shape: a thesis defended against opponents
- Movement: claim, objection, rebuttal, verdict
- Evidence: marshalled to win a point
- Ending: closure

Column B: The treasury model, used here
- Shape: a stocked store of numbered structures, scriptural phrases, inherited sayings, practices, and stories
- Movement: accumulation by category
- Evidence: laid out so later rulings can draw on it
- Ending: foundation, deliberately not closure

Shared row: both want the listener convinced. Only the treasury expects the listener to return and draw again.

Process flow: the opening proposition, from copied religion to the verdict the chapter passes on it.

Start: a person imitates the worship of God and the call of the Ahl al-Bayt
  down to
Test 1: is there knowledge behind the imitation
  no
Test 2: is there action proving the claim
  no
  down to
Verdict: no right to claim the name of faith
  down to
Consequence: no covenant and no honor before God, and none before His guardian
  down to
Stated reason: he has departed from faith

Branch, stated by the chapter as the failure chain:
- Imitation without knowledge, unstable
- Knowledge without action, unproven
- Action without walaya, cut off from the line by which religion is interpreted

End state: faith redefined as a formed life, right attachment, right knowledge, right practice, right recognition of the guides.

## The levels of faith

Hierarchy: the levels of faith as the source states them, with the first level opened into its named members.

- Level 1: Faith has eight levels, stated from the noble books
  - Level 2: The first level, charity
    - Level 3: Parents
    - Level 3: Relatives
    - Level 3: Orphans
    - Level 3: The needy
    - Level 3: The neighbor who is a relative
    - Level 3: The neighbor who is nearby
    - Level 3: The companion at one's side
    - Level 3: The traveler
    - Level 3: Those whom one's faith obligates one to care for

> Know, my brother, that faith has eight levels.
> Attribution: the noble books, as cited at the head of the chapter.

Comparison matrix: each member of the first level, and what the chapter says it tests.

| Member of the first level | What it tests | Why it resists imitation |
|---|---|---|
| Parents | Gratitude | The debt precedes any choice the person made |
| Relatives | Loyalty beyond preference | Kinship is not selected for congeniality |
| Orphans | Compassion toward those who cannot repay | No return is available to motivate it |
| The needy | The same, measured by want rather than status | Benefit cannot be the reason |
| The neighbor who is a relative | Two claims at once, blood and proximity | Convenience and duty overlap and must be told apart |
| The neighbor who is nearby | Duty without blood | Proximity alone creates the claim |
| The companion at one's side | Care outside the permanent household | The relation is temporary, the obligation is not |
| The traveler | Care for the one who will not remain | No ongoing relationship rewards it |
| Those whom faith obligates one to care for | Whether religion has trained the eye | The claim is invisible to ordinary social instinct |

Annotated structure: the shape of the first level, read as a map rather than a mood.

Whole: the first face of faith.
Position of the whole: outward before inward.
Parts and callouts:
- Nearest standing figures, parents and relatives. Callout: faith reaches those it did not choose.
- The vulnerable, orphans and the needy. Callout: faith reaches those who cannot repay.
- Those passing through, the companion and the traveler. Callout: faith reaches beyond the permanent circle.
- The open clause, those whom faith obligates. Callout: the circle is left deliberately unclosed.

Reading: the levels of faith are not levels of inward mood. They are levels of responsibility.

Genealogy chain: how the chapter derives its two numbered sets from a cited authority.

Mustaṇṣiriyya Councils → the fourth council of the Quran → the Imams of religion are seven, recurring in this world as Fridays recur until the Resurrection → the twelve proofs appear in the world → to know the limits of the testimony of God is to perform its rights → the rights are given in two groups → seven duties for religion, and twelve obligations for the pious

## The seven duties and the twelve obligations

Contrast pair: the two numbered sets, read as the vertical and the horizontal of one religion.

Column A: The seven duties, the vertical structure
- Member 1: Walaya
- Member 2: Purity
- Member 3: Prayer
- Member 4: Zakāt
- Member 5: Fasting
- Member 6: Ḥajj
- Member 7: Jihad
- Direction: upward, toward the acknowledged guide
- Function in the chapter: orders action by recognition
- Failure if taken alone: religion becomes a list of offices

Column B: The twelve obligations, the horizontal spread
- Member 1: Honoring parents
- Member 2: Maintaining ties of kinship
- Member 3: Protecting the neighbor
- Member 4: Enjoining good
- Member 5: Forbidding evil
- Member 6: Truthfulness in one's home
- Member 7: Good conduct with one's spouse
- Member 8: Kindness to those in one's care
- Member 9: Spreading peace
- Member 10: Feeding others
- Member 11: Building brotherhood
- Member 12: Visiting the sick
- Direction: outward, into family, neighborhood, household, community
- Function in the chapter: turns doctrine into mercy
- Failure if taken alone: religion becomes a moral handbook

Shared row: the two sets are not unrelated lists. Walaya prevents the duties from becoming private self-discipline detached from guidance. The social obligations prevent walaya from becoming a claim of attachment that never becomes mercy.

Source note, recorded as the source's own legal formulation and not expanded here: the source adds that the twelve are not obligatory for women.

Annotated structure: the placement of walaya at the head of the seven.

Whole: the ordered list of seven duties.
Part in question: walaya, occupying position one.
Callout on the position: the author does not place guardianship after the ritual duties as a later decoration.
Callout on the consequence: walaya is the gate through which the remaining six are rightly understood.
Callout on the alternative that is refused: a schedule of obligations with no acknowledged guide.

Process flow: the quiet pedagogy in the order of the twelve, traced as a single movement outward.

Start: those through whom life is received, parents
  down to
Kinship
  down to
The neighbor
  down to
Moral speech, enjoining good and forbidding evil
  down to
Inside the house, truthfulness at home and conduct with one's spouse
  down to
Dependents, those in one's care
  down to
Outward into the community: spreading peace, feeding others, building brotherhood
  down to
End: the bedside, visiting the sick

Reading of the flow: from the authority of God and His guardian all the way to a bedside. The highest doctrine is not betrayed by the smallest mercy, it is confirmed there.

Hierarchy: the doctrine of right attachment, as the chapter frames it through a saying of the Commander of the Faithful.

- Level 1: The community of the Children of Israel
  - Level 2: Seventy-two sects
- Level 1: This nation
  - Level 2: Seventy-three sects
    - Level 3: One sect, in Paradise
    - Level 3: Seventy-two sects, in the Fire

> The Children of Israel divided into seventy-two sects, and this nation will divide into seventy-three, one of them is in Paradise and the rest are in the Fire.
> Attribution: Mawlānā the Commander of the Faithful, ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, in his four hundred precepts.

Reading: the many sects are not presented as sociological variety. They are presented as the visible result of losing the true center.

Contrast pair: the two reductions the chapter refuses.

Column A: Hearing only the social ethics
- Result: the chapter becomes a moral handbook
- What is lost: the line of interpretation, the recognized guide, the limits of the testimony of God
- Symptom: charity that never asks where guidance comes from

Column B: Hearing only the hierarchy
- Result: the chapter becomes a list of offices
- What is lost: parents, kin, neighbor, spouse, dependents, brotherhood, the sick
- Symptom: recognition that never becomes mercy

Shared row: the author wants neither reduction. The point is a formed religious life, where obedience, knowledge, and recognition occupy the same house.

## Guardianship and knowledge

Comparison matrix: scriptural phrases read through the station of the Imams, as cited from Jaʿfar ibn Manṣūr al-Yaman in the Book of the Foster-Suckling.

| Scriptural phrase | Ordinary reading | Reading given in the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| God is his master, Jibrīl and the righteous of the believers | Righteous believers in general | The Imam of the believers, who is ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, the guide of every believer |
| Their father was righteous | A virtuous ancestor | An Imam |
| The earth is inherited by My righteous servants | Anonymous virtue inherits | The Imams from among God's servants inherit |

Reading of the method: words such as righteous and heirs are not left as broad moral terms. They are tied to an office, a lineage, a mode of inheritance.

Visual metaphor: guardianship given cosmological depth, as al-Muʿizz li-Dīn Allāh sets it out in a Friday sermon.

Abstract relation: the correspondence between the seen order and the unseen order.
Metaphor supplied by the source: pen and tablet.
Element assignments:
- The speaker in the physical world corresponds to the pen in the spiritual world
- Beneath it, four ranks, set in order
- Each of the four has a spiritual rank guiding it toward the one beyond it
- The foundation faces the tablet
- The foundation engraves in the tablet what it derives from the spiritual world, as a pen engraves in a tablet

Reading: guidance is not only historical succession. Knowledge descends, is inscribed, is made available through proper ranks.

Contrast pair: two things that can be confused when authority is discussed.

Column A: Possession of power
- Held by: those in political position
- Demonstrated by: command, wealth, office
- Tested in this chapter by: questions the holder cannot answer
- Outcome in the reports: anger, or referral elsewhere

Column B: Possession of knowledge
- Held by: the Father of Imams and his household
- Demonstrated by: precise answers that protect doctrine
- Tested in this chapter by: the same questions
- Outcome in the reports: the questioner bears witness

Shared row: in the logic of the chapter, knowledge is not an ornament of authority. Knowledge is one of the signs by which authority is known.

## The three tests of knowledge

Comparison matrix: the three reports, set against one another.

| Report | Who sends the questions | Where it happens | Who answers | Domain of the questions | How it ends |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The messenger from Rūm | The king of Rūm | Medina | The Father of Imams | Theology: unity, justice, the limits of knowledge | Testimony of faith and of guardianship, then the money distributed |
| The Syrian at al-Raḥba | Ibn al-Aṣfar, carried by a messenger of Muʿāwiyah | al-Raḥba | Al-Ḥasan, chosen by the questioner | Creation, scripture, geography, law, force | The answers written down and carried away |
| The caliphal inquiry | ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, asking on his own behalf | Not specified | The Father of Imams | The soul: dreams, love, memory | An acknowledgement of dependence |

Process flow: the first report, from the sending of the test to the giving away of the money.

Start: a messenger arrives in Medina from the king of Rūm, carrying money and a test
  down to
Instruction he was given: go to Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, and if the Prophet does not agree with him, ask about his successor and put proof before him
  down to
He poses three questions: what is not God's, what is not with God, what God does not know
  down to
Branch, the responses of those present:
  - Abū Bakr: grows angry, treats the questions as disbelief
  - ʿUmar: responds in much the same way
  - ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās: recognizes that the problem is not the questions but the inability to answer them, and directs the man to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib
  down to
The Father of Imams comes out, a pen behind his ear, ink on his finger
  down to
The three answers are given
  down to
The messenger bears witness
  down to
End: the money is given to al-Ḥasan and al-Ḥusayn to distribute among the Muslims

Comparison matrix: the three questions from Rūm, and what each answer protects.

| Question | How it could be heard | The answer given | What the answer protects |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is not God's | Impiety | God has no partner | Divine unity |
| What is not with God | Accusation | Injustice | Divine justice |
| What does God not know | A denial of omniscience | That the Messiah is the son of God, a thing that does not exist, and God has no knowledge of what has no existence | That falsehood has no real object, so nonexistence is not a hidden fact inside God's knowledge |

> I bear witness that there is no god but God, that Muhammad is the Messenger of God, and that you are his guardian.
> Attribution: the messenger of the king of Rūm, after the three answers.

Reading: knowledge reveals walaya, and the proof of knowledge ends in generosity. The right answer is followed by right distribution.

Annotated structure: the second report at al-Raḥba, dissected into its three movements.

Whole: a test carried from Ibn al-Aṣfar through a messenger of Muʿāwiyah.
Parts and callouts:
- The approach. A man greets the Commander of the Faithful and is discovered to be a messenger sent by Muʿāwiyah. Callout: the test enters disguised as a greeting.
- The lament, placed before any answer. The Father of Imams names those who severed his ties of kinship, wasted his days, denied his right, diminished his station, and conspired against him. Callout: the report places knowledge inside injustice, the rightful knower has been wronged, and his knowledge remains the proof.
- The delegation. He calls al-Ḥasan, al-Ḥusayn, and Muḥammad, and tells the Syrian to ask whichever of them he wishes. The Syrian chooses al-Ḥasan. Callout: guardianship is not a solitary brilliance that dies with one figure, the household itself is a school of inherited knowledge.

Comparison matrix: the questions carried from Ibn al-Aṣfar, and the answers al-Ḥasan gives.

| Question | Answer | What kind of measure it is |
|---|---|---|
| The distance between truth and falsehood | Four fingers, what the eyes see is truth, what the ears may hear is often falsehood | A bodily measure between two senses |
| The distance between heaven and earth | The prayer of the oppressed, and the reach of sight | A moral measure and an optical one |
| The distance between east and west | The arc of the sun in a single day | A temporal measure |
| The meaning of the galaxy | The lights of the sky from which rain descends | A celestial sign tied to provision |
| The rainbow | God's bow, and a sign of safety from drowning | A celestial sign tied to covenant |
| The darkening of the moon | Explained through the verse on the two signs | A celestial sign tied to scripture |
| The first thing that appeared on earth | The valley of Dab | A point of origin in place |
| The first thing that moved upon it | The palm tree | A point of origin in motion |
| The spring to which the souls of the polytheists flee | Salma | A named destination |
| The hermaphrodite | Treated as a legal question of discernment, with signs by which the body is examined | A juridical measure |

> And We made the night and the day two signs, and We erased the sign of the night and made the sign of the day visible.
> Attribution: the verse cited by al-Ḥasan in answer on the darkening of the moon.

Hierarchy: the ten things, each harder than the last, in the ascending order the report gives.

- Rank 1: Stone
  - Rank 2: Iron
    - Rank 3: Fire
      - Rank 4: Water
        - Rank 5: Cloud
          - Rank 6: Wind
            - Rank 7: The angel
              - Rank 8: The Angel of Death
                - Rank 9: Death itself
                  - Rank 10: The command of God, Lord of all worlds

Reading of the ascent: each rank is harder than the one below it, and the chain ends where nothing stands above it.

Process flow: what the second report proves, once the Syrian leaves.

Start: the Syrian writes the answers down
  down to
He carries them back
  down to
Ibn al-Aṣfar recognizes that this knowledge does not come from Muʿāwiyah
  down to
He locates its source: the substance of prophecy and the seat of the message
  down to
End: a foreign ruler's recognition exposes domestic illegitimacy, those who possess power do not thereby possess knowledge, and those whose right was diminished hold the prophetic inheritance

Comparison matrix: the third report, ʿUmar's three questions on the soul.

| Question | Answer | Mechanism named |
|---|---|---|
| Why some dreams are vivid and some are nothing | Every sleeping person is touched by a likeness of death, what the soul sees while separated from the body and journeying through the realm is truth, what it sees upon its return is falsehood and the delusion of Satan | The taking and returning of souls |
| Why a person loves or hates another without knowing why | God created souls before bodies by two thousand years, they met and mingled like horses, what they came to know of one another then they recognize and love now, what they came to dislike then they dislike now | The preexistence and meeting of souls |
| Why a thing is remembered long, forgotten when needed, then recalled | The heart carries a scar like the scar of the moon, when the heart is covered over the servant forgets what he has seen and heard | The covering of the heart |

> God takes souls at the time of their death, and those that have not died, during their sleep, He keeps those for whom He has decreed death, and releases the others until an appointed term.
> Attribution: the verse with which the answer on dreams begins.

> May God not spare him a land where ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib is absent and his name is not mentioned.
> Attribution: ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, closing the third report.

Visual metaphor: the range of the household's knowledge, made spatial.

Abstract relation: why a guide's competence must be comprehensive.
Metaphor: concentric circles of what requires interpretation.
Element assignments:
- Innermost: the soul, its dreams, affections, and memory
- Next: the body and its ambiguities, judged in law
- Next: scripture, read through its signs
- Next: creation, its distances, lights, and origins
- Outermost: God, unity and justice, and the limits of what can be known
Reading: a guide who could answer only ritual details would not suffice for the world this chapter imagines. The household's authority is comprehensive because creation, scripture, law, and the soul all require interpretation.

## Peace and belonging

Annotated structure: what the final movement names, laid out as one field with four quarters.

Whole: the practice of belonging.
Parts and callouts:
- Walaya of the Imams and the family of the Messenger of God, peace and blessings be upon him. Callout: the attachment already established, now carried into speech.
- The obligation of praying for Muhammad and the family of Muhammad. Callout: doctrine repeated daily on the tongue.
- The good proclaimers, the righteous borders, and their stations. Callout: the ordered ranks, named so they can be prayed through.
- The command to return peace, and the etiquette of replying to greetings. Callout: the smallest action the chapter asks for, and the one every listener will perform.

Contrast pair: the two halves of the Sunnah of salām, as the twenty-third Mustaṇṣiriyya council divides it.

Column A: Above, seven sections
- Source of the peace: what comes from God and from His angels
- Count: seven
- Character: descending
- Highest member: God's word, Peace be upon you, described as the first, most particular, most exalted, and most sincere

Column B: Below, twelve sections
- Source of the peace: what comes from human beings
- Count: twelve
- Character: returning and circulating
- First member: the greeting of the people of the right hand to the faithful prophet, peace be upon him and his noble family, Peace be upon you for your patience

Shared row: the same word crosses both halves. God gives peace, the angels carry peace, the faithful receive and return peace, and the community becomes visible through the words it exchanges.

## The ordered forms of salām

Hierarchy: the seven heavenly sections in the order the council gives them.

- Section 1: God's word, Peace be upon you
- Section 2: The address to Noah, O Noah, descend in peace from Us
- Section 3: The greeting on the day people meet God
- Section 4: Greetings and peace being conveyed
- Section 5: The angels entering upon them from every gate and saying, Peace be upon you
- Section 6: The command that God and His angels pray for the Prophet, peace be upon him, followed by the address to the believers
- Section 7: Peace be upon the family of Yasin

Reading of section 7: Yasin is interpreted as the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him and his family, and the family of Yasin as the family of Muhammad.

> O you who believe, pray for him and submit to him in full submission.
> Attribution: the address that follows the sixth section.

> When you enter houses, greet one another with a greeting from God, blessed and good.
> Attribution: the verse cited for the greeting on entering houses.

> And when the ignorant address them, they say, peace.
> Attribution: the verse cited for the believer's peace in the face of the ignorant.

Annotated structure: the statement of what salām is, from the twenty-fourth assembly.

Whole: the revelation of peace.
Parts and callouts:
- Legal standing. It is an obligatory Sunnah of Islam. Callout: not optional courtesy.
- Content. It speaks of affection and submission. Callout: two things at once, warmth toward the person and submission toward God.
- Divine name. Salām is one of the names of the One possessed of majesty and honor. Callout: greeting is not small talk, a divine name is being spoken.

Hierarchy: the opening members of the twelve human sections.

- Member 1: The greeting of the people of the right hand to the faithful prophet, Peace be upon you for your patience
- Member 2: The greeting upon entering houses
- Member 3: The peace of one who concludes the prayer
- Member 4: The peace of the believer in the face of the ignorant

Process flow: the etiquette of who initiates the greeting, as an ordered chain.

The rider on a camel → initiates to → the rider on a horse
The rider on a horse → initiates to → the rider on a donkey
The rider on a donkey → initiates to → the one walking
The one walking → initiates to → the one standing
The one standing → initiates, and returns

Comparison matrix: the three kinds of return, and when each belongs.

| Kind of reply | Rule | Scriptural or reported ground | Who it is for |
|---|---|---|---|
| The virtuous reply | Greet the initiator with something better than what was given | Return it with something better | Anyone, as the higher option |
| The complete reply | Respond in kind | Or return it as it is | Anyone, as the sufficient option |
| The just reply | Answer, And upon you | The reported deception, in which some said Death be upon you instead of Peace be upon you | Dhimmis, who do not initiate the greeting, and who receive a reply if they do |

> And when you are greeted with a greeting, return it with something better, or return it as it is.
> Attribution: the verse cited for the complete reply.

Reading of the just reply: whoever said something good received its like, whoever said something bad received its like.

Contrast pair: the two forces the etiquette holds together.

Column A: Order
- Carried by: the chain of who initiates, rider to rider, walker to standing
- Recognizes: rank and circumstance
- Prevents: chaos
- Risk if alone: coldness

Column B: Generosity
- Carried by: the reply that is better than what was received
- Recognizes: the opening for excellence
- Prevents: order becoming mechanical
- Risk if alone: naivety before deception

Shared row: justice answers deception with its like, virtue answers goodness with more than its like. The believer must know which response belongs where.

Comparison matrix: the prayers for the borders of religion, arranged by the day on which each is cited.

| Occasion | Who prays | What is asked for |
|---|---|---|
| Sunday prayer | Mawlānā Amīr al-Muʾminīn | After the higher borders, that God reach the lower five: the spokesman, the foundation, the culmination, the successor, and the wing |
| Saturday prayer | Mawlānā Amīr al-Muʾminīn | The purest, most honorable, and most noble prayers upon the seven days and the twelve months |
| Cited generally | The saints of the religion | The proclaimers and their gates, the imams of their stations, their proofs, and their preachers |
| Monday supplication | Cited without a named speaker | The seven proclaimers, the seven and eighteen, the seven caliphs, and the twelve successors |
| Tuesday supplication | Cited without a named speaker | God's manifest borders and righteous guardians, the spokesmen who call to His light, the proven foundations of the truth of His religion, the culmination, the successor, and the wing, through whom the gates of mercy were opened |
| Other prayers | Cited without a named speaker | Holy prayers upon them, their gates, their proofs, and their prayers, and blessing upon days, months, and the hours of day and night, both manifest and hidden |

Reading: cosmology becomes liturgy. The believer does not merely know a list of offices, the believer prays through them, and the hierarchy of guidance is carried on the tongue as blessing, gratitude, and orientation.

Process flow: the Four Hundred Counsels, from ordinary greeting to active courage before an enemy.

Start: someone greets you
  down to
Return the greeting with something better than it, or return it in kind
  down to
Extension: shake hands with your enemy, even if he dislikes it
  down to
Ground given: God has commanded His servants to repel with what is better
  down to
End state sought: until the enemy becomes like a close friend

Parallel case, one brother to another:
Opening: May God greet you with peace
Reply: You are the peace of God, and the finest of abodes and stations

Comparison matrix: the two closing questions of practice, and the rulings given.

| Question raised | Concern behind it | Ruling given |
|---|---|---|
| A question from India, on bowing or lowering the head before sheikhs, professors, and those close to them, instead of proclaiming peace with the hands | Some worried that this resembled the practice of unbelievers | Offering peace is better |
| How much of a greeting a Muslim must hear, whether replying is obligatory, and what is required when someone sneezes and praises God | Whether every courtesy becomes a duty | None of these is required by duty and one may do as one wishes, except for replying to a greeting, whether aloud or silently |

Reading of the modesty here: after the seven heavenly forms of peace, the twelve human divisions, and the prayers for the borders, the practical ruling could have grown heavy with anxiety. The source separates better practice from legal duty instead, honoring hierarchy without turning reverence into confusion, and honoring courtesy without making every social detail obligatory.

Genealogy chain: the closing citations, returned to the transmitted teaching of the Imams.

Jaʿfar ibn Manṣūr al-Yaman → the verse that the earth is inherited by God's righteous servants → meaning the Imams among His servants
Jaʿfar ibn Manṣūr al-Yaman → the verse that God wishes to be gracious to those oppressed in the land and to make them Imams and heirs → read as the descendants of ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, who were made weak in the land in his very person → he is the proof of Muhammad, and the earth is as he is
al-Muʿizz li-Dīn Allāh → a Sunday supplication → connected with the completion of the prayer of Raphael

## What this episode lands

Annotated structure: the definition of faith the chapter arrives at, dissected into its four requirements.

Whole: faith as a complete formed life.
Parts and callouts:
- Right attachment. Callout: walaya recognized first, before the acts are counted.
- Right knowledge. Callout: knowing where guidance comes from, not merely what to perform.
- Right practice. Callout: action that proves the claim rather than resembling it.
- Right recognition of the guides. Callout: the line through which the meanings of religion are preserved.

Hierarchy: the architecture the book will build on this foundation.

- Level 1: Walaya
  - Level 2: The six remaining duties
    - Level 3: Purity
    - Level 3: Prayer
    - Level 3: Zakāt
    - Level 3: Fasting
    - Level 3: Ḥajj
    - Level 3: Jihad
  - Level 2: The twelve social obligations
    - Level 3: Family, parents, kin, spouse, dependents
    - Level 3: Neighborhood, the neighbor, spreading peace, feeding others
    - Level 3: Community, enjoining good, forbidding evil, building brotherhood, visiting the sick

Process flow: the chapter's own path, start to finish.

Start: faith as an inward claim
  down to
Tested through parents, kin, neighbor, and the needy
  down to
Anchored in walaya and the limits of the testimony of God
  down to
Demonstrated through the household's knowledge, before Rūm, before Syria, before a caliph
  down to
Brought down into salām, prayers upon the Prophet and his family, and the borders of religion
  down to
End: a ruling is not merely an instruction, it is part of a guided life

Contrast pair: how the chapter ends, and how it could have ended.

Column A: Closure, the ending the chapter declines
- Shape: one subject exhausted
- Effect on the listener: a sense of completion
- Effect on the book: the later pillars would read as repetition

Column B: Foundation, the ending the chapter takes
- Shape: stones laid, building unfinished
- Effect on the listener: preparation to enter what follows
- Effect on the book: later rulings on prayer, fasting, purity, zakāt, and pilgrimage arrive already carried by recognition and by human responsibility

Shared row: the chapter is laying stones, not finishing the whole building. The episode lands with a sense of foundation rather than closure.
