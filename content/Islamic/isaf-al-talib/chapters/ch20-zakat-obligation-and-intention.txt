## Where this episode picks up

What would it mean to be told that your prayer does not exist? Not that it was late, or hurried, or performed without attention — that it is not there at all, that the years of standing and bowing have produced nothing a person could point to. This chapter quotes the sentence that says so twice, once near its opening and once as its final word, and the sentence is not about prayer. There is no prayer for one who does not pay zakat. Everything between those two occurrences is an account of how an unpaid charity reaches backwards through a life and empties an act of worship that was, by every visible measure, correctly performed.

The Book of Zakat opens here, and it opens before any of the arithmetic. Nothing in this chapter tells a reader how much he owes on a herd or a harvest; that reckoning comes next. What this chapter settles is prior to the sum: who is barred from receiving, what the obligation actually encompasses, where the payment has to be delivered, what counts as withholding when the money has visibly changed hands, and what a man is if he believes everything and spends nothing. Two source headings carry it — the intentions in giving zakat and related matters, then the hardening and the making of zakat — and the second is built almost entirely out of submitted questions and named replies.

The material arrives with attribution at every turn. Our master Ja'far ibn Mansur al-Yaman — may Allah sanctify him and grant us his intercession — supplies the opening, in a book the manuscript names as the book of sub-nursing; the title survives unevenly here and this chapter reproduces what the source gives rather than repairing it. Our master Zayd ibn Muhammad, may Allah be pleased with him, supplies a list of ten from a work the source calls the hidden message. Mawlana the Commander of the Faithful Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, supplies an aphorism from his four hundred manners. The longest single passage comes from our master and Mawlana, the owner of the councils of al-Mustansiriyya — may God's peace be upon its owner — in the twelfth council, delivered as a formal address to an assembly that has just finished hearing something else. And the closing section draws on the book of questions and answers already cited throughout this series, arranged according to the order of the chapters of the book The Pillars of Islam.

The Book of Funerals is behind us. It ended by turning the living back toward the cemetery and telling them the dead were glad of the company — an ending that left the reader outdoors, among people he could no longer help. This chapter puts him back inside a household with a ledger in it, and the first thing it does is tell him that one family in the community is forbidden to take anything from that ledger at all.

The chapter moves in three passes. The first assembles the obligation itself: the exclusion of the Prophet's household from charity, the nesting relation between the fifth and the tithe, and the scope of what zakat falls upon. The second is the twelfth council, rendered whole — an address that reads zakat as a structure of sevens and then fills each seven in, from what the payment is taken out of to the eight names it is disbursed among. The third turns to failure: what concealment means when the money was handed over openly, who exactly is forbidden to receive, what happens to a payment delivered to the wrong party, and the condition of a man whose belief is sound and whose hands are closed.

One conviction runs under all three and is easiest to see at the end, so it is worth naming now. In this chapter zakat is not a quantity to be surrendered. It is a delivery to be completed, and a delivery has an address. That is why a partial payment counts as concealment rather than shortfall, why a man who paid the wrong recipient may go and take the money back by any means available to him, and why the council states as a bare obligation that the payment must be brought to the Imam. Money that leaves a man's hands has not thereby been paid. It has only left his hands.

## Khums Above Tithe

The section opens by removing a family from the system.

Our master Ja'far ibn Mansur al-Yaman cites the saying of the Messenger of Allah, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him and his family: "No charity is permissible for me, nor for my family."

Read what that costs. Zakat is the institution by which a community moves wealth toward need, and the household at the summit of its sanctity is the one household categorically barred from drawing on it. The prohibition is not a rule about poverty; it makes no reference to whether the family has anything. It is a rule about a channel, and it closes the channel absolutely. Whatever else the people of the House are owed, they are not owed this, and a giver looking for the most honourable place to put his charity is told at the outset that the most honourable recipients he can think of may not have it.

Which is why the next sentence is about provision rather than prohibition. As for one who is able to provide for those of my household, the call after this is what he must do for them outwardly, in that he has abundant wealth and pays from it what he will beyond instinct, from among obligatory rights and voluntary acts.

The clause is compressed and the manuscript does not make it easier, but its shape holds. The household is not being left unsupported; it is being moved to a different account. What a man of means owes them is described as something done outwardly, out of abundance, and drawn from two sources at once — obligatory rights and voluntary acts. And the phrase that does the most work is the one that sounds least legal: what he will beyond instinct. Giving to this family is not the reflex of a man who sees need and responds to it. The reflex is precisely what has been disqualified, because the reflex routes through charity and charity is shut. What remains has to be deliberate.

Then the arithmetic that governs the two obligations, and it is stated as a containment.

Whoever fulfils the khums is spared from paying the tithe, except for voluntary giving; but whoever pays the tithe alone is not spared from paying the fifth that he must fulfil, because the tithe does not encompass the khums as the khums encompasses the tithe.

Two payments, and the relation between them is not parallel but nested. A fifth is larger than a tenth, and the reasoning takes that literally: the larger contains the smaller, so discharging the larger has already discharged what the smaller would have covered. The smaller cannot work in reverse. A man who pays a tenth has paid a tenth; he has not paid a fifth, and the remaining tenth is not a shortfall he may make up in goodwill — it is an obligation still standing.

Notice the one thing the fifth does not swallow. Except for voluntary giving. What is optional stays optional and stays available: a man who has discharged the fifth has closed his obligations and has not exhausted his generosity. The exemption reaches only the required tenth, never the offered one, which means the arithmetic cannot be used as a reason to stop giving. It can only be used as a reason to stop owing.

And then the sentence the whole chapter is built to explain. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him and his family, said: "There is no prayer for one who does not pay zakat."

It is worth registering how strange this is as a legal statement. It does not say that such a man's prayer is diminished, or unrewarded, or accepted with difficulty. It says there is no prayer — that the category is empty for him. Prayer is the act this series has spent thirteen chapters regulating down to the placement of a garment and the direction of a glance, and here a failure that happens nowhere near the prayer mat is said to have removed the whole of it. Whatever the relation between the two pillars is, it is not the relation of two separate duties which a man may discharge one at a time and score independently. One of them can void the other in retrospect.

The scope of the obligation follows. Our master Zayd ibn Muhammad, may Allah be pleased with him, said in the hidden message: Allah Almighty has made zakat obligatory on ten things — first, gold; second, silver; third, camels; fourth, cows; fifth, sheep; sixth, dates; seventh, raisins; eighth, wheat; ninth, barley; tenth, grains.

Ten items, and they fall into three kinds without being sorted into them. Two are metals, which is to say stored value that does nothing on its own. Three are livestock, which is to say wealth that eats, breeds and can die. Five are crops, which is to say wealth that appears once a year and then has to be kept. Nothing on the list is a house, a tool, a garment or a coin in circulation for its own sake; everything on it either holds value or produces it. Keep the list in view, because the twelfth council will shortly give a different one, of seven, and the difference between the two enumerations is not a disagreement about how much is owed.

The section closes on a sentence of two clauses from Mawlana the Commander of the Faithful Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, in his four hundred manners: "Cure your people through charity, and fortify your wealth with zakat."

Two instructions, two different targets, and the pairing is the point. Charity is aimed outward at people and described as medicine — it treats a community that is ill. Zakat is aimed at the giver's own property and described as fortification — it builds a wall around what he keeps. So the second half of the sentence makes an unexpected offer. The payment that removes a portion of a man's wealth is named as the thing that secures the remainder, which means the money handed over is not a loss offset by reward later; it is the defence of everything not handed over. A man who withholds it has not protected his wealth. He has left it standing in the open.

## The Twelfth Council's Reckoning

The longest passage in the chapter is an address, delivered to an assembly in the middle of a sequence, and it is given here whole.

*You have heard what was recited to you above in jurisprudence concerning guardianship, purity, and prayer — what constitutes the sincerity of testimony, which is the foundation of religion and worship and the first cause of mercy and happiness. Now hear what we recite to you: the mention of zakat, which is one of its rights as established above. Its sincerity lies in knowing its noble boundaries, fulfilling its rights, and performing the seven obligatory duties that are the pillars of Islam as the above system has shown. In zakat likewise there are seven obligatory duties and twelve years, like the number of chapters of testimony and its letters. Zakat must be brought to the Imam and taken from seven things: first, gold; second, silver; third, camels; fourth, cows; fifth, sheep; sixth, crops; seventh, minerals. Disbursements fall under eight names divided into seven categories: first, the poor and the needy — zakat is given to them; second, those who work in its collection — they are given the wages of collection; third, those whose hearts are to be reconciled — they are paid from it for protection; fourth, for the freeing of those in bondage; fifth, those burdened with debts incurred in settling the affairs of the community; sixth, in the way of Allah, meaning jihad, which is a collective obligation; seventh, the wayfarer who is cut off and stranded. As for its two sunnahs: first, it should not be taken until after the completion of the year; second, the nisab of gold is twenty dinars; third, the nisab of silver is two hundred dirhams; fourth, the nisab of camels is five head; fifth, the nisab of cows is thirty head; sixth, the nisab of sheep is forty head; eighth, zakat is taken from cattle of middling quality — neither the finest nor the lowest; ninth, a joint must not be added to it, nor must the community be divided; tenth, the stud animal must not be taken from them; eleventh, charity is given by the owner of crops that have not reached the nisab — which is five awsaq, measured with the squeeze and the fist — at the time of his harvest, for Allah says:* ⟪ar:وَآتُوا حَقَّهُ يَوْمَ حَصَادِهِ⟫, *"and give its due on the day of its harvest" (chapter 6, verse 141). There is no zakat on houses, jewelry, and whatever is for use; and each of these words carries many meanings and effects of wisdom.*

Take the opening first, because it tells the assembly where they are. What has already been recited to them is named in order — guardianship, purity, and prayer — and that is the order this series has travelled, from the foundations of allegiance through the whole law of purification to the shape of the prayer itself. Those doctrines were taught in their place and are not re-opened here; the address invokes them as completed business. What it adds is a hierarchy. All of it, including the zakat now being introduced, is described as a right belonging to testimony, which is called the foundation of religion and worship and the first cause of mercy and happiness. So zakat is not the fourth item on a list of five. It is one of the things a single testimony demands, and the pillars are being described as the contents of an oath rather than as a set of separate obligations that happen to number five.

Then sincerity, and the word is doing legal work. The sincerity of zakat lies in knowing its noble boundaries, fulfilling its rights, and performing the seven obligatory duties. Knowledge comes first in that sequence, before payment. A man who hands over the correct sum without knowing what its boundaries are has not been credited with sincerity in the sense the council means, which is why a chapter on intention spends most of its length enumerating thresholds and categories. The enumeration is the intention. There is nothing here about feeling generous.

The structural claim comes next and is the address's signature move: in zakat there are seven obligatory duties and twelve years, like the number of chapters of testimony and its letters. The same council collection made the same manoeuvre for purity earlier in this series, matching its obligations and sunnahs to the chapters of the witnessing and its letters, and the method is consistent — a pillar is shown to be the numerical image of the testimony that generates it. The phrase twelve years is what the manuscript gives, and it is left as it stands; the correspondence being asserted is a count rather than a span of time, and the council does not explain the term further.

Zakat must be brought to the Imam. Seven words, no argument attached, and every ruling in the chapter's last section depends on them. Zakat here is not a duty discharged by a reduction in a man's holdings. It is a duty discharged by a delivery to a named recipient, and the recipient is not the poor man at the end of the process but the authority at the start of it.

Then the seven things it is taken from — gold, silver, camels, cows, sheep, crops, minerals — and this is where the two lists have to be set beside each other. The hidden message named ten; the council names seven. But the ten included dates, raisins, wheat, barley and grains, all five of which are crops, and the council has one word where the other list had five. The council then adds one item the other did not carry: minerals, wealth taken out of the ground rather than grown on it. So seven is not an abbreviation of ten. It is the same field sorted by kind rather than by commodity, with an extra kind admitted. The two enumerations disagree about taxonomy and agree about scope, except at the mine.

The disbursement list is stated with its own arithmetic showing. Eight names divided into seven categories — which is a way of saying that one category holds two names, and the list shows which: the poor and the needy are a single heading with two words in it. What follows that heading is a description of where zakat money legitimately goes, and half of it does not look like charity at all. Those who work in its collection are paid the wages of collection, so the administration of the fund is financed by the fund; nobody is asked to gather it for nothing. Those whose hearts are to be reconciled are paid from it for protection — a payment made to secure a disposition rather than to relieve a hardship. The freeing of those in bondage buys a person out of a condition. And the fifth heading is precise in a way that repays attention: those burdened with debts incurred in settling the affairs of the community. Not debtors in general. Men who went into debt doing something on everyone's behalf, which makes the payment a reimbursement rather than a gift. Sixth is in the way of Allah, glossed immediately as jihad and classified as a collective obligation — the same category the funeral prayer was placed in earlier, a duty owed by everybody and discharged by somebody. Seventh is the wayfarer who is cut off and stranded, and the qualification is the whole ruling: what makes him eligible is not that he is poor but that he is stuck. A man of means a long way from his means is a legitimate recipient.

Then the sunnahs, and the list has two irregularities the chapter reproduces rather than repairs. It is introduced as two sunnahs and eleven items follow. The numbering runs one to six, skips seven, and resumes at eight. Both features are in the source and both are left as they stand, because straightening them would require deciding what the missing item was.

What the list contains is a set of floors and a set of protections. The floors: no zakat is taken until the year is complete; twenty dinars of gold; two hundred dirhams of silver; five camels; thirty cows; forty sheep. Below each of those numbers the obligation does not arise, which means the system is built so that small holdings are outside it entirely rather than taxed lightly.

The protections are more interesting because they all restrain the collector rather than the payer. Cattle are taken of middling quality, neither the finest nor the lowest — so the collector may not select the best animal, and the owner may not offload the worst. A joint must not be added to the herd, nor must the community be divided: two manipulations of a headcount, one inflating a holding and one splitting it, both closed off, which tells us both were tried. And the stud animal is not to be taken at all. That is an exemption reasoned from what the animal does rather than what it is worth — remove the breeding male from a herd and the assessment collects a year's value while destroying the herd's capacity to produce any. The rule keeps the assessment from consuming the thing being assessed.

The eleventh item reverses the direction of every rule before it. Charity is given by the owner of crops that have not reached the nisab — measured as five awsaq, taken with the squeeze and the fist — at the time of his harvest. Everywhere else the threshold is a wall: below it, nothing is owed. Here, below it, something is still given, and it is given on a different clock. Not after a year has been completed but on the day of the harvest, which is the verse's own word: ⟪ar:وَآتُوا حَقَّهُ يَوْمَ حَصَادِهِ⟫, "and give its due on the day of its harvest" (chapter 6, verse 141). A farmer whose crop never reaches the taxable minimum still has a due, and he owes it on the day the crop comes in, before it has become a stored quantity that could be weighed against a threshold at all.

And the exclusion that closes the list draws the boundary of the whole institution. There is no zakat on houses, jewelry, and whatever is for use. The line does not run between necessity and luxury — jewelry is on the exempt side. It runs between wealth that grows and wealth that is held. Livestock breed, crops come in, metals accumulate, mines yield; a house shelters and a bracelet is worn, and neither produces anything that could be shared out. Zakat falls on increase.

The council ends by declining to finish: each of these words carries many meanings and effects of wisdom. That is the collection's habitual closing gesture, and it means what it says. The enumeration just delivered is the outward form, correct as far as it goes and not the whole of what is there — and the inward reading is withheld from an audience that has been given the boundaries and told that knowing them is what sincerity consists of.

## Concealment and Misdirection

The last section is entirely correspondence, taken from the book of questions and answers arranged according to the order of the chapters of the book The Pillars of Islam, and it begins with a questioner who has caught a contradiction.

What do you say, he asks, concerning what is established in the Shari'ah, namely that it is forbidden for a person to conceal his zakat from his imam? Our master, what is meant — is it that he withholds zakat or something else? And how does he conceal zakat from his imam if he is supposed to hand it to him openly?

The objection is exact. Concealment is a property of things done out of sight, and this payment is by definition done in the open, delivered in person to a named authority. So either the prohibition is empty or it describes something other than hiding.

Answer: this hadith refers to one who pays some of the zakat while withholding the rest — he has abandoned an obligatory duty.

The concealment is not of the money but of the remainder, and the thing that hides it is the part that was handed over. A man who pays nothing has withheld visibly; a man who pays most of it has produced a receipt, and the receipt is the concealment. What the answer names as abandoned is the obligatory duty in whole, not the fraction unpaid — which is consistent with everything this chapter has said about delivery. A partial delivery is not most of a delivery. It is a failure dressed in evidence.

The next question narrows a prohibition. What of the words recorded in the Book of Pillars, that the Messenger of Allah, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him and his family, said: "We are the people of the House — no charity is permissible for us"? Our master, does this refer specifically to the Imams, or to all his descendants through Fatima, peace be upon her?

Answer: what is meant by this is the Imams, peace be upon them.

The reply restricts the exclusion to a line rather than extending it to a lineage. Descent from Fatima is not, by itself, what closes the channel; the bar falls on those who hold the office. Read against the first section, this also says something about who the prohibition protects. The one household forbidden to draw on the fund is the household to which the fund is brought, and the ranks this series established earlier are doing the work here without being re-argued: the position that receives on the community's behalf is the position that may not receive on its own.

The third question is the hardest to read, and the manuscript is against it. The questioner asks about what is stated in the book The Pillars of Islam, that charity does not resolve the five for one of the men who bore the beauty — and asks what is meant by "the sling" here. Answer: it is either a punishment from an unjust authority or blood money for a competent woman.

The question's own wording has not survived intact and this chapter renders it as it stands rather than reconstructing it. What can be taken from the exchange is taken from the answer, which offers two readings and does not choose between them. Both name a liability imposed from outside a man's own dealings — a penalty levied by an unjust power, or blood money owed on account of a woman — and both are the kind of charge a man might reasonably hope to meet out of a fund established for the burdened. The answer's two readings are given here as the source gives them, and nothing further is supplied.

The fourth question is a full case with a political geography attached. It concerns the covenant written by the Commander of the Faithful for Hanaf ibn Salim — one of the people of obedience to us, between the island and Kufa and the land of Damascus — who claimed that his charity was being directed to the agents of Damascus while he was in our possession, and that it is forbidden for our horses and men to intercept it. Is it permissible for him to do this, the questioner asks, given that if the truth is as he claims, he has no right to come down to our country and give the zakat of his wealth to our enemy? What is the ruling, and is it obligatory for the Sultan to forbid the land of India with his forces and men? And does he owe zakat or not?

The answer reads the covenant against the questioner's reading of it. The covenant written by the Commander of the Believers, peace be upon him, for al-Muhanaf ibn Sulaym — the manuscript gives the name in two forms and both are kept here — and his saying that unless his horses and men are not in his possession he should not be asked, means that zakat is not required from those who are not Shi'ites who have been seized by the enemy.

So the document the questioner read as a licence being abused turns out to be a relief. It is not describing a man who may divert his zakat to the wrong authority; it is describing people from whom the payment is not demanded at all, because they are held by a power that stands between them and the party they would deliver to. The questioner arrived wanting an enforcement — should the Sultan send men? — and was answered with an exemption. Where delivery is impossible because someone else holds the ground, the obligation does not stand as a debt against the person who cannot cross it.

Which sets up the ruling that states the delivery doctrine at its strongest. The successive transmissions from the Ahl al-Bayt, peace be upon them, recorded in the book of Pillars and elsewhere, state that one who handed over zakat to someone whom Allah did not command him to hand it to may reclaim it by any means, until he hands it over to the guardian or to whoever receives it by his command.

Money paid to the wrong recipient has not been paid. It is still the payer's, and he may go and get it back — by any means, a phrase that concedes he may have to take it rather than ask for it — and the recovery is not permitted for his benefit but so that he can complete the delivery he failed to make. Every other obligation in this chapter could be satisfied by a man who parted with the correct amount. This one cannot. A sum that has left his hands and reached the wrong party is, in the eyes of the ruling, still sitting in his hands.

And the chapter closes with the councils of al-Mustansiriyya returning to say what such a man is.

Likewise, the one who performs the outward prayer while holding inward conviction, yet does not spend of what Allah has provided for him as he has been obligated — giving zakat on his wealth and one-fifth of his gains — has bought himself, yet failed to pay off the debt he owed. He is one of those whom Allah described in the verse: ⟪ar:وَمِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ يُنفِقُونَ⟫, "and who spend out of what We have provided for them." The source does not name the chapter or verse and none is supplied here.

The image is worth slowing down for, because it is a legal one. A slave who purchases his own freedom by contract has bought himself; the transaction is real, and so is the debt it creates. The man being described has done exactly that. He is not an unbeliever with a convincing act — the council grants him the outward prayer and the inward conviction both, which is more than most censures concede. He has made the purchase. What he has not done is pay for it. And his condition is therefore not disbelief but default: he owns something he has not finished buying, and the instalments he is behind on are named precisely, the zakat on his wealth and the fifth on his gains. The verse then fixes the diagnosis by quoting the description he fails to match — those who spend out of what they have been provided — which locates the failure at the disbursement rather than at the belief.

Then the two sentences the chapter ends on, the first of them returning for the second time. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him and his family, said: "There is no prayer for one who does not pay zakat." And he said: "The one who withholds zakat is in the Fire."

## What this episode lands

This chapter has opened the Book of Zakat by settling what the obligation is before anyone calculates what it costs.

On the shape of the duty, three things. The Prophet's household is barred from charity absolutely — the exclusion falls on the Imams, and what is owed to them travels by another road, deliberately and out of abundance. The fifth contains the tenth and the tenth does not contain the fifth, so a man who has paid the larger has discharged the smaller and a man who has paid the smaller still owes the difference, while voluntary giving stays outside the calculation entirely. And zakat falls on wealth that increases: metals, herds, crops, and what is taken from a mine — never on a house, a piece of jewelry, or anything a person simply uses.

On the structure, the twelfth council supplied a reckoning in sevens. Zakat must be brought to the Imam. It is taken from seven kinds, which is the same field the earlier list of ten described, sorted differently and extended to minerals. It is disbursed under eight names in seven categories, and those categories pay the collectors out of the collection, secure the disposition of the reconciled, buy people out of bondage, reimburse men who went into debt on the community's business, fund a collective obligation, and rescue a traveller whose problem is that he is stranded rather than that he is poor. Thresholds keep small holdings outside the system; a set of rules keeps the collector from taking the best animal, from padding or splitting a herd, and from touching the stud that makes the herd possible. And below every threshold there is still a due on the day of the harvest. The council delivered all of it as boundaries to be known — sincerity, in its account, is knowledge of the limits before it is anything else — and then said the words carry meanings it was not unfolding there.

On failure, the rulings converge on a single doctrine. Concealment is what a partial payment does: the portion handed over is what hides the portion withheld, and the duty abandoned is the whole of it. A man prevented from reaching the authority by an enemy holding the ground does not owe a debt for the crossing he could not make. A man who delivered to the wrong party has not paid at all and may recover the money by any means in order to deliver it properly. And a man with sound belief and closed hands has bought himself on credit and stopped paying — which is why the chapter can end, without exaggeration, on a sentence that says his prayer does not exist, and then on one that says where he is.

Two passages survive unevenly in the manuscript and are rendered here as they stand rather than smoothed: the title of the book of sub-nursing, and the question about charity and the sling, whose answer offers two readings and settles on neither. The name in the covenant reaches us in two forms, Hanaf ibn Salim and al-Muhanaf ibn Sulaym, and both are kept. And the council's list of sunnahs is introduced as two, runs to eleven items, and skips the seventh; the count is left as the source leaves it.

What has not yet been touched is the arithmetic. Nothing here has said how many sheep are owed on forty, what a mine yields, what a household pays at the end of the fast, or what happens to a man's obligation when he is himself in debt. The thresholds have been named and the categories fixed; the next chapter does the counting.
