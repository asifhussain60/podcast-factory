## Where this episode opens

What happens when purity is no longer a simple question of washing before prayer, but a contested choice between a living body, a dead body, a garment, a floor, a vessel, and the last handful of water? This chapter picks up from the previous teaching on wudhu' without repeating it. As established earlier, bodily purity trains the soul toward knowledge; here the book asks how that training holds when the problem becomes major ritual impurity, illness, death, public surfaces, garments, and uncertain materials.

The source keeps its question-and-answer form. It does not present one abstract theory and then leave the listener to imagine cases. It asks whether a person in janabah who fears cold should wash or perform tayammum. It asks whether ghusl must be repeated after fainting, whether semen seen on a garment is enough to require washing, whether a menstruating woman or a person in janabah may recite words that might be Quran, whether the dead or the living takes precedence when water is scarce, and how far impurity can travel through wet contact. Then it turns outward: vomit, urine on land, dried traces, carpets that cannot be wrung out, milk, hair, animal products, clothing made or worn by polytheists, partly impure floor-coverings, stones, plaster, sweeping, vessels licked by dogs, unknown impurity in one of two vessels, grooming practices, siwak, and tayammum.

That range is the point. Purity here is not only an inward image and not only a private act. It is a way of judging the material world around worship: bodies, cloth, earth, mats, roads, shared water, household tools, animals, and the dead. The chapter repeatedly asks how the law protects prayer without turning every unknown contact into panic. It distinguishes visible trace from suspicion, necessity from negligence, drying from washing, usable earth from unusable stone, a Sunnah purification from an obligatory one, and the living claimant to water from the deceased who also needs washing.

The episode therefore has three movements. First, it teaches ghusl from janabah as a major purification whose urgency remains even under cold, sickness, doubt, sleep, menstruation, and death. Second, it teaches material purity as a discipline of traces: what must be washed, what counts as removed, what remains suspect, and when a surface or garment may be used for prayer. Third, it gathers practices of cleanliness, siwak, and tayammum to show how the worshipper moves through a world where water may be absent, dust may be hidden, and intention decides whether a lesson in purification actually purifies the one who teaches it.

## Major washing

The chapter begins with ghusl from janabah, and the first case immediately introduces hardship. A question asks about the rule found in books of Shari'ah: if a person fears cold while in a state requiring ghusl, he performs tayammum and prays, then when able to use water he washes and repeats the prayer. Does this apply to the resident and the traveller alike? The answer is direct: if he fears that water will harm him, whether resident or traveller, near or far, he performs tayammum and prays.

The ruling carries forward the previous chapter's teaching on tayammum, but now the pressure is greater. Minor impurity can already force a person to ask whether water is available or safe. Janabah makes the question more urgent because the whole body is implicated. The source does not say that the fear of harm abolishes the obligation of purity. It says that the truthful form of purification under harm is tayammum. Prayer is not abandoned. The person does not pretend to be able to wash. He uses the purification appointed for the condition before him.

A second question asks about the meaning of ghusl from janabah when intercourse has occurred, and about similar cases in which ghusl is required. The answer moves briefly from law into meaning: ghusl is like the removal of doubt and uncertainty, which only occurs when clarification and benefit are given. This is not a full return to the earlier doctrine of inner water; the source has already established that knowledge purifies doubt. Here the new point is that major washing carries that same pattern in a more forceful form. Janabah is not treated as a small residue. It is a condition whose removal is compared to the removal of uncertainty by clarifying benefit.

Then the source turns to doubt about one's own state. If a person faints for several days, or if it becomes known that perhaps he was in a state of janabah, what threshold requires ghusl when no trace of semen is visible? The answer says that if he does not know his own state, ghusl is still obligatory if he has doubt about it. The law is not content with convenient ignorance. Where the person's own bodily state is seriously in question after unconsciousness or possible janabah, doubt does not excuse him from ghusl. The listener should notice how different this is from cases where the impurity of an external vessel or garment is merely unknown. The body that bears janabah requires a more searching certainty.

The same pressure appears in the case of a weak man afflicted by janabah who fears the cold when the prayer time has come. May he perform tayammum as a sick person would? The answer: if he fears water, he performs tayammum and prays, and he need not repeat the prayer. That last phrase matters. The earlier question included a version in which he might repeat after later washing. Here the answer removes that repetition. The prayer performed through valid tayammum under fear of harm is not presented as a placeholder prayer waiting to become real. It is prayer under the lawful substitute.

The next case is simpler and stricter. A man wakes from sleep and sees the trace of semen on his garment, wet or dry, but does not remember a dream discharge. Must he perform ghusl? He must. The source does not require memory when the material trace is present. Dream recollection is not the proof; the trace is enough. The body and garment testify where the mind does not remember.

The chapter then asks about cutting hair or nails while in janabah, or during menstruation, where the soul remains in that state. May necessity allow it? The answer tells the person to leave it until he becomes pure. The ruling sounds severe because it extends the state of impurity to bodily parts that might be removed. Hair and nails are not treated as neutral scraps once they are separated in that condition. The body should be brought back into purity before trimming what belongs to it.

Another question asks about recitation. May a person in janabah or a menstruating woman recite supplications or books containing words the reader may or may not recognize as Quranic? May such a person recite takbir, praise, glorification, and the basmalah, given that these expressions appear in the Quran and the basmalah has been said to be a verse? The answer draws the line at intentional Quranic recitation: neither the menstruating woman nor the person in janabah may deliberately recite any part of the Quran, whether in prayer or in books, and neither may recite the basmalah with the intention of reciting the Quran.

The distinction is important. The source is not banning every word that also appears in scripture from every human tongue. It is asking about intention and recognition. Words of praise may overlap with Quranic wording, but deliberate Quranic recitation while in that state is not permitted. Even the basmalah is treated according to the intention with which it is recited. Purity, in this case, governs not only the body but the way sacred speech is approached.

The chapter also records a practical ruling: if a man forgets that he is in a state requiring ablution until he has performed ghusl, the ghusl suffices and he need not repeat the ablution. He completes his ghusl as ghusl, and ablution for prayer has been fulfilled. The greater washing includes what is needed for the lesser state. This is one of the chapter's careful reliefs. It does not make the person perform a second ritual simply because he remembered too late that a lesser purification was also needed.

Then comes one of the chapter's central scarcity cases. Some preachers of Yemen answered scholars from the Indian lands that if a person in janabah and a deceased person are together, and there is only enough water for one of them, the person in janabah should perform ghusl and tayammum should be performed for the deceased. The owner of the hidden message supports the seriousness of janabah by citing the command, "If you are in a state of janabah, then purify yourselves" (chapter 5, verse 6). The Prophet, peace be upon him and his family, says that beneath every hair is janabah, meaning that janabah reaches the roots of the hair, so one must purify oneself. Whoever neglects this is negligent and far from what he claims.

The force of this passage is not only legal priority. It is identity. To obey the state of janabah, to live under it willingly, is described as falling into ruin and falsehood. The source's language is strong because it treats purity as part of a person's truthful religious lineage. One who claims belonging but neglects purification stands far from the claim. The body either confirms or betrays what the tongue says.

A saying from Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, adds a nocturnal and eschatological dimension. A Muslim should not sleep while in janabah and should not sleep except purified. If he finds no water, he performs tayammum, for the soul of the believer ascends to Allah Almighty, who receives it and blesses it. If He has prepared a place for it, He places it in the finest form; if its appointed time has not yet arrived, He sends it back to the body with His guardian angels.

This saying makes sleep a threshold. The person who sleeps does not merely pause ordinary consciousness. His soul is described as ascending to God, being received, blessed, placed if its appointed time has come, or returned if life remains. That is why sleeping in janabah is not a casual matter. Purity prepares the soul for a journey it undergoes every night. If water is absent, tayammum still marks the person as one who has not surrendered to impurity.

Later in the chapter, the scarcity problem returns in a different form, and the source preserves more than one phrasing. If a man dies while traveling and there is no water, tayammum is performed over him. If the obligations of washing the deceased and performing ghusl coincide and there is only enough water for one complete wash, one passage gives the water to the bath for the living, while another says the water should be used to wash the deceased. The chapter does not stop to harmonize these reports for the listener. It lets the legal memory of the tradition stand with its tensions. What matters for this episode is that scarcity of water does not erase purification; it forces ordered judgment about who can receive water, who receives tayammum, and how prayer or burial proceeds without pretending abundance.

The same practical intelligence appears in Ramadan cases. If a man needs ghusl on a night of Ramadan and forgets until he leaves on a journey, then cannot find water, his prayer and fasting are complete through tayammum. If a woman becomes pure from menstrual blood during Ramadan before Maghrib and fears harm from ghusl, she may perform tayammum, keep her fast, and need not make up that day. The same applies to a man who has a nocturnal emission during Ramadan: if ghusl at night endangers his health, he performs tayammum and fasts without making up the fast. Again, the law does not confuse hardship with lawlessness. It gives a purified way through hardship.

The first movement of the chapter therefore lands on ghusl as serious, whole-body purification. It is required by trace even when memory is absent, by doubt when the body itself is under serious uncertainty, and by janabah even when cold or sickness forces tayammum in place of water. It governs sleep, recitation, hair and nails, prayer, fasting, and death. It also teaches a deeper pattern: major impurity cannot be wished away, but the law provides a truthful substitute when water would harm, when travel prevents washing, or when water cannot be found.

## Material purity

The chapter's second movement widens from major washing to the purity of bodies, garments, earth, carpets, and similar things. The first case is deliberately plain: vomit renders impure whatever it falls upon, as blood, pus, and other impurities do, and the first action is to wash the affected area. The source begins with visible contact. Something has fallen. It has affected a place. The response is washing.

Then it asks about land on which children have urinated and defecated, which has dried until smell and physical trace have disappeared. May one pray on it without further washing? The answer says that if the physical trace of impurity has been removed, the land is treated as pure, provided it is not a passage used for need. A related question asks about land struck by impurity, then washed by flowing water and dried until smell and trace vanish; it also asks why dry places where camels and sheep are penned may be used for prayer. The answer says that if sunlight and wind have purified it, and the physical trace and smell have been removed, prayer on it is permissible and valid.

This is the chapter's basic grammar of material purity: trace, smell, drying, sun, wind, water, and use. The land is not frozen forever by the memory of impurity. If the trace and smell have gone through the means named by the source, the land can be treated as pure. Yet the caveat about a passage used for need prevents careless generalization. Not every place that looks dry is a fit place to pray.

The question about touching a dead body after it has cooled sharpens the relation between impurity and moisture. What does "after it has cooled" mean? The answer says it means after the body has become impure; when it infects what is adjacent to it through moisture, that adjacent thing becomes impure, and this is the meaning of the obligation to wash. Washing after touching the deceased is Sunnah, except when the body has already been washed by bathing. The source is careful: impurity spreads through wet adjacency. Contact is not imagined as magic; moisture is the path by which impurity moves.

Large absorbent objects then pose a practical problem. What about something one wants to purify but cannot wring out three times: the scalp, mats, rugs, mosque quilts, and mattresses? The answer gives a procedure. One runs the hand over the object until impurity and smell are gone, lifts it from the water until it stops dripping, then repeats this three times. One may tread on it with the feet as described, remove it from the water, press it until water comes out, and return it three times. The method preserves the logic of washing and repetition when ordinary wringing is impossible.

This case matters because the source refuses a lazy answer. It does not say that rugs and mattresses are impossible to purify. It does not pretend they can be treated like a small cloth wrung in the hands. It adapts the act: moving water, removing smell, dripping, treading, pressing, returning. Material form determines method, but not the need for purification.

The following cases draw limits around what counts as impurity. The milk of a Muslim breastfeeding woman that falls on clothing is pure, for her and for others, by analogy with her saliva; the saliva of every Muslim is pure and does not make impure what it touches. Hair from a wet shaved head falling onto a garment or body is permitted in the easier position. A small amount from the milk, fat, and eggs of jallalah animals is also permitted on the analogy of the first issue. These rulings prevent the chapter from collapsing into generalized disgust. Bodily substances and animal products are not all treated alike. Some contacts are pure, some excused in small quantity, some require washing.

The source then considers sickness and travel. A traveler or sick person who cannot clean impure clothes is told that prayer should not be performed except with a pure garment; the sick person should instruct those around him to wash his clothes. The answer is strict because clothing directly accompanies prayer. Yet the very instruction to ask others also shows that inability has a social remedy. Purity is not only the isolated person's private task. Others may assist the one who cannot wash.

The long set of questions about garments made by polytheists is one of the chapter's most delicate material discussions. The Quranic phrase cited by the source says, "The polytheists are impure" (chapter 9, verse 28). Yet the answer does not infer that every garment made by a polytheist is automatically unusable. All garments are pure until they are worn while impure or until some impurity appears on them. The Imams permitted prayer in garments made by polytheists unless the makers have worn them or impurity is visibly present. A garment dyed by a polytheist is pure even if he wore it, but if he wore it while impure, it becomes impure.

The reasoning here is exacting. The source holds together a scriptural category of impurity and a practical rule about material evidence. Manufacture alone does not make a garment impure. Wearing may matter, but even wearing is specified by whether it occurred while impure. Visible impurity matters. The garment itself is judged by what has actually touched it and appeared on it, not by a vague fear detached from material signs.

A follow-up asks about garments cut by polytheists but not yet worn: can they be purified, and how? The answer says that one cannot purify such clothes produced before wearing in the way the question imagines; as for what they cut and washed, it is not permissible to consider them pure except by washing them. Those who heard the statement did not grasp the root of the question. Another case asks about garments made by polytheists, not worn, but splashed with impure water during weaving and stitching, after which the water dried. Once sold and bought, is prayer in them permissible? The answer permits it.

The listener should hear the source's concern with concrete transmission. Was the garment worn? Was it worn while impure? Was impurity visible? Was impure water splashed and then dried? Has it been washed? The chapter is not training suspicion without end. It is training material judgment: do not call something pure by wishful thinking when impurity is present, but do not invent impurity where the case does not establish it.

The wide floor-covering partly pure and partly impure creates another hard boundary. May one pray on the purified portion? The answer says no. The common people's saying about whether one part moves without the other end moving does not change the ruling. Unlike open land purified by sun and wind, this single floor-covering remains one object whose impurity affects its use for prayer. The source will not let the worshipper select a clean-looking patch when the larger connected object remains compromised.

The chapter then includes several shorter material rulings. Prayer in the clothes of boys prone to impurity is permissible unless impurity is known, and a small amount of discharge is excusable. One should not stop praying in innocent houses, though doing so is not recommended. It is permissible to pray on stones or plaster only within the principle stated by the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him and his family: "The earth has been made for me a mosque and a means of purification." Anything from the ground over which tayammum is not permissible cannot be prayed upon as ground. Sweeping houses at night, despite the practice of some people in India who avoid it, is not a reliable prohibition; there is nothing wrong with doing it.

The vessel licked by a dog receives the familiar seven washings, one with dirt. The pig is treated the same. If one of two vessels contains impurity and the person does not know which, he should not approach either and should pray. This last ruling again shows the law's structure under uncertainty. Where the object of impurity cannot be identified and both vessels are implicated by doubt, the worshipper does not gamble with one of them. He leaves both and turns to the valid path of prayer.

Sayings from the Four Hundred Precepts bring grooming and public cleanliness into the same field. Trimming the mustache is clean and Sunnah. Washing the head with sidr removes illness, belongs to the Sunnah, and removes filth. Light is described as a tonic and purification for the body. Trimming nails wards off severe disease, brings sustenance, and brings increase. Plucking the armpits removes offensive odor and is a Sunnah purification. Washing on feast days purifies the one seeking his needs before God and following the Sunnah. Washing clothes removes worry, suppresses the enemy, and purifies for prayer.

These sayings treat cleanliness as religiously consequential without reducing it to one narrow ritual. Hair, nails, head, armpits, clothes, scent, disease, and feast-day washing all belong to the moral texture of a purified life. The source even records the warning not to shorten the mustache, because it is a light for the Muslim, and the statement that a young man in appearance but old in Islam will have a light on the Day of Resurrection. Bodily signs, age in faith, and light are allowed to touch one another.

Public space receives a stern warning. Let no one relieve himself on the road or urinate into still water. If something then befalls him, he has only himself to blame, because the road is shared and the water is shared. Do not urinate in a way that splashes back, and do not let urine return upon oneself. Whoever is seen sleeping on his face should be warned. One should purify oneself with water from foul-smelling wind and keep to this, because God hates the filthy servant who repels those who sit with him. Dogs should be avoided. If a dry dog touches one's garment, sprinkle water on it; if the dog was wet, wash it.

The social dimension is unmistakable. Purity protects prayer, but it also protects shared roads, shared water, companions, garments, and the comfort of people who sit nearby. Filth is not merely a private embarrassment. It can harm others, repel others, and misuse what is shared.

The chapter closes this material movement with grooming details from the Indian question-and-answer material. A man who lets his hair grow and does not part it every day is asked about whether that deficiency affects prayer. The answer says that if the hair is in two halves, he should not leave it unparted; if he deliberately does so, the ruling invalidates his prayer. The severity may surprise a modern listener, but inside the chapter's logic it belongs to ordered bodily presentation before worship. The body prepared for prayer is not careless in form.

Then comes a brief inner saying already anticipated by the previous episode: "Whoever does not purify the sea, God will not purify him." The answer explains that just as water purifies and is not itself purified, so too is the knowledge of the Imam; whoever does not purify it, God does not purify him. This is only a callback, not a re-teaching. The new setting is material purity: after garments, land, dogs, vessels, hair, and shared water, the source reminds the listener that the appointed purifier is not purified by something below it. Water teaches this outwardly; knowledge teaches it inwardly.

## Tayammum paths

The final movement gathers siwak and tayammum, two practices that show purity's adaptability without dissolving its form. The mention of siwak begins with a custom in India. After finishing the siwak, some people take a piece of oud, bamboo, or another material and use it with their tongues to remove dirt. Is this permissible? The answer says that siwak is better, but there is nothing wrong with doing that.

The ruling is modest, yet it shows the chapter's balance. The preferred practice remains preferred. A secondary tool may still be permitted. The source is not anxious to forbid every local practice that serves cleanliness, but neither does it flatten the Sunnah into any convenient substitute. It says: this is better, and that is not blameworthy.

The section then records a few compressed notes. Some say one should not disturb the breach that has a knot of hashish. Others say it is disliked to seek it at Uhud while in janabah. A book of explaining reports defines basil as every pleasant-scented plant, while myrtle receives special attention. The interpretation of the pillars says that basil, in the tongue, means the tips of every plant made pleasant when wind passes over it, and that scholars especially cared for myrtle, that one should keep it and hold it close. Then Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, says that the siwak is pleasing to God Almighty, good for the mouth, and from the Sunnah.

The scented plant material may seem to wander, but it belongs to the chapter's concern with bodily nearness: mouth, scent, breath, and the practices that make a worshipper fit to stand before God and among people. Siwak is not merely dental hygiene. It is pleasing to God, good for the mouth, and Sunnah. The mouth that recites, remembers, greets, eats, and breathes is given a practice of purification.

Tayammum then receives a long series of cases. The first question asks whether tayammum is permissible on a flat stone with no dust on it. The answer says that if there is dust on it, tayammum is valid; if there is no dust, it is not valid. This is the material precision of tayammum. Not every hard natural surface counts in the same way. The dust matters.

A man traveling from a city to a place within the travel limit asks whether he may perform tayammum when prayer time arrives and he finds no water. The answer says he may not do so unless he is far from water while traveling and cannot perform ablution before missing the prayer time, or unless he fears for himself or his property if he goes to the water. Mere inconvenience is not enough. Absence, distance, time, fear for life, and fear for property define the permission.

If a man needs ghusl on the night of Ramadan and forgets until he travels, then does not find water, his prayer and fasting are complete. If a man dies while traveling and there is no water, tayammum is performed over him. If a traveler is sick and water is present but he fears harm, he completes his prayer. If a traveler has only a small amount of water sufficient for washing some limbs, the source invokes the principle that the purest available thing serves when there is a cause, and the absence of water is such a cause and the meaning of tayammum.

Several cases teach how limited water should be assigned. If a group of travellers has an imam and only enough water for one man's ablution, the imam performs tayammum before them and leads them in prayer; another summary says all perform tayammum and he leads them. If a man has only enough water for ablution but impurity is on his garment, he washes the garment with that water, then performs tayammum and prays. Here the garment's impurity takes the water, while the body receives tayammum. The law chooses the use of water that removes the impurity for which tayammum is not a substitute, and then uses dry purification for the person.

The source also distinguishes noon, afternoon, sunset, and nightfall cases. If a resident faces noon prayer with water sufficient only for ablution but not ghusl, he performs tayammum and prays. If the afternoon prayer arrives and that water is still with him, he uses it for ablution and does not repeat tayammum, because he is already purified from janabah. If water is found after tayammum and the prayer time remains, he returns to water only if he has not yet washed; if he prayed while traveling and later finds water, he may prefer to perform ablution and repeat the prayer while the time remains, and if the time has passed he is rewarded and may pray again.

The teaching is subtle. Tayammum is not a permanent identity. It answers a condition: absence of water, harm from water, danger in reaching water, or a limited supply that must serve another impurity. When the condition changes and the time remains, water can return. Yet the prayer already performed under valid tayammum is not treated as worthless.

The chapter includes social and interreligious cases as well. If a non-believer embraces Islam and cannot find water, he prays as he is. If a man travels with a Christian or Zoroastrian woman and she menstruates on the way, then becomes pure and finds no water, the husband should teach her complete tayammum and she acts accordingly. The source's categories are its own, but the legal point is clear: entry into prayer is not postponed indefinitely by lack of water. Instruction in tayammum becomes part of enabling the person to fulfill worship under constraint.

The Sacred Mosque and the Haram also appear. It is recommended that a man who sleeps in the Prophet's mosque while in major impurity, and wakes still in that state, should not pass through the Sacred Mosque until he has exited it; the same applies within the Haram. Sacred space intensifies the concern. The body in janabah is not to move casually through the holiest precincts as though no condition has attached to it.

The source then gives a memorable poetic rule from the Muntakhab: all who cannot find soil should shake their garments and clothes. Even if nothing visible clings to them, whatever dust may be present suffices. This is not a romantic image of improvisation; it is a legal answer. Dust may be hidden in the very clothing one wears. When soil cannot be found, the garment may become the place from which the necessary dust is brought forth.

Another case from Ta'wil al-Da'im asks about a man pressed by a crowd who cannot reach water when prayer time arrives. He performs tayammum and prays, then repeats the prayer if he is able afterward to find water and purify with it. The crowd is a form of practical inaccessibility. Water may exist nearby, but the person cannot reach it. The condition is judged by actual access, not by abstract existence.

The final story makes intention decisive. Two men prayed in one place because they could not find water. The prayer of one was valid, while the prayer of the other was not. How? One man said to his companion, "Teach me tayammum." The other taught him and struck the ground on his behalf. Both prayed. The prayer of the one who learned was valid, but the teacher's prayer was not, because he did not form the intention for himself.

This is the perfect closing case for the chapter. The teacher knows the act well enough to instruct another person. He even performs the outward motion that helps the learner. But he does not intend it for himself, so his own prayer fails. Knowledge of the procedure is not the same as purified intention. Helping another person perform tayammum is not automatically performing tayammum oneself. The act must belong to the worshipper as an act of worship.

The whole chapter has been moving toward that precision. Ghusl requires more than hoping one's state is clean. Garments require more than assuming no impurity matters. Land requires the removal of trace and smell. Rugs require adapted washing. Vessels require caution when impurity cannot be identified. Siwak remains better even when another cleaning tool is permitted. Tayammum requires dust, cause, access, and intention. Across all these cases, purity is neither panic nor laxity. It is disciplined judgment before God.

## What this episode lands

This episode lands on major and material purity as the law's training in truthful conditions. Janabah, menstruation, fainting, sleep, semen traces, recitation, scarcity of water, and death all show that ghusl is a serious whole-body purification, while tayammum becomes the lawful path when water is absent or harmful. The chapter also teaches that material purity depends on trace, smell, moisture, washing, drying, actual contact, and the status of the object being used for prayer.

Most importantly, the chapter refuses both carelessness and anxious excess. It does not let a person knowingly pray in impurity, invent purity for a compromised garment, or perform tayammum without cause and intention. But it also does not make every unknown contact, dried surface, local cleaning custom, sick body, crowded road, or scarce-water journey impossible. The purified worshipper is the one who learns to judge the actual condition honestly, use the means appointed for it, and enter prayer without pretending.
