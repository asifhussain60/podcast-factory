## Where this episode picks up

What is a community supposed to do when the sun goes dark on a day the astronomers have shown it cannot? In the month of Dhu al-Qa'dah of the year eight hundred and thirty-seven, an eclipse began on a Monday, two hours after sunrise, and the sun was darkened at midday. The believers already knew what the letters of the Brethren of Purity said in their treatise on astronomy: a solar eclipse occurs only at the end of the month. They also knew, from having lived through it more than once, that the sky had done this before in the first days of Ramadan while they were fasting. So the confusion was written down and sent as a question, and the answer that came back neither defends the astronomy nor discards it.

That exchange sets the temperature of the whole chapter. Everything gathered here is a prayer occasioned by something the ordinary calendar does not schedule. A body that cannot stand. A sun that goes out. An enemy too close to afford the usual length. A sky that will not rain. And, at the other end of the chapter, the prayers nobody is compelled to offer at all — Witr and its supplication, the night vigil, the prayer of praise, the prayer of sufficiency, the prostration a reader owes when a verse of prostration passes under his eye.

The chapter before this one followed the worshipper out of his settled life and kept deciding he was less displaced than he felt. This chapter reverses the direction of travel. The worshipper stays where he is and the world becomes strange around him, and the question is what the prayer becomes when the conditions it assumed are gone.

The material arrives the way most of this book's material arrives: as correspondence. The first book of questions and answers, arranged according to the order of the chapters of the Pillars of Islam, supplies most of the questions, and behind it stand the Book of Purity, Mukhtasar al-Musannaf, Mukhtasar al-Athar, al-Iqtisar, al-Akhbar, al-Muntakhabah, and the landmarks of the religion. Named men write and named men answer. Our master Muhammad ibn Fahd replies in his honorable message, From Him: With Love for the Path of Guidance for the Converted. Sheikh Hasan ibn al-Mawla Adam al-Hindi puts his difficulties to the masters of Yemen. And Salih ibn Ahmad writes from the island of Yemen to the island of Hind in the year 1135 to ask when, exactly, a person in disaster is permitted to pray.

The chapter moves in three passes. The first collects the prayers that answer an event: the sick, the eclipse, fear, and rain. The second settles into the seam at the end of every night — Witr, the qunut that belongs to it, the shafa'ah that precedes it, and the moment dawn closes the door on all of them. The third opens the wide country of voluntary devotion, where the questions stop being about what is owed and start being about what is permitted.

One instinct runs under all three. The prayer is never cancelled; it is reduced, postponed, transferred, or made up. A man who cannot move his limbs still prays with his heart. A community that cannot complete a prayer in public completes it later in private. The chapter is a long argument that the obligation outlasts whatever interrupts it.

## Sickness, Eclipse, Fear, And Drought

The chapter opens on the smallest possible congregation: one person, alone, unable to stand.

The question is put in the first book of questions and answers, arranged according to the order of the chapters of the Pillars of Islam. What do you say about a sick person who is unable to pray standing, or to stand while bowing and prostrating? The answer sets the principle before the practice: he should pray standing, or standing with bowing and prostrating, according to whatever God has excused the servant from in his condition. The Book of Purity then supplies the steps down. If he is unable to do so, he nods with his eyesight. And if he cannot do that either, he nods with his heart.

Read that ladder from the bottom. The last rung is not an exemption but still a prayer, performed inside a body that can no longer signal anything to anyone. The sequence never reaches a condition in which the prayer stops, only one in which it becomes invisible.

The same section then separates two kinds of debt. It is said in the abridged book of Athar that if a sick person is unable to pray beyond the obligatory prayer, he may make up — if he wishes — what he missed of the Sunnah and voluntary prayers once he recovers; that is a virtue and a reward, but it is not obligatory to make them up as it is obligatory to make up the obligatory prayers. Illness shrinks the worshipper down to his obligations and leaves the rest as an open invitation rather than an unpaid bill.

And a harder case. It is said in the abridged classified book that whoever takes something to cure himself and thereby loses his mind must make up whatever prayers he missed while in that state — whether his action was within the character permitted by God or was disobedient. The moral quality of how the man got there changes nothing about what he owes. The prayers were missed; the prayers are made up.

Then the sun goes out.

The eclipse question is the most vivid thing in this chapter because it is dated, located, and genuinely unsettling to the people who asked it — and the darkening of eight hundred and thirty-seven was not the first time the sky had contradicted the treatise. The answer is narrated from Mawlana the author of the letters, may God's peace be upon him, and has been explained by the pure Imams and those who follow their guidance. They said that a lunar eclipse occurs on the night of the fifteenth, and on the morning of the tenth, while a solar eclipse may occur at the beginning of the month or at the end of the month. The matter is in the hands of God, and there is no power except in God, the Most High, the Great.

That is a correction to the astronomy, not a dismissal of it. The framework stands; the rule inside it was stated too narrowly. What the answer will not accept is the premise buried in the question — that if the sky disagrees with a book, someone's faith is in trouble.

Having established that the event is real, the chapter turns to the prayer that answers it, from an unexpected angle: the person who arrives late. The book has already taught how a latecomer joins a congregation under way, and does not re-teach it here. What it supplies is the arithmetic peculiar to this prayer, whose shape is unlike the others.

Whoever joins the imam at the third rak'ah follows him through his first prayer, bows with the imam when he bows, and prostrates when he prostrates. When the imam rises from prostration, he stands with him and prays the five rak'ahs following him, prostrating with him. When the imam makes the final testimony, the latecomer sits and waits. When the imam says the salutation, the latecomer stands and completes the remaining rak'ahs, reciting the qunut, bowing, prostrating, making the final testimony, and saying the salutation — and he does not count the first two prostrations that he performed with the imam, just as one who catches up with the imam during the prostration of the final obligatory prayers does not count them.

A second case is given for the man who joins at the beginning of the imam's prayer but before the first two prostrations. He prays what remains with the imam, and when the imam prostrates in those first two prostrations he prostrates with him, since it is mentioned that one who joins at the beginning but is unable follows along. He then tracks the imam through the five rak'ahs, prostrating when he prostrates and sitting when he sits; and when the imam says the salutation, the latecomer prays what remains of the rak'ahs, prostrates, makes the final testimony, and says the salutation. Again the first two prostrations performed alongside the imam are not counted.

The detail worth holding is the uncounted prostration. The latecomer performs the act fully and it does not go into his total: he may not sit idle while the congregation prostrates, and he may not bank what he did. The congregation's motion binds him even where it earns him nothing.

Then comes the restriction that gives the whole eclipse section its edge. It is said in the Book of Purity: do not pray the eclipse prayer until the eclipse is actually seen. It is not prayed on the word of an astrologer or anyone else. And if the eclipse is obscured by clouds or something else so that it cannot be seen with the naked eye, the prayer is not performed.

Set that beside the earlier answer and the position becomes clear and rather bold. The tradition will happily discuss the astronomers' month, correct their generalization, and place the whole business in the hands of God. What it will not do is let a calculation start a prayer. A predicted eclipse behind cloud is, for this prayer's purposes, not an eclipse at all.

The prayer of fear compresses rather than waits. The question quotes the Book of Purity: when the situation involves danger, conflict, and combat, they perform their prayers with takbir, enlarging the place of each takbir — and is the takbir shortened as the prayer of fear itself is shortened? They shorten the takbir just as shortening is obligatory in it. Nothing inside the prayer is exempt from the emergency that shrank it.

Two further rulings come from Mukhtasar al-Musannaf. If the imam prays the prayer of fear with three people as his guard, and their number falls below three, it is not recommended for them to pray the prayer of fear, so that the group does not fall to fewer than three. The guard is not an escort outside the act; it is one of the act's conditions. And if several extraordinary prayers converge — the eclipse prayer, the funeral prayer, and the prayer of fear — the imam prays the funeral first, then the eclipse, then fear. The dead go first. The sky and the enemy wait.

The last of the four is the prayer for rain, and it is the only one in this group whose shape is liturgical rather than defensive. It is said in the books al-Iqtisar, al-Akhbar, and al-Muntakhabah that he faced the people and said one hundred takbirs, then he turned to his right and said one hundred praises, then he turned to his left and said one hundred expressions of joy — raising his voice throughout all of this. He then faced the people and praised God with one hundred praises and glorified Him.

The Book of Purity adds that he also delivered two sermons, as is done for the two feasts — the book has already set out the gathered order of those days — then descended and departed. He dismisses them, and he performs the prayer in a bow, in Arabic if he finds the Arabic, as the Messenger of God, peace and blessings be upon him, did. If they go out on a day other than Monday, that is also acceptable. And from the abridgement of al-Musannaf: whoever delivers the sermon before the prayer should pray and then deliver the khutbah.

Four hundred utterances in four directions, in a raised voice, over a congregation that has come out because the rain has not. Three of this section's four emergencies ask the worshipper to do less. Drought asks him to do vastly more. What the community lacks it cannot shorten its way out of, so it stands in the open and makes the largest sound this book has yet asked of it.

## Witr, Qunut, And The Dawn

The chapter now leaves the emergencies for a seam that arrives every single night, and spends more words on it than on the eclipse, the sick, the enemy, and the drought together. The reason becomes clear quickly. Witr sits in a category the tradition finds genuinely difficult to name. It is stated in the landmarks of the religion that Witr prayer is obligatory and it is not permitted to abandon it, even though it is a Sunnah, and it is among the appendages of the obligatory prayers. That sentence contradicts itself in a single line, and it means to. Witr is not an obligation and cannot be dropped; it hangs off the obligatory prayers rather than standing beside them as a peer. Almost every question in this section follows from that unstable position.

Start with the supplication inside it. What do you say about a man who forgets the qunut? The answer: in Witr he must repeat it, and in Fajr he performs the prostration of omission — the remedy the book has already taught for a gap in an obligatory prayer.

Then the same question is asked again at greater length, and the answer moves. What of a man who prays Witr and forgets the qunut, then remembers after he has bowed, prostrated, or sat? The answer: the qunut is a supplication that must not be deliberately abandoned. Whoever forgets it is not required to repeat the prayer, and there is no prostration of omission in the Sunnah prayers. Whoever deliberately omits it in Witr repeats it. Elsewhere the compilation carries a third report, that whoever prays Witr and forgets the qunut offers the two prostrations of forgetfulness after the salutation.

The compilation does not reconcile these; it reports them. But the axis the fuller answer turns on is not the size of the gap. It is the will behind it. Forgetting the qunut leaves the prayer standing; deliberately dropping it destroys the prayer and requires it again — two opposite verdicts on the same missing words, which is a strikingly interior test for a body of law that spends most of its pages on limbs, garments, and directions.

Having made the qunut that serious, the chapter makes it small. The minimum is three tasbihs or three takbirs, recited after the worshipper says, "Our Lord, to You belongs all praise" — and whoever achieves that has fulfilled it. A thing that must never be deliberately abandoned had better be something nobody has an excuse to abandon.

The shafa'ah prayer, the pair of rak'ahs preceding Witr, raises the question of what breaks a sequence. Whoever prays it and then engages in something until dawn arrives should repeat the shafa'ah as a make-up prayer. Even if he prayed three rak'ahs and remained sitting until dawn breaks, he does not repeat the three prayers but remains sitting with the intention of making up the prayers. Sitting still is continuation; getting up to do something else is departure. The man who stayed in his place has not left the prayer though he has stopped praying.

Which invites the sharpest question in the section. Is it permissible for a worshipper to pray from the first rak'ah of Witr, speaking or connecting the ihram to the ihram? Two maxims are set against each other in the question itself: it has been said that the prayer of one who is seated is half the prayer of one who is standing, and it has also been said that the prayer is begun by the takbir and concluded by the salutation — so which does he act upon?

The answer reaches for verse. Our master the Judge al-Nu'man — the highest of the world, may God sanctify him — mentioned in al-Mukhtar:

The thread of prayer may be broken by the salutation
Without any actions or words.
If one must speak, there is no harm in that, unless the speech is prolonged.

The book has already settled what speech and motion do to a prayer in progress. What is new is the image. A thread is not broken by being touched but by being cut, and the salutation is the cut. Only length severs it otherwise — and the ruling declines to measure the length, leaving it where the qunut's verdict was left, with the worshipper's own judgment.

Then the dawn itself, the hard boundary this section has been circling. Is it permissible to perform the shafa'ah prayer and Witr during the first dawn — what the Arabs of the desert call al-kadhib — before the second Fajr has set in, and to pray the two rak'ahs of the Sunnah of Fajr? The answer: the shafa'ah prayer and Witr are performed unless the true dawn has already set in, which would preclude them. The false light is not a boundary. Only the true one closes the door, and it closes it absolutely.

Two smaller cases press on the same seam. Whoever prays with the imam at Fajr and bows before the imam, then raises his head and bows again with the imam in bowing — his prayer is valid, for his prayer is like that of one who prays alone. If the imam prays alone, he repeats the prayer. And: if a person had prayed the prayer of intercession and then entered the mosque and prayed the greeting of the mosque during the time of intercession, must he repeat the intercession? He must repeat it, because he does not have the right to pray after it until he has prayed Fajr. When dawn comes he prays the greeting, then he prays Fajr.

That second ruling reveals the architecture. The book has already taught that entering a mosque carries its own two rak'ahs; here that courtesy is suspended, because the shafa'ah has sealed the night and anything after the seal reopens what was closed. The greeting is not wrong, it is out of order — and being out of order costs the worshipper the prayer he had already completed.

The form of Witr is then given in full from the Book of Purity. One should recite in the first rak'ah the opening of the book and a surah — "Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of the daybreak" — and in the second the opening of the book and a surah — "Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of mankind" — then testify and give salutation. These two rak'ahs are part of Witr. One sits in them as mentioned, spreads the palms upon the thighs with the fingertips resting on the knees, and reads. When the reading is finished, one bows and places the palms of the hands on the knees. Likewise when standing; and when raising the head from bowing, one places the right leg behind, lifts the feet, prostrates, then sits in testimony as one sits in prayer.

The book has already given the prayer's shape, and this is not a second lesson in posture but a closing choice. The two surahs of refuge are the last words a believer says over his own night, and the prayer that must never be deliberately abandoned ends by handing the sleeper over to the Lord of the daybreak.

The section's last exchanges concern a misjudged seam. Our master Muhammad ibn Fahd, in his honorable message, transmitted from him out of love for the path of guidance for those who seek it, was asked in his twenty-second question about a person who prays the prayer of intercession and Witr, sits as long as he is able, then prays Fajr as an obligatory prayer — and it later becomes clear that his dawn had come before it. The answer: his prayer of intercession and his Witr stand confirmed, and he is required to repeat. He should repeat it as a Sunnah and make it obligatory — except that if dawn is near him, the Sunnah prayer is also confirmed for him, because it is mentioned in the books of the saints of God, peace be upon them, that it is permissible to pray it before Fajr on account of the noble meaning that permits this.

The man was wrong about the sky and right in his intention, and the tradition lets him keep what he did while still asking him to do it again. Beside that the chapter sets a saying of the Commander of the Believers, Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, from his four hundred teachings: "Place your trust in God when you bow down at Fajr after you have finished it, for in it desires are granted."

The last word on making up what was missed comes from our master Sharaf al-Din Ja'far ibn Sayyidina Sulayman — may God bless them in the chambers of Paradise — in his book Elegant Psychological Gardens, which gathers letters of evidence drawn from the answers of our master Dawud ibn Ajab to his son, the religious Sulayman ibn Hasan. As for one who must make up Witr, is it permissible for him to pray voluntarily? It is permissible, and the first priority is to offer the Witr as a make-up for what was missed. Arrears before extras — the order the sick man's ruling set at the start of this chapter.

## Voluntary Devotion And Its Limits

The last movement walks out into open ground, where nothing is owed, and finds the questions get harder rather than easier. They begin with recitation. What do you say regarding the opening prayer, the prayer of praise, and the prayer of Tahajjud — has anything been transmitted specifying fixed recitations, and what surahs should be read? The answer: nothing fixed has been transmitted for them; they are like Sunnah and nafl prayers in which one reads whatever one wishes. And where a recitation is specified but the worshipper does not know it — as in Witr and the Eid prayer — he should recite as much as he is able and should not omit the prayer. The prayer outranks its own contents; a man who cannot supply the words is never told to stay out.

Tahajjud gets the same latitude and then a shape. One who has not memorized its supplication intends to pray Tahajjud and prays with the supplication if he has memorized it, or he prays as much as he is able and intends Tahajjud, which is twelve rak'ahs; and it has two times, one of them between Maghrib and Isha'. Intention carries what memory cannot.

Then a question about survival rather than devotion. What do you say regarding Tarawih prayer, if the believers pray with the imam for taqiyyah purposes with the intention of voluntary prayer, and the imam recites the Quran from beginning to end, which is not the practice in the obligatory prayers, nor in Witr, nor in the Sunnah of Fajr, nor in the greetings? The answer is short: there is nothing wrong with taqiyyah, but they do not complete it.

That describes a posture rather than a rule. The believers stand in a row and pray a prayer that is not theirs, with an intention that is theirs, and do not carry it to its end. Precaution is granted without embarrassment and without a lecture, and the line it draws is drawn at completion. The companion ruling closes the loop: when the same questioner raises the reckoning of rak'ahs from the book of evidence and statement — ten for Dhuhr, eight for Asr, five for Maghrib, ten for Isha' — the answer is that the prevailing view is based on the pillars, which is the gathering of rulings, and that even if a person omits something from the Sunnah for taqiyyah, he makes it up. Concealment suspends the performance; it does not cancel the debt. That is the sick man's ruling again in different clothes.

The prayer of praise then gets a question of pure mechanics. When one rises from the second prostration to perform ten glorifications of "My Master" — sitting upon the feet, or standing? One sits in the position of alertness after raising the head from the second prostration and glorifies ten times, then rises for the second rak'ah; after the second rak'ah one sits in that position again, glorifies ten times, then sits in testimony and gives salutation, following the same procedure in the last two rak'ahs. The proper practice, the answer adds, is to sit upright after raising the head from the second prostration in each rak'ah, glorify ten times, then rise — or to testify after the glorification while remaining seated in the glorification posture.

The prayer of al-Kifayah is an arithmetic of its own. In the first rak'ah one recites the opening of the book, al-Ikhlas, al-Kafirun, and the verse of the Throne once each; in the second, twice each; in the third, three times each; and in the fourth, four times each. Does al-Fatihah climb with the rest? One recites al-Fatihah once in each rak'ah, and God knows best. The opening of the book stands outside the escalation — the prayer's spine, not one of the items being multiplied.

Beside these precise forms the chapter sets a deliberately loose permission, from the summary of effects: "Whoever has not memorized the Quran, let him recite what is available to him." The Quranic sentence quoted alongside it says the same: "So recite what is available of the Quran." And whoever wishes may pass the whole night with one surah, repeating it, or with part of it, or with two or more surahs. A night spent on one repeated surah is not a poor night.

One clarification follows, from the brief speech in the jurisprudence — whose questions come from Sheikh Hasan ibn al-Mawla Adam al-Hindi and whose responses come from the masters of Yemen, may God sanctify the souls of them all. Whoever prays between the obligation of the latter prayer and its negation whatever he wishes of nafl prayer does not intend it as a make-up, for it is called the night prayer. That prayer is its own thing with its own name, not an instalment against arrears.

Then the chapter's strictest moment, in a letter written to the compiler of this very book. Our master and Mawlana Ja'far ibn Sayyidina Sulayman wrote to him concerning the prayer of Umm Dawud. The occasion: Qasim ibn Feer, the caller of the band al-Akmahiyyah al-Dawudiyyah, commanded his companions to pray it, to fast, and to supplicate in the month of Rabi' al-Awwal of the year 1042, when the fifteenth fell on a Friday while they were in prison, and likewise in Ramadan of that year. Was that permissible, and was that its practice?

The answer holds the line. What Mawlana al-Sadiq, peace be upon him, wrote concerning the prayer of Umm Dawud is mentioned only for the month of Rajab, and he commanded that one purify oneself for it. Asked whether it might be called upon with supplication outside Rajab, he said: "On the day of Arafah, and if it falls on a Friday, his companion will not be emptied until he has been forgiven." There is no apparent symbol indicating otherwise and no stipulation beyond seeking forgiveness in the month named, for the opening supplication already mentioned is sufficient and persuasive; and after that, the day of Arafah that is not a Friday is likewise sufficient, meaning the permission applies to that day alone. Accordingly one should not offer it except in accordance with what is stipulated. If they have it, let them follow it; otherwise let them do a little, for as the Prophet, peace and blessings of God be upon him, said: a little done consistently in accordance with the Sunnah is better than a great deal of innovation.

Consider who is being answered. Men in prison, reaching for a named prayer and a named fast on a day that looked auspicious. Nothing in the reply doubts their sincerity. It refuses to let devotion generate its own calendar.

The chapter then distinguishes two voluntary acts that look symmetrical and are not. Asked whether the believers should fast the Sunnah fasts as they pray the Sunnah prayers, some of the loyal preachers of Yemen answered the sheikhs of India: it is permissible for them to break the Sunnah fast without making it up, but a Sunnah prayer that is missed must be made up, as the Imams — peace be upon them — and their people did. They have their reward from God if they fast. The abandoned fast leaves nothing behind; the missed prayer becomes a debt. Witr's strange position at the top of this chapter is the rule for prayer in miniature: what is offered freely does not stay free.

The last correspondence is the widest. Our master the gift of God ibn Sayyidina Ibrahim was asked by the most humble of his servants, Salih ibn Ahmad, who wrote from the island of Yemen to the island of Hind in the year 1135, about what is mentioned in the books: that the prophets, such as Jesus and others, and the guides of the household — Ali ibn Abi Talib, Mawlana Zayn al-Abidin, and others among the Imams, may God's prayers be upon them all — would, at the time of a great calamity, call for water, perform ablution, and pray two rak'ahs, and in some cases four, for the relief of distress. It is not clear at what time they prayed. Was it at any and all times of hardship, calamity, and disaster, or only at certain times, according to Sunnah and nafl prayer?

The answer distinguishes the prayers of the guides before it answers. The testimony of Mawlay Badr al-Din — the inspiration — makes clear that the prayers of the prophets and the Imams in matters of great events, calamities, and disasters that befall them are distinguished, and arise from their gratitude for what He has imparted to them of the secret of things and their knowledge that these things only occur to the extent that is possible. Their reports did not specify at what times they prayed; rather they mentioned it in a general statement. Then the ruling: yes, it is permissible to pray Sunnah and nafl prayers at all times when such prayers are permitted, and the prohibition applies only to the known prohibited times, which are not hidden to the sound mind. The book has already fixed those hours and does not fix them again.

And the answer closes by routing the whole matter back through the guidance the book has taught from its first chapter. The picture is illuminated for their slaves and Shi'a: if they wish to pray that mentioned prayer and thereby repel all calamities and satisfy all evils and defects, let them be guided by their guidance, follow their Sunnah, obey them, be supported by their intercession, be sustained by their secret and their blessings.

Set that beside the Umm Dawud letter and the position comes into focus. Time is open; form is not invented.

The chapter ends with the smallest voluntary act in the book, and the only one it recommends without hedging. What of one who recites a supplication containing a verse of prostration — is he required to prostrate? If it is a firm recitation, he must. And what of a teacher of boys teaching them the Quran, passing the places of prostration while instructing them — must he prostrate every time he and those who hear him pass one? The answer refuses to choose: I prefer that he prostrate, and I also prefer that he leave it.

That is not evasion. Either rule, taken strictly, makes a teacher's day unworkable — he would spend the lesson on the floor, or pass the verses without honouring one. The answer approves both paths and leaves the man to his classroom. Others say that what a man says in the prostration of the Quran is what is said during prostration. And it is said in Mukhtasar al-Musannaf that if the reciter recites a prostration from among the prayers, he prostrates in his position; and if he recites the prostration a second time, neither of them is required to prostrate again as long as he remains in his position. As for the remaining prostrations, whoever wishes to prostrate may do so, and whoever does not wish need not.

Then the chapter drops its hedging. Prostration is praiseworthy, and the better course is to prostrate. And Mawlana, the Commander of the Faithful, Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, said in his four hundred points of conduct: "Prolong the prostration, for nothing is harder on Satan than to see the son of Adam prostrating — Satan was commanded to prostrate and refused, while the son of Adam was commanded to prostrate and obeyed, and was saved."

## What this episode lands

This chapter is built out of interruptions, and it never once lets an interruption end a prayer. The sick man descends from standing to nodding with his eyes to nodding with his heart, and the sequence has no bottom rung marked "excused." The community under threat shortens even the takbir. The believers concealing their allegiance stand in an unfamiliar row, decline to complete what is being done there, and make up afterwards what precaution cost them. Something is always reduced, deferred, or transferred; nothing is dissolved.

The eclipse holds the chapter's most interesting judgment. The astronomers are corrected rather than dismissed: a solar eclipse may fall at either end of the month, and the matter rests with God. But the prayer waits for sight. Behind cloud there is no eclipse prayer, and no astrologer's word will start one. The science is accepted as description and refused as trigger, which is a finer distinction than either rejection or deference would have been.

Witr is where the chapter spends most of itself, because Witr is where the categories buckle. It is obligatory and not permitted to be abandoned, and it is a Sunnah, and it is an appendage of the obligatory prayers — all in one sentence. From that instability the rest follows: a forgotten qunut leaves the prayer standing while a deliberately dropped one destroys it, so the verdict turns on the will rather than the gap; the prayer is a thread cut only by the salutation, not frayed by speech or motion; the false dawn is no boundary and the true dawn is an absolute one; and the greeting of the mosque becomes a fault when it is offered after the night has been sealed.

Out in the voluntary country the chapter is generous about time and immovable about form. A person may pray at nearly any hour, read whatever he has memorized, spend a whole night repeating one surah, and pray for relief in any calamity. What he may not do is give a prayer an occasion it was not given — which is why men praying in prison on an auspicious Friday are told to do a little consistently rather than a great deal inventively. The asymmetry left standing at the end says it from the other side: a voluntary fast abandoned leaves no debt, while a voluntary prayer missed must be made up.

The closing image earns its place. After hundreds of rulings about what may be shortened, postponed, concealed, restricted, or made up, the chapter's last word is the one act it will not qualify at all: prolong the prostration. It is the cheapest thing in the book to perform and the only one recommended without a condition attached — and the reason given is not legal but old, reaching back past every question here to the first refusal, and to the creature who was asked the same thing and agreed.
