## Where this episode picks up

What would it take for a whole community to sit down to its first pre-dawn meal two days before anyone in the city had seen a moon — and to be right? Not early by an hour, not early because the sky was dirty and somebody guessed: two full days ahead of the crescent everyone else was waiting for, in a year we can name, with the neighbours watching. That happened. The year was four hundred. And the letter that occupies most of this chapter exists because a man wrote to a scholar and said, in effect: they are laughing at us in the streets, and I need you to explain what we did.

This chapter covers the hinge between two documents. It opens at the tail of the correspondence the previous chapter left unfinished — the last page of a reply arguing about how the month of Ramadan begins, ending the way a formal letter ends, with a verse of poetry, a demand put back to the other side, a request for a courteous answer and a blessing. Then a short attached message on what looks like a different subject entirely, the inner meaning of the eclipse prayer. Then the opening of a new epistle, addressed to a named man in a named year about a named embarrassment, which will run for several chapters and which begins, as this chapter closes, with something that sounds like cosmology and is in fact the first move of a legal argument: that God created everything in pairs.

Three voices carry it. The first is the unnamed writer of the previous reply, who restates what he takes the disputed report to mean, sets his opponents two questions, and signs off. The second is our master Zayd ibn Muhammad, whose message on the hidden interpretation of the eclipse prayer is attached here and who supplies an arithmetic of the moon's absence that the manuscript has damaged. The third — and from here on, the dominant one — is our master and Mawlana, the summoner of the age, Ahmad Hamid al-Din ibn Abdallah al-Kirmani, may his intercession avail us, who is the proof of our master al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah, the Commander of the Believers, may God bless him and grant him peace. It is he who answers.

The previous chapter spent itself defending a single clause about who the crescent reports are addressed to, using astronomy, the occasion on which the report was spoken, an argument about what the Prophet could not have failed to know, and a count drawn from the law of expiation. None of that is re-argued here; it is assumed. What is new is narrower and stranger: a reading of one preposition, a decreed timetable for the moon's covering, a dated public scandal, a diagnosis of why people disagree at all, and the opening of a proof from the structure of creation itself.

One thing more. Both letters here use the same word for what they want: the first writer asks his opponents for a sound and healing answer, and the second, taking up his pen, speaks of the healing answer and what it does. Whether that echo is deliberate or a shared vocabulary, it tells you what kind of document these people thought they were producing. Not a victory. A cure.

## The Moon's Own Timetable

The reply ending here has been arguing about the length of the month, and it ends on mortality. It quotes a line of verse: *As for the destruction of the house, the house is built — for death, mothers feed their children.* Another poet is quoted as saying: "They were born to death and were born to ruin." And this, the writer says, aligns with the word of Allah, Exalted be He: "This is what you were promised for the Day of Reckoning." (chapter 38, verse 53)

Then a compressed clause the transmission has left hard to follow: the reckoning made the consequence bear the blame, and this is in keeping with what we have mentioned — which is the most fitting interpretation. The sense appears to be that the verse assigns responsibility to the outcome rather than the moment, which is what the poetry has just done with birth and death: the house is built so that it can fall, the child fed so that it can die. The clause is reproduced as it stands; nothing that follows depends on repairing it.

Now the operative sentence, worth slowing down for, because everything here turns on a preposition.

The correct view, the writer says, is that the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him — and his family fasted at the sighting of it, meaning at its emergence; and that you fast and break your fast at the sighting of it, meaning so that it may be seen.

Read that twice. Nobody disputes the report's wording; what is offered is a claim about what the phrase attaches to. In the first half, describing what the Prophet and his family did, "at the sighting of it" is glossed as "at its emergence" — what they timed themselves by was the moon's coming out, the event in the sky, not anybody's perception of it. In the second half, the instruction to the community, the gloss changes direction: fast and break your fast at the sighting of it, meaning so that it may be seen. Not so that you may see it. The clause points at the moon's visibility as a state of affairs rather than at your eye as the instrument.

The distinction is small and load-bearing. On the ordinary reading, a month begins when somebody looks up and catches it, which hands the start of a universal obligation to whoever has the clearest horizon. On this reading, the moon's emergence is the event, seeing is the evidence, and evidence is not the same thing as the fact it is evidence of. The previous chapter reached that conclusion from the sky and from the Prophet's access to news of the heavens; here it is reached through grammar.

Then the writer sets the other side an examination with two questions on it.

First: if you hold otherwise, then bring proof from the Qur'an for the completion of the month as mentioned therein. The party addressed holds that the month is sometimes complete and sometimes short — completion is a variable for them, something the sky decides. Very well, the writer says: show me where the Book treats it that way.

Second, and sharper: explain why the Messenger of Allah — may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him — did not say, "Fast after seeing it and break the fast after seeing it," so that the matter would be clearer to his community.

This is an argument from what was available and not used. If the instruction had been "wait until you have caught sight of it, and then begin," there was an ordinary way to say so, one word longer and free of ambiguity, and the Prophet did not use it. He is not credited here with carelessness. So the phrasing he did use is taken to have been chosen: at the sighting, because he meant the moon's emergence; not after seeing, because he did not mean to make the eye the trigger.

Then a second compressed passage the transmission has damaged. It might be said, the writer allows, that one acts for the day on which one will be resurrected, and the Hereafter holds the reward for what one does, and the Book of the House aligns with the saying of the Prophet sent to us. The Book of the House cannot be securely identified from anything in this slice, and the clause tying it to the Prophet's saying arrives without its connection spelled out. What survives legibly is that a deed is named by its final day — the figure the poetry opened with, transposed from death to reward.

And then the letter signs off, and the sign-off is the only place you can hear its temperature.

So grant us a sound and healing answer, the writer says, for we do not travel the road of arrogance and dismissiveness.

That is a request and also a claim about method, worth registering because the letter that follows will make the opposite accusation about the other party in the strongest terms available.

Then the valedictory, in full: Praise be to Allah, Most High, Almighty, Most Exalted, and may the blessings of Allah be upon His Messenger to all humankind and jinn, and upon his family and companions, who are clear vessels of the meanings of the Qur'an, unchanged through the passage of ages — peace, mercy, and generosity be upon them. Allah is our Protector.

Clear vessels of the meanings of the Qur'an, unchanged through the passage of ages. The phrase does more than decorate: the meanings of the Book are held in people who transmit them without the transmission degrading, and that is what keeps the Book intelligible across time. Everything the earlier chapters established about where knowledge sits stands behind that clause, spent here rather than re-argued.

The correspondence ends. What is attached next seems to have nothing to do with fasting.

Our master Zayd ibn Muhammad, in his message attached to the hidden interpretation of the eclipse prayer, teaches this. The attribution arrives damaged — a fragment reading "His soul" sits where a blessing on the speaker would normally go — and it is reproduced rather than reconstructed.

Allah, Blessed and Exalted, when He created creation, decreed among them wonders. Then a broken clause: the first to believe ended it, and made all that was created in the first age a proof for the people of the last age. The first half cannot be recovered; the second is clear and load-bearing. What was made at the beginning is evidence for those who come at the end. The world's oldest regularities are arguments addressed to the latest generation.

And then the specific wonder. He made the eclipse of the moon a covering for it, and decreed that its covering falls on the fourteenth night of the month, though it may sometimes fall on the thirteenth night. The majority of eclipses, however, occur on the fourteenth night. And all of this contains signs and arguments for Allah.

Notice what kind of claim that is. An eclipse is the sky's most frightening irregularity — the one occasion the law itself answers with a prayer, as the earlier teaching on eclipse and its companions in the register of dread established. And what is said about it here is that it is not irregular at all. It has a date, and a decreed one rather than one noted after the fact. The thing that looks like the sky breaking its own rules turns out to keep an appointment.

Then the evidence offered for it, damaged in a way that matters and given as it stands. Evidence lies in the fact that the moon is absent and hidden from the world for twenty-eight nights of every month, and it is absent and concealed for two days and two nights, which is forty-eight hours, and then it reappears after those forty-eight hours.

The two figures do not agree. A moon absent for twenty-eight nights of every month is not the moon anyone observes, and the sentence itself then gives the absence as two days and two nights — forty-eight hours, the figure the passage goes on to use and repeat. The twenty-eight cannot be reconciled from inside the slice, and nothing here says what it belonged to. It is left standing and named as damaged rather than quietly corrected, because a repaired number would be this chapter's invention rather than its source's claim.

What the passage is doing survives the damage. It argues from periodicity: the moon disappears for a fixed span and returns, the covering falls on a fixed night, and these are decrees rather than accidents — and a decree can be known in advance. Which is what the first half of this section said with a preposition. The moon has a timetable of its own. Your eye is how you find out where the moon is on it; your eye is not what puts it there.

And then the hinge. This blessed message on the matter of the month of Ramadan, the text says, is attached to the obligation of fasting the month of Ramadan. So the eclipse material was never a digression — it was filed next to the fast, because the fast is where the moon's timetable becomes a legal question.

It is addressed to our master and Mawlana, the summoner of the age, Ahmad Hamid al-Din ibn Abdallah al-Kirmani — may his intercession avail us — who is the proof of our master al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah, the Commander of the Believers, may God bless him and grant him peace.

The correspondence has a named recipient now. The rest of this chapter is his reply.

## The Necessary Message on Fasting

In the name of God, the Merciful, the Compassionate — and in Him I place my trust.

Praise be to Allah, by whose grace good deeds are accomplished, and through obedience to His guardians, blessings prevail. May Allah bless His messenger and His servant Muhammad, the best of people, the pride of those who came before him among the noble messengers, the righteous among the righteous, the light of guidance and the luminaries in darkness. And thereafter —

Its clauses are not ornamental. Good deeds are accomplished by grace; blessings prevail through obedience to His guardians. The second is the letter's thesis in miniature, laid down before any argument begins: whatever is said about months and moons will end up being about obedience.

Then the occasion, as the correspondent gave it to him.

Brother, you wrote to recall that the people of division and disagreement — in the habits they have adopted — have driven a wedge against the people of truth and the coalition, and have said much in challenging them regarding the community, opposing them in that they began fasting before the general public.

Every phrase there is doing work. A wedge driven against the people of truth and the coalition: not an argument lost but a community split off and made conspicuous. They have said much in challenging them: sustained public criticism, not a scholar's objection in a book. And the substance is embarrassingly concrete — these people started fasting before everyone else did.

Then the request, with its date and its scandalous detail.

You asked me to clarify the matter of entering the fast among the Shi'i community of the Commander of the Faithful, may God's peace be upon him, specifically the fast of the year four hundred, which was obligated by commands received from the Immaculate Prophet — entering it two days before the crescent moon was sighted with our own eyes — and to address the problem with a clear statement, explanation, and proof.

Two days. And the letter does not soften it: with our own eyes. Nobody claims a sighting the critics missed, or a cloud, or a dispute about the horizon. The community began the fast on the authority of commands received, and the moon everyone was told to watch for did not appear for another two days. That is the fact the whole epistle exists to justify, placed at the front in the plainest available words before a single proof has been offered.

Notice how much that changes the argument's shape. Until now the crescent question has been a question about the month — how long it is, whether it can shrink, what the reports about watching for it were addressing — and those are questions you can lose quietly. This one you cannot. The previous chapter argued that the fast begins by following rather than by squinting at a horizon. Here is what that looks like when it is done, in a dated year, before an audience that found it ridiculous.

Al-Kirmani accepts the brief. I therefore resolved, he says, to set that forth in a letter in which I cite some of the proofs.

Then a compressed sentence about what kind of letter it means to be: the healing answer does not alarm the discerning eyes of the saints in the structure of its arguments, and it warms the hearts of the notables while those who violate its meanings are left behind. The phrasing resists a clean reading and is reproduced rather than smoothed. Its direction is legible: such an answer holds up under the scrutiny of those best qualified to give it, comforts those who receive it, and leaves behind only those who will not take its meanings seriously. Note the phrase itself — the healing answer — which is exactly what the previous letter asked its opponents for.

I therefore say, he continues, with the help of Allah and His guardians in His land — peace and blessings of Allah be upon them — and I label it: the necessary message on fasting the month of Ramadan.

The letter names itself, which tells you it was written to circulate. And the claim made for it is deliberately wide: for it contains arguments that bind all Muslims, and no one loyal to the principle of reason and sense can turn away from them. Allah is the guardian of right success.

That is not the claim you would expect from a document defending a minority practice. It does not say: here is why our community is entitled to its own reckoning. It says: here are arguments binding on everyone. The letter intends to win on common ground.

Then the heading changes. **Chapter:** — and what follows is not yet an argument about fasting. It is a diagnosis of the people on the other side, running uninterrupted from an opening invocation to a closing prayer, and it is given here whole.

Know — may Allah protect you from ignorance and guard you from the way of misguidance — that the people of opposition, had they possessed discernment and insight, or had they a share in reason and understanding, or been divided from it, the devil's machinations against them would have been repelled, their concern would have been for the truth, and they would have been among those whom Allah excepts in His Book, saying: "They will always be in disagreement except those upon whom your Lord has mercy." (chapter 11, verses 118 to 119)

The clause "or been divided from it" arrives damaged and is left as it stands. The sentence is otherwise clear, and it is a conditional with a sting: the opposition's problem is not that they reasoned badly but that the equipment was not in use.

And the verse chosen to anchor it is a hard one. People will always be in disagreement — a permanent condition, not a temporary failure — except those upon whom your Lord has mercy. Disagreement is the default; agreement is an exemption, and the exemption is granted rather than achieved.

But when they mounted the steed of passion and the causes of personal opinion overwhelmed them, the devil gained hold of them and they followed him — all except a company of true believers.

The steed of passion is a chosen mount; someone climbs onto it. And personal opinion is named as a cause of the overwhelming, not as a harmless alternative method — reasoning from one's own view, in a matter where an authority exists, is not a rival procedure here but the mechanism of the fall.

Allah has said regarding them: "Have they not traveled through the earth so that they may have hearts with which to reason, or ears with which to hear? For it is not the eyes that go blind, but it is the hearts within the breasts that go blind." (chapter 22, verse 46)

That verse is the centre of the passage, and its placement in a dispute about seeing the moon is not accidental. The whole controversy has been about eyes: whose eyes, on what ground, under what sky, at what hour. And the verse says eyes are not where blindness happens. The organ that fails is the heart, and the failure is one of reasoning and of hearing — of taking in what is there, whether by thinking it through or by being told.

They are like camels in what they wallow in, yet like the dead — they do not feel. As Allah Almighty said: "Dead, not alive, and they do not perceive." (chapter 16, verse 21)

The two images say different things. The camel does what animals do, indifferent, at home in it; the dead do not even have that. To be dead and not perceive is to be past the reach of evidence altogether — a strong thing to say about a party you are about to spend a whole epistle trying to convince.

They died to the knowledge of realities and turned a blind eye to them, driven by arrogance toward the people of truth, by desire and contention. They became like beasts in their disputes — hesitating in their blindness, running without discernment or insight, unaware of the division and separation that Allah has forbidden, as He said: "And do not be like those who dispersed and disagreed" (chapter 3, verse 105) — except out of their licentiousness, arrogance, and envy, and their refusal to obey the one whom Allah has made obligatory to obey from among the rulers of the Command.

There the diagnosis reaches its object. Everything before it — the blindness, the passion, the beasts, the dispersal — converges on one named failure, and that failure is a refusal of obedience to a person. Not a doctrinal error, not a mistaken method of calculation.

Allah Almighty, in His glory, called for returning to them in all matters of dispute, saying: "Whatever you dispute about, refer it to Allah and the Messenger." And He said: "And if they had referred it to the Messenger and to those in authority among them, those who can draw conclusions from it would have known it." (chapter 4, verse 83)

The first of those two quotations is given without a reference, because the wording as the source transmits it cannot be securely assigned to one verse. The second is the operative one anyway, and it is what the whole admonition was built to arrive at. It does not merely say that disputes should be referred; it says what happens when they are. Those who can draw conclusions from it would have known it. Referral is not a gesture of deference that leaves the question where it was. It is the procedure by which the answer becomes available.

Which is why the diagnosis comes before any astronomy or lexicography. A dispute about when the month begins is, on this letter's account, a solved problem with a known address. The scandal of the year four hundred is not that one party calculated and another squinted. It is that there was somewhere to refer the question, and one party referred it.

Arrogance, licentiousness, envy, and disobedience to the people of truth prevent one from standing upon the facts and emerging from the company of the people of ignorance. Whoever opposes the people of truth and persists in his blindness and misguidance brings himself to ruin and evil. The word of punishment justly applies to him and he earns the verse of chastisement — we seek refuge in Allah from division after unity, from tyranny after faith, and from disobedience to His guardians — Allah's prayers be upon them — after submission and belief. We ask Him to make us among those who win His mercy by obeying Him and obeying His guardians, for He is ever good and responsible.

And it ends on the speaker rather than the opponent. The three things he asks to be kept from are each a loss of something once held: division after unity, tyranny after faith, disobedience after submission and belief. Nobody who never had them can suffer them. The prayer is for people on his own side.

## Nothing Created Without Its Pair

With the diagnosis delivered, the letter turns to the law, starting where the obligation starts.

He then made it obligatory upon His servants in the explicit text of His Book to fast, saying: "Fasting has been prescribed for you as it was prescribed for those before you, so that you may become righteous." (chapter 2, verse 183)

That verse has been through this book before and is not re-taught here. What matters is its function: the fast is commanded in the plain text, so nothing that follows is an argument about whether to fast. Everything at issue is about when.

The Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him — and his family said: "Sha'ban is a month and Ramadan is the month of Allah, and fasting is obligatory."

A short saying, and its asymmetry is the point. Sha'ban is a month; Ramadan is the month of Allah. The same unit of the calendar, and one of them is attributed to God in a way the other is not — which is what makes the question of Ramadan's length different in kind. It is stated here without being developed. The Imams — peace be upon them — said: "Fasting the month of Ramadan is obligatory upon every Muslim." The obligation is confirmed from the Book, from the Prophet and from the Imams in three consecutive sentences, and then the letter moves.

When Allah Almighty imposed the fast of the month of Ramadan upon His servants, He did not leave them — the Prophet, peace and blessings of Allah be upon him, and his family — blind to the knowledge of its time, just as He did not leave them in ignorance of how to fulfill other obligatory duties, such as the number and times of prayers.

The sentence is grammatically muddled in transmission: it begins with the servants and then specifies the Prophet and his family, so the "them" who were not left blind shifts mid-clause. The muddle is left visible rather than tidied, because the two readings are not the same claim. On one, God did not leave the community without access to the fast's timing; on the other, He did not leave the Prophet and his family without knowledge of it. The second is stronger and is the one the surrounding argument uses.

Either way, the analogy is worth taking seriously. The number of the prayers is known, and their times are known — not estimated each day from the look of the sky. And when He imposed fasting and the amount of zakat on its applicable categories, those amounts were specified too, as the previous chapters' catalogues of what is owed on coin, on herds, on watered ground and on the head of every person showed. The religion does not command an obligation and then leave its measure to be guessed at.

So, the letter says, He said: "Fast at the sighting of it and break the fast at the sighting of it."

And then a concession, made without reluctance and made precise. On this narration there is scarcely any disagreement among the sects of Muslims, and it is the pillar upon which the people of the outward rely in their argument.

That is the hinge of everything that follows. The report is not called weak, not said to have a defective chain or to be one transmitter's isolated testimony. It is granted as something nearly all Muslims accept, and credited as a pillar — the load-bearing support of the opposing case. The letter chooses to fight on the other side's strongest ground with the other side's own material, which is the method the previous chapter identified, arriving now from a different writer in a different document.

And then, with the pillar named, the letter makes a move that seems to have nothing to do with fasting.

We therefore say that Allah Almighty created all things in pairs, so that nothing in creation is left in solitary oneness. Nothing shares with Him in His oneness.

That "therefore" is doing enormous work, and the slice breaks off before it is discharged. But the claim itself is clear, and it is a claim about what kind of universe the law is legislating for. Everything made is made double. Solitary oneness — being one without a counterpart — is not a condition anything created may be in, because it is the condition of the Creator. Uniqueness is not a property things have; it is what marks off the one who made them.

Allah said: "Glory be to Him who created all the pairs," (chapter 36, verse 36) and He said: "And of all things We have created pairs, that you may remember." (chapter 51, verse 49)

The second verse adds the purpose, and it is not decorative. Pairs exist that you may remember: the doubleness of the world is a standing reminder, written into everything, of the one thing that is not double.

Then the survey, and it is offered as observation rather than as doctrine: we observe that all things come in pairs and forms. Essence and extension. Sky and earth. Land and sea. Plain and mountain. Night and day. Good and evil. Body and soul. Outward and inward. This world and the next. Male and female — along with other things that could be enumerated at length.

That list is worth reading slowly, because it is not a single kind of pairing. Sky and earth are two regions. Night and day are two phases of one cycle. Body and soul are two constituents of one creature. Outward and inward are two aspects of one thing, and that pair has been the book's working distinction for chapters now. What holds the list together is not that the members are alike but that in every case there are two, neither intelligible on its own. You do not know what "inward" means without an outward to set it against.

And then the move that turns cosmology into law.

We also observe, the letter says, that the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him — established the foundation of his law and its governing principle by pairing commands and obligatory duties with their counterparts — pointing thereby to what we have said about the singularity of Allah's oneness.

That is the claim: the revealed law reproduces the structure of the created world on purpose, and the purpose is to point at the fact that God alone is single. A law made of pairs never lets you mistake any of its parts for the One.

Then the evidence, institution by institution. Prayer, which he paired with zakat. The call to prayer, which he paired with the iqama. Hajj, which he paired with Umrah. Al-Safa, which he paired with al-Marwa. The obligatory prayer, which he paired with the Sunnah prayer. Bowing, which he paired with prostration. And the testimony that there is no god but Allah, which he paired with the testimony that Muhammad is the Prophet of Allah — and so on with many other examples that could be mentioned at length.

Seven pairs, and the series is not random. It starts with whole institutions — prayer and zakat, which the book's own chapters on giving showed to be inseparable — and works inward. The call and its answering call, which this book has spent a chapter on. The greater pilgrimage and the lesser. Two hills that must both be walked. Then, inside a single prayer, two postures. And finally, inside a single sentence of belief, two testimonies: the one naming God and the one naming the man who brought the message about Him. Every layer of the religion, from its largest divisions down to the shortest thing a person can say, is built in twos.

And there the slice stops.

It stops one step short of the conclusion it has been assembling. The letter named the sighting report as the pillar of the opposing case, then said "we therefore say" and laid out a universe and a law in which nothing stands alone and every command has its counterpart. You can feel where the next sentence is going — if everything in the law comes paired, a report about sighting is one half of something — but that sentence is not in this chapter and will not be manufactured here. What this chapter delivers is the premise, fully built, with the conclusion still on the following page.

## What this episode lands

This chapter is the seam between two documents, and each side says the same thing in its own idiom.

On the near side, a correspondence closes. Its last substantive act is to read a preposition: the Prophet and his family fasted at the sighting of the moon, meaning at its emergence, and the community is told to fast at the sighting of it, meaning so that it may be seen. The event is the moon's coming out; the seeing is how it is registered. Two questions are left with the other party: find in the Book the varying month you claim, and explain why the Messenger did not simply say "after seeing it." Then a request for a sound and healing answer, a disavowal of arrogance, and a blessing on those who carry the meanings of the Qur'an unchanged.

Attached to that closing is a message on the eclipse prayer's hidden interpretation, deliberately placed: the covering of the moon falls on the fourteenth night, occasionally the thirteenth, and the moon's absence and return run on a fixed span of two days and two nights. The passage's other figure — twenty-eight nights — contradicts its own sentence and is left standing as damaged rather than repaired. The argument survives, because it is about periodicity: the most alarming thing the sky does keeps an appointment, and an appointment can be known in advance.

On the far side a new epistle opens, addressed to the summoner of the age, and it opens with a public embarrassment rather than a principle. In the year four hundred a community began the fast two days before the crescent was seen with their own eyes, and their neighbours have not let it go. The letter names itself the necessary message on fasting the month of Ramadan and claims to argue on ground binding all Muslims. Before it argues anything it diagnoses: people disagree permanently unless mercy exempts them, the organ that goes blind is the heart and not the eye, and the failure at issue is a refusal to obey the one whom obedience is owed to — because a referred dispute is one the competent can resolve.

Then the law. The fast is commanded in plain text; Ramadan is attributed to God in a way Sha'ban is not; God specifies the measures of His obligations rather than leaving them to be guessed. The sighting report is granted in the strongest terms available — accepted across the sects, named as the pillar of the opposing case — and the reply to it begins not with astronomy but with creation: everything made is made in pairs, because solitary oneness belongs to God alone, and the pairs exist so that you may remember. The law reproduces that structure on purpose — prayer with zakat, the call with the iqama, Hajj with Umrah, one hill with the other, bowing with prostration, the testimony about God with the testimony about His Prophet.

And the chapter ends with the premise complete and the conclusion withheld. A report about sighting has been placed in a world where nothing is ever one thing by itself. What its counterpart is, this chapter does not say.
