## Where this episode opens

How far does a man have to go before his prayer is allowed to get shorter — and why should meeting his aunt on the road make it long again? That second question is not a curiosity at the edge of this chapter. It is the chapter's engine. The source is asked, over and over, in different words and from different cities, what makes a travelling man a traveller, and it keeps answering with things that have nothing to do with distance: who is waiting at the other end, what he meant when he set out, how many nights he told himself he would stay, and whether he has crossed the threshold of his own front door.

The chapter before this one ended by placing a boy of eight into a prayer he was two decades away from understanding, insisting that an act entered properly is hard to destroy from the inside. This chapter turns the question outward. The prayer here is altered not by anything that happens within it but by where the worshipper's body stands on the earth. A man in his own house prays four rak'ahs. The same man, on the road, prays two. Somewhere between the door and the horizon the obligation itself changes shape, and this chapter records a community trying to find exactly where.

The material arrives almost entirely as questions and answers, and the questions come from a long way off. Sheikhs of al-Hind put their difficulties in writing and wait for replies to come back across the sea from Yemen. Sayyidina Ali ibn Sulayman Qas, the author of this book, submits his own questions to our master and Mawlana Ja'far ibn our master Sulayman, may Allah Most High sanctify them in the chambers of the heavens. Sheikh al-Hasan ibn Nuh puts his eleventh issue to our master Idris ibn Hassan. Sheikh Shamun ibn Muhammad Ghuri al-Hindi receives the answer of our master Badr al-Din Hasan ibn Idris. Our master Muhammad ibn Fahd answers in his treatise The Love of the Path of Guidance for Those Who Have Converted. Behind the correspondence stand the books: the first question-and-answer book arranged according to the chapters of the Pillars of Islam, the Book of Purity, the concise book on the works of our master al-Nu'man, Mukhtasar al-Musannaf, Mukhtasar al-Athar, the Book of Sub-Nursing of Ja'far ibn Mansur al-Yaman, and Anas al-Munibeh.

The shape of the questions tells you what kind of life produced them. Nobody asks whether travel shortens the prayer; everybody knows it does. They ask about the seams. A man with a house in Ahmadabad and a house in Nahrwala — which is he travelling from? A man whose own land takes a day to walk across — is that a journey?

The chapter moves in three passes over the same ground. The first establishes what a journey is — how far, for what purpose, and what the licence consists of once granted. The second introduces the doctrine that governs everything else and is hard to guess from outside: the presence of a close blood relative cancels the concession, so a man among kin prays in full wherever on the earth he stands. The third gathers intention, length of stay, and the moment of coming home, and closes with the dispensations granted when conditions will not cooperate.

One instinct holds all three together. The shortened prayer is a relief for a man genuinely out of his ordinary life, and the tradition keeps deciding that he is not — at home in his relative's city, at home the moment he crosses his own doorstep, at home wherever he has decided to stay ten days. The concession is real and it is defended, but it is narrow, and most of this chapter is spent drawing that edge.

## The Journey And Its Licence

The first thing the source does is measure. Reporting from Mukhtasar al-Athar: a mail is four leagues; a farsakh is three miles; and a mil is three thousand cubits — that is, one thousand moderate paces measured by one leg, from where the right foot is raised to where it is placed down.

Notice how the smallest unit is defined: not against a rope or a rod, but against a walking man. The whole scale of legal distance is built upward from the body of someone actually going somewhere.

The rule that uses those distances is stated plainly. The traveller breaks his fast and shortens the prayer from the moment he leaves his house and begins to travel, until he returns and enters his house — if his travel is blameless. Two conditions, and the second is easy to read past. The licence attaches to blameless travel; a journey undertaken for something the tradition would not countenance does not earn what a journey earns.

Then the counter-cases begin. The Book of Purity says that one whose journey was merely a leisurely outing should not shorten the prayer, and the source is asked what a picnic journey means. The answer: if a man owns a great deal of land and enjoys walking through it for pleasure, that does not justify shortening the prayer.

The man in that answer may cover real distance. It does not matter. He has not left his life; he has walked around inside it. The concession is paid not for miles but for the displacement miles usually cause.

The same logic is then applied to whole occupations. It is said in the book Anas al-Munibeh, reporting the Commander of the Believers, may Allah's peace be upon him, that there are six categories of people who do not shorten their prayers or fasting: the collectors who are engaged in their rounds of collection; the merchant who travels in his trade from one market to another; the prince who is within his emirate; the man who goes out in pursuit of hunting; the shepherd who seeks pastures and grazing grounds; and the warrior who cuts off the road.

Read the list for what its members have in common and the principle comes clear. The collector's round is his work. The merchant's circuit between markets is his trade, not an interruption of it. The prince inside his emirate sits at the centre of his own authority, however far he rides. For all six, moving is not a departure from the ordinary; moving is the ordinary, and there is no broken routine to compensate.

That principle immediately settles a real case. The wandering sheikhs — such as Sheikh Abdullah ibn Nuh — travel among villages for the sake of the believing community, some villages close together and others far apart, sometimes more than a prayer's distance: should such a man shorten his prayer and break his fast? The answer of our master Idris, may God sanctify his soul: his case is like that of a prince who goes out to govern his emirate, and he is not required to shorten the prayer or break the fast.

The comparison is generous and precise at once. The travelling sheikh is not told his journeys are trivial; he is told they are his office. The ruling costs him the concession by raising the status of what he does.

The same measure decides for the boat: the owner of a boat and merchants all pray in full if the journey is no more than from Sofa to Aden, and nothing else applies to them. Nor should one be compelled to repeat the prayer after reaching his family. A run a sailor makes as his ordinary work is not a journey in the sense that matters.

Cutting the other way is the slave authorized to trade, whether or not on his master's behalf, during the month of Ramadan or at another time: yes, he shortens his prayer and breaks his fast. The relief follows the man who is actually displaced, whatever his standing and on whoever's account the profit falls.

The distances themselves then get their own seams. Whoever travels fewer than two postal stages and intends to stay, and the prayer time comes upon him on the way, prays in full and does not shorten; and whoever travels to a postal station intending to return, then returns to his family, and the prayer time comes upon him on the way, likewise prays in full. But the counter-case must be held beside it: even if the destination he intends to reach is only one mail away and there are no people or shops there, and he intends to return within ten days, he shortens and breaks his fast until he departs. A short trip into settled country where he means to stop is not a journey. A short trip into empty country is. Distance is a proxy; the thing measured is whether a man has been put outside the arrangements that ordinarily hold him.

And then one place refuses the whole apparatus. Whoever enters Makkah should pray in full and not shorten, regardless of whether he stops for an hour or a day — for the sake of Allah — whether or not he is engaged in worship there. Our master said: Isma'il ibn Sayyidina Hiba said that prayer is performed only in the sanctuary of the House of God. Makkah is a place where prayer is not shortened. Mukhtasar al-Musannaf says it again of the Sacred Mosque: there is no shortening in it, whether one is in a state of spiritual engagement or simply present there.

That is the sharpest exception in the chapter, indifferent to everything the chapter has been measuring — how far the man came, what for, how long he will stay. The relief is granted to a man away from the place he belongs, and in the sanctuary that argument collapses.

Having drawn the boundary, the source says what the licence actually contains. Our master Ja'far ibn Mansur of Yemen, may God sanctify him, said in the Book of Sub-Nursing, on the Sunnah of shortening: the three prayers that are shortened are the first — meaning Zuhr — and Asr and Isha, each obligatory as four rak'ahs; shortening reduces each four-rak'ah prayer to two, making six rak'ahs in total.

The concession is narrower than its reputation. The book has already taught the sacred times and the shape of the prayer, and neither is disturbed: nothing is cancelled, postponed, or merged. The traveller stands at the same five hours as everyone else and says less at three of them.

He does not, however, drop what is voluntary. Mukhtasar al-Athar reports that the scholars said the traveller should pray the Sunnah and nafl prayers: if he is walking during the day and the time for prayer comes while he is walking, he prays the obligatory prayer as two rak'ahs and defers the Sunnah until he dismounts at night, making it up with the night prayer. A tradition interested only in relief would have let the voluntary prayers go first. This one keeps them and moves them to the hour when he is off his feet, which says the concession answers circumstance rather than reducing what is owed.

And the concession cannot be declined. Mukhtasar al-Musannaf: if the traveller prays in full — whether forgetfully or deliberately — and there is still time, he must repeat the prayer, even if the time has passed. Praying four where two are owed is not extra devotion; it is the wrong prayer. On the road the shortened prayer is not a discount a pious man may refuse. It is the obligation itself in the form the road has given it.

The same book fixes the posture. Every prayer or prostration obligatory upon a person while he is on the ground does not count for him if he performs it as a rider; and every prayer or prostration obligatory upon him while riding, if he performs it on the ground, is valid and accepted. The asymmetry is deliberate: a man may always give more than his situation requires, and may never give less by claiming a situation he is not in.

Two reports about praying in motion sit beside each other without being reconciled, and the second is the one the tradition keeps. Mukhtasar al-Athar says that praying on the road is forbidden. The commentary on the Pillars says that the Messenger of Allah, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him, used to pray on his way to Medina, inclining his head for prostration, with his mount behind him. That image — a man bending his head for the prostration he cannot make, and keeping on — governs everyone here who prays while going somewhere.

The last ruling of the first movement concerns what the traveller carries. Should the Qur'an and other books be carried during travel and in settled life, given that the common people narrate from the Prophet, peace be upon him, that he said, "Do not carry the Qur'an"? Answer: the Qur'an should be placed on the head out of honour and reverence for it, not otherwise.

The answer neither forbids nor simply permits. It prescribes a manner. A book that travels in a saddlebag among the trade goods has been made cargo; a book carried on the head has been made something else, by a gesture that costs the carrier comfort and is visible to everyone on the road.

## Kinship That Completes The Prayer

Now the chapter produces the doctrine that makes it unlike any other in this book, and the first question states it without preamble. A man has two houses, one in Ahmadabad and the other in Nahrwala, and lives some days in each: is it permissible for his prayer to be completed until he enters the borders of the country where he is staying? Answer: if he stays five prayer times with his wife or with his mahram relatives, he should complete his prayer and fast.

The condition is not the house. It is the people. A man with two houses is resolved not by asking which house is really his, but who he is with and for how long. Five prayer times in the company of his wife or his close blood relatives, and he is a resident, wherever the roof happens to stand.

The source is then pressed on exactly which relatives, twice, and answers both times through scripture. Asked about the three categories Allah has forbidden in the Qur'an — the ten degrees of kinship in marriage — with respect to those one travels with and those with whom one prays and fasts, it answers: anyone who is a close relative through the womb is one for whom the traveller prays and fasts in full, standing for the five prayers wherever he happens to be. This also applies to a man's wife; it does not apply to the wife's relatives or to those related through nursing.

The second asking names the verse. Are the relatives who complete the prayer and fasting the ones whom Allah Almighty mentioned when He said, "Forbidden to you are your mothers, your daughters, your sisters, your paternal aunts, your maternal aunts, your brother's daughters, and your sister's daughters" (chapter 4, verse 23) — and what corresponds to these verses for men? And do the mothers of wives, stepmothers, and relatives through nursing complete the prayer and fasting or not? Answer: the relatives, both male and female, who complete the prayer and fasting are those connected through the womb. The mothers of wives and those related through the male line do not.

The scriptural list is a list of women a man may not marry. The chapter borrows it whole and puts it to a different use: the people you may never marry are the people whose presence ends your journey. That verse marks the circle within which a man is not a stranger, because the relation is closer than any relation that could be contracted, and this chapter reads the same circle as the circle of home. Wherever a man stands inside it, he has not gone anywhere.

That is why the exclusions matter as much as the inclusions. The wife's mother does not complete the prayer. The wife's relatives do not. Those related through nursing do not. Relation through the male line, in the second answer, does not. The wife herself does, and only she. Every relation that merely resembles blood or marriage is left outside, even though those same relations do bar marriage. The source is not repeating the marriage law; it is selecting from it.

Once the circle is drawn, the geography follows. Someone travelling from Nahrwala to Ahmadabad meets a relative along the way, outside the city: should he pray in full? He should not, until after he has entered the borders of the city where he has a relative. An encounter on the road is not enough. The kinsman must be met inside the place he lives, because what ends the journey is not a familiar face but arrival where the traveller is no stranger. The same answer extends it: as for travelling from his home to stay in a city or in the home of relatives, or stopping for a day or more, he should not shorten for as long as he remains there; and if he departs and then returns, or stops for a day or more, he again does not shorten.

The concise book on the works of our master al-Nu'man, may God sanctify his soul, states the general principle twice in this chapter, in nearly the same words, which is how the source signals a load-bearing rule. The traveller breaks his fast and shortens his prayer from the moment he leaves his house until he returns and enters it, if his travel is blameless. However, if along the way he encounters a mahram relative whose home is not at a full travel distance from his own country, and that relative resides in that country, then his prayer is completed in full.

Then the fine cases, where a doctrine shows what it really holds. Whoever travels and reaches the village where his brother-in-law resides, or his sister-in-law, shortens the prayer. The in-law is exactly the relation the earlier answers excluded, and the exclusion is enforced even when it produces the odd result that a man staying under a relative's roof is still legally on the road.

But if a woman comes to her father's house yet does not actually live there, having stayed as a resident or in the home of a mahram relative, and she is a minor who has not yet reached adulthood — or a child yet unborn — the prayer is performed for her in full. The father's house is the point. She is not resident there in any ordinary sense, and she is not old enough for the question to concern her judgment at all. The relation is what the ruling reads, and it does not depend on her having chosen anything.

Two cases turn instead on intention. What of the divorced women in India, if someone travels with them for trade or otherwise, in Ramadan or at another time — does he pray and fast as a resident or as a traveller? And the same if someone travels with a non-Muslim? Answer: if their intention was to travel with him, they pray and fast in full; if they set out for a different purpose, their ruling follows that of travellers. Here the presence is physical and undisputed, and the source still asks what the arrangement between them is: company deliberately kept is a settled arrangement, company that merely coincides is not.

The doctrine is confirmed by a questioner who wants the details spelled out. It is said in the book of the question of Sheikh Shamun ibn Muhammad Ghuri al-Hindi, answered by our master Badr al-Din Hasan ibn Idris, may God be pleased with him, concerning a woman who is a close relative travelling with someone, and whether one who does not shorten the prayer has all five prayers established through his presence. The answer: whoever is one of the guardians by kinship, the traveller prays and fasts by virtue of that person's presence and has the five prayers. The relationship with a wife is established, whereas it is not valid through the relation of a woman by kinship alone or through the relation of breastfeeding. The questioner presses for detail, and receives a sentence that is half rebuke and half invitation: among you, who is saved? The best of you are those who reflect on matters of religion and contemplate them. Then the ruling: it is only applicable when one of his mahrams is present in a place, and the traveller has all five prayer times established through that presence — and the same applies to his wife if she is staying with him through all five prayer times.

The last word on kinship belongs to Sheikh al-Hasan ibn Nuh, who asked our master Idris ibn Hassan the eleventh issue: whether a man may travel with his son's wife or with his wife's mother without a barrier between them, speak with them, and bury one of them — and what the status of nursing relatives is in this regard. The answer refuses to impose a restriction the law does not impose: it is not necessary to impose restrictions if there is no one else from her womb available. Allah knows best. Then it adds that the law's silence does not settle the question of conduct. The first and most important course is to be cautious about these matters, on account of the saying of the Prophet, may Allah bless him and his noble family, that one should leave what is permissible in order to be safe from falling into what is not. And it supplies the practical remedy: if one of their relatives is present — even if not a womb relative — that relative makes all of the above permissible.

That is the chapter's most honest moment. It has drawn a circle with legal precision, and now says the circle marks what is allowed, not what is wise, and that a careful man stands well inside the line rather than on it.

## Intention, Return, And Dispensation

The third movement begins where the traveller stops walking, and the first thing the source asks him is what he decided. The stay that ends the concession is ten days — as our master Muhammad ibn Fahd, may God sanctify his soul, puts it in the seventeenth question of his treatise The Love of the Path of Guidance for Those Who Have Converted: what is stipulated in the books of the guardians of God, peace be upon them, is that the stay referred to independently is ten days, neither less nor more.

His questioner's case is a man who travels the distance of a mile, stays eight days and returns on the ninth, given that a round trip covering a postal distance makes the shortening apply, since going and returning together constitute two legs. If he intends to stay for ten days, the prayer is completed. If it appears that he will return before ten days have elapsed, he has returned and is not required to fulfil the full ten-day stay, and this does not invalidate his completing the prayers mentioned above. What settles his standing is the calendar he held when he arrived, not the one he ends up keeping.

Because intention does the work, the source must say what happens when it changes. A man intends to stay in a house for ten days and then needs to leave after four or five, a need having arisen that he could not put off: as long as he still holds in his heart the intention to stay, he prays and fasts in full; and when he is on his way to travel, he shortens once he has resolved to do so and committed to it.

The hinge is resolve, and it is located precisely. The prayer does not change when the need arises, nor when he begins packing, but when he has resolved and committed — so a man whose departure is certain is still a resident until he has decided. The book has taught elsewhere that intention lives in the heart, and that teaching is simply spent here.

The next question tests whether intention attaches to a building. A traveller leaves a house where he had been staying for ten days and moves to another only a mile away: must he form a new intention for ten full days? Answer: as long as he intends to return to the place where he had originally intended to stay, he prays and fasts in full, even if he left it with the intention of staying elsewhere; moving does not nullify anything, and he need only renew his intention to stay there for ten days. He is not made a traveller again by walking a mile down the road: residency here is a state of mind about a place, not an address. These three answers, the source notes, are from our master Idris ibn Hassan, may Allah sanctify him.

Arrival at a village raises the same question from the other side. A traveller with family in a village enters its far side, or a house in it, without yet reaching his family and relatives: the prayer is not performed in full until the mahram agrees to host him, if he is residing with a mahram. This holds for five prayer times, unless he intends to stay ten days; so when he arrives in the village with the intention of staying, he prays and fasts in full. Presence alone does not do it; arriving with the intention already formed does it at once.

The same clock governs a short stop. A traveller reaches a town just before sunset: should he remain with the people until the day after sunrise, and does that count as a day and a night? Answer: if he intends to stay until he prays on the second day, he should pray and fast in full. The unit counted is not hours but prayers.

Then the homecoming, and the source is stricter than anyone would expect. A man asks about the outlying districts of Ahmadabad — Kanpur, Sarnak Pur and the rest — and whether they are collectively one jurisdiction permitting a homeward-bound traveller to pray in full. Answer: a traveller returning home may not pray in full until he enters his own house.

Not the district. Not the city limits. Not the gate. The house. Everything between the gate and the doorstep is still the road, and the symmetry is the argument: the same threshold opens the concession and closes it.

Two concessions soften the edge without moving it. If he enters one of the city gates but has not yet reached his house, and fears missing the prayer, he may pray shortened. And if he leaves his house while still in the city, without having passed through any of its gates, and the prayer time comes upon him while he is still within the city, he prays in full. The gate matters going out and the door matters coming back. A further ruling leans the same way: a man who enters the gate of a city or village returning from travel during Ramadan, if the sun has not set before he enters his house, performs his prayers and fasting in full — he came home inside the day, so the day is a resident's day.

The chapter's final movement concerns conditions that will not cooperate, and it opens with a man who cannot find the direction. Whoever travels and, when the prayer time arrives, finds the sky heavily overcast and cannot determine the qibla — he should pray facing four directions, one in each direction for each obligatory prayer. Allah Almighty excuses His servant when He afflicts him with hardship. Even if he later prays again when the sun appears and the qibla becomes clear, and the time has not yet expired, he should return to perform the prayer.

The book has already taught how much the guarded direction matters, and this does not soften it by a degree; it satisfies it by exhaustion. A man who cannot know which way is right prays every way. And the excuse expires the moment the sky clears: a dispensation granted for hardship lasts exactly as long as the hardship.

The sea produces the same problem in another form. A man praying the Sunnah and nafl prayers aboard a ship as it rotates — must he turn with it? He should establish the direction of prayer at the moment he enters into it. If the ship turns on its own, there is nothing upon him. If he is able to turn with it and reorient himself, that is better. If he is in an obligatory prayer, he should do whatever he is able. The direction is fixed at the door of the prayer, and the deck's later drifting is not charged to him.

The ship also strains the congregation. An imam leads people aboard a ship, sitting because the ship's movement makes it impossible otherwise, while some of the congregation are also unable to stand and others are able: it is permissible given the circumstances of the ship, if they are not able to manage otherwise, and if he is rushed he should pray alone. The book has already settled what standing behind an imam requires, and that teaching is bent exactly as far as the deck requires and no further.

The congregation on land raises the mirror problem: a traveller praying two rak'ahs among residents praying four. The source answers it twice. The thirty-fifth issue concerns a traveller who arrives to find the resident imam in prayer, joins for two rak'ahs and then says the salaam: if he wishes to leave after two rak'ahs he may depart with two, and if he waits for the imam to say the salaam there is no harm in that. And from the questions addressed to certain sheikhs of India with written replies from the loyal preachers of Yemen: if the traveller is the imam of the residents and prays two rak'ahs, they stand and complete the prayer; likewise, if the resident is the imam of the travellers, they depart after two rak'ahs.

The congregation is not broken by the fact that its members owe different amounts. Each man prays what is his and the shared act absorbs the difference. Muhammad ibn Fahd adds a restriction: the traveller praying alongside residents is permissible if the imam is one of the virtuous and prays two rak'ahs, whereupon those behind him stand and complete their prayers — and this ruling is specific to people of the higher ranks rather than to everyone. The same, he says, holds for the prayer of the one who completes his prayer, the mutamim.

He then tells the story behind that dispensation, and it is the most unusual passage in the chapter. Our master and Mawlana Da'i al-Zaman Ja'far ibn our master Sulayman, may God sanctify his soul, was in the Yemeni island and came down from a noble castle to the land of Haraz. He performed the noon prayer of one of his days in a state of purity like everyone else, but he did not agree with that, because he was one who investigated this and other matters and was not prepared to investigate purity alongside the purity of others. When he had completed tayammum in a way no one noticed, he prayed with the people in congregation. He then said to some of those close to his call, to clarify the matter: this is how the dispensation arose for praying the prayer of the one who performs ablution, when it is obligatory for some important reason that serves the good of the call, its tranquillity, and the establishment of its order — just as there is a dispensation for the traveller to pray with the residents.

The book has already taught the paths of tayammum and does not re-teach them. What is new is the reasoning. A leader satisfies himself privately, in a way nobody sees, and joins the congregation without turning his private scruple into a public separation. The good served is named exactly: the tranquillity of the community and the order of its call. And the analogy is why the story is told — the traveller among residents declines, in the same way, to let a difference in what he owes tear the row apart.

A small case closes the chapter on the same instinct. Ali ibn Sulayman Qas asked about a minor praying behind a resident imam, who sits after two rak'ahs while the imam rises to continue, with no one behind the imam except the minor: should the imam call out the takbir aloud for the remainder? The answer: there is no obligation upon him to do so; his voice serves its purpose only for one who can be trusted to follow him in bowing, prostrating, and standing, and when that trust is absent, calling out aloud serves no purpose. The audible takbir is a signal, and a signal exists for whoever is meant to act on it — so the practice lapses, not because the child does not matter, but because the thing the voice is for is not happening.

## What this episode lands

This chapter never once answers the question it appears to be about. It is asked how far a man must travel and keeps replying with something else: whether the moving was his ordinary life or a break in it, whether a blood relative received him at the other end, what he decided in his heart on arrival, and whether he has crossed his own threshold on the way back. Distance is in the chapter — a mail is four leagues, a mil is a thousand paces of a walking man — but distance never decides a case by itself. It is a rough sign of the thing that matters, which is displacement.

The doctrine at the centre is the hardest to guess: the presence of a close blood relative, or a wife, for five prayer times restores the full prayer anywhere on earth. The circle of people a man may never marry is read as the circle of places where he can never be a stranger — the brother-in-law does not count, the wife's mother does not count, the milk relation does not count, and a girl too young to have chosen any of it still does. Then, having drawn the line that precisely, the source adds that a careful man should not walk along it.

The relief itself turns out to be narrow and non-negotiable. Three prayers are halved and the rest stand. The voluntary prayers are kept and deferred rather than dropped. A man who prays in full on the road has prayed the wrong prayer and repeats it. Makkah takes the concession back entirely, from anyone, for any length of stay. And where conditions make the ordinary prayer impossible — an overcast sky, a turning deck, an imam who cannot rise — the dispensations are generous in the moment and expire the moment the difficulty does.

Set against everything the book has built so far, this is where the prayer meets the world's inconvenience and does not yield much ground. Earlier chapters fixed the prayer's shape, its hours, its direction, and its leadership. Here the worshipper is moved out of the settings all of that assumed — onto roads, onto water, into other men's cities, into houses that are not his — and the tradition follows him out, shortening what it must, excusing what it must, and quietly insisting that almost everywhere he goes, he is more at home than he thinks.
