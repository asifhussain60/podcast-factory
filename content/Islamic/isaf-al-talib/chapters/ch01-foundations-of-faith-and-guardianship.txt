## Where this episode opens

What if the book's first lesson about faith is that faith cannot be claimed by a person who copies the forms of religion while refusing the knowledge and action that make those forms alive? This opening chapter begins after a compressed authorial preface: Faidh Ali bin Sayyidna Sulaiman names his work, Iṣāf al-Ṭālib fī Jamīʿ al-Maṭālib, and says that he has gathered scattered rulings from the books of the saints of God and their learned preachers so that seekers may understand the doctrine of the Ahl al-Bayt. That wrapper matters only as orientation. The teaching itself opens with faith, with walaya, with the difference between Islam and iman, and with the line of guidance by which the religious life becomes more than imitation.

The chapter does not argue like a courtroom debate. It works like a carefully stocked treasury. It gives numbered structures, scriptural phrases, inherited sayings, legal and devotional practices, and stories in which knowledge exposes authority. The opening proposition is severe: someone may imitate the worship of God and the call of the Ahl al-Bayt without knowledge and without action, but that person has no right to claim the name of faith. Faith is not a badge attached to inherited behavior. Faith is a formed life: right attachment, right knowledge, right practice, and right recognition of the guides through whom the meanings of religion are preserved.

So the first movement names levels and duties. The second movement turns to the walaya of the Father of Imams and to stories in which questions reveal who truly carries knowledge. The third movement gathers the practice of salām, prayers upon Muhammad and his family, and reverence for the borders of religion into a single discipline of belonging. The chapter's shape is important: faith begins as an inward claim, but the author immediately tests it through parents, neighbors, prayer, greeting, truthful speech, and the living chain of guidance.

## The levels of faith

The source begins the teaching with a statement from the noble books: "Know, my brother, that faith has eight levels." It names the first of these as charity toward parents, relatives, orphans, the needy, the neighbor who is a relative, the neighbor who is nearby, the companion at one's side, the traveler, and those whom one's faith obligates one to care for. The first face of faith is therefore not a private feeling. It is a map of obligation. Faith reaches outward before it explains itself inwardly. It asks how a person treats those who stand nearest, those who are vulnerable, those who pass through, and those whose claim may not be obvious until religion has trained the eye to see it.

Then the chapter gives its boundary line. Whoever imitates the worship of God and the call of the Ahl al-Bayt without knowledge and without action has no right to claim the name of faith. Such a person has no covenant or honor before God or before His guardian, because he has departed from faith. The phrase is not casual. The author is warning against religion reduced to performed resemblance: the movements are copied, the names are repeated, the group identity is inherited, but the heart and the act have not been joined to knowledge. Imitation without knowledge is unstable; knowledge without action is unproven; action without walaya loses the line by which religion is interpreted.

This is why the chapter immediately distinguishes faith from Islam and asks what falls under each. It cites the Mustaṇṣiriyya Councils, especially the fourth council of the Quran, where the Imams of religion are described as seven, recurring in this world as Fridays recur until the Resurrection, while the twelve proofs appear in the world. To know the limits of the testimony of God is to perform its rights. Those rights are given in two groups: seven duties for religion and twelve obligations for the pious.

The seven duties are walaya, purity, prayer, zakāt, fasting, Ḥajj, and jihad. The list is striking because walaya stands first. The author does not place guardianship after the ritual duties as a later decoration. Walaya is the gate through which the duties are rightly understood. Purity, prayer, almsgiving, fasting, pilgrimage, and struggle are all acts, but the order says that action must be joined to recognition. Religion is not merely a schedule of obligations. It is a life ordered by an acknowledged guide.

The twelve obligations then turn that ordered life toward society: honoring parents, maintaining ties of kinship, protecting the neighbor, enjoining good, forbidding evil, truthfulness in one's home, good conduct with one's spouse, kindness to those in one's care, spreading peace, feeding others, building brotherhood, and visiting the sick. The source adds that these are not obligatory for women. The episode should present that statement as the source's legal formulation, without expanding it into a modern argument not made here. What matters for this chapter's architecture is that iman is not allowed to remain abstract. It becomes parents, kin, neighbor, household, dependent, peace, food, brotherhood, and illness. The same faith that recognizes walaya also feeds, visits, greets, and reconciles.

The chapter then brings in a saying attributed to Mawlānā the Commander of the Faithful, ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, in his four hundred precepts: the Children of Israel divided into seventy-two sects, and this nation will divide into seventy-three; one of them is in Paradise and the rest are in the Fire. The saying sharpens the stakes. The author is not presenting every religious affiliation as an equal path. He is building a doctrine of right attachment, in which salvation is joined to the living line of interpretation and obedience. The many sects are not merely sociological variety. They are the visible result of losing the true center.

This first teaching therefore holds two truths together. On one side, faith must be embodied in conduct: parents, kin, neighbor, spouse, dependents, brotherhood, the sick. On the other side, faith must be anchored in walaya: the recognized guide, the line of the Ahl al-Bayt, and the limits through which the testimony of God is rightly known. If one hears only the social ethics, the chapter becomes a moral handbook. If one hears only the hierarchy, it becomes a list of offices. The author wants neither reduction. The point is a formed religious life, where obedience, knowledge, and recognition occupy the same house.

The arrangement also explains why the opening paragraph rejected mere imitation. A person can imitate ritual. A person can imitate group identity. A person can even imitate charitable behavior when it is socially expected. But the chapter asks for something more demanding: knowledge that knows where guidance comes from, action that proves the claim of faith, and social conduct that turns doctrine into mercy. This is why the "levels" of faith are not levels of inward mood. They are levels of responsibility.

The first level, charity toward parents, relatives, orphans, the needy, neighbors, companions, travelers, and those whom faith obligates one to care for, also gives the listener the chapter's method. The author does not begin with rare mystical language. He begins where every person can be examined. Parents test gratitude. Relatives test loyalty beyond preference. Orphans and the needy test whether compassion reaches those who cannot repay it. The neighbor who is near and the neighbor who is related test the difference between convenience and duty. The companion at one's side and the traveler test whether care extends beyond the permanent circle of the household. The phrase "those whom your faith obligates you to care for" leaves the circle open, because religion may create claims that ordinary social instinct would overlook.

This is why the seven and the twelve should not be heard as two unrelated lists. The seven duties name the vertical structure of religion: walaya, purity, prayer, zakāt, fasting, Ḥajj, and jihad. The twelve obligations name the horizontal spread of that religion into the world. Walaya prevents the duties from becoming private self-discipline detached from guidance. The social obligations prevent walaya from becoming a claim of attachment that never becomes mercy. The author is building a foundation broad enough for the whole book: later rulings will concern prayer, fasting, purity, zakāt, pilgrimage, and many detailed acts, but the opening insists that the legal life must be carried by recognition and by human responsibility.

There is also a quiet pedagogy in the order of the twelve. It begins with those through whom life is received, then moves to kinship, then to the neighbor, then to moral command and restraint, then inside the house, then to dependents, then outward into peace, feeding, brotherhood, and sickness. Faith moves from origin to proximity to moral speech to domestic conduct to communal repair. By the time the list reaches visiting the sick, the listener has been led from the authority of God and His guardian all the way to a bedside. That is the chapter's whole imagination of religion: the highest doctrine is not betrayed by the smallest mercy; it is confirmed there.

## Guardianship and knowledge

The second movement names the walaya of ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, his love, and his station. It cites Jaʿfar ibn Manṣūr al-Yaman in the Book of the Foster-Suckling, where several scriptural phrases are read through the Imams. The phrase "God is his master, Jibrīl and the righteous of the believers" is interpreted as pointing to the Imam of the believers, who is ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, the guide of every believer. The phrase "their father was righteous" is read as meaning an Imam. The verse "the earth is inherited by My righteous servants" is read as referring to the Imams from among God's servants.

This method matters. The author is not only saying that the Imams are honored figures. He is showing how scripture becomes legible through their station. Words like "righteous" and "heirs" are not left as broad moral terms. They are tied to an office, a lineage, and a mode of inheritance. The earth is not inherited by anonymous virtue alone; in this reading, it is inherited through the righteous servants who carry the knowledge and authority of God among creation.

The chapter then recalls Mawlānā the Imam al-Muʿizz li-Dīn Allāh in a Friday sermon. He likens himself in the physical world to a pen in the spiritual world and sets four ranks beneath it, each with a spiritual rank guiding it toward the one beyond. The foundation faces the tablet and engraves in it what it derives from the spiritual world, just as a pen engraves in a tablet. The image gives a cosmological depth to guardianship. Guidance is not only historical succession. It is a correspondence between the seen and unseen orders, where knowledge descends, is inscribed, and is made available through proper ranks.

From there the chapter moves into stories of questions posed to the Father of Imams and to his household. The first story begins with a messenger from the king of Rūm who comes to Medina with money and a test. He is told to go to Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon him, and if the Prophet does not agree with him, to ask about his successor and put proof before him. The messenger asks three questions: what is not God's, what is not with God, and what God does not know. Abū Bakr grows angry and treats the questions as disbelief. ʿUmar responds in much the same way. ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās recognizes that the problem is not the questions but the inability to answer them, and he directs the man to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib.

The scene is deliberate. The test is not strength, wealth, or political possession. It is knowledge. The Father of Imams comes out with a pen behind his ear and ink on his finger, an image of active learning and judgment. When the messenger asks what is not God's, he answers: God has no partner. When asked what is not with God, he answers: injustice. When asked what God does not know, he answers: that the Messiah is the son of God, meaning that such a thing does not exist and God has no knowledge of what has no existence. The answers turn dangerous questions into precise theology. They protect divine unity, divine justice, and the truth that nonexistence is not a hidden fact inside God's knowledge.

The messenger's response is the point of the report: "I bear witness that there is no god but God, that Muhammad is the Messenger of God, and that you are his guardian." Knowledge reveals walaya. The money is then given to al-Ḥasan and al-Ḥusayn to distribute among the Muslims, so the proof of knowledge ends in generosity. Again the chapter refuses to separate doctrine from conduct. The right answer is followed by right distribution.

The second report takes place in al-Raḥba. A man greets the Commander of the Faithful and is discovered to be a messenger sent by Muʿāwiyah. He carries questions that Ibn al-Aṣfar had sent: the distance between truth and falsehood, the distance between heaven and earth, the distance between east and west, the meaning of the galaxy, the rainbow, the darkening of the moon, the first thing that appeared on earth, the first thing that moved upon it, the spring to which the souls of the polytheists flee, the hermaphrodite, and ten things each harder than the last. Before the questions are answered, the Father of Imams laments the people who severed his ties of kinship, wasted his days, denied his right, diminished his station, and conspired against him. The report therefore places knowledge inside injustice. The rightful knower has been wronged, yet his knowledge remains the proof.

He then calls al-Ḥasan, al-Ḥusayn, and Muḥammad, and tells the Syrian to ask whichever of them he wishes. The Syrian chooses al-Ḥasan. This is one of the chapter's quiet but powerful turns: guardianship is not a solitary brilliance that dies with one figure. The household itself is a school of inherited knowledge. Al-Ḥasan answers that the distance between truth and falsehood is four fingers: what the eyes see is truth, and what the ears may hear is often falsehood. Between heaven and earth are the prayer of the oppressed and the reach of sight. Between east and west is the arc of the sun in a single day. The galaxy is the lights of the sky from which rain descends. The rainbow is God's bow and a sign of safety from drowning.

He continues. The darkening of the moon is explained through the verse, "And We made the night and the day two signs, and We erased the sign of the night and made the sign of the day visible." The first thing to appear on the face of the earth is the valley of Dab; the first thing to move upon it is the palm tree; the spring to which the souls of the polytheists flee is Salma. The hermaphrodite is treated as a legal question of discernment, with signs by which the body is examined. The ten things ascend in force: stone, iron, fire, water, cloud, wind, the angel, the Angel of Death, death itself, and the command of God, Lord of all worlds.

The Syrian writes the answers down and leaves. Ibn al-Aṣfar recognizes that this knowledge does not come from Muʿāwiyah. It belongs to the substance of prophecy and the seat of the message. The report uses a foreign ruler's recognition to expose domestic illegitimacy: those who possess power do not thereby possess knowledge, and those whose right was diminished remain the seat of prophetic inheritance. In the logic of the chapter, knowledge is not an ornament of authority. Knowledge is one of the signs by which authority is known.

The third report brings ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb to the Father of Imams with questions he neglected to ask the Messenger of God, peace and blessings be upon him. He asks about dreams that are vivid and dreams that are nothing, about loving or hating a person without knowing why, and about remembering something for a long time, forgetting it when needed, and recalling it later. The answer begins with the verse, "God takes souls at the time of their death, and those that have not died, during their sleep; He keeps those for whom He has decreed death, and releases the others until an appointed term." Every sleeping person is touched by a likeness of death. What the soul sees while separated from the body and journeying through the realm is truth; what it sees upon its return is falsehood and the delusion of Satan.

Love and hatred are explained through the preexistence of souls. God created souls before bodies by two thousand years; they met and mingled like horses. What they came to know of one another then, they recognize and love now; what they came to dislike then, they dislike now. Forgetting is explained through the heart, which carries a scar like the scar of the moon. When the heart is covered over, the servant forgets what he has seen and heard. ʿUmar's closing words acknowledge dependence: may God not spare him a land where ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib is absent and his name is not mentioned.

Across these reports, the chapter teaches walaya through scenes rather than slogans. The Father of Imams and his household answer metaphysical, cosmological, legal, psychological, and scriptural questions. They know how to distinguish nonexistence from divine ignorance, how to connect celestial signs to revelation, how to judge bodily ambiguity, how to read dreams, and how to describe the memory of the heart. Their knowledge ranges because the religious life ranges. A guide who could only answer ritual details would not be sufficient for the world the chapter imagines. The household's authority is comprehensive because creation, scripture, law, and the soul all require interpretation.

The reports also protect the listener from confusing miracle with spectacle. The astonishing element is not that the questions are strange. The astonishing element is that each question is placed in order. "What is not God's?" could sound like impiety, yet the answer makes tawhid clearer: God has no partner. "What is not with God?" could sound like accusation, yet the answer protects divine justice: injustice is not with Him. "What does God not know?" could sound like a denial of omniscience, yet the answer clarifies that falsehood has no real object. The knower does not panic before difficult speech. He discerns what the speech is asking and restores it to truth.

The Syrian questions do the same at the level of creation. They range from truth and falsehood to the moon, the rainbow, geography, weather, law, bodily ambiguity, angelic force, death, and the command of God. A weaker report would simply make the household omniscient in a flat way. This report is more disciplined. The answers train the listener to see measures: four fingers between eye and ear; the prayer of the oppressed and the reach of sight between heaven and earth; the arc of a single day between east and west. Knowledge is not just possession of hidden facts. It is the power to measure creation rightly and to place each thing at its level.

The dream report then turns that same knowledge inward. Sleep is explained through the taking and returning of souls; love and hatred through the meeting of souls before bodies; memory and forgetfulness through the covering of the heart. The chapter is not offering these as scattered curiosities. It is showing that the guide's knowledge reaches the places ordinary people most often experience without understanding: the dream that feels true, the affection that arrives before reason, the hatred that seems to have no cause, the memory that disappears at the moment of need. Walaya matters because life is full of experiences whose meanings are not obvious from the surface.

For an audio episode, this section should let the reports breathe. They are not footnotes to a doctrinal thesis; they are how the thesis becomes imaginable. A listener can feel the difference between anger at a difficult question and knowledge that can answer it. A listener can feel the difference between political proximity to power and inherited proximity to the message. The chapter is teaching reverence, but it is also teaching criteria: recognize the one who knows how to answer without distortion, who gives wealth away after proof, who lets his sons answer because knowledge continues through the house, and who can explain the movements of the soul without breaking the unity of God.

## Peace and belonging

The final movement turns from proof of guardianship to the practice of prayer, greeting, and belonging. It names the walaya of the Imams, the family of the Messenger of God, peace and blessings be upon him, and the obligation of praying for Muhammad and the family of Muhammad. It also mentions the good proclaimers, the righteous borders, their stations, the command to return peace, and the etiquette of replying to greetings.

The twenty-third Mustaṇṣiriyya council is cited on the Sunnah of salām, the revelation of peace. The structure again uses seven and twelve. Above, peace is divided into seven sections: what comes from God and from His angels. Below, it is divided into twelve sections: what comes from human beings. The highest, first, most particular, most exalted, and most sincere is God's word, "Peace be upon you." The second is the address to Noah: "O Noah, descend in peace from Us." The third concerns the greeting on the day people meet God. The fourth concerns greetings and peace being conveyed. The fifth is the angels entering upon them from every gate and saying, "Peace be upon you." The sixth is the command that God and His angels pray for the Prophet, peace be upon him, followed by the address, "O you who believe, pray for him and submit to him in full submission." The seventh is, "Peace be upon the family of Yasin." Yasin is interpreted as the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him and his family, and the family of Yasin as the family of Muhammad.

The twenty-fourth assembly explains that the revelation of peace is an obligatory Sunnah of Islam, one that speaks of affection and submission. Salām is also one of the names of the One possessed of majesty and honor. This means greeting is not small talk. It is a practice in which divine name, social affection, and religious submission meet. To offer peace is to rehearse the order of the world: God gives peace, the angels carry peace, the faithful receive and return peace, and the community becomes visible through the words it exchanges.

The twelve human divisions begin with the greeting of the people of the right hand to the faithful prophet, peace be upon him and his noble family: "Peace be upon you for your patience." The next is the greeting upon entering houses, drawn from the verse, "When you enter houses, greet one another with a greeting from God, blessed and good." Then comes the peace of one who concludes the prayer. Then the peace of the believer in the face of the ignorant: "And when the ignorant address them, they say: peace." The chapter then gives an ordered etiquette: the rider on a camel initiates greeting to the rider on a horse; the rider on a horse to the rider on a donkey; the rider on a donkey to the one walking; the one walking to the one standing; the one standing initiates and returns.

The return itself has three kinds: the virtuous reply, the complete reply, and the just reply. The virtuous reply greets the initiator with something better. The complete reply responds in kind, following the verse, "And when you are greeted with a greeting, return it with something better, or return it as it is." The just reply concerns dhimmis, who do not initiate the greeting; if they do, one responds, "And upon you." The source explains this through a report that some deceived Muslims in their greeting, saying "Death be upon you" instead of "Peace be upon you." The Sunnah therefore answered, "And upon you," a just reply: whoever said something good received its like, and whoever said something bad received its like.

This etiquette may sound granular, but the chapter uses it to make a theological point. Peace is ordered. It descends from God and the angels, is embodied in prophetic and communal practice, and is returned according to justice, completeness, or excellence. A greeting can disclose affection; it can also conceal harm. The believer is trained to answer both without losing balance. The highest response is better than what was received, but the just response refuses to be naive about deception.

The chapter then shifts into prayers for the borders of religion. In a Sunday prayer, after praying for the higher borders, Mawlānā Amīr al-Muʾminīn asks God to reach the lower five: the spokesman, the foundation, the culmination, the successor, and the wing. In a Saturday prayer, he asks God to send the purest, most honorable, and most noble prayers upon the seven days and the twelve months. The saints of the religion are cited praying for the proclaimers and their gates, the imams of their stations, their proofs, and their preachers. A Monday supplication prays for the seven proclaimers, the seven and eighteen, the seven caliphs, and the twelve successors. A Tuesday supplication asks God to reach His manifest borders and righteous guardians, the spokesmen who call to His light, the proven foundations of the truth of His religion, the culmination, the successor, and the wing, through whom the gates of mercy were opened. Other prayers send holy prayers upon them, their gates, their proofs, and their prayers, or bless days, months, hours of day and night, both manifest and hidden.

These prayers show how the chapter imagines religious memory. The believer does not merely know a list of offices. The believer prays through them. The seven, the twelve, the gates, proofs, preachers, foundations, successors, and wings become part of daily devotion. Cosmology becomes liturgy. The ordered hierarchy of guidance is carried on the tongue, not as dry classification but as blessing, gratitude, and orientation.

The Four Hundred Counsels then return the subject to ordinary behavior: "When someone greets you, return the greeting with something better than it, or return it in kind." The counsel continues: shake hands with your enemy if he dislikes it, for God has commanded His servants to repel with what is better until the enemy becomes like a close friend. If a brother says, "May God greet you with peace," the answer should be: "You are the peace of God, and the finest of abodes and stations." The chapter has moved from divine salām to social courage. Peace is not sentimental avoidance. It can be an active discipline toward an enemy, a way of answering hostility with a better form without pretending hostility is not there.

The last cited exchange concerns a question from India about bowing or lowering the head before sheikhs, professors, and those close to them, instead of proclaiming peace with the hands. Some worried that this resembled the practice of unbelievers. The answer is brief: offering peace is better. Another question asks how much of a greeting a Muslim must hear, whether replying is obligatory, and what is required when someone sneezes and praises God. The answer says none of these is required by duty and one may do as one wishes, except for replying to a greeting, whether aloud or silently. The law does not inflate every courtesy into obligation, but the return of salām remains binding.

This answer is modest, and its modesty is important. After the grandeur of the seven heavenly forms of peace, the twelve human divisions, the prayers for borders, and the cosmic language of days and months, the practical ruling could have become heavy with anxiety. Instead, the source distinguishes better practice from legal duty. Offering peace is better than substitute gestures of deference. Replying to a greeting remains required. But not every related courtesy becomes a burden. In this way the chapter shows a law that can honor hierarchy without turning reverence into confusion, and can honor courtesy without making every social detail obligatory.

The salām material also completes the earlier teaching on faith. If faith requires knowledge and action, then greeting becomes a small action with large meaning. If faith requires walaya, then prayers upon Muhammad and his family and remembrance of the religious borders locate the believer within the line of guidance. If faith requires social obligation, then returning peace is one of the most ordinary ways that obligation is fulfilled. A person may never face a messenger from Rūm, a Syrian carrying questions from Ibn al-Aṣfar, or a caliphal inquiry about dreams and souls. But every person will be greeted, ignored, irritated, approached by a neighbor, confronted by an enemy, or asked to respond with more grace than he received. The chapter brings the doctrine there.

There is a subtle movement from hierarchy to reciprocity. The etiquette of who initiates greeting recognizes rank and circumstance: rider to rider, walker to standing person, one condition to another. But the reply opens a door to excellence. One may return the same greeting, but the more virtuous response is better than what was received. The chapter therefore holds order and generosity together. Order prevents chaos; generosity prevents order from becoming cold. Justice answers deception with its like; virtue answers goodness with more than its like. The believer must know which response belongs where.

The prayers for borders deepen this reciprocity into remembrance. When the source names spokesmen, foundations, culminations, successors, wings, gates, proofs, preachers, and guardians, the listener may hear a technical hierarchy. But in the prayers, these are not merely positions to memorize. They become recipients of blessing and channels through which mercy is remembered. The same mouth that returns a neighbor's greeting also sends prayers through the sacred order. This is why the section belongs in the chapter of faith. Speech is being trained: to confess, to greet, to bless, to return, to answer, and to remember.

The chapter closes by returning to the transmitted teaching of the Imams of the family of Muhammad, peace be upon him. Jaʿfar ibn Manṣūr al-Yaman is again cited on the verse that the earth is inherited by God's righteous servants, meaning the Imams among His servants. He also reads the verse about God wishing to be gracious to those oppressed in the land and to make them Imams and heirs as referring to the descendants of ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, who were made weak in the land in his very person. He is the proof of Muhammad, and the earth is as he is. The chapter then mentions al-Muʿizz li-Dīn Allāh in a Sunday supplication connected with the completion of the prayer of Raphael.

That ending may feel abrupt, but it reveals the chapter's thread. Faith began with duties to parents, kin, neighbor, and the needy. It passed through walaya and the proof of knowledge. It ended with salām, prayers upon the Prophet and his family, the borders of religion, and the etiquette of returning peace. The teaching is not a loose collection. It is an opening foundation: to have faith is to recognize the guided line, act with knowledge, fulfill obligations, and let even a greeting become part of submission.

The abruptness also tells us something about how the source expects to proceed. It is not trying to exhaust any one subject in isolation. It is gathering the foundations from which later rulings can be read. Faith is defined, walaya is centered, the household's knowledge is displayed, salām is ordered, and the borders of religion are prayed over. The chapter is laying stones, not finishing the whole building. That is why the episode should land with a sense of foundation rather than closure. The listener should feel prepared to enter the book's later pillars because the first pillar has already been set: a ruling is not merely an instruction, but part of a guided life.

## What this episode lands

This episode lands on a definition of faith as a complete formed life. The source will later arrange rulings according to the pillars of Islam, but it begins by making sure the listener knows what gives those rulings life: walaya first, then purity, prayer, zakāt, fasting, Ḥajj, and jihad; then the social obligations by which faith becomes visible in family, neighborhood, household, and community.

The chapter also establishes the book's governing claim that knowledge and guidance belong to the Ahl al-Bayt and the ordered ranks of religion. The reports about ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, al-Ḥasan, and the questions from Rūm, Syria, and ʿUmar are not decorative marvels. They show that the rightful guide is recognized through knowledge: of God, scripture, creation, law, dreams, souls, and the heart. The final teaching on salām then brings that doctrine down into daily speech. The believer returns peace, prays for the Prophet and his family, remembers the borders of religion, and refuses to let faith remain a name without knowledge or action.
