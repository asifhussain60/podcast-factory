## Where this episode picks up

Can a man still owe zakat after he is dead — and can the answer be yes even when everything he left behind is a house, its furniture, and the people who were living in it? This chapter opens on exactly that, and answers without hesitation. The estate is opened, the obligations come out in a fixed order, and the heirs receive whatever survives the subtraction, which may be nothing at all.

The previous chapter defined the obligation and deliberately declined to price it. This one prices it. Everything here is counting — silver, gold, months, camels, sheep, measures of grain, and the order in which five claims come out of a sum too small for all of them. Four source headings carry the reckoning: zakat on gold, silver and jewels; on livestock; on grains, fruits and plants; and zakat al-fitr. They move outward from the coin in a man's hand to the animal in his field, the grain in his ground, and finally a payment levied on persons rather than property.

The authorities are many. The book of questions and answers arranged according to the order of the chapters of the book The Pillars of Islam supplies most of the case law. Our master Idris — may God sanctify his soul — answers the hardest question on profit and supplies a summary of the classification; our master Sulayman ibn Hasan is asked about seeds and our master Dawud ibn Ajab answers; our master Qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad is cited from the Book of Himma and the Keys of Grace, Imam al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah, peace be upon him, from the Book of Interpretation of Sharia, our master Ja'far ibn our master Mansur al-Yaman for a fivefold scheme, and our master Zayd ibn Muhammad for the numbers, from the work the source calls the hidden message. The holder of the councils of al-Mustansiriyya — may God's peace rest upon its author — returns in the thirteenth council.

A warning about the surface of this slice, the roughest the series has handled. Dirham weights are given in carats that do not resolve; ounce-figures trail phrases that have lost their nouns; a unit of currency arrives only as "tanks"; the camel scale runs through a vocabulary of daughters, rights and trunks that is plainly damaged. This chapter reproduces what the source gives and says so. A repaired number would read better and would not be the book's own.

One conviction runs under all four headings. Zakat here is charged against wealth capable of increase, at the moment and in the manner of its increase — which is why a year must pass over money, why an animal that feeds itself is liable and an animal that is fed is not, and why the rate on a field falls by half when a man lifts the water onto it himself.

## Coin, Debt, and the Threshold

The heading opens on a death and a guardian's dilemma. A man dies having appointed two guardians, and one of them fears the second will neglect what the estate owes in khums, zakat, Hajj and the rest, so he pays it himself, secretly. Is there anything wrong with that? If the guardian discharged what was owed on behalf of the deceased there is nothing wrong with it, because zakat is a debt that must be settled first, then Hajj — and if the saints are not settled, nothing else clears his obligation, whether secretly or publicly.

The answer is not about trust between two men but about the ranking of claims, and the ranking is asked for directly a few questions later. A man dies leaving two hundred dinars, more or less, liable for zakat, Hajj, khums, al-Sharwa and expiation — which comes first? He begins with zakat, because that is a debt owed from his wealth; then Hajj, then khums, then expiation, then al-Sharwa. And the corollary is stated without softening: the Hajj obligation takes precedence over inheritance, because it is a debt, and debt comes before inheritance; the other duties are likewise obligatory if something remains in the estate, even if nothing is left for the heirs.

The surrounding questions push on that clause. A man did not pay the fifth and then died — must everything left, the house, the servants, the common goods, be set aside? Yes: the khums must be taken out of his entire estate. A man reached the threshold in life but paid nothing, and at death owned only the goods he left behind — if the estate reaches the threshold of what was subject to sale, trade goods and commerce, zakat is due on it, and Hajj is discharged after zakat. And a man whose estate proves sufficient for Hajj, expiation, al-Shurwa and khums, though he had little in life beyond the house and its furnishings: the obligations are discharged from it and the heirs receive what remains. The estate is the man's last statement of what he owned, and it is allowed to convict him.

Two smaller rulings sit alongside. A man does not pay zakat or deliver it to the one commanded to receive it — is it permissible to eat food in his house? There is nothing wrong with it; the defect is in his obligation, not in his property. And where an estate is settled without a bequest, khums must still be taken from the capital; if the heirs are pious it is their duty to discharge it, and if they refrain, there is no compulsion in religion.

One question here has not survived. It concerns a collector who takes ten dirhams from polytheists for every two dirhams and five from Muslims for every dirham received and forgives the remainder, an owner of a cloth, a fardah of one or two dirhams, and a proposal to write the unforgiven portion in another man's name on condition that what remains is divided equally. What survives is a principle rather than a resolution: what the partners have agreed upon is permissible.

Then the clock. Whoever pays zakat two years in advance has the second year's obligation waived. And a man with two hundred mithqals who pays at the beginning of the year, leaves one hundred idle at home and invests the rest: at the second year, does he pay on two hundred or one hundred? What if he buys a commodity on the understanding that it is held, and its price falls? Zakat remains obligatory on what is liable as long as it remains in the owner's possession — whether he trades with it or holds it — until what he owns falls below the threshold. Liability follows possession, not activity.

So the threshold has to be fixed, and here the text turns difficult. It is stated in al-Masa'il al-Sharwa' that the threshold is one hundred and nineteen dirhams, and that for those of modest means it is one dinar from our master, while for those of fewer means there is nothing — so what does a dinar weigh, and a dirham? Our master said: the threshold for the people of means, in wealth on which zakat is due after a year, is either one hundred and ninety-five dirhams of silver — each dirham being fourteen carats of silver and five carats — or twenty gold dinars. On this reckoning the weight of one threshold of silver is eighteen ounces by Arab weights, ninety-five and a quarter, which is a quarter of a debt and a quarter. As for gold, each dinar is twenty-four carats, and the weight of twenty dinars is three ounces of gold by Arab weights — that is sixteen portions of a religious debt and a half.

Both ounce-figures trail phrases that have lost their nouns; the two anchor numbers are intact.

What follows is unusually alert to the market. Look at how much this silver is worth in the common currency of the day: whoever owns that amount or more is counted among those of means. On gold there is a difference of opinion, because the price of the two metals varies at all times with people's demand for one over the other — twenty dinars may be worth more than two hundred dirhams, or the reverse. When that is the case, the believer may be assessed against whichever of the two currencies is the less costly.

Two thresholds describe one condition and the rate between them keeps moving, so the ruling takes the cheaper measure — which pulls more people into liability rather than fewer. The figures do not agree in any case: the question cited one hundred and nineteen dirhams, and later some of the guardians of God — peace be upon them — are reported as saying that a person of means can afford one hundred and nineteen mithqals of gold, and whoever cannot, one hundred and nineteen dirhams of silver. Three figures for one line, reconciled nowhere.

The hardest question concerns profit rather than capital. A man owes one-fifth of one hundred tanks, takes out five in the first year, and is left with four hundred; he trades and gains three hundred, household expenses consume two hundred, and one hundred of the principal remains. Does the fifth come out of the three hundred or the one hundred? From our master Idris: if he has already paid the fifth on the capital, it is not due on the profit except on what remains in his hands; if he pays it on everything, that is a virtue and not an obligation. The unit is a casualty of transmission, but the principle is plain: a man is not billed for money he has already lived on.

Our master Idris then supplies a summary of the classification: zakat is obligatory when wealth reaches the threshold on which it is due, is free of encumbrances, and a full year has passed over it. The cases test each condition at its edge. If a depositor alters a deposit, introducing something that removes it from the ruling of a deposit, or consumes it and makes it as if it were his own money, zakat is obligatory on him for all of it. If a year passes over a dead man's estate before the heirs have divided it, zakat is due not on the whole estate but on each heir's share: whoever's share reaches the threshold pays, and whoever's does not — or who has no other wealth — does not. If a man's wealth exceeds what he owes and a year passes while he is prevented from reaching it, then if he sells it voluntarily he must pay on the excess over his debt. For trade goods acquired in exchange for other trade goods, the second item's year is counted from the time he owned the first, whether or not he was fair in the exchange; the same applies if he bought them with cash on which zakat was payable — if the year arrives while the goods are in his hands, he pays on his money. Changing an asset's form does not restart its clock.

Three rulings close it. Whoever acquires wealth that is good for him and then finds it should pay zakat on it. An apostate receives zakat but does not owe it on what he held during his apostasy. And there is no zakat on an orphan's wealth or on the wealth of one lacking legal capacity — none on what such a person keeps for his own use, but what is held for trade is subject to it. The exemption protects the vulnerable, not the fund.

From extracted metals, one-fifth is due and the remainder belongs to the finder; one-fifth is due on ore; and dinars found along the routes of Islam are governed by the ruling of found property. And a question from the early period of our master Sulayman ibn Hasan, answered by our master Dawud ibn Ajab: if a person has sown five ounces of his wealth, must he give one-fifth? Yes — may God bless you — one-fifth of the seeds is obligatory. The clay, as Allah Almighty said: ⟪ar:وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّمَا غَنِمْتُم مِّن شَيْءٍ فَأَنَّ لِلَّهِ خُمُسَهُ⟫ — "Know that whatever you take as booty, a fifth of it belongs to Allah" (chapter 8, verse 41). These seeds fall within the category of "thing", as our master Qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad — may God sanctify his soul — explained in the Book of Himma. The verse names booty and then says "of anything", and the ruling takes the second phrase as operative.

Then the sum is given a meaning rather than a measure. In the Book of Interpretation of Sharia, Imam al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah — peace be upon him — was asked about zakat. He said it consists of five dirhams, and the poor are of five types, each category receiving a dirham, for zakat is a consolation; all people and all creation are like one soul, and when one limb of the body suffers, all the other limbs attend to it in order to relieve its pain. If God afflicts any of the children of the human race, it is the duty of the rest of humanity to cure him, each according to what he is able.

The word doing the work is consolation. And a hard coda follows. A man was mentioned, and it was said that there is nothing wrong with him; he — peace be upon him — said that if no defect had touched him, death would not have found him. Nobody is the healthy limb.

Our master the late preacher Ja'far ibn our master Mansur al-Yaman then sets the whole levy out in fives, in the first book of the work the source here calls Breastfeeding the Subconscious — the title the previous chapter received in another damaged form. The five prayers are known at five times, and zakat likewise has five forms. First, one-fifth of all wealth earned through trade, sales and gains. Second, zakat on wealth after a year has passed over it — one-fifth of forty dinars — which is obligatory for the Hajj and the khums for the Imam. Third, zakat on crops, which is one-tenth. Fourth, zakat on livestock: from forty heads, one head. Fifth, zakat of the soul, which is what the believer performs for himself to expiate his sins, to free his neck, and from his nature — fasting after these four obligatory zakats.

Four levies fall on property; the fifth falls on the person and is paid in fasting rather than coin.

The numbers close the section. Our master Zayd ibn Muhammad said in the hidden message: from a holding of gold, twenty mithqals obliges half a mithqal; whoever owns two hundred dirhams owes five dirhams — the two hundred are two covenants. There is no zakat on one who does not own twenty mithqals, nor on one who does not own two hundred dirhams. Whoever owns two hundred dirhams is forbidden to receive zakat or charity. And the owner of gold does not owe zakat on silver unless it is permissible to combine them.

Two rates at the same proportion, a fortieth of each — and the figure that makes a man liable to pay also disqualifies him from being paid.

## Grazing Herds and Watered Ground

Livestock is liable on a condition that has nothing to do with quantity. In the book Mukhtasar al-Musannaf: there is no zakat on livestock until they have grazed freely. If they graze in some of the year and are fed in other parts of it, the majority of rulings hold that whoever has five camels owes nothing on them in the first part of the year — one sheep is due — and nothing more in the second, because in the second a sheep becomes due as our religion stipulates. If a camel owner owes a camel and has a sheep of his own flock acceptable as a sacrifice, that is also permissible. Buffaloes and cows are treated in zakat the same as sheep and goats.

Those clauses about the first and second parts of the year contradict themselves in the form they reach us and are given as they stand. What survives clearly is the rule that makes a herd liable at all: an animal that feeds itself on open pasture has increased its owner's wealth without his expenditure, while an animal that is fed was paid for out of the purse the levy would come from. And in the interpretation of these pillars, if a given level yields nothing then nothing is due on it.

Then the scale. Ja'far ibn Mansur al-Yaman, in the Book of Sub-Suckling: the livestock zakat is from forty heads, one head from among them. And at length, from our master Zayd ibn Muhammad in the hidden message.

There is no charity on one who does not have five camels. Once five camels are completed and owned, one sheep of the flock is obligatory on him for every five up to twenty, and four are paid. When he reaches twenty-five he must give a daughter of labour; the daughter of labour is the one whose mother bore a child in her womb. Above ten, when he reaches thirty-five, a daughter of Labun is due. At forty-five, a right of the stallion is due. Then with every additional five above ten, and when one is added to sixty, a trunk is due in it; above the trunk, something is taken in charity. In sixty, one of the camels is a stallion that resembles a stallion and can bear what it can bear. If the number reaches one hundred and twenty camels, two rights are due, because one hundred and twenty is two times sixty. If the number of cows reaches thirty, they are sold; if it reaches forty, an old cow is due; in sixty, three annals are due; in one hundred and twenty, in every forty an old cow and in every thirty a sold one. When forty sheep are reached, one sheep is charity. At one hundred and twenty, one sheep is due. If it reaches two hundred sheep, two sheep are due; and for every hundred sheep, one sheep is due. Nothing is taken in charity that causes shame. No male sheep is taken in charity unless the believer wishes it.

That is the most damaged passage in the chapter and it is given whole rather than corrected. "Daughter of labour" and "daughter of Labun" may be one term twice transmitted; the cow scale says that thirty head "are sold", almost certainly a name for the animal owed rather than an instruction to sell; "three annals" at sixty is not recoverable. Supplying the standard scales here would be supplying another book's text.

What is legible is the shape. Liability begins at five camels, and below twenty-five the payment is made in sheep — a small herd is not broken into to satisfy a levy on itself. Above that it becomes a camel of a specified kind, rising as the herd rises. The closing restrictions are protections rather than rates: nothing that causes shame, and no male sheep without the owner's wish — the collector's hand kept off the animal the flock depends on.

Grain, fruit and plants are measured next. The threshold of gold is twenty dinars and of silver one hundred dirhams — how does this work for grain? It is stated in the Pillars that zakat is paid from everything the earth produces, and that is the position of our senior loyalists, so it is one's duty to follow their example; they took one-tenth of what was little or much, and that is a virtue. It is stated in the Sharia that the threshold for grains is five awsaq and sixty sa's.

Both answers are kept: a tenth of everything the ground yields however small, commended as a virtue; and a floor of five awsaq, one wasq reckoned at sixty sa's, repeated in the landmarks of religion and the section on the chosen view. It is also held there that zakat is obligatory on all grains resembling wheat and dates, and that a combination of grains and fruits where neither reaches half the threshold is not liable.

In al-Akhbar: a sow was not sold, meaning grain on which zakat is due; what is sold before it is contracted, zakat is not due on it, and the same was narrated regarding fruits. That first clause is damaged and reproduced as it stands. The owner of the land does not deduct what he spent on it — the levy falls on gross yield, not profit — and in a summary of the work the obligation falls on the young and the old, the rich and the poor, and those who carry a debt. Where money was concerned a debt could cancel liability entirely; on a harvest it does not. The grain exists.

Then a distinction about land rather than crops. From the Book of Akhbar: tithe land is all land of the Arabs on which Muslims did not settle through conquest or passage, and all land of the mountains whose people extracted and that was not overflowed by great rivers. Kharaj land is the land of Sudan and the mountain territories the Muslims conquered by force of cavalry and infantry; where people at war surrendered their lands, those became subject to the tithe. If a Muslim buys land from a dhimmi, its ruling does not change by the transfer — it remains subject to kharaj. If a dhimmi buys land from a Muslim, the tithe becomes obligatory upon him when produce is extracted, because what is obligatory is determined by what the earth yields, not by who owns it. The status belongs to the ground, not to the man standing on it.

Water decides the rate. If a field was irrigated partly by one source and partly by another, the ruling follows the majority source; if it was moderate — equally irrigated by both — three-quarters of a tenth is taken. And from our master Zayd ibn Muhammad in the hidden message: the tenth is obligatory on that which the heavens water and the rivers water; as for what is irrigated by channels and buckets, half the tenth is due. He also names five categories — dates, raisins, wheat, barley and grain — and says that if they reach five awsaq, half an awsaq of charity is due. In the summary of effects the tithe becomes obligatory on grain when it forms kernels, and is taken when the grain's soundness becomes apparent and beneficial to its owners: the claim fixed when the wealth comes into being, the taking delayed until it can happen without ruining it.

The book on breastfeeding then reads the tithe as a disclosure rather than a rate. The tenth is an established ruling that the Messenger of Allah, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him and his family, implemented — it is collected from the faithful and from those whose wealth is visible. Whoever pays a tenth of his apparent gain has truly discharged an apparent obligation. If he retains only nine-tenths he has done what he was commanded, even if his authority did not see any request upon him. Whoever exploits his gain and retains more than nine-tenths has not rightly fulfilled the tithe he was commanded and ordered to pay, and has not chosen to obey his authority in accordance with the truth.

The test is a plain fraction of what a man keeps, and one who holds more has not merely underpaid: he has not chosen to obey, which moves the failure from the ledger to the will.

The khums returns at the close, on the same verse. Allah has imposed obligations concerning the five shares of war spoils: ⟪ar:وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّمَا غَنِمْتُم مِّن شَيْءٍ فَأَنَّ لِلَّهِ خُمُسَهُ وَلِلرَّسُولِ وَلِذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ⟫ — meaning one fifth goes to five categories among whom the khums is distributed (chapter 8, verse 41). The khums is also taken from minerals and treasures, and from war spoils. And from Ja'far ibn Muhammad al-Sadiq, may Allah's prayers be upon him, it is transmitted that when spoils came to the Messenger of Allah, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him and his family, he would take his share and then divide the remainder among the relatives, orphans, the poor and the wayfarer.

## The Charity of the Head

The last levy is not charged on property at all, and the first question about it is about substitution. The common practice among Indian scholars is to combine four types — rice, corn, barley and salt — and take their monetary value. But in the Shari'ah zakat al-fitr is a sa' of dates, raisins, barley or wheat. Is a sa' of barley sufficient? The majority view, as mentioned in the Pillars, is that it is a sa' of barley, wheat, dates or raisins — and the guiding principle is that it should be from the best food one eats and the best food one stores. As for combining different grains, whatever one ate during the past month of Ramadan is divided proportionally, and whoever ate nothing other than barley will have fulfilled it with barley.

The guiding principle is the whole of it: the payment is measured against what the payer himself ate, at his own best, so rich and poor pay the same volume of different things and neither is asked for money.

Timing is the second question, asked by a careful man. He wants to pay before Ramadan begins, fearing that distance or travel will prevent him at the proper time — but the noble Shari'ah holds that obligatory duties are performed at their appointed time and made up only after being missed, so does he get reward for paying early? It is mentioned in the Book of Pillars that it is permissible to accept zakat in advance from those obligated to pay it, if there is a need, and such a person is blessed with the answer. And if he does it out of ignorance rather than need? If he submits his zakat al-fitr to one entitled to receive it, it is permissible, because the prohibition here is that a person should not break his fast before submitting it — and there is no delay in that. The obligation is to deliver it. The questioner assumed a fixed appointment; the reply says the guarded boundary is the far one.

The boundary is then fixed precisely. In Mukhtasar al-Musannaf: if a dhimmi submits his payment before the dawn of the day of al-Fitr he has discharged his zakat al-fitr; if he submits it after dawn he has not. In the transmitted reports: zakat al-fitr is paid on behalf of servants. In the landmarks of religion: if a newborn is born before the break of dawn on the day of al-Fitr, the obligation applies to him. And in the Book of Pillars it was also said that it is to be paid on behalf of the foetus before he is born.

A child a few minutes old is a head, and a head owes a measure of food: the levy counts persons, not earners, and on one reading it reaches back past birth. Measures for the unit come in three competing systems: in the chosen view, one sa', or half a sa' of wheat; in the landmarks of religion, a sa' weighs four and a half pounds by the Makkan measure, one pound by the Madinan measure equals five pounds less one third, and the Baghdadi weight is nine pounds and a fraction. They do not reconcile and are set down as the source sets them down.

Then the thirteenth council, from our master and Mawlana, the holder of the councils of al-Mustansiriyya — may God's peace rest upon its author. Having mentioned the zakat obligatory on the owners of wealth, he follows it with the zakat obligatory on every person individually, given as charity by those who have little: zakat al-fitr. It has seven obligatory elements and twelve recommended ones. The obligatory elements are: first, that a man pays it for himself; second, for his older children; third, for his younger children; fourth, for those among them who are free; fifth, for those who are enslaved; sixth, for the males among them; seventh, for the females. The recommended practice is that it be given from the staple food one eats — a sa' of barley, a sa' of wheat, a sa' of dates, or a sa' of raisins — and he is rewarded with half a sa' of wheat; it should not be given from anything other than these except in a sa'-for-sa' measure. Four provisions apply: it should be paid to the Imam, peace be upon him, or to one appointed by the Imam; it should be taken out before breaking the fast; it is to be spent from the moment it is taken out; and one should not give its equivalent in paper or gold. If a woman has no husband present, she pays it for herself and for those who depend on her.

The council announces twelve recommended elements and supplies a handful; the count is left as it stands, as the previous council's list of sunnahs was left with its own irregularity rather than tidied. The seven obligatory elements are not seven rules but one rule applied seven times, walking through every division a household might use to exclude somebody — age, freedom, sex — and refusing each in turn. And the provisions answer the question the heading opened with, against the convenience: never the equivalent in paper or gold.

The last authorities turn to the household standing outside the system. In the book on supplementary nursing topics, on the saying of the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him and his family, that natural disposition does not permit me or my family to receive it — our master Zayd ibn Muhammad stated in the hidden message, may God sanctify his soul: the charity of fitr is obligatory upon the Messenger of Allah, peace be upon him and his family, and upon all others. One must discharge it on behalf of males and females, young and old, even a child born on that very day, and on behalf of the foetus in his mother's womb; he made it obligatory at the rate of one sa' per head of wheat, dates, raisins, barley or other grains.

The pairing is exact. The family barred from receiving this charity — an exclusion established earlier in the series — is not barred from paying it, and the same passage lays that obligation on the Messenger of Allah and upon all others. The prohibition was always about where a payment goes.

The remaining rulings fix the hour and the name. The charity of fitr is called the charity of the head. Given before the prayer it is zakat al-fitr, given after it is merely voluntary charity. If a newborn is born after the Fitr prayer, his fitr is to be discharged before the next Fitr. And in the book of the Keys of Grace, attributed to our master Judge al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad, may God sanctify him: if it is paid before sunrise it is zakat al-fitr; if after sunrise it is charity, according to what has been transmitted. The two accounts set the line at different moments — the prayer in one, sunrise in the other — and both are recorded. What they agree on is that the same food, the same recipient and the same act change category by the clock: a man who is late has performed a different act and left the required one unperformed. And the meaning of natural disposition here, the source adds, is to discharge one's duty to God.

Two rulings close the chapter, both reaching us with their questions partly detached from their answers. A man for whom zakat has been given: in this case a man who possesses a thousand dinars and also owes a thousand dinars in debt, and therefore owes no zakat on it. Wealth exactly matched by liability is not wealth for this purpose — set that against the harvest a few pages earlier, where debt did not excuse the farmer because the grain had grown whatever he owed. And second: a man obligated to pay zakat but who is himself wealthy — meaning a man living among polytheists who could not find a Muslim on whom to spend it, and who is himself the person most deserving of it. The chapter ends there, on a man holding a payment he owes with nobody in reach to receive it.

## What this episode lands

This chapter did the counting the last one refused to do, in four currencies: coin, animal, measure of grain, and head.

On money, the rules turn on possession and time. A year must pass, the wealth must reach the threshold and be free of encumbrances, and afterwards it makes no difference whether the money worked or sat. Swapping one asset for another does not restart the clock; debt equal to the holding cancels liability outright. The threshold is one hundred and ninety-five dirhams of silver or twenty gold dinars, assessed against the cheaper when their prices diverge — though two further figures for the same line are recorded and none reconciled. And a death extinguishes none of it: the claims come out of the estate in order — zakat, Hajj, khums, expiation, al-Sharwa — and the heirs take what is left, if anything is.

On herds and fields, one principle governs both: the levy is charged against increase that arrived on its own. Livestock are liable only once they have grazed freely; a field watered by sky or river pays a tenth and a field watered by channel and bucket half of that. Liability on grain begins at the kernel and collection waits for soundness. The camel scale starts at five and is paid in sheep until twenty-five, cows run in bands of thirty and forty, sheep begin at forty and thereafter add one per hundred, and the rules closing it restrain the collector rather than the payer. Land carries its own status, tithe or kharaj, through every sale. And a man who keeps more than nine-tenths of his apparent gain has not underpaid; he has declined to obey.

On the end of the fast, nothing is calculated from property. Every person owes a measure of the food he actually ate, counted by head — his own, his children's, the free and the enslaved in his house, male and female, a child born that morning, and on one reading a child not yet born. It goes to the Imam or his appointee, before the fast is broken, and never as money in place of food. Paid before the line it is zakat al-fitr; paid after, it is a voluntary gift and the obligation stands unperformed. The household forbidden to receive it is not excused from paying it.

The manuscript is damaged in more places here than anywhere else in the series, and this chapter names each rather than repairing it: the collector's question about the fardah and the cloth; the ounce-figures trailing phrases that have lost their nouns; the unit that reaches us only as "tanks"; the camel vocabulary of daughters, rights and trunks; the cow that is "sold" at thirty; the clause in al-Akhbar about a sow; the council that promises twelve recommended elements and supplies a few; and the title of Ja'far ibn Mansur al-Yaman's book, arriving in a third damaged form. The numbers that carry the rulings are intact; the ones that are not have been left as the book has them.
