## Where this episode picks up

What if the prayer is finished, but the test of prayer has only just begun? This chapter opens at that strange threshold. The worshipper has stood, recited, bowed, prostrated, and given the salutation; the public form is complete. Yet the source immediately asks what should happen after that completion: whether one supplicates after ʿAṣr, whether the face is wiped at the place of prostration, how the hands turn during duʿāʾ, and why the culmination of prayer is not simply another spoken wish. From there the chapter moves back into the prayer itself, testing speech, gesture, bodily encumbrance, recitation, and finally the garments and surfaces in which the prayer is performed.

Earlier episodes already established the prayer's outward shape, the sanctity of its times, the discipline of attention, the form of the summons, and the shared boundaries of congregation. This chapter should not reopen those doctrines from the beginning. Its new contribution is narrower and more concrete: it asks how prayer is protected at the edges. The edge after the salutation becomes supplication. The edge inside the act becomes restraint in speech and motion. The edge around the body becomes dress, covering, ornament, images, impurity, and the ground of prostration. The chapter is therefore a manual of thresholds. It cares about the moment after prayer, the small movement during prayer, the object in the hand, the hair under the turban, the image on the rug, the exposed limb, the impure garment, the stone beneath the forehead, and the narrowness of a single cloth.

The source's shape is legal and devotional at once. It does not present a debate between named opponents. It gathers question-and-answer rulings, transmitted maxims, references to the interpretation of the Sharīʿa, the Mustanṣiriyya Councils, the Musannaf, the answers of Yemeni and Indian authorities, and sayings attributed to the Prophet, peace be upon him, and Amīr al-Mu'minīn Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him. The result is not a loose list. It is a sustained teaching about the prayer's surrounding dignity: when the servant asks, how the servant remains silent, what the servant carries, what the servant wears, and upon what the servant places his forehead before Allah.

## The Answerable Plea

The first question concerns supplication after the prayer, especially after the afternoon prayer. A questioner asks what is meant by statements in the books of the guiding call that supplication is not recited after the afternoon prayer. Is the ruling about the special supplication of drawing near, or about all supplications? The answer is concise: it refers to drawing near and to other acts, and no one is obligated to commit a sin through supplication. That last clause matters. The source is not trying to make duʿāʾ sound suspect. It is trying to place supplication inside inherited order, so that eagerness does not become self-directed improvisation.

A second question presses the same point more practically. Should the worshipper supplicate after the obligatory ʿAṣr prayer or not, and should he wipe his face at the place of prostration during every prayer or not? The answer says that it is not permitted until after ʿAṣr and Fajr, and that the loyal ones prescribed wiping after ʿAṣr and Fajr in particular. They received this from the pure Imams, may God's blessings be upon them. The source also preserves a softer note: a person's lifespan is not prolonged by a supplication made after ʿAṣr, yet if he prolongs it, he bears no sin for that. The ruling is therefore not a simple ban on asking. It marks the difference between received practice, devotional extension, and the mistaken thought that a post-ʿAṣr supplication mechanically lengthens life.

This is where the chapter turns from rule to meaning. In the interpretation of the Sharīʿa, daytime prayer is recited silently, night prayer is voiced in the depth of darkness, and dawn is announced like glass catching the light. The episode on sacred times has already taught the architecture of day, night, dawn, and certainty, so this chapter only needs a callback: prayer is never detached from time. Here that time-consciousness becomes the threshold of supplication. The body that prayed silently by day or aloud by night does not then spill into any kind of post-prayer speech it desires. Even the afterword of prayer has a discipline.

The Mustanṣiriyya Councils give the theological key. After completing the discussion of prayer's jurisprudence, where the outward has its exposition and the inward has its signs, the teaching follows with the mention of supplication, "which is the culmination of prayer, and what it contains." The word culmination should slow the listener down. Supplication is not a decorative tail added to a finished act. It is the place where the servant's completed standing, recitation, bowing, and prostration become explicit dependence. Prayer has just trained the body; duʿāʾ trains the need. Prayer has bound the worshipper to inherited form; duʿāʾ binds the worshipper's desires to repentance, praise, blessing, authority, and refuge.

The source then names seven forms of supplication. In the supplication of appeal, the supplicant spreads his hands with the inside of the palms facing the face and calls for help against what he fears, then turns the palms so their backs face the face. In the supplication of seeking refuge, he turns his palms outward. In the supplication of al-istikāna, he clasps his shoulders with his hands, and this is done only in solitude. The supplication with the rosary is one in which the finger counts the beads. Supplication by moving the finger is done by moving it. In the supplication of raising, he stretches his palms outward and extends his hands upward toward the sky. In the supplication of al-taʿaffur, he supplicates while pressing his cheeks to the ground.

These gestures could be heard as small liturgical details, but the source frames them as roots. It says that each of these seven supplications has mention in the Qurʾān, and that what has been mentioned consists of roots, like the roots of a tree waiting to branch out and bear fruit. The twelve are the principles and foundations of supplication. The image is generous: the seven gestures are not isolated motions; they are root-forms. Appeal, refuge, lowliness, counting, finger-motion, raising, and dust-marked humility become different bodily grammars of need. A palm turned inward, a palm turned outward, a shoulder clasped in solitude, a cheek pressed to earth, all speak before Allah in different ways.

The source then gives twelve foundations for the person who wishes his supplication to be answered. First, he precedes it with repentance from what God has forbidden. Second, he gives charity. Third, he fasts. Fourth, he prays, because God Almighty says that good words ascend to Him, and righteous deeds lift them higher (chapter 35, verse 10). Fifth, he perfects his intention in supplication, accepts it, and gathers his heart. Sixth, he begins by glorifying God Almighty and praising Him as He deserves. Seventh, he invokes blessings upon the Prophet, peace be upon him. Eighth, he draws near to God Almighty through the imams of the religion, naming them one by one until he reaches the imam of his own time. Ninth, he beseeches him and them before God in answering his supplication. Tenth, he asks for the good of this world and the Hereafter. Eleventh, he seeks refuge from the evil of this world and the Hereafter. Twelfth, he calls upon God for whatever he wishes and wills.

This is the chapter's first major teaching: answered supplication is not pictured as raw emotion released into heaven. It is ordered dependence. The supplicant begins by turning away from forbidden acts, then loosens his grip through charity, disciplines appetite through fasting, and stands within prayer. Only then does the good word ascend with righteous action lifting it. The heart must gather, the intention must be perfected, praise must come before request, blessings upon the Prophet must come before personal need, and the imams of the religion are named in order until the imam of the supplicant's own time. The servant asks, but he does not ask as a religious solitary inventing his own route.

This point reaches back to earlier teachings without repeating them. The book has already taught guardianship and knowledge, sacred ranks, disciplined loyalty, and affection as law. Here those doctrines become the structure of duʿāʾ. Drawing near through the imams of the religion is not reargued as a separate doctrine; it is assumed as the proper path of supplication. The listener should hear the forward motion: the earlier episodes taught what authority means; this chapter shows what authority does to the act of asking. It gives the supplicant names, order, mediation, and belonging. The plea is personal, but its grammar is communal and hierarchical.

The sayings of Amīr al-Mu'minīn Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, intensify the urgency. In the Four Hundred Etiquettes, he says: remember God Almighty and He will accept your supplication; remember Him and He will preserve you and care for you. He says to be prompt in supplication before calamity descends, for the gates of heaven are opened at six times: at the descent of rain, when the army advances, at the call to prayer, when the Qurʾān is recited, at the setting of the sun, and at the rising of dawn. He says that supplication responds to a decree that has already been concluded, so call upon God and hasten. He says to ward off affliction through supplication before calamity descends, because affliction reaches the believer faster than a torrent from the mountaintop to the valley below. He says that the one who does not supplicate is like the thrower who aims without an arrow. He says that whoever recites "Say: He is God, the One" ten times until sunrise, and "Indeed, We sent it down on the Night of Power" ten times, no sin will be recorded against him even if Iblīs exerts his utmost effort.

These maxims make supplication feel active. It is not a resignation after events have happened. It is prompt, preventative, and armed. The image of the thrower without an arrow is especially sharp. A person may aim well, know the target, and possess strength, but without the arrow nothing flies. In the same way, a believer may understand duty, know the prayer, and recognize authority, but if he does not supplicate, his need is unlaunched. Duʿāʾ is not a replacement for righteous action; the verse has already said that righteous deeds lift good words. But it is also not optional emotional overflow. It is a weapon before calamity, a gate at sacred moments, and the culmination by which prayer becomes pleading.

The first concept lands here: supplication after prayer is powerful precisely because it is not lawless. It has times, gestures, roots, foundations, mediating authority, repentance, charity, fasting, praise, blessings, worldly need, otherworldly need, and refuge. The prayer ends with salutation, but the servant remains in relation. The chapter asks the listener to imagine the post-prayer moment not as a blank space after obligation, but as a disciplined opening where the worshipper learns how to ask without severing asking from obedience.

## Speech And Motion

The chapter then turns inward, into words and deeds during the prayer itself. The first case is deliberately awkward: a man prays behind an imam who is intoxicated, and he signals to him with a bow or another gesture without completing the prayer properly. Does anything fall upon him? The answer says that the prayer is invalidated if such gesturing is done deliberately and without excuse, whether directed toward the imam or accompanied by praise. Earlier episodes already taught invalid leadership, following, and the seriousness of congregational order. This chapter's new angle is the worshipper's improvised correction. Even when something is wrong in the setting, the worshipper cannot turn prayer into a field of casual signals and half-performed gestures.

The next question asks about a man who bows excessively in prayer out of habit. If he does so deliberately, he must repeat the prayer. Another asks about a woman praying in congregation or alone while carrying her infant to prevent him from crying. The answer says that it is not permissible for her to engage in prayer in such a manner. Another asks about a person praying while experiencing internal discomfort. If it is something unavoidable, nothing falls upon him. These cases together create a moral scale. Deliberate extra bowing breaks form. Carrying the infant in the act is not permitted. Unavoidable bodily distress is excused. The source is neither indifferent to bodily conditions nor permissive of every bodily interruption. It distinguishes chosen disorder from constrained endurance.

A question about hair shows the same precision. What of a man who prays with his hair gathered inside his turban, drawing it from the back of the neck to the area of the forehead above the temple without inserting it in the middle of the head? The answer says there is nothing wrong with that; the forbidden practice is drawing the hair back to its origin, meaning gathering it to the middle of the head. The listener does not need a separate doctrine of grooming here; that has already been treated under grooming boundaries, hair, skin, and bone. The new point is how grooming intersects with prayer's bodily integrity. The hair may be arranged in a way that does not violate the forbidden form. Not every visible adjustment is disorder; not every gathered hair is prohibited.

The chapter also considers objects in or near the body. If something is inside the worshipper's mouth resembling a lobe, a dirham, or the like, and it does not prevent the proper formation of the letters, the prayer is complete. If he holds something in his hand that does not prevent him from fulfilling the conditions of prayer, nothing falls upon him; still, being free of such encumbrances is preferable. The rule turns on function. The object is not condemned simply because it exists. It becomes a problem when it blocks the prayer's requirements: letters, motion, conditions, completion. The preferable state is still uncluttered freedom, but the legal boundary is whether the act can be fulfilled.

Eating or drinking is treated more severely. A classified book states that one who eats or drinks during prayer, whether forgetfully or deliberately, has interrupted his prayer. Here the source draws a harder line because eating and drinking do not merely add a small encumbrance; they introduce an ordinary bodily activity into the worship itself. Prayer is not a container into which all bodily habits can be carried. Some acts are compatible if they do not obstruct the required form; others are interruptions by their nature.

Recitation, voice, and silence sharpen the same lesson. The interpretation of the Sharīʿa states that recitation comes before bowing. It says not to raise one's voice above the voice of the imam. It says that recitation in the noon and afternoon prayers should not be audible, because these are daytime prayers, while night prayer is recited aloud. It says that the Prophet, peace be upon him, forbade wiping over the feet and left aside the public utterance of the basmala. It says that once the worshipper rises, he does not speak until he offers the salutation. Earlier chapters already explained prayer's form, day and night recitation, and following the imam; here the source applies those doctrines to the discipline of sound. The voice has a place. It may not compete with the imam. It changes with day and night. It begins and ends according to the act's boundaries.

This is why the chapter's small rulings matter. They are all about preserving prayer as prayer. A gesture that replaces proper completion invalidates. Excessive bowing done deliberately requires repetition. Carrying a child into the prayer as a strategy for quiet is not permitted. Unavoidable internal distress is excused. Hair arrangement is judged according to the specific forbidden form. An object in the mouth is judged by whether it prevents letters. An object in the hand is judged by whether it prevents conditions. Eating and drinking interrupt. Recitation precedes bowing. The worshipper does not raise his voice over the imam. Speech stops once he rises and does not resume until salutation.

The source then brings in Jaʿfar ibn Manṣūr al-Yaman, may God sanctify him, from the book of the subconscious on the meaning of prayer. Prayer has doors, a few of which must be perfected for the prayer to be complete: Wuḍūʾ, Opening, Adhān, Iqāma, Orientation, Recitation, Bowing, Praise, Praise, Prostration, Tashahhud, Taslīm, Duʿāʾ al-Qunut, and Duʿāʾ al-Witr. These are fourteen doors that the worshipper must observe; they are the pillars of prayer, and without them his prayer is not complete.

The fourteen doors gather the whole section into an architectural image. A prayer is not only a sequence of movements. It is a building with entrances and thresholds. Wuḍūʾ brings the worshipper toward the act. Opening, Adhān, and Iqāma mark entry. Orientation turns the body. Recitation gives the prayer its voiced spine. Bowing and prostration bend the body into submission. Praise, Tashahhud, and Taslīm articulate reverence and completion. Duʿāʾ al-Qunut and Duʿāʾ al-Witr keep supplication inside prayer's structure rather than outside it alone. The earlier episodes have taught many of these elements in full; this chapter's contribution is to show why little interruptions matter. If the prayer has doors, then careless speech, needless gestures, eating, drinking, obstructed letters, and disorderly movement are not trivial. They are breaches in an architecture.

The second concept lands on restraint. Prayer requires more than sincerity somewhere in the heart. It requires the worshipper to let the inherited form govern the body and voice. The source is merciful toward what cannot be avoided and precise about what does not obstruct completion. But it refuses to let prayer become an ordinary situation in which one gestures, carries, eats, drinks, talks, raises the voice competitively, or bows by habit. The act is made of doors. To pass through them, the servant must accept their shape.

## Garment And Ground

The final and longest movement of the chapter surrounds the praying body with dress, images, impurity, ornament, and the ground of prostration. It begins with the prayer hall. If rugs or furnishings are decorated with engravings facing the qibla or in another direction, is it permissible to pray on them, and does the ruling concern only the place of prostration or the space more broadly? The answer says that if such decoration distracts the worshipper, his prayer is not permissible in that space; if it does not distract him, the prayer is permissible, except that one should not pray in the direction of images.

The source returns to the same issue later through the Musannaf. A carpet on which figures are depicted may be prayed on unless the figure is in the place of prostration, where it distracts from prayer. Another answer states more strongly that a rug or garment bearing figures is not permissible for prayer if the worshipper can see them; if he cannot see them, it is permissible. Amīr al-Mu'minīn Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, says that a man should not prostrate on a picture, nor on a carpet on which he stands facing one. He also says that a man should not tie a dirham bearing a picture into his garment while praying; however, if the dirham is in a money-pouch or garment and is pure, that is permissible.

These rulings show the chapter's concern with attention taking material form. A picture at the place of prostration is not merely decoration. It competes with the forehead's act. A figure visible on a garment or rug draws the eye into an image when the body should be turned toward Allah. Yet a dirham hidden in a pouch, if pure, is treated differently. Visibility, distraction, and placement matter. The source does not turn the world into a generalized enemy of prayer; it asks whether the material thing disturbs the act's direction, purity, and attention.

The next set of questions concerns clothing. A man prays in a garment that a woman is permitted to wear; a woman prays in pure silk or a very red garment, whether dyed with saffron or not. Is the prayer permissible? The answer says this is a matter of etiquette. Such garments do not invalidate the prayer, whether the wearer is a man or a woman, though pure silk is disliked for men. The distinction is important. Not every disliked or unfitting choice destroys the act. Some belong to adab rather than validity. The source can dislike a garment for a man without declaring the prayer void.

Covering, however, is stricter. The question asks about the books of the Sharīʿa saying that if any part of a woman's ʿawra becomes exposed during prayer, her prayer is invalidated whether she knew of it or not. It mentions women of the people of the daʿwa in India whose legs are sometimes exposed during prayer once or twice. The answer says that covering is obligatory for all women. If any part of her ʿawra becomes exposed, she may not pray; the respondent says he knows this ruling. He then indicates that the question perhaps actually concerns special cases: the person swimming in water, the person in fear, and the traveler. Later, the Musannaf states that if a free woman or an umm al-walad prays with her ʿawra exposed, knowingly or not, the prayer is void. A separate issue says that a free woman praying with a net over her clothing is not covered by that net if the shield or veil is not thick; the net does not suffice.

The episode should present these as the source's own legal formulations, without expanding them beyond the chapter. The earlier chapters on purity under conditions and shared boundaries have already taught that the law can make accommodations for fear, travel, water, and necessity. Here the source keeps the covering rule firm while recognizing that some exceptional cases may be governed by their own conditions. The point is not to soften the ruling into vagueness. It is to keep two truths visible: obligatory covering is real, and exceptional constraint must be understood as exception rather than habit.

Travel and weapons create another boundary case. If a traveler carries weapons that prevent him from praying properly, such as feathers in arrows, daggers, and pure silk garments, must he pray without repeating the prayer? The answer says that a prayer need not be repeated except when clothing is pure and there is nothing impure on the worshipper that he has not removed. The wording is compressed, but the direction is clear: repetition is tied above all to purity and removable impurity, not to every burden of travel. This belongs with the chapter's practical mercy. The traveler may be encumbered. The prayer remains required. What cannot be changed is endured; what is impure and removable must be removed.

Jewelry and adornment are treated with similar differentiation. A man prays wearing forged gold or silver weights and similar items; the answer says his prayer is complete and valid. Another question asks about rings on the hand or foot for adornment rather than practical benefit. Men should not wear rings for adornment; women are permitted to do so, though leaving it is better. For men there is no valid reason, yet whoever does it is not sinful. The source again distinguishes validity, etiquette, gendered practice, and sin. It can say the prayer is valid, the adornment lacks proper reason, leaving it is better, and the person is not sinful. These are not contradictions. They are different registers of judgment.

Clothing borrowed or worn by others raises the question of impurity. It is permissible to pray in the clothing of Muslim boys and Muslim slaves unless the garments show impurity or one does not pray in them. Whoever prays and then, upon finishing, notices impurity on his garment, and knew of it beforehand, has invalidated his prayer and must repeat it, whether he was making up a missed prayer or not. The decisive issue is knowledge and removable impurity. Earlier chapters already taught material purity, food and contact, and the pathways of purification. Here the purity doctrine is not retaught; it is applied to the garment as the immediate environment of prayer.

The source adds that a person should not pray in a state of distress, nor in darkness, nor in a garment that distresses him. If he finds no alternative, he prays as he is. This is one of the chapter's most human lines. The law cares about distress because distress can break attention and dignity. A garment can burden the act. Darkness can unsettle it. Yet obligation does not disappear when conditions are hard. If there is no alternative, the worshipper prays as he is. The prayer's mercy is not the cancellation of prayer; it is the recognition of constrained performance.

Leadership in inadequate covering receives its own ruling. Whoever prays while wearing only a small cloth on his shoulders, meaning he has only that garment, may not lead others in prayer; if he cannot find anyone else, his own prayer in that state is permissible. Again, validity and leadership are not identical. A person's own prayer may stand under necessity, while he may still be unfit to lead others in that same state. The chapter's earlier material on ranked leadership and standing behind need not be repeated; here the new point is that the leader's public representational role demands a dignity that private necessity may not meet.

Jaʿfar ibn Manṣūr al-Yaman, may Allah sanctify him, is cited again from his book on obligatory laws and the limits of religion. It is permissible for a man to uncover his head in public and to go out to the congregation and pray with his head uncovered. If a man prays while wearing something resembling an earring, a dirham, or something similar, and it does not prevent him from completing the motions of prayer, his prayer is valid. Even if he holds something in his hand that constrains him but does not prevent him from fulfilling the requirements of prayer, there is nothing wrong with that; if he is free of it altogether, that is preferable.

This repetition is not accidental. It re-centers the test around completion. Does the thing prevent motion? Does it block the conditions? Does it obstruct letters? Does it introduce impurity? Does it distract the eye? Does it leave covering insufficient? The source is building a practical logic. Prayer is not invalidated by every unusual object, visible head, or constrained hand. It is threatened when the object prevents the required act, when the garment fails the covering, when impurity remains knowingly, when the image captures attention, or when wrapping makes bowing and prostration impossible.

The cases of impure garments make the logic concrete. A man who has nothing but an impure garment and cannot obtain another, nor water to purify it, should pray in it and then repeat the prayer when he finds another garment or water to purify it. If he has two garments and one is impure but he does not know which, he must pray twice, once in each garment, intending the obligatory prayer with each. These rulings may sound laborious, but they reveal a profound seriousness. The obligation is not delayed into nonexistence. The worshipper prays now under constraint, and repairs later when repair becomes possible. Uncertainty is answered by doubling the act rather than ignoring impurity.

The ground of prostration receives equal care. The source asks whether it is permissible to pray on stone, milk, or something similar. The answer says it is not permissible, and only the obligatory prayer is binding upon him; the nafl and sunnah prayers are not. Later, in questions answered by Idris ibn al-Hasan, may Allah sanctify him, someone asks whether it is permissible to pray on stone and plaster. The answer cites the Prophet, peace be upon him: "The earth has been made for me a mosque and a means of purification." Anything that does not qualify as earth in the complete sense cannot be prayed on. The interpretation of the Sharīʿa states that there is no valid prayer except on the ground or what grows from it, such as grass.

At first glance this might seem like a technical surface rule. In the source's logic, it is more than that. Prostration is the lowest bodily sign of servanthood, and the forehead needs the right kind of receiving place. The earth is both mosque and purification. What grows from it shares in that suitability. Food does not. A heap of wheat or barley, anything edible, or bread is not a place for prostration. Amīr al-Mu'minīn Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, says that a man should not prostrate on such things. The forehead should not make the sign of submission upon what is ordinarily consumed. The act needs ground, not appetite.

Plaster introduces a subtle case. The Musannaf says that plaster and the like, when burned with the dead, becomes counted as part of the mosque and the house, and prostration on it is permissible, since fire has purified it. The listener should hear the principle rather than become lost in the material. A substance can change legal character through treatment. Fire can purify in the relevant case. The surface becomes part of the prayer-space rather than a distracting or unsuitable object. This is one of the chapter's many reminders that law attends to material reality: garments, water, fire, impurity, thickness, images, ground, and food are not abstractions.

Several garment forms are then interpreted. The saying that there is nothing wrong with praying in a single dense shirt means that if the worshipper bows, his side may become apparent. The phrase "like the wings of the hook" refers to a garment wrapped around the back with the limbs resting on the shoulders when no other option is available. But the garment known as ishtimal al-samma' is forbidden: a man wraps himself in a single garment, placing its middle over his head or shoulders, holding one end in his left hand against his body, turning the other from his right side behind him, and gathering it with his right hand so it becomes like a sewn garment with both hands underneath it. If he prays in this manner, he cannot pray properly. He cannot place his hands on the ground as required, cannot bow or prostrate correctly, and cannot complete the raising of his hands. Such a prayer is incomplete and does not earn reward.

This passage is the garment section in miniature. The problem with ishtimal al-samma' is not that it looks unfamiliar. It binds the body against the acts prayer requires. It hides the hands from the ground, blocks bowing and prostration, and prevents the raising of the hands. The source's concern is worshipful capacity. A garment that dresses the body while disabling prayer has failed the act it was supposed to accompany. Dress is not an aesthetic layer over worship; it either enables or obstructs obedience.

The chapter closes the dress section with rulings about women of different legal statuses and covering. An umm al-walad, a correspondence slave woman, a concubine, and a slave girl who has not yet reached menstruation may pray without a face-covering. Those among them who are disobedient and do not know this must repeat every prayer performed without a face-covering from the time of that lapse. The source's social categories are its own, and the episode should not modernize them into something else. Within its framework, it is again asking what covering suffices for which body, under which status, and with what consequence when the rule has been neglected.

The final miscellaneous rulings reinforce the material discipline. A small amount of milk, fat, or eggs from camels, cows, sheep, and chickens that comes into contact with clothing or body is permissible. There is no prayer in camel pens, while there is nothing wrong with praying in sheep pens after they have been swept and sprinkled with water. The Prophet, peace be upon him, says that every prayer in which the Fatiha is not recited is incomplete and deficient, which ties the surface of prayer back to its recited center. Amīr al-Mu'minīn Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, says that none should stand before Allah Almighty in a state of distress, that a man should not pray in a shirt alone without a waist-wrapper, and that it is not permissible to pray in a garment worn loosely around the neck or immodestly. The chapter ends where it began: the body before Allah must be protected from distraction, impurity, immodesty, obstruction, and unsuitable surfaces.

The third concept lands on the dignity of the praying body. Dress is not merely cultural presentation; it is the body’s immediate prayer-space. The rug is not merely furniture; it can either receive prostration or distract from it. The ground is not merely floor; it must be earth or what grows from it, not an edible heap or an image. Ornament is not merely personal preference; it may be valid, disliked, better avoided, or impermissible depending on use and effect. Impurity is not merely a private stain; if known and removable, it can undo the prayer. The chapter teaches listeners to see prayer as an act surrounded by material loyalties. Even cloth, stone, plaster, silk, gold, images, nets, rings, pouches, and wheat are drawn into the question of how a servant stands, bows, and prostrates before Allah.

## Closing

This chapter makes prayer feel like an act with living edges. After the salutation, the servant does not simply dissolve into private wishes; supplication has roots, foundations, gestures, repentance, praise, blessing, and approach through the imams of the religion. Inside the prayer, the servant does not treat devotion as permission for ordinary behavior; speech, motion, carrying, eating, drinking, recitation, and gesture are governed by the doors of the act. Around the prayer, the servant does not treat clothing, ornaments, images, impurity, and ground as neutral; they either preserve or disturb the dignity of standing before Allah.

The episode should close with the chapter's practical severity and mercy held together. It is severe because prayer cannot be casually interrupted, wrapped in disabling garments, performed knowingly with impurity, directed toward distracting images, or placed upon unsuitable surfaces. It is merciful because unavoidable discomfort is excused, constrained travel is recognized, hidden objects may be tolerated when they do not obstruct the act, necessity permits a person to pray as he is, and later repair is possible when water or pure clothing becomes available. Prayer is not fragile because Allah needs perfect fabric, flawless rooms, or empty hands. It is guarded because the servant needs a form strong enough to train desire, discipline the body, and make supplication rise with righteous action.
