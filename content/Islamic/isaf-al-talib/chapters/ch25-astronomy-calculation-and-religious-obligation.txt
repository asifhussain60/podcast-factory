## Where this episode opens

Here is a claim that sounds like a riddle and is offered as law: of your two eyes, the more reliable one is not in your head. The letter running through this chapter is going to argue that the eye in your face is the junior instrument — later, coarser, more easily fooled — and that the other one, the one that belongs to the heart and to knowledge, is the senior partner, and that when an obligation from God has to be timed, the senior partner is the one you are bound to obey.

This chapter covers the technical core of the crescent argument. The previous chapter left its letter one step short of a conclusion: it had built a universe and a law in which nothing created is permitted to stand alone, and it had set the disputed sighting report down inside that universe without naming the thing it was half of. This chapter names it. The counterpart of sight is sight. Seeing, like everything else God made, comes in two — natural vision and intellectual vision, the eye and the heart — and the rest of the chapter is the working out of that single division.

Three moves make it up. The first establishes the division and transfers it onto religious duty: if the more precise vision is the one belonging to knowledge, then a person who *knows* a prayer's time has arrived is obligated to pray, a person who *knows* dawn has broken is obligated to stop eating, and a person who *knows* the crescent has emerged is obligated to fast — regardless of what his eye has caught. The second move is the astronomy, the most technical passage in the book: the conjunction as astronomers define it, the moon's degree and minute measured against the sun's, how the crescent's light appears and grows, and what stops the eye from following any of it — cloud, dust, a wall, a mountain, and, most interestingly, the sun itself. The third move is the grammar, and it is the sharpest: a sustained argument about one Arabic letter, the lam, in the Prophet's instruction to fast and break the fast upon seeing the crescent.

A word on what kind of document this is. It is a running argument with an imagined opponent in the room, and the opponent gets his lines — unnamed, quoted in the second person: *you say that a person does not know anything until his sight falls upon it*. He is allowed one counter-argument near the end, drawn from the Qur'an, and it is answered. But he never gets a turn of his own. This is one voice, unfolding one position, absorbing objections as it goes.

Two things the chapter assumes rather than re-argues: the doctrine of pairs built out at length in the previous chapter, used here in a single clause; and the earlier astronomy of the month, the emergence reading of the sighting report, and the scriptural material on counted days, all treated as ground the listener has already walked.

One last thing to watch for. This chapter ends in wreckage. Its final argument needs a geometrical diagram, says so explicitly — *as shown in this picture* — and the picture is gone. What survives is a scatter of labels, badly transmitted, reproduced here as they stand, because a broken figure is part of the record.

## Two kinds of sight

The chapter opens by applying to eyesight the same division the previous chapter applied to creation as a whole. We say, the letter begins, that sight too is of two kinds, each coupled with the other: natural vision and intellectual vision.

Natural vision is the vision of the eye, and its jurisdiction is named precisely: it perceives colors — white, black, red, yellow, and others — and forms — circular, triangular, square, and so on. On this basis, the letter says, it is relied upon, because it falls within the domain of the senses. That is not a dismissal; nobody here argues that the eye lies about whether a thing is red.

Intellectual vision belongs to the realm of the heart and the soul, and its jurisdiction is the complement of the first: it perceives what natural vision cannot perceive, and specifically, the letter says, the causes that are hidden from natural perception. Not hidden things in general — causes. The eye sees the effect in front of it; the other vision sees why it is there.

Then three verses, and the third one does the work.

Allah said: "The heart did not lie in what it saw." (chapter 53, verse 11) And He said: "Did you not see how your Lord stretched out the shadow?" (chapter 25, verse 45) And: "Have you not seen what your Lord did to the companions of the elephant?" (chapter 105, verse 1)

The first verse assigns seeing to the heart and vouches for what the heart saw. The second uses the language of sight for something no eye is watching in the moment of address. And the third is the proof, because it can be dated. We know, the letter says, that the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him — was not alive at the time of the companions of the elephant and thus did not see them with his eyes. Yet the address is *have you not seen*. So the seeing the verse asks him to confirm is not ocular; what is meant is the vision of the soul, which belongs to the domain of the heart and knowledge. The argument depends on no theory at all — only on a date.

Then a homelier proof, from ordinary Arabic speech. It is also said in proverb that such-and-such a person is blind, meaning that he is slow and late in knowing things and seeing them in their absence. The insult has nothing to do with his eyes. Everyone who uses it already believes there is a kind of seeing a person with perfect vision can fail at — and, note the phrase, a kind whose proper object is things *in their absence*. That is precisely the case the chapter is building toward.

Since it is established that sight is of two kinds — like all things, the letter says, which are divided into two states and two aspects to complete the oneness of Allah, so that nothing shares in that oneness — we may now compare them. That clause is the previous chapter arriving in a single breath, used as a tool and not defended again.

The comparison is short. Natural vision, which belongs to the side of the eye, only perceives things that are physically present within limits and time — colors and forms, present, bounded, now. Intellectual vision, which belongs to the side of the heart and knowledge, perceives what natural vision cannot perceive and what the eye misses from sight. It follows that intellectual vision is the more precise, and the more refined in its perception, for its knowledge of things reaches their realities rather than merely perceiving their outward appearance.

Then the example, and it is the best image in the chapter.

If a person takes from a pool a single drop of water, the wisdom of intellectual vision holds that the amount of that water has decreased by a drop, and it perceives this as a true perception — whereas this decrease is not perceptible to the outward eye and sight.

Stand at the edge of the pool and watch. Your eye reports: unchanged — a falsehood about a quantity, delivered with complete confidence, because the change is real and lies below its threshold. The other vision reports: less by one drop, and is exact about something the eye has no access to. Note what carried it: not revelation, not a faculty granted to a few, but the ordinary inference that a quantity you have taken from is smaller. Anyone can run it, and it is more accurate than looking.

Hold that pool in mind, because a moon below the threshold of visibility is the same case at a larger scale.

Now the transfer from theory to obligation, made in one step. Since intellectual vision is the more prior and more refined in perception, it is all the more obligatory to act upon it in fulfilling the duties imposed by Allah Almighty.

The first principle, the letter says, is to restrict oneself not to what is inferior — that is, the level of the ignorant — but to what reason demands, which binds people to the truth: namely, to take the more precise and superior, which is the vision of knowledge and the soul. When two instruments are available and one is better, using the worse one for God's business is not caution.

Three applications follow, arranged from the uncontroversial to the contested.

First, prayer. If a person knows in his heart and perceives that the time for one of the prayers has come, the prayer becomes obligatory for him even if he has not heard the call to prayer. Nobody will fight about this one. The call, taught earlier in this book as the summons, announces a time; it does not create one.

Second, fasting at dawn. One who is fasting — if he sees with his heart and knows that dawn has arrived, even though his eyesight is prevented from seeing it — would be obligated to abstain from food. A blind man at dawn is bound exactly as a sighted man is, and nobody disputes that. But notice what everyone has just conceded: the daily beginning of the fast is already governed by knowledge rather than eyesight, every morning, for anybody whose window faces a wall.

Third, and this is the one the whole letter exists for. By the same logic, we say that the beginning of the fast becomes obligatory with the knowledge that the cause of fasting — which is the appearance of the crescent moon — has occurred at its greatest strength, even if sight is prevented from seeing it due to light, clouds, dust, or mountains.

The reason given is causal rather than legal. For it is customary that when the cause occurs, its effect becomes obligatory — so that neither should precede the other except in rank. The crescent's emergence is the cause; the fast is the effect; causes do not wait for witnesses. And the letter adds a claim about reliability: the call in their entering the fast by virtue of intellectual vision is one in which they scarcely fall into error, on the basis of knowledge and understanding.

Then the bridge into the technical section, and it is scriptural. When the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him — made fasting obligatory in certain months, it is reported that Allah Almighty made the sun and the moon the means by which years, months, their times, and their people are known. And He said: "Allah is the One who made the sun a radiance and the moon a light, and apportioned for it phases so that you might know the number of years and the reckoning." (chapter 10, verse 5)

Read the purpose clause the way the letter reads it. The phases were apportioned *so that you might know* — the moon is an instrument of reckoning, issued for a calculating purpose. And He said: "They ask you about the crescents." (chapter 2, verse 189) The year consists of twelve months, as Allah said: "Indeed, the number of months with Allah is twelve months in the Book of Allah from the day He created the heavens and the earth." (chapter 9, verse 36) A fixed count, set at creation: the frame is regular before anybody looks at anything.

## What blocks the eye

Now the letter does something unusual for a work of religious law: it explains the mechanics.

These months, it says, are not complete until their appointed periods are fulfilled, and a lunar month is not perfect in its days except through the moon's journey from the point where it is one degree and one minute ahead of the sun — the conjunction recognized by astronomers — until it completes its full arc and returns to that same design and conjunction.

Take the terms slowly. Degrees and minutes are positions on the circle of the sky: sixty minutes to a degree, three hundred and sixty degrees around. The conjunction is the moment the moon and the sun occupy the same position in that reckoning. The month, on this definition, is not a stretch of nights and not a sequence of appearances. It is one complete circuit, measured from a named geometrical instant back to the same instant. And the letter is explicit that the definition is borrowed: *this is the conjunction recognized by astronomers*. It does not hide the borrowing.

From this knowledge it follows, the letter continues, that when the moon's degree and minute within its zodiacal position become identical to those of the sun, it has departed from the old month; and when it leaves the sun on its way to the next month, it is moving toward the conjunction.

Then the optics, and here the transmission begins to strain. At the conjunction, the light of one half of the moon's mass is drawn away from the side facing the earth until it passes over, and the upper half of the mass is illuminated. At that moment, the letter says, the ocean side reflects light the way each half of its mass does on the night of opposition when facing the center — that is, the earth — and the light of the half facing us appears at the crescent and grows as the moon increases its distance.

The phrase rendered here as *the ocean side* is damaged and is not repaired. What survives clearly is the account itself, essentially the modern one: the moon is a sphere with a lit half and a dark half, the lit half is always lit, what changes is how much of it faces us, and the crescent is the first sliver swinging into view as the moon pulls away from the sun. The night of opposition — the moon opposite the sun, facing the center, which the letter glosses for us as the earth — is the full moon.

That is the mechanism. Now the point of describing it. The causes that prevent the eye from perceiving all of this, the letter says, are many; the eye's inability arises from within itself, and from the fact that it sees light emanating from the moon's body as the moon moves toward conjunction at the end of the month.

And then the list, which is the argument's spine.

The sun prevents the eye from seeing the moon's light, just as other things prevent the eye from seeing and knowing — such as clouds, dust, a wall, or a mountain.

Four obstacles, and one does not belong with the others — which is exactly the point. Nobody has ever claimed that a mountain stops a month. If the crescent has risen and a ridge stands between you and it, the crescent has still risen; the obstacle is on your side and the moon is unaffected. The letter puts the sun in that list and asks why it should be treated differently.

Then the everyday demonstration. Do you not observe that the planets are not visible during the day because of the sun's light and its overwhelming radiance, yet when that light is broken, the eye can perceive them? This is because light is diminished by refraction.

Anyone can check this, and it costs nothing. The planets are overhead at noon; nobody sees them, and nobody concludes they are gone. The daytime sky is a standing, universally accepted case of a real celestial body made invisible by light — and no legal consequence has ever been attached to that invisibility.

Then the sharpest observation in the passage, and it is a genuine paradox. If it is true that the amount of light from the moon at its departure from the sun would not exist were it not for the sun's own light and rays, then what the eye sees is that the sun's light is itself the barrier, just as other barriers are, like clouds and the atmospheric cycle.

All of the crescent's light is borrowed from the sun. So the sun both makes the crescent and hides it, and the young crescent is most vulnerable precisely when it is nearest the sun. The source that generates the evidence is the source that suppresses it.

The conclusion follows in one sentence. Since the eye is prevented by sunlight from seeing the crescent after its departure, the soul — from the perspective of knowledge — is not prevented from seeing it as it truly is. Fasting therefore becomes obligatory through the vision of knowledge, derived from the established signs we have outlined. By these testimonies, the people of the Sayyid's call are on the truth and the right path in their entry into fasting through the soul's vision. And if they are correct, then whoever disagrees with them is in error.

Having claimed the ground, the letter puts a question to the other side. Where does the person of right — and others who differ — derive the basis for seeing by sight alone, without the vision of the heart and knowledge? The evidence rests on seeing from two angles, and no one denies this except out of apparent arrogance.

Then the opponent's position, in his own voice: you say that a person does not know anything until his sight falls upon it, and that once he sees it, the ruling of knowledge-vision is lifted from him. It is a strong doctrine, stated fairly — knowledge is a stand-in for sight, and the moment sight is available the stand-in retires.

We say your argument is corrupt and invalid, the letter answers — for three reasons.

**First, the doctrine cannot see itself.** If we ask you whether you have seen that Allah Almighty has made this obligatory upon you, you must say no. Yet you have made it a rule that a person does not know anything until his sight falls upon it — and your sight does not fall upon the very rule you are enforcing. You are therefore compelled to abandon the position of sight-seeing and return to the hadith of knowledge. And the letter puts your answer in your mouth, because it is the only one available: *we know, and we have the proofs, and we have confirmed it — that is our position and our foundation.* Which is the other party's own position, adopted under pressure and renamed.

**Second, the doctrine is not how learning works.** The learned man has commanded the world regarding its events, the letter says; and if we had asked you about something, you would have to tell him that you are wise enough to employ the vision of knowledge and proof, and that proof does not rule by the vision of the eye alone — which is precisely what we say.

**Third, and this is the trap, the thirty-day case.** We ask what you say about a man who broke his fast and did not see the crescent moon with his own eye, but thirty days of Ramadan had passed. Was his breaking of the fast valid or not? Every party to this dispute accepts the thirty-day rule — if the new crescent is not sighted, the month completes at thirty — so the question is a fork with no third exit.

If you say it was valid — then how, when the crescent was not seen with the eye, and your doctrine holds that fasting and breaking the fast are not permissible until after seeing the crescent with the eye? You are compelled to say that we know that after thirty days the crescent has been seen, even though it was obscured. Which means you have abandoned the ruling of sight-vision and returned by necessity to the hadith of knowledge-vision — and that is our position. You have used knowledge to certify a sighting nobody made.

If you say it is not valid — then you must hold that one cannot know anything except through the physical senses, things physical in their form and quality only. And that, the letter says, is false about the soul, which dispenses with the aid of the senses in its knowledge by making use of other faculties within it. The fate of the senses and their yield is that the soul rises above the need for them.

The opponent has been pushed into a claim about the human mind, and the letter now goes after it directly.

## What seeking the crescent seeks

Within man, the letter says, the soul is one of the instruments by which he perceives things — such as hearing, sight, smell, taste, and touch — and it has a capacity none of these other faculties possesses: the ability to rise above all of them, after the images of their perceptions have been received by it, and to perceive what hearing, sight, smell, taste, and touch do not perceive.

The argument is built by analogy from the senses themselves. Just as hearing perceives what sight, smell, taste, and touch do not, and smell perceives what the others do not — so too, in the wisdom of Allah Almighty, each sense has its own domain, lest man be deprived of perceiving some part of what Allah has created in the worlds. The senses do not overlap, deliberately, so that between them nothing in creation goes unregistered. Sight is deaf; hearing is colorblind; each is sovereign in its own territory and blind everywhere else.

Which invites the obvious question: if each sense is sealed inside its domain, what perceives the world as a whole? His perception is thus multiple, the letter says, and beyond the five it extends to what the senses do not reach, for he perceives by inference what they cannot hand him. The mechanism given is memory. When it is necessary to know something, the soul accepts the image of the thing as perceived by the senses at the first perception, and that image remains present to the soul in itself when it returns to that thing without needing the senses again. The example is astronomical, chosen because it is the case at issue: images of the planets and their qualities were perceived through sight, and the soul then dispenses with that sight for a second perception of the image in itself.

You looked once. The look deposited something. From then on the soul works with the deposit — which lets the letter ask a question nobody in the dispute had thought to ask: *what is the sighting actually for?*

What is meant here, the letter says, is not that one should seek the crescent and see it with the eye every month and year, knowing its appearance in its form, precision, and coarseness — for that would require sight as the instrument. What is actually meant in seeking it is to know the validity of its being in the west in front of the sun after having been in the east.

There is the whole case in one sentence. When you go out at sunset to look for the new moon, you are not going to find out what color it is, or what shape; you have known both since childhood. You are going out to settle one fact: whether the moon has crossed over and now stands west of the sun. That is a question about position, and position is not a color or a form.

And perceiving this validity of its being in the west is a matter for the soul, not for sight — because sight, even without obstructions, only perceives shapes and colors.

Notice the phrase *even without obstructions*. The letter has spent a whole section on cloud and dust and mountain and glare, and now sets all of it aside as beside the point. Give the eye a perfect night and a sharp young crescent; it still reports only a shape and a color. The obstructions were a practical nuisance; the real problem is one of jurisdiction.

The letter then closes the two remaining exits. The request regarding the crescent was not to know its color and shape — and had it been, that would already be impossible to request from one who knows how it looks from having seen it, and who trusts that it will not change as long as the heavens and the earth endure. You cannot be sent to learn what you already know and what cannot change. And similarly for the crescent's fineness and thickness, its precision and coarseness: had he been asked to know those, it would have been impossible to make fasting obligatory if the crescent is precise while the condition is coarseness, or coarse while the condition is precision. If the sighting existed to certify a thickness, the law would have had to specify which thickness, and it never has.

This condition of shape, color, precision, and coarseness was not present in the obligation of fasting, so it is invalid to say that the obligation is attached to the perception of the crescent by sight. Once this is invalidated, it is established that fasting is attached to the perception of the soul, because what is sought is whether the crescent is in front of the sun.

Then a comparison of reliability. As for sight-vision, it may err in perceiving things as they really are, and the symptoms are listed like a physician's: it sees the large as small and the small as large, the fine as coarse and the coarse as fine; it may only see from near and not from far, or from far and not from near; and it can barely err in what it encompasses. Taking what does not err in establishing obligatory duties, according to the people of hearts, is more deserving than taking what does err. So when the people of the call entered the fast, they took what does not err — on a clear and established basis.

And now, at the end, the grammar — because everything so far has been argument, and the other side has a text.

The saying of the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him — is: "Fast upon seeing it and break the fast upon seeing it."

The letter says this is not without two possible interpretations, and here the transmission has collapsed: the two branches as they reach us read almost identically, both describing the community fasting from the time of the crescent's sighting. The distinction intended cannot be recovered and is not reconstructed here. What is fully legible is the question asked next, and the question is the argument.

What is the meaning of saying "upon seeing it," and what is the reason for his restricting the expression to this particular form, given his distinction and his saying "I am the most eloquent of the Arabs"? There is no significance in the lam without some other purpose being served by the expression.

The lam is the Arabic prefix carrying the sense of *for* or *toward*, and the claim is that a man who called himself the most eloquent of the Arabs did not attach a preposition by accident. So what does this lam do elsewhere? Three examples from ordinary speech, all doing the same thing. Say to a group: "Prepare such-and-such a thing for the coming of so-and-so" — the thing is to be ready *before* he walks in; nobody begins cooking when the guest arrives. Or: "They composed a poem to meet so-and-so, to recite it to him" — they will have it ready when they find him, not compose it from the moment of meeting. Or: "Work for the Hereafter" — do the work before the time of the Hereafter arrives, for it is known that in the Hereafter there is no work. That last is decisive, because the deadline is absolute. The lam cannot mean *at the moment of*; it must mean *in readiness for*.

With these evidences, the letter concludes, it is established that the lam was not used to mean that the people fast when seeing the crescent with their own eyes. And it adds a concession in passing: even if it were the intention of the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him — that the crescent be seen and the community fast upon it, that is what we have said, and we have demonstrated its proof.

Then the opponent gets his counter, and it is the best move he makes all chapter. He does not dispute the grammar; he finds the same construction in the Qur'an and shows it does not behave the way the letter needs it to.

You do not deny, he says, that the lam is from the words of the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him. Then see this lam as in the words of Allah Almighty: "Establish the prayer for the decline of the sun." (chapter 17, verse 78) That verse does not require that the prayer precede the sun's decline, nor that the prayer be offered before the sun.

That is a real hit. Here is the identical construction in a verse timing worship by a celestial event, and nobody has ever read it as *pray in readiness for noon*. If the lam meant what the letter says, the noon prayer would be early every day.

The reply concedes the parallel and turns it around. The saying regarding the lam in the saying of Allah Almighty about the sun is like the saying regarding the lam in the saying of the Prophet — "to see it" — because the sun's decline is its disappearance from the dome of the sky, as has been explained.

And the perception of this decline, the letter says, is of two kinds: psychological perception and natural perception. Psychological perception precedes natural perception, because the decline in reality within the dome of the heavens can only be perceived by the soul through the perspective of knowledge — since natural vision is limited in its perception until the sun reaches the azimuth point and descends, securing its range in its descent at the intersection of the circle line and the straight line, as shown in this picture.

Follow what happened there. The counter-example has been accepted whole and read the letter's own way. Yes, you pray at the decline, not before — but *when* is the decline? The sun crosses the meridian at a geometrical instant, and nobody sees that instant; what the eye registers is a sun already on its way down. Even for the noon prayer, the event is one thing and the perception of it another, and the knowing runs ahead of the looking. The opponent's verse is not a counter-example. It is the same case.

And then: *as shown in this picture*. The picture is gone. What the manuscript preserves is a scatter of labels, some plainly corrupted: a straight line and its sections, a tangent line, a meridian line, a rising circle, a center, the midday line where elevation is taken, and two labels marking east and west — the westward one rendered as though it named the country of Morocco, which is the same Arabic word. Alongside them sit fragments that attach to nothing, one of them reading *medium of poison*.

Enough survives to know what the figure was: a horizon circle, a meridian, and the sun's descending path crossing them, drawn to show the exact geometrical instant the argument depends on — the instant no eye can catch. The claim was to be settled by a drawing, and the drawing has not survived.

## What this episode lands

The chapter took the doctrine of pairs and applied it to the one faculty the whole dispute turned on. Seeing comes in two. Natural vision belongs to the eye and is sovereign over colors and forms. Intellectual vision belongs to the heart and knowledge and reaches the causes behind what is shown. Scripture uses the language of sight for a man describing an event from before his birth; ordinary Arabic calls a man blind when his eyes are fine and his understanding is slow. A drop taken from a pool leaves the eye reporting *unchanged* and the mind reporting *less by one* — and the mind is right.

From there the obligation follows. If the better instrument is the one that does not err, the duties God imposed are to be timed by it: a man who knows the prayer's hour is bound though no call reached him, a man who knows dawn has broken is bound though he cannot see the sky, and a community that knows the crescent has emerged is bound though light or cloud or a mountain stands in the way. Causes do not wait for witnesses.

The astronomy is offered as proof that the knowing is available. The month is a circuit from conjunction to conjunction, measured in degrees and minutes, on a definition openly taken from astronomers. The moon's lit half is always lit; the crescent is that half swinging into view. And the reasons the eye misses it are ordinary — cloud, dust, a wall, a mountain — and then the sun, which is the strange one, because it both makes the crescent's light and drowns it, as it drowns the planets standing overhead unseen at noon every day of our lives.

Then the opponent, pressed three times. His doctrine that nothing is known until sight falls on it cannot account for his own knowledge of the obligation, which he has never seen; cannot account for how any scholar has ever settled anything; and cannot survive the thirty-day case, where a fast broken with no crescent sighted is either valid — and he has certified an unseen moon by knowledge — or invalid, and he must claim the soul knows nothing the senses have not handed it.

The deepest move came last and was the quietest. What is a sighting *for*? Not to learn the moon's color, which is known; not its shape, which cannot change; not its thickness, which no law has ever specified. It is to settle whether the moon now stands west of the sun, having been east of it — a fact about position, which no eye has ever been able to report, even on a perfect night with nothing in the way. The eye was being asked a question outside its jurisdiction.

And then the grammar, the opponent's one good counter, and the answer. The lam in *upon seeing it* is the lam of preparation — ready the room before the guest arrives, have the poem written before you meet the man, do the work before the Hereafter, where no work is done. The opponent replies that the same lam commands the noon prayer at the sun's decline and nobody prays early. The letter accepts the case and reads it its own way: the decline is a crossing point in geometry, no one has ever seen it happen, and what the eye registers is a sun already on its way down.

The proof of that last point was a diagram, and the diagram is lost. A few labels survive, garbled — a meridian, a rising circle, a center, east and west — and one has come down to us naming a country instead of a direction. The letter says *as shown in this picture*, and there is no picture. A thousand-year-old argument about the limits of eyesight closes by asking us to look at something we cannot see.
