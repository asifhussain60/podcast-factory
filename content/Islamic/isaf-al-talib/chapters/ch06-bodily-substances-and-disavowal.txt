## Where this episode opens

What if the hardest purity questions are not about washing at all, but about the body's leftovers: hair, bone, blood, birth, desire, lineage, and the hidden womb? This episode picks up after the book has already taught bodily purity, material purity, food contact, grooming boundaries, and the strict discipline of ghusl and tayammum. As established earlier, the source does not treat purity as vague neatness or as anxious suspicion. Here it presses into cases where the body itself leaves traces behind, and where lawful relation depends on knowing what kind of bodily state is actually present.

The chapter begins with skin, bones, hair, wool, and the remains of slaughtered or dead animals. Some materials become lawful only through proper slaughter. Some are usable after washing. Some do not transmit impurity unless moisture carries it. Others cannot be sold, worn, patched into a human body, or turned into ordinary benefit. The listener has already learned the broad grammar of material purity; the new question is what happens when purity law meets animal products, human bodies, and commerce.

The middle of the chapter turns to menstruation and postpartum bleeding. It is a long series of questions because the state itself can be uncertain: bleeding may continue beyond ten days; purity may occur near the end of a prayer time; a blind woman may need another woman to examine a cloth; Ramadan fasting may collide with fear of harm from ghusl; postpartum bleeding may be measured by habit, by visible change, by twenty, forty, or sixty days, or by the possibility that menstruation followed childbirth. The source is not simply repeating the rule that a menstruating woman leaves prayer, fasting, mosque entry, Quran-touching, and marital approach. It is asking how the law acts when blood does not fit the simplest pattern.

The final movement is titled "disavowal," but its cases are not abstract denunciations. They concern sexual wrongdoing, marriage, pregnancy, inheritance, waiting periods, ownership, and the clearing of the womb. A woman wronged by adultery, a purchased pregnant woman, a wife connected to a deceased child's estate, a free Muslim mother, a slave, a polytheist, and a child whose lineage belongs to the bed all appear in compressed legal form. The chapter lands on a severe continuity: the same law that asks whether water, wool, blood, and bone are pure also asks whether a relation, a womb, a marriage, and an inheritance claim can be treated as clear.

## Hair, Skin, and Bone

The chapter opens with a question from a book of questions and answers arranged according to the chapters of the book *Pillars of Islam*. The questioner asks about "the rays of guidance" coming into contact with impure garments, skins of the dead, or "the seven" when the body is wet. Should the body be washed? The same question is asked about clothes washed by the hands of polytheists. The answer says that if the body comes into contact with such things, it must be washed, and the same applies to whatever polytheists have washed with their hands.

This opening should not be heard as a new doctrine of every contact. The earlier chapters already established the role of moisture, trace, washing, and real contact. The new stress is that certain materials remain legally serious because of their source: skins of the dead, impurity on garments, and washing by hands treated as impure in the source's legal world. When a wet body meets such things, the law does not ask the worshipper to pretend nothing has happened. It requires washing before prayer.

The next question turns to goat hair. The source asks about the interpretation of the pillars: it is not permissible to shear a goat's hair while the goat is alive, as one shears sheep's wool for benefit, yet it is permissible to dress in that hair, pray in it and upon it, and regard the remaining hair, skin, and bones as free of impurity if the animal was slaughtered properly. May one pray on the hair and skins if they were taken after proper slaughter?

The answer says yes. If the hair and skin are taken after proper slaughter, it is permissible to pray upon them. The source then identifies the goat as a kind of sheep, with a defect beginning from its sin upward and with two horns, a common type in India. That descriptive note is brief, but it shows how local animal classifications enter the legal question. The law is not floating over a generic animal. It is being applied to a known animal in a known region, where people want to know whether its hair behaves like sheep's wool.

The distinction is sharp. Hair cut while the animal is alive is not treated in the same way as wool that may be sheared for benefit. Hair and skin taken after lawful slaughter are treated as pure and prayer-usable. The animal's death under lawful slaughter changes the status of the material. The chapter therefore extends the practical purity arc into timing: not simply what material is involved, but when and under what legal act it was taken.

Another question asks about wool, fleece, or hair cut from an animal while it is still alive. Must it be washed like an impure garment? Before washing, if it touches a garment or the body, does it render them impure? The answer says that it is not permissible to pray in it until it is washed, although it does not itself make other things impure.

This is one of the chapter's careful middle distinctions. A thing may be unfit for prayer until washed without being a contagious source that contaminates everything it touches. The law can say, "Do not pray in this yet," without saying, "Everything near it is now impure." That distinction matters for household life. If every doubtful material transmitted impurity by mere proximity, the law would become impossible to live. If unwashed material were treated as already fit for prayer, the law would lose its seriousness. The answer holds both together.

A more commercial case follows. A vessel made from the skins of water animals comes from Vaghar, a large province where most inhabitants are non-believers. The vessel is filled with ghee or oil and brought to Ahmedabad for sale. Is this permissible? If that vessel is used to make soap by mixing lye and soda ash, may the soap be bought and sold?

The answer says that if the vessel is made of something that renders its contents impure, then the ghee becomes impure, and one may not pray in a garment soiled by it. It is permissible to sell and buy the ghee or soap if the buyer knows that it is impure. It is not permissible, however, to sell or buy the skins of the dead.

The chapter is again not merely concerned with private scruple. It ties purity to honest commerce. A seller may not hide the status of an impure product. The buyer must know. Soap can be made and traded under disclosure, but the skin of the dead itself is excluded from sale and purchase. The ruling protects the market from deception while still allowing limited use of an impure derivative.

The source then repeats the rule from the Book of the Interpretation of the Pillars: goat hair may not be cut except after slaughter. While the goat is alive, that ruling applies in full. It is not permissible to shear a goat's hair as one shears sheep's wool for benefit during life, though clothing may be made from it and prayer may be performed in it and upon it when the lawful condition is met. The rest of its hair, skin, and bones are free of impurity when purified by proper slaughter.

That repetition is not ornamental. It fixes the rule in a world where benefit could easily override discipline. Hair has value. Skin has value. Bones have value. A community may want to harvest usable material as early as possible. The text insists that lawful benefit is not simply whatever can be used. It is benefit ordered by the act that renders the material fit.

The chapter then moves from animal material to human repair. In the summary of works, if a person has bones that need patching, it is not permissible to patch them except with the bone of an animal whose flesh may lawfully be eaten after proper slaughter. If a person patches with the bone of a dead animal, or with the bone of an animal whose flesh is not eaten, or with the bone of a human being, then a ruling applies: he must repeat every prayer he prayed, and the authority compels him, if he knew what he had done, to remove it. If he did not know until he died and did not remove it, nothing is removed after his death.

The case is striking because it moves impurity into the body. The question is not a garment, mat, vessel, or soap. It is a foreign bone incorporated into a living person. The answer treats the wrong bone as religiously consequential. Prayer performed with that unlawful patch must be repeated when the person knew. Authority may compel removal. Yet the rule stops at death when the person did not know and died without removal.

Here the source's discipline has two edges. On one edge, the body may not be repaired with whatever material is available. The bone must come from an animal whose flesh is lawful and that was properly slaughtered. On the other edge, the dead body is not violated to correct an unknown past error. The law cares about purity, but it also cares about the boundary of the deceased.

The same paragraph states that one may not benefit from the hide of a human being as one benefits from the hide of an animal. This short sentence prevents a grotesque extension. Animal hides have lawful and unlawful uses depending on slaughter, death, and purification. Human remains do not become a useful material in the same way. A human body is not placed into the economy of skins.

The source then mentions a quilt made from the dead that does not adhere, meaning it does not stick to anything pure, yet one of them is wet and therefore becomes a source of impurity. The phrasing is compressed, but the rule returns to the recurring principle: moisture matters because it can carry impurity. A dead-derived material that does not adhere in a dry state is one case; wetness changes its effect.

This is why the chapter's first movement should be heard slowly rather than as a list of odd materials. The questions are arranged around increasingly intimate degrees of contact. First the body touches impure garments or dead skins. Then the worshipper wears or prays upon hair, wool, skin, or fleece. Then food or oil sits in a suspect vessel. Then bone is patched into the body itself. With each step, impurity is no longer merely outside the person. It comes nearer to clothing, commerce, prayer, and bodily structure. The source is training the listener to ask not only whether something looks clean, but how it entered the circle of use.

It also teaches that purity law has a memory. The goat hair remembers whether it was cut during life or taken after slaughter. The ghee remembers the skin-vessel that held it. The soap remembers the impure substance from which it was made, even when it has become useful. The patched bone remembers whether its animal was lawful and slaughtered. That memory is not superstition in the source's logic. It is the moral history of the material, and prayer is not built on materials whose history the worshipper has chosen to ignore.

Finally, from the Book of Reports, the chapter notes that they considered it reprehensible to make use of pork. This reference is brief, but it belongs to the same set. Pork is not discussed here as food alone. It appears among materials whose benefit is morally and ritually suspect. The chapter's first movement therefore gathers hair, skin, wool, bone, dead matter, human remains, vessels, soap, commerce, and wet contact into one question: how can material be used without pretending that its source, state, and transmission do not matter?

## Menstruation and Nifas

The chapter then enters menstruation and postpartum bleeding. The first question asks what has been established in Islamic law about a menstruating woman wearing a garment from the navel to the knees while her husband may approach her above the garment. Without such a garment, may he begin with her entire body, without intercourse in the vulva?

The answer says yes, he may begin with her body, though the first obligation is that he avoid what is beneath the garment out of concern that fluid may reach her womb. This is not a re-teaching of bodily purity. It is a regulation of marital nearness during a state in which the body is not approached in the ordinary way. The source permits intimacy, but draws its boundary at what may reach the womb.

The next ruling asks what happens when a woman menstruates and the bleeding continues for the full ten days, then continues for two, three, four, or five more days. She does not pray, does not fast, and her husband does not approach her, if it is confirmed that the blood is menstrual blood. She does not need to make up those prayers after purification. If there is doubt about the blood, then she is considered mustahadah, and so forth.

The distinction between menstruation and istihadah has appeared already in the purity arc, but here it becomes the center. The source does not treat every instance of blood after ten days as one simple category without examination. Confirmation matters. Doubt matters. Menstrual blood carries one set of consequences: no prayer, no fasting, no marital approach, and no make-up of those prayers. Istihadah carries another set, as later paragraphs explain. The listener should hear the law's concern for classification. The ruling depends on what the blood is.

The chapter briefly states that whoever misses the Eid al-Ghadir prayer and its fast may make it up on any day afterward as though it had been performed in its proper time. The statement appears suddenly within the menstrual discussion, but it shares one concern with the surrounding rulings: devotional time can be missed under lawful conditions, and the law tells the worshipper whether replacement is required or available.

Another question asks about a woman who becomes pure from menstruation or postpartum bleeding at the time of prayer. If she finishes her ritual bath just as the prayer time ends, must she make up that prayer? The answer says that if she completed the bath in good time, meaning her washing was sound, but the time passed before she could pray, she is not required to make up that prayer.

This ruling is mercifully precise. The obligation is not measured by a fantasy in which no time is needed to wash. If purification is completed properly and the prayer time expires before she can pray, she is not charged with making up that prayer. The law judges the actual sequence of purification and time, not only the abstract fact that a prayer time existed.

The episode should let that precision breathe, because it keeps the section from becoming merely restrictive. The woman is not told to pray before purification, and she is not punished for the time required to purify herself soundly. The demand is exact, but so is the exemption. In the source's legal imagination, obligation attaches to real capacity, real sequence, and real bodily transition. The worshipper does not become liable because the clock and the body crossed at an impossible angle.

The answers of Qadi al-Nu'man to Khattab bin Waseem then give a practical case. A blind woman asks about her menstruation and how she should determine it. She should take a white piece of cotton or cloth and have it checked by a woman she trusts in her religion. If the cloth comes out with color, she is menstruating. If it comes out white, she has become pure, and she should wash, pray, fast, and allow her husband to come to her. She follows the same procedure at the beginning of her menstrual cycle if she cannot sense it herself.

This case is one of the chapter's clearest teachings on embodied trust. The blind woman is not abandoned to uncertainty. Nor is she told that her inability to see cancels the law's distinctions. A trusted woman becomes part of the discernment. The test uses white cloth because the question is color. Purity is known through a practical method, religious trust, and observable sign.

Ramadan introduces another difficulty. If a woman becomes pure from menstrual blood before sunset but fears that ghusl would harm her, she may perform tayammum and fast, and she does not need to make up that fast. The same applies to a man who experiences a nocturnal emission during Ramadan: if he fears illness from washing that night, he may fast without ghusl, and his fast is valid.

This is a callback to the earlier teaching on tayammum paths, but with a new location. Tayammum is not merely a travel rule or a scarcity rule. Here it protects fasting when ghusl would cause harm. The fast remains valid. The law does not make bodily harm the price of ritual correctness. It gives the appointed substitute and preserves the worshipper's duty.

A question then asks about a woman who becomes pure in Ramadan and fasts for nine days, then menstruation returns to her on the tenth day. Is her fast for those nine days valid? The answer recalls that the minimum of menstruation is three days and the minimum of purity is ten days. Another question asks whether fasts during a separation period are valid when menstruation exceeds ten days and the blood remains thick and strong. The answer says the distinction between menstrual blood and istihadah blood has been mentioned with their differences, so one should rely on the relevant text.

Both answers point away from improvisation. The law has minimums, maximums, and markers. A nine-day interval raises one kind of question because the minimum purity is ten days. Thick blood beyond ten days raises another because the blood's character may still resemble menstruation, yet the category of istihadah may apply. The source does not solve every case by counting alone or by appearance alone. It asks how count, habit, color, thickness, and established distinctions work together.

The postpartum discussion is more complex. One question states that in Islamic law the maximum limit of postpartum bleeding is forty days, while some books state sixty days. Which is more correct? The answer comes from Hasan bin Noah. He says that the postpartum period is reckoned according to what the woman is accustomed to in duration, and the blood of the puerperium is distinguished from the blood of istihadah. When she becomes pure and the blood stops, she performs ghusl, arrives at prayer, and remains silent. Her husband may then approach her.

The source is resisting a single flat number. Habit matters. The character of the blood matters. Cessation matters. Ghusl marks the transition back into prayer and marital approach. The phrase "arrives at prayer, and remains silent" is terse, but the movement is clear: once pure, she returns to worship and lawful relation.

The Book of Purity and Souls gives a related rule. If a woman gave birth, or saw blood before giving birth while in a state of menstruation, and the blood continued until it ceased, then the menstruating woman washes when she becomes pure. If her bleeding is excessive, she is mustahadah. The blood of the puerperium is distinguished from istihadah just as menstrual blood is distinguished from istihadah. A summary says she is like a menstruating woman, and she and her husband must observe what is obligatory during menstruation. She washes when the blood of the puerperium is cut off.

The chapter's pattern is now unmistakable. Menstruation and nifas are treated as states that suspend particular acts. Istihadah is treated differently. The same outward sign, blood, can belong to different legal states. The worshipper's task is not to panic at the sign but to discern the state.

Another summary states that the woman in postpartum bleeding leaves prayer for the number of days she is accustomed to, according to her habit and as laid out in the *Pillars of Islam*. The author then says he saw several mentions in Shari'ah books of the Ahl al-Bayt, prayers of Allah upon them, concerning days: the minimum postpartum period is twenty days, the middle is forty days, and the maximum is sixty days. If blood continues beyond this, it does not exceed sixty days. Menstrual blood is distinct from istihadah blood, and the puerperium does not fall below twenty days. The woman in postpartum bleeding is never considered pure before twenty days, according to that book.

The author also admits that he did not find the exact original phrase at the time of writing and therefore did not write it. If he lives to receive and find that book, he says, he will write its exact phrase, God willing.

That admission matters. The chapter is full of authority, but here the compiler limits himself. He conveys the meaning he has seen, while refusing to fabricate an exact quotation he does not possess. For listeners, this is a small window into scholarly responsibility. The law may be strict, but the writer does not pretend to have an exact text when he does not.

He then clarifies the practical ruling. When the postpartum woman is free from blood, she is considered pure after twenty days. That ruling applies definitively. She then follows her accustomed pattern. If her bleeding exceeds her habit, she observes whether the blood has changed in character. If it has changed, it is istihadah. If not, then its ruling remains as first described.

Qadi al-Nu'man is quoted in verse-like lines from *The Chosen One*, and the author explains their meaning. If the blood of the puerperium extends beyond its customary duration and remains thick like postpartum blood, she ceases prayer and fasting and abstains from her husband for two months. After two months, if blood continues, she is undoubtedly mustahadah. She washes and makes up the prayers and fasts omitted after the end of her habitual period up to exactly two months, as a precaution, because the matter is doubtful: she may have menstruated following the postpartum period.

This is the chapter's densest legal judgment. Habit establishes an expectation. Thick continuing blood may justify treating the state as postpartum, or as menstruation following postpartum, for up to two months. After that, the continuing blood is istihadah. Because the interval between habit and two months is uncertain, prayers and fasts omitted after the habitual period are made up as precaution. The law does not pretend uncertainty is certainty; it builds a precaution around the uncertainty.

The source then returns to menstruation. If a woman prays at the proper time and then menstruates, she does not make up that prayer. If she menstruates for one or two days, then the bleeding stops and she sees purity for ten days, there is nothing wrong unless the menstrual period was less than three days. If she did not pray during that short time, she must repeat those prayers. If she sees blood for a day or two, then sees purity for less than ten days, then sees blood again, the first and second instances of blood are counted as menstruation.

The rule depends on minimums. Less than three days does not complete menstruation. Ten days of purity can separate states. Less than ten days does not. The source is training the listener to think with thresholds without turning thresholds into empty arithmetic. The body is observed through time.

The chapter then gives a narrative illustration from the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him. One of his wives was with him in his bed, and he confirmed what she herself testified, saying "Yes," meaning she had menstruated. The mustahadah is then defined as one from whom blood does not stop, but whose blood is distinct from menstrual blood. When her menstruation comes, the blood is thick. When the menstrual period passes, the blood becomes thin.

As long as menstrual blood is present, she is in menstruation: she does not pray, does not enter the mosque, and her husband does not approach her. When menstrual blood ceases and is replaced by the other blood, she purifies herself as from menstruation, then prays, fasts in purity, enters the mosque, and allows her husband to approach her. Whatever blood issues in that state requires ablution, and this is its ruling.

This passage gives the episode its central distinction in its simplest form. Menstruation suspends prayer, mosque entry, fasting, and approach. Istihadah does not do so after purification; it requires ablution for what issues. The law is not saying that blood no longer matters. It is saying that the kind of blood matters.

This is why the chapter keeps returning to trusted signs. A blind woman uses a white cloth and the eyes of a religiously trusted woman. A fasting woman considers whether ghusl will harm her and uses tayammum when it will. A postpartum woman measures habit, change, and duration. A mustahadah watches the blood's thickness and thinness. These are not separate techniques scattered through the chapter. They form one epistemology of the body: the law asks the body to be read truthfully, through signs that can be checked, not through embarrassment, concealment, or guesswork.

The distinction also protects worship from two opposite errors. One error would force a woman to pray, fast, or permit approach while she remains in menstruation or nifas. The other would keep her away from prayer, fasting, the mosque, and her husband after the state has changed into istihadah or purity. The source resists both. It does not treat caution as automatically pious when caution ignores the category God has assigned to the state. Correct classification is itself obedience.

The Book of the Interpretation of the Pillars then states that whoever approaches a menstruating woman and does what he should not have done must seek God's forgiveness, repent to Him for his sin, and give charity despite having sinned. Another ruling says that if a wife is called by her husband, she must obey him; if he dislikes her, nothing is upon her. If the man does not know her condition, even if it is her own secret, then the sin lies with her, and nothing is upon him if he does not know she is menstruating.

These are severe rulings, and the chapter should present them as the source presents them. The source places responsibility on knowledge and concealment. The man who knowingly does what he should not must repent and give charity. The woman who conceals her condition bears the sin when he does not know. The episode should not convert these rulings into modern therapeutic language. It should let the source's moral allocation stand, while making clear what the source actually says.

A menstruating woman should not touch the Quran. Questions from Shaykh Shimon ibn Muhammad Ghori al-Hindi to Hasan bin Idris then ask about a person in janabah or menstruation who wishes to recite supplications or texts containing Quranic verses, whether aware of the verses or not. Should such a person recite them or refrain? Since tahlil, takbir, tahmid, tasbih, and the basmala appear in the Quran, may such a person recite them?

The answer is firm: a woman in menstruation, or a person in janabah, may not recite the Quran or any portion of it, whether in supplication or in written form. Nor may such a person recite the basmala with the intention of Quranic recitation. The respondent closes by saying to hold firmly to what has been mentioned and be among the believers who worship their Lord until certainty comes to them.

This final ruling ties the chapter back to major washing and Quranic recitation, but with a new precision. The issue is not simply whether a phrase appears in the Quran. Intention matters. The basmala as Quranic recitation is barred in that state; formulas that overlap with Quranic wording cannot be treated carelessly. The purity of speech, text, body, and intention converge.

That convergence is the bridge into the final legal cases. In the menstrual section, the hidden state of the body governs prayer, fasting, touch, speech, and marital approach. In the disavowal section, the hidden state of the womb governs marriage, inheritance, sale, and lineage. The subject changes, but the method remains: do not proceed as though a concealed bodily fact has no legal force.

## Disavowal and Waiting

The last section is titled "On the Mention of Disavowal." The word is stark, but the cases are technical. The first ruling says that whoever commits adultery with a virgin woman and then wishes to marry her may not contract marriage with her until after she has been discharged. The phrase points to clearing the womb, ensuring that any pregnancy or bodily consequence of the unlawful act is not hidden beneath a new contract.

The logic is not only punitive. It is classificatory. Marriage is not allowed to cover uncertainty. The womb must be cleared before the relation can be lawfully reconstituted. The chapter therefore moves from purity of materials and purity of bodily states to purity of lineage and legal relation.

Another case asks about a man whose wife has a son from another man. That child dies leaving an estate. The husband must observe a waiting period from her until her womb is cleared. If she is pregnant, the child inherits from the deceased, but only if the mother is neither a slave nor a polytheist. In such cases, there is no valid inheritance claim. If the mother is a free Muslim woman, the inheritance belongs to her and not to the brothers. She need not observe a waiting period in that case.

The compressed wording is difficult, but its concerns are visible. Pregnancy may create an heir. The mother's legal status affects whether the inheritance claim is valid. The brothers cannot simply take what belongs elsewhere. Waiting protects lineage and property from haste. The clearing of the womb is therefore not only a bodily matter; it determines who may inherit.

The source then states from the summary of legal opinions that a free woman observes a waiting period of two months. From the book of transmitted reports, scholars held that a woman who has reached forty-five nights is considered to have completed her waiting period. For one who is young and reaches the age of menstruation, the seller must declare that she has been discharged for nine months.

These numbers belong to a legal world in which waiting is evidence. Two months, forty-five nights, and nine months are not decorative figures. They are attempts to make hidden bodily possibility visible through time. The source is concerned with whether the womb is clear, whether pregnancy could be present, and whether a buyer, husband, or claimant may proceed without fraud.

The repeated concern is disclosure. Earlier, the seller of impure soap had to tell the buyer what the soap was. Here, the seller must declare discharge when the young woman reaches menstrual age. The object of disclosure is different, but the moral structure is similar. A transaction or relation cannot be clean when the decisive fact has been hidden. The law therefore makes time, declaration, and waiting serve truth.

The Book of Adornments states that the child belongs to the bed, and the fornicator is entitled to nothing. He has no claim to the child's lineage. This principle then governs the final case. If a man commits adultery with a woman and then purchases her while she is pregnant, and he does not approach her until she gives birth, the child may be sold, because the child belongs to the bed and the fornicator has no claim.

The ruling is hard to hear, but its legal spine is clear. Illicit sexual action does not create lineage rights for the fornicator. The child's legal belonging follows the bed, not the claim of the adulterer. The source's language also reflects institutions of slavery and sale that must be presented as the source's world, not modernized away. The episode should not soften those terms or translate them into something more comfortable.

What connects this disavowal section to the rest of the chapter is the demand for lawful clarity. Hair is not harvested as if timing did not matter. Bone is not patched into the body as if source did not matter. Blood is not treated as one undifferentiated sign when menstruation, nifas, and istihadah carry different rulings. Quranic recitation is not approached as if intention did not matter. Marriage, purchase, waiting, pregnancy, inheritance, and lineage are not treated as if desire or convenience could erase the womb's legal uncertainty.

The chapter therefore closes the purity sequence by expanding purity beyond washing. Purity includes lawful material, truthful disclosure, bodily discernment, valid worship, guarded speech, marital restraint, and lineage clarity. Its severity is not random. It is the source's insistence that worship and communal life be built from recognized states rather than from hidden mixtures.

## What this episode lands

This episode lands on purity as disciplined recognition of bodily states and material origins. The source does not allow the worshipper to treat hair, skin, bone, blood, birth, recitation, marriage, or lineage as neutral once their legal conditions are known. At the same time, it does not collapse every uncertainty into panic. It distinguishes unwashed from transmitting, menstruation from istihadah, habit from excess, harm from obligation, known sin from ignorance, and lawful waiting from concealed confusion.

The forward motion from earlier chapters is that purity now becomes a grammar for hidden transitions. Earlier the book taught washing, contact, food, and grooming; here it asks how a community knows when a body has changed status, when a material can be used, when speech may touch Quranic wording, and when a womb is clear enough for marriage, inheritance, or sale. The chapter advances the book's larger argument that faithful practice depends on authoritative knowledge applied to real conditions, not on impulse, convenience, or inherited habit.
