## Where this episode picks up

A man goes an entire day without food or water, and spends the whole of it tearing a neighbour's reputation apart. Has he fasted? The book's answer, delivered in its opening sentences on the subject, is that he has fasted with one part of himself and broken the fast with several others — and that the arithmetic of the day is not a yes or a no but a proportion.

The previous chapter closed on the last levy of the Book of Zakat, the one counted by head rather than by wealth and owed at the close of the fast. The book now turns to the fast itself. Its heading arrives in this transmission as the Book of Lent, which is the Christian fast and plainly not what follows; everything under it concerns the month of Ramadan. The oddity is reported once and not repaired.

Two source headings carry this chapter. The first is the mention of the obligation of fasting in the month of Ramadan, and it does three things: it defines what a fast is, says who owes one, and says whose fast does not count at all. The second announces a Shari'ah ruling that fasting becomes obligatory for a boy once he can distinguish right from left — and then supplies something much larger and only glancingly about boys: the complete division of fasting into its categories, seven obligatory and twelve recommended, recited from the fourteenth council of al-Mustansiriyya.

The authorities are fewer than in the zakat chapters and more varied in kind. Mukhtasar al-Musannaf supplies the definition and the scope. Our master Ja'far ibn Sayyidina Sulayman — may God bless him — supplies a passage of rebuke from his noble epistle entitled the Sharpest Sword in Nullifying Innovations, addressed to the people of Yemen. Our master al-Qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad, may God sanctify him, supplies a report from our master the Imam al-Baqir Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali, peace be upon him, out of Mukhtasar al-Athar al-Sharifa. The fourteenth majlis of the Mustansiriyya councils, may God's peace be upon its author, supplies the catalogue. Our master Zayd ibn Muhammad — may God sanctify his soul — returns from the hidden message, the same work that fixed the rates in the chapter on coin and herd. And the chapter closes on a sentence from Mawlana Amir al-Mu'minin Ali ibn Abi Talib, may Allah's peace be upon him, out of his four hundred manners.

One conviction runs under all of it, stated at the very start rather than argued toward. A fast is not a subtraction of food from a day. It is a restraint laid across every gate through which a person meets the world — and because the mouth is only one gate, two men can pass the same day in identical outward abstention and have performed two different acts, or, in the limiting case the source names without softening, one act and no act at all.

That conviction is what makes the chapter's second movement intelligible. If the tongue is the first sense the definition names, then how a person speaks about the month is not separable from how he keeps it — which is the ground on which an epistle here takes an entire community to task, not for breaking a fast, but for what they call the month they are fasting.

## The Shield of Every Sense

It is stated in the book Mukhtasar al-Musannaf that fasting is a shield from the Fire.

The image is worth slowing over, because it decides everything that follows. A shield is not a payment and not a merit badge. It is a thing held up between a person and something coming at him. To call the fast a shield is to say its function is defensive — it stands between the one who keeps it and the Fire, and its value is measured by how much of him it covers.

And the source immediately tells us that the covering is not uniform. Fasting, it says, is a shield of the tongue, the ears, the eyes, and all the other senses. It is not simply a matter of abstaining from food and drink.

Read the order of that list. The tongue comes first, ahead of the ears and the eyes, and the mouth's other function — taking food in — is not mentioned in the list at all. It appears only afterward, and only to be demoted: not simply a matter of abstaining from food and drink. The definition is built so that the thing everyone means by fasting arrives late and diminished, as one item in a set rather than the set itself.

Notice too that the source names organs rather than offences. A tongue restrained is one not saying what it could say; a restrained ear is not hearing what it could hear, and a restrained eye is not looking where it could look. Each is a refusal rather than an action, which is why all of them can run at once across a single day without competing for the person's attention. And the list does not close. It runs tongue, ears, eyes, and then all the other senses — an open clause, so the definition cannot be turned into a checklist a person completes and sets down. Whatever number of gates a body has, the fast is laid across all of them.

Then comes the clause that turns the definition into an accounting system. For every sense that is restrained, there is a reward, and whoever upholds that is truly fasting.

Two things are being said. The first is that reward is distributed rather than granted in a lump: it accrues per sense, so the fast is not one act but several running in parallel through the same day. The second is carried in the phrase truly fasting, which only makes sense if there is a way of fasting that is not true — not fraudulent, necessarily, but partial, thinned, a fast in some restricted sense. The source does not leave that implication to the reader. It states the other half directly: whoever abandons any of it diminishes the virtue of his fast proportionally to what he has left.

Proportionally to what he has left. The fast does not shatter when it is violated; it shrinks. A man who guards his mouth and his eyes but not his tongue keeps a fast of some size, smaller than the one he might have kept, and the size is set by how much he released. This is a different mechanism from the one the book uses elsewhere. In the chapter on the corrections of the prayer the question was always binary — which interruptions void a prayer, which leave it standing, which require a compensating prostration — and the answer was a line with two sides. Here there is no line, only a scale, and a person can stand anywhere on it.

That difference suits the kind of act a fast is. A prayer is a bounded performance with a beginning, a fixed sequence and an end, and can be broken the way a sequence is broken. A fast is a condition held across a whole day in every direction at once, and a condition held can be held more or less well — which is what the shield image says spatially, since a shield can be raised, lowered, or half dropped and remains the same shield throughout.

Having defined the act, the source states who owes it, and states it with unusual economy: fasting the month of Ramadan is obligatory upon every adult and sound Muslim, male and female.

Three conditions and one explicit inclusion. Adulthood, soundness of mind and body, and Islam — and then, said plainly rather than left to inference, male and female. The obligation is not differentiated by sex anywhere in this sentence, and the sentence goes out of its way to say so.

More striking is what the sentence does not contain. This book has been meticulous about exemptions elsewhere: it gave the traveller a worked-out licence with distances and conditions, and it settled the case of the sick, the frightened and the community under drought. Here there is nothing of the kind — no traveller, no invalid, no pregnant or nursing woman, no aged person past the reach of the obligation. The scope arrives as a single clause, and the qualifications a reader of the earlier chapters expects simply do not come in this slice. That silence is reported here as silence, not filled in from what other books on fasting are known to say.

Then the chapter's hardest sentence, which the source places immediately afterward and joins to nothing: he also stated, there is no fasting for one who is a hypocrite and disobeys.

Not diminished. Not thinned in proportion. There is no fasting — the scale has a floor, and beneath the floor the account is simply closed. Two disqualifiers are named, and they are of different kinds. Disobedience is a matter of what a person does. Hypocrisy is a matter of the distance between what he is and what he presents, which is to say it is not an action at all but a relation between two things.

That the fast should be singled out for this warning repays attention, because of all the obligations in this book it is the one with no outward form whatsoever. A prayer can be watched. An ablution can be watched. A payment of zakat leaves a recipient who can testify it arrived — the previous chapters spent great effort on exactly that, on who receives, in what order, and on whether a partial payment performs a concealment. But nobody can watch a man not eat. There is no posture, no motion, no article of dress, no witness. A fast is invisible when kept and invisible when merely claimed, and the two look identical from every angle available to anyone else.

Which is why the definition at the top of this section and the disqualification at the bottom of it belong to one argument, though the source sets them side by side without a connecting word. If the fast were only abstention from food, hypocrisy would have almost nothing to work with — a man either ate or he did not, and he would know. But the fast has just been defined as the restraint of the tongue, the ears, the eyes and every other sense, which is precisely the portion nobody audits, including, much of the time, the one performing it.

So the first heading stacks three things in about a hundred words. The fast is a shield across the whole person; its worth rises and falls with how much of the person it covers; and beneath a certain condition of the heart it stops being a fast at all, however flawlessly the visible portion was performed. It has also, without announcing it, put the tongue at the head of the list — first among the senses to be shielded, and first, by implication, among the ways a fast is lost. The next movement takes that up and applies it to a word nobody would have thought was at stake.

## The Crescent and the Name

Before the rebuke, a clarification — and it is the largest claim in the chapter, made in one sentence and then dropped.

What has been transmitted concerning fasting upon sighting the crescent moon, the source says, and what is mentioned in this chapter and in the two chapters before it, pertains to those who are distant from the Imam and do not know the determination of the crescents by our master and Mawlana.

Three separate things happen in that sentence. First, the transmitted material about beginning the fast when the new moon is seen is not abolished, corrected or argued with; it is scoped — declared to address a particular condition rather than everyone. Second, the condition is one of distance: those far from the Imam, who do not know the determination. Third, and this is easy to read past, the scoping reaches backward over this chapter and the two before it, which is to say over the calendrical machinery the series has just worked through — the year that must pass over wealth, the boundary at the dawn of the day of al-Fitr, the whole apparatus that assumes somebody knows which day it is.

The underlying teaching about where knowledge sits was laid down at the very beginning of this book, in the movement on guardianship and knowledge, and is not re-argued here; it is spent. What is new is its application to the calendar. For the one with access, the beginning of the month is a determination — something settled and announced. Sighting is the method of the person who cannot reach the determination, and the reports about sighting are addressed to him.

And the fast is where that distinction bites hardest, for a reason peculiar to it among the pillars. Prayer times are read off the sun every day, and the book has already walked through them; a person who loses one has lost a few hours and can find the next. A month's beginning is a single judgement that then governs thirty consecutive days at once. Get it wrong by one day and every day afterward is wrong — the fast begins on a day that is not the first and ends on a day that is not the last, and no scrupulousness inside the thirty repairs the frame around them. A crescent is a thin thread on which to hang a month, which is why a determination, where it can be had, is what sighting substitutes for.

From that clarification the source moves, without transition, to a man with a sharp title and a sharper posture. Our master Ja'far ibn Sayyidina Sulayman — may God bless him — stated in his noble epistle entitled the Sharpest Sword in Nullifying Innovations that he urged those who were awake to wake others, and warned those of the people of Yemen who had fallen into deep slumber, who had gone along with it and been complacent.

Note what the charge is and is not. The sleepers are not accused of denying anything or of adopting a rival doctrine. The accusation is drowsiness, and its two named components are going along and being complacent — consenting to a practice because it is the practice, and being comfortable there. The remedy is correspondingly social rather than doctrinal: the awake are to wake the others. Nobody here is being argued out of a position; they are being shaken.

Then the epistle itself speaks, addressed directly to an assembly of hearers, and it is given here in full as the source gives it.

This too is something that most of you have heard and known: that the holy month of Ramadan is not honoured with the full honour that God Almighty and His Messenger, peace be upon him, have prescribed for it, because it is the month of the great and generous God. The proper reverence is to precede the word month as Allah Almighty said, the month of Ramadan — yet you address it by calling it Ramadan alone, which is not appropriate at all and does not lead to what is right. A person does not address one he holds dear except with a word of honour placed before his name, as is well known among you, who know his glory and how to draw near.

A clause follows in the transmission that has lost whatever it was attached to: as for what has been stated, and to Allah all things return. The formula is a Quranic tag, and here it sits after a lead-in that introduces something the manuscript no longer carries. It is reproduced as it stands rather than joined to a sentence somebody else supplies.

The argument is not a legal one and does not pretend to be. It is an argument from address, and its force is a social analogy anyone hearing it recognises at once: you do not call a person you honour by his bare name. You put a word in front of it. Everyone in the room already does this daily, with people far less deserving of it than the month under discussion — as is well known among you is the epistle pressing exactly that point. The fault is therefore not ignorance of a rule but an inconsistency the hearers carry around without noticing, which is what makes drowsiness the right word for it.

And there is something quietly strange about the word being demanded. It is not a title of praise — not holy, not blessed, not noble. It is month, the plainest noun available, adding no glory of its own. What it adds is agreement with a wording: the reverence consists in saying it as scripture said it. The epistle is precise on this — the proper reverence is to precede the word month, as Allah Almighty said, the month of Ramadan (chapter 2, verse 185).

The proof then arrives from the tradition, and it is compact. It has been narrated from our master al-Qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad, may God sanctify him, in the book Mukhtasar al-Athar al-Sharifa, that our master the Imam al-Baqir Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Ali, peace be upon him, said: do not say Ramadan, but say as Allah Almighty said, the month of Ramadan.

The report has two halves and the second is load-bearing. A prohibition — do not say — and then an instruction whose authority is explicitly not the speaker's preference but the wording of revelation: say as Allah said. The Imam is not adding etiquette to the language; he is pointing at a phrase already in the Book and declining to improve on it.

What follows presses the point into a question. Whoever has heard the word of Allah Almighty — and His permission is in that saying — how can it be permissible for him to disobey and turn away from it? The parenthetical clause about permission is obscure in the form it reaches us and is left as it is; the question around it is not obscure at all, and asks only how a person who has heard the wording can then decline it.

Then the source escalates, and this is where the chapter's two movements lock together. As for the people of indifference, and those who, after standing on this matter, belittle the command of Allah Almighty and His decree — the said Imam al-Baqir, peace be upon him, has made this serious, as stated in this noble book, on the grounds that it falls under the censure contained in the words of Allah Almighty in the verses of His Holy Book, the place where the Almighty says:

⟪ar:يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لِمَ تَقُولُونَ مَا لَا تَفْعَلُونَ⟫ — "O you who believe, why do you say what you do not do?" (chapter 61, verse 2)

⟪ar:كَبُرَ مَقْتًا عِندَ اللَّهِ أَن تَقُولُوا مَا لَا تَفْعَلُونَ⟫ — "Greatly hateful in the sight of Allah is that you say what you do not do." (chapter 61, verse 3)

Follow the classification being made, because it is doing real work. The verses censure a gap — between what a person says and what he does. The offence under discussion is a manner of naming. To place the naming fault under these verses is to say that the gap can run in either direction: a man may say what he does not do, and he may equally fail to say what he claims to hold. He professes reverence for the month and then declines the one word that would enact the profession. That is not a lapse of vocabulary. It is the same discrepancy the verses name, arriving from the other side.

And this is why the naming rule belongs in a chapter about fasting rather than in a chapter about speech. The definition given in the first heading made the fast a shield of the tongue before it made it anything else. The naming ruling is that shield applied to the month itself — the tongue's discipline, exercised on the very thing the tongue is being disciplined for. The person whose fast does not count, the one the first heading called a hypocrite, and the person censured here for saying what he does not do are described in two different vocabularies, and the distance between the two descriptions is very small. In both, the defect is a seam between the inward and the outward. In both, the visible performance is intact.

The epistle's addressees, it should be said, were not accused of failing to fast; nothing in the passage suggests the people of Yemen were eating in daylight. They were keeping the month and calling it by the wrong name, and the epistle treats that as a failure of honour serious enough to draw a sword-titled letter and a verse of censure. The chapter's estimate of what a fast consists of has by now expanded twice: from the stomach to all the senses, and from the senses to the words a community uses about the season it keeps.

## Seven Obligations, Twelve Devotions

The second heading announces a Shari'ah ruling about when a boy becomes obliged to fast — once he can distinguish right from left. Hold that promise in mind, because the material recited under the heading is a great deal larger than that and settles the boy's case somewhere other than where the heading points.

It is stated in the fourteenth majlis of the Mustansiriyya councils, may God's peace be upon its author: let us recite for you what pertains to the meaning of fasting and its division into categories.

The council offers meaning and then delivers a catalogue, and the substitution is the point rather than a broken promise. In the first heading, fasting was defined by what it guards; here it is defined by what occasions it — the complete list of situations in a life that call a fast into being. The first says what a fast is while it is kept, the second says why there is one to keep.

It divides into sections, the council says, seven of which are obligatory, twelve of which are recommended or delegated.

The seven obligatory fasts, as the council gives them:

First, fasting the month of Ramadan every year. Second, fasting two consecutive months for one who deliberately breaks his fast on a single day and cannot afford an expiation. Third, fasting two consecutive months for one who kills a believer by mistake, if he cannot arrange for manumission — and likewise in expiation, until resurrection, a compressed clause that does not resolve in the form it reaches us and is left standing. Fourth, fasting two months in expiation for zihar, for one who cannot free a slave; the council names the offence without describing it, and no description is supplied here. Fifth, fasting in expiation for perjury, for one who can find neither food nor clothing — fasting three days. Sixth, fasting in lieu of the gift offering for tamattu' hajj when one cannot find a sacrifice — ten days. Seventh, fasting for a vow.

Now look at the shape of the list, because it is not seven parallel duties.

The first is the only one that is a season. It arrives on the calendar, comes to everyone at once, and recurs annually whether or not anything has happened to the person who owes it.

Then five in a row share a single structure, and once seen it is impossible to miss. Cannot afford an expiation. Cannot arrange for manumission. Cannot free a slave. Can find neither food nor clothing. Cannot find a sacrifice. Five times the same hinge: something is owed, the ordinary discharge of it is a payment or a manumission or a sacrifice, and the person cannot make it. The fast is what he pays with instead.

That gives the fast an economic character it did not have in the first heading, and a severe proportion: a single day broken on purpose costs sixty consecutive days from the man without means, while the man with means settles the same fault with an expiation he can buy. The council does not remark on the disparity and neither does this chapter. What it records is the structure — the body is the currency of last resort. Fasting is the one form of satisfaction that requires no property at all, which is why it is the fallback in five situations that have nothing else in common.

And then the seventh breaks the pattern completely. Fasting for a vow is not an expiation, substitutes for nothing, and answers no fault. It is the only obligatory fast a person manufactures for himself: he was under no duty, he bound himself, and the binding is enforceable like the rest. So the seven sort into three kinds — one imposed by the calendar, five imposed by inability, one imposed by the person on himself.

The recommended list — which the council introduces the second time as the recommended and mourning fasts where it first called them recommended or delegated, a variance reproduced here rather than settled — runs as follows.

First, the fasting of i'tikaf, named without explanation. Second, fasting of Sha'ban. Third, fasting every Thursday on the first day of each month — a clause that cannot be read as it stands, since a Thursday and the first day of a month are not the same thing, and it is given here in the damaged form the source carries. Fourth, fasting the middle Wednesday. Fifth, fasting the last Thursday. Sixth, fasting the three white days. Seventh, the fast of Dawud, peace be upon him, which is fasting one day and breaking the fast the next — and this is the most rigorous fast. Eighth, fasting on Friday, making it continuous with Thursday or Saturday, without singling out Friday for fasting alone. Ninth, fasting the day of Arafah for those who are not performing the pilgrimage. Tenth, fasting for a boy when he reaches the age of seven. Eleventh, the expiation fast for hunting, which varies according to what Allah has ruled in that matter. Twelfth, fasting to compensate for shaving one's head due to harm.

Several things in that list are worth stopping on.

Items three, four and five form a pattern even in their damaged state. A middle Wednesday and a last Thursday imply a first of something, and the third item is where that first belongs; what survives of it no longer parses. The three describe a rhythm laid across each month rather than three unrelated days, and the shape of the rhythm is legible even though one of its points is not. It is left broken here: the standard forms of this triad are available elsewhere, and importing one would be supplying another book's sentence in place of this book's damage.

The seventh item rates itself — the fast of Dawud is the most rigorous fast — and the rating sits inside the recommended category, not the obligatory one, which means severity and obligation run on separate axes here. The heaviest discipline in the list is one nobody is required to take up, while the sixty-day expiations, heavier still in duration, fall on a person rather than being chosen by him.

The eighth is a restriction wearing the clothes of a recommendation. Friday may be fasted, but only joined to the day before or the day after, and never alone — which keeps the fast from becoming a marker on a day that already carries a gathering of its own. The book established Friday's assembly and its order earlier in the series, and this clause protects that arrangement from being quietly supplemented by a private observance setting the day apart a second time, at the faster's own initiative.

The ninth splits the community by location. The day of Arafah is fasted by those not performing the pilgrimage — meaning it is not fasted by the pilgrims standing on the plain that gives the day its name. The fast is the non-pilgrim's way of taking part in something happening elsewhere, and the people at the centre of the event are precisely the ones who do not keep it.

The tenth is where the heading's promise comes due, and it is not paid in the coin the heading offered. The heading announced a Shari'ah ruling that fasting becomes obligatory for a boy once he can distinguish right from left — a criterion of discernment, and an obligation. What the list supplies is a fast for a boy when he reaches the age of seven — a criterion of age, and a place among the recommended rather than the obligatory. The two do not obviously describe the same ruling, and the source does not reconcile them; both are recorded here as they stand. The book has already taught, in its chapter on the forming of children, how a child is brought into the prayer by stages rather than at a single threshold, and the same gradualism is visible in the seven-year mark — but the discrepancy between this heading and its own list is reported rather than dissolved into that earlier teaching.

The eleventh and twelfth sit oddly, and the oddity is worth naming. Both are expiations — one for hunting while in the sacred state, one for shaving the head because of harm — and expiation was the defining feature of the obligatory list, not this one. The eleventh is also the only fast in the catalogue whose length is not fixed: it varies according to what Allah has ruled in that matter, proportioned to the case rather than stated in days, and the council declines to enumerate the variations. The two categories overlap at their edges, and the boundary is less clean in the delivery than in the promise.

One thing the council does deliver exactly: it promised twelve and gives twelve, though the last two arrive after a break and in a changed voice, introduced as the eleventh and the twelfth where the first ten were numbered plainly. That is a better record than the council quoted in the previous chapter managed of its own enumeration, noted here in passing rather than made into an argument.

Two short passages close the heading, and both reach backward through the chapter.

Our master Zayd ibn Muhammad — may God sanctify his soul — stated in his hidden message that after prayer and zakat, it is obligatory upon worshippers to fast the month of Ramadan. He commanded that one month out of the twelve be fasted, saying: the month of Ramadan is thirty days.

Three details. The ordering places fasting third, after prayer and after zakat — the sequence this book has itself just followed, arriving at the Book of Fasting directly out of two chapters on zakat. Framing the obligation as one month out of the twelve states it as a proportion of the year rather than as a named season: how much of a life is claimed, rather than which part. And the flat assertion that the month is thirty days is more interesting than it looks, because a month whose beginning waits on a crescent cannot have its length announced in advance with that confidence. A determined calendar can. The clarification at the top of this chapter is what makes a plain thirty sayable at all.

A small confirmation also runs through this whole slice, easy to miss and impossible to unsee once noticed. Every authority quoted here uses the full form. Mukhtasar al-Musannaf: fasting the month of Ramadan is obligatory. The council's first obligatory item: fasting the month of Ramadan every year. Zayd ibn Muhammad, twice in one sentence. The rule the epistle demanded is the rule this chapter's own sources keep — and Sha'ban, which carries no such ruling, is named bare throughout.

Finally, Mawlana Amir al-Mu'minin Ali ibn Abi Talib — may Allah's peace be upon him — stated in his four hundred manners: fasting three days of each month, along with the fast of Sha'ban, dispels the obsessions of the chest and brings tranquility to the heart.

The collection arrives here under another of its damaged titles; the series has met this same work before as four hundred counsels and as four hundred points of conduct, and each form is left as it comes.

It is the only sentence in the chapter describing what a fast does to the person keeping it, in this life. Everything else has spoken of reward, obligation, expiation, of what is owed and to whom. This speaks of a chest and a heart, and of two effects — something dispelled and something arriving. And it draws its fasts from the council's own list rather than proposing new ones: Sha'ban is the second recommended item, and three days of each month is either the sixth, the three white days, or the monthly rhythm of items three to five. The source does not say which, and this chapter does not choose.

So the chapter ends where it began. Its first sentence called the fast a shield, a thing held between a person and what is coming at him; its last says that certain fasts dispel the obsessions of the chest. Both describe something kept out. What differs is the direction of the threat — and that in the closing sentence the person defended can feel the result.

## What this episode lands

This chapter opens the Book of Fasting by refusing, in its first hundred words, the definition most people carry. The fast is a shield across the whole person — tongue first, then ears, then eyes, then every other sense, with food and drink arriving late and named as insufficient on their own. Reward accrues per sense restrained; virtue diminishes in proportion to whatever was released; and beneath the scale is a floor, where hypocrisy and disobedience close the account and there is no fast at all. The obligation falls on every adult and sound Muslim, male and female, stated so briefly that the expected exemptions are simply absent from this slice.

The month is then honoured in two registers. In its timing: the reports about beginning the fast at the sighting of the crescent are scoped to those distant from the Imam who do not know the determination, and the scoping reaches backward over the two preceding chapters as well. In its name: an epistle titled as a sword takes the people of Yemen to task not for breaking the fast but for calling the month by its bare name, the Imam al-Baqir requires the wording scripture used, and the fault is classified under the verses censuring those who say what they do not do.

Then the fast is counted out in full. Seven obligatory — one seasonal, five that are the fallback of a person who cannot pay or manumit or sacrifice, one a person binds upon himself. Twelve recommended, running from seclusion and Sha'ban through a monthly rhythm whose first point is damaged, the alternating fast rated the most rigorous of all, a Friday that may never be fasted alone, a day of Arafah kept by everyone except the pilgrims, a boy at seven where the heading promised a ruling about discernment, and two pilgrimage expiations sitting inside the recommended list although expiation is what defined the obligatory one.

The thread tying the three movements together is the tongue: named first among the senses the fast shields, disciplined in the naming ruling, and censured in the closing verses. A man who eats nothing and speaks carelessly has, by this chapter's account, held up a shield with a hole in it — and the size of the hole is the size of the loss.
