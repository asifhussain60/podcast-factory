## Where this episode opens

What if purity is tested not at the edge of the prayer mat, but in the marketplace, the kitchen, the comb, the child, the haircut, and the customs a community has repeated so long that no one remembers to ask whether they are lawful? This episode picks up after the book's major teaching on bodily and material purity without re-teaching it. As established earlier, purity is not mere neatness and not mere suspicion; here the source asks how that discipline enters ordinary food, wet contact, grooming, gendered adornment, circumcision, and public modesty.

The chapter begins with practical food questions. Can oil pressed from pits that passed through crows be sold or eaten? What happens to honey, ghee, oil, or broth when a mouse dies in it? Can impure liquid be turned into soap? How many traces of droppings in food become too many? What about edible goods sold by non-Muslims in Muslim markets, or the cooking of a servant who is not careful about ritual purity? These are not abstract cases. They are market cases, kitchen cases, household cases, and cases where disgust, necessity, and evidence pull in different directions.

Then the source turns to natural cleanliness: circumcision, hair parting, beard trimming, combs, henna, tattooing, filing teeth, piercing ears, and the grooming customs of India. Some of these rulings are brief answers to questions. Others open into a long admonitory letter from Mawlana Ja'far ibn Sulayman, who rebukes practices he sees as innovations among the communities of Yemen and Hind. That letter is the chapter's strongest voice. It does not merely give scattered rulings. It argues that bodily customs carry religious meaning, that gendered adornment should not be inverted, that a private act should not be turned into public display, and that haste or delay in circumcision can both violate the Shari'ah when each is applied to the wrong case.

The episode therefore has three movements. First, it studies food and contact through the source's practical grammar of impurity: what may be eaten, sold, washed, reused, buried, fed to animals, or avoided. Second, it studies grooming and bodily alteration as lawful discipline rather than local habit. Third, it renders Ja'far ibn Sulayman's formal warning as a single sustained address, because the source itself shifts from answers into communal admonition. The chapter lands on an uncomfortable but central point: everyday purity is not a small appendix to religion. It is one of the ways a community either lets authoritative teaching shape its habits or lets habit slowly become its own law.

## Food and Contact

The chapter opens with a question about Nimi oil. The questioner describes a known oil whose pits are eaten only by crows. The pits pass out through the birds' droppings, dry in the sun, and are then collected, bought, sold, pressed, and turned into oil. The oil itself shows no detectable impurity. May it be sold, purchased, handled, touched, and used?

The answer is terse: whatever is not permissible to eat is impure, and what is impure may not be eaten, sold, or bought. The ruling refuses to let the final cleanliness of appearance erase the path by which the substance arrived. The chapter has already taught, in earlier episodes, that impurity is not imagined without conditions; here the condition is not vague suspicion but a named process. The pits have passed through droppings, and the oil is treated through that history.

The next question asks how to reconcile two teachings. The book of purity does not object to the touch of a menstruating woman, a Jew, a Christian, a Sabian, or animals, yet it says that the dog is impure. The answer returns to a distinction already established in the previous chapter: wet contact transmits impurity in a way that dry handling does not. If moisture is involved, what contacts impurity becomes impure; if the contact is dry, impurity does not arise merely from handling. The source compares this to the skin of a dead animal, where selling is not permitted, while the first ruling applies to water above the threshold.

The new point is not a full doctrine of material purity, but its application to ordinary social contact. The text does not say that every encounter automatically contaminates. It asks whether moisture is involved, whether the thing contacted is impure, and whether the medium can carry impurity. That is how the chapter keeps the law from becoming either careless or hysterical.

The food cases continue with honey, clarified butter, oil, and liquid fat in which a mouse has died. Should such substances be fed to animals or buried? If soap is made from them and placed on a mat, may that soap be sold in the market or given away? The answer says that when an animal dies in liquid substances, those liquids may not be eaten and may not be fed to a carnivorous animal, because that would be forbidden. It is permissible to make soap from the substance, but the soap may not be sold unless the buyer is told of its impurity. A garment washed with that impure soap must then be rinsed again with pure water.

This is one of the chapter's most revealing judgments. The substance is not simply wasted, but neither is its impurity hidden. It can be transformed into soap, yet disclosure becomes part of lawful exchange. A buyer must not be deceived. A garment that benefits from the soap still needs pure water afterward. The ruling protects use, market honesty, and ritual consequence at the same time.

A report then gives a related allowance: such a substance may be fed to dogs and to animals whose meat is not eaten. The distinction matters because the earlier prohibition concerned carnivorous animals in one formulation and forbidden consumption in another. The source is working through a world where waste, animals, market goods, and purity overlap. It does not flatten all animals into one category. It asks what the animal is, whether its meat is eaten, and whether feeding it this substance introduces forbidden consumption.

The broth case is more granular. If a dead mouse is found in a pot of broth, the meat should be washed and may be eaten. Other food may also be eaten if a single grain of the mouse's droppings appears; the grain is removed and the food is eaten. The same applies if two grains appear. But if three grains appear, the person may not eat it.

That counting may sound startling to a modern listener, but the source is doing something precise. It is not telling the worshipper to ignore visible contamination. It is setting a threshold for small traces that can be removed without condemning the whole food. One or two small grains are treated as removable; three mark a point at which the food is no longer eaten. The law is teaching judgment under conditions of scarcity and real household life, where the question is not always whether an ideal kitchen existed but what to do when a small trace is found.

The chapter briefly raises the milk of an animal that has drunk impure liquid, then moves to a squirrel that is eaten and contains a small amount of foreign matter. The answer says that if the foreign matter is small and causes no harm to a person, it is permissible to eat it with the rest. Likewise, if there is a small amount of dust in the water of a vessel, it is not forbidden unless the matter is foul and would cause harm if consumed. Allah and His guardians know best.

Here the source distinguishes impurity from ordinary smallness, and legal concern from bodily harm. A tiny harmless presence does not automatically forbid the whole. Dust in water is not treated like filth. The decisive question becomes whether the matter is foul, harmful, and meaningfully present. The chapter therefore continues the same disciplined middle path: do not consume what impurity has made forbidden, but do not invent prohibition from every small trace.

The most socially charged food question concerns clarified butter, oil, sugar, honey, and other edible goods sold by disbelievers in Muslim markets. May such goods be eaten? The answer begins, "By Allah, success comes from Him." It says that the sacrificial animals of disbelievers are forbidden because they do not mention the name of Allah over them and do not slaughter them facing the qibla. As for their other foodstuffs and wet goods, if they have not handled them with their hands or impure implements, there is no objection to selling, buying, eating, or being served by them.

The answer then becomes cautious. Their work, it says, is rarely free of impurity, because they are unclean and their implements touch wet goods. It is therefore obligatory to avoid what they have handled when an alternative exists. What is definitively forbidden is their slaughtered animals, and their moisture is impure. If moisture from the practices of the people of shirk falls on a garment, or if wet food is known to have been defiled, then whatever touched the garment or body must be washed.

This ruling needs to be heard as the source's own legal world, not as a modern social essay. It holds several distinctions together. Slaughtered animals are treated as categorically forbidden because of the absent invocation and qibla orientation. Other goods are not automatically forbidden merely because they appear in the market. Wet handling and impure implements matter. Known defilement matters. Alternatives matter. The ruling is strict, but its strictness still operates through contact, moisture, and evidence rather than through one undifferentiated fear.

The source then records another view: if a Jew, a Christian, a Magian, or a cat touches a large vessel, purification with it remains permissible unless saliva is evident. That report narrows the concern again to a detectable medium. Touch alone is not the same as saliva. A large vessel is not automatically lost. The law continues to ask how impurity could have moved.

Questions from Sheikh Hasan ibn Nuh to Husayn ibn Idris add a further distinction about the slaughtered animals of the People of the Book. If a Muslim witnesses that they faced the qibla and invoked Allah's name over the animal, then they and the rest of their food are permissible, as stated in the text of the Holy Book. But if they touch the food with their moisture, it becomes impure. Allah knows best.

This passage is important because it refuses a careless slogan. The People of the Book are not treated in only one way. Witnessed slaughter with the required orientation and invocation changes the case. Moist contact changes the case again. The source's legal imagination is concrete: who witnessed, what words were invoked, which direction was faced, what moisture touched the food, and what the Holy Book permits.

The chapter closes the food movement with a brief question from the exposition of the Shari'ah. Eating the cooking of a slave girl who is not careful about ritual purity is permissible. The answer is short, but it adds one more check against excess suspicion. Carelessness about ritual purity is not automatically treated as proven impurity in every prepared meal. The source can be severe where impurity is known, transmitted, or materially present, yet permissive where the concern remains general.

The food section therefore lands on everyday evidence. Nimi oil is forbidden because its process is named. Liquids in which a mouse dies cannot be eaten, but they may become soap with disclosure and later rinsing. Small traces may be removed until a threshold is crossed. Non-Muslim market goods are judged through slaughter, invocation, qibla, wet contact, implements, and known defilement. The law is not teaching revulsion as a personality. It is teaching the worshipper to ask what has actually happened to the food, the vessel, the garment, and the body before God.

## Grooming Boundaries

The second movement begins with natural cleanliness and bodily alteration. The first question asks about a rule described as the lowest obligatory act in Islam: circumcision may not be abandoned, even if a man reaches eighty years of age. If a woman grows old and has not been reduced, should it be left? The answer says it should be reduced and not left unreduced.

The wording belongs to the source's legal vocabulary and historical medical world. The episode should neither amplify it into a new doctrine nor soften it into something the text does not say. The point for this chapter is that the source treats certain bodily practices as religious obligations or regulated acts, not as optional local preferences. Age does not cancel the obligation in the male case, and the answer extends the ruling to the female case as the source frames it.

A series of grooming questions follows. In India, men and women part their hair from the front of the head to the middle, not down to the nape of the neck. Does this distinguish them from one another? The answer says one should part the front hair down to the nape and should not deliberately leave it unparted at the back. The detail is small, but the logic is large: bodily order is not left entirely to inherited custom when the Shari'ah has attached meaning to it.

The beard receives a similar answer. If some hairs are long and some are short, may the beard be trimmed so that the shorter hairs are leveled with the longer ones, intending to improve its appearance? The answer permits it because it is permissible to trim some offerings in such a way; otherwise, trimming is not permissible. The ruling allows grooming for proportion while maintaining a boundary around the beard. Care is allowed. Erasure is not.

Another question asks whether the hair of the human body, other than what there is a ruling to remove, must be shaved, shortened, or plucked. The answer says one need not do anything with it except the beard, from which nothing may be removed. The source is therefore not demanding restless bodily management. It identifies specific governed areas and leaves the rest alone.

A practical household custom appears next. Some Indians dislike a man and his wife sharing one comb to comb the head. The answer says there is no reliable basis for that dislike. This is one of the chapter's gentler corrections. Not every inherited aversion is law. A shared comb between spouses is not made suspect simply because some people disapprove.

The source then reflects on Abraham. The book of purity says that the first to be circumcised was Abraham, peace be upon him, at the age of eighty. Does that apparently indicate that this ruling and what follows had not been revealed before that point? The answer says that this is apparently the case, because Abraham was not commanded to purify himself until he reached eighty years of age. The chapter treats law as something revealed in time, even when later communities inherit it as settled obligation.

The Prophet's reported instruction about female reduction is then quoted: "O women who have circumcised, if you reduce your daughters, do not go to excess, and leave something of it, for it is better for the face and more pleasing to the husband." The question asks whether there is sin if they go to excess, and what "better for the face" means. The answer says one should act upon what has been mentioned; moderation is better for the husband's sake, and that is taken for granted.

Again, the source's language should be kept in its historical frame. The ruling is not presented as a free field of bodily control. It is explicitly bounded by moderation and by a warning not to go to excess. The chapter's concern is not to build a modern medical argument but to show how the source regulates even practices that the community might otherwise perform by inherited habit.

The source then asks about the Prophet's words that it is not for a menstruating woman to take a qaṣṣa or a qaṣṣa. The answer explains the terms. The nail of a thing refers to the portion from the temples to the nape of the neck. Al-qaṣṣa refers to cutting the hair of the head and leaving a quarter of it at the front. Al-qaṣṣa also refers to taking the hair and leaving some of it out, and this too is forbidden. The ruling links menstruation, hair-cutting, and prohibited styles, continuing the chapter's concern that bodily form should not be casually reshaped in states or ways the law forbids.

Adornment comes next. Most women of India engrave their palms and feet with henna for adornment, and some dye their teeth red or with other materials. The Shari'ah says engraving with dye is forbidden. What is their condition? The answer says that engraving with pigments and dyeing the teeth are forbidden. A man who tattoos his hands and feet for adornment, without benefit, is also asked about. The answer says it is not permissible for a man to do so for adornment. Women are commanded to adorn themselves, while leaving such adornment is better for men without a reason; the one who does it is not condemned outright.

The chapter is drawing boundaries around adornment by gender, purpose, and form. Not every decoration is treated alike. Some acts are forbidden outright. Some are inappropriate for men because they imitate adornments assigned to women. Some are discouraged without condemnation. The listener should notice that the source is not only asking, "Is this beautiful?" It is asking, "What kind of person, body, gendered role, and religious discipline does this act perform?"

Teeth receive another ruling. If a man or woman has uneven teeth, some longer and some shorter, and files them level with stone, iron, or another material, is there sin in that? The answer says there is nothing wrong with that for women. Ear piercing receives the same kind of allowance: women of India pierce their ears and the ears of infant daughters so they can wear ornaments, and the answer says there is nothing wrong with that. These allowances matter because they prevent the listener from hearing the chapter as a blanket rejection of adornment. The source allows certain forms of beautification in the bodies for which it considers them appropriate.

Hair parting returns with more severity. A man takes the hair of his head and, with a comb or without one, does not part it every day. Is there deficiency in his prayer, and how often should he comb and part his hair? The answer says that if the hair is parted in two halves there is no deficiency, and it should not be left without a parting. If one deliberately leaves it unparted for the purpose of ruling, the prayer is invalidated. The wording is difficult, but the underlying concern is clear: deliberate bodily disorder, when tied to defiance of the rule, reaches the prayer itself.

Friday night combing is treated differently. Most people in India think it is necessary to leave combing out on Friday night. The answer says there is no reliable basis for that. Once again, the source separates law from custom. Some customs are corrected because they are too lax. Others are corrected because they forbid without evidence.

The chapter then asks whether someone should be circumcised after death, whether child or adult, when the Shari'ah states that the most important act is not left in Islam until a person is circumcised. The answer says that the Sunnah recommends circumcision after death. Others say the prayer of a woman who has not been reduced, and her position with her husband, are valid, as is the case with a man. The source preserves more than one report, and the episode should let that tension remain: the obligation is weighty, yet validity is not simply erased in every unresolved case.

A response of Qadi al-Nu'man, may God sanctify his soul, to al-Khattab ibn Wasim gives the male case in fuller form. A man had married and fathered children while ignorant and uncircumcised. Should he be circumcised? The answer says the most ignorant person in Islam is not left uncircumcised, even if he reaches eighty years of age, unless he was born already circumcised. Some prophets, peace be upon them, were narrated to have been born in that state. If the glans is exposed, he is not compelled to be circumcised, but if he is young it is recommended to roll back the foreskin and cut away whatever skin remains over the glans.

This closes the grooming movement with a pattern that has run throughout the chapter. Obligation is serious, but the body is still examined. If the relevant part is already exposed, compulsion changes. If youth allows a remaining correction, it is recommended. The source's judgments are bodily, not merely verbal. They ask what is present, what is absent, what has been done, and what kind of correction is still possible.

## Ja'far's Admonition

Mawlana Ja'far ibn Sulayman, may God sanctify him and grant us his intercession, wrote a noble letter to refute innovations about which he warned his people and admonished those who had grown heedless among the people of the Island of Yemen. The source now shifts from brief answers into a formal address, and that shift should be heard in full.

He begins with women's hair. He says that it has come to his knowledge that some women, especially certain women within the communities, are shaving their heads as men shave theirs. This practice, together with the matters already mentioned, belongs to the category of what is forbidden, because the face and head are adorned differently according to gender. Whoever reflects on this will see the command clearly: God Almighty has made the adornment of men in the growth of their beards, their grooming, their care, and their masculinity; and He has made the adornment of women in the growth of their hair, its combing, its lengthening, and its beauty.

He says that it is established in the hadiths of the Prophet, peace be upon him and his family, that the shaved head of a woman is a source of shame for her, and that covering her head is obligatory except in illness. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him and his family, said words to the effect that this removes the reverence of God from hearts. He also said: "If one of you wishes to marry a woman, let him inquire about her hair just as he inquires about her face, for hair is one of the two beauties." And it is narrated from him, peace be upon him and his family, that he said: "Beautiful hair is among the garments of God, and whoever strips his body of this garment of his Lord Almighty, with which He has clothed him, has disfigured his own form. Whoever disfigures his form is cursed in this world and the next."

Ja'far is astonished that some have permitted this among themselves and within their law, while others have not been forbidden from it within theirs, even though all of them have learned what is mentioned in the book Pillars of Islam concerning the hair of women's heads. That teaching indicates the prohibition of shaving women's hair for the people of the Rightly Guided Da'wah and those who dwell within its reach. The author of that book, may God sanctify his soul, reports from the Imams of the Household of Prophethood, peace be upon them, that when a woman purifies herself, she should loosen her hair unless she is certain that water reaches the skin of her head and wets all of her hair, which is possible when her hair is soft and fine.

He also recalls the rulings on trimming hair at the end of ihram for men and women, and the distinction between them in the purified Shari'ah. The discussion is grounded in established principles. It is narrated from Ali ibn Abi Talib, prayers of God be upon him, that regarding al-Aqra', the Musa passes over his head. When the woman exits her ihram, she takes from the tips of her braids. Qadi al-Nu'man, may God sanctify his soul and be pleased with him, says that the bald man and the one with close-cropped hair have the Musa passed over their heads, while the woman, when she comes out of ihram, shortens her hair by cutting something from the ends and does not shave any of it.

Ja'far treats this as one of the clearest proofs that women may not shave their heads. If even the small amount normally cut at the end of ihram is restricted, and many are prevented from substituting shaving for that small cutting, then full shaving is even more strictly prohibited. Whoever is certain that no such garment exists, whether young or old, is not included in this prohibition if he follows the path of guidance and removes the mark. Whoever desires a religion other than that of Abraham has only wronged himself. Such clarification is set forth only for the people of religion.

He then addresses women who remove hair without fully shaving it. Among the things observed, he says, is that women among them who remove their hair shave only to the tips of their heads, specifically at the circles of the head, and shave the nape as well. God Almighty is not pleased with this from those who do it. Such a person is heading toward perdition, because Qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad has already narrated that the bald and close-cropped have the Musa passed over their heads, while the woman, when she breaks ihram, cuts something from the ends of her hair and does not shave any of it.

If it is not permissible for women to shave the ends of their hair even at the height of ihram, then their heads must never be shaved at all. They may only cut from them what they cut, and it is never permissible to continue beyond that, for this command calls only to guidance. Whoever violates it has violated the obligations of the true religion and destroyed its foundation. Whoever desires a religion other than that of Abraham has only wronged himself. This clarification is for the people of religion.

Ja'far then turns to the hastening of circumcision for maidservants and girls. He says that it has also been observed that they hasten the circumcision of girls in fewer than ten days, or up to forty days at most, making that the limit in public and private practice. This is contrary to the Shari'ah of the Prophet, peace be upon him and his family, and to the two pillars of Islam. Qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad, may God sanctify his soul, mentions in the noble Pillars of Islam, narrating from Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, that he said: "Hasten the circumcision of your children, for it purifies them." He also said: "Do not circumcise the maidservant before she is seven years old." Ja'far calls this a clear prohibition that calls only to guidance.

It is never permissible, he says, to transgress this limit, because it is among the limits of God Almighty, the Arbitrator, blessed are His names. Whoever transgresses the limits has wronged himself. He has also observed that some people, on the occasion of circumcising a maidservant, gather family and neighbors, offer hospitality, have the call to prayer made, establish prayers for those who remain, and carry news of the circumcision to those near and far, including both parents. This is not part of the matter.

Purity for male children and circumcision for maidservants is a private affair. It should be witnessed only by the one being circumcised, the one performing the circumcision, and the women of the household. Whoever publicizes it has violated the laws of his religion and compounded that violation, acting against the modesty of which the Prophet, peace be upon him and his family, said: "Modesty is from faith, and whoever has no modesty has no religion." On this basis, the circumcision of the maidservant carries one of the intended meanings in the matters of his dear religion.

It is not permissible to act against God's will or transgress His command in what He has bounded. This applies in all matters, their branches and their roots, every tangible matter whose rational counterpart is knowable, and every parable that is manifest with its inner meaning. Whoever departs from the path in this has invalidated it and veered from the way. It is not permissible to do that at all, as indicated by the guardians of God, peace be upon them, the guardians of their times, who warn. God Almighty says: "Whatever the Messenger has brought you, take it; and what he has forbidden you, refrain from it" (chapter 59, verse 7).

Ja'far then addresses the purity of boys. Among the things learned, he says, is that most of them delay the circumcision of male children, even though they are commanded in the hadith of the Prophet to hasten it. One of the Imams, peace be upon them, said: "Hasten the circumcision of your children, for it is purer for them." This is the opposite of how they treat the maidservants, so they have reversed the matter in both cases, acting contrary to the command of the Dominant and the Righteous.

He notes that people from protected communities rush to circumcise their children very quickly, doing so on the seventh day in compliance with what was prescribed for them in the law given by God Almighty to Moses, the Word of God, peace be upon him. They have continued this across thousands of years, enduring the hardship it imposes on their children at such a young age. The community Ja'far addresses is closer to the era of its Prophet, peace be upon him, and subject to his command, yet it delays what he commanded to be hastened. Glory be to the great God. How tremendous is their affair in this. God admonishes them never to return to the like of it if they are believers. God has made His verses clear, and God is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

The letter then turns to men imitating women in henna. It has been learned and narrated from some callers in the mountain regions that men imitate women of their households by dyeing their hands with henna, and some dye their hands and feet as well. This is not permissible by the law of the Prophet, peace be upon him and his family, nor by the religion of Islam, which has made this practice for women and not for men. It belongs to the adornments of women and in no case to men. A man who does this as adornment imitates them, like one who adorns himself with ornaments of silver or gold that belong only to women and chooses this for himself. No one of sound mind would do so.

Ja'far warns them to beware of this practice because it is among the acts of disobedience to God Almighty. They should replace it with fear of God Almighty, for they reach what they hope for in all affairs only through fear of Him. They have heard the hadith that circulates in the Sunnah and is well known among the people and the Arabs: the adornment of men is conduct and character, while the adornment of women is gold. The opposites of the Guiding Da'wah and those who belong to it have already done what they were obligated to do, in accordance with the Lord of their affairs, the One who owns and reckons, and He will not let them persist in that detestable deed. God will grant from that a whitening of faces among them on the Day when faces whiten and faces turn black.

After the letter, the chapter preserves further answers tied to the same issues. Ja'far, answering questions sent by Maluka from the Island of Hind, was asked about what the purified Shari'ah books say: maidservants are not to be circumcised before seven years of age. If a girl below that limit dies, should she be circumcised as male children are circumcised when they had not yet been circumcised in life, or buried as she is? The intent is that if there is a prescribed age for her circumcision, that limit may not be transgressed.

Muhammad ibn Fahd al-Mukarrami was asked about a girl who dies before reaching seven. Should she be circumcised? He replied no, according to the questioner. She is not circumcised because she has not reached the age of circumcision. Then a ruling from Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, is cited: circumcise your children on the seventh day, and let no cold prevent you, for it is a purification of the body, and the earth is filled, by God, from the urine of the uncircumcised upon it.

The formal address lands on a pattern of reversal. The community has hastened what should not be hastened, delayed what should be hastened, publicized what should be private, permitted what should be forbidden, and turned local bodily practices into rival law. The letter's severity belongs to that pattern. Ja'far is not treating hair, henna, and circumcision as isolated etiquette. He treats them as signs that a community may still quote the Shari'ah while letting custom govern its body.

## What this episode lands

This episode lands on everyday purity as disciplined obedience in the ordinary places where habit feels strongest. Foods are judged by process, contact, moisture, visible trace, lawful slaughter, disclosure, and harm. Grooming is judged by obligation, gendered adornment, bodily form, reliable textual basis, and the difference between permitted beautification and forbidden alteration.

The chapter also shows why the book does not separate legal detail from communal formation. A pot of broth, a market jar of oil, a shared comb, a child's circumcision, a woman's hair, a man's henna, and a garment touched by moisture all become places where the community learns whether it will be ruled by evidence and authoritative guidance or by inherited impulse. The forward motion from earlier purity chapters is clear: after learning what purification is, the listener now sees how purity disciplines daily life.
