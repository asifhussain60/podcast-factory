## Where this episode picks up

What if the real dispute is not whether the adhan may name Ali, but whether a community can hear the Prophet's own pattern of appointment without flinching? This episode picks up in the middle of the formal proof-letter introduced in the previous chapter. As established earlier, the book has already taught guardianship, sacred rank, disciplined loyalty, affection as law, attentive prayer, sacred times, the summons, the adhan's witness, and the opening of the Discussion Letter. This chapter therefore does not need to rebuild the whole doctrine of wilaya from the ground up. Its work is more exact: it completes the proof sequence for the disputed adhan phrase, answers a second objection about addition and omission, and explains why the testimony to Ali's guardianship is not traced to a late invention.

The central phrase remains the one defended in the previous episode: Muhammad and Ali are the best of human beings, and their 'atrah are the finest of perfumes. The prior chapter showed how the adhan became a site of public witness, how Imam al-Hakim bi Amr Allah answered the preacher who objected, and how the formal letter began with the demand for decisive proof. The letter had already moved through purification of the Ahl al-Bayt, the family of Yasin, the best community, and the beginning of the Moses-and-Aaron analogy. Now the source resumes at the precise point where that analogy turns toward the Prophet Muhammad, peace and blessings of God be upon him and his family.

The listener should feel the chapter's forward motion. Earlier, the book argued that the adhan may bear witness to the Prophet's household. Here it asks whether that witness can be grounded through repeated scriptural patterns: the prophet strengthened by a brother, the guardian named by revelation, the household preferred above others, and the testimony established before hostile or hesitant hearers. The proof is cumulative rather than merely ornamental. Each verse is brought in to close a possible escape: perhaps Ali is near but not authorized; perhaps authorized but not best; perhaps best only in one sense; perhaps the phrase is still an innovation; perhaps adding it is no better than omitting another phrase. The chapter patiently answers each escape.

It also changes argumentative register near the end. The first large movement finishes the formal proof-letter from the earlier chapter. The second answers objectors who compare the Ismaili adhan addition with the omission attributed to Omar ibn al-Khattab and the phrase "prayer is better than sleep." The third gives the source's account of where the testimony to guardianship originates: not with Imam al-Hakim bi Amr Allah as a late Fatimid policy, but with Ali's own testimony to the guardianship that came to him after the Prophet, just as the Prophet testified to his own mission and God testifies to His own oneness.

The episode should therefore sound like a legal-theological closing argument. It is not a quiet survey of prayer etiquette. It is the source's attempt to make a public ritual phrase stand on Quranic language, prophetic precedent, accepted hadith, the rank of the Ahl al-Bayt, and the moral difference between adding truthful witness and removing prophetic summons. Its question is sharp: when the adhan gathers the people to prayer, what truth is the community required to let that summons carry?

That question gives the chapter its discipline. It would be easy to hear the material as a quarrel over liturgical wording and miss why the author spends so much effort on proof. For him, the adhan is not a decorative threshold before the real worship begins. It is the first communal proclamation of what the prayer stands under. If the call is malformed, shortened, or deprived of rightful witness, then the community is trained before prayer in a distorted memory. If the call bears truthful testimony, then even before the worshipper stands in the line, the ear has already been instructed in tawhid, prophecy, and wilaya.

## The Remaining Proofs

The fourth proof in the formal letter had begun with Moses asking God for Aaron. Moses asks for a minister from his family, Aaron his brother, to strengthen his back and share in his affair, so that God may be glorified and remembered abundantly. God grants the request. In another verse, Moses says that Aaron is more eloquent than he is, and asks that he be sent as a helper to confirm him, because Moses fears being called a liar. God answers that He will strengthen Moses' arm through his brother, grant authority to them both, and give victory to them and those who follow them.

The source now applies that prophetic pattern to Muhammad, peace and blessings of God be upon him and his family. The Quran commands the Prophet to follow the guidance of those whom God has guided. It says that nothing is said to him except what was said to messengers before him. It says that God has ordained for him of the religion what He enjoined upon Noah, what He revealed to him, and what He enjoined upon Abraham, Moses, and Jesus: establish the religion and do not be divided in it. The argument is that the Prophet's mission does not float outside the earlier prophetic order. The pattern of divine support, family appointment, and strengthened authority belongs to the way God deals with His messengers.

From that pattern, the source says it is obligatory upon the Prophet to appoint Ali ibn Abi Talib as his wali, minister, brother, and the one through whom his strength is reinforced and his affairs are shared. This is not presented as a personal preference of the Prophet or as a merely social kinship. The source treats it as the continuation of a divine pattern already visible in Moses and Aaron. If Moses may ask for a brother-minister who shares in the affair and strengthens the mission, and if the Prophet is commanded to follow the guidance of previous messengers, then the presence of a brother and minister beside him is not strange. It is expected.

The source then tightens the claim with the Quranic principle that no change is found in the way of God and no alteration in the way of God. The point is not that every prophetic story repeats mechanically in every detail. It is that divine appointment, support, and authoritative kinship are not accidents. The source hears a stable sunnah of God: the messenger is strengthened by one appointed near him, the mission is shared in authority, and the community is not left to guess where the strengthening hand has been placed.

This is where the well-known saying enters: "You are to me as Aaron was to Moses, except that there is no prophet after me." The source uses that report to seal the analogy while preserving the boundary. Ali is not made a prophet. The exception says exactly that. But once prophethood is excluded, the remaining force of the analogy remains: brotherhood, ministry, support, partnership in the affair, and authority under the Prophet's mission. This is why the source can move from Moses and Aaron to Muhammad and Ali without erasing the unique rank of Muhammad as master of the messengers.

For the listener, this is the chapter's key interpretive discipline. The source's argument is not "Ali is another prophet." It is "Ali occupies the place of the divinely appointed supporter after prophethood itself is excluded." That distinction protects the proof from the objection that naming Ali beside Muhammad in the adhan confuses prophetic rank. The source insists that the phrase "best of human beings" is not about making two prophets. It is about the Prophet and the one associated with him in command and authority by God's pattern, the one whose rank is known through the same prophetic structure that made Aaron a support to Moses.

The letter then says that this is proven and confirmed by the noble verses of the glorious Quran, the revelation to which falsehood cannot come from before it or behind it. It cites the Quran's statement that it guides to what is most upright. Therefore, the Prophet's affirmation that Ali is among the best of human beings after him is one of the clear and unambiguous matters. The source repeatedly uses this language of clarity because the dispute concerns more than a devotional phrase. It concerns whether the evidence is decisive enough for ritual proclamation.

The fifth proof moves from prophetic analogy to the verse of guardianship. The source cites the verse: "Your guardian is only God and His Messenger and those who believe, those who establish prayer and give zakah while bowing in worship." It then cites the verse: "Whoever takes God and His Messenger and those who believe as guardians, then the party of God, they are the victors." The argument is that those named as believers in this verse, and described through prayer and zakah while bowing, belong to the victorious party of God. They are people of virtue named by revelation.

The source then gathers supporting verses around that claim. It says the bounty is in God's hand and He gives it to whomever He wills. It says no human being was more deserving of that virtue than the family of the Messenger of God. It recalls the purification verse, in which God wishes to remove impurity from the people of the household and purify them completely. It recalls the verse of the best community and the verse of peace upon the family of Yasin. These are not new doctrines in the book. Earlier chapters have already taught the rank and authority of the Ahl al-Bayt. Here those teachings function as proof-texts inside one narrow question: is the adhan phrase true?

The conclusion of the fifth proof is direct. The first and best of the family was the one whom the Prophet made his brother, wali, minister, and successor in his community. Therefore Ali is the wali after the Prophet, as specified in the guardianship verse, and he is partner in guardianship over the believers. How, then, could he be anything other than the best of human beings after the Prophet, given his partnership in authority?

The word "partner" here has to be heard carefully. The source does not mean a rival prophet or an equal origin of revelation. It means partnership in the authority that God established through the Prophet's appointment: the authority of guardianship, guidance, and command over believers. The fifth proof therefore answers a possible hesitation left by the fourth. The fourth showed the brother-minister pattern; the fifth shows the guardianship verse and the victorious party. The claim is not only analogical, but textual.

The source then adds the public declaration at Eid Ghadir. It recalls the Prophet's saying: "Whoever I was his master, this Ali is his master. O God, support whoever supports him, oppose whoever opposes him, grant victory to whoever grants him victory, abandon whoever abandons him, and turn the truth with him wherever he turns." The earlier chapter already used Ghadir as the root of audible witness in the adhan; this chapter invokes it as the decisive public clarification of guardianship during the Prophet's own lifetime. The phrase in the adhan does not arise from private sentiment. It is tied to a public declaration of authority.

The sixth proof is shorter but important. It cites the verse: "Those who believe and do righteous deeds, they are the best of all creation." The source says the people of the Prophet's household are the best of the righteous, because they are the best of those who believe in God and do righteous deeds. It also recalls the statement, "You are the best community ever brought forth for humanity." Since Ali is known as the best of the Ahl al-Bayt, the source says it is right and proven that the adhan may say Muhammad and Ali are the best of human beings, and their fragrance the finest of perfumes.

This proof works by linking righteousness, household, and excellence. The phrase "best of all creation" could be heard broadly, but the source reads the highest instance of it through the household purified and praised by revelation. Ali's rank within that household then supplies the adhan's wording. Again, this is cumulative. The source is not relying on one isolated verse as though it alone carried the entire argument. It piles up revelation, prophetic saying, family rank, and accepted reports until the disputed phrase appears, in its own logic, less like an addition than a condensation.

That cumulative method is part of the chapter's rhetoric. The respondent does not merely answer the objector once and move on. He approaches the same contested phrase from different angles because each angle protects against a different misunderstanding. Purification answers the fear that the household is ordinary. The family of Yasin answers the fear that Quranic blessing does not reach them as a family. The best community answers the problem of excellence-language. Moses and Aaron answer the need for a divinely strengthened brother. The verse of guardianship answers the question of authority over believers. The best of creation and the chosen families answer the scale of rank. The result is not one proof repeated eight times, but eight pressures placed on the same resistance.

The seventh proof turns to divine choosing. The Quran says that God chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of 'Imran above all the worlds, descendants of one another, and God is All-Hearing, All-Knowing. The source says this makes clear that God chose the family of Abraham and the family of 'Imran just as He chose Adam and Noah, singling them out for message and prophethood. Since the Messenger of God is better than Adam and Noah, the source reasons that his family is better than the family of Abraham and the family of 'Imran. And since Ali is the best of the Prophet's family, the adhan phrase has its authenticity and proof testified to by the Quran.

This proof is about families, not only individuals. The source wants the listener to see that revelation speaks of chosen lineages and households. If earlier families were chosen and preferred, then the family of the final and best Messenger cannot be treated as spiritually ordinary. The phrase about Muhammad, Ali, and the 'atrah therefore names a hierarchy of chosen nearness: the Messenger at the summit, his household as the most honoured household, and Ali as the foremost member of that household after him.

The eighth proof extends the same logic through the Children of Israel. God saved them from Pharaoh's humiliating torment and chose them knowingly above the worlds. The source explains that "the worlds" here means humanity, and that the Children of Israel were preferred in their era over all others. Then it argues that the family of the Prophet were the best of human beings in their era, and indeed surpassed earlier families, because the Messenger is the master of all messengers and prophets, the most honoured and best of them all.

The conclusion is plain: his family, his household, are better than the family of every prophet and more honoured than them; and Ali is the best and most honoured of the Messenger's family. The source calls this the true judgment, attested by the verses of the Holy Book cited here and by others not quoted. It ends the proof sequence with praise of God, Lord of the worlds, and prayers upon Muhammad, master of the messengers, and upon his pure family until the Day of Judgment.

This first movement lands as the completion of the letter's eight answers. The earlier chapter opened the debate and gave the first proofs; this chapter completes the ladder. The Prophet's household is purified, greeted, called the best community, set within the Moses-Aaron pattern, named through guardianship, placed among the best of creation, understood through chosen families, and preferred above other households through the rank of the final Messenger. The source wants all of that to gather inside one public phrase in the adhan. The phrase is not a stray embellishment. It is the compressed sound of a whole proof-world.

## Unequal Errors

After closing the proof-letter, the source opens another answer with the basmala. The new objection comes from people who, in the source's words, have fallen into delusion regarding the honorable school. They argue that what Omar ibn al-Khattab omitted from the phrase "live on well" does not make the call to prayer unacceptable, because no such narration appears in their books. They also argue that if the omission was an exercise of ijtihad, even mistaken ijtihad, it is not ugly or wrong in the relevant sense. Then they draw the comparison that matters: adding the phrase "I testify that our master Ali is the wali of God" is likewise wrong, because any addition to the adhan is equivalent to any omission from it.

The source answers by refusing the equation. Even if one grants, for the sake of argument, that increasing or decreasing the adhan can count as error with respect to obligation, it does not follow that addition and omission are equal in degree. The chapter's new contribution is not simply another proof that Ali may be named. It is a theory of unequal ritual error. Some changes reduce the summons and deprive multitudes; some additions, even if disputed by opponents, proclaim a truth. The source's point is that a formal similarity, "both change the adhan," hides a moral and legal difference.

The first reason concerns scale and loss. The source says to consider the ratio of what was omitted from the adhan relative to its followers compared with the addition. The omission, it says, is as one to a hundred thousand or less. The meaning is that the omitted phrase affected an enormous number of people and produced widespread loss. If the Prophet's wording was removed from the repeated summons of a vast community, the damage cannot be measured merely by counting words. It changed what countless hearers were invited to hear and say.

The addition, by contrast, did not erase an existing prophetic summons from the people. It added witness. The source therefore weighs consequences, not only formal categories. To omit something from the adhan may deprive generations of a commanded sound. To add testimony to guardianship, as the source understands it, introduces a disputed claim into the call, but it does not cancel an inherited prophetic phrase. The two acts are not symmetrical merely because both alter the soundscape.

The second reason concerns time. If the addition is attributed to Imam al-Hakim bi Amr Allah, then, the source says, the one who had previously erred misled the community for more than four hundred years, because this phrase was not introduced until after the fourth century. The earlier and longer error would bear a greater degree of wrong. Here the source turns the opponent's chronology against him. If they say the addition is late, then the omission is earlier. If duration matters, the omission has had more centuries to spread its damage.

This argument also exposes a hidden assumption. The objector treats lateness as a mark against the addition, but does not treat earliness as a mark of danger for the omission. The source says that if an error begins earlier and governs more people for longer, that increases its gravity. The objection cannot use time only when it disadvantages the adhan's witness to Ali. Time must be weighed consistently.

The third reason is more pointed. The source says that if the omission were merely equivalent to the omission, the two would share one degree; but beyond the omission, Omar's change added, in the source's view, a sentence that is objectionable. The text then identifies the phrase "prayer is better than sleep." It responds with astonishment: glory be to God, where is prayer and where is sleep, that one would liken them in virtue and inferiority? The comparison is like someone saying, "this sword is better than wood," without knowing whether the result is praise or blame.

This is one of the chapter's most vivid moments. The source is not only concerned that a phrase was added. It is concerned with the phrase's meaning. "Prayer is better than sleep" appears, to its opponent, like an innocent exhortation. Who would deny that prayer is better than sleep? But the source hears the comparison itself as malformed. Prayer and sleep are not two comparable goods of the same order. To praise prayer by placing it beside sleep is, in the source's eyes, a diminishment disguised as praise. The sentence contains two errors: a ritual alteration and an unworthy comparison.

By contrast, the addition "I testify that our master Ali is the wali of God" contains, from the opponent's point of view, only one alleged error: addition to the adhan. The source does not accept that the content is false. It argues that the content is true. But even if speaking on the opponent's terms, the comparison remains unequal. One change adds a phrase the source regards as truthful testimony. The other both omits and introduces a phrase that the source sees as rhetorically and theologically defective. The equalizing formula collapses under the weight of content.

The fourth reason concerns consistency. The source says Omar applied his own ijtihad but did not command the abrogation of the Prophet's saying at the five prayer times. He attached his phrase only once, in the morning prayer. The source says it leaves aside many objectionable aspects of this sentence for fear of excessive length. But the point still lands: if the phrase were a true correction or replacement, why attach it only to one prayer? If it is merely an exhortation against sleep, why place it inside the adhan at all? The limited placement makes the action look less like universal law and more like discretionary interference.

The source then turns to the attribution of the Ali testimony to Imam al-Hakim bi Amr Allah. It calls this attribution invalid and baseless, especially because the twelve Shi'a, despite differing from him in school of thought, use the phrase in the adhan. That observation functions as counter-evidence. If a community outside his school and opposed to him on other matters still uses the testimony, then the phrase cannot plausibly be reduced to his personal invention or dynastic policy.

The chapter is careful, however, to keep the argument polemical. It does not present a neutral history of Shi'a adhan practice. It says the attribution is confusion and delusion. The point is to break the claim that the phrase began with one late Fatimid authority. If the testimony appears among those who do not share Imam al-Hakim's school, then its roots must be sought elsewhere. The source will give that account in the final movement.

This is also why the chapter treats attribution as more than antiquarian bookkeeping. If the phrase were merely the policy of one ruler, then objectors could say it belonged to state power rather than revealed truth. If it is shared across communities that differ from that ruler, the source can argue that it belongs to a deeper inheritance. The question "who first said it?" becomes a theological question, because origin determines whether the adhan is carrying an authorized testimony or a later ruler's preference.

The next objection concerns reports. The opponents say that attributing the omission to Omar ibn al-Khattab is unacceptable because the narration does not appear in their books. The source answers that this is not a sound argument, because the books attributed to authenticity, such as Sahih al-Bukhari and the rest of the five, are themselves disputed by their own people in terms of soundness. The source's scholars have grounds to reject and dismiss the hadiths contained in them and deny them as evidence.

This is not a general lesson on hadith criticism; the book has already established its own authority structure. Here the point is narrower. Absence from the opponent's books cannot decide the question when those books' authority is itself contested. A community cannot make its own archive the measure of what counts as transmitted, especially when the source believes that archive has suppressed hostile evidence. The argument is epistemic: whose books get to control the proof?

The source then names Qadi al-Nu'man and Ja'far al-Sadiq. It says the narration transmitted by Qadi al-Nu'man from Ja'far al-Sadiq predates the composition of those later books. Their scholars, it says, were able to see it and debate against it, yet suppressed and erased it out of fear of appealing to the people of truth. The claim is severe. It treats the absence of the report not as innocence, but as evidence of concealment.

For the episode, this material should be handled as the source's polemical claim, not softened into modern ecumenical abstraction. The source is arguing from within a world where competing hadith canons, school loyalties, and claims of suppression matter. Its answer is that the people of truth possess an earlier transmission through Qadi al-Nu'man from Ja'far al-Sadiq, and that the opponent's failure to preserve it does not nullify it. That is the response concerning the addition to the call to prayer.

This second movement lands on a distinction the chapter will not let the listener evade: formal alteration is not the whole moral question. What was omitted? What was added? Who was deprived? What did the content mean? How long did the change govern the community? By what archive is transmission measured? The source says the omitted phrase and the added testimony cannot be treated as equal merely because both touch the adhan. One removes; the other witnesses. One compares prayer to sleep; the other names wilaya. One rests on an archive the source does not accept as final; the other is defended through earlier Ahl al-Bayt transmission.

## The First Testimony

The final movement answers the question of origin. If the testimony to guardianship is not simply a late invention of Imam al-Hakim bi Amr Allah, where does it come from? The source says the phrase of testimony to guardianship was uttered by Ali ibn Abi Talib for himself when the matter of guardianship came to him after the Prophet. This is the chapter's final and boldest claim. The testimony in the adhan is rooted, not in a later ruler's innovation, but in the first holder of that guardianship after the Prophet.

The source explains the analogy by placing three kinds of testimony side by side. The Prophet testified for himself regarding his prophecy and message. God Almighty testified for Himself that there is no god but He. Likewise, Ali testified concerning the guardianship that came to him after the Prophet. The form of the argument is striking: self-testimony is not automatically illegitimate when the office being testified to is divinely grounded. God bears witness to His oneness; the Prophet bears witness to his mission; Ali bears witness to the wilaya that came to him.

This does not mean the three testimonies are equal in rank. The source is not collapsing God, prophet, and wali into one level. It is defending the legitimacy of testimony uttered by the one whose office is being named. If God may bear witness to Himself and the Prophet may testify concerning his own message, then Ali's testimony to his guardianship is not invalid merely because it comes from him. The authority behind the testimony is the divine appointment and revealed verse, not private self-praise.

The source links this to the verse of guardianship: "But Allah and His Messenger are your guardians," and so on. It says this came after the verse of guardianship was revealed. People differed regarding that revelation, some denying it. Yet they are still charged with the testimony revealed concerning the Father of Imams. Here the source returns to a theme that has run through the adhan chapters: denial does not cancel obligation. A community may dispute the meaning or transmission of a verse, but the source says the responsibility to bear the correct testimony remains.

This last point also explains why the adhan is such a serious location for the dispute. The adhan is not a private theological essay. It is repeated, public, embodied memory. To place a testimony in it is to train a community's hearing. To remove or refuse a testimony is also to train a community's hearing. The source's argument is that the testimony to Ali's guardianship belongs to the truth that should be heard with the summons to prayer, because the matter of guardianship came after the Prophet and was itself the subject of revealed obligation.

The source's analogy to God's self-witness and the Prophet's self-testimony also gives the final answer a formal shape. Testimony, in this argument, is not only something others say about a bearer of office. It can be pronounced by the one whose office must be known, provided the office itself is grounded in divine disclosure. The adhan then becomes the community's repetition of that recognized truth. What began as Ali's testimony to the wilaya entrusted to him becomes, in the source's account, the community's testimony whenever the summons names him as wali.

The source then divides hearers into two moral postures. In this is sufficient guidance for the one who seeks it; as for the arrogant, they have nothing but seduction. Every vessel flows with what it contains, and God guides whom He wills to a straight path. The language is concise but severe. The problem, in the source's view, is not a lack of available proof. It is the condition of the vessel receiving proof. A seeking heart can be guided by the cumulative evidence; an arrogant one turns even evidence into seduction.

This closing aphorism should not be turned into a new doctrine of inward purity. Earlier chapters have already treated inner water, bodily purity, and the moral discipline of belonging. Here the image functions as the final judgment on argument. After eight proofs, a rebuttal of equal-error reasoning, a challenge to rival archives, and an account of first testimony, the source says the remaining difference lies in receptivity. The vessel pours what it contains.

The chapter closes with a scribal note. It says the text was written by the pure and loyal Diaa Ali ibn Ibrahim al-Haydar Abadi, who asks God to forgive his sins, conceal the flaws in what he has written, and attribute whatever is right in it to the honorable loyalists. It dates the writing to the twenty-second of Jumada al-Awwal, 1306 AH, in the town of Baruda, under the quiet call. This is book-object provenance, so it should be compressed rather than made into a full teaching. Its value is simply that the source preserves the humility and location of the copyist or writer at the close.

That closing note also helps the listener feel the material's layered transmission. The chapter has argued about reports, canons, suppression, and the provenance of adhan phrases; then it ends with a human scribe asking forgiveness for flaws and giving date and place. The grandeur of the argument meets the fragility of manuscript labor. A public call to prayer, an inter-school polemic, a Quranic proof sequence, and a dated act of writing all converge on one question: what should remain audible?

The chapter's answer is that true testimony should not be made silent. The phrase naming Ali's wilaya is not, for this source, a decorative sectarian marker. It is the audible form of a revealed guardianship, supported by the pattern of Moses and Aaron, the verse of guardianship, the excellence of the household, the rank of the best of creation, chosen families, and the Prophet's own public declaration. To resist it is not merely to prefer a shorter adhan. It is to refuse the sound by which the community remembers where guidance continues after the Prophet.

At the same time, the chapter keeps the Prophet's rank clear. Muhammad remains the master of the messengers, the one whose family is honoured because of him, the one through whom the final community receives guidance. Ali's excellence is argued as the excellence of the foremost member of that household after him, the divinely appointed supporter and wali whose guardianship continues the Prophet's authority without becoming prophethood. That boundary matters because it is the answer to the original objection. The phrase is bold, but not confused.

The chapter also shows that ritual words carry history. "Prayer is better than sleep" is not heard by the source as a harmless motivational line, because every ritual word teaches the community what to compare, what to value, and what to remember. "I testify that our master Ali is the wali of God" is not heard as an arbitrary addition, because testimony to wilaya is part of how the community remembers the order of guidance. The adhan is therefore a school of attention. It teaches before the prayer even begins.

## What this episode lands

This episode lands on the form of the prayer summons as a contested vessel of authority. The previous chapter showed that the adhan could carry inward and public witness to the Prophet's household; this one completes the proof and asks whether that witness is addition, obligation, memory, or innovation. The source answers by finishing the formal proof-letter, refusing the equation between truthful addition and harmful omission, and rooting the testimony to guardianship in Ali's own witness after the Prophet.

The listener should leave with three linked insights. First, the adhan phrase about Muhammad, Ali, and the 'atrah is defended through a cumulative Quranic and prophetic argument, not through mere emotional loyalty. Second, ritual alteration must be judged by content, scale, duration, and authority, not by a flat rule that every addition and omission is the same. Third, for this source, the testimony to Ali's wilaya belongs inside the community's sound of prayer because the summons is not only an invitation to worship; it is a public memory of where guidance and authority stand after the Prophet.
