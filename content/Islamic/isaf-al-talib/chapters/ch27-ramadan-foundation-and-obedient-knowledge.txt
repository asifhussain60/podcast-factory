## Where this episode picks up

What if the moon's monthly performance — the vanishing, the thin blade of light, the slow filling out, the vanishing again — was never primarily a way of keeping time, but a portrait: the biography of one particular man, drawn in the sky before he was born, which the community rehearses twelve times a year without knowing whose life it traces? That is where this chapter arrives — as the conclusion of an argument about degrees and dawn, not as a flight from one.

This chapter covers the final stretch of the crescent letter, across three registers usually kept apart. It opens in law. The previous chapter closed by claiming that every pillar of the religion is deposited with the community under the leader standing in the Prophet's place; this chapter puts that claim to work on the fast, and lets an objector strike at it: if everyone must begin on a single day, some people are made to fast a day belonging to Sha'ban, which carries no obligation at all. The answer is legal architecture the series has not had — every obligation stands on two causes, not one — demonstrated across five pillars.

Then the register changes. The letter announces that the call to Allah has two dimensions, outward practice and interpretation, and having secured the outward ruling it turns to the second and reads the sky. The sun becomes the figure of the Prophet in four correspondences; the moon becomes the figure of the foundation, the rank the book calls the Imam, in four more; and the moon's phases are read one by one as the history of that authority. By the end, Ramadan itself has become a sign, the verse commanding the fast an instruction about silence in a teaching council, and the day of doubt at the month's edge the exact double of a community's failure to recognise the authority set over it.

Then a third register, where the letter does what symbolism cannot: it shows its work, with dates. It gives the calculation for the year four hundred, and then faces the last objection in the correspondence, the most fundamental one — that the Prophet forbade taking the stars as a guide, so the whole method is a prohibited science. The reply is a defence that turns into a demand.

Two objectors are quoted here and neither is named; each gets his challenge in full before it is answered. The manuscript is damaged in four places, where the broken word is rendered as it stands and named as broken.

## A cause general, a cause particular

The chapter opens mid-sentence on the principle the previous one established, and does not re-argue it: the pillars of Islam are entrusted to those who preserve them, so the community must follow its leader in them. What is new is the question put to the fast. How, the letter asks, is it permissible for people to enter the fast — one of those pillars — on the basis of their own individual judgement, without following the one who stands in the place of the Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) in this matter?

And the letter names what individual reasoning costs. Did that interruption lead them to enter the fast through their own reasoning, which causes them to treat what is not obligatory as obligatory and what is obligatory as abandoned, or to count as a Sunnah what is in fact a violation of Allah's command and a departure from the infallibility of His authority? *We seek refuge in Allah from that.* The charge is not that private judgement produces a slightly early month, but that it counterfeits obligations in both directions at once and then dresses the result in the Prophet's name.

Which is where the objector finds his opening, turning that principle against it. *According to your principle — which is that the beginning of fasting requires concurrence, and that the entry must fall on a single day — the concurrence implies that some of those days are from Sha'ban and some are from Ramadan, and it becomes obligatory for some to fast in a manner the Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) did not command.* One word there has not survived: where the sense requires the entry into the fast, the manuscript hands us a word meaning irony. It is left as it stands.

The objection is not a quibble. Take the letter's position seriously and the day the community begins on will, for some of them, still belong to Sha'ban by any local reckoning — a day that carries no obligation. So the rule does not merely risk making people late; it conscripts them into an obligation on a day that has none, the exact fault just charged against the other side.

The answer is the new teaching here. The Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) and his family imposed the obligatory duties by Allah's command, and for each established two causes rather than one. The first is a preceding cause that is *general* — general in the precise sense that it causes this obligation and other obligations as well. The second is *specific*, particular to this obligation alone, and it distinguishes this duty from every other duty standing on the same general ground.

The letter walks it through five pillars.

Begin with prayer. The first prayer Allah enjoined falls during the day, not at night. So it is not obligatory except during the day, and the day is the general preceding cause — general because it causes this prayer and the other daylight prayers too. But the day's arrival does not oblige it. It becomes obligatory only when the sun has passed its zenith, and that crossing is the second condition, specific to this prayer and no other.

Zakat next; the obligation itself needs no re-teaching, having had its own chapters. Zakat is not to be given except in the presence of wealth, and wealth is the general preceding cause. Yet zakat is not obligatory with the mere occurrence of that cause: not until a full year has elapsed. The passage of the year is the second condition, and it is specific.

Then the pilgrimage. Hajj is not to be performed except when it is possible for a person to do so, and ability is the general preceding cause. Yet Hajj is not obligatory with the occurrence of that cause either — only on the greatest day in Mecca. That is the second cause in its ruling, and it is specific.

Then jihad, which Allah Almighty imposed on the community. It is only to be waged when there is discord and when the banner of disobedience and defiance of authority has been raised: that is the general preceding cause. Yet it does not become obligatory in the presence of discord and defiance. It becomes obligatory through the standing of the Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) and of a leader who leads the community into battle. That is the second cause, and it is specific.

And so fasting likewise has a general preceding cause, which is the conjunction of the moon and the sun, and a specific second cause, which is the time of dawn at which one must enter into fasting — just as prayer is not obligatory during the day until the day reaches its end in a particular respect.

Then all five are recapitulated from the other side. Prayer's time falls due even if wealth is withheld. Zakat falls due when wealth reaches its threshold amount at the appointed time. Hajj falls due with the possibility of performing the standing on the greatest day at its appointed time. Jihad is obligatory only when a divinely guided leader — one who stands in the place of the Lord of all worlds — is present to lead the community. And in the same way, fasting is not obligatory merely because the moon exists at dawn.

That last line answers the objection without denying anything the objector said. He had assumed the conjunction *is* the obligation. The reply is that it was never the obligation: it is the general half of a cause, the half that also governs every other lunar reckoning in the religion. What obliges a person to fast is the arrival of dawn, and dawn is local and universally available. Nobody is asked to fast a day of Sha'ban as though it were Ramadan; they are asked to recognise that the month their dawn falls in has already begun.

The letter then notes what the opponent's fallback concedes. He had held that since a given period spans part of Sha'ban and part of Ramadan, only the Ramadan portion should be fasted — which in fact strengthens the position of others, since to fast only that portion you must be able to say where it starts, and if you can say that, you have found the month's edge by something other than the crescent you did not see.

What settles the matter, the letter says, is the evidence of the great outward testimony: the testimony bearing witness to the truthfulness of the Messenger of Allah, may Allah bless him and his family, in what he conveyed from Allah Almighty. And that testimony is a balance — on one side the revealed Law and its religious obligations, on the other what Allah Almighty has created in His natural order among the horizons and within souls, as stipulated in the words of Allah: *"We will show them Our signs on the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth"* (chapter 41, verse 53).

A damaged clause follows. No human being could imitate the Prophet in his actions, the letter says, except one uniquely prepared — and here the manuscript gives a phrase naming a barn in Jerusalem, where the sense requires that city's sanctuary. It is left broken. What survives is legible: one set apart by his attribute and his readiness to receive the revelation of Allah Almighty as a gift and a preparation.

And with that the letter turns. This, if we return to interpretation, is one of the two callings by which Allah commanded His Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) to summon His servants to worship Him, as expressed in: *"Call to the way of your Lord with wisdom"* (chapter 16, verse 125). The call divides in two. First, outward practice: the natural bodily worship performed through the obligatory duties and the Sunnahs the Prophet brought. Second, interpretation, which is knowledge — the great hidden worship of the soul, peculiar to particular souls, taken from the perspective of the truths of belief, concerned with abstracting the oneness of God so as to *support* the outward natural worship that is action.

Hold onto that last word: the second dimension is not a replacement for the first but what holds it up, and everything that follows runs on that relation.

## The moon and the foundation

In the interpretive call, the sun has been appointed in its very being — not assigned a meaning afterwards — and everything it is stands as evidence pointing to the status of the Prophet, may Allah bless him and his family.

Then the four correspondences. Just as the sun is the noblest of bodies, so the speaking soul — the Prophet — is the most honoured and most knowledgeable of souls. Just as the sun is the source of light for the other planets, so the Prophet (peace be upon him) is the source for human souls in the divine sciences that lead to the oneness of God. Just as the sun causes the birth of children in the natural world, so the Prophet is the cause of spiritual births in the world of the soul. And just as the sun is a cause linked to the causes of the physical world, so the Prophet is a cause linked to the causes of the world of religion, which is the worship of Allah Almighty — one dimension in the body, the other in the soul.

Then the moon — ordered and receptive, drawing from the sun's light more than any other planet draws, and an intermediary through its acceptance and interaction. All of this points to the rank of the foundation, the Imam.

Four correspondences again. Just as the moon ranks above other bodies in receiving light from the sun, so the foundation ranks above all others in receiving the light of creative existence from the Prophet, peace be upon him and his family. Just as the moon's reception of sunlight exceeds that of every other body, so the foundation's reception from the speaking Prophet of the blessings of divine oneness that flow through him from Allah exceeds that of all other people. Just as the moon illuminates the darkness of night, so the foundation illuminates what the Prophet conveyed of the sealed symbols, the simplified law, and the multiplied parables. And just as the moon stands as an intermediary between the sun and others in the emergence of newborns, so the foundation is an intermediary between the Prophet and the community, nurturing spiritual children in the world of the soul through mutual benefiting and instruction.

Then the method, stated openly: if the conditions of the sun and moon in the corporeal world correspond to their spiritual counterparts in the world of religion, the moon's states can be read. They are read in order.

The first is conjunction. When the moon meets the sun it illuminates half of its own mass, on the side facing the sun — a state which astrologers hold releases a force that renders it virtually the cause of all events and entities until the next conjunction. This is like a minister meeting the king: the king commands him, and he in turn commands the kingdom to carry out whatever the king signs. Likewise, when the moon emerges from conjunction, this resembles the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him and his family) teaching the foundation (peace be upon him) whatever he wished to teach him, so that the foundation could take his place in guidance, in education, and in the bestowal of benefit — a benefit the foundation could not convey before that meeting. And this is why the Prophet pointed to Ali ibn Abi Talib and said: *"I am the city of knowledge and he is its gate; whoever wishes to enter the city, let him come through the gate."*

The second state is concealment. After conjunction the moon is hidden from the general public; it lies beneath the sun's rays and is invisible to everyone except those who possess the vision of knowledge — the same blocking of the eye the earlier chapters established as physical fact. This is analogous to the foundation's position in what has been determined for him of his rank and of the binding nature of his obedience: he is veiled from the guardians of ignorance and from the common people, and none recognise him except those endowed with virtue and discernment.

The third is the crescent's appearance, when the sun sets from its rising point, so that both old and young can see it. This is like the appearance of the foundation's authority, as stipulated by the speaking Prophet, peace be upon him, and the binding of obedience to him when he emerged to the world, so that even the least could recognise the greatest.

The fourth is the plainest. The moon has no light during the day while the sun stands above the horizon; it has no presence at all until after the sun has set. And this is like the foundation having no authority during the life of the Prophet, peace be upon them, until after the Prophet's departure from this world. 

The fifth: at the beginning of its appearance the crescent is weak, only those with sharp sight can see it, and its sighting differs from person to person. This is like the weakness of the foundation's affair at the outset, and the divergence that occurred regarding his Imamate — with a group being told to obey him except those whose days were devoted sincerely. That last clause has come down compressed, and is left as it stands.

The sixth: the increase of the moon's light as the days pass, matched by the gradual revelation of his Imamate among the people, gaining strength over the years.

The seventh: the completion of its light on the fourteenth night, when young and old see it or can hardly doubt the sighting of it — just as the light of his Imamate illuminates all nations when the seven repetitions are completed at the end of the cycle, so that no one doubts his leadership and his precedence.

Then the pairing, turning on the one day of the month where practice and doubt collide. The people of knowledge and faith affirm the day when the moon is hidden after conjunction with the coming month — they affirm it as belonging to the new month, though nothing has been seen. And that affirmation is like the affirmation of those with true faith who knew what they knew from the Prophet's signs, recognising the particular obedience owed to the Commander of the Faithful before it had been explicitly stipulated for the general public. The ignorant and the common people, by contrast, doubt that day: unsure whether it should be fasted from the prior month, because their knowledge does not reach that far, they break their fast until they see the crescent with their own eyes. And likewise they regard the foundation's obedience as though it were no different from the obedience owed to anyone else whose obedience is not obligatory — established neither by sign nor by explicit text — and they violate obedience to him out of ignorance of its obligation, until the truth of his Imamate becomes plain to them.

That is the hinge of the whole figure: the doubtful day and the unrecognised authority are one failure in two registers, and in both the person is called limited rather than malicious.

From the moon the letter moves to the month, and the correspondence extends without a seam. Ramadan is the most honoured and greatest of the year's months, and it corresponds to the foundation's position — for Allah honoured his rank through interpretation, which is knowledge, and marked it through signs. He is the one who gathered all the knowledge of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him. Therefore Allah Almighty said: *"The month of Ramadan in which the Quran was revealed as guidance to the people and as clear proofs of guidance and the criterion"* (chapter 2, verse 185) — taken here as a sign pointing to the foundation.

The argument for that reading is plain arithmetic. We know the Quran was revealed over twenty-three years. It did not descend all at once in the month of Ramadan; it came down day by day, month by month, and year by year. So the verse, on its apparent face, points to signs, to the criterion, and to the distinction, rather than reporting a single night's delivery of a finished book — and for this reason interpretation is needed, to reconcile the statement of Allah Almighty, the statement of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him and his family), and the obligation of fasting with its being bound to the sighting of the crescent.

Then the two perspectives. From the perspective of the soul this applies first to the people of knowledge, when they sight it; from the perspective of the senses, to the people of ignorance. Just so, obedience to the foundation (peace be upon him) becomes obligatory upon the Prophet's departure, may Allah bless him and grant him this station: his designation of the foundation came through signs apprehended by knowledge and discernment, and through explicit text and formal declaration for the general public at the end. It is binding upon those present from the outset by way of instruction and learning, while they remain ahead of the wider community.

And now the commands themselves. Hence Allah Almighty said: *"Do not raise your voices above the voice of the Prophet"* (chapter 49, verse 2) — addressing the community against transgression until the judgement of Allah Almighty is brought forth, narrowing the matter rather than widening it, drawing on the precedent of the Prophet in his time and upon the foundation and their place, peace be upon them, so that the Imam is distinguished from those who are led, and the chief from those who are subordinate. Then He said: *"So whoever among you witnesses the month, let him fast"* (chapter 2, verse 185) — and this is a signal from the spokesman to the nation to follow the foundation and to heed what he says. Its meaning: whoever attends the foundation's council of outward and hidden knowledge, let him be silent, for it is the foundation who is given the honour and entrusted with the affairs of religion and nothing else.

The letter argues for that reading with an inference it uses twice: you do not forbid what nobody was doing. This is proof, it says, that the people — before obedience to him became binding — used to engage freely in matters of religion and knowledge as they pleased, and were then forbidden from it. And the parallel is the fast itself. Just as, before fasting was made obligatory, people used to eat as they liked and were then forbidden through the binding of the fast, so the Prophet commanded likewise in the chapter of marriage, saying: *"There is no marriage without a guardian and two witnesses"* — which indicates that marriage had in fact been conducted without a guardian or two witnesses, as people did as they liked in the early days of Islam, and were then forbidden from it.

Then the exception clause, read the same way. He said: *"And whoever is sick or on a journey, then a number of other days"* (chapter 2, verse 185). This is an exception to the ruling about witnessing the month, and its interpretive sense is that silence has been made obligatory for all people who attend the interpretive council attributed to the foundation — except for one whose words serve as a means of benefit: one who questions, and one who teaches. For them, remaining silent after drawing near, when attending that council, is the spiritual prayer. And it remains obligatory to observe the proper etiquette in the practical prayer of the Sunnah and in the establishment of rulings.

The outward ruling is left intact — the sick and the traveller still owe their number of days, as the earlier chapters established — and the exception is carved for two functions rather than two ranks: where silence is the discipline, the only voices permitted are those that carry knowledge across.

## Calculation and its licence

Having spent several pages in the sky, the letter comes down and names a year, a set of nights, and a number of degrees, inviting anyone to check.

It points first at its own earlier work — we have mentioned in the treatise that includes the discussion of fasting that it too is a means of explanation and clarification, to the extent that benefits were made available to us from the side of Allah's guardian, peace be upon him — and then gives the case. In the month of Ramadan of the year four hundred, by agreement, the people of truth entered their fast two days before the common sighting of the crescent moon, knowing with certainty that there was no discrepancy between the fasting of the people of truth and the fasting of the people of disagreement of more than one day. *The matter was obscure to the ordinary mind except to the wise.* Those two figures sit oddly together and the letter does not reconcile them; they are left as stated rather than smoothed.

Here is the reckoning. The conjunction occurring in the month of Ramadan fell on the night of Monday, making it the first Monday. The crescent was not seen on Tuesday night by looking for the light of its arc. At sunset it stood at six degrees and some minutes above the horizon — and at such a distance, with half of its mass turned toward us, the sight cannot catch it, because it falls beneath the blocking ray of the sun, the physical fact the earlier chapters established. So fasting was not broken that day, out of adherence to proper conduct.

Then the counterfactual, where the case gets its force. The moon was at its end on such degrees from the sun; and had one broken the fast that day, one would have fasted the month of Ramadan only twenty-nine days. Why? Because the conjunction of the sun and moon for Shawwal fell on the night of Wednesday at two hours and some minutes — making Wednesday the day of the forbidden Eid. A breach of one of the obligatory duties would then have occurred, since the month of Ramadan would have lasted thirty days, without any diminishment of the words of the followers, peace of Allah be upon them: that there is no deficiency in Sha'ban or in Ramadan.

The phrase *the day of the forbidden Eid* names the sharp end of the dispute: the error does not leave a month a day short, it converts a day of obligation into a day of festival.

Then an external check, and the strongest evidence on the page. The proof of the validity of our entering the fast on Monday, the letter says, is that the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) said: *"Fast on the day of your fasting"* — a saying that reaches us compressed, and is left as it stands — and that the slaughter in the Haram fell on Monday the tenth of Dhu al-Hijjah in the year four hundred, based on the sighting through knowledge, which was the ninth of that month based on the sighting by the eye. And the matter continues every year, as he said, peace be upon him. That takes the calculation out of the study: the rites in Mecca fall on a day nobody chooses privately, and they landed where knowledge said.

And that brings the last objection in the correspondence, which disputes no number at all. *A dispute arises if someone says: what you have mentioned of the report of the sun and the moon, and the degrees, the distances, and the hours — making all of this your guide — is something the Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) forbade, namely taking the stars as one's guide and acting on them.* It concedes the arithmetic and attacks the licence: the method is not wrong but prohibited.

The reply has two limbs. The first is consistency. The inference by which the time of fasting is established and determined has come through the path of the sun and the moon, which are the cause of months and years — and it comes only through their motion, their course, their conjunction, and their separation. And drawing inference the same way to establish the direction of prayer, and to know the times of prayer and recognise them, stands exactly where fasting stands: these too are pillars of Islam, and they are settled through the lunar mansions and the height of the sun, by calculation. The passage that follows breaks off in the manuscript — a fragment of scripture, *"We have decreed for you"*, then the question whether that is anything other than the division of this measure, then a clause that stops mid-sentence. It is rendered as it stands. The limb survives even so: nobody objects to a prayer direction found by computation, or a prayer time read off the sun's altitude, and those are pillars too.

The second limb re-reads the prohibition itself. The Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) prohibited that pursuit only because of the risk that a student might chase astronomical knowledge without first grounding himself in the divine sciences — a danger that afflicted the followers of Ptolemy and those who attributed divinity to the celestial bodies. The legitimate religious sciences, by contrast, provide exactly what is needed: an understanding of how the heavens and the planets were arranged, so as to deepen the soul's recognition of Allah's oneness, strengthening faith and sharpening the heart's perception of His unity. The prohibition is thus taken as a rule about the order of study — the sky before the source of the sky — and not a ban on a subject.

Then the positive case, a cascade of scripture treating the sky as a text. This is why Allah the Almighty said: *"The creation of the heavens and the earth is greater than the creation of humanity, yet most people do not know"* (chapter 40, verse 57). He also said: *"We will show them Our signs on the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the Truth"* (chapter 41, verse 53) — and the horizons, the letter notes, are nothing other than the heavens and all that they contain. Given all this, Allah the Almighty swore by the stars and identified them as a sign of His oneness in His Book, saying: *"Blessed is He who placed constellations in the heavens and set therein a lamp"* (chapter 25, verse 61). He swore by the positions of the stars, saying: *"And I swear by the positions of the stars — and that, if you only knew, is a tremendous oath"* (chapter 56, verses 75 and 76). He said: *"I swear by the planets that recede, that run their course and set"* (chapter 81, verses 15 and 16). He said: *"By the sky adorned with the zodiac"* (chapter 85, verse 1). He said: *"By the sun and its radiance"* (chapter 91, verse 1). And He mentioned the sun and the moon together when He recited His signs, saying: *"It is He who made the sun a light and the moon a light and determined its phases, so that you might know the number of years and the reckoning"* (chapter 10, verse 5).

That last verse carries the weight: the phases were determined *so that* the reckoning could be known, and a faculty given for a purpose cannot be forbidden in the exercise of it.

Having secured the licence, the letter converts it into a demand. To delay one's knowledge of the planets' positions — their orbits, their peaks, and their nadirs — is to delay engagement with the very testimony to what Allah the Almighty has established of His religion, and to the truth of the Prophet's words concerning what he conveyed from Allah. Such knowledge also encompasses the ranks of the degrees of awareness of what occurs as the planets move through their constellations, south and north, and the aspects they form with one another, affecting the world through the appearance of births and other events that point to the ranks of Allah's guardians. And the argument closes on a chain of questions woven from more than one verse, which is why no single reference can be given for it: *Are darkness and light equal? Are the blind and the seeing equal? Are those who know and those who do not know equal? Only those of understanding take heed.*

Then the result. From all the evidence set forth, it is established that the sighting is of two kinds; that acting upon the superior of the two in fulfilling one's obligations takes precedence; and that the Prophet's intention (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) in his use of the letter *lām* — in his saying that it is the sign to see it — was to direct the people's fasting to the sighting of the crescent with their eyes and nothing else. The letter does not say the eye-rule is false; it says it is what the Prophet directed *the people* to — a rule addressed to a rank rather than a definition of what a month is.

And then the letter turns from argument to address. Know, then — and may Allah grant you success in obeying Him and obeying His guardians — the meanings of what we have set forth. Seek Allah's help in all your affairs, draw near to Him with sincere intention, and exert yourself in worshipping Him and in obeying His guardians, for He is the best of helpers in attaining what one seeks to know of the realities. *I thank Allah the Almighty for the blessings He has bestowed through His trustees.*

## What this episode lands

Three things arrive here that the series has not had. The first is legal architecture: every obligation stands on two causes, one general and shared with other duties, one specific and its own. Demonstrated across five pillars, it dissolves the charge that a shared first day forces anyone to fast a day of Sha'ban — because the conjunction was never what obliged them. Dawn was.

The second is the correspondence, the chapter's centre of gravity. The sun is the Prophet in four respects, the moon the foundation in four more, and the phases become a history: the minister receiving what the king has signed, the concealment beneath rays that blind the ordinary eye, the thin first crescent, the full disc nobody can doubt. Ramadan becomes the sign of the rank that gathered all the Prophet's knowledge, the verse commanding the fast an instruction about silence in a teaching council, and the day of doubt at the month's edge the double of a community's failure to recognise an authority not yet announced.

The third is the licence. The letter shows its work for the year four hundred — the Monday conjunction, the six degrees at sunset, the crescent no eye could have caught, the Wednesday that would have become a forbidden Eid, the slaughter in the Haram that fell where knowledge said. Then it answers the charge that the method is a prohibited science, and does not stop at permission: it makes the study a duty, on the strength of a verse saying the phases were determined so that the reckoning might be known.

And then the treatise ends, with this chapter's central image arriving in the closing blessing. It concludes with praise of Allah the Almighty and prayers upon the Prophet Muhammad al-Mustafa, upon the guardian ʿAlī al-Murtada, upon the pure Imams — the moons amid the darkness — and upon the Imam of the age, our master, our guide, and the guardian of our blessings, the ruling Imam by the command of Allah, the Commander of the Faithful — may Allah's prayers be upon him and upon his pure forefathers and sons, the noble ones who are awaited until the Day of Judgment — the finest of greetings and salutations. We ask Allah to make us steadfast in obeying Him and His guardians, and to illuminate our minds and hearts through His generosity and grace. He is the best of those who are relied upon and the most generous of those in whom hope is placed. Allah is our sufficiency and the best of guardians, and the best of supporters. May Allah bless our master Muhammad and his family.

*The moons amid the darkness.* The letter spent its final pages arguing that a body shining with nothing of its own is the truest figure of an authority holding nothing of its own — and then signs off with a formula the community had been reciting all along, which was the argument in miniature the whole time.
