## Where this episode picks up

When a plague settles over a town, is a believer permitted to pack up his household and leave — and if he goes, has he actually escaped anything? The question was put in writing, by name, to a named authority, and the answer that came back runs two clauses long and pulls in two directions at once. This chapter opens the Book of Funerals, and it opens it at the place where most people meet death for the first time: not at a grave, but in a doorway, standing over somebody else's sickbed, deciding whether to stay.

The source heading names three things — visiting the sick, the clinics, and the dying — and the chapter begins by defining the last of them. It is stated in the book Tafsir al-Arkan that the dying state is the presence of death and the imminence of transition from this world to the hereafter. That definition is doing more work than its length suggests. The dying state is not described as an ending. It is described as a nearness: a transition that has not happened yet and is about to. Everything this chapter later says — about lifespans that are fixed and lifespans that are said to lengthen, about the wretched man overtaken by the Hour before he has died, about the fever that announces what is coming — is measured against that one sentence. Death here is a crossing, and the whole of the chapter's anxiety is about who has made it and who has not.

The material arrives the way most of this book's material arrives: as correspondence, with names attached at both ends. The bulk of the rulings come from the book of questions and answers compiled by the late Sheikh Yusuf ibn Isma'il al-Samati al-Hindi, with the responses of our master Idris ibn Hasan, may Allah be pleased with him — a volume arranged, as the chapter notes later, according to the order of the chapters of Arkan al-Islam, so that a reader with the Pillars open in front of him can find the disputed corner of any ruling. Behind it stand two more books: Arkan al-Islam itself, which supplies the reward for visiting the sick, and the questions Sheikh Hasan ibn Nuh put to our master Husayn ibn Idris, may Allah sanctify his soul, whose tenth issue produces the hardest case in the chapter. And the chapter's opening paragraph also reaches for an epistle this book has already put before us in full — the noble letter of our master and Mawlana Ja'far ibn Sayyidina Sulayman, may Allah sanctify him and grant us his intercession and forgiveness, which he titled The Sharpest Sword in Nullifying Innovations, written to advise his people and to warn the heedless among the people of the island of Yemen after he had settled and lived there. The admonition in that letter was taught earlier in this series and is not re-opened here; it is named because the compiler wants it standing behind the sickroom rulings that follow, as the voice that has already established what happens to a community that lets its practice drift.

The chapter before this one stayed with the worshipper and asked what his prayer becomes when the conditions it assumed have been taken away — a body that cannot stand, a sun that goes dark, an enemy too close to permit the usual length. This chapter turns the camera around. The sick man stops being the one who prays and becomes the one who is visited, and the rulings are now addressed to everybody else: the neighbour at the door, the scholar who knows something the neighbour does not, the family waiting in the next room, the woman who must decide whether a burial can proceed. What the sick man owes has been settled. What is owed to him, and what is owed on account of him, has not.

It moves in three passes. The first gathers the conduct of the visit — the permission to flee an epidemic, the reward that begins the moment a visitor sets out, the meal he must refuse when he arrives, the duty of anyone who knows this to say so, and a short discipline of the sleeping body placed among these rules for reasons worth examining. The second turns from the sickroom to the arithmetic behind it: whether a lifespan can be lengthened, what is lost by a man whom the Hour catches before death has moved him, and the ruling on a woman who dies with a living child still inside her. The third is the chapter's destination, announced in its own heading: the command to remember death, and the four sayings of the Commander of the Faithful that turn illness, bereavement, and loss into a kind of accounting.

One instinct runs under all three, and it is stated in the chapter's very first answer. The matter is in the hands of Allah Almighty. That sentence is not offered as consolation. It is offered as a jurisdictional fact, and everything the chapter permits or forbids follows from it: a man may move because his movement decides nothing; a visitor is rewarded from the hour he sets out because the reward is not a payment for arriving; a burial waits on a certainty because nobody present is entitled to presume the moment of a death.

## The Visit and Its Restraint

The first question is about running away, and it is asked without embarrassment.

What is your ruling, the questioner writes, on people who move from place to place out of fear of epidemic and plague, thinking they can escape it — is this permissible or not? Notice how much the question has already conceded. It does not ask whether flight works. It describes the fleeing party as people who think they can escape, and then asks only about permission. The questioner has separated two things that people under threat almost never separate: whether an action is lawful, and whether it achieves what the person doing it believes it achieves.

The answer keeps them separate. They may go wherever they wish, for the matter is in the hands of Allah Almighty.

Read the two halves against each other. The first half is a permission with no conditions attached: no waiting period, no requirement to seek advice, no distinction between the strong and the frail, no obligation to stay and serve. They may go wherever they wish. The second half withdraws the reason they were going. If the matter is in God's hands, then the road out of the town is not an exit from anything; it is simply a road. The answer therefore refuses both of the positions a frightened community tends to take. It does not forbid the movement, which would have made flight a sin and loaded the terror of an epidemic with guilt. And it does not endorse the theory behind the movement, which would have made the traveller's survival his own achievement and the dead man's death his own failure.

This is a characteristic shape in this book, and it is worth naming because the chapter will use it twice more. A permission is granted at the level of action and denied at the level of explanation. You may do the thing; you may not believe what you believe about it. Applied here, it leaves a man free to take his family to cleaner air without letting him think he has outrun an appointment.

The compiler then marks a seam — the following has also been narrated — and moves from what is permitted to what is owed.

Let none of you be remiss in visiting the sick. The reason given is not sentiment but an established reward, recorded in the book Arkan al-Islam, may Allah sanctify his soul, on the authority of our master the Commander of the Faithful, peace be upon him, who heard the Messenger of Allah, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him, say: "No Muslim slave visits the sick without seventy thousand angels praying over him from the hour he sets out — if it is daytime, until the sun sets; if it is at night, until dawn."

Three details in that sentence repay attention, and all three are easy to read past.

The first is when the reward begins. From the hour he sets out. Not on arrival, not on being admitted, not on the length of the visit or the comfort it gives. The intention has already been converted into an act by the fact of walking, and the seventy thousand begin their prayer while the visitor is still in the street. A visit turned away at the door has still happened, and so has a visit that ends in awkward silence.

The second is the unit of measure. The reward is not a quantity of merit but a span of time, and the span is bounded by the sky rather than by anything the visitor controls. If he sets out in the daytime, the angels pray over him until the sun sets. If he sets out at night, until dawn. A man who goes early in the morning receives a longer accompaniment than a man who goes an hour before sunset, not because he was more generous but because the day was. There is no mechanism here for maximising it. The reward runs to the edge of the daylight and stops.

The third is that the reward is attached to a whole day and can be undone in a moment — which is the subject of the very next ruling, and the reason it follows this one.

He also narrated, may Allah sanctify his soul, announcing his command regarding the Commander of the Faithful, peace be upon him, that he said: the Messenger of Allah, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him, forbade eating the food of the sick man in his home, for Allah will thereby nullify his reward. In another narration: Allah will nullify his reward and even set it against him.

So the visitor who accepts the plate loses the seventy thousand. And in the second, harsher narration he does worse than lose them: what was credited to him is turned around and counted against him. The visit does not merely fail to earn; it becomes a debt. This is the only place in the chapter where an act of worship is described as reversing polarity, and the act that causes it is not an obvious sin. It is eating what you were offered — the most ordinary courtesy there is, and in most circumstances the courteous thing to do, since refusing a host's food is itself a small insult.

The chapter does not leave the ruling standing on its authority alone. It gives the reason, and the reason is entirely material. Eating and drinking in the sick man's home harms him and his family, given the difficulty of his situation. He may be weak and become distressed on account of it, as may his family.

That is a ruling built out of a household under strain. Somebody has to cook. Somebody has to find the food, and somebody has to serve it, in a house where the person who would ordinarily be doing all three is lying ill and the rest are frightened and short of sleep. The sick man himself, weak already, is made to feel the expense and the trouble; the family, stretched thin, must perform hospitality on top of nursing. None of this is visible to the visitor, because a household that is being hospitable is, by definition, concealing its cost. So the tradition removes the decision from the household's hands. The food is not refused because it is unclean or because fasting is better. It is refused because accepting it takes something from people who cannot afford to give it and cannot say so.

Which is precisely why the next sentence is about publication rather than piety.

Whoever is knowledgeable of this must make it known to those who are not, for not everyone is a scholar — yet among the scholars is the one who warns about it. The one who has been warned is excused, and truth has come while falsehood has perished.

A rule of this kind cannot survive on private observance. Its violation is committed with good intentions by people who have never heard of it, and the visitor who eats is not being greedy; he is being polite, and destroying the reward of his own good deed while he does it. So knowledge of the ruling generates an obligation to circulate it. Not everyone is a scholar — that is stated as a simple fact about a community rather than a complaint — and therefore the burden falls on the one who is. Among the scholars is the one who warns about it: the sentence quietly distinguishes a scholar who knows from a scholar who tells, and only the second discharges the duty.

Then the clause that governs the transaction: the one who has been warned is excused. The warning transfers the liability. Before he is told, a man who eats at the sick man's table is inside an excuse; afterwards he is not. This is the same structure the chapter has already used on the fleeing family — action and understanding treated separately — and it explains why a book of rulings bothers to include an instruction about circulating rulings. The ignorant are covered. The informed are not. Which means every scholar who stays silent is keeping people in a state where their good deeds quietly cancel.

A short discipline of the body follows, from the same authority. Mawlana the Commander of the Faithful Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, said in his four hundred points of conduct: "Let none of you sleep on his back or face down. If you see someone sleeping face down, wake him. And a woman should not sleep lying on her back."

At first glance this has wandered in from another chapter. It has not, and the giveaway is the middle clause. If you see someone sleeping face down, wake him. Like the ruling before it, this is a rule addressed to the bystander rather than to the person it concerns, and it authorises an intervention that would otherwise be an intrusion — waking a sleeping man in his own house. The visitor who is forbidden to accept a meal is here required to interrupt a sleeper. Both rules assume a community in which people are in each other's rooms and are expected to act on what they see, and both place the duty on whoever has the knowledge rather than on whoever has the need.

There is a second reason the sleeping body belongs in a chapter about dying, and the third section will make it explicit. Sleep is the posture death borrows. A chapter that will shortly command its readers to remember death often begins by regulating how they lie down at night.

The section closes on a prayer rather than a ruling. He said: "I ask Allah for well-being from the severity of calamity, for the severity of calamity is a trial for one's religion."

Read that beside the opening permission and the pair completes itself. A man may move away from a plague, and he may ask to be spared the severity of one; neither is a failure of trust, because neither pretends to decide the outcome. And the reason given for the request is not that severe calamity hurts. It is that severe calamity is a trial for one's religion — the danger named is to the faith of the person undergoing it, not to his body. The chapter has been describing what the healthy owe the sick. It ends the description by conceding what everyone in the room already knows: that severity of this kind can take a man's religion off him, and that it is entirely permissible to ask not to be tested that way.

## The Measure of a Lifespan

The second movement leaves the sickroom and goes after the assumption underneath it. If the matter is in God's hands, how long is a life, and is that length a settled quantity?

The question comes from the same book of questions and answers, arranged according to the order of the chapters of Arkan al-Islam. What do you say about people's lifespans — are they fixed with Allah Almighty, neither increasing nor decreasing? And if so, what is the meaning of the statements found in books that lifespans increase and decrease?

This is a question about a contradiction in the literature, not about metaphysics in the abstract. The questioner has read both kinds of statement. He has read that a term is appointed and cannot be moved, and he has read, in books he respects, that lifespans grow and shrink — that a charity lengthens a life, that a severed tie shortens one. He is not asking which book to discard. He is asking how both can be saying something true.

The answer: there are deferred lifespans that neither increase nor decrease. But the meaning of increase and decrease is sound in the sense that what decreases from the past portion increases in what remains of the future.

Two clauses again, and again they must be read together rather than chosen between. The first names a category — the deferred term, fixed, immune to movement in either direction. The second rescues the other books' language by relocating what it describes. Increase and decrease are real, but they are movements within the term rather than movements of the term. The single boundary that divides a life into the part already spent and the part still to come is what travels; the sum does not. So the statements that speak of lengthening are describing the division, and the statements that speak of a fixed appointment are describing the total, and the two are not in competition because they are not measuring the same thing.

The sentence is terse and it is meant to be — it is an answer in a reference book, not an essay — but its practical effect is easy to state. Nothing a person does moves the end. What his actions bear on is the standing of the portion he is still inside. And that is exactly the doctrine the first section needed: a man who leaves a plagued town has changed his address and not his term, which is why he may go wherever he wishes and why his going settles nothing.

The next question is stranger, and it goes to the definition the chapter opened with.

What is your understanding, the questioner asks, of the words of the Prophet, may peace be upon him, regarding the wretched and dissolute person who is overtaken by the Hour while still alive and not yet dead — does this mean he is more severely punished than those who have already died, and that in the state of his sensory, animal life he has not yet made the transition to the spiritual life? Or is it something else?

The answer is short and it confirms the reading. If the Hour comes upon him while he is still in the animal state, that is the case.

Everything hangs on the phrase the questioner supplied and the answer accepted: the animal state — the condition of sensory, bodily life, the life a man shares with everything else that breathes. Set that beside the definition from Tafsir al-Arkan at the head of the chapter, where the dying state is the imminence of transition from this world to the hereafter, and the architecture becomes visible. Death is not being treated here as the thing that ends a person. It is being treated as the thing that moves him — the passage out of sensory life into spiritual life. On that reading, a man who is still alive when the Hour arrives has not been spared anything. He has been caught on the wrong side of a crossing that everyone else has already made, holding a form of life that has no use where he now finds himself, and the answer's brevity is a way of saying that the questioner has understood the horror correctly.

This inverts the ordinary intuition about dying, and it is the hinge the chapter turns on. The people in the graves are not the unfortunate ones. The dissolute man still walking about at the end is. A reader who takes that seriously will find that the remembrance of death commanded in the next section is not a morbid exercise but a rehearsal of the only transition that matters — and that the fever, the bereavement, and the loss which the closing sayings describe are all, in their way, appointments being kept early rather than punishments arriving late.

Then the chapter descends, without warning, into the single most difficult case it contains.

It is stated in the questions of Sheikh Hasan ibn Nuh to our master Husayn ibn Idris, may Allah sanctify his soul, and in his answers to them — Issue Ten: regarding a woman who dies pregnant with a fully formed, living child in her womb — should the fetus be left inside her or removed?

The answer: If a woman is available to extract it, it is obligatory to do so if it is possible, or if the fetus is six months or older and shows signs of life. The mother should not be buried until the child's death is confirmed. And Allah knows best.

Three conditions and one prohibition, and they are worth separating.

The first condition is who. If a woman is available to extract it. The propriety that governs the handling of a body is not suspended by the emergency; the ruling asks for a woman's hands and makes the whole obligation conditional on there being one. This is not a minor clause. It means the duty to save the child is not framed as an override that sweeps everything else aside, but as an obligation that arises when it can be discharged properly.

The second condition is whether it can be done at all — if it is possible — which concedes that sometimes it cannot.

The third is the child's own standing: six months or older, and showing signs of life. The ruling wants a threshold of viability and an indication that there is in fact somebody there to extract, and the two together are what convert a desperate act into an obligatory one.

Then the prohibition, which is the part that reveals the chapter's mind. The mother should not be buried until the child's death is confirmed. Burial is urgent in this tradition; delay is not treated lightly. Here the urgency is overruled, and it is overruled by a requirement of knowledge rather than by a competing urgency. Not until the child is presumed dead — until the child's death is confirmed. Nobody standing in that room is permitted to assume what they are almost certainly right about.

Which brings the section back to its own first answer. If lifespans are deferred and fixed, then the length of this child's term is not available to anyone present, and a burial performed on the strength of a reasonable assumption would be a decision made in a jurisdiction that belongs to somebody else. The same principle that allowed a frightened family to flee without claiming an escape forbids a grieving household from proceeding without a certainty. And the closing formula, and Allah knows best, is doing genuine work rather than decorating the ruling: it is the respondent marking the edge of what he has been given to know, in an answer that is entirely about the difference between knowing and presuming.

The section ends where the source ends it, with the counsel that immediately precedes the command to remember death. Mawlana the Commander of the Faithful Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, said in his four hundred points of conduct: "Hold fast to what Allah Almighty has commanded you. Let none of you be present at something he loves unless the Messenger of Allah, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him, would also attend it — otherwise it holds no good. Stay, and good news will come to you."

That is a test of attendance, and its form is unusual. It does not list forbidden gatherings. It supplies a question to be asked in the doorway of any gathering a person is drawn to: would the Messenger of Allah be at this? The thing being examined is not the event but the man's own attraction to it, and the verdict, if the answer is no, is stated economically — otherwise it holds no good. Then the last instruction, two words long: stay. In a chapter that opened by permitting people to move, this is the counterweight, and it is addressed to a different kind of movement. A man may leave a plague. He should not be moving towards whatever he happens to love.

## Remembering Death

The chapter now names its own subject — the mention of the command to remember death and related matters — and the four sayings that follow are the reason everything before them was arranged the way it was.

He swore by Allah to make this clear: "Remember death often on the day you emerge from your graves and on the day of your resurrection before Allah — this will make your calamities easier to bear."

The oath is part of the report, and it marks emphasis rather than doubt: the speaker is insisting. What he then prescribes is a practice, and the practice is given a stated purpose, which is the surprising part. Remembrance of death is not commanded here as a preparation for dying. It is commanded as a remedy for living — this will make your calamities easier to bear. The benefit is present-tense and it is measured in what a person can carry.

The mechanism is not spelled out, but the chapter has already supplied it twice over. A calamity is severe in proportion to what it threatens, and a man who habitually holds the day he will emerge from his grave beside the thing that has just happened to him is measuring his loss against a horizon that makes most losses finite. Set this beside the prayer that closed the first section — well-being from the severity of calamity, for the severity of calamity is a trial for one's religion — and a pair emerges. A believer is permitted to ask to be spared the severe trial, and he is commanded to practise the one discipline that reduces severity from the inside. The permission and the practice are not alternatives. One is addressed to God and one is work.

Note also which day is named. Not the moment of dying, which is what "remembering death" usually means, but the day of emergence and the day of standing before God — the far side of the crossing that the second section defined. The remembrance being prescribed is not of the body's end but of the appointment after it.

"Fever is the herald of death. Allah imprisons in the earth whomever He wills among His servants, and it wipes away sins as thistles are removed from camel fur."

Three images in one sentence, and each one does something different.

Fever is the herald of death. A herald arrives before the event, sent by the one whose arrival he announces, and his function is to give notice. Illness, on this reading, is not a random malfunction of the body; it is an announcement, and it belongs to the same order as the thing it announces. Which reframes the whole of the first section: the visitor at the sickbed who is accompanied by seventy thousand angels is not standing beside an accident. He is standing beside a notification that has been delivered, and his refusal of the household's food is a small recognition that this room is not an ordinary room.

Allah imprisons in the earth whomever He wills among His servants. The verb is deliberate and it is a hard one. To be held in the ground is described as a detention, done by God's will, to whom He wills — which returns the chapter, for the third time, to the jurisdiction claimed in its opening answer. Nobody escapes an epidemic because nobody is holding the keys.

And it wipes away sins as thistles are removed from camel fur. This is the chapter's best image and it should be seen concretely. Camel hair collects burrs, which work their way in, are painful, and do not come out on their own; someone has to pull them out, and the pulling hurts, and the animal is better afterwards. That is what the fever is doing. The pain is not incidental to the cleaning — it is the cleaning — and the point of the picture is that the thing being removed had no business being there, whereas the animal underneath is unharmed. A believer on a sickbed is therefore being groomed rather than punished, and the visitor at his door has walked in on a process, not a verdict.

The third saying extends the same accounting past illness into everything else a person can lose. "None of our Shia will leave behind something we have forbidden, and then die, without being struck by a calamity that purges his sins — whether in his wealth, his child, or even in his own person — so that he meets our love with none of his sins remaining upon him. He is pressed at the moment of death, and his sins are purged."

The sentence is compressed and its opening clause admits more than one reading in English — whether the man in view has abandoned the forbidden thing or has failed to — but its architecture is not in doubt, because the outcome named is identical either way. No one from among them arrives at the meeting carrying a debt. That is the claim, and the rest of the sentence is its machinery.

The machinery has three sites. The calamity may fall on his wealth, on his child, or on his own person — a loss of property, a bereavement, or an affliction of the body — and they are listed in ascending order of what they cost. The word for what the calamity does is purges: not punishes, not balances, but cleans, which is the same verb the thistles image supplied a moment earlier and the reason the two sayings sit together.

Then the purpose clause, which is the whole reason this saying is in a chapter about death rather than in a chapter about hardship: so that he meets our love with none of his sins remaining upon him. The calamity is not the end of the process; it is the clearing of the way to a meeting. Everything painful in the sentence is subordinated to that final encounter, and the earlier teaching of this book on affection and allegiance as a binding relationship rather than a sentiment is what makes the phrase legible — it is spent here as a term already established, not re-argued.

And the last clause adds one more moment. He is pressed at the moment of death, and his sins are purged. Whatever remains at the end is dealt with at the end. The pressing is not described in detail and no attempt is made here to describe it further than the report does; what matters structurally is that the accounting is stated to close. The man does not carry a remainder across.

Which sets up the last sentence in the chapter, and it is the one that reverses everything. "The dead among our Shia is a martyr — one who believed in our cause, loved us, and allied with us."

Three conditions, and they are the three this book has been teaching from its opening chapters: belief in the cause, love, and alliance — the doctrines of affection and of disciplined loyalty that earlier episodes established in full and that this sentence simply invokes. What is new is the verdict attached to them. Not that such a person dies well, or is forgiven, or is received kindly. That he is a martyr. The word belongs to the man who dies on a battlefield in the middle of an act; it is here given to a man who dies in a bed, of a fever, in a house where the neighbours are trying not to accept a plate of food.

Follow the chapter backwards from that sentence and the arrangement is complete. The fever was a herald. The illness was a cleaning. The losses of wealth and child and body were a purge with a meeting at the end of it. And the death itself, which the whole chapter has been standing beside, turns out to be the completion of a relationship rather than the interruption of a life. Which is also, finally, why the fleeing family at the top of the chapter was told they might go wherever they wished. There was never anything out there to escape to, and by the end of the chapter there is nothing here that a believer needs to escape.

## What this episode lands

This chapter has settled the conduct of the visit and the frame that makes sense of it.

On conduct, four rulings, each addressed to somebody other than the patient. A household may move away from an epidemic, freely, and may not imagine that its movement has changed anything. A visitor is accompanied by seventy thousand angels from the hour he sets out, for the remainder of the daylight or the remainder of the night. He must refuse the food he is offered in that house, because the hospitality costs a strained family more than it can say and the acceptance turns his whole day's reward into a liability. And anyone who knows this is obliged to publish it, because the warning is what transfers the excuse — the uninformed visitor is covered, the informed one is not. A short discipline of the sleeping body sits among these, addressed, like them, to whoever happens to be in the room.

On the frame, three settlements. A term is deferred and fixed, and the books that speak of lifespans lengthening are describing the division of that term rather than its total. Death is a transition from sensory life to spiritual life, which is why the man whom the Hour catches still in the animal state has been caught short rather than spared. And a woman who dies carrying a living, formed child is not buried until that child's death is confirmed — a burial overruled not by another urgency but by the requirement of a certainty, in a chapter that has three times refused to let anyone presume what belongs to God.

The closing sayings give all of it a single reading. Remember death often, because a calamity measured against the day of resurrection is easier to carry. Fever is a herald, and what it removes from a believer are burrs pulled out of an animal's coat. A loss in wealth, a child, or the body purges what remains, so that the meeting at the end is met clean. And the believer who dies holding belief, love, and alliance is counted a martyr.

The Book of Funerals has only just been opened here. It has defined the dying state, regulated the doorway, and taught the reader what to hold in mind while he stands in it; the grave itself, and the rites owed to a body once the transition has actually been made, are still ahead.
