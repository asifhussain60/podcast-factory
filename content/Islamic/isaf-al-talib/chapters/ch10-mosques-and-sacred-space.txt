## Where this episode opens

What makes a place a mosque: the walls around it, the permission given to people, or the discipline it demands once a worshipper crosses its edge? This episode turns from the sound that summons prayer to the spaces where that summons gathers bodies. As established earlier, the book has already taught attentive prayer, missed obligations, sacred times, the summons, the adhan's witness, the Discussion Letter, the Remaining Proofs, Unequal Errors, and the First Testimony. This chapter therefore does not rebuild the doctrine of prayer or public witness from the beginning. Its new work is to ask how sacred space is made, guarded, ranked, entered, and used.

The source opens in the style of a legal compendium: question after question, answer after answer. At first the material can feel scattered. A mosque near graves; a mosque above a shop; a mosque inside a private house; a neighbor who hears the adhan; a latecomer arriving during Fajr; a funeral prayer inside the mosque; a traveller with no water who sees a mosque along the road; a sword facing the qiblah; a mosque courtyard; ranked prayers in the greatest sanctuaries; and short sayings from Amīr al-Mu'minīn Ali ibn Abi Talib about sitting in the mosque, spitting toward the qiblah, and approaching the mosque after eating something harmful. The chapter does not argue through one formal proof-letter. It builds a map through cases.

That map has one central tension. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him and his family, is reported to have said that the earth was made a mosque and a means of purification for him. The whole earth is opened as a field of worship. Yet the same source immediately names places where prayer is not to be performed, or where it is disliked, or where a mosque must not be built, or where the direction of prayer requires careful investigation. The openness of the earth does not dissolve the discipline of sacred space. A place can invite prayer, but it can also confuse, pollute, distract, or disorder it.

The chapter also distinguishes between a private devotional corner and a mosque that has been made open to people. This distinction matters more than architecture. A person may set aside a place in his house, or own a structure above a house or shop, or build a small mosque for those around him. But once he makes it available for others, the space is no longer treated as a merely private convenience. It carries claims upon the owner and obligations upon those who enter. Sacred space becomes public through permission, use, and dedication.

From there the source moves toward practice. Who is the neighbor of the mosque? The one who can hear the call. May he pray alone at home? The prayer counts, but it is disliked without excuse. May a worshipper pass the nearest mosque for a farther one of greater rank? Yes. Should a latecomer pray the greeting, the Sunnah, a missed obligation, or join the congregation? The answer depends on time, priority, and what can still be joined before the imam rises from bowing. The mosque is not a magic container where every act is equally well placed. It is a field of ordered decisions.

The episode should therefore sound like a careful walk through thresholds. What turns space from private to communal? What kinds of places are too entangled with death, filth, commerce, or confusion to hold prayer well? What does the worshipper owe the mosque on entering, leaving, returning, standing, facing, and choosing? And why does the source end with ranked sanctuaries and the conduct of the body, as though the highest sacred spaces and the smallest bodily gestures belong to the same grammar?

## Public Dedication

The first question asks about the prohibition of praying between graves and the dislike of building a mosque near graves. The questioner cites the report that the earth has been made a mosque and a means of purification for the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him and his family, except for certain places: pigeon lofts, cemeteries, and graveyards. The answer is firm: one does not pray in those places, and a mosque should not be built near graves. The whole earth may be opened for worship, but openness is not the same as indifference. Sacred space still needs boundaries.

This opening case establishes the chapter's pattern. The source does not begin with a theory of architecture. It begins with contact: prayer near death, prayer in places associated with defilement or distraction, prayer where the living body's orientation may be confused by what surrounds it. A mosque is not simply any covered room in which a person decides to pray. It is a place whose surroundings must support the dignity, direction, purity, and public recognizability of worship.

The next cases ask who controls such a place. What about a permissible mosque situated above a house or a shop? May its owners remove and demolish it? The answer depends on whether the owner has made it permissible for others. If one person owns it alone and has not opened it to others, he may demolish it. If he has made it permissible for others, he may not demolish it. If it is demolished, there is nothing against him. The answer is compact, but the principle is powerful: dedication changes the moral status of property.

The word "permissible" in this context does real work. The space above the shop or house does not become sacred by geometry alone. It becomes bound by the way the owner has allowed others to use it. Before public permission, it remains under private control. After public permission, it carries a communal claim. The owner may still be materially connected to the property, but he has invited the people of prayer into a different relationship with it. The mosque is not only built; it is made available.

The same distinction appears in the question about a man who wants to build a mosque and other structures on his own property. Must he seek permission from the governor? The answer says no. He may do as he wishes and need not seek permission. At one level this is a straightforward property ruling. At another level, it protects the act of dedicating space to worship from unnecessary political mediation. The owner does not need the governor's license in order to prepare his property for prayer.

Yet the source does not turn that freedom into private absolutism. When another question asks about a mosque established inside a house without being made open to people for God's sake, the answer again distinguishes private devotion from public dedication. If the mosque has been made open to people, the greeting prayer is to be prayed in it. If it is confined to the house and not open to others, the greeting prayer is not prayed in it, and the owner may modify it as he wishes unless he has made it open. In other words, the same room can fall under different rules depending on whether it has been offered to communal worship.

This is why a closed mosque inside a house is later disliked in a report attributed to Abu Abd Allah. The dislike is not merely aesthetic. A mosque closed behind a private door carries an ambiguity. It has the name of a mosque, but lacks the public availability that makes a mosque a place of communal access and shared devotion. The chapter is not denying private prayer. It is guarding the word mosque from being reduced to a personal label.

The case of jointly owned land extends the principle. If land is jointly owned and a mosque is built on it, prayer there is permissible, just as it is permissible when one owner builds a mosque on his own portion and the other builds on his. The source recognizes the complexity of ownership without letting that complexity erase the validity of worship. If the space is lawfully available and its use is properly established, prayer can stand within it.

The chapter also treats scale modestly. Whoever builds a small mosque for the people around him, once it is permissible to pray in it, has made a valid space. The mosque does not have to be grand, famous, or politically authorized. It must be available for prayer and not established in a way that violates the boundaries the source has already named. A small neighborhood mosque can be real sacred space when it is rightly made and rightly used.

This modest scale also keeps the chapter from confusing sanctity with spectacle. A mosque built for nearby people may not possess the prestige of a congregational mosque, and it may never be known beyond its immediate neighborhood, yet the source treats its validity seriously. The ordinary act of giving people a lawful place to pray is already a religious act. Sacred space begins where worship is made possible under right conditions, not only where a famous sanctuary or royal foundation announces itself.

That modesty matters because the chapter later lists seven levels of prayer by place, from a prayer in one's house to prayer in the market mosque, the mosque of the tribe, the congregational mosque, al-Aqsa Mosque, the Mosque of the Messenger of God, peace be upon him and his family, and the Sacred House of God. The highest places carry immense rank, but the small local mosque is not erased by them. Sacred space is graded, not flattened. A place may be humble and still valid; another may be higher in merit without making the nearer mosque worthless.

The source also resists another confusion: the mere presence of a grave does not always work the same way in every case. Early in the chapter, the answer says one should not pray between graves and should not build a mosque near graves. Later, Muhammad ibn al-Fahd is quoted on praying in a domed structure or a mosque in which a grave is located on one of its sides. His answer says that, with God's help, the correct position is that both the domed structure and the mosque are permissible places of prayer, whichever was built first. If the grave was placed there by the builder, or if the grave was there before the structure was built, prayer there remains permissible in obligatory and voluntary forms.

This may sound like a contradiction if the listener expects one flat rule. The chapter is doing something subtler. The source preserves a general caution about building mosques near graves and praying among graves, but it also records a later juristic answer that permits prayer in a structure or mosque where a grave is on one of its sides. The difference is not fully theorized in the slice, but the practical effect is clear: sacred space is governed by concrete circumstances, not slogans. One must ask where the grave is, what was built first, how the space is used, and whether prayer itself is being directed into confusion.

The qiblah-facing cases sharpen this point. It is disliked for the qiblah of a mosque to face a bathroom, graves, a latrine, or, in another summary, a crowd. This is not the same as saying every such prayer is void. It is saying that the direction of prayer should not be framed by things that compromise attention, dignity, or purity. The qiblah is more than a compass line. It is a bodily and visual discipline. What stands before the worshipper matters because it shapes the act of facing.

The chapter's first concept therefore lands on a social definition of sacred space. A mosque is made through lawful dedication, permission, access, and proper boundaries. It may be small, privately owned at first, built above ordinary property, or placed on shared land. But once opened for God's sake and made available to people, it becomes more than personal space. Its sanctity is guarded by the way it is built, what surrounds it, who may alter it, and whether the people have been genuinely admitted into it.

This also explains why the source cares so much about demolishing, moving, expanding, and modifying. These are not merely construction decisions. They are decisions about promises made to worshippers. If the space has not been opened, the owner may alter it as part of his house. If it has been opened, he must respect what public dedication has done. The sanctity of the mosque does not abolish ownership by magic, but it does transform ownership into a responsibility.

## Entering the Mosque

The next movement asks what a worshipper owes the mosque after the space has been established. The most repeated duty is the greeting of the mosque. The source asks whether a mosque inside a private house requires the greeting; whether a latecomer at dawn should pray the greeting and Sunnah before joining the imam; whether someone entering to make up a missed obligatory prayer should pray the greeting; whether someone who entered, prayed the greeting, went out for a need, and returned should repeat it; and whether the greeting belongs only inside the mosque or also in its courtyard and surrounding precinct.

The first answer ties the greeting to public mosque status. If the mosque has been made open to people, the greeting prayer is prayed in it. If it is confined to the house and not open to others, the greeting is not prayed. The greeting is therefore not a charm attached to any private corner called a mosque. It is the worshipper's recognition that he has entered a dedicated sacred space. Where the public character is absent, the ritual of entry is absent too.

The dawn latecomer case adds timing. A man arrives at the mosque at Fajr while the imam is already leading the obligatory prayer. If he can still pray whatever Sunnah he wishes and join the prayer before the imam rises from bowing, he should do so before the imam raises his head. The answer assumes that the worshipper is measuring time closely. He must honor Sunnah devotion, but not in a way that misses the congregation. The mosque greeting, the Sunnah, and the obligatory prayer are not interchangeable goods. They are ordered acts inside a narrow window.

Another question makes the priority clearer. A man enters the mosque before the prayer concludes in order to make up a missed obligatory prayer. Should he pray the greeting? The answer says he should not pray a Sunnah when he has an obligatory prayer to make up, except the Sunnah of Fajr, which is prayed alongside the obligatory prayer. The greeting is not prayed except at times when Sunnah prayers are permissible. As established earlier in the teaching on missed obligations, the source does not let voluntary devotion displace a debt. Here that same principle is applied at the mosque door.

The Fajr make-up case preserves a small mercy. If someone joins the obligatory Fajr prayer and has not prayed the greeting or the Sunnah, he may make up the Sunnah prayer after the obligatory Fajr prayer if the time passes, unless the sun has risen, or after midday, or at night. The exact details are compact, but the principle is familiar from the prayer chapters: the mosque has etiquette, but sacred time still governs what may be done. Entering a holy place does not suspend the rules of prayer-time.

The chapter then quotes Muhammad ibn al-Fahd on the mosque greeting in relation to the courtyard and immediate surroundings. He is asked whether the greeting is obligatory only inside the mosque and not in the courtyard or surroundings, and whether one who performs it inside, goes out to the courtyard, and returns must repeat it. His answer says that the greeting is obligatory only inside the mosque itself, not in the courtyard or immediate surroundings, though performing it there is commendable because those areas are considered part of the mosque and its precincts. If he goes out from the mosque and returns, he need not repeat it if he has not left its precincts.

This refines an earlier, stricter-sounding answer that said whoever enters the mosque, prays the greeting, goes out for a need, and returns should pray the greeting again. The later answer introduces the idea of precincts. The question is not simply whether a foot crossed one doorway twice. It is whether the person truly left the sacred complex. The mosque has a core and surroundings, obligation and commendation, inside and precinct. Sacred space is not only a point; it has edges.

The neighbor of the mosque is another kind of edge. The source asks what distinguishes the neighbor of the mosque, given that a person's prayer alone in his house counts as one prayer. The answer says the neighbor is one who can hear the call to prayer, and a solitary prayer for such a person counts as one prayer in congregation. It is disliked for the neighbor of the mosque to pray alone in his house without an excuse. The adhan does not merely inform him of time. If he hears it, he stands in relation to the mosque.

This is a crucial transition from architecture to sound. In the previous episodes, the adhan was the contested public witness and summons. Here it becomes a spatial measure. The neighbor is defined by audibility. The mosque reaches beyond its walls by voice. The person who can hear the call is not treated like someone far away, untouched by the immediate claim of congregation. The sound of the mosque creates a field of responsibility around it.

The same principle appears when a man hears the call to prayer while praying, with another person present, and then prays in his house with another person. Have they missed the merit of congregational prayer? The answer says praying in the mosque is better, citing the saying: "There is no prayer for the neighbor of the mosque except in the mosque." The point is not that the house prayer is nothing at all. It is that the mosque's claim upon the neighbor is stronger than the minimal validity of a prayer elsewhere.

This distinction between validity and preference runs through the chapter. A prayer may count and still be less fitting. A place may be permissible and still not ideal. A gesture may be allowed and still disliked. The source repeatedly trains the listener to hear legal language at different strengths: prohibited, disliked, permissible, commendable, better, obligatory, valid, repeated, not repeated. Sacred space is not governed only by yes or no. It is governed by degrees of fittingness.

The case of bypassing the nearest mosque shows this most clearly. In a place with several mosques, may a man leave his house at prayer time, bypass the nearest mosque, and go to a farther one because it is of greater rank? The answer says yes, this is established as permissible, and there is nothing wrong with it. The neighbor who hears the mosque should not neglect it without excuse, yet the worshipper may travel past a nearer mosque toward one of higher rank. The obligation to value communal prayer does not trap the worshipper in the nearest building when a more meritorious sacred place is available.

The source also regulates repeated congregations. What if an imam has prayed the congregational prayer in a mosque and then another imam comes? Should he lead the same prayer again in congregation in that mosque? The answer says two imams should not lead the same prayer simultaneously in the same gathering, unless the first imam has vacated his place and another has been appointed in his stead. Later the source says that if the imam leaves, the congregation disperses, and a second imam arrives with a congregation, he prays alone. The imamate should not be held in one mosque more than once.

This rule gives the mosque a memory of its own prayer. Once the congregation has gathered behind an imam for a particular obligatory prayer, the space has already hosted that communal act. A second performance of the same prayer in the same mosque is not treated as a harmless duplication, because public worship is meant to gather rather than fragment. The mosque's unity is protected by limiting how leadership repeats itself inside the same sacred enclosure.

This rule looks ahead to the next chapter on prayer leadership, but here it belongs to sacred space. A mosque is not a stage for competing or repeated claims to the same prayer. Congregation must have order. The same space, the same prayer, and the same gathering cannot be fractured into overlapping leaderships. The mosque gathers people into unity; it should not become a place where the public act of prayer is duplicated in ways that confuse authority.

The roof case also belongs here. If one imam leads noon prayer in the mosque while another leads people on the roof, is the prayer valid, and should the greeting be offered from below to above and above to below? The answer says there is no objection to greeting from above toward those praying below. The source does not turn vertical separation into a fatal break. It recognizes the mosque as a layered space while still asking how worshippers relate across its levels.

Funeral prayer brings another threshold. If the imam leads funeral prayers in the mosque, how should they offer the greeting? The answer says that if they entered the mosque at a time when optional prayers are permissible, they pray the greeting first. Even the urgency and gravity of funeral prayer do not erase the mosque's entry etiquette when the time allows it. Yet the source also keeps priorities intact: optional prayer is not performed when it is not permissible, and an obligatory make-up is not displaced by a greeting.

The traveller without water adds an unexpected link to earlier purity teachings. If a traveller's prayer time arrives on the path and he finds no water, yet there is a mosque along the way, he should go to the mosque and pray there. The chapter does not pause to re-teach tayammum or purity under conditions. It simply applies the earlier framework to a new setting: when a mosque is accessible, the worshipper should not treat the road as equal to the sacred place. The mosque draws him toward ordered prayer even in constrained circumstances.

This second concept lands on the mosque as a disciplined threshold. To enter it is to make choices in an ordered field: greeting, Sunnah, obligatory prayer, missed prayer, congregation, repetition, precinct, rank, time, and leadership. The worshipper must not flatten all devotion into one emotional impulse. He must know what is due first, what is better, what may be delayed, and what should not be multiplied. The mosque is sacred not because it suspends order, but because it intensifies order.

## Guarded Direction

The third movement gathers the chapter's many rules about direction, barriers, animals, weapons, unlawful acts, and ranked places. These may look like small etiquette cases, but together they show how the mosque disciplines the body. Sacred space is guarded by what the worshipper faces, what he places before him, what he brings inside, what sounds he tolerates, and how he sits between dawn and sunrise.

The qiblah cases come first. Whoever does not know the qiblah direction should investigate and then pray. If he discovers his error while still in prayer and the time is short, he should turn toward the correct direction and complete the prayer, provided the correct direction lies between east and west. Another summary says that if he discovers the error while praying, he should turn in a brief movement and complete the prayer. If a person is uncertain between two directions and considers the qiblah equally likely to lie in either, he may pray in both directions; another version says he may pray facing either of the two directions, and the same applies across all four directions.

The details are technical, but the logic is humane. The worshipper is responsible to investigate. He is not responsible for omniscience. If he learns the truth while praying, he corrects himself without theatrical disruption. If uncertainty remains balanced, he acts according to the available possibilities. The qiblah is absolute as a direction of worship, but the worshipper's access to it may be uncertain. The law trains him to seek, correct, and complete.

The sutrah cases show similar concern for order. Whoever prays in open ground and finds nothing to use as a sutrah should draw a line. He should not leave his sandals facing the qiblah to serve as a sutrah. If another arrives and places his sword facing the qiblah, where a barrier prevents him from moving away from the prayer, it is still permissible for him to pray. Yet Amīr al-Mu'minīn Ali ibn Abi Talib says that none should pray with a sword directed at the qiblah while it is safe to do otherwise. The difference between necessity and choice matters. A sword may be tolerated under constraint, but it should not be deliberately made the object before which the worshipper stands.

The same distinction governs praying between two rows. It is disliked unless no other spot is available. If another place within the mosque is free, even if finding it requires effort, it is permissible to pray there. The mosque should not be made disorderly by convenience. If space exists, the worshipper should use it properly. If no space exists, the law does not turn necessity into sin. Again, the chapter teaches degrees: preference, dislike, constraint, and valid completion.

The source also names animals and places that disturb the facing of prayer. One should not pray facing camels in the direction of the qiblah, though cows, sheep, and the like are permissible. In the councils of clarification, prayer is disliked in twelve places: a cemetery, a slaughterhouse, a pigeon loft, a place of weeding, camel shelters, facing a sleeper, praying without a garment, facing unclean wet earth, on top of food, in a place of commerce, in churches, and in the houses of the Magi unless they have been sprinkled with water. The list is varied because distraction, impurity, danger, religious ambiguity, and bodily exposure are all different ways prayer can be compromised.

One might expect the chapter to simplify the list into "impure places," but it does not. A market or place of commerce may distract; a cemetery may entangle prayer with graves; camel shelters may create disturbance or danger; facing a sleeper may compromise attention and dignity; praying without a garment exposes the body; wet unclean earth threatens purity; churches and houses of the Magi carry another religious use unless ritually addressed. Sacred space is guarded not only against filth, but against confusion of attention.

The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him and his family, is reported to have forbidden carrying out ḥudūd punishments in mosques. He also told people who heard someone singing astray in a mosque, "I did not find it." The phrase is terse, but the point is that the mosque is not a site for every public act, even when the act belongs to law or social life. Punishment has its place; misdirected song has no welcome there. The mosque is not merely a public hall.

At the same time, the Prophet is reported to have said that it is permissible to pray in sheep pens. This balances the earlier lists. The source does not create a fragile sacredness that collapses at every sign of ordinary life. Some animal spaces are permitted; some are disliked. Some locations require sprinkling; some should be avoided. The worshipper must learn the specific texture of places rather than treating all non-mosque settings alike.

Weapons receive their own caution. From Ta'eefil al-Da'a'im comes the saying that bringing weapons into mosques is not permissible except for the mosque imam on Friday. The exception suggests public responsibility, not casual permission. The mosque is a place of safety, prayer, and order. Weapons belong there only under a narrow need tied to the imam's role on Friday, not as ordinary display. This fits the sword-facing-qiblah case: even when a weapon is present, it should not dominate the worshipper's orientation.

The chapter then rises from these concrete cautions to the seven levels of prayer by place. The owner of the councils of al-Mustansiriyya says that prayer has seven levels: prayer in one's house counts as one prayer; prayer in the market mosque counts as twelve; prayer in the mosque of the tribe counts as twenty-five; prayer in the congregational mosque counts as one hundred; prayer in al-Aqsa Mosque stands at a higher rank; prayer in the Mosque of the Messenger of God, peace be upon him and his family, counts as ten thousand; and prayer in the Sacred House of God counts as one hundred thousand. Each level, the source says, carries deep meanings and words of wisdom.

The numerical ladder gives the chapter its vertical scale. Earlier cases asked whether a small mosque is valid, whether a private mosque is truly public, whether the neighbor should come, and whether one may pass a nearer mosque for a farther one of greater rank. Now the source shows why rank matters. Sacred space is not equalized into a generic devotional backdrop. Place carries merit. A house prayer may count; a market mosque carries more; tribal and congregational mosques intensify communal value; al-Aqsa, the Prophet's Mosque, and the Sacred House of God carry ranks beyond ordinary measure.

This ranked vision does not cancel the chapter's legal caution. In fact, it explains it. If place can multiply prayer's merit, then place can also damage prayer's fittingness. If the mosque can gather the community toward God, then what enters the mosque, what faces the qiblah, and what happens inside the mosque matter. Rank and restriction are not opposites. They are two sides of the same reverence.

The ranking also helps explain why the source permits passing a nearer mosque for a farther one of greater rank. The worshipper is not abandoning the mosque as such; he is seeking the form of mosque-prayer that carries higher merit. This is not spiritual tourism or contempt for the local mosque. It is an acknowledgment that sacred places are ordered by meaning, history, congregation, and divine favor. Movement toward the higher-ranked place can itself be an act of discernment when it does not break another obligation.

The final sayings from Amīr al-Mu'minīn Ali ibn Abi Talib bring sacred space down to bodily conduct. Sitting in the mosque from dawn until sunrise, he says, is a swifter means of seeking provision than striking the earth in pursuit of it. The mosque is not only a place to pass through for the obligatory prayer. It is a place where waiting, sitting, remembering, and remaining can become a form of provision. Time spent there after dawn is not wasted time. It is a mode of seeking from God.

He also says that a Muslim must not spit toward the qiblah; if he forgets, let him seek forgiveness. He should not blow toward the place of his prostration, nor into his food, nor into his incense. These are small bodily acts, but the chapter places them beside rules about mosques and sanctuaries because reverence lives in small gestures. The same body that faces the qiblah must not dishonor that direction. The same mouth that recites must not treat the place of prostration casually.

Finally, he says that whoever eats something that harms him should not approach the mosque. The source does not spell out every example, but the ruling fits the chapter's pattern. The mosque is shared sacred space. A person's private consumption can affect the comfort, cleanliness, and dignity of others. Approaching the mosque is not only a matter of personal desire. One brings a body into a community. The body must be made fit for that approach.

This third concept lands on a complete grammar of guarded direction. The mosque has a qiblah, but the qiblah is not only a compass. It is a discipline of sight, objects, barriers, weapons, cleanliness, posture, and bodily restraint. The mosque has rank, but rank is not only a number. It is a demand for reverence proportionate to the place. The mosque opens the earth for prayer, but it also teaches the worshipper not every place, object, sound, smell, or gesture belongs before God.

## What this episode lands

This episode lands on sacred space as a disciplined trust. The chapter begins from the Prophet's expansive gift that the earth has been made a mosque and a means of purification, but it refuses to turn that gift into carelessness. Mosques must be lawfully dedicated, publicly opened when they claim public status, protected from confusing surroundings, entered with proper priority, and guarded through bodily reverence.

The listener should leave with three linked insights. First, a mosque is not merely a building; it is property transformed by permission, dedication, and communal claim. Second, entering a mosque places the worshipper inside an ordered field of greeting, Sunnah, obligation, congregation, time, and rank. Third, sacred space trains the whole body: where it faces, what it places before itself, what it brings inside, how it sits, and how it honors the qiblah. The chapter's quiet thesis is that prayer needs space capable of teaching reverence before the worshipper even begins to speak.
