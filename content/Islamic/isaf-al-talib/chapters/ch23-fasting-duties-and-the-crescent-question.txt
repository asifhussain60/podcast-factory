## Where this episode picks up

Two believers stand a night's ride apart. One of them, on high ground under a clear sky, sees the thin new moon; the other, under cloud or below a ridge, sees nothing. Have they just begun different months — so that tomorrow is the first day of the fast for one and an ordinary day for the other? And if so, what exactly has been made to depend on the weather?

The chapter before this one finished by laying the whole of fasting out as a catalogue, and it passed over the crescent in a single clause, remarking only that the reports about watching for the new moon address people who cannot reach a determination. That clause was spent in a sentence and not defended. Here it becomes the subject, and the defence runs the length of a chapter.

The heading announces the sighting of the crescent moon: what has been said about it and its established course. That last phrase is the tell. A course that is established is one that can be known in advance, and everything under this heading argues that the month of Ramadan has one.

Four authorities carry the argument in different registers. Our master and Mawlana Suleiman bin Hassan — may God sanctify him in the chambers of the Gardens of Eden — supplies the astronomy. Our master the long-serving preacher who supports the religion of God — may He be exalted above all — supplies the refutation proper, from the forty-second council of the first hundred of his honoured supporting councils; the same figure returns later under the name our master the Supporter of Religion, may God sanctify his soul, standing in a king's courtroom in Shiraz. One of the preachers — may God sanctify their souls — supplies a formal question put to the scholars of all the people of Islam, which this slice leaves standing without its answer. And at the end a narration from the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him and his family — reported by al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad, peace and blessings of Allah be upon him, arrives damaged and opens a reading of one word on which a good deal turns.

The proposition is stated plainly by the party holding it. They say the month of Ramadan is sometimes complete and sometimes short, and that the fast depends on the sighting of the crescent; their evidence is a report from the Prophet — peace and blessings be upon him — "Fast upon seeing it and break your fast upon seeing it; if it is obscured from you, then complete thirty days." Against that stands what this chapter sets out to prove: that the month of Ramadan is never deficient and never varies in any way.

And this is a refutation, so it runs on its opponent's material. The report about sighting is never denied, never called weak, never set aside. It is re-read — given back an occasion, a questioner and an audience — until it says something narrower than the claim it was brought to support, which is why the chapter can end, as it does, on the meaning of one verb.

## Absence and the Moon's Arithmetic

The chapter opens by naming who it is talking to, and the answer is not everyone.

In the Book of Clarification, the source says, the discussion of fasting and the sighting of the crescent moon — along with what is mentioned in this chapter and the two chapters before it — is directed to those who are absent from the Imam's presence, for it is he who knows the appointed times of the crescent moons.

That is the same scoping the previous chapter stated once and left, and it is not re-argued here; the locus of that knowledge was settled at the very beginning of this book and is spent in a clause. What is worth noticing is the shape of the sentence. It does not say that sighting is wrong. It says a body of discussion has an addressee, and that the addressee is defined by a distance. Absence is the condition under which finding the month's beginning becomes a question at all.

And then, immediately, the source does something that ought to be surprising in a book of rulings. It explains how the month works.

Our master and Mawlana Suleiman bin Hassan addressed the matter in his first honoured message, the one bearing the tag of the Ijdiyyah, with the Ahmadiyyah secrets and the Asjadiyyah jewels drawn from the benefits of miracles. Those titles are reproduced as the transmission gives them; they are ornamental descriptions of one message rather than separate works, and the series has met this habit of layered titling before. After discussing what is primary, what is preferable, and its restatement, he explained the mechanics.

When the moon has travelled twenty-eight degrees after separating from the sun, it needs to traverse the first two stations again in order to catch up with the sun, because during that period the sun has advanced two full stations and a step into the third — so that the moon meets the sun at thirty degrees. The astronomers therefore count this as one month.

That passage is difficult, and it is left as it stands rather than smoothed: it runs degrees and stations together in a way that resists a clean reading, and the arithmetic cannot be checked from inside the sentence. What survives is the structure of the claim, and the structure is the point. The moon separates from the sun, travels, and catches the sun again; the catching-up follows from the two motions rather than from anybody's eyesight; and because the motions are regular, the interval between one meeting and the next is a quantity. Which is why the last sentence is about astronomers. They count it. They do not watch for it. Set that beside the heading's established course and the alignment is exact: what can be computed in advance need not be waited for.

This matters more than a technical aside, because of what it does to the two believers a night's ride apart. If the beginning of the month is a meeting of two bodies in the sky, it occurred at one moment for both of them. What differs is not the month, but whether a thin crescent has risen far enough above a particular horizon, at a particular hour, through particular air, to register on a particular eye. Sighting, on this account, is not what makes the month begin; it is one local, obstructed, unevenly available way of finding out that it has.

From there the source turns to the report the other party relies on, and the move is not the one a reader expects. It does not dispute the wording. It grants the report and asks a different question: on what occasion were these words said, and to whom?

We say to them, the source replies, that we acknowledge that this report indicates something — and then it supplies the scene. The Messenger of Allah — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him and his family — when he wished to depart on certain campaigns near the month of Ramadan, was approached by his companions. They said: "O Messenger of Allah, we used to fast with your fasting and break our fast with your breaking of the fast — so what will our situation be in your absence?" The Messenger of Allah — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him — replied: "Fast upon seeing it and break your fast upon seeing it."

Everything in this movement rests on that exchange. The companions are not asking how the month begins. They are describing a practice they already have — fasting when he fasts, breaking when he breaks — and reporting that it is about to fail, because the man whose practice they follow is leaving. Their question is about absence. They are asking for a substitute.

And the reply is the substitute. Read as an answer to the question actually asked, "Fast upon seeing it" is not a definition of the month; it is a provision for people about to be out of reach of the person they were synchronising with. The source draws the conclusion in the form of a pair. The report indicates that fasting is obligatory by following the Messenger when he is present, or whoever takes his place when he is absent — and that breaking the fast is obligatory by following his breaking of the fast. Fasting upon the sighting of the crescent is therefore one of the matters of necessity only during the absence of the Messenger and of the Imam who takes his place. When the Messenger is present — or when the Imam is present — the ruling is to fast by following his fast and to break it by following his breaking of it. And the proof that this is the ordinary case is the companions' own sentence: we used to fast with your fasting and break our fast with your breaking of the fast.

Notice what has happened to the report. It has not been weakened; it has been given back its question. A sentence that looked like a general rule about lunar observation turns out, once the occasion is restored, to be an answer to men about to be left on their own — and an answer about absence cannot be the standing rule for presence, or the companions would have had nothing to ask. They already knew what to do when he was there.

One more thing is folded into that pairing, and the source states it without emphasis. The obligation runs through following — not through the moon, and not in the first instance through the eye, but through a person whose practice the others match. Sighting enters as what you do when the person cannot be matched. Which means the whole dispute about crescents is, underneath, a dispute about what kind of thing the beginning of a fast is: an astronomical fact each believer separately detects, or a communal act that has a head.

## A Prophet Who Need Not Look

Having read the report back to the occasion of its saying, the source turns to the harder version of the opposing case — the claim that the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him — himself fasted upon seeing the crescent moon. If he did it, the argument runs, then the practice is not a provision for absence but the thing itself, done by the one person who could never be absent from himself.

This claim, the source says, is flawed on three counts: two legal and rational, and one other. The tally is odd as it stands, and is reproduced rather than tidied: two of the three are clearly delivered — the transmitted argument and the rational one — and the third arrives later without a label, as the scriptural case that opens the following movement.

The first argument is about what the Prophet already had.

It is well established that the Prophet — peace and blessings be upon him — while he was sincere, had the angel Gabriel — peace be upon him — coming to him morning and evening with revelation and the Holy Quran. Since Gabriel would come to him morning and evening with news from the heavens, the Prophet had no need to turn his face toward the sky in search of the crescent, for he had one who brought him certain news.

The argument is one of redundancy rather than prohibition. Nobody says it would have been forbidden for him to look; the claim is that looking would have had nothing to do, since a man who receives the news of the heavens twice a day does not scan the western sky at dusk for a thread of light. And the source presses this further: had he been observed seeking the crescent, it would have contradicted his own statement that Gabriel was descending to him, and had anyone asked him about that, it would have been established that he was self-sufficient in that regard. That is an argument from what a watching community would have concluded — his contemporaries knew what he claimed about his own access, and the two facts would have stood against each other in public.

The source reinforces this with a saying that does a great deal of work in this chapter — it will return, almost word for word, in the courtroom scene later on. It is narrated from him — peace be upon him — that he said: "I know the pathways of heaven as you know the pathways of the earth."

Then the consequence, which is put as a scene rather than a syllogism. If, after that great saying, he had been seen seeking the crescent in order to be told about it, people would have risen and said: "Where is this from what you said yesterday — that you know the pathways of heaven as we know the pathways of the earth?"

The force of it is in the ordinariness of the objection. It is not a theologian's refutation; it is what any listener with a memory would say the next evening. A man who has told you he knows the roads of the sky as you know your own district does not then stand in the street asking directions.

The second argument moves from what the Prophet knew by revelation to what he could not have failed to know by observation — and it introduces the fact that decides the whole dispute.

It is well established, the source says, that the differing conditions of crescent sightings and their gradations cannot be hidden from him — a matter that is barely concealed from a mine-worker or from one who knows the positions of the earth. For the earth's positions differ. Some are elevated enough to sight the crescent faster, such as the peaks of mountains and whatever follows the same course, while others are lower and slower in sighting.

This is the empirical heart of the chapter, and it is stated almost in passing: the visibility of the new moon is not uniform across the earth, and height helps where low ground delays. If a labourer who knows the lie of the country knows that, the argument runs, it was not hidden from the Prophet; and to suppose otherwise would be the gravest of deficiencies, from which the Prophet is free.

Then the question that follows, put as a challenge to reason itself. How can reason demand that he, who is required to know the difference between the positions, should impose upon all people an obligation of fasting tied to the obligation of Hajj on the same single basis — knowing full well that this is untenable?

That clause about the Hajj is compressed and is left compressed. What it appears to press is the parallel between two obligations dated to the lunar calendar in the same way, so that making a month's beginning local and variable destabilises both at once.

The untenability is then spelled out in the plainest terms the chapter offers. Some people sight the crescent on a given night while others do not. It cannot be right that one day is counted as belonging to Sha'ban by those who cannot see it, and as belonging to Ramadan by those who can. This is something no sane person doubts, and only an ignorant person would dismiss it.

There are the two believers a night's ride apart. The argument is not that their situation is inconvenient but that it is incoherent: the same twenty-four hours would carry two names, two months and two different obligations — one man owing a fast, the other owing nothing — with the whole difference produced by a ridge line or a bank of cloud. An obligation laid on all people cannot be indexed to whether a particular horizon was clear.

The same argument then walks into a room and is delivered aloud, which is the chapter's one piece of narrative. Its lead-in is damaged and is reproduced as it stands: a line about what Allah the Most Holy said in a similar case, in his book of noble biography. What follows is plainly an account of an occasion rather than a saying, and the attribution cannot be read straight.

He came to the court of King Abu Kalijar al-Daylami, the ruler of Shiraz, Iraq al-Ajam, and its kingdoms. There the king brought forward a nobleman from the nobles of the Zaydi school of thought, who delivered a statement among them on the matter of fasting — whether its obligation is tied to the sighting of the crescent or not.

A formal disputation before a ruler, then, with a champion produced by the king. Al-Sharif, the source reports, ruled in favour of the Messenger of Allah — peace and blessings be upon him — and then made his demonstration with his hands. He gathered his five fingers and said: "We are unlettered and do not count," repeating the gesture until he had completed thirty counts with his fingers. He then began again with his fingers, and when he finished the second round, he fell one finger short.

It is a piece of theatre, and an effective one. The hands make the argument without a premise: this month is thirty, that one twenty-nine, and the community that keeps them neither reads nor reckons — it counts on its fingers and watches the sky, because that is what it was given to do.

Our master the Supporter of Religion — may God sanctify his soul — then answered, and his answer goes at the gesture rather than at the conclusion.

God forbid, he said, that the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him and his family, whom God honoured, and who could see beyond the veil with full awareness and consciousness — should be described as unlettered to the point of being unable to distinguish thirty from twenty-nine with his tongue, such that he would need to resort to counting on fingers and moving his hand in the manner of a shepherd counting cattle and sheep.

The reply works by taking the demonstration literally. A man performing that gesture as an argument is asking his audience to picture the Prophet performing it; the reply completes the picture and lets it be seen — a hand moving over a flock. The objection is not that thirty and twenty-nine are the wrong numbers, but that a man who saw beyond the veil is being asked to reach twenty-nine by running out of fingers.

Then the saying returns, in the same words it carried earlier, because the courtroom is exactly where it bites. Moreover, the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him and his family — said: "I know the pathways of heaven as you know the pathways of the earth." If, after that great saying, he had been driven to look to the sky seeking the crescent, people would have risen and said: "Where is this from your statement yesterday, that you know the pathways of heaven as we know the pathways of the earth?"

And the argument from Gabriel returns too, now pushed a step further than before. Beyond this, if seeking the crescent were something obligatory and necessary for us in particular, he would have been free of that need — for Gabriel — peace be upon him — informs him of the universe; and if Gabriel informs him that the crescent has appeared and then he proceeds to ask about it, he would be more likely to have revelation about his own affairs explained to him instead.

Then the closing move, which generalises the point away from prophecy altogether. Even if one of us had access to a king of angels whom he could question about unseen matters, he would not rely on himself in asking and striving. This is something upon which no proof or evidence can be founded.

The scene ends badly for the champion, and the source does not spare him. From the shame of this answer, the man stood up while stumbling in his clothes, until the people rose in the courtroom. The two scholars laughed at him and mocked him, and each of them went to his home.

The detail of the clothes is the kind of thing a report keeps only because someone watched it happen. For twenty-two chapters this book has argued in the register of transmitted rulings; here it argues in the register of a court, and wins in front of an audience.

## A Number of Days

The third argument is the one the earlier tally left unlabelled, and it is the longest. It leaves the Prophet's practice aside for the wording of scripture, and asks a question not yet put: not how the month begins, but how long it is.

Allah the Almighty said in His book: "Fasting is prescribed for you as it was prescribed for those before you, so that you may be pious." (chapter 2, verse 183)

The source reads that comparison as carrying information rather than encouragement. Those before you, it says, refers to the Christians — and their fasting is not based on sighting, but on calculation. The verse establishing the fast establishes it by likeness to a fast reckoned in advance.

Then He confirmed this, the source continues, by saying "a number of days" (chapter 2, verse 184) — and a numbered count of days is one that is already determined, and requires neither sighting nor looking.

That is the pivot of the whole chapter, and it turns on ordinary usage rather than on doctrine. If it were possible that Ramadan is sometimes thirty days and sometimes twenty-nine, He would not have said "a number of days" with certainty — just as one does not say "these are a calculated account", or "these are a few dirhams", about an amount that varies. A man who offers you a few dirhams has counted them.

From the verse the source turns to the calendar itself, and states an arrangement. According to the established Sunnah, six months of the year are complete and six are short. The original and foundational arrangement is built on completeness without deficiency, and the source lays it out in order: the first month, Muharram, is complete; Safar is short; the first Rabi' is complete; the second Rabi' is short; Jumada al-Ula is complete; Jumada al-Ukhra is short; Rajab is complete; Sha'ban is short; and the month of Ramadan is complete.

The list alternates straight down the year, which makes it an arrangement rather than a tally, and it stops at Ramadan with the last three months unenumerated — by then the pattern has done its work. Sha'ban, the month immediately before the fast, falls on a short beat; Ramadan on a complete one.

That placement is then confirmed by the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him — who said: "Sha'ban is short, not Ramadan."

And then the source proves the first half of that saying with an argument so neat it is almost a riddle. The proof of Sha'ban's shortness is the night of its middle. Its fifteenth night is indeed the middle in fact, because fourteen nights precede it and fourteen follow it, making it the true centre.

That only works for twenty-nine. Fourteen, then one, then fourteen. The middle night of Sha'ban is a fixture of practice — everyone knows which night it is — and that shared knowledge is here made evidence about the length of the month containing it.

This cannot hold for Ramadan, the source continues, and shows why by trying both candidates. If you make the fifteenth night its midpoint, it fails, because fifteen days would remain after it. And if you make the sixteenth night the midpoint, that too fails, since sixteen is not half of thirty.

An even month has no middle night — only a seam between two halves. So the feature that proves Sha'ban is twenty-nine proves, by its absence, that Ramadan is not.

Then a second confirmation, drawn from a ruling the previous chapter had already put on the table — the expiation owed by one who deliberately breaks his fast on a single day. He must fast two consecutive months as repentance to Allah — that is, sixty days, equivalent to Ramadan. If he cannot do that, he feeds sixty poor people.

The inference is arithmetical and, once seen, hard to unsee. If it were possible that Ramadan is twenty-nine days, then the expiation would allow for feeding fifty-eight poor people rather than sixty.

Sixty is two thirties. Were a month able to be twenty-nine, the expiation would have to be a variable quantity; it is not, and a flat number of that size is already a statement about the month behind it. What gives the argument its force is that the sixty was not devised for this dispute. It sits in the law of expiation, where nobody was thinking about crescents at all, carrying an assumption about the length of the month the entire time.

The movement then closes with an address to the listening community. So praise be to Allah, O believers — be aware of the completeness of your religion and of your success in faithfulness to it, by holding to the cords of faith through following your guardians, the guardians of the Most Merciful.

Completeness is doing double duty there, and deliberately: a complete month and a complete religion said with one word, bridged by a last clause that keeps that completeness by following. Which returns the argument to where the first movement left it — the beginning of a fast is not a fact each believer detects for himself, but something held in common by holding to a guide.

The chapter then changes form again. One of the preachers — may God sanctify their souls — posed a question in argument against those who deviate from the path of guidance, in the formal style of a disputation: what do the learned scholars of all the people of Islam say regarding the words of the Lord of Majesty and Honour?

The scripture is then quoted at length: "O you who believe, fasting is prescribed for you as it was prescribed for those before you, so that you may be pious — a number of days. If any of you is sick or on a journey, then a number of other days. If any of you witnesses the month, let him fast; and whoever is sick or on a journey, then a number of other days. Allah wants ease for you and does not want hardship for you, and that you complete the count and glorify Allah for having guided you, and perhaps you will be thankful." (chapter 2, verses 183 to 185)

Then the questions come, one after another, with no answers attached. What is the meaning of "so that you may be pious"? Who are "those before"? How many is "a number of days"? And what is the meaning of witnessing the month?

On that last the source supplies the state of the dispute rather than a verdict. Scholars of the Arabic language said that *al-shahr* means the crescent moon; others said it means the full set of days that constitute the month. And on the second reading, the phrase "the month of Ramadan in which the Quran was revealed" (chapter 2, verse 185) is evidence that the Quran was revealed during days that are numbered, and not during the crescent itself.

A fine point with a large consequence. If the word means crescent, to witness the month is to catch sight of a sliver of light; if it means the set of days, it is to be present for a stretch of time — and the second reading draws support from a phrase nobody disputes, since the revelation came down within a span of days rather than upon a crescent.

A further verse is brought in support: "The number of months with Allah is twelve months in the Book of Allah, on the day He created the heavens and the earth, of which four are sacred." (chapter 9, verse 36) Only a single crescent is known — one that waxes and becomes a full moon, then wanes throughout the days of the month. The months themselves are known from Muharram to Dhu al-Hijjah.

There is one crescent, doing the same thing every month and belonging to none of them, while the months are twelve and known by name in a fixed order. Whatever a month is, it is not identical with the moon that crosses it.

The other reading is then given its due. Some scholars said, regarding His saying "whoever among you witnesses the month", that it means whoever among you sees the crescent — and that one who witnesses it is the one who is present in the city, as opposed to the absent person who is on a journey. It is he who must fast and is not permitted to break his fast.

Then the questions narrow onto the arithmetic. As for His saying "and complete the count" — what is that count, and how much is it? And the takbir on Eid al-Fitr, after the completion of fasting: is it complete? Is the prescribed waiting period for the month thirty days, twenty-nine days, or twenty-eight days — and which of these is truly a number of days?

The twenty-eight is the surprise, and it is left standing. Two of the three figures are the ordinary candidates for a lunar month; the third is not, and no explanation accompanies it.

The questioner then closes with a principle and applies it. The Prophet — peace and blessings be upon him — said: "Whatever reaches you attributed to me, present it to the Book of Allah; if the Book of Allah does not agree with it, then I did not say it, and I am not contradicted by it."

That is a test, and the questioner runs the opposing party's evidence through it. So how could I contradict the Book of Allah, he asks, given that fasting by the crescent sighting may prevent the fulfilment of the count of thirty days? They have used as their evidence the saying "Fast upon seeing it and break your fast upon seeing it" — yet, by their own account, this does not agree with the completion of the count as expressed in the Holy Book. This is a reminder for the people of understanding hearts.

The move is precise. The report is not rejected on its own authority; it is presented to the Book, exactly as the report about reports instructs. And the mismatch alleged is arithmetical rather than doctrinal.

The chapter's last movement is a narration that arrives badly damaged. It was narrated from the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him and his family — in what was narrated by al-Sadiq Ja'far ibn Muhammad — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him — and then the transmission breaks. What follows is a fragment: it is established that Sha'ban had passed and the month of Ramadan had not yet begun, and the book aligns with this. The narration's own content is lost; what remains is the conclusion someone drew from it.

Then a restatement of the thesis in the plainest words the chapter has used: it is clear and true that the completion of a month is thirty days.

And then the last argument in the slice, which is lexical, and goes back to the report one final time. We find the saying of the Prophet — peace and blessings of Allah be upon him and his family — "Fast at the sighting of it and break the fast at the sighting of it" — and it points to the reception of that sighting. In the light of what the scholars of language have mentioned regarding the term *lamas*, we found this *lamas* to be one of reception.

The term arrives damaged and cannot be securely traced, so it is reproduced as the transmission carries it. The reading it supports is clear enough, and it is the natural end of everything this chapter has been doing. Throughout the dispute the verb in the report has been treated as ocular sight, a person catching the moon with his eye; the reading offered here takes it as reception — coming to have it, being given the fact. On that reading the report never told anyone to watch the sky. It told them to act on the month's arrival once they have it, however they come to have it: by their own eye for want of anything better, or from the one who knows the appointed times.

Some of the scholars also cited lines of poetry in support of that reading — and there the slice ends, mid-argument, with the verses about to be quoted.

## What this episode lands

This chapter takes a single clause from the previous one — that the reports about watching for the crescent address those who cannot reach a determination — and defends it in four registers.

In astronomy: the moon separates from the sun, travels, and catches it again at a computable interval, which is why astronomers count the month rather than watch for it, and why the heading can speak of an established course.

In the reading of a report: "Fast upon seeing it" is restored to the occasion of its saying, where companions facing the Prophet's departure ask what to do without him. The answer to a question about absence turns out to be a provision for absence, and the practice it replaces was to follow the one they could see.

In argument about the Prophet: he had news of the heavens twice a day and said he knew its pathways as his listeners knew their own roads, so a man seen searching the dusk for a thread of light would have been contradicting himself in public. And the gradations of sighting were not hidden from him, which makes it untenable that he should tie a universal obligation to a local accident, leaving one day to be Sha'ban for a man under cloud and Ramadan for the man on the ridge. The same case is then argued aloud in a king's courtroom in Shiraz against a champion who counted the month out on his fingers, and answered by a picture of a shepherd counting cattle.

And in scripture and arithmetic: a fast prescribed on the pattern of people who calculate; days described as a number, which is what one says of an amount already known; an alternating calendar in which Sha'ban is short and Ramadan complete; a middle night that proves twenty-nine and cannot be found in thirty; and an expiation fixed at sixty, which has been assuming a thirty-day month all along.

The slice ends unfinished, and deliberately so. A formal question has been put to the scholars of all the people of Islam — about piety, about who came before, about how many days, about what it means to witness a month, about whether the count is thirty, twenty-nine or twenty-eight — and none of it has yet been answered. What has been offered instead is a direction for reading: that the verb in the disputed report may mean reception rather than eyesight. The poetry that would support it is the next thing on the page.
