## Where this episode picks up

Can the people standing around a dead body reach the person inside it — and hurt him? This chapter says yes, and one narration comes from a man still alive to describe it: an angel standing over him with an iron club heated in fire, raising it every time a woman in the room cried out. The rulings that follow are not written out of sentiment, but because the tradition holds that what the living do at the edge of a death lands on somebody.

The chapter covers the whole distance a body travels — condolence first, where grief is told what shape it may take, then washing, embalming, shrouding, the walk to the graveyard, the prayer said over it, and the earth closed over it. Six source headings, one journey, and one question asked at every stage: who decides what happens to a body that can no longer decide anything?

The material arrives as correspondence with names at both ends. The bulk comes from the first book of questions and answers already cited in this series, arranged according to the chapters of *Arkan al-Islam* — the Pillars of Islam. Alongside it stand *al-Mukhtasar al-Musannaf*, the summary of the classification, the summary of the effects, the book of purity, the interpretation of the Pillars, and the Book of Reports. Two exchanges arrive by sea: the answers our master and Mawlana Ja'far ibn Sayyidina Sulayman — may Allah sanctify their souls in the chambers of the heavens — sent from the island of Yemen to the island of Hind in reply to the author of this book, and a book of questions from scholars of India answered by preachers of the Yemeni peninsula. Behind the opening section stands the noble epistle this series has already taught in full, *The Sharpest Sword in Nullifying Innovations*, from which the chapter now draws a passage it has not yet quoted.

The previous chapter stayed at the doorway, settling what is owed to a person still in the room. Here the crossing has happened, and the address widens: to the women in the house, to the man who will wash him, to whoever carries the bier, leads the prayer, or owns the ground.

## The Wailing Admonition

It is stated in *al-Mukhtasar al-Musannaf* that no hair is to be shaved over the deceased, and no nail is cut for him — a prohibition this chapter states twice, which in a book this compressed means it was expected to be ignored.

Then a question. What do you say about the practice of some people who place certain weapons on the deceased while he is at home, while others consider it obligatory to sit in attendance? Answer: such a practice has no reliable basis. The respondent then points to where the matter has already been settled, and hands the chapter its longest passage. Our master and Mawlana Ja'far ibn Sayyidina Sulayman — may Allah sanctify their souls in the chambers of the heavens — addressed this in his noble epistle titled *The Sharpest Sword in Nullifying Innovations*, in which he advised his people and warned the heedless and deeply negligent among the people of the island of Yemen, after he had settled there. This series has taught that epistle and does not re-open it; what follows is a passage from it not yet placed before us. The compiler marks the seam — the following is also among what he made known to his community — and lets the letter speak:

*Among you there are those who have taken up the practice of wailing for the dead, with all the innovations that accompany it. Groups of women gather from their various places, mourning the dead through their nights and successive days, as though it were a scripture from your Lord, exalted be He — accompanied by singing and its consequences of amusement and play beyond the bounds of permissibility, even in the days of weddings. The distinction between what is forbidden and what is lawful has become so blurred that what is unlawful is not made permissible even if your adversaries from among the people of this noble school of thought have far more right to such restraint than you. So do not exhaust yourselves in ways that only stand against you before Allah, exalted be He, and before His guardians, peace be upon them. May Allah bless you in your time — this fleeting, perishing world — for you are the cream and the essence of all creation, so how is it that you are not deterred by your own failings, and do not guard yourselves against falling into ruin? May Allah protect you from this henceforth, and from any return to it, if you are believers.*

Three things in that address govern what follows. The charge is not that the mourners feel too much but that the mourning has acquired a form — nights and successive days, women arriving from their various places — treated *as though it were a scripture from your Lord*: condemned for behaving like revelation. Second, it pairs the wailing at a death with the amusement at a wedding in one breath, the same complaint pointed two ways. Third, the comparison that must have stung: people called the cream and the essence of all creation in one sentence are told in the next that adversaries they consider beneath them keep the discipline they dropped. Rank is an accusation here, not a comfort.

The proofs arrive from a book the readers own. Some of you have read in *Arkan al-Islam* the words of its author, may Allah sanctify his soul, in a narration he cited with intent and deliberation, from Ja'far ibn Muhammad, may Allah's blessings be upon him, who instructed at the time of his own death that no one should wail for him and no garment should be torn. No woman should tear her garment in grief, for every such tear is a crack in Hell that only grows wider. He is not legislating for other people's funerals; he is arranging his own, and what he arranges away is the display.

Then an administrative proof. From Ali, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, who wrote to Rifa'ah ibn Shaddad, his judge over al-Ahwaz: *Forbid wailing over the dead in whatever land you govern.* Not advice to a family but an instruction to a sitting judge about a province: wailing is among the things a government suppresses.

Then the narration explaining the pairing of funerals and weddings. It is narrated from the Messenger of Allah, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him and his family, that he said: *Two voices are cursed and hateful to Allah: a voice at the time of calamity — meaning wailing — and a voice at the time of blessing — meaning singing.* One fault with two occasions: the voice raised past the point where it is saying anything.

And then the claim the section is built to support: whoever wails over the dead thereby inflicts on the deceased the causes of the torment by which his soul is afflicted, as is established from the Prophet, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him and his family.

The chapter produces a witness. The account is given in the Holy Book referred to above, concerning ʿAbdullah — the manuscript leaves his father's name incomplete — a man who was ill and expected to recover. He was found in severe pain, unconscious and unresponsive, while the women around him wailed. The Messenger of Allah, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him, called to him three times and received no answer, prayed for him as he prayed to his Lord Almighty, then turned away. ʿAbdullah felt a lightness within himself and regained consciousness, and what he described on waking makes the doctrine concrete. *O Messenger of Allah, I saw an angel of the kings holding an iron club ignited with fire. Every time a woman wailed — "O my grief, O my courage!" — he would raise it toward me.* The Prophet said, *You were brought to it.* And ʿAbdullah said, *No — but Allah restrained her after her outburst.* Then when she screamed, *O my consolation, O my strength!* the angel raised it again and said, *Is she not your pride?* He said, *No, but Allah* — and He restrained her after her outburst.

The exchange is about attribution rather than punishment. Each time a woman names ʿAbdullah as the thing she has lost — *my courage, my strength, my pride* — the club comes up, and each time the dying man hands the description back. The wailing endangers him because of what it says about him, at the one moment he is least able to correct the record. He corrects it anyway.

The Messenger of Allah then confirmed the account and said: *What is the matter with your deceased, that they afflict one another?* He follows it with a warning the manuscript renders unevenly, which this chapter leaves as it stands: *I greet you with something beyond this — yet something far less than this is sufficient for your lifetime. Perhaps the world has been explained to you and its rites and landmarks made clear, so that you follow a course toward your end in a way that is not within your control and not encompassing you.* What a person conceals from himself by dwelling on his household is called a breach of the covenant of Allah he has imitated, reverting from his knowledge to his ignorance.

And now the counterweight, which is why this is not a ban on grief. Ali, may Allah bless him and grant him peace, said: *The Messenger of Allah wept when some of his children died.* He was told: *O Messenger of Allah, you wept, yet you forbade us from weeping?* He said: *I did not forbid you from weeping — I forbade you from wailing and lamenting. But this is tenderness and mercy that Allah, Mighty and Exalted, places in the hearts of whomever He wills among His creation. And Allah has mercy on whomever He wills — He has mercy on the most merciful of His servants.* The answer does not retreat: it draws a line not yet drawn, then re-describes the tears as something placed in a person rather than performed by him.

The measure comes next. He spoke regarding weeping at a calamity and said: *The soul is grieved and the eye is tearful, and the parting is near. Say only what pleases Allah, and do not say what constitutes abandonment — for that will be your deed, hidden from you.* The boundary falls not on the tears, conceded in the first clause, but on *what is said*. Grief is permitted its body and denied its script.

The section's last movement settles where singing stands. The permitted occasions are explained in the same Holy Book — what is allowed at banquets and weddings, so the forbidden and lawful can be told apart. This is the position of the author on which his pen has dried, may Allah sanctify his soul. He narrates from the Messenger of Allah that he heard music being played and asked, *What is this?* They said, *O Messenger of Allah, so-and-so has gotten married.* He said, *He has completed his religion. This marriage is not like secret liaisons — it is not a secret union until smoke is seen or the sound of a tambourine is heard.* And: *The distinction between marriage and illicit union is the beating of the tambourine.* So the noise is not harmless amusement but a legal function: it publishes the marriage. Whoever goes beyond what the author permits has those matters counted against him. In this is a reminder for whoever has a heart, or lends an ear while being present of mind. Whoever does good, it is for himself; whoever offends, it is against himself — and your Lord is not unjust to His servants.

Four short instructions close the section, each replacing a forbidden behaviour rather than merely removing it. Mawlana the Commander of the Faithful Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, said: *Command your family to say good things over your dead.* Fatimah bint Muhammad, may Allah's peace and blessings be upon him, was prevented by her father from wailing; he summoned the daughters of Hashim and told them: *More supplication and less lamenting.* And: *Whoever strikes his thigh at the time of calamity has forfeited his reward.* Even the gesture is covered. Grief may have a face and tears; it may not have a performance.

## Washing and Shrouding the Dead

From the admonition the chapter turns to procedure, and nearly every ruling here is decided by who is harmed rather than what is ideal.

Should a man who washes the dead perform ablution three times with three washings? Yes, and at the final washing he divides them, per the Book of Reports. And if a person is injured and has not been moved from his place, yet regains consciousness for an hour, two hours, or a day, and then dies, he must still be washed, because he was not removed from his place.

Now the hard cases, put as one question. What of a person who dies while travelling with no water available? Or a man who dies among women with no man present? Or a woman who dies with neither a husband nor another woman to wash her? Are such people left unwashed and given dry ablution with dust instead? The answer works by analogy: if a person in a state of major ritual impurity — *janabah* — and a deceased person are in the same situation and there is only enough water for one, the living person washes and the deceased is handled accordingly. A living man's purification for prayer outranks a dead man's washing.

The same principle again: if a man dies and the washer would thereby harm himself, he performs *tayammum* — dry ablution — and then washes the deceased, and this is permissible when nobody else is available. If the living person is in greater need of washing than the deceased, it is permissible. And the summary of the classification states that the first among those present with the deceased has the right to wash him, even if that person is himself in a state of major ritual impurity. Presence beats purity: a body waiting while the qualified are sent for is worse than one washed by the imperfect man already in the room.

Then the cases where there is barely a body. In drowning, burning and dismemberment — if a part is found belonging to a person known to have perished, it is treated accordingly. Whatever falls away from the deceased is washed and prepared along with the rest, even if it is a wound or part of one: each wound is washed in its place, the part is shrouded, and it is buried. And it is stated again that one does not shave the hair of the deceased or cut his nails. A severed part receives the full rite because it belongs to the person, and a hair may not be removed for the same reason: the body is not to be edited in either direction.

The chapter now reaches the one place where a named community is told it is wrong. Ja'far ibn Sayyidina Sulayman replied from Yemen to Hind to questions the author of this book had sent him. Among them: what of a woman who dies and is washed using the *zar* method — the tip of a cloth passed behind her over the pudendum and between the thighs until the other end emerges behind her, forming a bundle? It is a practice used by unbelievers and by certain Muslims alike, the questioner says, since the Dawoodi community uses it and regards those who do not as being in error. Should it be used in washing her, and in her shroud, which they insist upon? And they also use the *sarah* when washing the deceased, covering her with it — is it permissible to do only that, or may she be washed without a cover while a man is present?

Nothing is mentioned in the Book of Pillars except that a cloth is placed over the deceased when washed, covering from the navel to the knees, with the water passing beneath, and the washer wrapping his hand in a cloth and reaching beneath the covering to wash the pudendum and whatever other private parts are not beneath the prohibition. Working according to what I have described, the reply concludes, is therefore contrary to the method you mentioned. The practice is set aside not as indecent but because the Book of Pillars describes something else in enough physical detail to leave no room for it.

As for the shroud, it is mentioned in *The Pillars of Islam* on the authority of Ja'far ibn Muhammad, peace be upon him, that it consists of three garments: a shirt neither buttoned nor closed, a wrap, and a covering cloth. And as for the pleasant practice in washing, particularly with regard to sanctity, the most important thing is that it be done in a garment.

A question about a word follows. What did our master Qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad — may Allah sanctify him and grant us his intercession and blessing — mean in his book on purity, at the end of the section on washing the dead, when he mentions the woman being *'ajar*? Answer: the well-known *'ajrah* in Arab usage refers to a gathered shirt, where the portion from the navel downward is collected and secured, with the clothing and its fullness tucked into the *zar* fastening. This is the distinction between men and women, just as the face-veil — the *niqab* — marks the distinction between them.

Two rulings then extend the consequences to the washer. Mawlana the Commander of the Believers, Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, said in his *Four Hundred Counsels*: if one touches the body of the deceased after it has cooled, ghusl is required. And: whoever washes a deceased person and is then clothed must wash after the shrouding, and if he touches the deceased afterward, he must perform ghusl again. The phrase *after it has cooled* was itself queried, and the answer is not what a reader expects: it means after the deceased has been washed. Whoever washes the deceased should do so according to the Sunnah, because the body has become impure — so a living person who comes into contact with it becomes impure through its moisture, and this is the meaning of the obligation of ghusl being established by contact.

The section's last case comes from the scholars of India answered by preachers of the Yemeni peninsula, may Allah sanctify them and grant us their intercession and blessing. A woman gives birth to two conjoined children, and one of them dies. What is to be done? The reply begins — Allah Almighty creates what He wills — then reasons carefully. If the adhesion can be separated without harm or fear for either of them, it should be separated. If separation carries a high degree of risk, and if their life and death are one, that is, they cannot survive independently, then Allah knows best and does what He wills. If one of them dies without the other, the dead one is separated from what connects it to the living one, insofar as the living one does not feel his parts being severed. If this is possible, the dead one is buried and the other left, even if pain is felt from the body of the other — since their life is one and their death is one. And Allah knows best. Every clause turns on the surviving child's sensation rather than the urgency of burial, and where the answer runs out it says so twice.

Embalming and the shroud open with a question about money. The Book of Reports states that the first claim on the estate of the deceased is the shroud and its preparation, then the debt, then the bequest. But if the debt exceeds what the deceased left behind, does the shroud still take precedence, however great the cost? Answer: it is obligatory to take out the shroud and its preparation first, regardless of the amount. A creditor with a valid claim waits behind a length of cloth, even when the money spent is money he will never see: the covering must exist before there is an estate to argue over.

The embalming preparation is placed on the sites of prostration — the forehead, the nose, the knees, between the big toe and the next, and on the navel, armpits, chest, mouth, eyes, ears, hands, palms, and knees — using a small amount of oil and camphor mixed with a little water. On the third night, water is poured on the grave. If the deceased is from among the people of rank, the Quran is recited over his grave for seven days; over the graves of others, for three. This is what our followers did, may Allah extend their lives; and it is narrated on the authority of Sayyiduna Hasan ibn Idris, may Allah grant him His grace, that he would recite the Quran for the people of rank seven times and for others three times, whether in the mosque, at the grave, at home, or elsewhere.

The summary of the effects states that what is good to use in embalming does not include musk or saffron for men, though these may be used for women. The book of purity places the camphor on the forehead, the nose, inside the hands, the knees, and the backs of the feet — the sites of prayer prostration — and puts cotton on the face, under the armpits, between the knees, the legs, and the feet, when available. And the interpretation of the Pillars settles incense: the deceased is not personally incensed, nor is his funeral procession accompanied by incense. Rather his shroud is perfumed, as is the place where he was washed and shrouded — nothing beyond that.

The principle behind all of it is one sentence. Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, said in his *Four Hundred Counsels*: *Do not fear your dead, and do not touch them with anything fragrant except camphor, for the dead is like one in a state of ihram.* A body is treated as a pilgrim under the restrictions of the sacred state, camphor admitted because that state permits it — which is why the last case resolves itself. What of a man who died while in *ihram* for *hajj*, whose head and face were not covered and who had no embalming applied? Answer: since he died as a pilgrim in *ihram*, his head and face are not covered and nothing fragrant is applied, because that remains forbidden for the span of those days. And the counsel's first half matters too — *do not fear your dead* — because everything this section requires assumes people willing to touch a body.

## Procession, Prayer, and Grave

The body is ready to leave the house, and the first question is where to walk.

Is it blameworthy to walk in front of a funeral procession rather than behind it? The Book of Reports draws a distinction that has nothing to do with etiquette. Walking in front of a *nasib*'s funeral procession is disliked, because of the angels of wrath that accompany it. As for a believer's funeral, it does not matter where one walks, since the angels of mercy surround it — though walking behind is better. The space around a bier is occupied, and the ruling navigates that company. 
Two further rules govern the street. The Reports state that the call to prayer — the *adhan* — at funerals is disliked, though notifying people of a death without making the call is permitted: the announcement may not borrow the form of the summons to prayer. And from the interpretation of the Pillars: it is obligatory not to remain seated when a funeral procession passes — except for one who intends to follow it — and not to sit until the deceased has been placed at the edge of the grave. Standing is required of a bystander who may not know the dead man at all. From the summary of the monuments: when the procession reaches the graveyard, the bier is set down crosswise, and the deceased laid on his back so his right side faces the qibla and his left faces away, in the direction a worshipper would stand.

Then a harder narration. It is narrated in the interpretation of the Pillars that the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, saw women following a funeral procession. He stood and asked them whether they would pray over the deceased; they said no. He asked whether they would carry it; they said no. He asked whether they would find it with the one who would protect it; they said no. He said: *Then return, bearing a burden of sin and no reward.* Three questions, one test, and the test is function: attendance that neither prays nor carries nor sees the body through is harmful, not merely insufficient.

The carrying is described from the summary of the effects, narrated from the Imams of the family of the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him. After the shrouding, the deceased is laid on the bier and carried on the shoulders of the men if they are present. If this is not possible, the body may be placed on an animal or whatever is available, with the shroud covered from above by a garment if one is found. A coffin is placed over the bier as a canopy, covered with a garment, and the body secured if there is fear it might fall.

Now the prayer, and it opens on who may say it. What of a man who dies where there are no men present, only women — may the women pray the funeral prayer over him, or should men pray over him alone and then bury him? Answer: a man's prayer is more in accordance with the madhhab of the Ahl al-Bayt, may Allah bless them — a preference stated by naming the school, not by giving a reason.

Where it happens is settled generously. May the deceased be prayed over after he has been carried from the place where he was prepared, or must one wait until he reaches the graveyard? He may be prayed over wherever one wishes — in the graveyard, in his home, or elsewhere. Whoever wishes may recite supplications between the takbirs, and whoever wishes may leave that to the imam. Funeral prayers are permissible even at times generally disliked for prayer, and also during an eclipse.

Its obligation is defined in the councils of the statement as falling on those Muslims who know about it, until at least one among them has performed it — at which point it becomes a voluntary act for the rest. A duty owed by everyone and discharged by anyone.

The Reports add, from narrators who transmitted from the Ahl al-Bayt, peace be upon them, that it is recommended to pray the funeral prayer during the regular prayer times, unless one fears missing an obligatory prayer — in which case the obligatory prayer comes first. The interpretation of the Pillars applies the same logic to leadership. Whoever oversees or is delegated authority over the affairs of the Muslims has the greater right to lead the funeral prayer. If a group of those close to the deceased attend by order of the imam, or the imam himself attends, this raises their standing. If only one of them attends, the guardian of the deceased — whether appointed by the imam or acting in one of the general affairs of the Muslims — has the greater right to lead. Whoever is established to lead people in prayer has the greater right to lead this one if he attends. And if none of the guardians attend, then they take precedence in praying over the deceased. Underneath the layers, one rule: the right to stand in front of a dead man belongs to public authority before family.

The outer boundary of who may be prayed over is stated without elaboration in the summary of antiquities: prayer is offered over the martyr, over those who committed major sins, and over those killed by hadd punishment or by retaliation. A short book adds that the reciter lowers his voice in supplication during the day and raises it if he prays over the deceased at night. Two brief items close the subject. Ali prayed over himself; and from him: his shroud knot is tied, then loosened, his right hand brought out from beneath his side, and his cheek pressed to the earth. And: if a woman dies and the child in her womb has reached six months, it should be delivered if possible — the ruling the previous chapter reached by another route, here a line of procedure. Then a small exchange from the correspondence between the islands. Concerning a funeral prayer where the imam has come forward and one is content to follow his recitation rather than recite independently — should the follower also recite if the prayer takes place during the day? The answer sends him back to what the book of Shari'ah questions and answers has settled: whoever wishes may recite the supplication between the takbirs, and whoever wishes may leave it and be content with the imam's recitation.

And so to the ground. Burial and graves open with something smaller than a body: it is established in the Shari'ah that whatever falls from a living person should be buried in the earth. Should he bury it wherever he wishes, or only in the cemetery? And if he loses it and does not bury it, has he sinned? Answer: he may bury it wherever he wishes, and there is nothing wrong with him if he loses it. The obligation is real and light.

Then property. What of a man whose land others used to bury their dead without his permission, and who now wishes the graves removed? Answer: it is not permissible for him to remove those buried in his land without his consent, for he has nothing but the value of the grave plots, and it is not permissible to exhume them.

Then distance. It is established in the Shari'ah that the dead are buried where they die and not transported. But what if the place of burial is far away? Answer: the deceased is buried where he dies, in the graveyard of the people of that locality, and is not taken to another country. If he dies in the wilderness, there is nothing wrong with carrying him to the nearest burial place.

Then a woman who dies where no male relative lawfully permitted to look upon her can be found. Who lowers her into the grave? Answer: if there is no male relative whose gaze is lawful upon her, then those closest to her among the people shall bury her; and if that is not possible, the injustice of necessity applies.

The last question concerns what may be placed on a grave. The questioner asks about what appears in the books of the Calm Call, may Allah's peace be upon its owner, regarding placing milk on the deceased, or wood, or whatever they wished, and then placing dust over it — and about placing wages, stones, and other existing materials upon the grave, noting that the people of the apparent path dislike it. The passage is damaged in the manuscript here and the chapter reproduces what it gives rather than repairing it into fluency. The answer is clear enough: it is permissible to place these materials upon the grave. Rely on this and pay no attention to anything else, except what is required for *taqiyyah*. The permission is doctrinal, the restraint circumstantial: the one reason to refrain is the protection of the community the practice would expose.

And the chapter ends by sending the reader back to the graveyard. Mawlana the Commander of the Faithful, Ali ibn Abi Talib, peace be upon him, said in his four hundred points of conduct: *Attend to the graves that you need — their inhabitants visit them and seek provision there; they will be glad of your visit.* A man should seek what he needs at the grave of his father and mother after he has prayed for them. Two claims there reverse the chapter's direction of travel: the dead visit their own graves, and they are glad — the visit benefits them, not only the visitor. And the order is the point: pray for them first, then ask.

## What this episode lands

This chapter has taken a body the whole distance and settled, at every stage, who has authority over it.

On grief, the line runs through speech rather than feeling. Weeping is permitted and defended by the Messenger of Allah's own tears, described as a tenderness and mercy placed in a person by Allah. Wailing is forbidden for a reason stated plainly: it reaches the dead. ʿAbdullah's narration shows how: each cry names the man as the mourner's courage, strength and pride, and he hands every description back with *No, but Allah.* What may be said is redirected rather than silenced: good words over the dead, more supplication and less lamenting.

On the body, the deciding question is repeatedly who is harmed. A living man's water outranks a dead man's washing. The first person present holds the right to wash, even in a state of major impurity. A severed part receives the whole rite, and a hair may not be cut. The *zar* method is set aside by a rival description detailed enough to leave no room for it, and a shroud outranks a creditor even in an insolvent estate. The governing image is a pilgrim in *ihram*: no perfume but camphor, nothing of ordinary life — which is why the man who actually died in *ihram* needs no special ruling at all.

On the journey out, function decides everything. Women following a procession who will not pray, carry, or see it through are sent home with sin and no reward. The prayer may be said anywhere, at disliked hours, during an eclipse; it is owed by everyone who knows of the death and discharged by any one of them; and the right to lead it belongs to public authority before family, even over the executed. Burial belongs to the place of death rather than the family's ground; a landowner buried on without permission is owed the value of the plots and nothing more; and where no lawful man can be found to lower a woman, the necessity is permitted and called an injustice.

Two passages — the warning to ʿAbdullah's household, and the question about what may be placed on a grave — survive unevenly in the manuscript; this chapter renders what is there rather than smoothing them into a fluency the source does not supply.

What remains after all the procedure is the counsel the chapter closes on, which quietly undoes the distance every preceding rule has been managing. Do not fear your dead. Attend the graves you need, because their inhabitants come to them too and are glad to find you there. Pray for your father and your mother first, and then ask. The Book of Funerals began at a sickbed, with a term that could not be lengthened; it has now closed a grave and, in the same breath, given the reader a reason to walk back to it.
