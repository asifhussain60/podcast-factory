## Where this episode opens

What if prayer fails not because the body cannot perform it, but because the heart arrives late, distracted, intoxicated, confused about time, or unsure what debt it still owes? This episode turns from the earlier purity sequence into the Book of Prayer. As established earlier, the source has already taught bodily purity, material purity, ghusl, tayammum, and the legal seriousness of hidden bodily states. Here the question changes: once the worshipper is able to stand before Allah, what kind of attention, timing, memory, and repentance must accompany that standing?

The chapter is not a single smooth sermon. It is a gathered legal-devotional dossier. It opens with sayings attributed to Ali ibn Abi Talib, Father of Imams, from the four hundred precepts of good conduct, then moves into difficult questions from books of legitimate legal inquiry, then into the detailed timing of prayers, Sunnah prayers, missed prayers, forbidden prayer-times, combined prayers, night prayer, Witr, Fajr, and make-up duties. Its surface is a chain of cases. Its inner argument is simpler: prayer is the pillar by which the rest of religious life is tested, and time is not an accidental container around it.

The listener should not hear this chapter as a restart of the book's faith teaching. The levels of faith, guardianship and knowledge, disciplined loyalty, and affection as law have already been taught in earlier chapters. The new contribution is that the book now presses those commitments into the daily clock. Faith becomes visible in whether one rises without laziness, resists distraction, does not treat intoxication as an excuse, makes up what was missed, respects the order of obligations, and knows when a prayer belongs to the day, the night, or the hidden border between them.

The chapter also has a distinctive rhythm. Some rulings are severe: abandoning prayer for a long time is treated as religious collapse until sincere repentance restores the person to accepted worship. Some rulings are precise and merciful: a meal may be eaten first if prayer will not be missed; a person uncertain under clouds should wait for certainty; a weak elderly person makes up missed prayers only to the extent of ability, and no one replaces him. The episode therefore follows the source's own tension: prayer is non-negotiable, but the law does not pretend that the worshipper lives outside fatigue, memory, illness, hunger, weather, age, or death.

## Attentive Prayer

The chapter begins with the virtue and danger of prayer before it turns to timetables. Ali ibn Abi Talib, Father of Imams, says that keeping vigil at night benefits the body, pleases the Lord, exposes the servant to divine mercy, and follows the conduct of the Prophets. Night vigil is therefore not presented as a decorative extra. It is a practice that trains the body, aligns the servant with prophetic conduct, and places the worshipper where mercy may meet him.

The next instruction is immediately practical: a man should not stand for prayer in laziness, and he should not pray while drowsy. The source treats bodily presence as insufficient when the inner condition contradicts the act. Standing, bowing, and reciting may occur, but prayer requires a servant who knows he is standing before Allah. That is why the worshipper is told to reflect, when he stands before God Almighty, that one person's prayer may be accepted while another's may not. The outward form does not automatically carry the inward acceptance.

This is the first major tension of the chapter. Prayer is obligatory, yet the obligation is not reduced to mechanical completion. A person may be physically present and spiritually absent. The source names several forms of absence: laziness, drowsiness, worldly remembrance, fidgeting, and turning attention outward. None of these are discussed as small aesthetic faults. They are threats to the act because they divide the worshipper at the very moment he is meant to stand whole before his Lord.

The most striking image comes when the servant turns away during prayer. God Almighty says, in the reported wording: "I am better for you than that toward which you have turned." The rebuke is not only that the worshipper became distracted. It is that distraction reveals a false comparison. The servant behaves as though whatever caught his attention were more urgent, more interesting, or more worthy than the One before whom he stands. The saying reverses that false hierarchy. Allah is better than the thing toward which the face, mind, or desire has turned.

The source then warns: beware of laziness, for whoever is overcome by laziness will not fulfill his true obligations to God Almighty. This warning belongs with the opening command not to pray lazily, but it is wider than prayer's posture. Laziness becomes a governing weakness. It prevents the servant from carrying the obligations that define him. Prayer is the daily place where that weakness is exposed because its times return whether the servant feels ready or not.

Fidgeting is treated the same way. None of you should play with his beard during prayer, or with anything that distracts him from it. The detail is small, almost ordinary, but its smallness is the point. The law does not only speak to dramatic sins. It attends to the little bodily habits by which attention leaks away. A hand in the beard, the eye drifting, the mind rehearsing business, the body settling into drowsiness: these are not separate from worship. They are the visible signs of whether the servant has gathered himself.

Prayer is then called an offering for every pious person. That phrase gives the earlier warnings their warmth. The point is not simply that defective attention violates a rule. Prayer is something presented. It is the servant's offering, and therefore the servant must not present it carelessly. If an offering is brought before a king, one does not bring it while half-asleep, distracted, and turning elsewhere. The chapter's devotional opening teaches the same instinct before the legal questions begin.

The source strengthens the claim by saying that no deed is more beloved to God than prayer. Therefore the affairs of this world must not distract the servant from it or cause him to be heedless of its times. The Quranic warning is then cited: ⟪ar:فَوَيْلٌ لِّلْمُصَلِّينَ الَّذِينَ هُمْ عَن صَلَاتِهِمْ سَاهُونَ⟫, "Woe to those who pray, those who are heedless of their prayer" (chapter 107, verses 4-5). The verse is aimed not at people who have no prayer at all, but at people whose prayer is real enough to name and careless enough to be condemned.

That is the chapter's first lesson on time. Prayer has times before the legal timetable is even discussed. To be heedless of those times is not a scheduling error only; it is a spiritual failure. The worshipper who lets worldly affairs swallow the appointed hour has not merely missed an appointment. He has allowed the lesser thing to displace the greater. This is why the opening sayings make attention, time, and acceptance inseparable.

The first legal riddles sharpen the same concern by showing that a person may be obligated to pray and yet unable to pray lawfully in his current state. A question asks: what do you say about a man for whom prayer is not permissible, yet who is not excused for omitting it? The answer is the man who drank alcohol until he lost his reason. Prayer is not permissible for him because of his intoxication, but he is not excused for abandoning it, in light of the words of God Almighty: ⟪ar:لَا تَقْرَبُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَأَنتُمْ سُكَارَىٰ⟫, "Do not approach prayer while you are intoxicated" (chapter 4, verse 43).

This ruling is severe because it refuses two evasions at once. The intoxicated person may not say, "I prayed anyway," because prayer is forbidden in that state. He may not say, "Then I am excused," because the forbidden state was self-induced. The obligation of prayer remains, and the obstacle to prayer is his own sin. The law therefore holds together the sanctity of prayer and the moral responsibility for becoming unfit to perform it.

Another riddle asks about a man who prayed while ritually pure, yet whose prayer is not permissible. The answer is a muhājir who prayed behind Arabs whose interpretations are not accepted, because those Arabs are described by the source as disbelievers and hypocrites, and prayer behind them is not permissible. This is not a new teaching on communal belonging; earlier chapters have already treated peace, loyalty, sacred ranks, and disciplined affiliation. Here the point is narrower: the validity of prayer is not only about the worshipper's body. The prayer's leadership and interpretive allegiance also matter in the source's legal world.

A third riddle asks about an obligatory prayer requiring neither bowing nor prostration. The answer is the person floating in water who cannot reach dry land. Such a person does not bow or prostrate. This case prevents the listener from turning the chapter's severity into rigidity. Prayer remains obligatory, but the physical form adapts where the body and environment make the ordinary form impossible. The one floating in water is unlike the intoxicated man. His incapacity is not a self-made violation; it is a condition under which obligation takes the possible shape.

Together, the opening cases define prayer as a disciplined meeting between obligation, attention, bodily capacity, moral responsibility, and valid order. The worshipper may not approach prayer lazily, drowsily, intoxicated, distracted, or under invalid leadership. Yet the worshipper who truly lacks the power to bow or prostrate is not abandoned. The law is strict about what the servant can govern, and exact about what he cannot.

## Missed Obligations

The chapter then enters the world of missed prayers, Sunnah prayers, optional prayers, and make-up. Its questions are technical, but the governing principle is plain: one does not erase a debt to Allah by preferring a fresh devotional act. If a missed obligation remains and the current prayer window allows it, the worshipper must take the debt seriously.

The first question asks what should happen when a man remembers missed prayers, al-fāʾitāt, during the time of a current prayer, and there is enough room in that current window. Should he pray the missed prayers first and then pray the current prayer in its time, or should he leave the missed prayers and perform the current prayer on time? The answer says yes: he sins if he delays while time remains and nothing prevents him from praying.

The wording is compressed, but its force is clear. The source does not let the worshipper use the presence of a current prayer to bury the memory of what he owes. Time is not only the current moment. Time also carries debt. If there is enough room to address what was missed and still keep the current prayer, delaying is sinful because the servant is refusing the order that would repair the past without damaging the present.

The following questions turn to the Sunnah of Fajr and other Sunnah prayers. When the Messenger of God, may God bless him and his family, prayed Fajr after its time had passed, he began with the Sunnah before the obligatory prayer. How then should one who missed the Sunnah of Fajr make it up at Dhuhr? The answer says the Sunnah of Fajr is made up after the missed prayers. If a man has prayed some rakʿahs of the Sunnah and then joins the obligatory prayer in congregation, what remains of the Sunnah is made up if its time has not elapsed, except for Maghrib. If a man prayed the obligatory prayer with the imam and missed the Sunnah, he may pray optional prayers as long as time remains, though the preferable course is to perform optional prayers first and then make up the Sunnah.

These rulings show that the source has a layered sense of devotional order. Obligatory prayer, Sunnah, and nawāfil do not collapse into one undifferentiated category called "extra worship." Each has a place. A missed Sunnah may be made up under certain conditions. Optional prayer may still be offered. Maghrib carries its own special immediacy. The law is not hostile to voluntary devotion; it simply refuses to let voluntary devotion scramble priority.

Maghrib is treated with particular tightness. If the Sunnah of Maghrib is prayed only after some time has passed since Maghrib, how is it intended? The answer says that the Sunnah of Maghrib is prayed after the obligatory prayer without any separation, even something minor. The congregation should not delay the Sunnah of Maghrib in order to perform additional voluntary prayers. Here the legal value of closeness in time is unmistakable. Some acts belong so closely together that a gap changes the act's character.

Other cases test the boundary between performance and make-up. A man prays the obligatory prayer at the very end of its time and misses the time; should he pray optional prayers or make up what he missed? The answer says he should pray optional prayers, since he has already performed the obligatory prayer. A worshipper owes Dhuhr, sets it aside to perform optional prayers, works until the end of Dhuhr time, and then wishes to pray. Is his prayer performance or make-up? The answer says that if he is still within the remaining time, his optional prayer is still a performance.

These answers require careful hearing. The source does not praise delay. Earlier it already condemned heedlessness of prayer-times. But once the question is whether an act fell inside its time, the legal classification depends on the remaining window. Performance is performance when performed within the time. Make-up is make-up after the time has passed. The law condemns sinful delay without falsifying the technical status of an act that still occurred before the window closed.

Congregational prayer complicates the order further. If a man arrives and joins people in ʿAṣr while it is still noon time, knowing they are praying ʿAṣr, should he intend Dhuhr or ʿAṣr? The answer says that if he enters and follows the imam in ʿAṣr while Dhuhr time remains, he may intend Dhuhr with him. If Dhuhr time has elapsed, he prays ʿAṣr with the imam if he wishes and then makes up Dhuhr; praying behind the imam in that case is considered commendable.

The ruling is a good example of the chapter's legal imagination. The outward congregation may be performing one prayer, while the latecomer's intention may rightly assign his act to another obligation if the time permits. The body stands with the congregation; the intention orders the debt. Earlier chapters taught intention as a boundary of worship in purity contexts. Here intention becomes the hinge by which participation, missed duty, and current time are reconciled.

Several rulings concern disliked or forbidden times. Making up prayers at sunrise and sunset, and voluntary prayers at those times on Friday, are described as makrūh on Friday and other days. The chapter adds that whoever prays the later ʿIshāʾ with the imam while still owing Maghrib should first make up Maghrib, then make up the Sunnah of Maghrib and ʿIshāʾ, while the optional prayer of ʿIshāʾ may be performed until midnight and is called the night prayer. Whoever prays Dhuhr while owing Fajr should first make up Fajr, then the Sunnah of Dhuhr, then the optional Dhuhr prayer as make-ups.

The order again matters. Maghrib debt precedes ʿIshāʾ. Fajr debt precedes Dhuhr's dependent prayers. Optional acts do not outrank missed obligations. The chapter is training the worshipper to carry memory into worship, not to treat each prayer as isolated from yesterday's omission.

Then the source confronts the gravest form of omission: a man who did not pray the obligatory prayers in his youth and repented after a long time. Should he perform only the obligatory prayers, or also the Sunnah and optional prayers? The answer says that whoever abandons prescribed prayers for a long time without valid excuse is not considered a Muslim during that period, because the Prophet, may God bless him and his family, said that between a servant and disbelief is the abandonment of prayer. No legitimate act of worship is accepted from such a person. Ali ibn Abi Talib, Father of Imams, is quoted: "Prayer is the pillar of religion; it is the first thing God examines from what the son of Adam has done. If it is sound, He looks at the rest of his deeds, and if it is not sound, no deed of his will be regarded."

The same answer says there is no share of Islam for one who does not pray, and that by abandoning prayer he becomes like a disbeliever in a series of legal consequences, though the two testimonies have still brought him under the rule of Islam. The source is drawing a hard distinction between outward legal inclusion and inward religious ruin. The person is under the rule of Islam because of the shahadatayn, yet the abandonment of prayer has devastated his accepted worship.

The answer then opens the door of repentance. Whoever was in this state and then repents, resolves to perform the prescribed prayers sincerely for Allah's sake, seeking His pleasure and free from hypocrisy and ostentation, will have his repentance accepted. The verse is cited: ⟪ar:يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا تُوبُوا إِلَى اللَّهِ تَوْبَةً نَّصُوحًا عَسَىٰ رَبُّكُمْ أَن يُكَفِّرَ عَنكُمْ سَيِّئَاتِكُمْ وَيُدْخِلَكُمْ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِن تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ⟫, "O you who believe, repent to Allah with sincere repentance, that your Lord may remove from you your evil deeds and admit you into gardens beneath which rivers flow" (chapter 66, verse 8).

This is one of the chapter's most important balances. The abandonment of prayer is described with maximum severity, yet sincere repentance is not treated as theatrical or impossible. The repentant person is truly a Muslim, and his legitimate deeds are accepted. What remains is repair. He must repeat the prayers missed before repentance, perform the prayers he encounters after repentance with their Sunnah and optional components, and ask Allah's forgiveness. If he deliberately broke fasts in Ramadan during that period, he must make them up and expiate for each missed obligation.

The answer closes by explaining that "O you who believe" in the verse refers to those who affirmed the two testimonies; faith here means affirming that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is the messenger of God. That explanation prevents the listener from missing the pastoral logic. The person is addressed as one who can repent because the testimonies still mark him as reachable by command and mercy. The same chapter that refuses to excuse abandonment also refuses to deny the possibility of return.

Later reports soften a different edge. In matters asked of Sulayman ibn Hasan and answered by Dawud bin ʿAjab, a man who abandoned prayer for a period, repented sincerely to Allah Almighty, and did not know how many prayers he had missed, is told that if he has repented and entered the Holy Covenant, he should not abandon prayer further, and there is no sin upon him for what has passed. Whoever returns to Allah, Allah accepts his return, and whoever reverts, Allah will hold him to account.

This answer does not erase the earlier command to repair missed obligations. It addresses a person whose number of missed prayers is no longer known and whose repentance enters a covenantal seriousness. The emphasis falls on not returning to abandonment. The law's severity is directed toward repair where repair can be measured, and toward faithful perseverance where the past cannot be counted cleanly.

The chapter also considers obligations after death and incapacity. Muhammad bin al-Fahd bin Salah is cited in a letter about a man who died and left a small payment for those who would make up prayers and fasts on his behalf, but the amount was insufficient. The answer says the wage must first be used to make up all obligatory prayers, then missed fasts, then, if anything remains, intercessory prayers, Witr, and Sunnah prayers to the extent the sum covers. Priority follows obligation. Scarcity does not abolish order.

Another answer from Hibatullah ibn Ibrahim asks whether women may make up prayers on behalf of men, since missed fasts are made up by men for men, and whether someone else may make up prayers for a weak man who cannot do so. The answer says that making up prayers is like fasting: no one makes them up on behalf of a man except the man himself. If he has reached an age too weak to fulfill his missed prayers, he prays to the extent of his ability, and others may not substitute for him after he has acknowledged his incapacity.

The chapter's make-up teaching therefore lands on personal accountability. Prayer debt can be ordered, repented, prioritized, and performed according to ability. It can even be addressed after death within the limits of a bequest. But prayer is not finally transferable as though the self were absent from its own worship. The servant who can pray must pray. The servant who can make up must make up. The servant who is too weak does what he can. The chapter never lets devotion become either despair or outsourcing.

## Sacred Times

The final movement gathers rulings on the daily structure of prayer. Some are practical: food, combining, clouds, sunrise, sunset, Friday voluntary prayer, and late ʿAṣr. Others are symbolic: hidden times, outward and inward aspects, Fajr as a middle prayer, and the relation of night recitation to the day. The source's central claim is that prayer lives inside a sacred architecture of time.

One practical ruling says there is nothing wrong with delaying prayer if food has been prepared and one will not miss the prayer, because the Prophet, may God bless him and his family, said: "When the evening meal is served, begin with it." This is not permission to neglect prayer. The condition is that prayer will not be missed. The ruling recognizes that hunger and prepared food can disturb attention. Better to eat first and pray within the appointed time than to stand physically in prayer while the mind is captured by the meal.

The chapter then treats combining Dhuhr and ʿAṣr due to a valid excuse. Whoever wishes to combine them should pray Dhuhr before the sun declines and ʿAṣr after it. If someone reverses the order and prays ʿAṣr first after the sun's decline and then Dhuhr, the Dhuhr is permitted, but the ʿAṣr must be repeated. If he prayed Dhuhr after the sun's decline without ablution and then performed ablution and prayed ʿAṣr, both prayers stand. But if Dhuhr was invalid for any reason, then it was not permissible to build ʿAṣr upon it.

This set of rulings returns to the principle of sequence. A later act may depend on an earlier act. If the foundation is invalid, the act built upon it cannot stand as though nothing happened. But if the first defect is corrected in the way the source recognizes, the later prayer is not automatically destroyed. The law is not only a clock. It is an ordered chain.

Uncertainty about time receives its own rule. If someone is uncertain because of clouds or similar conditions and has not reached certainty, then praying at the end of the time with certainty is better than praying at the beginning with doubt. Whoever prays some or all prayers before their time and later becomes certain of this must repeat them. This is a profound statement about time and knowledge. Early zeal is not better than certain obedience. A prayer before its time is not made valid by eagerness.

The reports also say that whoever misses a prayer should pray it whenever he remembers it, except at sunrise, at the zenith, or at sunset. There is scholarly consensus that no prayer is performed after Fajr until the sun rises, unless one is making up a missed obligatory prayer or performing an obligatory act such as the prayer of ihram, the two rakʿahs of tawaf, the two Eid prayers, and the like. Voluntary prayer in those times is disliked, though some held that on Friday it is not blameworthy.

The chapter then gives a Friday-specific discussion attributed to questions found in the handwriting of Hassan bin ʿAbd al-Malik. Voluntary prayer on Friday is permissible from the beginning of the day until the afternoon prayer, alongside Dhuhr at the beginning of its time. After ʿAṣr has been prayed, voluntary prayers are not permissible. Before that point, one may pray Dhuhr and then volunteer in additional prayers until the end of ʿAṣr time. Once ʿAṣr is delayed until it becomes very late, voluntary prayer is no longer permissible.

These rulings show that time is qualitative, not merely numerical. Sunrise, zenith, sunset, after Fajr, after ʿAṣr, Friday before ʿAṣr, and late ʿAṣr are not interchangeable minutes. Each carries a legal atmosphere. A missed obligatory prayer may cut through a time where voluntary prayer is disliked, because debt has a different weight from extra devotion. The worshipper must therefore know not only what he wants to pray, but what kind of time he is standing in.

The Book of Clarification adds that the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, combined Dhuhr with ʿAṣr and Maghrib with ʿIshāʾ without journey, illness, or pressing need. The chapter does not expand this into a general theory, but its placement matters. It sits amid rulings that treat time seriously, so the report cannot be heard as casual indifference to time. Rather, it belongs to the authorized flexibility of the prophetic model. Combining is real, but it is not the same as heedless delay.

Night prayer appears next. The two rakʿahs of the midnight prayer are best prayed at the end of the night. This answer appears more than once in the chapter, attributed through different question-and-answer lines. The repetition underlines the chapter's devotional arc: the night is not merely a leftover space after the obligations of day. Its last portion is especially suited to rising from sleep, gathering oneself, and standing before Allah.

Qadi al-Nuʿman then writes about Dhuhr and ʿAṣr. When the sun declines, that time has entered, and it is not forbidden to pray ʿAṣr during the presence of the sun, which is the beginning of the seventh hour. In the interpretation of the Shariʿah, whoever forgets a prayer should pray it whenever he remembers it. The same teaching states that there are five concealed prayer times known only through proof: Dhuhr when the shadow of everything equals its own length; ʿAṣr when the shadow doubles; Maghrib at sunset; ʿIshāʾ al-Akhirah at the disappearance of the white twilight; and Fajr from the break of dawn until the sun is about to rise.

The phrase "concealed prayer times" is crucial. The prayers themselves are apparent because they consist of visible movements and sensible positions. Their times, however, are hidden and known by proof. The source therefore names ten aspects in every day and night: five outward and five inward. This is not a full return to earlier inner-meaning doctrine; that has already been taught as part of guardianship and knowledge. Here the inwardness is attached specifically to time. The body can be seen bowing and prostrating, but the correct moment must be discerned.

Prayer is then described as having four boundaries like the boundaries of a house: time, qibla, wuduʾ, and intention. This image gathers much of the book's earlier teaching without repeating it. As established earlier, wuduʾ and purity under conditions have already been explained. The new emphasis is that time belongs beside qibla, wuduʾ, and intention as a boundary. A prayer outside its time is like a house missing one of its walls. It may contain movement, direction, water, and words, but the form is not properly enclosed.

The obligatory elements of prayer are listed as seven: takbir, recitation, bowing, prostration, praise, tashahhud, and the sealing. It is not permissible to pray except within the prayer's time. Fajr's proper performance is known. Maghrib begins when the sun's disc sets. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, said that when heat is intense, Dhuhr should be delayed until it cools, for the intensity of heat is from the heat of Hell. Once again, prayer is bound to real conditions: light, shadow, heat, and the body's ability to stand attentively.

Imam al-Muʿizz is asked whether Fajr belongs to the night prayers or the day prayers. He answers that it belongs to neither. If it were among the night prayers, the fasting person could eat at its time; if it were among the day prayers, it would not be recited aloud. The prayers are five from the day's portion and two from the night's portion, and the intermediate prayer between the four belongs neither to night nor day. This is described as great wisdom.

Fajr is therefore a threshold. It is obligatory as two rakʿahs after the Sunnah, because it is a call following an earlier call, and there is no call after it; therefore there is no prayer after it. Night prayers are recited aloud. Every prayer in which recitation is aloud belongs to the night prayers and shares in their character. Regarding the morning prayer, the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, says: "The angels of the night and the angels of the day are both present."

The hidden inner matters then arrange the prayers in a symbolic sequence: the first prayer has seven hours of the day; ʿAṣr follows it; there is no prayer after ʿAṣr; then comes the ʿIshāʾ prayer, meaning Maghrib; then the darkness prayer; then ʿIshāʾ al-Akhirah after the disappearance of twilight. After the night prayer comes Witr, three rakʿahs. In the first rakʿah after Surah al-Hamd, one recites Surah al-Aʿla, beginning ⟪ar:سَبِّحِ اسْمَ رَبِّكَ الْأَعْلَى الَّذِي خَلَقَ⟫ (chapter 87, verse 1). In the second, after al-Hamd, one recites Surah al-Kafirun, beginning ⟪ar:قُلْ يَا أَيُّهَا الْكَافِرُونَ⟫ (chapter 109, verse 1). In the third, after al-Hamd, one recites Surah al-Ikhlas, beginning ⟪ar:قُلْ هُوَ اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ⟫ (chapter 112, verse 1).

In Witr, qunut is observed. The worshipper extends his hands after bowing and raising his head, and supplicates while standing in duʿaʾ al-qunut. After the three Witr rakʿahs comes something additional: two seated rakʿahs of shafʿ al-fuʾd. In the first, one recites Surah al-Falaq, beginning ⟪ar:قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ الْفَلَقِ⟫ (chapter 113, verse 1), and in the second Surah al-Nas, beginning ⟪ar:قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ النَّاسِ⟫ (chapter 114, verse 1). Then follows Fajr.

The source identifies Fajr as the middle prayer about which Allah says: ⟪ar:حَافِظُوا عَلَى الصَّلَوَاتِ وَالصَّلَاةِ الْوُسْطَى وَقُومُوا لِلَّهِ قَانِتِينَ⟫, "Guard strictly the prayers and the middle prayer, and stand before Allah in devout obedience" (chapter 2, verse 238). Qunut is performed in Fajr. When it is four rakʿahs, two are nafl and two are obligatory.

This section is dense, but its auditory shape is beautiful: night, Witr, qunut, the two protecting surahs, Fajr, the angels of night and day, and the middle prayer. The law of prayer-times becomes a map of transition. The worshipper moves from darkness toward dawn, from voluntary devotion toward obligation, from night-recitation toward the border that belongs fully to neither night nor day.

The author of the hidden epistle then states the five prayers in day and night as seventeen rakʿahs: Fajr from morning until sunrise; the first prayer for seven hours of the day; ʿAṣr for nine hours when the shadow doubles; Maghrib at sunset when darkness appears; and ʿIshāʾ al-Akhirah at the disappearance of twilight and onset of darkness. These are the outward times in which Allah has commanded prayer in two modes of obedience. There is no prayer after ʿAṣr until the sun sets. For Maghrib, there is no difference between adhan and iqamah as there is for the other four prayers.

The chapter's last practical answer returns to make-up prayer under incapacity. A question asks whether women can make up prayers on behalf of men, and whether others may make up prayers for a weak man who cannot fulfill what he missed. The answer says no. Prayer is like fasting in this respect: no one makes it up on behalf of a man except the man himself. If he has reached weakness, he prays to the extent of his ability, after bowing and acknowledging his incapacity.

This final answer brings the whole chapter back to the servant before Allah. Prayer has sacred times, inherited forms, preferred sequences, forbidden moments, make-up rules, exceptions for uncertainty, and deep symbolic correspondences. Yet none of that removes the personal address. The worshipper is the one who must stand, remember, repent, make up, intend, and perform according to ability. Time can guide him, law can order him, but no one else can simply become him before Allah.

## What this episode lands

This episode lands on prayer as the pillar where faith becomes disciplined time. The chapter moves from attention in the moment of standing, to the repair of missed obligations, to the sacred architecture of day, night, shadow, twilight, Witr, and Fajr. It teaches that prayer is beloved to Allah and dangerous to neglect because it gathers body, intention, leadership, memory, repentance, and time into one act.

Its forward motion from earlier chapters is decisive. The book has already taught the body how to become pure; now it teaches the worshipper not to waste that purity through laziness, heedlessness, wrong order, forgotten debts, or careless timing. The listener leaves with a demanding but coherent picture: prayer must be performed with attention, within its boundaries, in the right order, with sincere repentance for what was lost, and with humble acceptance of what the servant can genuinely do.
