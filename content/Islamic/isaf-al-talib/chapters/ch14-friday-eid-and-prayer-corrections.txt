## Where this episode opens

What happens when a prayer is not only worship, but a public test of distance, leadership, silence, memory, and certainty? This chapter opens there. Friday prayer is not treated as a private noon prayer with a larger audience. Eid is not treated as a decorative festival prayer after a month of fasting or after sacrifice. The prostration of omission is not treated as a mechanical patch for every anxious doubt. The source gathers all three because each one asks the same deeper question: when worship takes place under pressure, how does the community know where the valid act is?

Earlier episodes have already taught the summons, attentive prayer, sacred times, ranked leadership, following without fracture, shared congregational boundaries, prayer's shape, speech and motion, and garment and ground. This episode should not reopen those teachings. Friday prayer tests public authority and lawful attendance. Eid tests the difference between outward festivity and inward disclosure. Omission tests the border between a real error and a merely unsettled mind.

The chapter's source-shape is the familiar one for this book: question-and-answer rulings, abbreviated legal summaries, transmitted reports, interpretive readings of the Sharīʿa, hidden epistles, and sayings attributed to earlier authorities. It does not stage a sustained named debate. Its governing method is accumulation. A ruling appears, then a condition, then an exception, then an inward meaning. The listener is meant to feel the density, but also the pattern: prayer is preserved by measure.

That measure begins with distance. Two congregations, two cities, a single city, three miles, one authorized mosque, one preacher, one pulpit, one adhan, one sermon, and a people who must know whether they are gathering correctly. It then turns toward Eid, where the community emerges from hiddenness into manifestation through takbir, ghusl, khutbah, and the name of fitr. Finally it turns to the worshipper who has forgotten, added, doubted, followed an imam, arrived late, spoken an intention, missed recitation, or risen into an extra rak'ah. The chapter keeps asking: is this a defect that breaks the act, a defect that is repaired, or not a defect at all?

## Friday's Gathered Order

The first cluster concerns the Friday prayer. The opening question asks about a ruling in the book of purity: if two congregations are separated by three miles, Friday prayer may be held in each of them, whether they are in one city or two cities; but if they are less than three miles apart in a single city, only one of them holds the Friday prayer. The question asks whether the ruling still applies when the two groups are less than three miles apart but are in two cities. The answer is direct: Friday prayer should not be held in both if the distance between them is less than three miles, whether they are in one city or two.

That answer gives the chapter its first public principle. Friday prayer is not multiplied merely because people can gather in more than one place. The three-mile measure keeps the act from becoming a set of local conveniences. The earlier teaching on shared boundaries already established congregational order; here the source gives that order a spatial form.

The next question turns from distance to leadership. It cites the statement of al-Mu'ayyad, may Allah sanctify him, about the Friday sermon: whoever deliberately abandons it or treats it with contempt without valid excuse, whether the imam is just or unjust, will not be gathered by God. The answer immediately qualifies the matter. It is not permissible to pray behind the people of injustice except out of taqiyya. The source cites the Shari'ah from Mawlana al-Baqir Muhammad ibn 'Ali, may Allah's blessings be upon him, who says not to make a Nasibi or a Haruri an imam in prayer; rather, one should stand at the pillars of the mosque and recite to oneself as though praying alone.

The tension is sharp. Friday is a gathered public obligation, yet unjust leadership threatens the validity and integrity of following. The answer does not let contempt for the sermon become casual withdrawal, but neither does it make public attendance a surrender to every leader. Taqiyya becomes the narrow category by which presence behind an unjust figure may be endured without turning that figure into a rightful imam. As established earlier in the teaching on ranked leadership and following without fracture, leadership is not merely who happens to stand in front. This chapter applies that principle to the most public weekly prayer.

The source then preserves a report: al-Husayn ibn 'Ali, peace be upon him, placed the agent of Mu'awiya before him in prayer for his brother al-Hasan ibn 'Ali, peace be upon him, and said that were it not for the sunna, he would have placed him ahead. The explanation given is that this refers to the sunna of the Ahl al-Bayt of praying behind such figures in taqiyya. The source also cites the saying, "taqiyya is my religion and the religion of my fathers." The public act is therefore not flattened into one rule. There is a difference between rightful following, coerced or protective accommodation, and making a Nasibi or Haruri into one's imam.

The chapter then explains who is meant by "the unjust" in the cited saying. It refers to a preacher who has openly displayed injustice and transgression; behind such a person, according to this line, prayer becomes obligatory in the particular public circumstance. If the people observe this, they should refer the matter to the guardian and await his response. The source adds the admonition: O righteous people, investigate and do not become doubtful after certainty, for you have taken refuge in the strong fortress of God. This short line is a doorway into the entire chapter. Certainty must not be surrendered to agitation. The community must investigate, refer, and wait rather than dissolve worship into panic.

From there the chapter moves into the Friday prayer's hierarchy of places. In the summary of the Musannaf, Friday prayer behind the Imam of the Muslims is better than praying behind his deputies. If the Imam is delayed because of a matter of Muslim welfare, his deputies pray on his behalf in nearby mosques. Yet the prayer held in his mosque, the one he designated for prayer before all others, takes precedence. The order is visible: Imam, deputies, designated mosque, nearby mosques. Again, the question is not merely whether a prayer occurs. It is where the authorized center stands.

The obligatory adhan for the one bound to Friday prayer is also specified. It is the adhan given in the presence of the preacher when he sits in the place from which he delivers the sermon. Earlier the book has already taught the adhan's witness and the iqama's nearness to prayer. This chapter adds Friday's public mark: the binding summons is tied to the preacher's sitting for khutbah. The sound is not floating in the city. It is attached to pulpit, sermon, and public obligation.

Several practical cases then show how embodied and administrative the Friday prayer is. If the two officers on Friday accept the pledge of allegiance at the appointed time, their transaction is not thereby cancelled. The imam on the pulpit may drink because of thirst. If he delivers the khutbah, descends, and performs ablution, he may repeat the sermon or proceed to pray; both are rewarded. If distress interrupts the sitting between the two sermons, the ruling turns on whether that sitting had been completed. The source keeps the ceremony from becoming brittle, but not shapeless.

The minimum congregation produces another boundary. If the imam delivers the Friday sermon and then the people leave him with only one man remaining, that man prays four rak'ahs. If the imam addresses the people on Friday and prays two rak'ahs with them, then they all leave, and he remains alone to pray two rak'ahs of Friday prayer, the ruling depends on timing. If he opened the prayer and they departed before he bowed, he prays four rak'ahs of noon prayer. If the imam prays Friday prayer with the people and, after finishing the second rak'ah, rises and then sits back down, he should say the testimony and perform the two prostrations of forgetfulness.

The legal imagination here is precise because Friday prayer has a public form. When the congregation collapses before the prayer has passed a decisive point, the act becomes Dhuhr. If the error occurs after the second rak'ah, the repair belongs to the grammar of forgetfulness. The source is teaching the listener to ask: which act is this now? Has Friday remained Friday, or has it become noon prayer? Has the imam merely erred within the act, or has the act itself lost the public condition that made it Friday?

The chapter even considers the worshipper who falls asleep while waiting for the Friday prayer. If the prayer is held without him and his state of purity does not allow him to join the imam in time, he must repeat it four times. The phrasing is compressed, but the underlying pressure is recognizable. Friday is bound to presence, purity, and timely joining. Sleep does not give the worshipper a new Friday after the public act has passed. The earlier teaching on sacred times remains in the background; here the missed threshold is specifically the public weekly gathering.

The preacher's posture also carries meaning. In the book of narrations, he leans on his sword; if he does not draw it, he leans on a staff. In the interpretation of the Shari'ah, the sermon comes before the prayer, and the prayer after the sermon consists of two rak'ahs. The sermon is divided into two parts with a sitting between them, and recitation aloud distinguishes Friday prayer from the noon prayer of other days. The source also says that the sunna of respondents in the council of the da'i resembles the sunna of the Friday congregation behind the imam.

This is one of the chapter's inward turns. The Friday gathering becomes a pattern for the da'i's council. The congregation listens; respondents attend; the preacher speaks from an elevated place; the act depends on silence, direction, and hierarchy. When the preacher mounts the pulpit and greets the people, silence becomes obligatory. No one may speak or act without listening and attending to him. When the imam recites, listening is the sole obligation; turning away is not permissible. Nor is it permissible for the respondent to prompt the preacher or question him about every kind of knowledge, for the preacher knows how to raise his children.

The phrase is striking: he knows how to raise his children. The source is not imagining the congregation as an audience interrupting a lecturer. It imagines a disciplined household of instruction. The preacher is not to be prompted as though he needs correction from below. The respondents are not to turn the sermon into inquiry and cross-examination. As established earlier in the teaching on guardianship and knowledge, knowledge is not a private possession wrested from authority by impatience. Here that doctrine becomes conduct during the khutbah: silence, attention, and trust.

Our master Zayd ibn Muhammad ibn al-Fadl, may Allah honor him, gives the interpretive key in his hidden epistle. Friday prayer points to Muhammad, peace be upon him and his family, who is the gate of Paradise. It has two invitations: an outward invitation to the exoteric dimension of his Shari'ah, and an inward invitation to the esoteric interpretation of his law. Because prayer on other days consists of four rak'ahs, Friday prayer was made two rak'ahs, with the sermon replacing the other two. The sermon consists of praise, exhortation, supplication, and a statement from the Lord of Manifestation, which is warning. The sitting between the two sermons is the separation between the upper light and the lower light. The one reminded of this stands and says peace upon the two lights.

This section should be heard slowly. The khutbah is not a preliminary speech before the "real" prayer. It replaces two rak'ahs. It is part of the Friday act's architecture. The public word does what bodily prayer ordinarily does. Praise, warning, supplication, and exhortation occupy ritual space. The two sermons and their sitting become a map of light and separation. The chapter's outward rulings about distance, adhan, pulpit, silence, and congregation are therefore not administrative trivia. They preserve a form whose inward meaning depends on visible order.

The same epistle says that the muezzin before the pulpit gives the adhan after the Imam has ascended. Another report says the call is given only in the mosque to inform the public, and that whoever remains in another mosque on that day has erred; if he did so deliberately, he is penalized and commanded to perform the Friday ghusl. The chapter then preserves an admonition to know Friday's virtue until God reunites the people on Saturday.

The saying attributed to Amir al-Mu'minin adds the devotional horizon. Whoever has a need to petition God should do so in three particular hours: on Friday when the sun begins to set; when the wind blows, the gates of heaven open, and mercy descends; and in the final hour of night at daybreak. At that last hour, two angels call out: Is there anyone who repents? Is there anyone who seeks forgiveness and is forgiven? Is there anyone who asks for something? So answer the call of God. The earlier chapter on supplication taught how prayer culminates in ordered asking. This chapter locates one of those openings inside Friday's sacred time.

Finally, Friday's recitation is specified. Before the bowing of the second rak'ah, the recitation on Friday should be Surah al-Jumu'ah after the Opening in the first rak'ah, and the Opening followed by Surah al-Munafiqun in the second. The chapter ends the Friday cluster with a rare case: if a preacher is delivering the Friday sermon and a man enters through the door of the mosque, disrupting the sermon and the prayer of those praying with him, what is the ruling? The answer says that the preacher "owns" that man who entered through the mosque door; the man has become his follower, and it is not permissible for him to come before his master or pray without him.

The language of ownership should not be softened into modern informality. It marks the chapter's conviction that entry into the Friday space creates subjection to the public act already underway. To step through that door is not to remain an independent worshipper arranging one's own prayer. It is to come under the preacher's authority and the congregation's discipline. Friday's gathered order is therefore spatial, vocal, hierarchical, and inward at once.

## Eid's Revealed Interior

The next movement turns to the two Eid prayers. The first question asks about the statement in religious law that the traveler is not obligated to attend the mosque except in Egypt, and whether Tashriq here refers to the Eid prayer. It also asks what should be done if someone forgets one or more of the Eid takbirs. The answer says that Tashriq takes place only in Egypt, meaning in the mosque of a city, because the two Eid prayers are performed there at sunrise, and such a worshipper is described as radiant. If someone forgets some of the takbirs, he completes them before bowing; if he has passed the point of bowing and is certain he omitted them, he performs the two prostrations of omission.

Already the chapter has linked Eid to the chapter's final theme. A forgotten takbir is not repaired by anxiety. It is repaired according to place in the prayer. Before bowing, complete what was missed. After bowing, when the omission is certain, the prostration of omission applies. The source will later spend many pages distinguishing omission from doubt. Here it plants the distinction early: certainty matters.

The second question concerns the takbir from the day of 'Arafah to the last day of Tashriq. The number is twenty-three takbirs, yet in the questioner's country they add one takbir after the Eid prayer, making twenty-four. The answer says that the takbir in those days is twenty-three, corresponding in a manner to the five pronouncements of the first intentions aligned with the five prayers, along with mention of the spiritual ten hudud, the ten corporeal hudud, and the male of the presiding speaker and his guardian. As for what has passed, take what you have received and be grateful.

This is one of the chapter's dense symbolic moments. The twenty-three takbirs are placed in relation to intention, the five prayers, spiritual and corporeal hudud, and ordered authority. The listener does not need a full re-teaching of sacred ranks here. The point is that even a festival takbir count can carry worship's inner grammar.

Several rulings then establish Eid's practical boundaries. If people pray the Eid prayer while still owing a makeup of the Fajr prayer, the Eid prayer is valid and need not be repeated; the same applies to the funeral prayer. Whoever prays Eid before distributing Zakat al-Fitr need not repeat the prayer and may give Zakat al-Fitr afterward. The ghusl for the two Eids is a Sunnah from daybreak until the Eid prayer ends, and a valid excuse permits praying before the end of the time. The source is not careless about order; it refuses to make every neighboring duty into an invalidating condition for Eid.

The abridgment of the Musannaf repeats that the ghusl of the two Eids is an annual Sunnah, from dawn until the time when the prayer lapses. The transmitted reports preserve the qunut: O God, forgive me, have mercy on me, and pardon me in religion and in life; You are over all things Most Powerful. If people from outlying valleys come, together with travelers who choose to attend, witnessing Eid is good and meritorious for them. For inhabitants of a city within two leagues or less, witnessing the prayer is binding.

The congregation consists of five, one of whom is the Imam, as with Friday prayer, and it is obligatory in the presence of the custodians just as Friday is obligatory. Whoever misses a rak'ah and joins the second rak'ah with the Imam adds another rak'ah with the additional takbirs. The Sunnah of takbir begins after the completion of the fast and continues until the prayer ends. Whoever prays Eid raises his hands with each takbir. If he forgets some takbirs, he completes them before bowing; if he has passed bowing and is certain he omitted them, he performs the two prostrations of omission.

The repeated line about forgotten takbirs is not accidental. Eid is full of additional takbirs; its joy has a measured excess. But even that excess is ordered. The hands rise with each takbir. The takbir has a beginning and an ending. Missing one is repaired according to the stage of prayer. Celebration does not dissolve discipline. It gives discipline a festive sound.

The source then marks a special place: Eid prayer is not performed in Mina. Qadi al-Nu'man ibn Muhammad, may God sanctify his soul, in his interpretation of the Pillars as verified by Sayyiduna Idris ibn Hassan, may God be pleased with his holiness, says that Eid is not performed there in congregation but prayed individually, each person on his own. The reason is not fully unfolded here, but the ruling matters because it refuses an automatic transfer of ordinary Eid congregation into every sacred geography. Mina has its own ritual setting.

The question-and-answer book adds that sexual intercourse with one's wife is permissible on the night of Eid al-Fitr and before the Eid prayer. Ja'far ibn Mansur of Yemen, may God sanctify him, says in the Book of Suckling that it is permissible to fast on the day following Eid al-Fitr, but not permissible to fast after Eid al-Adha in the same way. These rulings locate Eid inside bodily and calendrical life. Fitr opens what fasting had closed. Adha carries its own prohibition and rhythm. The law differentiates the feasts rather than turning both into one general holiday category.

Zayd ibn Muhammad ibn al-Fadl gives the chapter's richest inward reading. Eid al-Fitr is called fitr in the language of appearance and emergence. The earth "fatarat" when it cracks and what was within it appears. A tree "fatarat" when its concealed fruit appears. A camel's tusk "fatara" when it emerges. The common thread is the appearance of what was hidden. Breaking the fast is called fitr because fasting is silence and secrecy. The source cites the story of Mary: "I have vowed to the Most Merciful a fast, so I shall not speak to anyone today" (chapter 19, verse 26), meaning that she will not reveal what she carries within her. Thus fitr signifies the appearance of interior realities and hidden knowledge.

This passage transforms the festival. Fitr is not merely eating after abstinence. It is disclosure after concealment. The fast had kept something hidden, silent, restrained. Eid is the day when what was within appears. The source also invokes the phrase "when the sky was split open" (chapter 84, verse 1), interpreting the split as a cracking through which what was within comes forth. It adds other witnesses: the new mushroom, and the "fitr of God" as the beginning of His creation of them in one day. Eid al-Fitr is therefore the day of revelation of what had been hidden.

The legal rulings now feel less like a list. The takbirs, the ghusl, the sunrise prayer, the city congregation, the distinction between Fitr and Adha, and the timing after Ramadan all point toward emergence. Whoever fasts the days of Ramadan attains al-Fitr, the feast, and divine pleasure. Whoever does not know the meaning of fasting and its proper limits gains nothing from God. The source cites the warning image of one who prays over a blazing fire, presenting it as a sign that outward exertion without inward knowledge can become loss rather than gain.

Eid al-Fitr has no adhan and no iqama. Its khutbah comes after the prayer, and the recitation is aloud. Eid al-Adha is held on the day of al-Adha, the day of the Hajj. The beginning of the sermon is a matter of dispute, and the opening of sermons itself is proof that the matter appears differently from what was hidden in the depths: every sermon begins with praise except on the day of sacrifice, which begins with four takbirs. The different opening is not treated as ornament. It signals that Adha discloses in another way.

This second concept lands on the chapter's most beautiful inversion. Friday gathered the community under a public center. Eid reveals what fasting and sacred time had concealed. The feast is not release from worship into mere festivity. It is worship at the point of emergence: hidden knowledge appears, silence breaks, takbirs rise, and the community stands in a prayer whose time and meaning have their own public brightness.

## Certainty And Omission

The final movement is the longest because correction is where prayer's form meets human fallibility. The first question asks whether someone who stands in for another and does not know all the places requiring prostration of omission must prostrate after completing every obligatory act in which something appears uncertain. The answer gives the rule: if it is established that he has indeed committed an omission, he prostrates for it; otherwise he does not. This is the governing line. Prostration of omission is not a ritual for every unease. It responds to an established omission.

A question about the opening takbir makes the stakes high. The Book of Purity says that whoever errs by not pronouncing the opening takbir, or leaves it deliberately or ignorantly, must repeat the prayer. If a man forgets to open the prayer after every two rak'ahs in four- and six-rak'ah prayers, does that invalidate his prayer? The answer says that if he did not sit after each pair of rak'ahs, his prayer is invalidated. Here the missed element is not a small repairable slip. The structure has failed.

Another question asks about inadvertent addition. The Book of Purity says that whoever adds to his prayer, even without sitting for the tashahhud, must repeat it. If he adds an extra bowing, he repeats the prayer; if he adds an extra prostration, he completes the prayer and performs the two prostrations of omission. The answer explains: if he adds a bowing and has not sat for the tashahhud, he must repeat the prayer. But if he has sat for the tashahhud and completed two rak'ahs, then whether the addition was bowing or prostration, he has done so in order.

The difference is subtle but important. Prayer is not repaired simply because the worshipper feels sorry, and it is not invalidated simply because an extra movement occurred. Bowing, prostration, tashahhud, completion of two rak'ahs, and sequence all matter. The source is protecting the structure of prayer from both laxity and overreaction. A real invalidation must be repeated. A repairable omission receives its repair. An addition after a completed order is judged differently from an addition before that order is secured.

The role of the Imam then appears. If a man praying behind an Imam neglects one of the pillars of prayer while following, how is his prayer? The answer says that the Imam bears responsibility for the omission, except in the case of bowing and the opening takbir, and the first thing is that he should not neglect those. This applies earlier teachings on following without reopening them. The follower is carried by the Imam in many omissions, but not in the foundational acts that make his own participation real.

Several cases clarify timing. If someone forgets a prostration, he prostrates if he remembers before delivering the salaam; if he has already left the prayer, he has no recourse. If a man recites the obligatory prayer in reverse order, he does not have to perform the prostrations of omission. If someone realizes after the salaam that he prayed only three rak'ahs of Dhuhr, he prays the missed rak'ah, recites tashahhud, gives salaam, and performs the two prostrations of omission. Correction is not one thing: sometimes the time has passed, sometimes no repair is required, and sometimes a missing unit is completed before sujud al-sahw.

Some omissions have no explicit stipulation. If a worshipper neglects the first tashahhud but recites the final tashahhud and remembers before the salaam, the answer says no explicit text states that he must prostrate. If a man repeats a single surah in the obligatory prayer after the Opening, that too has not been stipulated. If a man omits one obligation, or three obligations, the prostration of omission is not multiplied; two prostrations cover the omissions. The source is again limiting anxiety. More omissions do not necessarily mean repeated repair rites. Lack of stipulation is not filled by devotional nervousness.

Intention receives special attention. If a man praying behind an Imam makes it clear that he intends to be a follower, but the Imam gives the salaam of the dhikr while not intending the prayer, is the prayer valid? The answer says that if the Imam intends the prayer on his behalf, then it is so; if not, the prayer lapses. Another question invokes the statement that deeds are accepted only with intentions. If a person neglects to form the intention when purifying himself, beginning prayer, fasting, or performing any duty of God, and then contracts the intention verbally at the moment he resolves in his heart to act, is his action valid? The answer says that intention resides in the heart, and what is said with the tongue is merely an expression. If he has made the intention in his heart, he has fulfilled it.

This is a crucial correction to a possible misunderstanding. The source is exacting about outward acts, but it does not make verbal declaration the essence of intention. The heart contracts the intention. The tongue may express it, but the tongue is not the place where intention lives. Later, in questions to scholars of India and letters to the preachers of Yemen, the source repeats the point: if one omits the tongue-utterance while striving to hold the intention in the heart, the action is valid. Yet it also records a strict answer: if the imam deliberately expresses his intention verbally, his prayer is void; if he does not say it deliberately, it stands. The deliberate public utterance by the leader is treated differently from the heart's inward resolve.

The chapter then distinguishes omission from doubt. In the councils of instruction, doubt in prayer is said to be rooted in the prostration of omission because it causes bewilderment, and many agreed cases seem to resemble omission though they do not. The one who commits an omission is certain of what he omitted; the doubter is not certain of what he doubts. This is the hinge of the entire final section. The prostration of omission belongs to a known lack, not every clouded thought.

The transmitted reports say that the prostration of omission is performed after the salaam. After it, one recites a brief testimony: In the name of Allah, and Allah is great, and I bear witness that there is no god but Allah, and may Allah bless Muhammad and his family. A person who experiences doubt during prayer has not thereby omitted any obligatory act, whether leading prayer or praying supererogatory prayer. If he has already stood up for what was written and then realizes he has been excused and believes it to be voluntary, he treats it as voluntary. Negligence does not harm him in that case; if he rises for a voluntary prayer, it counts as voluntary.

Time-bound doubts are handled with the same concern for certainty. If enough of 'Asr remains, he acts within that time; if the sun has set and only then doubt arises, there is no need to make it up. Doubt about Fajr or Maghrib is answered by praying them. Repeating the same prayer without cause does not require sujud al-sahw. The source trains the worshipper to act on what time, certainty, and duty make available.

The Mukhtasar al-Musannaf gives the memorable case of a fifth rak'ah. If a man prays and then rises at the fifth position, having already witnessed the fourth, he should give salaam while standing and depart. If he did not witness it, he should sit, give the testimony, and perform the two prostrations of omission. If he remembered that he rose to a fifth rak'ah after bowing, having witnessed the fourth, his prayer is sound and the additional rak'ah becomes voluntary. But if he did not witness the fourth and did not sit the length of the testimony, he repeats the prayer.

This example condenses the chapter's whole discipline. The same outward fact, a fifth rak'ah, can carry different rulings depending on whether the fourth was secured, whether the testimony-length sitting occurred, and when memory returned. One cannot judge from the surface alone.

The source then turns to uncertain missed prayers and recitation. If someone does not know whether he missed two, three, or four prayers, he follows the imam in praying aloud what is prayed silently, and his prayer is complete. If he does so deliberately, he must perform sujud al-sahw, but if he does it from uncertainty, nothing is obligatory upon him. The same law applies in settled prayer and travel. A question from scholars of al-Hind and preachers of Yemen asks about neglecting obligatory recitation after the first rak'ah and reciting a surah from the beginning in the second; the answer says this is neither stipulated nor deliberate.

Then the Mustansiriyya council gives the formal map. The places of omission in prayer are seven, and for six of them the prostration of omission is appropriate. These two prostrations are named because they force the devil's nose down when he causes forgetfulness. First, whoever neglects to prostrate and remembers it performs the two prostrations of omission. Second, whoever forgets to sit for the first tashahhud and stands for the third rak'ah: if he has not yet knelt, he sits, gives the testimony, and when he gives salaam, he performs the prostrations of omission. Third, even if the omission exceeds the first tashahhud and accumulates, he performs the prostration, completes the prayer in the most complete manner, and the two prostrations are upon him. Fourth, whoever neglects the second tashahhud before the salutation must perform the two prostrations. Fifth, whoever inattentively prays only two rak'ahs and then prays two more after them must perform the two prostrations. Sixth, whoever inattentively rises from the final tashahhud after completing it to add a fifth rak'ah must perform the two prostrations. Seventh, whoever follows an imam bears no responsibility for omission; the Imam bears that.

The list is both practical and theological. Forgetfulness is not treated as moral rebellion, but neither is it dismissed. It is repaired by an act that humiliates the cause of forgetfulness. The devil's nose is forced down; the worshipper returns to order. The repair is small, embodied, and decisive. It does not reopen the whole prayer unless the structure itself has failed.

As for doubt, the council says there are twelve situations. Some require prostration of omission, and some require nothing. If the worshipper doubts whether he has bowed while still in prayer, he bows, and the prostration of omission is upon him. If he doubts whether he has prayed one rak'ah or two, he acts on the lesser certainty. If he doubts whether he has prayed two or three, he acts on certainty. If he doubts whether he has prayed three or four, he sits and performs the two prostrations. If he doubts whether he has prayed two or four, he gives salaam, then prays two rak'ahs standing, and performs the two prostrations.

But if he doubts whether he made the opening takbir after already bowing, he continues and nothing is required. If he doubts whether he recited after bowing, nothing is required. If he doubts whether he bowed after prostrating, nothing is required. If he doubts about the first prostration after the second, nothing is required. If he doubts about prostrating after the tashahhud, nothing is required. If he doubts about something after the salutation, nothing is required. The line attributed to Amir al-Mu'minin gives the principle: whoever is upon certainty and is then visited by doubt should proceed upon his certainty, for doubt does not displace certainty or diminish it.

The final question repeats the governing rule: whoever doubts in his prayer and does not know what he has prayed should act on certainty and perform the two prostrations of omission after the salutation. If he is certain of nothing and does not know how many rak'ahs he has missed, he restarts from the beginning, and the same applies to the imam. The source does not want carelessness, but it also does not want worship ruled by formless anxiety. A real omission has a repair, structural invalidation has repetition, intention lives in the heart, and certainty is not displaced by doubt.

## What this episode lands

This chapter gathers Friday, Eid, and prayer correction because all three are forms of disciplined worship under pressure. Friday asks whether the community is gathered around the right public center. Eid asks whether celebration reveals the hidden meaning of fasting, takbir, and sacred emergence. Omission asks whether the worshipper can distinguish an established defect from a passing uncertainty.

The forward motion from earlier chapters is important. The book has already taught prayer's summons, shape, leadership, time, attention, and material boundaries. This chapter tests those teachings at their difficult edges: the public sermon, the second congregation, the unjust preacher, the Eid takbir, the city obligation, the missed tashahhud, the doubtful rak'ah, the unspoken intention, and the late realization. In every case, the answer is measure. Worship is neither private improvisation nor frightened repetition. It is ordered action held by authority, meaning, and certainty.
