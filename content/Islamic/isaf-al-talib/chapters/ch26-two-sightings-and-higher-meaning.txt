## Where this episode picks up

What if the moment that makes a prayer obligatory is a moment no human being has ever actually watched happen? Not because it is hidden in the way a secret is hidden, but because it is the one instant in the sun's whole day when nothing visible changes — the shadow stops shortening, starts lengthening, and for a while around the turn it does neither in any amount an eye can register. What this chapter's letter maintains is that the turn happens regardless, that it is the true trigger of the noon prayer, and that the community has been timing that prayer by knowledge rather than by eyesight every single day without noticing that this is what it was doing.

This chapter covers the finish of the argument the previous chapter left standing mid-proof, and then a turn outward that is larger than anything the crescent dispute has so far required. The previous chapter ended by pointing at a geometrical figure to settle the question of the sun's decline — and the figure did not survive. This chapter opens in the wreckage of it, with a scatter of labels that reach us as broken words, and then proceeds without it: the sun's motion near the top of its arc, why the senses lose track of it there, and the crossing point that obliges the prayer, which the letter locates at the halfway mark of the arc rather than at the later moment when a person can finally see that the sun is coming down. With that, the letter declares the opposing position invalidated.

Then it faces the strongest objection anyone has brought against it, and the objection is not a philosophical one — it is a hadith. The Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) said that if the sky is obscured, you count thirty days. That instruction seems to assume eyes, clouds, and a fallback for nights when the eyes are defeated. If knowledge settles the month, why would there be a fallback at all? The answer has two limbs. The first builds a structure of rank: two modes of vision, both genuine, one superior, one belonging to the select who are advanced in knowledge and one to the general public who are bound to follow them — and behind that, the claim that everything in existence has an apparent side and a hidden side, and that both sides carry obligations. The second limb turns on the literal reading and takes it apart from the inside: a fallback that produces a different month in one town than in the next, a command to count that requires you to already know the day you are counting from, and a rule that would have the Prophet ordering his community to fast on a day that would otherwise be forbidden to them.

And then, in its last movement, the letter stops talking about the moon altogether. Having established that the sighting instruction was never about eyesight, it takes one step outward and makes a claim about every pillar of the religion at once. Prayer, fasting, the alms, the pilgrimage, and the struggle in the path of Allah are not five private assignments handed to each believer to discharge by his own judgement and his own senses. They are deposited with a community, ordered under a leader standing in the Prophet's place, and the individual's part in each of them is participation — which means, in every single case, that he may not run ahead and may not lag behind. Two of those five pillars have already had their own chapters in this series; two of them have not appeared until now; and the principle that gathers all five was never stated until this page.

A word on what kind of document this remains. It is one voice arguing, with an opponent in the room who is quoted but never named, who gets his objection stated fairly and at length before it is answered. He is not a straw man here. His hadith is real, it is well known, and on its face it says what he says it says.

## The decline no eye catches

Before the argument resumes, the wreckage. What has come down to us at this point in the manuscript is not a sentence but a spill of labels, the kind that sit around the edge of a drawing: a phrase about something being on this line, a single bare letter, a fragment about something coming out, a fragment about disappearance, a broken clause with the words for altitude and arc in it, the word for the east, a fragment reading only *the land that*, a stray feminine pronoun, the word for the centre, and — the label the previous chapter already noted — a westward direction that has reached us as the name of a country rather than as a direction of the compass. Nothing can honestly be reconstructed from that. They are rendered here as they stand and named as damaged, which is the convention this book has followed every time the manuscript's condition has intruded, and the reason is simple: a repaired label is an invented one, and an invented label in a proof about the limits of human certainty would be a peculiar thing to hand a reader.

What survives whole is the reasoning the drawing was meant to illustrate, and it survives well enough to follow.

Start with what the senses can do, because the letter is careful to grant it. The senses do perceive the sun's movement, and they perceive it through the shadow. When the sun rises in the east and climbs, shadows shorten, and the shortening is visible; when it descends toward the west, shadows lengthen, and that is visible too. This is within reach of the senses precisely because of the sun's elevation along its arc — the rising and the falling are what make the shadow's change large enough to see. Nobody needs an instrument to know that the morning is passing. A stick in the ground reports it.

Now take the sun to the top. As it approaches the highest point of its daily arc, the shadow's rate of change collapses. The decrease and increase that were obvious an hour earlier become imperceptible, and the senses, as the letter puts it, grow weary of perceiving the sun's course and movement. The sun has reached the summit point of its passage, its motion becomes hidden from the senses and absent from them, and a person who does not know what is happening might well suppose that the sun has stopped.

It has not stopped. This is the pivot of the whole passage, and the letter states the reason geometrically rather than rhetorically. The sun at the top of its arc is at its greatest remove from the position it rose over; it has climbed above that position; and its movement there has passed into the part of its circuit that runs along a flat line tangent to the curve. Motion along that flat stretch is still motion. What it is not is motion that changes the shadow by an amount an eye can catch. The eye's report at the top of the day — *nothing is happening* — is not a report about the sun. It is a report about the eye.

The geometry behind that, as best it can be recovered without the figure: a straight line runs across the circle of the sun's daily path, touching it and extending from the position where the two intersect, east to west. The senses perceive the shadow's decrease or increase only through the sun's rising and falling, and along that flat stretch there is neither rise nor fall to speak of.

And so the conclusion, which the letter states as the settled result of everything the previous chapter built: the true disappearance that makes the prayer obligatory occurs when the sun's disk crosses the first line — the line perceived by the vision of the soul — which marks the halfway point of its daily arc. It does not occur at the second line, the one perceived by natural vision, which corresponds to more than half of that arc. Reaching the first line involves descent even when the senses can detect no movement in the shadow and no displacement of the sun at all, because the sun there is travelling along the flat stretch, and its passage only becomes apparent to the senses after it has crossed the intersection of the circle and the tangent. The same reasoning applies to the sun's rays as to seeing the sun itself: by the time the rays report the change, the shadow has had to come into existence, and the crossing that produced it is already behind us.

Sit with what that means practically, because it is the least exotic claim in the book and the most quietly devastating one. The noon prayer is obligatory from the decline of the sun. The decline, on this account, is a geometrical crossing at the exact midpoint of the arc. No one has ever seen that crossing. What a person sees is a shadow that has already begun to lengthen — which is to say, a sun that crossed some time ago and has now travelled far enough past the crossing for the eye to notice. So the person who insists on waiting for visual confirmation before he prays is, on his own principle, praying late every day of his life. And the person who prays on time is timing his prayer by something other than his eyes. He may be using a marked stick, or a table, or the muezzin's call, or the habit of a lifetime, but whatever he is using, its authority does not come from having watched the sun turn, because nobody watches the sun turn.

There is a precision in the letter's wording that is easy to skim past and worth stopping on. It does not say that the eye's line comes a little after the soul's line. It says the soul's line marks the halfway point of the daily arc, and that the eye's line corresponds to *more than half* of that arc. Those are two different kinds of statement. The first is a definition — half is half, and it does not vary with the observer, the season, the latitude, or the clarity of the air. The second is a description of a delay whose size nobody has fixed, because it depends on how sharp a particular pair of eyes is, how long a shadow the marker casts, how steady the ground is, and how much change a person needs to see before he is willing to call it change. One line is a fact about the sky. The other is a fact about the watcher, wearing the costume of a fact about the sky. The letter's charge against the opposing view is exactly that it has mistaken the second for the first — and this is the same charge, transposed from the moon to the sun, that the previous chapter laid against the party who would not let a month begin until somebody's eye had caught the crescent.

There is a sentence here that the manuscript has compressed or damaged, and it is left as it stands rather than smoothed: the worshipper is in his prayer as the crescent is inhabited and perceived by the senses, and the fasting person is in his fast, both being cautious in fulfilling their obligations. The intent is legible even where the wording is not. The man praying and the man fasting are in the same position with respect to the thing that obliges them: each is discharging a duty whose trigger is a fact about position in the sky, each has that fact on the authority of knowledge rather than a witnessed instant, and each is being careful — which is the letter's point about why the careful person is not the one squinting at the horizon.

*With these testimonies,* the letter says, *the opposing view is invalidated.*

It is worth registering how the ground has shifted under the opponent for him to lose here. The noon prayer was his example. He raised it as a case where the law plainly waits for something visible, where no one prays before the sun has visibly declined, and where knowledge of the abstract geometry is beside the point. The letter has taken that case, agreed that no one prays early, and then argued that the reason no one prays early has nothing to do with sight: the moment of obligation is the crossing, the crossing is invisible, and the visible lengthening of a shadow is merely the delayed announcement of an event that has already occurred. The opponent's own daily practice, five times over, turns out to be the clearest instance of a duty timed by knowledge — and this is a callback to the division of the two visions set out in the previous chapter, not a re-argument of it. The senior instrument is the one that does not err, and the argument has now shown it working in the most ordinary case there is.

## Obscured skies and two ranks

Here the opponent gets his best shot, and it is a good one.

*If the matter is as you have described,* he says, *and fasting is obligatory on the basis of the soul's sighting as you maintain, then what is the meaning of the Prophet's words (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him): "If you are obscured, then count thirty days"?*

The force of that question is easy to feel. The saying presupposes a sky you are trying to look at, a sky that can be blocked, and a remedy for the nights when looking fails. Why legislate a remedy for the failure of an instrument that was never the instrument? A doctrine that says the month is settled by knowledge should have no use for clouds at all.

The reply begins by refusing the frame. *That is not an objection against psychic vision.* The soul's sighting has been established by the evidence already presented, and it is more honourable and more appropriate to apply it in the fulfilment of obligatory duties. And then the sentence that does the real work: *we therefore did not restrict the ruling to naked-eye vision alone, because both modes of vision are foundational in performing obligatory duties, and one is superior to the other.*

Notice what has just been conceded and what has not. The letter does not say that eyesight is irrelevant to the law. It says eyesight is a foundation — a real one, sufficient to discharge the duty — and that it is the junior of two foundations. The thirty-day instruction is not a rival doctrine that must be explained away. It is provision made for the junior foundation.

What follows is the structure that makes that coherent, and it is the doctrinal heart of this chapter. The superior mode of vision belongs to the select, who are advanced in knowledge and are the first to understand. The other belongs to the general public, who are obliged to follow the people of knowledge and who lack the insight to distinguish between the two modes of vision. That last clause repays attention, because it is not an insult. The public are not described as wrong, or lazy, or disobedient. They are described as unable to tell the two visions apart — and if you cannot tell them apart, you cannot be given a rule that requires you to choose between them. So you are given a rule that works without the distinction: look, and if you cannot see, count thirty.

The relationship between the two modes, the letter says, is like the relationship between two prayer times, the earlier of which is superior to the later. That comparison is doing a great deal in a small space, and it is the most generous thing in the chapter. Everyone knows that a prayer performed in the second of its permitted times is a valid prayer. The man who prays at the later time has not sinned, has not incurred a debt, and does not repeat. He has discharged the obligation in the lower of two acceptable ways. Transfer that onto the month: the man who waits for the sky to clear and then counts is not a man in error. He is a man praying at the later time. What the letter is denying is not the validity of his practice but the claim that his practice is the only one the law recognises — and, more pointedly, the claim that his instrument is the one that defines when the obligation begins.

Then the generalisation, stated flatly and without hedging: this is true of all things, which have hidden and apparent dimensions. The hidden is known only by those of virtue; the apparent is known by the select and the general public alike. Two consequences follow immediately, and the second is the one people forget. The select are obliged to act upon what they have learned. And the common people are obliged to act upon what they have come to know. Nobody in this scheme is excused from action, and nobody is given knowledge that carries no duty. Rank here is not privilege; it is the measure of what you are answerable for. This is the epistemology the earlier chapters on guardianship and knowledge assumed without setting out, and it is set out here in two sentences.

From which, the letter says, no one can deny that a scholar is better placed to know when fasting becomes obligatory through the hidden sighting of the soul, and that the ignorant must follow the scholar in this.

And then the analogy that makes the whole position feel suddenly obvious. When an astronomer, through his hidden knowledge, knows that dawn has arrived — and therefore that eating, drinking, and marital relations are now forbidden, and that fasting has begun — the person who is ignorant of dawn's timing must follow his guidance. Nobody objects to this. The community has always accepted that one end of the fasting day is settled by a person who knows rather than by every individual's own inspection of the horizon; the earlier chapters of this book have already granted that a man bound at dawn is bound whether or not he can see the sky. The letter simply asks that the same courtesy be extended at the other end of the scale. If a knowledgeable man's calculation can start your fast this morning, why can a knowledgeable man's calculation not start your month?

That is the first limb of the answer. The second limb goes after the literal reading directly, and it is not gentle.

Take the saying to mean naked-eye sighting, and the trouble begins at once. Fasting by sight depends on obstacles clearing away, and those obstacles can leave a month standing at twenty-nine days rather than thirty. So the rule produces different results depending on the weather. If that is what the Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) intended, then he left his community holding one of Allah's commands under a rule that may generate doubt — a rule that can add a day to an obligation or take one away, leaving the community to perform a divine duty haphazardly. And worse: he would have been commanding his nation to fast an additional day in a month that had in fact fallen short, when but for that command it would have been known that fasting on that day is forbidden and breaking the fast is required. That is the sharpest point in the passage. On the literal reading, the fallback does not merely risk imprecision; it can order a believer to fast on a day the law forbids him to fast.

Against which the letter sets the character of the act itself. Fasting is worship of Allah Almighty and one of the religious duties in which Allah commands His servants to be sincere: *And they were not commanded except to worship Allah, sincere to Him in religion* (chapter 98, verse 5). Sincerity stands apart from doubt and suspicion, and doubt and suspicion are dismissed by that principle. Worship performed in uncertainty about whether it is even owed is not the thing the verse is describing.

So the Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) did not expose people to doubt and suspicion in their worship. And since he did not call for doubt, the days of the month stand at their fullest and most complete count, without deficiency or reduction. The permissibility of an incomplete month under naked-eye sighting, along with the doubt attached to it, was only ever meant to point toward the sighting of the soul, whose count neither increases nor decreases. It was an exhortation toward what Allah Almighty called for in completing the prescribed period: *and complete the number* (chapter 2, verse 185).

Then the argument comes down out of principle and into geography, and this is where it bites hardest. Where does the obligation actually lie for people whose fast falls under an overcast sky? A man in one place is counting a particular day while people of his own school elsewhere, following the same rule of sight and contending with their own weather, are counting a different one. In Kirman, where overcast skies prevail, the fast differs from the fast of Shiraz, where the sky may be clear. The divergence is not theoretical and it is not small: fasting becomes obligatory for some people on one day and for other people on the next. Run the same logic on the prayer and it is worse still — a prayer obligatory for one person at one time and for another person at a different time. That cannot be permissible. A prayer time comes in which the whole community is obliged to establish the prayer together, and whoever delays it and abandons it has neglected an obligation. The same holds for the fast: when the time comes for the community to fast, the one who fasts and the one who breaks are both bound by that time.

And finally the instruction to count is examined on its own terms. Error can fall in naked-eye sighting; error does not fall in the sighting of the soul, which is knowledge. The Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) said to count the days of Sha'ban, with the end of the fast determined by counting the days of Ramadan. But counting requires two things: someone to count, and knowledge of the first day of the month. And in most months the crescent cannot be sighted at the outset at all — particularly where the air is turbid, in Tabaristan, in the mountain regions, along the coasts, in the islands of the sea. If the crescent is invisible at the beginning of Sha'ban because of some impediment, a person has no way of knowing how to prepare for Sha'ban, let alone for a Ramadan whose beginning has never been established. The fallback needs the very thing it exists to replace.

To read the report that way, then, is to say that the Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) and his family led the community into perplexity and called them to what they are unable to do. Whoever counts the days of a month without knowing its beginning and without having seen the crescent cannot fulfil the duty at all. And Allah Almighty says: *Allah does not burden a soul beyond what it can bear* (chapter 2, verse 286).

Which leaves a fork with only two prongs, since attributing incapacity-imposing legislation to the Prophet is inconceivable — he is far above burdening the servants of Allah with what they cannot do, or casting them into dispute. Either the report is not correct, or he and his family said it in a sense that is sound, that settles the matter properly and without ambiguity for him and for everyone else. Take the second, as the letter does, and the testimonies confirm what the whole argument has been driving at: what creation has been entrusted to is the sighting of the rational soul.

## The pillars held in trust

That word — *entrusted* — is where the chapter turns, and the turn is abrupt enough that it is easy to read past.

Up to this point the subject has been the moon: how a month begins, what an eye can and cannot report, which faculty times a duty. Now the letter says: the discussion has established that naked-eye sighting is not what was intended, and that the general public who possess knowledge must follow accordingly, and the opposing view on this has been invalidated. And then, without a paragraph of preparation, it makes a claim that covers the entire religion. Any pillar of Islam brought by Muhammad (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) and his family — whether prayer, fasting, zakat, Hajj, or jihad — is entrusted to the infallible community that is safeguarded from error.

Two phrases in that sentence are carrying the weight, and neither is decorative. The first is *any pillar* — the letter is not arguing from the fast outward by analogy, cautiously, one step at a time; it states the rule at full generality first and then supplies the instances. The second is the description of the body the pillars are lodged with: a community that is safeguarded from error. That is what licenses the whole arrangement. A duty can only be deposited with a holder reliable enough to keep it, and the argument of the last three chapters has been precisely that the instrument which errs cannot be the instrument that times an obligation. The same test is now applied to the holder rather than to the faculty, and it yields the same kind of answer.

Read that as a claim about ownership. An obligation, on this account, is not issued to each believer as a private commission to be discharged by his own judgement, his own calculation, and his own senses, with the community as a convenient setting in which he happens to perform it. It is deposited with a body. What the individual does is take part in that body's act. And the letter proceeds to walk the five pillars one at a time to show that this is not a novel doctrine but a description of what every one of them already looks like.

Prayer first, and briefly, because this series has already given the congregational prayer and the ranks behind the leader their own chapters. The acts are named — the opening magnification, the recitation, the bowing, the prostration, and submission to the Imam who stands in the place of the Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) in leading the people — and the rule attached to them is stated with unusual precision: the believers follow the Imam in these acts without introducing or delaying any of them on their own initiative. Whoever introduces or delays an act in his prayer has deviated.

Look at what the fault is there. It is not doing the wrong thing. The man who bows early is bowing; the man who rises late is rising; every action he performs is an action the prayer requires. His fault is entirely in the timing — the right act at the wrong moment relative to the person he is following. And that is the bridge the letter has been building toward for three chapters without saying so. The entire crescent dispute has been an argument about timing. Here, at the level of the daily prayer, timing is precisely the thing the follower surrenders. He does not keep a private schedule and synchronise it with the congregation as a courtesy. He takes his moments from the leader.

Zakat next, also briefly, since the chapters on the alms have already set out who collects and how. The alms are collected by the standing Imam in the place of the Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him). He may not distribute what he receives from those who respond to him without the community's involvement, as was the case in the Prophet's own days. And whoever gave his alms to an authority other than the one holding the Prophet's office would have been required to render it again, properly — exactly as a man who paid his alms to the wrong hand during the Prophet's lifetime would have had to pay it again to him in the appropriate manner.

The point worth extracting is the one the earlier chapters did not make: the payment is not simply a transfer of wealth from a person who has too much to a person who has too little. If it were, a correct sum delivered to a needy recipient by any route would settle the account, and the identity of the collector would be an administrative detail. On this reading the duty is an act of attachment to an office, and the sum is the occasion of the attachment rather than the substance of it. The right amount into the wrong hand leaves the obligation exactly where it was.

Then the pilgrimage, which this series has not treated before, and which gives the principle its most uncompromising statement. The Hajj is performed only under an Imam who leads the group, so that they follow him in the rites. Whoever performs a rite ahead of the others or behind them disrupts the order — and an individual Hajj is not valid. There is no softer way to put that. A man may travel to the sacred house, complete every rite in the correct manner, omit nothing, and if he has done it as an individual rather than as part of an ordered body under its leader, the letter says he has not performed the pilgrimage. And once again the failure mode is named as a timing failure: ahead, or behind.

And then the struggle in the path of Allah, also new to this series. Whoever takes charge of the Muslims must preserve their order and all their affairs — advancing them in the fighting, withdrawing them from it, pursuing their interests in truces and in battle alike. The community that fights must follow the command of Allah rather than following their own desires, in peace as in war. And whoever fights without his command and sheds blood without his knowledge is — here the manuscript hands us a phrase that has not survived cleanly, reaching us as a man on a leash, which is rendered as it stands and named as damaged rather than repaired into something tidier. The sense that can be held with confidence is that such a man is acting outside the order and is condemned for it; the exact image the letter chose for him is lost.

Set the five side by side and the same shape appears in each: a leader standing in the Prophet's place, a community ordered around him, and an individual who is forbidden to run ahead and forbidden to lag behind. Prayer, alms, pilgrimage, and fighting are all described here in terms of position relative to a leader rather than in terms of correct private performance — and the fast, the pillar this whole letter has been arguing about, is being placed in that same list.

Which makes the crescent argument read differently in retrospect, and this is the last move the chapter makes. The objection to naked-eye sighting was never really that eyes are weak. The letter has argued that too, at length, in the previous chapter; but weakness alone would only mean eyes need help. The deeper problem is that eyes are *private*. Each person's sky belongs to him. The cloud over Kirman is not over Shiraz. An instrument that returns a different answer to every holder cannot time a duty that was deposited with a community and that the community is required to perform together — any more than a congregation could pray behind a leader while each member kept his own count of the bowings. Knowledge does not have that defect. What the scholar knows about the moon's position is the same in Kirman as in Shiraz, on a clear night and under cloud, and it is the same for the man who worked it out and the man who accepted it from him. A shared obligation needs a shared trigger, and only one of the two visions can supply one.

## What this episode lands

The chapter began in a ruin and ended in a doctrine.

The ruin was the lost figure, of which a handful of broken labels survive and nothing else. The argument it was meant to prove survives intact: near the top of its arc the sun's motion produces no shadow change an eye can register, so the senses fall silent and a person who does not know might think the sun has halted. It has not. It is crossing. The letter puts the obligation of the prayer at that crossing — the midpoint of the arc, available to the soul — and not at the later moment when the lengthening shadow finally announces what already happened. Which means the noon prayer, the most routine obligation in the religion, performed by everyone, has always been timed by knowledge. Nobody has ever seen the sun turn.

The objection was the strongest one available: a hadith that seems to legislate for failed eyesight. The answer did not deny the hadith. It ranked it. Both visions are real foundations for discharging a duty; one is superior; the superior belongs to those advanced in knowledge, and the other to the general public, who cannot tell the two apart and are therefore given a rule that does not require them to. Everything has an apparent side and a hidden side, and both sides bind: the learned must act on what they have learned, and everyone else must act on what they have come to know. Nobody is idle in this scheme.

And then the literal reading was dismantled on its own terms. It makes a divine command depend on the weather. It can order a fast on a day that would otherwise be forbidden. It starts Ramadan in one town and not the next, which the prayer would never tolerate for a moment. It tells you to count from a first day it has no way of giving you, in places where the air is never clear enough to see one. Read that way, the instruction asks of people what they cannot do — and the Book says no soul is charged beyond its capacity. So either the report is unsound, or it means something that leaves no one in doubt. The letter takes the second, and finds the whole case confirmed: what creation is entrusted to is the sighting of the soul.

Then the step nobody was braced for. If that word is *entrusted*, ask who holds the trust — and the answer covers everything. Every pillar is deposited with the community under the leader standing in the Prophet's place. The alms paid to the wrong office are not paid. The pilgrimage performed alone is not performed. The fighter who draws blood outside the order is condemned. And in the prayer, where the acts themselves are all correct, the man who moves early has still deviated, because what he owed was not the bowing but the bowing *with*.

That is why the eye could never have been the instrument. Not because it is weak. Because it is yours alone, and the duty is not.
