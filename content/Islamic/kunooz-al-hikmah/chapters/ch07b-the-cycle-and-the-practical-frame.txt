## Where this teaching opens

Is the sin that is not forgiven the gross idolatry of the ancients with their stone images, or is it something far more intimate — an inner inclination that grants, in the secret of the heart, a small portion of devotion to one whose due that portion is not? And if the latter, then how many of the believer's daily acts have, in their thin edges, the shape of the same wrong? The author's appendix has carried the reader through his own posture, the higher-rank measure of sin, the discipline of concealment, the closed door of speculation about origination, and the doctrine of the Imam's soul flowing through the chain. The teaching now turns to the subtle daily forms of the one wrong that is not forgiven, and from there carries the believer up through the doctrines that locate him in his cosmos.

This second movement of the appendix begins with the unforgiven wrong and its subtle forms, the doctrine of the believer at the summit of beings, and the eternal number twenty-eight that organizes the higher world. It moves to the four-phrase litany matched to the four layers of the soul, the two stations of the Dai, and the doctrine of visitation. It then distinguishes the two Mehdis, traces the great cycle to its term in the absorption into the First Emanator, and arrives at the practical frame — charity, salt, the rule about the foundational gift, the call to prayer, the straight path as the believer's own chain — by which the cosmology is lived. The book itself closes there, on the same prayer for the present Dai and the line.

## The unforgiven wrong, the summit of the believer, and the twenty-eight

The verse the believer carries is famous. In the thirty-ninth chapter of the Quran, the believer hears the divine address: "O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins" (Quran 39:53, Saheeh International translation). The believer who has been weighed down by his own transgressions returns to that verse for reassurance. The verse stands. But it is paired in the doctrine with a strict exception, set out in the fourth chapter: "Indeed, Allah does not forgive associating others with Him, but He forgives what is less than that for whom He wills" (Quran 4:48, Saheeh International translation). The one wrong that is not forgiven is the joining of something to the divine that is not the divine, and it is not forgiven until it is given up. The mercy is wide; the exception is narrow; and the narrow exception is, in the inner reading, the one the believer has most to learn to recognize in his own life.

The doctrine of those who are slowly moving toward faith without yet being inside the circle is, the lecture noted, the doctrine of the gradually ascending. They love the family of the House. They follow the outward law. They observe what is permitted and what is forbidden. They are noble people, the teacher said, whether their outward religion bears the name of Islam or another name. They ascend, gradually. One of the great masters of the line set this out in his treatise on the gradual ascents. But the disbeliever who insists on associating others with the divine does not ascend. He waits. Allah's clemency for his case is not engaged until he gives up the association. Why? Because the wrong is itself an active opposition to the order of being. It is not the absence of faith; it is the joining of something else to the divine, which is structurally incompatible with the divine. Until the association is dropped, no entry is possible. The wrong is a great wrong in the literal sense of placing-out-of-place — placing what is not the divine in the position of the divine.

And here the lecture pressed the believer to recognize the subtle forms. The verse warning that one's works are nullified by association — "If you should associate anything with Allah, your work would surely become worthless, and you would surely be among the losers" (Quran 39:65, Saheeh International translation) — is not, in the inner doctrine, addressed only to the idol-worshipper. It is the association of including anyone in the authority of the Father of Imams by even an iota. So the wrong, in this register, can be very subtle. It can be an inclination, an inner deflection, a granting of a small portion of devotion to one whose due that portion is not. The believer's daily prayer is, in part, a daily clearing of even these small deflections. To honor the rank-holders is to be in their chain. To divide one's honor among the rank-holders and others is, in the inner reckoning, a small association of the same kind. The discipline of the line is also a discipline against the diluted attention that would otherwise creep in.

From this severity the doctrine opens, almost paradoxically, into its great elevation. The believer, the appendix returns more than once, is the term of perfection of the human order. For other orders of being, the term of perfection can be named. The stones have their term: the ruby, with its compressed clarity. The animals have their term: the elephant, with its intelligence that approaches the human. The earth's growths have their term: the various fruits and grains in their full bloom. But for the human, the term of perfection is not a physical perfection. It is a state of being: the believer. To be born human and not become a believer is to fail of the term. To be born human and become a believer is to attain it. This doctrine elevates the believer enormously. The believer is not a small person in the world. The believer is the term of the world, in the human sphere.

This is also why the verse that Allah is powerful over every thing, in the inner reading, has the believer as its thing. Allah is powerful over every believer. This is no rhetorical flourish. It is a statement of how the believer is positioned in the divine attention. The believer is the thing Allah's power especially attends to. The believer is the thing Allah's knowledge especially encompasses. The believer is the thing Allah's reach especially holds. When one of the great masters of the line wrote of another master as the most excellent of existing things, the puzzlement of his readers was the puzzlement of those who had not yet absorbed this doctrine. Most excellent of existing things — surely this is the Imam? Surely this is the Messenger? The teacher answered: in this particular phrasing, the most excellent of existing things means the most excellent among the believers present in the call to faith. The believer is again at the summit of the meaning of existing thing. And of the believers, the one who occupies a particular elevated station is most excellent in this sense. The great Dai of one generation lived this doctrine. He stood up to receive the believer. The believer, after all, is his companion in the household of the noble. The believer takes his portion; the Dai takes his portion; in the distribution of the household of the noble, they are fellow-portioners. Honor goes to the fellow. And so the Dai stands.

The number twenty-eight recurs at every level of the teaching, and the lecture repeatedly drew it out. Two — the two designated grandsons — plus three — the three veils of the natural, the trailing, and the special — plus four — the four ranks of messengership, sending, the imamate, and the holding-of-trust — plus seven — the seven sections of the testimony of faith — plus twelve — the twelve islands, the twelve dais, the twelve hearts, paralleling the twelve letters of the great testimony. The total is twenty-eight. This is the order of the higher world, eternally. The first formal designation written by one of the great Dais on his successor used twenty-eight titles, in honor of the twenty-eight letters of the testimony. The twenty-eight letters of the Quran classify into the seven-by-seven categories of luminous-light, luminous-heavy, dark-light, and dark-heavy; the detached letters at the openings of the chapters are entirely luminous, with no dark letter among them.

The twenty-eighth in any sequence is the gatherer of those who came before — the station of rest. The twenty-ninth in any sequence is the breaker, the one who enters in and reorders. The lecture pointed to the verse in the Chapter of Victory, where the testimony of the Messenger appears only once in the entire Quran, precisely at verse twenty-nine — the verse that names the Messenger and those with him as "forceful against the disbelievers, merciful among themselves" (Quran 48:29, Saheeh International translation). The numbering is not decoration. The teaching is: when you see the number, look for the structure. When you see the structure, look for the doctrine. The doctrine is not in the number; the doctrine is in what the number points to. But the number is a reliable pointer. The pen lifts seven times in writing the testimony; the seven lifts of the pen are the seven Imams' stations of rest; the seven stations of rest are the seven Imams who gather and complete their lines. The teacher closed this with the soft reminder: do not be a hunter of numbers. Be a hunter of doctrine. The numbers will come to the seeker as he finds the doctrine. If he starts with the number and chases the doctrine, he will likely end with neither. If he starts with the doctrine, the numbers will arrange themselves around it; the patterning is part of the doctrine, not a separate game.

## The four-phrase litany, the two stations of the Dai, and the doctrine of visitation

The four-phrase litany commended by the Messenger — glory to Allah, all praise is for Allah, there is no god but Allah, Allah is greater, with there is no power and no strength except in Allah added as the fifth — is, the lecture unfolded, the most precise of teachings about the soul's structure. The Prophetic tradition that anchors the four reads: "The most beloved speech to Allah is four: glory to Allah, all praise is for Allah, there is no god but Allah, and Allah is greater" (one of the canonical hadith collections, Book of Remembrance and Supplication, tradition 2137, translated by Abdul Hamid Siddiqui). The phrases are not interchangeable. Each operates on a particular level. Allah is greater acts on the growing soul. The growing soul is what gives the believer his increase in matter and form. It is what makes the child grow into the adult. It is what makes the body persist in its development. Allah is greater humbles this growth before the greatness that exceeds it. The growing soul is reminded, by the phrase, that whatever it grows into is small before the divine.

All praise is for Allah acts on the sensitive soul. The sensitive soul is what feels, what perceives, what reacts to pleasure and pain. All praise is for Allah orients the sensations themselves toward praise. Every sensation, in the moment of its arising, can be received as a sensation for which Allah is praised. The sensitive soul that has learned this orientation does not get caught in the sensation. It opens upward. Glory to Allah acts on the rational soul. The rational soul is what thinks, what argues, what understands, what speaks. Glory to Allah transcends the rational. The rational soul learns to praise what is beyond its own reach. Glory is the praise that recognizes incomparability. The rational soul that pronounces glory is the rational soul that has acknowledged its own boundary.

There is no god but Allah is the form upon the luminous soul. The luminous soul is the form on which the testimony is inscribed. This is the highest soul in the structure. The phrase is not an act of one of the three lower souls; it is the form of the highest soul. To say it is to recognize what the luminous soul itself is constituted by. There is no power and no strength except in Allah is the fifth phrase, and it has its own work. It denies the believer's own power and strength. It returns all power and strength to Allah. It is the formula of complete dependence. And the closing pair of the formula — the Highest, the Mighty — is, in the inner doctrine, the essence of the Imams. To say the formula is to deny one's own power and to attach the affirmed power to the essence of the Imams. The discipline of saying these phrases is a discipline of working through the soul-layers in their proper order. Allah is greater humbles the growth; all praise is for Allah orients the sensation; glory to Allah transcends the reasoning; there is no god but Allah inscribes the form; there is no power and no strength returns the power. A believer who learns to say these in their proper order is a believer who is being remade by the very phrases. The rational soul, the lecture also noted, absorbs the sensitive soul through life and especially at death. A believer who has lived in praise has been gathering the sensitive into the rational throughout his life. At death, the rational soul takes the gathered sensitive with it. The sensation does not remain in the body. The death is noble. The believer who has not done this gathering, by contrast, leaves the sensation in the body at death; the body retains the sensation; the death is not noble. This is why the discipline of praising is itself the discipline of dying well.

One of the great Dais was, the lecture said, at the human station for his Imam and at the divine principle station for his fellow believers. Two simultaneous stations. This is not a contradiction. It is a structure. The Dai serves the Imam. In the relation to the Imam, the Dai is the human, the embodied, the vessel. The Imam is the divine principle; the Dai is the human. The Dai is gathered into the Imam's purpose as a human form is gathered into the divine purpose. But the Dai also serves the believers. In the relation to the believers, the Dai is the divine principle. The believers are human in relation to him. He is, for them, the principle of order. They look toward him as believers look toward what is higher than themselves; he gives them what they require, as the Imam gives him what he requires. The two stations are not at war. They are two directions of the same Dai. To his Imam, he is the vessel; to his believers, he is the principle. To his Imam, he is the receiver; to his believers, he is the giver. The Dai's life is constituted by these two directions running through him at every moment.

This is also why the Dai must never confuse the two. The Dai who takes himself to be the divine principle in relation to the Imam has fallen out of his station. The Dai who takes himself to be the human in relation to the believers has also fallen out of his station. The discipline of the office is to hold both with precision: human upward, divine principle downward. The title bestowed on one of the great Dais — the trusted of the trusted — is one of the high titles of trust. It acknowledges that the Dai bears two trusts: the trust given to him by the Imam and the trust given to him by the believers. He is trusted of both; and the believers' trust in him is itself part of his being trusted before the Imam.

Visitation is a connection. The visitor at the grave is not merely paying respect; he is opening a channel. The channel runs upward, from the visited to the rank above the visited, to make intercession at the gathering of forms on behalf of the visitor. The doctrine is concrete. The visitor benefits, by the visitation, through the chain that the visited can mobilize on his behalf. The rank-holders of the call to faith are angels in potential during their lives and become angels in actuality after death. In life, they were angels in potentiality; their daily service was the work of angels in the inner reality. In death, the potentiality becomes actuality. They are now angels at the gathering of forms. The visitor who visits them is opening a channel to those angels-in-actuality. Intercession is no metaphor; it is the work of the visited angel. The Messenger's saying that parents feel joy when children visit their graves is, in the inner reading, the literal sense. The parents do feel the visit. The joy is not a poetic phrasing of the visit's value to the children. The joy is the parents' own response, in their angelic state, to the children's presence at the grave. It is the same joy as when the children sat in their laps in life.

Holy graves — the graves of the rank-holders, of the pious, of the friends of Allah — retain their blessing by their trace. Even after the refined elements of the buried have progressed beyond, the trace lingers. The lecture used the comparison of the empty perfume bottle: the fragrance lingers in the bottle long after the perfume is gone. The grave is the bottle of the refined elements; the trace of those elements persists. The visitor at such a grave is breathing in the trace; the trace is itself a vehicle of blessing. There is a precise rule for the reverse case. If the visitor at a grave is of higher rank than the buried, the flow of aids reverses: it descends from the visitor to the buried. The ordinary direction is the buried interceding for the visitor; in the reversed case, the visitor's higher rank causes the descent to go the other way. This is why the great Dais, when they visited the graves of the lesser, brought benefit to those buried. The visit was a gift descending. The verse in the fifth chapter of the Quran — "O you who have believed, fear Allah and seek the means of nearness to Him" (Quran 5:35, Saheeh International translation) — is the structural doctrine. The means is the chain — the Messenger, the family of the House, the dais, the proofs, the Imam. The Quran is the source of guidance, but the means is the chain through which the guidance is held. To approach Allah through the chain is the way; to bypass the chain is not the way. Gnosis of the Worshipped is gnosis of the rank-holders. The gnosis of Allah is inside the gnosis of the rank-holders. The believer who learns to know the rank-holders is the believer who is learning, by that very act, to know the Worshipped.

## The two Mehdis, the great cycle, and the practical frame

There are two Mehdis. The first is the eleventh Imam, called Mehdi. He is in the line of the Imams. He is the Mehdi of the Imams. His office is within the circle of the imamate. The second is the fiftieth Imam, the Mehdi of the End of Time. He is outside the circle of the imamate. He is the closing letter of the latter end. He is a different personality. This must be held distinctly. To merge the two is to confuse the doctrine. They are two different Mehdis with two different offices. One of the great masters of the line taught that the Mehdi is the Riser and the Riser is the Mehdi. This applies at the level where the two functions overlap. The Riser of the era of concealment was, in his time, also called Mehdi — and that doctrine must be understood within the doctrine of the two. The Mehdi is the confluence of eight chapters corresponding to the eight angels who carry the Throne. The structural number for the Mehdi is eight. He gathers the eight as the Throne is carried by the eight. The Riser, when he comes, takes the succession of the Tenth on his departure from this abode. And he remains in office for only three days. The duration is short in this dispensation. The work of the Riser, on his manifestation, is concentrated into those three days.

Today's Imam, the thirty-fifth of the line, combines two weeks of seven and possesses fourteen Imams within himself. The seventh of his week, he is also the gatherer of the prior six and of the prior six of the prior week — fourteen in all. His Dai's station rises with him; the proximity to the Imam who carries fourteen elevates the Dai's office in turn. The doctrine of the week is the doctrine of how the Imams are grouped in their structure. Seven Imams form a week. The seventh of each week is the gatherer of his seven. The combining of two weeks in today's Imam means that the thirty-fifth gathers within himself not seven but fourteen. The believers of today, then, are believers in an extraordinary moment of the line. The accumulation in the thirty-fifth is itself a gift of the era.

The teacher closed the cosmology with the doctrine of the great cycle. One great cycle contains four hundred thousand rounds multiplied by four hundred thousand rounds. The number is enormous. The total time is past human comprehension. One great cycle has passed; nine remain. The French historian of Islamic philosophy who devoted a whole monograph to the doctrine of cyclical time in this tradition observed that the cycle is "not a measurement of time but a measure of being" — its function is not chronological but ontological, locating the believer within an unfolding hierarchy of returns (Henry Corbin, *Cyclical Time and Ismaili Gnosis*, pages 30 to 35, Bollingen Series). Within each great cycle, the era of concealment and the era of unveiling succeed each other like night and day. Eras of concealment are followed by eras of openness; eras of openness are followed by eras of concealment. The pattern of night and day is the pattern of the eras. At each end-of-great-cycle, the place of the Tenth is taken by the ninth intellect. The ascending forms gather in the domain of the First Emanator. And in the end, all are absorbed into the First Emanator. Only the domain of the First Intellect and the First Emanator remain. This is the horizon. The horizon is settled. The great cycle has its term. The pattern of nights and days runs its course. At the end, the great absorption brings all that has ascended into the First Emanator; the domains of the highest emanations remain; what was below is gathered into what was above. This is no cause for fear. It is a cause for orientation. The believer who knows this horizon is the believer whose life is rightly oriented. He does not pour his life into what will be gathered up. He pours his life into the chain through which he himself, in his small way, ascends. The chain is what carries him forward through the eras. The chain is what carries him to the First Intellect and the First Emanator in the end.

The practical frame follows the cosmology in the lecture as it does in the appendix. The middle path of generosity — not tying the hand to the neck, not opening it completely — is the path of the ordinary believer. The Messenger's state of giving everything, to the point that a single goat-leg came home, is not the state for ordinary believers. That state belongs to the protected ones. The ordinary believer is commanded a middle measure. He gives without being miserly; he holds without being extravagant. The servants of the Merciful are praised for precisely this balance — "those who, when they spend, do so neither extravagantly nor sparingly, but are ever, between that, justly moderate" (Quran 25:67, Saheeh International translation). The Father of Imams's instruction to the wealthy man's son is the model. The son was imitating the Father of Imams's asceticism; the Father of Imams himself sent him back to enjoy what Allah had given him. Do not take my example as yours. The example of the protected is not the example of the ordinary believer. The ordinary believer is to use what he has been given. A saying from the same source captures the principle in a single line: "Generosity without extravagance, and economy without stinginess" (the book *The Brilliant Aphorisms*, the eleventh-century compilation of sayings of the Commander of the Faithful, saying 6249, contemporary English edition).

The salt at the meal is the refined trace of the rank-holders of the call to faith. The pigeons of the House of Allah and of Medina, whose natural death is permitted, are from the same refined trace. The salt and the pigeons are not mere items in the world. They are bearers of the trace. The believer who salts his food is in contact with the rank-holders; the pilgrim who sees the pigeons of the House is seeing the rank-holders in their feathered form. The foundational gift offered for the infant and the meat of the Festival of Sacrifice must not be given to those outside the community. A nursing mother must not eat from her child's foundational gift. The disciplines are precise. The reasoning is precise: the foundational gift is, for the child, his own gift; what passes through the mother becomes the milk that returns to the child; to eat the gift is to send the child his own gift back as milk — and that, the doctrine says, makes the milk not lawful for him. The child cannot lawfully drink what is, in the inner reckoning, his own gift returning. The discipline guards the channel. The supplication recited three times in the morning and three times in the evening — in the name of Allah, with Whose name nothing in the earth or in the heavens can harm — protects from all harm. The name of Allah here is the formula of protection in the most concentrated form. The believer who keeps this supplication is a believer whose mornings and evenings are armored. Wild animals dying their own death remain in the fallen state until the great return. The slaughter discipline applies because the animal's sensitive soul is to be separated from it; the natural death does not do this separation. The pigeons are excepted because they are the refined trace of the rank-holders; their station is already different. The general rule and the exception both have their reasons in the inner doctrine.

The lecture took some care with the call to prayer, because the small disciplines of the call carry weight. The taking of refuge is not done in the call; only the name of Allah is said; the taking of refuge is for when the recitation of the Quran begins. The two have different functions, and they should not be mixed. The last there is no god but Allah in the call should be said softly. The first is recited with emphasis; the last with a soft tone. The reason is the trembling of a particular being whose departure from his entire circle will happen in the blink of an eye — the last counselor. His passing will be instantaneous; the tone of the closing alludes to this instantaneity. To elongate it would be to misrepresent the trembling. The soft tone is the right tone. The closing verse of the Chapter of the Children of Israel — "And say: all praise is for Allah, who has not taken a son and has had no partner in dominion, and has no protector from humiliation, and magnify Him with great magnification" (Quran 17:111, Saheeh International translation) — is called the Verse of Honor. It anchors the magnification of the call. The order of praise, blessings, and magnification has been clarified by the Imam: praise is the higher matter and should come first; blessings come after; this is the right order. Some hold that the name of Allah should also be said before the magnification. The lecture noted that this is permissible; it does not have to be done, but there is no harm in doing it. The discipline is not rigid; the principle of praise first is what must be preserved.

The straight path is not abstract. It is the believer's own chain. The believer's straight path is the chain that runs from him through the lower rank-holders to the higher rank-holders to the Door of Doors. The path is constituted by the line of those who carry the call to faith above him. This means that the believer's path is also his own. No two believers have exactly the same chain. The deputy who teaches a believer is the immediate rank-holder above him; the Dai is the next level; the proof above the Dai is the next; and so on, until the chain reaches the Door of Doors. Each believer's chain is constituted by the specific persons through whom his life in the call passes. To stand in the straight path is to stand in this chain. To leave the chain is to leave the path. The verse "guide us to the straight path" (Quran 1:6, Saheeh International translation), recited in every prayer, is the believer's request that his own chain be preserved — that the rank-holders above him remain firm, that his connection to them remain intact, that the line through which his life in the call is carried not be broken. The verse in the thirty-sixth chapter — "Indeed, you are from among the messengers, on a straight path" (Quran 36:3 to 36:4, Saheeh International translation) — refers, in the inner reading, to the grandfather of the line being on the straight path of the partial dais. The grandfather, the elder of the line through the Father of Imams, was on the straight path that he himself, in his time, constituted for those below him. The verse is, in the outer reading, the speaker's affirmation; in the inner reading, it is the affirmation of every grandfather of every line, that his own path of the partial dais is straight.

In one round there are three hundred and sixty manifestations of the line's integration. The human body contains three hundred and sixty souls; of these, one part progresses per round. The body is not a single soul; it is three hundred and sixty souls gathered. Across a round, one of these souls makes its progress. The rate is slow; the rate is precise; and the rate is built into the body's very structure. The believer who knows this is a believer who is patient with himself. He is not surprised that progress is slow; he knows the rate. He is also not despairing; he knows that the rate is real. One part per round is movement. Across the rounds that lie ahead, the three hundred and sixty souls will all make their progress in turn. The body is being processed. The thousand-year period of return is fixed for both believer and non-believer alike. The believer dies in his rank; he returns a thousand years later, in the same place, at the same rank or above, depending on his covenant and his addition. The eras are long, but they are not unbounded. At the end of the great cycle, the absorption into the First Emanator is the term.

At the end of one round the lava-released souls who had done some good deeds emerge before the completion of the great cycle through volcanoes. They become soil, vegetation, and food for further refinement. This is a strange and rich teaching. The earth's eruptions are not merely geological; they are spiritual. Souls held in the lower levels for their offenses, but who had done some good deeds, are released through volcanoes. Through the eruption they come into the world again. They become soil; they nourish vegetation; they enter food. Each cycle of soil, vegetation, food becomes a cycle of further refinement. What was held below is being moved upward, slowly, through the food chain itself. Even the most ordinary act of eating is, in the inner reading, an act of participating in the refinement of souls. And when offering and receiving occur in a gathering of teaching, the souls of the deceased believers attend. The questioner asks through the tongue of a higher rank-holder. The teaching of the gathering is not only for the living in the room.

## Where the teaching has landed

The appendix has carried its disciplines to both edges at once — the daily acts of the believer and the absorption of all that has ascended into the First Emanator. The one wrong that is not forgiven is, in the inner reading, also the subtlest of dilutions; the believer's daily prayer is, in part, a daily clearing of those dilutions. The believer is the summit of the human order; he is the thing Allah's power especially attends to. The twenty-eight is the order of the higher world, with the twenty-eighth as the gatherer and the twenty-ninth as the breaker. The four-phrase litany works through the four layers of the soul in their proper order, and the rational soul absorbs the sensitive soul through life and especially at death, which is why the discipline of praising is the discipline of dying well. The Dai stands at two stations at once; visitation opens a channel through which the buried intercede for the visitor — unless the visitor is of higher rank, in which case the flow reverses. The two Mehdis are two persons; the great cycle contains four hundred thousand rounds multiplied by four hundred thousand rounds, of which one has passed and nine remain. The practical frame holds the cosmology in daily reach: the middle path of generosity, the salt at the meal, the pigeons of the House, the rule about the foundational gift, the supplication of protection, the small disciplines of the call to prayer, the straight path as the believer's own chain. Three concept sections, one continuous teaching — the line received at every scale.

## Closing

The book closes as it began, with prayers for the dais across the ages and for the present Dai of the line. The author prays that whoever reads these treasures may not read them save by the permission of one who has authority of advancement and postponement, and that the discipline of concealment may be guarded; and that whoever reads with permission may carry away what is correct, attributing the correctness to the Dai who caused the tongue to flow, and whatever error is in the book is attributed to the author himself — for he is the sinner. These are the stored treasures of wisdom, gathered for the masters of partial protection, that from them the line of the call to faith may continue to draw forth substance and light. The closing is not modesty alone. It is doctrinal. The line is the source; the speaker is a vessel; the listener carries on what was given. The treasures are stored for those who will need them, and the line continues to draw. The believer who has heard the appendix to its end knows now what he is standing inside. He has been instructed in the disciplines of his day and in the horizon of his cosmos. The book has opened slowly; each return brings out a layer not visible before; and the prayer that has closed the body of the book also closes its appendix, on the same note with which the whole opened — for the dais across the ages, for the present Dai of the line, and for the Imam of the time, blessings of Allah upon them all, forever and ever.
