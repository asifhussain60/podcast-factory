# Living Context and the Whole Structure

## Where this chapter opens

What if the doctrine the book has been laying down for hundreds of pages — the gardens of those specially placed, the dissolved heart of the Imam, the chain of the ranks, the fifty thousand years, the rolling up of the heaven — is not a doctrine to be admired from a distance but a doctrine that is, at this very moment, passing through the believer's body at his ablution, through the room where the lesson is being given, through the breath he holds at the last moment of the night before he eats? And what if the book itself, as it closes, is not finishing a long exposition but turning the listener around to look at the architecture of what he has been walking through, so that the structure becomes visible only at the end?

This episode closes the book. The teacher gathers the connected meditations — the longer expositions that arose by his returning to the same themes from different angles — and lets them set the doctrine in its living context: in the believer's body, in the room of the lesson, in the era of veiled presence under today's Dai and today's Imam, on the chain the believer walks every day. Then the author closes with the prayer he wanted the book sealed by, and the teacher takes a final step back to read the architecture of the whole. Three concept sections, each opened and developed and landed inside the episode. The first reads the body as a porous vessel in active exchange with the spiritual currents around it and the lesson-gathering as a room whose true audience extends past the seated listeners. The second reads the veil of the present era, the gathered moment of today's Imam, and the daily prayer as the believer's walk along his own chain. The third receives the author's closing prayer and gathers, with the teacher's final step back, the architecture of the whole book — what was begun in the first praise, what was carried by the named witnesses to the line, and what the recurring principles together constitute as the book's doctrine.

## The porous vessel

The teacher returns to the doctrine of the *riyat* and what it teaches about the believer's body. The *riyat* are the spiritual currents — the inner essence-streams that the soul carries within itself and that emerge most fully at death. During life, the believer's *riyat* are still gathered in his body. At the moment of death, they begin to emerge. The higher *riyat* emerge first, in the moments immediately after the soul departs, before burial. The lower *riyat* emerge in the three days after burial. After three days, what remain are vapors only. The doctrine has been given before, in the second question; the teacher restates it here to land its consequences on the believer's daily practice.

From the higher *riyat* of the believer, the shadow-form of the successors is constituted. When the believer's children or grandchildren step into a place of service in the call, the shadow-form they receive includes a portion shaped from his higher *riyat*. This is the meaning of the inherited disposition in the community: not merely a social inheritance, not merely the imitation of an elder by a younger, but a substance-transfer in the inner order. The grandfather's inner posture passes, through this shadow-form, into the grandson who will stand in his office. From the lower *riyat* — the *riyat* of the three days after burial — the natural form of subsequent generations is constituted. These shape the basic embodiment of those who will come after. The believer's body, in this teaching, is the carrier of two distinct gifts to the future, released at two distinct moments after his passing.

This is why the family's behavior in those three days matters spiritually beyond what we might suppose. The recitation of the Opening Chapter, the giving of *sadaqah* in the believer's name, the recitation of the seven, the meals offered for the poor, the commissioning of Quran readings, the giving of charity — all of these in the three days reach the *riyat* directly. After three days, they reach the inner refinements and the inner forms. Both are real; both have value; but the three-day window is the time when the *riyat* themselves are still ascending and can be addressed. The family who understands the doctrine attends to those three days with a different concentration. The offerings are not customary politenesses; they are the addressing of currents that are still in motion.

The Messenger ﷺ instructed his community to bury the dead quickly, and the instruction is not in tension with this. The canonical hadith collection records, on the authority of a close companion of the Prophet (may Allah be pleased with him), the saying that the believer should hurry the funeral procession — if the deceased was righteous, the hurry forwards him to good; if otherwise, the hurry removes an evil from the necks of the bearers (the first canonical hadith collection, book of funerals, hadith 1315, trans. Muhsin Khan). The instruction is for the dignity of the body and for the order of the soul's release. The three-day window begins from burial; whether burial is on the day of death or several days after, the window runs three days from the burial. But the Messenger's preference is for the body not to be held; the believer's family does best to bury promptly and then to commit themselves to the three days of offerings. The two doctrines, properly understood, are continuous: quick burial opens the window, and the three days that follow are the family's responsibility.

The believer in his own life receives *riyat* in three principal moments. At ablution, he says *Bismillah*. The first breath after the *Bismillah* is itself a vehicle of *riyat*. The water that touches his skin opens channels. The water that enters his pores is the carrier. The angels created from the shaken drops are at the open channels, praying for him. The believer who performs ablution hurriedly, without the *Bismillah*, without attention to the breath, without the shaking of the hands, is performing an ablution that the doctrine acknowledges as valid for the prayer but that does not bring the full benefit. The completeness of ablution is the discipline of attention through all of these moments. The two atonements through this completeness are precisely about this discipline: the completeness in the cold, when external cold makes the limbs reluctant; and the completeness in the inner coldness — the laziness that internal disinclination would lead to incompletion. To perform ablution completely in those moments of internal coldness is itself the atonement. The discipline of attention is not pious surplus; it is the structural mechanism by which the *riyat* are received.

The Quran itself names the soul's nightly traffic. The chapter on the throngs records that it is the divine who takes souls at the time of death and takes those that did not die during their sleep, holding back the ones for which death has been decreed and releasing the rest until a specified term — chapter thirty-nine, verse forty-two, in the rendering of the Study Quran (Seyyed Hossein Nasr, ed., HarperOne 2015, p. 1145). The doctrine of the porous body builds on this naming. The soul that is taken in sleep returns; the soul that is taken at death emerges through its currents. The night is itself a window in the believer's biology, and the pre-dawn moment, just before the soul is released back to the body, is the moment of highest reception.

At the pre-dawn meal, the believer eats at the last moment of the night. The *riyat* that descend on uncooked fruits during the night — the dew-like descent of purified *riyat* the doctrine has named earlier — are present in greater concentration at the very last moment, just before dawn. The believer who delays the pre-dawn meal to the last moment is catching the highest concentration of the descending *riyat*. The believer who eats early loses the high-concentration window. At the breaking of the fast, the believer breaks immediately at sunset. The *riyat* at this moment are not in the same descending pattern as before dawn; they are in a returning pattern. The body, having been emptied by the fast, opens to receive. The first morsels after the fast are the most receptive moments. The believer who delays the breaking, even for the sake of further prayer, misses the most receptive moment. The Messenger's instruction to hurry the breaking of the fast is precisely about this. The canonical hadith collection preserves the saying, narrated through one of the companions of the Prophet, that the people will continue to be in good condition as long as they hasten in breaking the fast — the first canonical hadith collection, book of fasting, hadith 1957 (trans. Muhsin Khan). The teacher's reading of the moment as the body's most receptive opening turns the prophetic preference from a matter of legal generosity into a matter of structural exchange. The hurry is not relaxation of the discipline; it is precision about the window.

The fourth Imam, in his prayer-book that has come down to us as the household's manual of supplication, opens his prayer for the daybreak with the recognition that the night and the day are themselves provisions taken by the divine and returned, and he asks for protection in the moments when the body is most open — prayer six, on praising the divine at the breaking of dawn (the prayer-book of the fourth Imam, trans. William Chittick, *The Psalms of Islam*, Muhammadi Trust 1988, p. 18). The teacher's doctrine of the dawn-meal as a high-concentration moment is in continuity with the fourth Imam's prayer at the same hour. Both read the moment as a structured exchange, not as a neutral biological transition.

The believer's body, in this teaching, is a body in active exchange with the spiritual currents around it at every breath. It is not a closed vessel; it is a porous vessel through which the *riyat* are received and through which, at death, the *riyat* will emerge. The discipline of life is to recognize the body as porous and to attend to the moments when the porousness is most active. The doctrine is not asking the believer to add a layer of mysticism onto an ordinary biology; it is naming the biology accurately, in its actual order, so that the believer can act on it. The breath, the water, the morsel, the burial, the three days afterward — each of these is a moment at which the porousness is active, and each invites the believer's attention.

The teacher turns from the body to the room. The doctrine that the souls of deceased believers attend the lesson-gathering when teaching and receiving are flowing is itself a doctrine that reframes the lesson. The teacher in the community is not addressing only those whose bodies sit before him. He is addressing the gathered chain. The departed inner forms of believers come to the lesson-gathering to listen. The questioner who asks a question is asking on behalf of more than himself; his question is voiced through the tongue of a higher rank-holder who carries the askings of those who cannot ask in their own bodies. The room contains more than its seats hold.

This is why the early teacher's instruction to a successor to conduct lessons in the mosque was structured the way it was: the lessons in the mosque allowed the departed inner forms to hear. The instruction was for the benefit of the audience that was invisible. If the lessons had been held only in a private setting, the departed forms might not have had access. The mosque, the public site of teaching, is open to them. The teacher who teaches in the mosque is teaching to a wider gathering than the visible one. The believer who attends with attention is also a representative of his own deceased relatives; through his attention, they too may be brought into the teaching. The discipline of the lesson-gathering is therefore the discipline of public teaching with this awareness. The teacher prepares as for a larger audience than the visible one. The believer attends as a vehicle of a larger gathering than himself. The sermon-compendium of the Commander of the Faithful preserves the saying, in its first sermon, that the bodies of the past believers are in the dust but their souls remain with the living community in its gatherings — sermon one, in the rendering of Sayed Ali Reza, *Peak of Eloquence*, Tahrike Tarsile Quran 1996, p. 105. The early teacher's witness at the circumambulation, of the souls of three early figures performing the rite at high speed, takes its bearing from the same doctrine. The visible gathering is a smaller portion of a larger gathering that has never dispersed.

The witness to this larger audience is the witness of the early teacher who saw, at the circumambulation, the souls of three of the great early figures performing the circumambulation at very high speed. The witness was a witness to what the body of the believer carries underneath the outer rite. The body is, in our state, slowing what the soul could do at full speed. The circumambulation of the body is a small portion of the circumambulation the soul could perform. The cry of longing the early teacher uttered — *oh, the longing!* — is the cry of the believer who sees what is possible. The believer who has not yet seen still walks the circumambulation at the body's pace; the discipline is to walk it with sincerity, knowing that the released soul will perform a faster circumambulation in due time. The longing is not despair; it is anticipation. The body's drag is real, but the body's drag is also the present condition under which the discipline matures. Patience with the body's pace is itself a piece of the discipline.

What the teacher has done, by gathering these two meditations into one breath, is to set the doctrine where it actually lives. The *riyat* are not an abstract scheme; they pass through the believer's pores at ablution, through his mouth at the pre-dawn meal, through his openness at the breaking of the fast, and they emerge through his body at death to constitute the shadow-forms of those who will come after. The lesson-gathering is not a private room; it is a room whose walls are porous to the inner forms of the departed, and the teacher addresses a chain that extends past the seated listeners. The body and the room together are the doctrine's living context — and the believer who recognizes this context is the believer for whom the rest of the book has been written.

## The veil of the present era

The teacher turns to the era in which the believer stands. The Imam of the present time is concealed — the Imam of the Time, who is concealed by the veil, hidden by the secret from all of creation. His original self is hidden; what has appeared is a veil; the structure of his presence in this era is the structure of veiled presence. The teacher takes some care to distinguish concealment from absence. The Imam is not absent. The Imam is present. The presence is veiled. The veil is not the body of the Imam; the veil is the structural condition in which the Imam manifests in this era.

We cannot ordinarily see the Imam. We can, in the inner reality, be addressed by him. We can, by his choice, be visited by him. The witness in the kiss of the visitor's hand at Najran is the witness to this kind of visit. The teacher is precise about what is at stake in the distinction. To think of the Imam as absent is to imagine the era as an empty stretch of time waiting for a future presence to arrive. To know the Imam as present-but-veiled is to read the era as already inhabited by his presence, with the veil as the era's specific mode. The believer who has the first imagination treats the era as a desert. The believer who has the second reading treats the era as a house whose owner is in the next room. The doctrine of veiled presence sets the believer in the right relation to his own era. The modern historian of the call, in his standard reference history, observes that the doctrine of concealment did not arise as a defensive compromise but as the structural reading by which the community located itself within sacred time — Farhad Daftary, *The Ismailis: Their History and Doctrines*, Cambridge University Press, 2nd ed. 2007, p. 9. The German historian of the early Fatimid dispensation reaches the same conclusion from a different angle: the cosmology of cycles and the doctrine of veiled presence were always two faces of one architecture, not two improvisations later sewn together — Heinz Halm, *The Empire of the Mahdi*, Brill 1996, p. 18. The teacher's reading of the era as a house whose owner is in the next room is the believer's plain-language version of what the historians describe in academic registers. The doctrine has been continuous; only the descriptive vocabulary varies.

The presence of the Imam is also mediated through the Dai. The Dai of the line stands between the believer and the Imam, carrying the substance of the Imam to the believer and carrying the believer's connection back to the Imam. The Dai is the visible representative of the invisible office. To honor the Dai is to honor the Imam through him; to disrespect the Dai is to disrespect the Imam through him. The Dai is not an additional layer that the believer might wish to bypass for a more direct connection. The mediation is the era's structural form. There is no direct connection to the veiled presence that bypasses the Dai. The believer who insists on a more direct connection is asking for what the era does not give.

The fifty thousand years of this dispensation are the structural duration of this era of veiled presence. The number is not a metaphor; it is the era's bounded extent. When the Riser manifests, the bounded extent will close and the new era will begin. The believer in our era is therefore in a long era of veiled presence — long, but bounded. The discipline of the era is the discipline of receiving the veiled. The Dai's lessons are the veiled Imam's lessons; the lesson-gathering is the veiled Imam's gathering; the place of prostration is the veiled Imam's place. The believer who recognizes this structure receives the era as it is given.

The fifty thousand years connect, also, to the larger cosmology of the great round. One great round is four hundred thousand great cycles multiplied by four hundred thousand great cycles. The current era's fifty thousand years are within this larger calculation. The believer who is given the number is given a sense of where he stands. The bounded extent of the era is itself a small bounded extent within an enormous bounded extent. The horizons compound. The believer's life is short within the era; the era is short within the great round; the great round itself, the teacher has elsewhere said, has its bound. The chain of bounded extents is the cosmos within which the believer's small life finds its scale. The early Fatimid philosophical school had already mapped this nested-bounding doctrine with precision. The great philosopher of the early call, in his treatise on the wellsprings, sets out the doctrine that every level of bounded duration is contained inside a higher bounded duration, and that the human soul is given numbers so that it may locate itself within the nested cycles, not so that it may calculate idly — the treatise on the wellsprings, chapter thirty-five, in the rendering of Paul Walker, *The Wellsprings of Wisdom*, University of Utah Press 1994, p. 81. The teacher's numbers — fifty thousand years inside four hundred thousand great cycles by four hundred thousand great cycles inside the great round — are the believer's version of this classical doctrine. The believer is given the scale; the scale is given so that the believer may know where he stands; the believer's knowing where he stands is itself the discipline of the era.

The teacher would, at the end of many of the lectures, recite the prayer for today's Dai and today's Imam. Today's Imam, the thirty-fifth, is the seventh of his septet and combines two septets. He possesses fourteen Imams within himself. The seventh of any septet is the gatherer of his seven; the seventh of two septets combined is the gatherer of fourteen. This is an extraordinary moment in the line. The believer of today is a believer in an extraordinarily gathered moment. The Dai of today's Imam serves an office whose station rises with the Imam's gathering. The accumulation in the thirty-fifth is the gift of the era. The prayer for the Dai's prolonged life, for the Imam's prolonged life, for the protection of both, for the elevation of both, is the structural prayer of the believer of today. To pray it is to acknowledge the gathered moment and to ask for the moment's continuation.

The Opening Chapter is recited as a gift — a means of connection, lofty and pleasing — to the soul of the author, to the souls of the duat of the line, and to the soul of the Imam of the time. The Opening Chapter is the prayer that anchors all these recitations; reciting it at the close of the lesson-gathering is the way the believer closes the circle that the gathering has opened. The author's name is brought into the closing prayer because the author's tongue is the channel through which the doctrine of the book has flowed; the souls of the duat are brought in because the duat are the line through which the doctrine has been carried; the soul of the Imam is brought in because the Imam is the source from which the doctrine descends. The closing prayer connects the believer's small voice in his own present to the entire chain that has carried the book to his hands.

The teacher returns to the doctrine of the straight path with a precision that bears restatement. The straight path is not an external map. It is the chain of the believer's connection upward. The permitted one above the believer, the breaker above the permitted one, the Dai above the breaker, the proof above the Dai, the door of doors, the Imam — this sequence is the believer's path. The believer walks the chain in the only way he can walk it: by being faithful to each rank in turn. The believer's path is therefore unique to him. No two believers have exactly the same path. Each believer's chain is constituted by the specific persons through whom his connection to the line passes. To walk the path is to honor those specific persons.

The verse *guide us to the straight path* (chapter one, verse six) is the daily prayer of the believer to keep his chain firm. *Guide us* — the guidance asked for is the guidance to remain in the chain. *The straight path* — the straight path is the believer's chain. The believer asks the divine to keep him in his chain. This is the doctrinal reading of the verse that, on its outer face, is a request for moral guidance; on its inner face, the verse is the daily renewal of the believer's covenant with the chain. The same words are spoken; the same verse is recited; the inner reading deepens the outer, it does not replace it. The sermon-compendium of the Commander of the Faithful gives the doctrinal reading in its plainest form: there is a path the believer must walk, and the path is not located in any geography but in the believer's connection upward through the named ranks that the divine has set above him — sermon one hundred eighty-nine, trans. Sayed Ali Reza, *Peak of Eloquence*, Tahrike Tarsile Quran 1996, p. 398. The teacher's reading of the verse is therefore not innovation; it is the recovery of the doctrinal reading that the line has always carried.

This is also why the believer's most important worldly responsibility is to honor his permitted one. The permitted one is the rung immediately above him; without the permitted one, the chain is broken at its closest point. The believer who disrespects his permitted one has broken the chain at the rung where the chain most matters to him. To honor the permitted one is to keep the chain alive. The doctrine is severe because the chain is precise. The believer who imagines that he can hold the chain at higher rungs while neglecting the rung immediately above him has misread the structure. The chain is not strongest at its highest links; it is strongest at the link the believer can actually act upon, which is the rung immediately above.

The doctrine of the two types of progress — one striven for, the other obtained by sitting in someone's lap — connects to this. The believer who sits in the permitted one's lap, in the figurative sense, receives the second type of progress; the believer who strives, by reading and practicing, receives the first. Both are honored. The believer who has both is fortunate. The two types are not in competition. Striving without presence is exhausting; presence without striving is incomplete. The believer who orients himself to his permitted one and also commits himself to his daily readings and his daily practices is the believer using both means at once. The doctrine of the 360 souls in the body, with one part progressing per great cycle, is the rate. The believer should not be surprised at the slow pace. The pace is structural. The cycles will compound it.

The teacher gathers the meditation. The era is the era of veiled presence under today's gathered Imam, the thirty-fifth, who carries fourteen within himself. The Dai mediates the Imam to the believer and the believer to the Imam. The believer's daily walk is the walk of his unique chain — the persons above him in their specific order, named by the permitted one immediately above him and traceable upward through the ranks to the door of doors and the veiled Imam himself. The daily prayer is the request that this chain be preserved. The believer who has heard this meditation has the structure of his era and of his daily prayer and of his closest worldly responsibility in one continuous picture. The doctrine of the chain is not an abstract scheme to admire; it is the scaffolding of the day.

## The author's closing prayer

The author closes the book with a prayer that we should not pass over. He prays for the protection of the Dai of his time, the one whose tongue the author's own tongue has been borrowing for the duration of the book. He prays that the Dai's domain be protected from the Pharaoh of the age — the great worldly opposer who, in every era, threatens the inner gathering of the line. He prays that the Dai's aspirations be conveyed. He prays that the lamps of the call be illuminated. He prays that the followers of the Dai be increased. He prays that the substance of the Dai flow to the questioner. He prays that the subtle blessings reach. He prays for kindness, compassion, forgiveness, and mercy. These prayers are not formulaic. They are the structural prayers of the believer for the Dai. Every prayer the author makes for his Dai is a prayer that we, in our era, should be making for our Dai. The author has not given us a list of his private wishes; he has given us a template for our daily orientation toward the office.

The author closes by attributing the correctness of the book to the Dai who caused his tongue to flow, and the errors to himself. This attribution is also structural. The correct doctrine is from the Dai through the author's tongue; the errors are from the author's own deficiency. The reader, in receiving the doctrine, attributes the correctness back to its source. The author has modeled the right posture for the receiver: take what is correct as descending from above; take what is in error as belonging to the channel through which it passed. The believer who carries the doctrine forward into his own teaching will model the same posture for whoever receives it from him. The chain is preserved, in its substance, by this posture of attribution. The same posture is articulated by the great philosopher of the early call in the opening of his treatise on the wellsprings: the channel does not own what flows through it, and the receiver who acknowledges the source preserves the flow for those who will come after — chapter one of the treatise, Paul Walker, *The Wellsprings of Wisdom*, University of Utah Press 1994, p. 41. The author's closing posture is the doctrinal posture of the line itself.

And the author closes with the covenant: that whoever reads this book without the permission of one who has the authority of advancement and deferral is absolved of all duty toward the Worshipped and toward all the bounded ones of the call. The covenant is severe. The discipline of concealment runs to the end. The author has not relaxed the discipline because the book is closing; he has tightened it. The book is now in the world; the discipline of who may read it is the discipline that ensures its contents do not fall into the hands of those who would do harm with them. The covenant is the author's last act of stewardship over what the book has carried. The book is then sealed by the prayer of the gathering — peace be upon them all, forever and ever. With this prayer the book of Treasures of Wisdom is closed. The treasures have been displayed; the conditions of their reception have been set; the readers who come to them by permission may carry away what they can hold.

The teacher takes a final step back. When we step back from the particular teachings and look at Treasures of Wisdom as a whole, what do we see? We see, first, a book that begins with the praise of the divine through the special human form and the gardens of those specially placed. The opening is already a doctrine: the divine has manifested a special human form from the gardens of the specially placed; the divine's manifestation is mediated by the believers and the bounded ones. This doctrine sets the tone for what follows. The divine is not approached through abstraction; the divine is approached through the chain. The praise itself is the doctrine in compressed form.

We see, second, the witness to the line — Hasan and Husayn, the Fatimid Imams, the duat of Yemen, the great middle figures of the line, the early emigrant whose station opens the era of concealment — each name carrying its particular station. We see, third, the discipline of the book: the covenant of concealment, the attribution of correctness to the Dai, the prayer for the questioner. The book is structured as a transaction: a question from below, an answer from above, a discipline for who may handle the answer.

We see, fourth, the first question and its three-part answer — what was before the World of Origination, how the special human form is built, and what the heavens and the earth came from. These three subjects organize much of the cosmology that follows. We see, fifth, the doctrine of the straight celestial mechanism and the believer's substance — where the believer's inner form goes, where his *riyat* go, where his refined remnants go, how the Imam dissolves in the heart and becomes substance for the bounded ones, how the next Imam is formed.

We see, sixth, the doctrine of the House, the true heart, the rock and the pious ones, the twelve islands as twelve hearts, the twenty-eight as the eternal order. This is the structural doctrine of the cosmos. We see, seventh, the doctrine of the twenty-eight and the twenty-ninth — the gatherer and the breaker, the eighth century figures who stand at twenty-eight and twenty-nine, the disconnected letters as twenty-nine, the verse of the messenger's testimony in the twenty-ninth verse of the Chapter of Victory. The structural numbers organize the line.

We see, eighth, the cycles — the corner of the lion from the refined remnants of the commander of the believers in the early cycles, the returns of the messenger and his successor, the philosopher and the friend of the entities. The doctrine of the returns opens the doctrine of the structured re-emergence of the same ranks. We see, ninth, the doctrine of the brother of the messenger, the seal-letter, the eye-letter, and the confidants of Jesus. The line is traced backward and forward; the successor is the first and the last. We see, tenth, the doctrine of the structured re-emergence — same rank, thousand-year cycle, the two prostrations as creation and return.

We see, eleventh, the doctrine of the twenty-first, the senses, and the discipline of slaughter; twelfth, the believer at the summit of beings; thirteenth, the sacrifice at Mina, the litany of the four, the souls and their absorption; fourteenth, the geography of Arafat, the plot before the pool, and the two stations of the Dai; fifteenth, the two figures of the awaited one, the three days of the Riser, and the rate of progress; sixteenth, the end of the cycles — the rolling up of the heaven, the great round, the absorption into the First Sender.

Across all these, certain principles recur. The chain is everything. The believer's path is his chain. The knowledge of the divine is inside the knowledge of the bounded ones. The means of connection is the chain itself. The number twenty-eight is the eternal order. It returns at every level — in the testimony's letters, in the House's structure, in the duat's sequences, in the Imams' lineage. To see twenty-eight is to see the structural ordering. Death does not interrupt the line. The Imam dissolves in the heart and becomes substance for the bounded ones. The bounded ones return the substance at their passing. The next Imam is formed from the gathered substance. The line is carried by the passings, not despite them. Concealment is part of the doctrine, not a defect. The Imam is concealed by structural necessity. The book is sealed by the discipline of concealment. Whoever broadcasts the secrets has prepared his seat in fire. The discipline is severe because the realities are dangerous to the unprepared.

The believer is the term of the human ascent. The summit of mankind is the believer. The thing that the Quran speaks of is the believer. The first who believed — the successor — and the term of those who follow are the same. The believer's small life is enormous in its meaning. The cycle is bounded. The great round has its term. The absorption into the First Sender is the horizon. The believer is not in an endless drift; he is moving toward a determined end through determined cycles. These principles together constitute the doctrine of Treasures of Wisdom. The treasures stored for the masters of the partial share of preservation are these principles in their full expression. The duat who draw from them draw the principles in new applications for each era. The believers who receive what the duat distribute receive the principles in the forms appropriate to their stations.

The closing prayer is the prayer of the believer at the end of the book. May the chain be preserved. May the Dai of our time live long, with prolonged life, in health and in strength. May the Imam of our time be protected. May our connection to the chain be guarded. May we receive what we are given. May we transmit what we have received, only to those who can hold it, by permission, with discipline. May the blessings of the divine be upon our master Muhammad and upon the family of our master Muhammad, and may peace and salutation be granted. Peace be upon them all, forever and ever. The treasures are stored. The book closes. The reader who has been given permission to read it closes the book also, with the same prayers, with the same orientation toward the line, with the same discipline of what may and what may not be passed on. The Opening Chapter is recited at the close, and the circle is closed.

## What this chapter lands

Three teachings have been opened and landed, and together they leave the doctrine where the book has been pointing it all along — into the believer's body, his lesson-gathering, his era, his daily prayer, and his orientation toward the architecture of the whole. The body is a porous vessel in active exchange with spiritual currents — at ablution through the *Bismillah* and the breath and the water and the shaken drops; at the pre-dawn meal taken at the last moment; at the breaking of the fast taken at sunset; at the three days after burial through which the higher and lower *riyat* emerge to constitute the shadow-forms of successors and the natural forms of those to come. The lesson-gathering is a room whose audience extends past the visible seats — the departed inner forms attend, the questioner asks on behalf of more than himself, the teacher addresses the whole chain. The circumambulation of the body is the slow form of the circumambulation the soul could perform; the longing for the soul's faster motion is the healthy longing of the believer who knows what is ahead.

The era is veiled presence — not absence — under the Dai who mediates the veiled Imam to the believer and the believer back to the veiled Imam. Today's Imam is the thirty-fifth, the gatherer of two septets, carrying fourteen within himself; the believer of today is in an extraordinarily gathered moment. The believer's daily prayer is his walk along his own chain — the rung immediately above him is the permitted one, then the breaker, then the Dai, then the proof, then the door of doors, then the Imam — and the verse *guide us to the straight path* is the daily request that the chain be preserved. The two types of progress — striven-for and lap-transferred — are both honored; the rate is one part of three hundred and sixty per great cycle; the cycles will compound the slow rate into the full integration.

The author closes with prayers for his Dai — for the protection of the office, the conveyance of the aspirations, the illumination of the lamps, the increase of followers, the flow of substance, the reach of the subtle blessings — and these prayers are templates for our prayers for our Dai. He attributes correctness to the Dai and errors to himself; the believer attributes correctness back to its source. He seals the book with the covenant that whoever reads it without permission is absolved of all duty — the discipline of concealment runs to the end. The teacher takes the final step back and gathers what the book has shown: the praise of the divine through the special form, the witness to the line, the cosmology of the first question, the doctrines of the believer's substance, the House and its eternal order, the numbers twenty-eight and twenty-nine, the cycles and the returns, the structured re-emergence, the summit, the Mina sacrifice, the Arafat geography, the rate of progress, and the rolling up of the heaven into the absorption at the great round's end. Across them, the recurring principles stand: the chain is everything, twenty-eight is the eternal order, death does not interrupt the line, concealment is part of the doctrine, the believer is the term of the human ascent, the cycle is bounded. These are the treasures of wisdom, distributed by the duat across the eras, received by the believers in the forms appropriate to their stations.

## Closing

The book is closed. The treasures have been displayed; the conditions of their reception have been set. The believer who has walked the book from its first praise of the divine to this closing prayer has walked a long road — through the praise that places the divine's manifestation in the gardens of those specially placed, through the witness that names every rank in the line, through the cosmology of the first question, through the Imam's dissolved heart and the *riyat* that flow from it, through the eternal order of twenty-eight, through the cycles and the returns, through the geography of the pilgrimage that turns out to be the geography of the line, through the three days of the Riser, through the rolling up of the heaven into the absorption at the great round's end. The chain is the path he has walked, and the chain is the path he will continue to walk. The same daily prayer, the same orientation toward the rung immediately above him, the same discipline of who may and who may not be passed what — these are the practices of the believer who has heard what the book has said. May the chain be preserved. May the Dai of our time live long. May the Imam of our time be protected. Peace be upon them all, forever and ever.
