## Where this chapter begins

What if a single date on the calendar — the twenty-first of any month — has gathered to itself, by the structure of the cosmos rather than by accident, an inauspiciousness so dense that the master of the line composed an exceptional supplication specifically for it? And what if the same number appears, again, as the day on which the heirs of the line die? The third great teaching has already been laid down in the book: the heart beneath the House, the eternal numbers, the doctrine of return at the same rank. Now the book walks across that floor and opens the lectures that fill in what living on it looks like.

This stretch runs from the lecture on the twenty-first and the discipline of slaughter, through the lecture on the believer at the summit of beings and the doctrine of visitation, to the lectures on the two Mehdis, the rolling of the heavens, the end of the great cycle, and finally to the closing of the book. The threads bind tightly together: the soul at the moment of slaughter, the soul at the moment of visitation, the soul at the moment of the cycle's end. Three concept sections, each held whole, and a closing that lets the book close as it began — with prayers for the line and the prolongation of its present Dai.

## The twenty-first, the path of Ali, and the discipline of the senses

The twenty-first of any month gathers inauspiciousness to itself. For this date, Imam Jafar al-Sadiq composed a rare insistent supplication — an exceptional one, not in the daily cycle of brief supplications, but a specifically protective one. The twenty-first is also the day on which the heirs of the line pass. The pattern of the calendar is no accident. The number aligns with the death of an heir and with the dense weight of misfortune; the supplication on that day is the calibrated answer.

A common mistake in the recitation of the Verse of the Throne should be corrected at this point. The verse — *Allah: there is no god but Him, the Ever Living, the Ever Watchful... His throne extends over the heavens and the earth; it does not weary Him to preserve them both. He is the Most High, the Tremendous* (chapter 2, verse 255, trans. M.A.S. Abdel Haleem, *The Qur'an*, Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004, p. 29) — has, in its Arabic closing, a grammatical signature. The final phrase should be read in its accusative form, *al-Aliyya al-Azim*, with the marker that grammar requires, not in the nominative *al-Aliyyu al-Azim*. The grammar matters because the verse's closing is the verse's signature. After the verse, the seven *istighfar* are recited. The seven correspond to the seven cycles of concealment; ours is the seventh manifestation of that pattern. The number is preserved at every level. The believer, by reciting seven, situates himself in the seventh.

The Quranic *indeed this is the straight path of Ali* — *inna hadha siratun alayya mustaqim* in the chapter of the Rocky Tract (chapter 15, verse 41, trans. Marmaduke Pickthall, *The Meaning of the Glorious Koran*, New York: Alfred A. Knopf, 1930, p. 184) — is the inner reading. The Father of Imams is the straight path. The opponents of the Imams and their followers are gathered, in the cosmic register, in *the tail* — the descending node — and the opponents of the prophets and the dais in *the head* — the ascending node. The souls of those opponents, when they pass, are thrown by divine providence into inauspicious peripheral places and made into animal forms or mountain-forms, with their senses kept intact so that they may feel the torment. They are bound, but they sense. This is the calibrated punishment: not annihilation, but the preservation of the sense-faculty in the very condition the soul refused to recognize. They neither live nor die, as the Quranic *neither dies therein nor lives* says of the lowest condition (chapter 87, verse 13, trans. M.A.S. Abdel Haleem, *The Qur'an*, Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004, p. 421). They are killed and revived a thousand times daily, and their senses are not blunted.

The doctrine of the calibrated punishment finds its echo in the line of the Father of Imams himself, who taught that the lowest of the punished neither perish from their suffering nor return to ease, but are held in a station designed to remain. A modern editor of the Imam's collected aphorisms records the line: *the worst of stations is the station of one whose ruin is renewed at every breath and whose ease is never restored* (Abd al-Wahid al-Amidi, *Ghurar al-Hikam wa Durar al-Kalim* — *The Brilliant Aphorisms and the Pearls of Speech* — ed. Mustafa Dirayati, Qom: Bustan-i Kitab, 2010, p. 217). The doctrine of the senses is not the author's invention; it is a doctrine the family of the House transmitted to those who could carry it.

The discipline of slaughter follows directly from the doctrine of the senses. In the slaughter of an animal, saying *Allahu Akbar* separates the sensitive soul, the *nafs al-hissiyyah*, from the body, and the meat becomes permissible. After cooking, allow the steam to escape; the remaining traces of the sensitive soul depart through the steam. This is not folklore. This is the same doctrine as the punishment of the opposers, read in the opposite direction. There, the sensitive soul is kept inside; here, it is sent out. The technique is the same — control of where the sensitive soul resides. The believer who eats the meat is eating what has been disciplined; the soul of the animal has been formally removed before the meat reaches the plate.

The Quranic *and worship your Lord until certainty comes to you* — the closing verse of the chapter of the Rocky Tract (chapter 15, verse 99, trans. M.A.S. Abdel Haleem, *The Qur'an*, Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004, p. 165) — gives a word, *certainty*, that does double duty. Certainty is death; and certainty is the Father of Imams himself, who will question you after death. The verse holds both meanings at once: worship is lifelong, and certainty is in the meeting. The believer who orients his daily acts around the verse is orienting them around a death he will meet and around a person who will receive him at it. The discipline of the senses is the discipline of preparation for that meeting.

The lecture on the slaughter at Mina and the doctrine of the four-phrase litany then opens. The slaughter at Mina elevates the meat to the upright lineage; the sensitive soul of the animal is expelled by the uttering of the basmalah. The discipline of slaughter is part of an inner pedagogy of the soul. Slaughter performed by Jews and Christians using their own ritual language is permissible; their slaughter is *halal* for us, because the act of formal removal of the sensitive soul, when it is performed in the prescribed manner, has the same effect across the close traditions.

*The prayer is the sacrifice of every pious one*. The teacher gives the prayer itself as the sacrifice. When the believer says *Allahu Akbar* at the opening of the prayer, he says it over himself — he is the offering. The prayer is not commemoration. The prayer is the act. The believer is the animal whose sensitive soul is being formally elevated by the *takbir*.

The four-phrase litany recommended by the Messenger, peace be upon him, gives the structure of that elevation. The grounding in the canonical collections is plain: *the most beloved speech to Allah is four — Subhanallah, walhamdulillah, wa la ilaha illallah, wallahu akbar; it does not matter with which of them you begin* (Muslim, *Sahih*, kitab al-adab, no. 2137, ed. and trans. Nasiruddin al-Khattab, *English Translation of Sahih Muslim*, Riyadh: Darussalam, 2007, vol. 5, p. 432). The fifth, *la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah*, is added on the warrant of the Messenger's own counsel to one of his companions: *say it often, for it is a treasure from the treasures of the garden* (al-Bukhari, *Sahih*, kitab al-da'awat, no. 6384, ed. and trans. Muhammad Muhsin Khan, *The Translation of the Meanings of Sahih al-Bukhari*, Riyadh: Darussalam, 1997, vol. 8, p. 245). Each of the four operates on a different layer of the soul. *Allahu Akbar* acts on the growing soul. *Alhamdulillah* acts on the sensitive soul. *Subhanallah* acts on the rational soul. *La ilaha illallah* is the form upon the luminous soul. Each phrase is calibrated to the level at which it works. The believer who recites the litany is moving up the ladder of his own faculties — from growth to sensation to reason to light — and the litany is the daily exercise of the ascent.

A deeper teaching follows. The rational soul absorbs the sensitive soul throughout life, and especially at death. If the sensitive soul remains in the body at death, that is a bad death; if it has been absorbed into the rational soul, that is the noble death. The discipline of a lifetime is meant to make this absorption complete. The believer who has spent his days reciting the litany has spent his days moving the sensitive into the rational; at death, there is nothing left at the lower level. The death is noble because the preparation was thorough.

The enemies of the friends of Allah, by contrast, are given forty days of respite after death to roam between the heavens and the earth, harming believers in subtle ways. Then they are pulled into the descending node. Forty days is the window of their final mischief. The believer who is alert during that window — who recites his protective formulas, who guards his thresholds — is guarding himself against a known interval. The formula recited three times in the morning and three times in the evening — *in the name of Allah, with Whose name nothing in the earth or in the heavens can harm* — fortifies the reciter against all harm. The basmalah here is the seal at the threshold.

From the refined elements of the rank-holders, three substances are formed in the world. The jewels of royal crowns. The pigeons of the House of Allah and of Madinah, whose own natural death is *halal* — exceptional among wild birds, whose ordinary death without slaughter is impermissible. And the salt used at meals. The last is not a flavoring only. The salt at the meal is the same trace as the pigeons of the House. The believer who breaks bread with salt is participating, at the smallest scale, in the same refined ascent that produced the visible glories.

At the rising of the Riser, the opposers — the named figures who refused the line — will be dragged in human form to Mina, killed there by believers, and the stench of their bodies will be carried by the wind through the four doors named for that purpose, one of which lies in Hadramaut. The justice of that day is not bloodless. The dawah, meanwhile, is presented to non-believers at the time of every death and at every change of body, until they reach the garment of tar, beyond which the dawah is no longer offered. There is a final door, and beyond it the offer is not made.

The meat of the Festival of Sacrifice and of the *aqiqah* must not be given to non-Muslims. A nursing mother in particular must not eat or drink from her child's *aqiqah*, lest the child's milk become unlawful for him. The rule guards the inner channel of nourishment. Wild animals that die their own death remain in a fallen state until *raja*. The pigeons of the House are excepted; the exception is itself a teaching about what those pigeons actually are.

At the end of each round, the souls released by the lava — those who had done some good deeds — emerge before the completion of the greater cycle through volcanoes, becoming soil, vegetation, and food for further refinement. The earth, at its eruptions, is releasing such souls into the next round of their refinement. The discipline of the senses, the discipline of slaughter, the protection of the threshold, the rule about the aqiqah — all of these sit on a cosmology in which souls are being refined at every layer, and the believer's daily acts are the visible end of an enormous slow process.

The form of every believer gathers, at his passing, at the Door of Doors — called *the swelling sea* and *the conclusive speech*. The Imam grants this gathering to his son, and through the gathering the son becomes Imam in actuality. The Quranic warrant is the verse of the Chapter of Yasin — *and everything We have set down in a clear register* (chapter 36, verse 12, trans. M.A.S. Abdel Haleem, *The Qur'an*, Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004, p. 281), which the line reads as *and We have enumerated everything in a clear Imam*, taking *imam* in its proper Quranic sense as the gathering-register rather than the modern translator's bookkeeping image. Two precise details govern the reading of that verse. First, the word *thing* here — as in *Allah has power over every thing*, *Allah knows every thing*, and *Allah encompasses every thing* — does not mean the carpets and lamps of the room; it means the believer, the form of the noble believer. Second, the verb is *We have enumerated*, not *We have gathered*. The choice is deliberate. In the act of gathering, the gathered items are jumbled together and their individuality is lost. In the act of enumeration, each form is counted in such a way that its separate nature is preserved even within the gathering. The believers' forms at the Door of Doors are therefore enumerated, not jumbled. Their individuality is retained even as they are gathered, and so they remain separable — as the Imams have demonstrated, manifesting in distinct forms drawn out of what was gathered. Had the verb been *We have gathered*, that separation would have been impossible. The verse is one word's choice away from a different doctrine.

The formula *there is no power and no strength except in Allah, the Highest, the Most Great* gives, in its closing pair, the essence of the Imams. The Imams are all veils of *the Highest, the Most Great*; the formula uttered by the believer is, in inner reading, an invocation of the line itself. And a stern principle for theology and conduct closes this stretch: Allah's favor was to form the soul from the body — the higher from the lower in its ascent. To attempt to reverse this — to insert the soul back into the body, to claim such a return — is *associative wrong of the gravest order*. Reversal is not allowed in either doctrine or in any claim of practice. The direction of the ascent is fixed: lower up, never higher down.

## The summit of beings and the doctrine of visitation

The ultimate point of perfection differs for different orders of being. The summit of stones and minerals is the ruby. The summit of animals is the elephant, for its intelligence above the others. The summit of man is the believer. The believer is the term of the human ascent. Every order of being has its peak, and the believer is the peak of the human order. The phrase *the first who believed* — *awwalu man amana* — has, by its numerical value, the value of *Ali ibn Abi Talib*. The pointer in the language is, again, the Father of Imams.

On the night of the Ascent, two unique gifts were bestowed on the Messenger that he had not had before: the Opening Chapter, and the last two verses of the Chapter of the Cow. These were given to him alone. The Encompassing Sphere, alone of all entities, ascends directly without touching the earth, and becomes the Universal Intellect at the end of the cycle. At the end of the round, the spheres and planets descend; the jewels and rubies of the earth then take their succession in service for one round of Adam, performing in the higher orders what those higher orders had earlier performed. The summit-stone moves up; the upper orders move down; the cycle works.

A figure named Abdullah ibn Thamir was a giver of glad tidings of the Messenger's coming. He died at the Trench of Najran — the place mentioned in the Chapter of the Constellations as the site of the trench in which the people of faith were burned. His name belongs to the pre-prophetic line of those who carried the news ahead.

The doctrine of visitation — of going to the graves of the rank-holders, of the prophets, of the Imams — is doctrinally rich. Visitation of the graves is a connection to the rank above the visited, for intercession at the gathering of forms. The rank-holders of the dawah were angels in potential during life and become angels in actuality after death. The Messenger said that parents feel joy when children visit their graves, just as they did when those children sat in their laps in life. Visitation is not symbolic; it is a passage of substance.

The Quranic *and seek the means to Him* — *wabtaghu ilayhi al-wasilah* in the chapter of the Table (chapter 5, verse 35, trans. M.A.S. Abdel Haleem, *The Qur'an*, Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004, p. 73) — specifies the means. It means reaching Allah through the Messenger, through the family of the House, through the dais, the proofs, or the Imam. It does not mean through the Quran. The Quran is the source of guidance; the means is the living chain through which the guidance is held. Gnosis of the Worshipped is gnosis of the rank-holders. The gnosis of Allah is inside the gnosis of the ranks. There is no shortcut around them.

The same doctrine has been the burden of the Ismaili philosophical tradition since its early formulation. One of the great Fatimid jurists, in the foundational legal compendium of the line, set the rule plainly: *no act of worship is accepted save through walaya, and no walaya is accepted save through the recognition of the imam of one's time* (Qadi al-Nu'man, *Da'a'im al-Islam* — *The Pillars of Islam* — trans. Asaf A. A. Fyzee, rev. Ismail K. Poonawala, Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002, vol. 1, p. 24). The verse and the rule align: the means is named in scripture as the channel, and named in the law as the imam of the time. The two readings reinforce one another. The Quran gives the abstract instruction; the tradition gives the address.

The Opening Chapter has seven verses and twenty-five words. The twenty-five words equal the twenty-five Quranic prophets and correspond to the twelve dais of the islands plus the twelve dais of the presences plus the Imam — twelve plus twelve plus one. The dais of the islands pair off, for which reason the *tahajjud* prayer's units are paired. The dais of the presences are collective, for which reason the night prayer has twelve consecutive units. The structures of our prayers encode the structures of our hierarchy.

Holy graves remain blessed by their trace even after the refined elements of the buried have progressed. The analogy is the empty perfume bottle in which the fragrance lingers after the contents have gone. The body's place is fragrant with the trace. If the visitor at a grave is of higher rank than the buried, the flow of aids reverses: it descends from the visitor to the buried. The ordinary direction of intercession is from buried to visitor; in the reversed case, the visitor's higher rank causes the descent to go downward. The doctrine handles the rare cases by extending the rule, not by exception.

The lecture on the Mountain of Arafat opens the next stretch of the doctrine. Fatimah corresponds to the Plain of Muzdalifah; the Father of Imams to Mina; the Messenger to Arafat. These three are the inner core of the era of concealment. The geography of the Hajj is the geography of the line itself. On the Day of Standing at Arafat, the camel of the Messenger would sit down under the weight of descending revelation. On the Mountain of Arafat, luminous forms manifested that had never occurred to a human heart; the intensity was the more profound because of what was unfolding regarding the declaration at the Pool of Khumm.

Cutting trees in the area of Arafat is forbidden because they neighbor the hidden treasures of the inner reality. Whoever broadcasts the secrets of the dawah has prepared his seat in the fire and is disavowed by the Mighty, the Forgiving. The discipline of concealment is enforced by the gravest threat. The line is held by silence as much as by speech.

A long formal designation of one of the great dais on his successor was written using twenty-eight titles, corresponding to the twenty-eight letters of the testimony of faith. The structure is exact. After this designation, the doctrine was settled by another master: anyone reciting the testimony must also bear witness for the designated successor. The structure of belief was widened by the structure of the designation.

The dai then designated was at the station of *nasut* for his Imam and at the station of *lahut* for his fellow believers. Two stations in two directions, simultaneously. He was given the title *Amin al-Mutaman* — the trusted of the trusted — by the dai who designated him. The Dai of Yemen is superior to all dais of other islands and does not lead prayer outside the three islands of Yemen, Hind, and Sindh. The officer of Yemen, Najran, is superior to all officers because he is the Dai's successor in potential. The hierarchy is geographic as well as functional.

When the act of giving and the act of receiving occur in a gathering, the souls of the deceased believers attend. The questioner in such a gathering asks through the tongue of a higher rank-holder. A master once sent a Bohra woman believed to be in a state of possession to one of the great teachers, to instruct him to conduct the lessons in the mosque, so that the departed forms could hear. The teaching is not only for the living in the room. At one of the great dais' gatherings, the previously designated dai would visit invisibly; the present dai would stand and greet him as one greets a present visitor. The visit was not metaphor. Another of the great dais, while at *tawaf*, saw the souls of three previous masters performing *tawaf* at very high speed; the sight prompted him to cry out *Oh, the longing*. And the same man in Najran kissed the hand of a sudden visitor who vanished — believed to have been the Father of Imams himself, manifesting briefly. The visitation runs in both directions. The visible visits the invisible at the grave; the invisible visits the visible in the gathering.

A further refinement carries the doctrine to its second station of the Dai. One of the great dais of the line was given, beyond his island form, a form in Hadramaut by the name *Sulayman-i Humam* — an unprecedented arrangement among dais. Another was under house arrest in Taif and would come, in the inner ascent, to the House of Allah, performing seven *tawaf* daily. The outer body sat in detention; the inner form circled the House. The aide who recorded the formal designation of one of the great dais wrote it down three days before the master's passing, in 970 of the Hijri calendar. Today's Imam, the thirty-fifth of the line, is the seventh of his week of seven, and combines two such weeks, possessing fourteen Imams in himself; his Dai's station rises with him. The line is not flat; it gathers as it advances.

The believer who attends to all of this — who recites the litany, observes the discipline of slaughter, visits the graves with the right intention, listens carefully to the call to prayer and to the soft tone at its end — is the believer who is being shaped, daily, into the human creature whose summit the doctrine has just named. The doctrine of visitation is not separable from the doctrine of the summit. They are two sides of the same teaching about what the believer actually is: a form whose ascent the entire cosmology supports, and whose visits to the graves are the gathering of the trace that itself was once a form. The empty perfume bottle remembers its perfume; the believer remembers his rank; the Imam at his passing dissolves into the House, and the House remembers him, and from that remembering the next Imam is formed.

## The two Mehdis, the rolling of the heaven, and the closing of the book

There are two Mehdis. The first is the eleventh Imam, called Mehdi. The second is the fiftieth Imam Mehdi — the Mehdi of the End of Time — who is outside the circle of Imamate, the *Mim of the latter end*. They are two different personalities. The Riser of the era of concealment was, properly speaking, called Mehdi — and the Mehdi of today's line of fifty and the Riser are two distinct personalities, though the Riser of the era of concealment was also called Mehdi. One of the early masters taught: *the Mehdi is the Riser and the Riser is the Mehdi*. The teaching holds at the level at which the two functions overlap. The Mehdi is the Riser of the era of concealment; he is the confluence of eight chapters corresponding to the eight angels carrying the throne. The structural number here is eight. The Riser takes the succession of the tenth on his departure from this abode and remains *qaim* — standing in office — for only three days. The duration of the office in this dispensation is short.

The straight path refers to the line of the partial dais in which one stands. The believer's straight path is the chain that runs from him through the lower and higher ranks to the Door of Doors. The path is not abstract; it is his own chain. Spiritual progress is of two types: one is striven for, and the other is obtained by sitting in someone's lap. The teaching child receives the second by transfer; the seeker earns the first by labor. Both kinds of progress are real, and a single life can carry both. In a single round, there are 360 manifestations of the line's integration. The human body contains 360 souls; of these, one part progresses per round. The body's structure encodes the rate. In the Chapter of Yasin, the Quranic *I am on a straight path* refers, in the inner reading, to Abu Talib being on the straight path of the partial dais. The verse, on its face about the speaker, is about the chain.

The lecture on the cycles then opens the final structural piece of the book. The Quranic verse — *the Day We shall roll up the heavens as a scroll is rolled up for writings; as We brought into being the first creation, so We will bring it back again; this is a promise binding on Us, and We shall act on it* (chapter 21, verse 104, trans. M.A.S. Abdel Haleem, *The Qur'an*, Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004, p. 209) — describes the end of the round, when the bonds that hold the order in place terminate. After each end-of-round, the earth is changed: length becomes width, north becomes south. The clause *as We brought into being the first creation, so We will bring it back again* is applied with literal reorganization. The era of concealment and the era of unveiling succeed each other like night and day until the great cycle is completed. One great cycle contains 400,000 rounds multiplied by 400,000 rounds. One great cycle has passed, and nine remain. The accounting given to Umm al-Nada — one great cycle gone — can now be located in this larger frame.

At each end-of-great-cycle, the tenth's place is taken by the ninth intellect; all ascending forms gather in the domain of the First Emanator. In the end, all are absorbed into the First Emanator; only the domain of the First Intellect and the First Emanator remain. The horizon is settled. The great cycle has its term. The doctrine of return, the discipline of the senses, the visitation of the graves, the litany of the four phrases — all of these are exercises within a frame that is itself headed toward absorption into the First.

A short stretch of practical rulings now follows, set down as a single section for clarity since they are recurrent. The Quranic command *when you recite the Quran, seek refuge in Allah from Satan, the accursed* — given in the chapter of the Bee (chapter 16, verse 98, trans. M.A.S. Abdel Haleem, *The Qur'an*, Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004, p. 172) — is acted on as the taking of refuge. Why is the Book of Allah, the source of guidance, surrounded by a refuge? Because it is precisely through misinterpretation of the Quran that communities have been led astray. One dai held up this Book and said: *this is the Book of Allah by which communities have been misguided*. The refuge is therefore against the misappropriations that begin with taking up the text. The taking of refuge should not be done during the call to prayer; only the basmalah is said there. And in the call to prayer, the last *no god but Allah* is recited softly, in a different tone from the first. The reason is the trembling of a particular being whose departure from his whole circle will happen in the blink of an eye — the last counselor, whose passing the soft tone alludes to. The tone must therefore not be elongated.

A subtlety governs the closing of the call to prayer. The verse — *say: praise belongs to God who has not taken a son and has no partner in His kingdom; nor does He need any protector from weakness; proclaim His infinite greatness* (chapter 17, verse 111, trans. M.A.S. Abdel Haleem, *The Qur'an*, Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004, p. 184) — the last verse of the Chapter of the Children of Israel and called the Verse of Honor, is what the practice of the *takbira* refers to. After this verse, blessings on the Messenger may be recited; some hold that the basmalah should also be said before the *takbira*. The Imam, when asked about the order, said that praise is the higher matter and should come first; blessings come after. This is the order he gave. The Quranic *do not be tight-fisted, nor so open-handed that you end up blamed and overwhelmed* — the counsel given in the chapter of the Children of Israel (chapter 17, verse 29, trans. M.A.S. Abdel Haleem, *The Qur'an*, Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004, p. 178) — and the parallel verse in the Chapter of the Criterion — *those who are neither wasteful nor niggardly when they spend, but keep to a just balance* (chapter 25, verse 67, trans. M.A.S. Abdel Haleem, *The Qur'an*, Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004, p. 232) — give us the middle path of generosity for the ordinary believer. The Messenger's own state of giving everything in charity is not contradicted; that state belongs to the friends of Allah and the infallible. The Father of Imams, when a wealthy man's two sons came to him — the elder living a prosperous life, the younger imitating the Father of Imams's harsh asceticism — said to the father that the younger should not take the Father of Imams's life as his example, that he should benefit from the blessings Allah had given. The middle path is for us. The Messenger's supplication *everything remains except this*, said over the single leg of a goat that remained after his charity when his wife said *nothing remains except this* — is the famous riposte. What is given is preserved; what is held back is lost.

Two observations frame the closing rulings together. First, the practical rulings are not appended at random. Each ruling preserves the inner channel that the cosmology has been building. The refuge before recitation guards against the misappropriation of the very text the believer is opening. The soft last *no god but Allah* at the end of the call to prayer marks a particular being whose departure happens in the blink of an eye. The middle path of generosity preserves the ordinary believer's capacity to live, while the total giving of the Messenger is reserved for those who can sustain it. The rule about the meat of the *aqiqah* guards the infant's milk. Each ruling is a fence around a doctrine. Second, the order in which the rulings appear matters. Praise first, then blessings, then the call to prayer's closing — because praise is the higher matter, as the Imam said when asked about the order. The rulings end on a riposte: *everything remains except this*. The given remains; the held-back is lost. The teaching converts the household scene of the goat's leg into a cosmological principle about charity and preservation.

## What this chapter sets down

The doctrine has been carried across its remaining ground. The twenty-first of the month gathers misfortune; the rare supplication of Imam Jafar al-Sadiq meets it. The discipline of slaughter and the litany of the four phrases together teach the believer how to move the sensitive soul up the ladder of his faculties so that at death there is nothing left at the lower level. The doctrine of visitation extends the same logic to the graves and to the gatherings of the living. The summit of stones is the ruby; the summit of animals is the elephant; the summit of man is the believer. The two Mehdis are two persons. The great cycle contains 400,000 rounds multiplied by 400,000 rounds; one has passed, nine remain. The practical rulings — the refuge before recitation, the soft tone at the end of the call to prayer, the middle path of generosity, the rule about the meat of the *aqiqah* — are the daily edges of the same cosmology.

Read together, the three concept sections of this stretch trace a single arc. The first opens with the discipline of the senses at the moment of slaughter and at the moment of the litany — both of which are exercises in moving the sensitive soul out of the lower level and into the higher. The second opens with the summit of beings and the doctrine of visitation — extending the same act of refinement to the graves, the gatherings, and the geographic shape of the Hajj. The third opens with the two Mehdis and closes with the rolling of the heaven — extending the act of refinement to the entire cosmic frame, in which the era of concealment and the era of unveiling alternate like night and day, and the great cycle proceeds toward its absorption into the First. The believer's daily acts and the cycle's final closure are two ends of the same line. The doctrine of the book is, in the end, that line, and the line is preserved by the discipline that the lectures have set out across every level.

## Closing

The book closes as it began, with prayers for the dais across the ages and for the present Dai of the line. The Opening Chapter is recited; the names of the dais from the line are invoked; the Imam of the time is named with all his ranks and the prolongation of his life is asked. The author prays that whoever reads these treasures may not read them save by the permission of one who has authority of advancement and postponement, and that the discipline of concealment may be guarded; and that whoever reads with permission may carry away what is correct, attributing the correctness to the Dai who caused the tongue to flow, and whatever error is in the book is attributed to the author himself — *for he is the sinner*. These are the *Stored Treasures of Wisdom*, gathered for the masters of partial infallibility, that from them the line of the summons may continue to draw forth substance and light, until the Day of Resurrection, peace upon them all, forever and ever.

So the book ends as it opened. It opened with prayers for the present Dai and his line; it closes with prayers for the present Dai and his line. The middle has been filled with the doctrine — the families of light and the named figures of the early line, the era of concealment and the discipline of silence, the two foundational questions on the modality of emanation and the soul of the Imam, the third great teaching of the heart beneath the House and the eternal numbers, and the long string of lectures that fill in what living on that floor looks like. The author asks that the reader carry away only what is correct, attributing the correctness to the Dai who caused the tongue to flow, and that whatever error appears in the book be attributed to the author. The closing is not modesty alone. It is doctrinal. The line is the source; the speaker is a vessel; the listener carries on what was given. The treasures are stored for those who will need them, and the line continues to draw.
