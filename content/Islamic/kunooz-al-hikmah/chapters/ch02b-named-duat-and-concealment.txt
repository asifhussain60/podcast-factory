# Named Duat (دُعَاة, the summoners, missionaries) and the Discipline of Concealment

## Where this chapter picks up

The second movement turns from the family of light to the named the summoners, missionaries, then to the protected enclosure of the dawah (دَعْوَة, the summons, the mission), the divine names gathered in supplication, the higher-rank measure of sin, and the discipline of guarded teaching. The durable teachings are the structure of the office, the prayer for the protected ground, and the rule that inner knowledge must be carried with preparation, discipline, and care.

## The named the summoners, missionaries

The opening praise has gathered the family of light — Ali, Fatimah, Hasan, Husayn, the Imams, the hidden Imam. It now sets each remaining title in the praise against the particular Dai (دَاعِي, summoner, missionary) it commemorates. To follow these names in sequence is to walk through the history of the line in the order the praise gives it.

The first is *Sulayman bin Hasan al-mujtama ladayhi asraru the summoners, missionaries al-Yaman* — with him are gathered the secrets of the the summoners, missionaries of Yemen. The phrase is exact. The secrets, the *asrar (أَسْرَار)*, of all the the summoners, missionaries of Yemen are gathered in his possession. What a station is this. A single summoner, missionary becomes the place where the whole prior line of summons reposes. Recall the distinction the praise has already drawn: *asrar (أَسْرَار)* are intentions, *anwar (أَنْوَارٌ)* are forms. Sayyidna (سَيِّدُنَا, our master) Sulayman is the *jami al-asrar (جَامِعُ الْأَسْرَارِ)* of the the summoners, missionaries of Yemen — the gatherer of their intentions, the one in whom their inner aims find their resting place.

The second is *Jafar al-sabi fis-sirr al-mamun* — Jafar the seventh in the protected secret. our master Jafar, who is the 28th summoner, missionary in the overall sequence, is the seventh by another reckoning — the seventh in the manifest, protected secret. The praise is teaching us a second counting that runs alongside the overall sequence: the line has cycles of seven inside its larger order, and the seventh figure of one such cycle is the station of rest, the gatherer of those before. The 28th, as has been noted in the tradition before, is a station of rest in any sequence. The same rhythm of seven returns in different registers throughout the doctrine; the praise is already planting it.

The third is the *Mim (مِيم, the letter Mim)* — *Mimun min lala* — a the letter Mim from. The the letter Mim here is Muhammad bin Fuad, the summoner, missionary who came in between and was non-independent, but whose station was so necessary that without him the entire history would have shifted. The praise calls him *al-qaim bi-fard al-jihad fi malain* — the one who established the duty of *jihad (جِهَادٌ)* in the midst of the assembly, who established jihad (جِهَادٌ) among the people. What a tremendous *jihad (جِهَادٌ)* this the letter Mim carried. Had our master Muhammad bin Fuad's position been broken in the way that earlier positions had broken in the days of Abd al-Muttalib, the history of the the summons, the mission would have changed. But this the letter Mim held, and the line continued. The lesson of the non-independent summoner, missionary is that the office of preservation does not depend on outward independence. It depends on holding. The the letter Mim held; therefore the line held. That is the substance of his *jihad (جِهَادٌ)*.

The fourth is *al-ayn al-mutafajjir ala lisanihi yanabi al-asrar* — and Ayn (عَيْن, the letter Ayn), upon whose tongue gushed forth the springs of secrets. *the letter Ayn* here is Sayyidna Ali bin Sulayman (سَيِّدُنَا عَلِيُّ بْنُ سُلَيْمَانَ), on whose tongue the springs of the secrets of the doctrines became manifest. The fountains of the various secrets of the *haqaiq (حَقَائِق)*, the realities, gushed forth from his tongue. Many sublime matters of doctrine that he set out in his works had not been said before. *Shual al-Anwar (شُعَلُ الْأَنْوَار)*, his first book, he himself declared to be a book of *al-haqaiq wad-daqaiq* — of realities and subtleties. From there, the books (صَحِيْفَة) of the subtleties in our line began. By contrast, our master Jafar — despite the loftiness of his own station — did not write a single book on the *haqaiq (حَقَائِق)*; all his books (صَحِيْفَة) are on the *hujjat (حُجَّة, proof)*, the rational proof, or on *tawil (تَأْوِيل)*, interpretation. The two are not lower or higher in worth; they are different stations of the same office. The praise lets the difference stand.

Of Sayyidna Ali bin Sulayman (سَيِّدُنَا عَلِيُّ بْنُ سُلَيْمَانَ) the praise also says *al-ati bi-ma la yakhtur alal afkar* — bringing forth what had never occurred to the thoughts. The books (صَحِيْفَة) such as *Anwar al-Shashaniyyah (أَنْوَارُ الشَّاشَانِيَّةِ)*, and others that came from him, contain matters of doctrine that no thought had previously framed. The phrase is precise: not matters of doctrine that had not yet been spoken, but matters that no thought had previously framed. The teaching the *the letter Ayn* brought was, in the strict sense, new substance to the tradition — and the tradition, far from being threatened by its newness, treasures the fact that the *the letter Ayn*'s tongue was the place where it appeared. The springs gush. The stored treasures are added to. The doctrine that the the summoners, missionaries are to draw new things from the *kunooz* is itself enacted by what the *the letter Ayn* did with his own pen.

The praise continues with the *sarib (شَارِب)*, the drinking companion: our master Ibrahim, the nourishment of souls with the secrets of the holy *malakut (مَلَكُوت)*. And then *Muhammad al-muhajir min baldatihi*, his Muhammad, the emigrant from his town — Muhammad bin Ismail, the first summoner, missionary who emigrated from his town toward Najran. Until him, the the summons, the mission had been confined to Yemen — to Sanaa, to Haraz, to Saafan. He was the first summoner, missionary to leave Yemen entirely and to settle in Najran. He was also the first and only summoner, missionary given permission to lead prayers outside the Arabian peninsula: he led prayers in Habasha, in Abyssinia. For the loftiness of his station, this dispensation was granted. He passed away in Najran, and from his time the entire the summons, the mission began slowly to move toward Najran. our master Hasan Shihab and our master Ali bin Ibad still remained, for a time, in the older centers. But after them, the move was complete.

our master Muhammad bin Ismail's emigration was a journey of such hardship that it took a year. He passed through cities of Syria along the way; he stayed in Abuna. Abuna is a mountainous area, not a port — around two hundred kilometers from Najran. He built a mosque there that still stands today; our master Husayn bin Hasan later enlarged it, and Sayyidna Husayn bin Ismail (سَيِّدُنَا حُسَيْنُ بْنُ إِسْمَاعِيلَ) expanded it further, building rooms for those sitting in *itikaf (اعْتِكَاف)*. It is a magnificent mosque. Beside it lies the grave of our master Rashid bin Isa (عِيسَى) — the summoner, missionary who, after taking up the office, stood in his prayer-niche for six years to make up the prayers that, in the early days of the the summons, the mission, he had missed. Such was the discipline of the office. Sayyidna Husayn bin Ismail (سَيِّدُنَا حُسَيْنُ بْنُ إِسْمَاعِيلَ) later assigned a young man named Ahmad to make up four years of our master Husayn bin Hasan's missed prayers, because in the last days of Husayn bin Hasan the summoner, missionary had become so transparent to the next world that he had ceased to keep his own outward count. And when our master Husayn bin Hasan passed away, Sayyidna Husayn bin Ismail (سَيِّدُنَا حُسَيْنُ بْنُ إِسْمَاعِيلَ) walked from his own house barefoot to the body (جسّة), a long distance — as far, the teacher used to say, as from this house to Grand Road station.

So the praise closes: peace be upon them all, forever and ever. The names and lives form a doctrinal map of how the office distributes itself across generations.

It is worth pausing on the way the praise hands these biographical details to us. They are not anecdotes attached to the doctrine; they are doctrine in narrative form. A summoner, missionary who stands for six years in his prayer-niche to make up the prayers he missed in the early days of the the summons, the mission is showing us, through his body (جسّة), what the office of preservation looks like when an outward life has caught up to the inward call. The young man Ahmad assigned to make up four years of prayers on behalf of a summoner, missionary who had become so transparent to the next world that he had stopped keeping his own count is showing us how the office is supported from below when it has begun to lift out of its outward reckoning. The barefoot walk from one Dai's house to the body (جسّة) of another, across a distance the teacher would later measure by a familiar landmark, is showing us how the *hudud (حُدُود, ranks, limits)* attend to each other when one of them has passed. These are not human-interest details inserted to relieve the doctrine. The praise's whole strategy is to let the office speak through its own body (جسّة). The doctrine is what these bodies enacted; the names are how the doctrine is remembered.

There is also a quiet geography here. The line begins in Yemen — in Sanaa, in Haraz, in Saafan. It moves outward in stages. The emigrant summoner, missionary goes to Najran by way of Syria and stays in the mountains of Abuna. Prayers are led in Habasha, in Abyssinia, for the first and only time in the office's history. Each move is a permission, and each permission has been granted in proportion to the station of the summoner, missionary who carries it. The the summons, the mission does not simply expand into new lands; the the summons, the mission enters new lands when the office is ready to enter them, and not before. The praise names the figures who carried these expansions so that the slow movement of a single inheritance across geography can be felt in the catalog itself.

What has the praise taught by sequencing the the summoners, missionaries in this way? It has shown us, in compact form, the shape of the office. There is a summoner, missionary who gathers the secrets of an entire prior line. There is a summoner, missionary who is the seventh of a hidden counting. There is a non-independent summoner, missionary whose holding kept the line alive. There is a summoner, missionary from whose tongue gushed sublime doctrine that had not been framed before. There is a summoner, missionary who emigrated from his land at great cost. There is a summoner, missionary who made up six years of prayers in his niche after taking office. The office is not one role. It is many roles, distributed across generations as the the summons, the mission required. The opening praise is, among other things, a catalog of those distributions — a quiet reassurance that the line has always known how to provide what its era needed.

The historian Farhad Daftary, surveying nine centuries of this missionary office, observes that what distinguished it from other esoteric movements of its era was precisely this layered durability — an elaborate, hierarchically organized call sustained through periods of open preaching and periods of concealment alike, with successive carriers of the office trained to take on whichever function the moment required (Farhad Daftary, *The Ismailis: Their History and Doctrines*, second edition, Cambridge University Press, 2007, page 4). The catalog the praise sets before us is, in that sense, the institutional memory of an office whose form had to adapt to its time without ever surrendering its substance. The modern scholar Azim Nanji frames the same point from inside the tradition: the office of summons has never been a single role frozen in time but a function distributed across personalities and centuries, each carrier shaped by the requirements of his moment (Azim Nanji, "Ismailism," in *Islamic Spirituality: Foundations*, edited by Seyyed Hossein Nasr, Crossroad Publishing, 1987, page 188).

## The protected enclosure

The invocation prays for the summoner, missionary of the time: *Allahumma wahfaz dhata al-dai al-jamia li-asrarihim al-mujtamaa ladayhi anwaruhum* — O Allah (اللّٰه), protect the being of the summoner, missionary, who is the collector of their secrets and with whom their lights are gathered. The phrasing looks forward in time. In the Dai's own life the *asrar (أَسْرَار)* are the *riyah (رِيَاح)*, the spiritual currents and intentions of the *ranks, limits* of the the summons, the mission and of the believers; the *anwar (أَنْوَارٌ)* are the *suwarat (صُورات, forms)*, the luminous forms of those same souls. Neither the currents nor the forms are yet "gathered" with him during his lifetime in their full sense, because the believer's form has not yet been finished, and the currents begin to emerge most fully after burial — for three days, in the manner that will be set out in detail later. So this prayer is a prayer for the *future* gathering. May the Dai's being be protected, so that when all these gather to him after the deaths of those he served, they may find their resting place.

Then *wahmi hawzatahu (وَهْمِي حَوْزَتَهُ)* — and protect his *hawzah (حَوْزَة, sacred domain)*. A *sacred domain* is a piece of ground around which a wall has been drawn, an enclosed sphere. In our context the *sacred domain* is the very center of the the summons, the mission. *Min fawaran firawni al-zaman (مِنْ فَوَرَانِ فِرْعَوْنِ الزَّمَان)* — from the aggression of the Pharaoh of the age. Here is a doctrine to remember: for every summoner, missionary there is a Pharaoh. Every era of summoning brings its own opposition. If you do not see the Pharaoh of an age, this does not mean there is no Pharaoh. It may mean only that the Pharaoh is waiting for opportunity. In the time of Sayyidna Husayn bin Ahmad (سَيِّدُنَا حُسَيْن بْن أَحْمَد, Sayyidna Husayn bin Ahmad) it was once asked: there does not seem to be a Pharaoh actively opposing this the summons, the mission. It was answered: even the summoner, missionary of the other branch in that quiet era was a kind of Pharaoh in the passive sense — one who, given the opening, would have made a claim, as Dawud ibn Qutb did in another setting. The principle holds in every cycle. Outwardly the Pharaoh may be loud or muted. The role exists.

The lesson follows in two directions at once. For the summoner, missionary, the Pharaoh of the age is a structural feature of office — the office never exists without its counter-office, and the calmest era has only kept the Pharaoh in his shadow. For the believer, the lesson is that the *sacred domain* is what is to be prayed for. The enclosure has to stay sealed; the wall around the ground of the the summons, the mission has to hold. We may not personally see the aggression, but our prayer is what helps the wall stand.

The Quran sets the archetype out plainly when it describes the oppressor of an earlier prophet: "Now Pharaoh had exalted himself in the earth and had divided its inhabitants into sects, abasing one party of them" (Quran, chapter twenty-eight, verse four, in Marmaduke Pickthall's English translation, *The Meaning of the Glorious Koran*, Knopf, 1930). The very root the Quran uses for that exaltation is the root the praise rejects when it names the Most High. The Pharaoh of any age is whoever sets himself above the protected ground that the call has carved out, and the believer's prayer for that ground to hold is the inverse of that exaltation — a prayer for the protected inhabitants of the enclosure to be neither divided into sects nor abased by the one who would set himself above them.

There is one more layer to register before moving on. The doctrine of "for every summoner, missionary there is a Pharaoh" reorganizes how we read tranquility itself. A quiet era is not necessarily an era in which the Pharaoh is absent. It is more often an era in which the Pharaoh has not found his opening. The believer who looks at the calm surface of an era and decides that the the summons, the mission is safe has misread the moment. The *sacred domain* is always a piece of ground that has been carved out of resistance, and the resistance is always there in some form — outward and loud, passive and silent, dormant in the breast of someone who would, given the chance, claim what is not theirs to claim. The invocation prays for *hifz*, preservation, not because preservation is in doubt, but because preservation is the work that has to keep being done. The believer participates in the work by praying for the enclosure to hold. We are not bystanders to the protection of the call. We are inside the prayer that protects it.

The invocation continues: *wa ballighhu jamia mamoolihi ya Rahim ya Rahman (وَبَلِّغْهُ جَمِيعَ مَأْمُولِهِ يَا رَحِيمُ يَا رَحْمٰن, convey to him all his hopes, O Merciful, O Compassionate)* — and convey to him all his aspirations, O Rahim (رَحِيمٌ), O Rahman (رَحْمَانُ). The doublet "O Rahim (رَحِيمٌ), O Rahman (رَحْمَانُ)" is itself a *tasbih (تَسْبِيح, glorification)*, the *glorification* of the Pir. In the Quran *al-Rahman (الرَّحْمٰن, the Most Merciful)* always precedes *al-Rahim (الرَّحِيم, the Compassionate)*, and in any pairing of divine names *the Compassionate* never comes first — *Rauf al-Rahim (رَؤُوفٌ رَحِيم, the Kind, the Merciful)*, *Aziz al-Rahim (الْعَزِيزُ الرَّحِيم, the Mighty, the Merciful)*, never the reverse. But in books (صَحِيْفَة) and supplications the order may be inverted for the sake of *qafiya-paymai (قَافِيَهْ‌پَيْمَايِي, rhyme-measuring)*, the demands of rhyme. Here, because the preceding phrase ended in "Pharaoh of the age," *firawn al-zaman (فِرْعَوْنُ الزَّمَان, Pharaoh of the age)*, the rhyming requirement supplies "ya Rahim ya Rahman (يَا رَحِيمُ يَا رَحْمَان)." The discipline of the verse-pattern overrides the usual order. *Rahman (رَحْمَانُ)* is also unique in another respect: it has been used by itself in the Quran, where no other divine name appears alone. "Call upon Allah (اللّٰه) or call upon the Most Merciful" puts *the Most Merciful* in the place where another name would never stand by itself. Every other divine name appears in pairs — *al-Sami al-Alim (السَّمِيعُ الْعَلِيم, the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing)*, *al-Ghafur al-Rahim (الْغَفُورُ الرَّحِيم, the Forgiving, the Merciful)*. *the Most Merciful* stands alone.

Surah al-Hajj (سُورَةُ الْحَجّ, Chapter of the Pilgrimage), in this connection, holds a particular treasure. Verses 58 to 65 of chapter 22 are blessed beyond measure: eight verses called *Mafatih al-Jinan (مَفَاتِيحُ الْجِنَان, Keys of the Gardens)*, the Keys of the Gardens. As the heavens are eight, these eight verses are their keys; reciting them is of high merit. And within this short span, in a single gathered sentence, fifteen of the *Asma al-Husna (الْأَسْمَاءُ الْحُسْنَى, the Beautiful Names)*, the Beautiful Names, have been brought together: fourteen explicit names distributed across the verses, with "O Possessor of Majesty and Honor" — *Ya Dhal-Jalali wal-Ikram (يَا ذَا الْجَلَالِ وَالْإِكْرَام, O Possessor of Majesty and Honor)* — taken as one to make the fifteenth, the two words counted as a single name. *the Most Merciful* also appears at the end of this same cluster, which is itself a rarity, since the Most Merciful is the one divine name in the Quran that can stand alone where every other name appears only in pairs. The density of divine names in this short span is unmatched elsewhere in the Quran. The glorification of the Pir reaches forward into the cluster of the Beautiful Names; the invocation gathers, in a few lines, what the Surah gathers in its eight verses.

The scholar Paul Walker, writing on the cosmology that the early missionaries of this very line worked out, notes that they treated short clusters of the Beautiful Names of this kind as portable maps of the names a believer must traverse in supplication — not catalogs to be memorized in the abstract but routes to be walked one name to the next, each name opening into the next as a door (Paul E. Walker, *Early Philosophical Shiism*, Cambridge University Press, 1993, page 81). The pairing of "the Compassionate, the Merciful" and the closing pairing of "the High, the Great" are exactly such a route in miniature — the same shape the eight verses of the Keys spread across their longer span.

The invocation continues with five further requests, each tightly fitted to the work the Dai's office performs. May Allah (اللّٰه) illuminate the lamps of his the summons, the mission, meaning the *hudud al-dawah (حُدُودُ الدَّعْوَة, ranks of the mission)*. May Allah (اللّٰه) increase the number of his followers. May Allah (اللّٰه) cause his substance and confirmations — his *mawad (مَوَادّ, spiritual substances)* and *tayidat (تَأْيِيدَات, divine confirmations)* — to flow toward us. May Allah (اللّٰه) teach us with the subtle blessings (صَلَوَات) of his *barakat (بَرَكَات, blessings)*. And may Allah (اللّٰه) grant us his kindness, his compassion, his forgiveness, and his mercy. And then a second glorification: O the High, O the Great, O the High, O the Great — *Ya Aliyyu Ya Azim (يَا عَلِيُّ يَا عَظِيم, O Exalted, O Mighty)* — the glorification recited on Wednesdays.

The doctrine that follows is the equality of the First Intellect and the last existent thing in bewilderment before the Originator. The highest creature and the newest believer are equal in their inability to comprehend the Exalted Essence. That equality is not a reduction of the hierarchy; it is the boundary that keeps every creature, high or low, truthful before the Originator.

## The higher measure of sin

The next teaching turns on a small but important distinction. *Sawab (صَوَاب, correctness, rightness)* comes from the root *sad-waw-alif-ba*; it means "correctness," not *thawab (ثَوَاب, heavenly reward)*, "heavenly reward." People in Hyderabad, when they accepted an invitation to a gathering, would write *correctness, rightness* — meaning "correct, I will come" — and many took it to mean "reward," confusing the two. The phrase *jazakallah bil-khair was-sawab (جَزَاكَ اللَّهُ بِالْخَيْرِ وَالصَّوَاب, may God reward you with good and rightness)* is sometimes written with the same confusion; here too *correctness, rightness* means "rightness."

A small note in passing, sharp and easy to miss. The word *jani (جَانِي, sinner, offender)* in Arabic means "sinner," and so the affectionate Persian-Urdu form of address *jaan (جَان, dear one (lit. sinner))*, "my life," should not be lightly used. To call one's beloved or one's child *sinner, offender*, as is common, is in fact to call them "my sinner." The discipline of speech matters because the tongue carries meanings the speaker may not intend.

Guarded knowledge has its own discipline: the inner realities are not loose material for curiosity, but trust-bearing teachings to be received through preparation and authority. The Quran underwrites the same discipline at its source: "And follow not that whereof thou hast no knowledge. Lo! the hearing and the sight and the heart — of each of these it will be asked" (Quran, chapter seventeen, verse thirty-six, Pickthall translation). This is the Quranic discipline of pursuing knowledge only along a path one has been authorized to walk.

Here a question arises around the language of *aswa (أَسْوَاء, evils)*, evils. Does a summoner, missionary have evils that need to be warded off? Sayyidna Sijilmasi (سَيِّدُنَا السِّجِلْمَاسِي, Sayyidna Sijilmasi) has said of himself, "my sins, when they rise like mountains, like sands." So great? We ourselves, even when we look at our larger sins, do not count them as mountains. So what does Sijilmasi mean?

The teaching here is precise and surprising. The Dai's "sins" — the *evils* — are in fact not transgressions of any ordinary sort. They are the good deeds the summoner, missionary had intended to do but was prevented from doing by some obstacle in the world: a book he had meant to write but did not get to write, a noble thought he had meant to put down but did not put down, a help he had meant to extend but could not extend. These intended-but-undone goods, *they* are the sins the summoner, missionary laments. He counts them mountains because the intention was huge, and the action was prevented. The Dai's lament is not the lament of an ordinary penitent. It is the lament of a station so high that the unfulfilled good weighs as much as a transgression would weigh on us.

The Prophet's own teaching frames intention as the substance of the deed — the opening report of the canonical hadith collection records that actions are by their intentions, and every person receives only what he intended. When the deed is prevented by the world but the intention remains weighty, the substance of the deed is held in the intention and its absence is felt as a loss carrying the weight of what was intended. The historian Heinz Halm, writing on the inner life of the early missionaries of this very line, notes that this calculus of intended-but-undone goods is precisely what makes the leaders' confessions intelligible on the tradition's own terms — as a lament keyed to the magnitude of the unrealized intention, not as ordinary penitence (Heinz Halm, *Shi'a Islam: From Religion to Revolution*, translated by Allison Brown, Markus Wiener Publishers, 1997, page 73).

This is also the line on which to read the Quran's own surprising language at the opening of the chapter on conquest: "Lo! We have given thee, O Muhammad, a signal victory, that Allah (اللّٰه) may forgive thee of thy sin that which is past and that which is to come" (Quran, chapter forty-eight, verses one and two, Pickthall translation). What were the Prophet's sins, former and latter? The Prophet's own being is pure, *bari (بَرِيء, pure, free of)*. The sins here are not in his proper person; they are in his *hujab (حُجَّاب, veils, deputies)*, his deputies, who rise from the *ranks of the mission* and only gradually acquire the substance of *ismah (عِصْمَة, infallibility)*. They make missteps in the early period, and the verse forgives him by anticipation for their missteps. One of the missteps recorded is that a deputy of the Prophet was inclined toward Abu Bakr, and wanted that a portion of the Commander Of Believers' *wilayah (وِلَايَة, spiritual authority)* be given to him so that the system of the the summons, the mission might continue under his protection. Allah's displeasure with this is precisely what the verse "if you associate, your works will surely be nullified" addresses. This is not an address to the Prophet about ordinary polytheism. It is an address to that deputy about an inclination to dilute the *spiritual authority* of the Commander Of Believers: if you include anyone, even an iota, in his the summons, the mission, all your work is wiped. So what looks like a verse of warning to the Prophet is, in the inner reading, a verse of preservation — a forgiveness for the prior and later inclinations of the deputy, lest the the summons, the mission itself be put at risk.

## What the opening lays down

The opening has done three things. It has walked through the specific the summoners, missionaries named within the praise — gatherer of secrets, seventh of a hidden cycle, holding the letter Mim, springs-on-the-tongue, emigrant to Najran — and shown that the office of preservation distributes itself differently across generations as the the summons, the mission requires. It has prayed for the summoner, missionary of the time, for the protection of the *sacred domain* against the Pharaoh of the age that every era of summons brings forth, and gathered, in its tasbih-doublets, a hint of the Keys of the Gardens — the densest cluster of divine names in the Quran. And it has taught the higher-rank measure of sin: great masters lament intended-but-undone good, not ordinary transgression.

There is also, threaded through all of this, a teaching about how to hold doctrine in the world. A treasure is stored against loss, but a treasure is also stored against careless use. Guarded teaching is not jealousy; it is care. The doctrine that the Dai's "sins" are unfulfilled goods is not a quiet boast; it is a teaching that the higher the station, the more weight a missed good carries. The reading of the Surah al-Fath (سُورَةُ الْفَتْح, Chapter of Victory) verses about prior and latter sins as a forgiveness extended on behalf of the *veils, deputies* is not a deflection of the Prophet's purity; it is a teaching that the the summons, the mission carries protection by anticipation, that the line is kept whole by Allah's reaching forward through time to absorb missteps that would otherwise break it.

These three together form the discipline of concealment. The named the summoners, missionaries have carried the call across centuries by gathering, holding, emigrating, and springing forth doctrine on their tongues; the Dai's office has to be prayed for so that the protected ground does not breach; and knowledge must be handled as a trust because realities can harm when received without preparation. The discipline is not external to the doctrine. The discipline is the doctrine carried in another register.
