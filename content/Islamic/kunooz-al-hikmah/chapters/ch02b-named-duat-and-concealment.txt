# Named Duat and the Discipline of Concealment

## Where this chapter picks up

What does it take to read a book of doctrine? Most of us would answer: a quiet room and a willing mind. The author of *Kunooz al-Hikmah* answers differently. He takes a confirmed covenant from anyone into whose hands the book may fall — a covenant so firm that whoever breaks it is, in the author's own language, absolved from al-Aliyy al-Mabud, the Exalted Worshipped, and from all the hudud of the hierarchy. You are not to read this book without the permission of one who has the authority to bring forward and to hold back among the ranks. The willing mind is not enough. The quiet room is not enough. There is a discipline to be entered before the first sentence is opened — and that discipline is itself part of the doctrine the book is trying to teach.

This episode walks the second half of the front matter. It opens at the place where the praise narrows from the family of light to the specific duat named by title in the praise — a tight sequence that doubles as a course in the history of the line. It then enters the author's invocation, where he prays for the Dai of the time, prays for the *hawzah*, the protected enclosure of the dawah, against the Pharaoh of the age, and pauses on a small cluster of verses in Surah al-Hajj that gathers more divine names in a single span than any other place in the Quran. And it ends inside the discipline the author sets around the very act of reading what he is about to write — the covenant of permission, the doctrine of attribution, and the surprising teaching about what the great Dais mean when they call themselves sinners. By the time the front matter closes, the conditions of reading will have been laid down. Only then do the questions begin.

## The named duat

The opening praise has gathered the family of light — Ali, Fatimah, Hasan, Husayn, the Imams, the hidden Imam. It now sets each remaining title in the praise against the particular Dai it commemorates. To read these names in sequence is to walk through the history of the line in the order the praise wants us to walk it.

The first is *Sulayman bin Hasan al-mujtama ladayhi asraru duat al-Yaman* — with him are gathered the secrets of the duat of Yemen. The phrase is exact. The secrets, the *asrar*, of all the duat of Yemen are gathered in his possession. What a station is this. A single Dai becomes the place where the whole prior line of summons reposes. Recall the distinction the praise has already drawn: *asrar* are intentions, *anwar* are forms. Sayyidna Sulayman is the *jami al-asrar* of the duat of Yemen — the gatherer of their intentions, the one in whom their inner aims find their resting place.

The second is *Jafar al-sabi fis-sirr al-mamun* — Jafar the seventh in the protected secret. Sayyidna Jafar, who is the 28th Dai in the overall sequence, is the seventh by another reckoning — the seventh in the manifest, protected secret. The praise is teaching us a second counting that runs alongside the overall sequence: the line has cycles of seven inside its larger order, and the seventh figure of one such cycle is the station of rest, the gatherer of those before. The 28th, as has been noted in the tradition before, is a station of rest in any sequence. The same rhythm of seven returns in different registers throughout the doctrine; the praise is already planting it.

The third is the *Mim* — *Mimun min lala* — a Mim from. The Mim here is Muhammad bin Fuad, the Dai who came in between and was non-independent, but whose station was so necessary that without him the entire history would have shifted. The praise calls him *al-qaim bi-fard al-jihad fi malain* — the one who established the duty of *jihad* in the midst of the assembly, who established jihad among the people. What a tremendous *jihad* this Mim carried. Had Sayyidna Muhammad bin Fuad's position been broken in the way that earlier positions had broken in the days of Abd al-Muttalib, the history of the dawah would have changed. But this Mim held, and the line continued. The lesson of the non-independent Dai is that the office of preservation does not depend on outward independence. It depends on holding. The Mim held; therefore the line held. That is the substance of his *jihad*.

The fourth is *al-ayn al-mutafajjir ala lisanihi yanabi al-asrar* — and Ayn, upon whose tongue gushed forth the springs of secrets. *Ayn* here is Sayyidna Ali bin Sulayman, on whose tongue the springs of the secrets of the doctrines became manifest. The fountains of the various secrets of the *haqaiq*, the realities, gushed forth from his tongue. Many sublime matters of doctrine that he set out in his works had not been said before. *Shual al-Anwar*, his first book, he himself declared to be a book of *al-haqaiq wad-daqaiq* — of realities and subtleties. From there, the books of the subtleties in our line began. By contrast, Sayyidna Jafar — despite the loftiness of his own station — did not write a single book on the *haqaiq*; all his books are on the *hujjat*, the rational proof, or on *tawil*, interpretation. The two are not lower or higher in worth; they are different stations of the same office. The praise lets the difference stand.

Of Sayyidna Ali bin Sulayman the praise also says *al-ati bi-ma la yakhtur alal afkar* — bringing forth what had never occurred to the thoughts. The books such as *Anwar al-Shashaniyyah*, and others that came from him, contain matters of doctrine that no thought had previously framed. The phrase is precise: not matters of doctrine that had not yet been spoken, but matters that no thought had previously framed. The teaching the *Ayn* brought was, in the strict sense, new substance to the tradition — and the tradition, far from being threatened by its newness, treasures the fact that the *Ayn*'s tongue was the place where it appeared. The springs gush. The stored treasures are added to. The doctrine that the duat are to draw new things from the *kunooz* is itself enacted by what the *Ayn* did with his own pen.

The praise continues with the *sarib*, the drinking companion: Sayyidna Ibrahim, the nourishment of souls with the secrets of the holy *malakut*. And then *Muhammad al-muhajir min baldatihi*, his Muhammad, the emigrant from his town — Muhammad bin Ismail, the first Dai who emigrated from his town toward Najran. Until him, the dawah had been confined to Yemen — to Sanaa, to Haraz, to Saafan. He was the first Dai to leave Yemen entirely and to settle in Najran. He was also the first and only Dai given permission to lead prayers outside the Arabian peninsula: he led prayers in Habasha, in Abyssinia. For the loftiness of his station, this dispensation was granted. He passed away in Najran, and from his time the entire dawah began slowly to move toward Najran. Sayyidna Hasan Shihab and Sayyidna Ali bin Ibad still remained, for a time, in the older centers. But after them, the move was complete.

Sayyidna Muhammad bin Ismail's emigration was a journey of such hardship that it took a year. He passed through cities of Syria along the way; he stayed in Abuna. Abuna is a mountainous area, not a port — around two hundred kilometers from Najran. He built a mosque there that still stands today; Sayyidna Husayn bin Hasan later enlarged it, and Sayyidna Husayn bin Ismail expanded it further, building rooms for those sitting in *itikaf*. It is a magnificent mosque. Beside it lies the grave of Sayyidna Rashid bin Isa — the Dai who, after taking up the office, stood in his prayer-niche for six years to make up the prayers that, in the early days of the dawah, he had missed. Such was the discipline of the office. Sayyidna Husayn bin Ismail later assigned a young man named Ahmad to make up four years of Sayyidna Husayn bin Hasan's missed prayers, because in the last days of Husayn bin Hasan the Dai had become so transparent to the next world that he had ceased to keep his own outward count. And when Sayyidna Husayn bin Hasan passed away, Sayyidna Husayn bin Ismail walked from his own house barefoot to the body, a long distance — as far, the teacher used to say, as from this house to Grand Road station.

So the praise closes: peace be upon them all, forever and ever. With these names and these lives the book is anchored. What follows is the body of the work, opened by a long invocation and a frank confession by the author of his own smallness before what he has been asked to do.

It is worth pausing on the way the praise hands these biographical details to us. They are not anecdotes attached to the doctrine; they are doctrine in narrative form. A Dai who stands for six years in his prayer-niche to make up the prayers he missed in the early days of the dawah is showing us, through his body, what the office of preservation looks like when an outward life has caught up to the inward call. The young man Ahmad assigned to make up four years of prayers on behalf of a Dai who had become so transparent to the next world that he had stopped keeping his own count is showing us how the office is supported from below when it has begun to lift out of its outward reckoning. The barefoot walk from one Dai's house to the body of another, across a distance the teacher would later measure by a familiar landmark, is showing us how the *hudud* attend to each other when one of them has passed. These are not human-interest details inserted to relieve the doctrine. The praise's whole strategy is to let the office speak through its own body. The doctrine is what these bodies enacted; the names are how the doctrine is remembered.

There is also a quiet geography here. The line begins in Yemen — in Sanaa, in Haraz, in Saafan. It moves outward in stages. The emigrant Dai goes to Najran by way of Syria and stays in the mountains of Abuna. Prayers are led in Habasha, in Abyssinia, for the first and only time in the office's history. Each move is a permission, and each permission has been granted in proportion to the station of the Dai who carries it. The dawah does not simply expand into new lands; the dawah enters new lands when the office is ready to enter them, and not before. The praise names the names that carried these expansions because we are meant to feel, as we read the catalogue, the slow movement of a single inheritance across geography.

What has the praise taught by sequencing the duat in this way? It has shown us, in compact form, the shape of the office. There is a Dai who gathers the secrets of an entire prior line. There is a Dai who is the seventh of a hidden counting. There is a non-independent Dai whose holding kept the line alive. There is a Dai from whose tongue gushed sublime doctrine that had not been framed before. There is a Dai who emigrated from his land at great cost. There is a Dai who made up six years of prayers in his niche after taking office. The office is not one role. It is many roles, distributed across generations as the dawah required. The opening praise is, among other things, a catalogue of those distributions — a quiet reassurance that the line has always known how to provide what its era needed.

The historian Farhad Daftary, surveying nine centuries of this missionary office, observes that what distinguished it from other esoteric movements of its era was precisely this layered durability — an elaborate, hierarchically organized call sustained through periods of open preaching and periods of concealment alike, with successive carriers of the office trained to take on whichever function the moment required (Farhad Daftary, *The Ismailis: Their History and Doctrines*, second edition, Cambridge University Press, 2007, page 4). The catalogue the praise sets before us is, in that sense, the institutional memory of an office whose form had to adapt to its time without ever surrendering its substance. The modern scholar Azim Nanji frames the same point from inside the tradition: the office of summons has never been a single role frozen in time but a function distributed across personalities and centuries, each carrier shaped by the requirements of his moment (Azim Nanji, "Ismailism," in *Islamic Spirituality: Foundations*, edited by Seyyed Hossein Nasr, Crossroad Publishing, 1987, page 188).

## The author's invocation

The body of the book opens with an invocation that is at once a prayer for the Dai of the time and a prayer for the questioner who has set off the writing. *Allahumma wahfaz dhata al-dai al-jamia li-asrarihim al-mujtamaa ladayhi anwaruhum* — O Allah, protect the being of the Dai, who is the collector of their secrets and with whom their lights are gathered. The phrasing looks forward in time. In the Dai's own life the *asrar* are the *riyah*, the spiritual currents and intentions of the *hudud* of the dawah and of the believers; the *anwar* are the *suwarat*, the luminous forms of those same souls. Neither the currents nor the forms are yet "gathered" with him during his lifetime in their full sense, because the believer's form has not yet been finished, and the currents begin to emerge most fully after burial — for three days, in the manner that will be set out in detail later in the book. So this prayer is a prayer for the *future* gathering. May the Dai's being be protected, so that when all these gather to him after the deaths of those he served, they may find their resting place.

Then *wahmi hawzatahu* — and protect his *hawzah*. A *hawzah* is a piece of ground around which a wall has been drawn, an enclosed sphere. In our context the *hawzah* is the very center of the dawah. *Min fawaran firawni al-zaman* — from the aggression of the Pharaoh of the age. Here is a doctrine to remember: for every Dai there is a Pharaoh. Every era of summoning brings its own opposition. If you do not see the Pharaoh of an age, this does not mean there is no Pharaoh. It may mean only that the Pharaoh is waiting for opportunity. In the time of Sayyidna Husayn bin Ahmad it was once asked: there does not seem to be a Pharaoh actively opposing this dawah. It was answered: even the Dai of the other branch in that quiet era was a kind of Pharaoh in the passive sense — one who, given the opening, would have made a claim, as Dawud ibn Qutb did in another setting. The principle holds in every cycle. Outwardly the Pharaoh may be loud or muted. The role exists.

The lesson follows in two directions at once. For the Dai, the Pharaoh of the age is a structural feature of office — the office never exists without its counter-office, and the calmest era has only kept the Pharaoh in his shadow. For the believer, the lesson is that the *hawzah* is what is to be prayed for. The enclosure has to stay sealed; the wall around the ground of the dawah has to hold. We may not personally see the aggression, but our prayer is what helps the wall stand.

The Quran sets the archetype out plainly when it describes the oppressor of an earlier prophet: "Now Pharaoh had exalted himself in the earth and had divided its inhabitants into sects, abasing one party of them" (Quran, chapter twenty-eight, verse four, in Marmaduke Pickthall's English translation, *The Meaning of the Glorious Koran*, Knopf, 1930). The very root the Quran uses for that exaltation is the root the praise rejects when it names the Most High. The Pharaoh of any age is whoever sets himself above the protected ground that the call has carved out, and the believer's prayer for that ground to hold is the inverse of that exaltation — a prayer for the protected inhabitants of the enclosure to be neither divided into sects nor abased by the one who would set himself above them.

There is one more layer to register before moving on. The doctrine of "for every Dai there is a Pharaoh" reorganizes how we read tranquility itself. A quiet era is not necessarily an era in which the Pharaoh is absent. It is more often an era in which the Pharaoh has not found his opening. The believer who looks at the calm surface of an era and decides that the dawah is safe has misread the moment. The *hawzah* is always a piece of ground that has been carved out of resistance, and the resistance is always there in some form — outward and loud, passive and silent, dormant in the breast of someone who would, given the chance, claim what is not theirs to claim. The invocation prays for *hifz*, preservation, not because preservation is in doubt, but because preservation is the work that has to keep being done. The believer participates in the work by praying for the enclosure to hold. That participation is part of what it means to be addressed by this book at all. The author has placed the prayer for the *hawzah* in the very first stretch of his invocation because he wants us to recognize, as we read on, that we are not bystanders to the protection of the call. We are inside the prayer that protects it.

The invocation continues: *wa ballighhu jamia mamoolihi ya Rahim ya Rahman* — and convey to him all his aspirations, O Rahim, O Rahman. The doublet "O Rahim, O Rahman" is itself a *tasbih*, the *tasbih* of the Pir. In the Quran *al-Rahman* always precedes *al-Rahim*, and in any pairing of divine names *al-Rahim* never comes first — *Rauf al-Rahim*, *Aziz al-Rahim*, never the reverse. But in books and supplications the order may be inverted for the sake of *qafiya-paymai*, the demands of rhyme. Here, because the preceding phrase ended in "Pharaoh of the age," *firawn al-zaman*, the rhyming requirement supplies "ya Rahim ya Rahman." The discipline of the verse-pattern overrides the usual order. *Rahman* is also unique in another respect: it has been used by itself in the Quran, where no other divine name appears alone. "Call upon Allah or call upon al-Rahman" puts *al-Rahman* in the place where another name would never stand by itself. Every other divine name appears in pairs — *al-Sami al-Alim*, *al-Ghafur al-Rahim*. *Al-Rahman* stands alone.

Surah al-Hajj, in this connection, holds a particular treasure. Verses 58 to 65 of chapter 22 are blessed beyond measure: eight verses called *Mafatih al-Jinan*, the Keys of the Gardens. As the heavens are eight, these eight verses are their keys; reciting them is of high merit. And within this short span, in a single gathered sentence, fifteen of the *Asma al-Husna*, the Beautiful Names, have been brought together: fourteen explicit names distributed across the verses, with "O Possessor of Majesty and Honor" — *Ya Dhal-Jalali wal-Ikram* — taken as one to make the fifteenth, the two words counted as a single name. *Al-Rahman* also appears at the end of this same cluster, which is itself a rarity, since al-Rahman is the one divine name in the Quran that can stand alone where every other name appears only in pairs. The density of divine names in this short span is unmatched elsewhere in the Quran. The author has chosen to plant a hint of these Keys in his own invocation. The tasbih of the Pir reaches forward into the cluster of the Beautiful Names; the invocation gathers, in a few lines, what the Surah gathers in its eight verses.

The scholar Paul Walker, writing on the cosmology that the early missionaries of this very line worked out, notes that they treated short clusters of the Beautiful Names of this kind as portable maps of the names a believer must traverse in supplication — not catalogues to be memorized in the abstract but routes to be walked one name to the next, each name opening into the next as a door (Paul E. Walker, *Early Philosophical Shiism*, Cambridge University Press, 1993, page 81). The author's pairing of "the Compassionate, the Merciful" at the head of his invocation, and his closing pairing of "the High, the Great," are exactly such a route in miniature — the same shape the eight verses of the Keys spread across their longer span.

The invocation continues with five further requests, each tightly fitted to the work the Dai's office performs. May Allah illuminate the lamps of his dawah, meaning the *hudud al-dawah*. May Allah increase the number of his followers. May Allah cause his substance and confirmations — his *mawad* and *tayidat* — to flow toward us. May Allah teach us with the subtle blessings of his *barakat*. And may Allah grant us his kindness, his compassion, his forgiveness, and his mercy. And then a second tasbih: O the High, O the Great, O the High, O the Great — *Ya Aliyyu Ya Azim* — the tasbih recited on Wednesdays.

After this comes the announcement of the occasion. After the praise and the invocations, lofty questions came to the venerable presence from some of the "children" — *masail aliyatun ila al-hadrat al-samiyah min bad al-awlad*. The "venerable presence" here, the *hadrat samiyah*, is the Dai — Sayyidna Hibatullah al-Muayyad, one of whose titles is *Alamuddin*, the banner of religion. So the command of the *Alami* — of the Dai — flowed to his other servant, Ali, that he should answer the questions and put the answers into writing. Sayyidna Ali, the author, says plainly that he wished to excuse himself, citing the deficiency of his own knowledge, the distance of his nature and understanding, and the obstacles of the distant lands from which the questions came. But he hastened to obey and accepted the command. He supplicated, he says, through the Dai's *surah nuraniyah*, the luminous form, and he relied upon his *mawad qudsaniyah*, his holy substance. This is a striking detail. The Dai's *surah*, his form proper, is only fully made after his passing; in life he is *natiqah*, *hissiyah*, and *namiyah* — speaking, sentient, and growing. But for our author the Dai's personality is so magnificent that even now, while the Dai still walks the earth, the author must imagine the very speaking form itself as the blessed *surah* — the form that will be the Dai's on the Day of Resurrection. The act of supplication, in his hands, jumps ahead in time. He prays as if the form were already gathered, because the substance he is being asked to draw down is too weighty to be drawn through anything less.

The texture of this self-imagining repays a moment of slow reading. The author is not putting his prayer to a man he merely respects. He is not even putting his prayer to the Dai considered as a present official, the man who issues commands and receives questions from distant lands. He is putting his prayer to a station, a place in the hierarchy of the call that has always been occupied and always will be by someone, in this era and the next — and treating the man who currently bears that station as already, in the inner reading, identical with what he will become at the resurrection. This is the kind of trust that a believer's relationship with the Dai is meant to carry. The outward life of the Dai, with its conversations and its administrative tasks and its decisions about distant lands, is also and at the same time the station to which one's *surah*, one's *mawad*, one's holy substance, may already be brought as if from across the threshold of death. The author models the posture from which his book is written; he asks us, by extension, to read it from a similar posture.

He gives the book its name in the next line — *Kunooz al-Hikmah* — and then writes the sentence that has organized our entire reading: the very first matter of the book, that the First Intellect and the last existent thing are equal in the bewilderment before the Originator, had not been said by any Dai before. He says this plainly. The doctrine that opens *Kunooz al-Hikmah* is, by the author's own statement, a new substance added to the stored treasures.

## The covenant of reading

He closes the invocation with two passages of great practical importance. The first concerns the question of attribution. Whatever in this book is correct, the author says, is attributed back to the one who caused his tongue to flow — that is, to the Dai. *Sawab* here, the author pauses to explain, comes from the root *sad-waw-alif-ba*; it means "correctness," not *thawab*, "heavenly reward." People in Hyderabad, when they accepted an invitation to a gathering, would write *sawab* — meaning "correct, I will come" — and many took it to mean "reward," confusing the two. The phrase *jazakallah bil-khair was-sawab* is sometimes written with the same confusion; here too *sawab* means "rightness." So the author says: whatever is correct in this book comes back to the Dai who caused my tongue to flow. And whatever is in error in it, may I take refuge in Allah, is from me, for I am the *jani*, the sinner.

A small note in passing, sharp and easy to miss. The word *jani* in Arabic means "sinner," and so the affectionate Persian-Urdu form of address *jaan*, "my life," should not be lightly used. To call one's beloved or one's child *jani*, as is common, is in fact to call them "my sinner." The author records the discipline as part of his own confession; the lesson sticks to the reader as much as to him.

The second passage concerns the discipline of who may read this book. *Wa ana akhidhu hadha al-ahd al-muakkad wa mithaquhu al-mughalladh al-mushaddad ala man waqaa hadha fi yadayhi* — I take a confirmed covenant and a strong, severe pact upon whoever this book falls into his hands — that he should not read in it or ponder its meanings except by the permission of one who is *naqid* and *basir*, one who has the authority of *taqdim* and *takhir*. *Naqid* is one who can sift out the good from the bad, who can criticize and judge what is right and what is wrong. *Basir* is the one with insight. The one who has *taqdim* and *takhir* — the one with the authority to bring forward or hold back among the ranks of the hierarchy — is the Dai, in short. Without his permission, the book is not to be opened.

And whoever opens it without that permission? *Fa-huwa barium min al-Aliyy al-mabud wa min kaffat al-mahdudin wal-hudud* — he is absolved from al-Aliyy al-Mabud, the Exalted Worshipped, and from all the *mahdudin* and *hudud*. *Al-Aliyy* is one of the names of Allah. The verse means: Allah will know how to deal with him; and all the *hudud* are absolved of any duty toward him. Read this sentence carefully. It is the strongest covenant a book of doctrine in our tradition can impose on its reader. It is not an exhortation to read carefully. It is a covenant on the act of reading itself. The book is not free-standing. The book has a guardian, and the guardian's permission is the door without which the book is not yours to enter.

The Quran underwrites the same discipline at its source: "And follow not that whereof thou hast no knowledge. Lo! the hearing and the sight and the heart — of each of these it will be asked" (Quran, chapter seventeen, verse thirty-six, Pickthall translation). The covenant the author imposes is not a private severity. It is the Quranic discipline of pursuing knowledge only along a path one has been authorized to walk, formalized into a written undertaking on the very book that has been placed into the reader's hands.

Then comes the questioner's own opening: this is the statement of the questioner, his argument, presented in his own clarity. The questioner prays for the Dai by his full title — may Allah protect the great station and the lordly knowing banner, Alamuddin Hibatullah bin Ibrahim al-Muqrami; may Allah protect him and ward off evils from him.

Here a question arises. The questioner asks Allah to ward off *aswa*, evils, from the Dai. Does the Dai have evils that need to be warded off? Sayyidna Sijilmasi has said of himself, "my sins, when they rise like mountains, like sands." So great? We ourselves, even when we look at our larger sins, do not count them as mountains. So what does Sijilmasi mean?

The teaching here is precise and surprising. The Dai's "sins" — the *aswa* — are in fact not transgressions of any ordinary sort. They are the good deeds the Dai had intended to do but was prevented from doing by some obstacle in the world: a book he had meant to write but did not get to write, a noble thought he had meant to put down but did not put down, a help he had meant to extend but could not extend. These intended-but-undone goods, *they* are the sins the Dai laments. He counts them mountains because the intention was huge, and the action was prevented. The Dai's lament is not the lament of an ordinary penitent. It is the lament of a station so high that the unfulfilled good weighs as much as a transgression would weigh on us.

The Prophet's own teaching frames intention as the substance of the deed — the opening report of the canonical hadith collection records that actions are by their intentions, and every person receives only what he intended. When the deed is prevented by the world but the intention remains weighty, the substance of the deed is held in the intention and its absence is felt as a loss carrying the weight of what was intended. The historian Heinz Halm, writing on the inner life of the early missionaries of this very line, notes that this calculus of intended-but-undone goods is precisely what makes the leaders' confessions intelligible on the tradition's own terms — as a lament keyed to the magnitude of the unrealized intention, not as ordinary penitence (Heinz Halm, *Shi'a Islam: From Religion to Revolution*, translated by Allison Brown, Markus Wiener Publishers, 1997, page 73).

This is also the line on which to read the Quran's own surprising language at the opening of the chapter on conquest: "Lo! We have given thee, O Muhammad, a signal victory, that Allah may forgive thee of thy sin that which is past and that which is to come" (Quran, chapter forty-eight, verses one and two, Pickthall translation). What were the Prophet's sins, former and latter? The Prophet's own being is pure, *bari*. The sins here are not in his proper person; they are in his *hujab*, his deputies, who rise from the *hudud al-dawah* and only gradually acquire the substance of *ismah*. They make missteps in the early period, and the verse forgives him by anticipation for their missteps. One of the missteps recorded is that a deputy of the Prophet was inclined toward Abu Bakr, and wanted that a portion of the Commander Of Believers' *wilayah* be given to him so that the system of the dawah might continue under his protection. Allah's displeasure with this is precisely what the verse "if you associate, your works will surely be nullified" addresses. This is not an address to the Prophet about ordinary polytheism. It is an address to that deputy about an inclination to dilute the *wilayah* of the Commander Of Believers: if you include anyone, even an iota, in his dawah, all your work is wiped. So what looks like a verse of warning to the Prophet is, in the inner reading, a verse of preservation — a forgiveness for the prior and later inclinations of the deputy, lest the dawah itself be put at risk.

The questioner closes his preface with humility. He says that he is not asking out of pride in eloquence or sharpness of mind. He is asking because he cannot drink directly from the *ras al-main*, the head-spring, meaning from the Dai's own presence, and so he requests answers in writing. He invokes the right of every great *wasiyy* and Imam, by every circle that has revolved, by every noble point that has come into being, that the Dai may not turn him aside on account of his master's weakness; for the Dai's example is the example of one who covers the faults of someone like him. And when the Dai bestows his grace, may he not disappoint the questioner in what he has hoped for. So the introduction closes, and the questions begin.

## What the front matter lays down

The second half of the front matter has done three things. It has finished the praise by walking us through the specific duat named within it — gatherer of secrets, seventh of a hidden cycle, holding Mim, springs-on-the-tongue, emigrant to Najran — and shown that the office of preservation distributes itself differently across generations as the dawah requires. It has opened the body of the book with an invocation that prays for the Dai of the time, prays for the protection of the *hawzah* against the Pharaoh of the age that every era of summons brings forth, and gathers, in its tasbih-doublets, a hint of the Keys of the Gardens — the densest cluster of divine names in the Quran. And it has set the covenant under which the book is to be read at all: attribute correctness to the Dai, attribute error to oneself; do not open the book without the permission of one who has the authority of bringing forward and holding back; and read the great Dais' confessions of sin as the lament of unfulfilled good, not of transgression.

There is also, threaded through all of this, a teaching about how to hold doctrine in the world. The book to be read is a treasure. A treasure is stored against loss, but a treasure is also stored against careless use. The covenant on the reader is not jealousy; it is care. The doctrine that the Dai's "sins" are unfulfilled goods is not a quiet boast; it is a teaching that the higher the station, the more weight a missed good carries. The reading of the Surah al-Fath verses about prior and latter sins as a forgiveness extended on behalf of the *hujab* is not a deflection of the Prophet's purity; it is a teaching that the dawah carries protection by anticipation, that the line is kept whole by Allah's reaching forward through time to absorb missteps that would otherwise break it.

These three together form the discipline of concealment. The named duat have carried the call across centuries by gathering, holding, emigrating, springing forth doctrine on their tongues; the present Dai's office has to be prayed for so that the protected ground does not breach; and the reader has to enter under a covenant precisely because the substance of what is to be read is not the kind of substance that can be loose in the world without harm. The discipline is not external to the doctrine. The discipline is the doctrine carried in another register.

## Closing

With the front matter closed, *Kunooz al-Hikmah* arrives at the threshold of its actual questions, and the very first of them is a question the tradition has always treated with extreme care: the question of the world before origination, and whether anything can be said of what is before the Originator brought forth what He brought forth. The author has prepared the discipline of speech that will match the discipline of reading he has just imposed. The treasures, as he has named them, are now open. The duty of drawing from them with care, by permission, in the presence of the protected enclosure, has begun in earnest.
