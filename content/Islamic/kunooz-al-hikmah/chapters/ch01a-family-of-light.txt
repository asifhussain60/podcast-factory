# Family of Light

## Where this episode opens

What if the body in which the Prophet ascended on the Night Journey was not, in the deepest sense, his own? What if the very substance of that ascending form was woven out of the inner essences of the believers — of the ranks of the summoners in the regions, of the dignitaries gathered around them, of the small struggling souls who had just barely entered the circle of faith? That is the quiet astonishment buried in the opening pages of a treatise called *Kunooz al-Hikmah al-madhkurah li-arbab al-ismah*, "The Stored Treasures of Wisdom for the Masters of Infallibility." It is a book whose title has been read past for centuries as if it merely meant what it appeared to mean. It does not. The "masters of infallibility" in this title are not the figures we first imagine, and the "treasures" are not in storage for the reasons we first assume.

This episode walks the front matter of the book — author's note, opening praise, and the long doctrinal portrait of the family of light that the praise unfolds — and surfaces the three teachings the author lays down before the questions even begin. The first concerns who the book is for and what the special human form is made of. The second concerns a bewilderment so radical that the highest existing thing and the newest believer stand on the same ground before it. The third concerns the chain of veils that runs from Ali through Fatimah, through Hasan and Husayn, through the Imams of the line, and finally arrives at the Imam who is hidden in our own time. None of these is a side-doctrine. Each is a frame the rest of the book will be read through.

## Treasures stored, for whom they were stored, and what the special form is made of

The title of the book is, on its own, already a doctrine. *Kunooz* are treasures that have been laid up, stored, guarded against loss — not merely possessed, but kept aside for the moment of need. *al-Hikmah* is wisdom, but a particular wisdom: the wisdom of the realities, the inner knowing by which the universe is read. And *arbab al-ismah*, "the masters of infallibility," is the phrase that turns the title from a piety into a doctrine.

In our tradition there are two orders of *ismah* — two orders of preservation from error. The first is *kulli ismah*, total infallibility. This belongs to the *Aimmah Tahirin*, the Pure Imams, the Ahl al-Bayt. For them, sin is impossible. Error is impossible. The preservation is complete. The second order is *juzi ismah*, partial infallibility. This belongs to the *duat*, the summoners who carry the call after the Imams have entered concealment — and especially to the *qaimi duat*, those who stand for the era of the Qaim. They are preserved enough to bear the trust. They are not, like the Imams, beyond every possibility of slip. One of the great Dais of the line, Sayyidna Ali bin Husayn al-Jamari, wrote an entire treatise to fix the distinction precisely. The Aimmah are masters of total infallibility. The duat are masters of partial infallibility. It is not an interchangeable phrase.

It is to *these* second masters — the duat, the *arbab al-juzi ismah* — that the treasures of this book are addressed. The treasures are stored *for them*. So that they may draw new things out of them. So that what was once handed down may continue to bear fruit in their hands. The author is not writing a manual for the Imams; he is writing a reservoir from which the bearers of the call may keep drawing across the centuries.

The author himself stored these treasures in obedience to a command he could not refuse. His name is Sayyidna Ali bin Hibatullah, known as al-Shamsi — the eldest son of Sayyidna Hibatullah bin Ibrahim. Hibatullah had several children, and on this eldest son, called Ali bin Ibad, he performed the *nass*, the formal designation. But Sayyidna Ali bin Hibatullah passed away during his father's own lifetime. In the language of our tradition he therefore became a *ghayr-munfarid Dai* — a non-independent Dai, one whose station was confirmed though he did not live to take up the office in its full external manifestation. His brother Sayyidna Ismail bin Hibatullah came forward in his place, the tenth in the order of succession, and of all the children of Sayyidna Hibatullah it is said that Sayyidna Ismail had the most excellent form, the most excellent *surat*, in the community of the *wulat*. Another brother, Sayyidna Hasan bin Ibad, became a Dai of great station after him. A fourth brother, Sayyidna Jafar bin Hibatullah, did not himself become Dai, but his rank was nonetheless lofty: he wrote *qasidahs* of profound depth, and the opening *qasidah* of the great book *al-Kawakib al-Durriyah* is from his pen. Sayyidna Jafar, while not the author of a treatise on the realities, ranked among the most cultivated of his line.

Sayyidna Ali bin Hibatullah himself wrote only one book in all his life. He wrote it during his father's lifetime, in obedience to a request that came to him through his father's command. He never wrote a second. That single book is the one we are reading now — the treatise that has come into our hands across the centuries, naked of the author's other works because the author left no others.

Pause for a moment on the architecture of that distinction. The duat are not lesser Imams. They are not approximations. They are a different kind of office altogether — an office that exists precisely because the Imams have entered concealment, and because the call must continue to be carried in the world while the Imam is hidden. The Imam is the master of total preservation because the Imam is the one through whom the inner reality flows without interruption. The Dai is the master of partial preservation because the Dai stands in the world, takes counsel in the world, faces the contingencies of the world, and yet must carry the trust without breaking it. Total infallibility belongs to those who are above contingency. Partial infallibility belongs to those who walk through contingency without letting the trust fall. The architecture of the title is the architecture of the call itself. The treasures of wisdom in this book are the substance the Dai keeps drawing on so that the partial preservation he has been given may continue to hold across an era whose end he will not see.

A small mark of style on the very first leaf is worth pausing on. Above the *Bismillah al-Rahman al-Rahim* on the original page is written the *Ism al-Azam*, the Greatest Name. A treatise by Sayyidna Abd al-Ali bin Hasan al-Wajih considered, at length, whether the light of the *Ism al-Azam* is greater than the light of the *Bismillah*. After protracted argument he established that the light of the *Ism al-Azam* is the more excellent. For this reason we write the *Ism al-Azam* above the *Bismillah* whenever the occasion calls for the full inscription. The sign itself is rendered in *khatt al-Teseri*, the Teseri script: the first dot above is a *khatam*, a seal-ring, alluding to the *Ahl al-Sharif*, the noble family. The *madd*, the elongation mark above, points to the *muawanah*, the assistance received from the *Hudud Aliyah*, the higher ranks of the hierarchy. The Commander of the Faithful himself composed verses about the *Ism al-Azam*. One does not pass over such a sign without acknowledging what it carries.

Now the book opens with a praise that is also a doctrine. All praise is for Allah, the Great, who manifested a special human form, *nasut khas*, from the gardens of the people of specialty, *riyadh ahl al-ikhtisas*. Two technical terms appear in this single opening line, and they organize much of what follows. *Nasut*, and *riyadh*.

There are three types of *nasut*, three forms of the human vessel. The lowest is *nasut adna*. The middle is *nasut awsat*. The highest is *nasut khas*, the special. By another naming, the lowest is also called *nasut tabii*, the natural, or *nasut tanasuli*, the procreative — the form that is born of loins and wombs in the ordinary way. After this comes *nasut dhilli*, the shadow form. And after this, the *nasut khas*, the special form, which is of an order altogether apart.

The *nasut khas* is made from the *riyadh*, the gardens, of the *ahl al-ikhtisas*, the people of specialty. The *riyadh* are the spiritual gardens of particular souls: the *hudud al-dawah*, the ranks of the summoning hierarchy in the regions; the *muminin*, the believers; and the *hadarat*, the dignitaries gathered in those regions. From the spiritual essences of these is the *nasut khas* composed. The lower form, *nasut dani*, is by contrast made from the *riyadh* of those who are at a further distance, lesser in station — the wider community of souls.

This is the substance behind the astonishment that recurs throughout the book. When the Prophet ascended in the Miraj, through which *nasut* did he ascend? Through the *nasut khas* — formed from the gardens of the people of specialty. That is, the very souls of the believers and the ranks of the regions, in their inner essences, were the substance from which the Prophet's ascending form was woven. The Prophet did not ascend in any other body. He ascended in this one. And we, in our small ways, are gardens that contribute to such a form. This is why, throughout the opening praise, two parallel hierarchies are honored: the Imams, in whom the *nasut khas* is fully realized, and the duat, who serve as the bearers of the work in the era of concealment. The praise is not a piety prepended to a doctrinal book. The praise *is* the doctrine — and what is being praised is also what is being said about us.

## A bewilderment that joins the first existent to the newest believer

The author then bears witness in the language of the *shahadah* and unveils a teaching that, by his own statement, had never before been said by any Dai. He testifies that there is no god but He — and then immediately adds the clause that is the teaching: the first of existing things and the last of His creation are equal in the inability to comprehend the exalted essence.

To feel the force of this we have to fix the two ends of the hierarchy. The *awwal mawjud*, the first existent thing in the order of creation, is the *aql-e-awwal*, the First Intellect — the highest creature in the chain of being, the radiant source from which the orders below it proceed. The *akhir mawjud*, the last existent thing, is the believer — and not the believer who has spent half a lifetime in study and practice, but the soul that has just newly entered the *dairah-e-iman*, the circle of faith. The just-arrived believer. These are the highest and the lowest poles of the order of creation, separated by what one would suppose is an unbridgeable distance of station.

The claim made here is breathtaking. In the matter of *marifat*, the gnosis of the Exalted Essence, these two are *equal*. The First Intellect, despite its lofty station, has not advanced in the gnosis of the Originator beyond the point where this last believer stands. The believer, low in the order of being, has not fallen behind by any measurable distance. Both are equal in the *ajz*, the inability. The First Intellect is perplexed before the Originator in exactly the same way that the newest believer is perplexed. The gnosis of the *Mubdi al-Taala*, the Originator, is simply impossible to either. It is not that one has a smaller portion than the other; it is that the very thing called *gnosis of His essence* lies altogether outside the reach of any creature, however high it may stand in the chain of being.

This teaching reorganizes the picture we carry of where we are. We are used to imagining a long ladder up which different creatures stand at different rungs, with the closeness to God measured by the rung you have reached. The praise reverses this. The ladder is real. The rungs are real. But on the question of the essence, the ladder does not climb at all. The highest rung looks up; the lowest rung looks up; both see the same impossibility. To put it another way: in matters of being, the order matters; in the matter of the gnosis of the essence, the order falls away.

The word *shay*, "thing," in the Quran must be re-read in this light. The Quran says, in many places, that Allah has power over every thing, knows every thing, encompasses every thing. The word translated "thing" is *shay*. And *shay*, on the doctrinal reading, does not mean every random object in the world. *Shay* means that which is real, that whose value endures. *La shay*, by contrast, is the mirage — what looks like water in the distance but when you reach it there is no water there. The mirage has no real value. So *shay* points to the believer, who has value; and at the highest reach, *shay* points to the Imam of the time, in whom all value is gathered. When the Quran says Allah has power over every *shay*, knows every *shay*, encompasses every *shay*, it is the believer above all who is being kept in view. The verses are spoken about the universe; they are addressed, in their inmost layer, to the believer.

This is not a doctrine that stayed in books. The duat who held this teaching lived it outwardly. Sayyidna Ali bin Sulayman, when believers came to meet him, would stand up to receive them. He would rise from his seat. When asked why a Dai should rise for a believer who had merely come to greet him, he answered: because in the *tawzi*, the apportioning of the *Ahl al-Sharif*, the noble family, the believer is my companion. Just as an Imam takes his share in the noble family, so the believer takes his share. In the matter of belonging to the *Ahl al-Sharif*, the believer is my fellow. And so out of honor, I stand. The gesture is small. The doctrine behind it is total.

Likewise Sayyidna Husayn bin Ismail wrote of Sayyidna Abdullah ibn Muhammad and of Sayyidna Ahmad al-Jamari that one of them was *afdal al-mawjudat* — the most excellent of existing things. People who heard the phrase puzzled at it. The most excellent of *all* existing things? More excellent than the angels, the celestial spheres, the First Intellect? The phrase was not careless. *Afdal al-mawjudat* meant: most excellent among the believers present in the dawah at that moment. The believer is not a peripheral figure in this universe. The believer is *afdal al-mawjudat* — and the Quran's "every thing" over which the Lord has power has the believer, above all, as its hidden referent.

There is one more consequence to register before moving on. If the gnosis of the essence lies altogether outside the reach of any creature, then the language of "approaching" Allah, which the supplications of the tradition use so freely, has to be heard correctly. It cannot mean a movement toward the essence. The essence does not admit of distances closing. What the language of approach refers to is the closing of the believer's distance from the *hudud* — from the ranks above him through which the substance descends to him. The believer climbs the hierarchy of the call. He does not climb the essence. He cannot. No one can. To approach is to draw nearer to the Imam who is above us in the chain of being, who is *Aliyy al-Azim* veiled in his own way, and who is the highest place toward which the creature can be oriented at all. The Originator remains where He has always been — at a distance no creature has begun to measure. The believer's whole movement is, properly speaking, a movement within creation toward the highest reach of creation, and there it ends. This is why the equality of bewilderment is good news rather than disappointment. It removes the false picture in which someone, somewhere, is "ahead" of us in the gnosis of the essence. No one is. The First Intellect is not ahead. We are not behind. The journey is all on the side of the ladder, and the ladder is where the journey is supposed to happen.

If you sit with these three readings together — the equality in bewilderment, the believer as the *shay* of the Quran, and the believer's movement as a movement within creation rather than toward the essence — you start to see what is happening in the book's opening. The author has not begun with mystical flourishes. He has begun with the strongest statement of the believer's *dignity* that the tradition will allow. The newest believer stands on the same ground as the First Intellect before the Originator. The Quran's most exalted verses about Allah's power and knowledge keep that very believer in view. The Dai stands up for him in his own house. The whole book that follows will assume this dignity as already established. We are not eavesdropping on a conversation among scholars. We are being addressed as the *shay* the Quran has been speaking of all along.

## The veiling chain that runs from Ali to the hidden Imam

From the opening praises the book turns to the line of the Imams and to the secret of their veiling — and what unfolds is not a sequence of independent biographies but a single doctrine of veil-and-veiled that runs from the Prophet's lifetime to our own era.

After the declaration of Ghadir Khumm — "of whomsoever I am master, this Ali is his master" — the Prophet lived his remaining months in the *hijab* of Ali, and Ali, for the rest of his own life, lived in the *hijab* of the Prophet. The two did not simply exist as separate persons after Ghadir. They had become veil and veiled, each interchangeable with the other in the inner reality. The Prophet himself had declared, "My flesh is your flesh, my blood is your blood," and the two were of *ruh wahidah*, a single soul. Sayyidna Ismail bin Ibrahim, in one of his writings, drew out the structural consequence of this. The *awsiya*, the successors, pass away before the *natiq*, the speaker-prophet, in the inner reality. What this means is that during the Prophet's remaining time after Ghadir Khumm, it was Ali who, in the inner ordering, had already given his form to be the veil of the Prophet; the Prophet, while his body still walked the earth, was already living in the inner *hijab* of Ali. When his body departed, what departed was a veil. The inner Ali was already in continuity.

The proof of how deeply the "our selves and your selves" of the Quran ran is in the famous matter of Aisha. The Prophet had said in the presence of the Father of Imams that he gave him the right that, after his own passing, he could divorce Aisha. The companions found this hard. How could this be? But the proof is from the Quran itself. In the verse of *mubahalah* the Prophet was commanded to call our sons and your sons, our women and your women, our selves and your selves. For "our sons" he brought Hasan and Husayn. For "our selves" he brought the Father of Imams. So the Father of Imams became the *nafs*, the self, of the Prophet — and what the Prophet may decide, the *nafs* of the Prophet may decide. The divorce-authority over Aisha is a direct corollary. The "selves" of the *mubahalah* verse is not a poetic flourish. It carries legal consequence.

In the same vein the praise speaks of *al-ashir*, the Tenth, who is veiled by the Prophet. This refers in the broadest sense to the Tenth Intellect. But more precisely, as our teacher Abdullah Sahib used to insist, *al-ashir* here does not point to that Tenth alone. It points to the *juz-e-idafiyyah*, the relative part, of the *sahib al-qawr*, the master of the cycle, in this cycle of ours. The Tenth has many "relative parts" running through history; what is veiled by the Prophet in this praise is the particular relative part of *this* cycle's master of the cycle, the Dai who carries the line of summons through our *qawr*.

The praise next turns to Fatimah al-Zahra, and what it says of her is among the most quietly extraordinary teachings of the entire book. The Prophet, before his passing, said of her: *khalifatu abiha*, the successor of her father — and *hujjatu baliha*, the *hujjah* of her husband. And in the inner reckoning, *afdal al-hujjah*, the most excellent *hujjah* of all, is Fatimah al-Zahra herself. She is the *hujjat al-uzma*, the greatest *hujjah*. Because of this, the first night of Rajab, the month of supreme station attributed to the Father of Imams, has its own supreme station as well: the first night of Rajab alludes to Lady Fatimah, the greatest *hujjah*.

The consequence for our doctrine is decisive. The successor whom the Prophet appointed over the Muhammadan community at the inner level was Fatimah al-Zahra. For this reason her passing came so quickly: within six months of her father, she departed. And while the Prophet was alive, whenever she arrived in his presence he would stand up and seat her in his own place. He seated her in his place. This was no mere gesture of paternal love. It was a continual public sign of who would sit, in the inner reality, in the Prophet's seat after him. We are conditioned to read "the Prophet rose for his daughter" as a charming detail of his tenderness. Read it again with the praise in mind. It is a designation, made repeatedly, in plain view.

This is also why, in this cycle, those who came after Husayn — the Imams from his progeny — are called *Aimmah Fatimiyyin*. Sayyidna Hibatullah, in *Kanz al-Ulum*, has written that the station of Fatimah al-Zahra is such that even the Pure Imams could not attain its gnosis; the Imams who follow her in the line are bewildered before her station, and for this reason they bear her name. The *qudar al-Fatimiyyah*, the Fatimi powers, are the Imams from Husayn's progeny — and the title rests on the awe in which they stand before her. Sayyidna Ibrahim bin Muhammad al-Fad has set this out clearly in his *Kanz al-Ulum*, and Sayyidna Ibrahim bin Husayn al-Hamidi wrote a parallel *Kanz al-Walad* for his son Sayyidna Hatim that takes the same vein further.

The hostility of the early caliphs was not pure ignorance of all this. Some of the matters of the realities were known to those near the family. Abu Bakr and Umar had been around long enough — from the time of Abd al-Muttalib — to have caught hints of some of these things. There are even traditions that point to it. Umar once, when a young Hasan or Husayn climbed onto the *minbar* and said, "This *minbar* belongs to my father," himself answered, "Yes, this *minbar* belongs to your father," and stepped down. He was acknowledging publicly that the *minbar*, in its inner significance, was not his. Another well-known saying among the common people goes that Umar admitted of one of these grandsons, "These hairs were not grown by you" — meaning, the hairs on your head were not grown through any work of mine, but through yours and your father's. The rank was known. That was the bitterness of the rejection.

After Fatimah the praise turns to *al-Hasan al-mustawda sirr al-imamah*, Hasan, the trustee of the secret of the Imamate. Hasan is the *mustawda*, the entrusted one who carries the secret of the Imamate forward in a particular manner. Even while bearing the trusteeship he has, like any Imam, his *nasut* and his *lahut*; he rises from the human form to the divine, as the *arbab al-zaamah*, the masters of leadership, do. From him a particular line proceeds: all the *duat* are connected to Imam Hasan. From Husayn, by contrast, the line of the Aimmah descends; the Aimmah are connected to Sayyidna Husayn. The doctrine, then, is a forked inheritance. One branch runs through Hasan to the duat who carry the summons through the eras of concealment. The other branch runs through Husayn to the Aimmah themselves. To recognize Hasan is to recognize the source of every dai.

The Quran itself points to these two branches at the very end of its recitation. "Say, I take refuge with the Lord of mankind." The *nas* here are those who are *manusina bid-dawah*, those intimate with the summoning, who have affection for it. Their Lord — the *Rabb al-Nas* — is Sayyidna Hasan. "Say, I take refuge with the Lord of the dawn, the Lord of the splitting." *Falaq* is to split. From there, from the splitting, the line of Imamate proceeds. The two final Surahs, *al-Nas* and *al-Falaq*, encode the two branches of the inheritance. The Quran closes by sending us into both refuges at once — into the Lord of those who love the call, and into the Lord of the splitting from which the line of Imams descends.

Husayn himself, as the praise notes, is the one who made manifest the occultation through death at the hands of the people of doubt. The killing at Karbala was the manifestation of an occultation. What the people of doubt saw killed was a veil. The real self was not the thing that was struck. The deeper doctrine of the Imam's veils will return again later in the book. But here at the start, the theme is set: Husayn's body was killed, his real self was protected, and the occultation of the inner Imam was, by that very killing, made publicly visible to those who could read what was happening.

The praise next embraces *al-aimmati min dhurriyyatihi majami al-anwar* — the Imams from his progeny, the assemblages of lights. The distinction between *asrar* (secrets) and *anwar* (lights) is important. *Asrar* are intentions, *niyyat*. *Anwar* are the luminous forms, *suwarat*. Sayyidna Sulayman is, in this book, the *jami al-asrar*, the assembler of the secrets, of the duat of Yemen who came after Sayyidna Dhuayb bin Musa. He gathered their secrets; he assembled their luminous forms in himself as one assembles lights. The Imams from Husayn's progeny are, in the same fashion, assemblages of luminous forms.

The praise then reaches the Imam of the present time, the hidden one: the Imam of the Time, who is concealed by the veil, hidden by the secret from all of creation. Whatever has appeared of him outwardly is the veil; the original self is hidden. And all the Imams who will come, even unto the Day of Resurrection, are veiled by the Commander of the Faithful: they are *his* veils, and he is their veiled essence. The familiar phrase "there is no power and no strength except with Allah, the High, the Great" carries this teaching inside it. The "High, the Great" — *al-Aliyy al-Azim* — here is *ayn al-aimmah*, the essence of the Imams. All the Aimmah are *hijabs* of *al-Aliyy al-Azim*. Every prayer-formula we say that names the High and the Great is, in its inner orientation, an acknowledgment of this essence veiled by every Imam.

Notice the texture of what is being said. The veiling chain is not a sequence of independent events. It is a single doctrine repeating itself in different registers. The Prophet veils Ali during the months after Ghadir Khumm. Ali veils the Prophet for the remainder of his own life. Fatimah is veiled by her father in the role of inner successor — covered by him while he lives, revealed by him in the gesture of rising to seat her in his own place. The Imams of the line are veiled by the Commander of the Faithful, who is their veiled essence. The duat are veiled by the Imams. And the Imam of the present time is veiled by every appearance — every face that might be taken for him is the veil, every secret that might disclose him is precisely what conceals. There is one doctrine and many enactments. To read the praise correctly is to hear it as variations on a single chord.

The praise also brings in *abwabihim*, the gates, whose lights shine forth in the forms, and their *hujjahs*, and the duat of this auspicious *dawr* that will last fifty thousand years. The number is striking. The *dawr al-satr*, the era of concealment, ended with Sayyidna Sulayman Sahib — seven eras, all completed. Numerically, by the reckoning, seven cycles of concealment are finished. This present *dawr* of ours, our particular *qaimi* era, is included within the great *dawr* of the Qaim: fifty thousand years of what you count. When the Qaim comes, fifty thousand years will begin from after him. So when the praise says "the duat of this auspicious *dawr* that will last for fifty thousand years," it is gathering our own duat into the company of those who carry the Qaim's era. We are not late arrivals to a finished story. We are early arrivals to a story whose horizon is fifty thousand years long.

## What this episode lands

The opening of *Kunooz al-Hikmah* has done three things before the first question is even asked. It has told us who the treasures are for — the duat, who hold *juzi ismah*, partial infallibility, and to whom the stored wisdom is addressed across the centuries so that fresh substance may keep being drawn out. It has told us what the special human form is made of — the gardens of the believers and the ranks in the regions, the very substance from which the Prophet's ascending form was woven. And it has told us how the line of veils runs — Ali in the Prophet and the Prophet in Ali, Fatimah as the inner successor to whom the Prophet rose, Hasan as the trustee from whom every dai descends and Husayn as the source of the Aimmah, the Imams as assemblages of lights, and the Imam of our own time veiled in concealment beneath the masters that hide him.

What ties these three together is a single doctrine of dignity. The newest believer is *shay*, the real thing the Quran speaks of. The believer's inner essence is the garden from which the Prophet's own *nasut* was composed. The duat who carry the call across the eras are the *arbab* to whom the treasures of wisdom are addressed. We are not the audience of this book. We are inside its address.

It is worth noticing how rare this combination is. Most opening prayers in scholarly Arabic books address themselves to a learned reader and quickly leave the believer behind, returning to him only as the object of exhortation. The opening of *Kunooz al-Hikmah* does the opposite. It begins with a praise that names the highest figures of the tradition and, in the very same breath, refuses to leave the believer out. The First Intellect and the newest believer are made equal. The Prophet's ascending body is woven of the believer's substance. The Dai rises from his own seat for the believer who has come to greet him. The believer is not gathered in at the end as an afterthought; the believer is held at the center of the doctrine from the first sentence. That is a deliberate choice. The author wants us to read everything that follows with the certainty that we have not been excused from any of it. Whatever doctrine he is about to unfold concerning the cycles of concealment, the ranks of the hierarchy, the inner reading of the Quran, the secrets of the sacred letters — all of it is being said for us, because the wisdom is stored to be drawn out, and the ones who will draw it out include the believer who has only just entered the circle.

## Closing

The next episode picks up at the place where the praise narrows from the family of light to the specific duat named by title in the opening — a quiet sequence that doubles as a course in the history of the line — and then turns to the author's invocation itself, where he prays for the Dai of the time, prays for the *hawzah* against the Pharaoh of the age, and lays down a discipline of attribution and a covenant about who may even read what he is about to write. The treasures, by then, will have been formally entrusted to those for whom they were stored. The questions can begin in earnest.
