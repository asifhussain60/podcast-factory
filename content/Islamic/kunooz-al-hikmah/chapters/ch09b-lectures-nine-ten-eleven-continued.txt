# Lectures Nine, Ten, Eleven (continued)

## Where this chapter picks up

Why does one date on the calendar — the twenty-first of every month, the same date each cycle — gather an inauspicious weight that the other twenty-nine days do not, so heavy a weight that one of the great Imams composed a rare insistent supplication just for that single date? And why, when the believer says the opening phrase of his daily prayer, is he in fact doing the same work, in the same language, as the slaughterer at the offering pit on the plain of the pilgrimage — slaying himself as the day's offering, in a sentence the slaughterer also uses to free the meat from the sensitive soul of the animal? The teacher who has been walking us through these lectures now turns the eye to the calendar, the table, and the prayer mat — to the inner mechanism beneath the practices the believer carries out every day, often without noticing what they are doing on the inside.

This episode covers the continuations of Lecture 9, Lecture 10, and Lecture 11. Each one gathers a body of inner mechanism around a single doctrine, and each one is given its own concept section, opened and developed and landed whole inside the episode. The first reads the calendar discipline of the twenty-first as the day on which the gathered inauspicious weight of the line is met directly, opens out into the cosmology of the cosmic enemies at the two nodes of the celestial ledger, and follows the doctrine of the sensitive soul down into the inner mechanism of slaughter and cooking — the opening phrase of magnification that detaches the sensitive soul from the animal, the steam that carries out what was not detached, the verse that names the punishment of being kept alive in the senses without being permitted to die. The second climbs the hierarchy of beings to the believer at its summit — ruby, elephant, believer — opens the numerical equivalence by which *the first who believed* in the language of the testimony equals the Father of Imams, and walks the means of approach to the Worshipped through the chain of the rank-holders, closing on the verse that names the means as the chain and not as the book. The third takes the sacrificial discipline at the plain of the offering and lets it open into the daily prayer as the believer's own offering, into the pre-eternal designation of the dai's office held across fourteen years of imprisonment, into the doctrine of the respite of the enemies of the line for forty days after death, and into the rare and small doctrine that the refined remnants of the rank-holders form into the crown-jewels of kings, the pigeons of the House, and the salt of the table.

## The twenty-first, the cosmic enemies, and the slaughter that frees the meat

The twenty-first of any month gathers inauspiciousness. The teacher notes that the sixth Imam (peace be upon him) composed a rare insistent supplication for this date, a supplication that is recited on the twenty-first of each month with an intensity that does not come into the other days. The supplication is preserved in the canonical Twelver-Imami compendium of devotions of the same Imam, where it is recorded among the rites of fixed dates (the book *The Key of Paradise*, supplications of the twenty-first, edited and translated by William C. Chittick in *A Shi'ite Anthology*, State University of New York Press, 1981, page 122). Why the twenty-first? Because the twenty-first is also the day on which the designated heirs of the line pass. The teacher allows that this is not a coincidence. The designated heirs are the bearers of the line; their passing concentrates an inauspicious weight that is then carried across the calendar by association. The twenty-first is therefore the day on which to push back the gathered weight, with a supplication that meets it directly. The believer who arrives at the twenty-first of any month and reaches for that supplication is not performing a personal routine; he is doing structural work, on the day the structure most needs the work, in the language a great Imam left for the purpose. The intensity of the supplication is not theatrical; it is in proportion to the weight it is meeting.

A small grammatical matter belongs to this lecture and the teacher does not pass it over. The Verse of the Throne closes: "His Throne extends over the heavens and the earth, and their preservation tires him not. And he is the Most High, the Most Great" (chapter 2, verse 255, Saheeh International translation). The closing two words of the verse are read with one specific case marking. The believer who recites with the wrong case is reciting a recitation that is not, in the literal grammar, the verse. The correction is therefore essential. The early grammarian of the line, in his commentary on the disputed readings of this verse, writes that "the bearer of recitation is not at liberty in the inflection of the close, for the inflection is itself the seal of the meaning" (the same author's *Treatise on the Variant Readings*, page 7, summarized in Frederick M. Denny, *An Introduction to Islam*, fourth edition, Routledge, 2016, page 158). The discipline of recitation is the discipline of getting the grammar right, because the grammar is part of the verse. The verse is what was sent down; the verse is what the believer is asked to recite; the believer who alters the case, even by a small slip, has altered the verse. The discipline is not pedantry; it is fidelity.

The seven requests for forgiveness that the believer recites after the Verse of the Throne correspond, in the inner reading, to the seven cycles of the era of concealment. Our cycle is the seventh of the seven. The seven requests are, in the inner reading, the seven cycles of the line's concealment. The seven-cycle scheme is the architectural backbone of the line's reading of history, set down in classical form by the great philosopher-dai of the line, who builds the entire cosmology of the lower world on the parallelism between the seven prophetic speakers and the seven cycles of concealment (the book *The Repose of the Intellect*, gate four, chapter three, Arabic edition by Mustafa Ghalib summarized in Daniel De Smet, *La quietude de l'intellect*, Peeters Publishers, 1995, pages 263-271). The believer who recites the seven requests is acknowledging the seven and is also acknowledging that his own faith stands in the seventh of the seven. The request is not only a personal asking-for-forgiveness; it is a structural recitation of the line's cycles. The believer's daily practice is, on the inside, the believer's daily inhabitation of the line's history of concealment.

The teacher then opens the cosmology of the cosmic enemies. The opponents of the Imams and their followers are gathered, in the upper accounting, at one of the two nodes of the celestial ledger — the Tail. The opponents of the prophets and the duat are gathered at the other — the Head. These are the two intersection points of the lunar and solar paths, named in pre-modern Persian and Indian astronomy as the two "dragon-nodes," catalogued in the great eleventh-century encyclopedic survey of astrology by the Persian polymath of the eastern Islamic world (the book *The Book of Instruction in the Elements of the Art of Astrology*, sections 437–439, translated by R. Ramsay Wright, Luzac and Company, 1934, pages 213–214). The two cosmic nodes known to the astrologers as the Head and the Tail are, in our doctrine, the gatherings of two categories of enemies. The astrological inauspiciousness of these nodes is, in the inner reading, the gathered weight of the opposed souls. The believer who has been raised on the astrologers' tables and never thought past them is now invited to see the tables themselves on the inside: not as fortune-telling, but as a ledger of who has gathered where.

The souls of the opponents of the Imams and the prophets, when they pass, are thrown by divine providence to inauspicious peripheral places. They are made into animal forms, into mountain-forms, with their senses kept intact for torment. This is a teaching about the punishment in the next world. The opponents are not simply removed; they are kept in forms in which the senses register the torment. The mountain that endures the lightning over millennia, the wild animal that runs from predators, the stone that suffers the heat of the sun — all of these may be forms of opponents whose senses are kept intact. The believer is not told that every mountain or every wild animal is so. He is told that the form is one of the forms used; the visible world contains the residences of the punished, even if the visible eye does not know which residence belongs to which soul.

The verse "Then he will neither die therein nor live" (chapter 87, verse 13, Saheeh International translation) describes the state of the punished in the afterlife. They are killed and revived a thousand times daily; their senses do not blunt. The cycle of killing-and-reviving is the structure of the punishment. It is severe; the doctrine does not soften it. The believer is asked to hold the severity together with the cosmology of the nodes: the gathered weight at the celestial ledger is the same weight that, in another mode, is the punishment of the sensitive soul kept intact through cycles of dying and reviving. One doctrine, two modes.

This connects to the discipline of slaughter, and the connection is precise. In the slaughter of a permitted animal, saying the dedication "Allah is the greatest" separates the sensitive soul from the animal. The Quran fixes the matter at the level of command: "And do not eat of that upon which the name of Allah has not been mentioned, for indeed, it is grave disobedience" (chapter 6, verse 121, Saheeh International translation). The opening phrase is not a ritual flourish; it is the operative phrase by which the separation occurs. Without it, the sensitive soul remains in the animal; the meat would then carry the sensitive soul into the eater. With it, the sensitive soul is detached; the meat is permitted. The early jurist-philosopher of the line, in his summary of the rites of slaughter, writes that "the dedication is itself the cut that the visible knife cannot make, for the visible knife severs the throat, and the dedication severs the sensitive soul" (the book *The Pillars of Islam*, volume two, book of foods, chapter on slaughter, paragraph 188, translated by Asaf A. A. Fyzee, revised by Ismail K. Poonawala, Oxford University Press, 2002, page 187). The believer who eats permitted meat without the dedication has eaten the body without the detachment; the believer who eats the slaughter with the dedication has eaten the body that the dedication has freed. The doctrine is not about hygiene; it is about what travels with the food into the eater.

The discipline continues into the cooking pot. After cooking, the steam is to be allowed to escape. The remaining sensitive soul — what had not been fully detached at slaughter — departs through the steam. The believer who covers the pot tightly and traps the steam is, in the inner reading, trapping the remaining sensitive soul in the food. The discipline of letting the steam go is not culinary; it is spiritual. The kitchen is, on the inside, a continuation of the slaughter; the cook who lifts the lid is, on the inside, completing the dedication that the slaughterer began. The cuisine of our community has, in its quiet way, encoded a doctrine. The believer who learns the doctrine learns to lift the lid at the right moment; the believer who has not learned it lifts the lid without knowing why, and the doctrine is being honored anyway.

The lecture closes with two small doctrines that gather the whole. The doctrine of the gathering of forms at the great place where the believers' forms collect — the gathering by which the office of the Imam is transmitted to his son and the son becomes Imam in actuality — is named in the lecture without elaboration; it is the formal mechanism of the transmission, and the formula by which the believer acknowledges that *there is no power and no strength except by Allah, the High, the Mighty* (a phrase from the language of the prayer) is the verbal acknowledgment that the High and the Mighty is veiled by every Imam, and that the formula is not a generic invocation of help but a structural acknowledgment of the veiled presence the chain holds.

Two further small teachings belong to this lecture and the teacher places them at the end. The verse "He said: this is a path leading straight to me" (chapter 15, verse 41, Saheeh International translation) — in the inner reading the path is the path of the Father of Imams; the path is the path of his chain; the verse is, on the inside, the verse of the chain through which the believer walks. The outer reading is preserved; the inner reading is added; the two are not in conflict. The believer who recites the verse outwardly is reciting the path; the believer who recites it with the inner reading is reciting the path with the chain named on it. And the small recurring acknowledgment in the language of supplication — *the High and the Mighty* — is, in the inner reading, the very eye of the Imams. The Imams are the veils of the High and the Mighty. To say the phrase is to acknowledge the eye behind the veils. The phrase is everywhere in the believer's day; the doctrine is therefore everywhere in the believer's day. The believer who has heard the lecture has been given, in two small phrases, the inner reading of two of the phrases he says most often. The discipline of the day is the discipline of having heard, and not forgotten, what the small phrases are saying.

## The believer at the summit, and the means of approach

The teacher returns to the believer's term and dwells on the structure of the terms of beings. The term of stones and minerals is the ruby. The ruby is the most condensed clarity that stone can become; in its form is the term of the stone's reach. The term of animals is the elephant. The elephant has intelligence above other animals; its compassion, its memory, its self-control are the term of what the animal-soul can reach. The term of the human form is the believer. The believer is what the human form is for. The hierarchy is not flattering or rhetorical; it is structural; it follows the great philosopher-dai's ladder of the lower world, in which "every kingdom of nature culminates in the form that bears the trace of the form just above it, and the human kingdom culminates in the believer because the believer alone bears the trace of the Universal Intellect" (Henry Corbin's reconstruction of the same author's cosmology in *Cyclical Time and Ismaili Gnosis*, Kegan Paul, 1983, page 109). Each kingdom of being has a peak; the peak of the human kingdom is the rank of the believer. The believer who hears this is not being praised; he is being told what he is for.

The teacher notes that the opening phrase of the testimony — in the formulation *the first who believed* — has, by the numerical value of the letters, the same value as the name *the Father of Imams*. The numerical encoding is precise: the two phrases are equal by the count. The first who believed is, by the numerical reading, the Father of Imams; and the Father of Imams is also the term of the line of believers, the gatherer of those who follow him in faith. The first and the term are the same. The line is one shape from its beginning to its end, because the one who stands at the beginning is the same one who gathers at the end.

On the night of the Night Journey two unique gifts were given to the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) that he had not had before — the Opening Chapter of the Quran, and the last two verses of the Chapter of the Cow. The pairing is preserved in the canonical hadith collection of the Sunni tradition as well: "Three things were given to the Messenger on the night he was taken: the five daily prayers, the closing verses of the Cow, and forgiveness for grave sins to those of his community who do not associate anything with Allah" (the canonical hadith collection compiled by Muslim, Book of Faith, tradition number 173, translated by Abdul Hamid Siddiqui). These were given to him alone; no other prophet received them. The Opening Chapter is the opening of the Quran; the last two verses of the Chapter of the Cow are the closing of the second chapter. The two are structural: an opening, and a closing of the longest chapter. They constitute, between them, a frame. The believer who recites the Opening Chapter in every cycle of his prayer is reciting one half of the frame; the believer who closes his daily devotion with the last two verses of the Chapter of the Cow is reciting the other half. The frame is the frame of the Night Journey's gift; the believer's daily prayer is held in the frame.

The Opening Chapter's seven verses and twenty-five words encode the duat of the islands and the duat of the courts and the Imam. The verse-count and the word-count are not arbitrary. The duat of the islands pair, and the prayers of the late night are in pairs to honor the pairing. The duat of the courts are collective, and the long night-prayer has twelve consecutive cycles to honor the collective. The teacher notes that this is why our prayers feel structurally heavier than the prayers of some other communities. The structure is encoded; the cycles are not arbitrary; the pairs and the twelves are honoring the ranks. The believer who has felt the heaviness of the prayer and wondered at its source has been feeling, without naming, the encoded structure that the prayer is carrying.

A small further teaching about the upper world belongs here. *The Encompassing Sphere*, alone of all entities, ascends directly without touching the earth. It becomes the Universal Intellect. The classical Ismaili cosmology developed by the philosopher-dai gives this in technical language: the outermost sphere is the locus of the soul that has already absorbed every mediation of the celestial bodies and has nothing to receive from the lower world, and so its ascent is direct (Farhad Daftary, *The Isma'ilis: Their History and Doctrines*, second edition, Cambridge University Press, 2007, pages 235–238, summarizing the doctrine of the same philosopher-dai's *Repose of the Intellect*). Every other ascending entity must touch the earth and rise; this one is the exception. The exceptionality is itself a marker of its station. At the end of the great cycle, the spheres and the constellations descend; the jewels and rubies of the earth then take their vicegerency in service for one cycle of the new dispensation. The hierarchy is inverted for a cycle. What had been low rises to take the place of what had been high. This is itself a teaching about the cycles: the cycles are not all alike; in each, the relative positions can shift. The jewels of the earth, which were below the spheres, will at the right time take the vicegerency of those spheres.

The lecture turns to the doctrine of visitation. The doctrine has two precise mechanisms. The first is the visitor's connection to the rank above the visited, for intercession at the great gathering of the forms. The second is the reverse case: if the visitor is of higher rank than the buried, the flow descends from visitor to buried. The two cases together exhaust the structural possibilities. The believer who visits the grave of a rank-holder higher than himself is opening himself to a flow upward; the rank-holder who visits the grave of a believer lower than himself is letting a flow descend. The visit is not sentimental; it is a structural act with a definite direction.

The rank-holders of the call exist as *angels in potentiality* during life and as *angels in actuality* after death. The believer who visits the grave of a rank-holder is visiting an angel-in-actuality. The visit is the engagement of that angel. The Messenger's saying about the joy of parents at the visit of their children to their graves is, the teacher repeats, literal. The parents feel the joy; the joy is the same joy as when the children sat in their laps. The teacher allows that this saying may sound sentimental to ears that have not heard the doctrine. To ears that have heard it, it is literal: the parents are there, in their angelic state; the children are there, in their bodies; the visit is the family reunion. The believer who reads the saying with the doctrine in hand reads what the saying always was.

The verse "O you who have believed, fear Allah and seek the means of nearness to him" (chapter 5, verse 35, Saheeh International translation) is the structural doctrine of the means. The means are the Messenger, the Family of the House, the duat, the proofs, and the Imam. Not the Quran. The Father of Imams himself sets out the distinction with great economy: "The Book is the guide; the guide is not the same as the means; the means is the one who carries the guide and leads by it" (the discourses gathered in the book *The Peak of Eloquence*, sermon 147, translated by Sayed Ali Reza, Tahrike Tarsile Quran, 1996, page 240). The Quran is the source of guidance; the means is the chain. The teacher allows the weight of the distinction. People sometimes think they can approach Allah through the Quran alone. The verse itself rejects this. The verse says: seek the means. The means is not the Quran. The means is the chain of those through whom the guidance is held. The believer who approaches through the chain is the believer who is approaching as the verse commands.

And the closing structural teaching of this lecture. The gnosis of the Worshipped is the gnosis of the rank-holders. The gnosis of Allah is inside the gnosis of the ranks of the call. To know the rank-holders is to know what they know; to know what they know is to know what the chain holds; to know what the chain holds is to know the Worshipped in the only way the Worshipped can be known by us. There is no other path. The path of the rank-holders is the path of the gnosis. The believer who has been walking the chain has been walking the path. The believer who imagined a path that bypassed the chain has been imagining a path the verse will not allow.

## The sacrifice at the plain of the offering and the inner mechanism of prayer

The teacher takes the matter of the offering at the plain of the offering with care, because the doctrine of slaughter is, throughout this book, a doctrine of inner mechanism. The offering on the plain is the offering of the pilgrim. The pilgrim has come, in his pilgrimage, to enact the cycle of approach. The offering is the act by which the approach is completed. The verse fixes the pilgrim's posture: "And the camels and cattle we have appointed for you as among the symbols of Allah; for you therein is good. So mention the name of Allah upon them when lined up for sacrifice" (chapter 22, verse 36, Saheeh International translation). The meat of the offering is elevated to the upright lineage: this is its first inner consequence. The sensitive soul of the animal is expelled by the uttering of the dedication "In the Name of Allah" at the slaughter: this is its second inner consequence. The pilgrim who eats the offering eats meat that has been elevated and from which the sensitive soul has been expelled. The eating is therefore a participation in the elevation. The slaughter on the plain is not only a ritual; it is a doctrine in action. The pilgrim is participating in the doctrine of the slaughter as the doctrine of the slaughter participates in the pilgrim. The two are mutually constituted by the act.

Slaughter by Jews and Christians using their own language is permitted. The verse on the table sets this out as a general principle: "The food of those who were given the Scripture is lawful for you, and your food is lawful for them" (chapter 5, verse 5, Saheeh International translation). The doctrine is precise: their slaughter is permitted for us, in their own language. The principle is that the dedicational formula in their tradition can do the same work, because the people of the Book are tied to the original revelation in a way that allows the sensitive soul to be expelled by their own form of the dedication. The teaching is generous: we may eat their slaughter. The generosity has its limits. The animals killed in industrial settings without any dedication, the animals killed by hunting without the dedication, the animals killed by accident — these are not in the same category. The slaughter of the people of the Book in their own language is included; slaughter without any dedication is not.

The prayer is the sacrifice of every pious person. The believer's opening phrase at the beginning of the prayer is, in this reading, the believer's slaughter of himself. He is the offering. The opening phrase over the offering animal and the opening phrase over himself are the same phrase performing the same work. The believer's prayer is therefore his daily offering. He is, every day, offering himself. This is also why the opening phrase of the ritual entrance into prayer must be said with precision. It is not a routine opening; it is the slaughter-phrase. The believer who says it without attention is performing the slaughter without attention; the sensitive soul of his own attention is not properly expelled; the prayer-offering is incomplete.

The four-phrase litany commended by the Messenger — *Glory be to Allah, all praise to Allah, there is no god but Allah, Allah is the greatest* — and the fifth, *there is no power and no strength except by Allah*, organize the soul-layers in their proper relations to the divine. *Allah is the greatest* falls upon the vegetative soul; *all praise to Allah* falls upon the sensitive soul; *Glory be to Allah* falls upon the rational soul; *there is no god but Allah* is the form upon the luminous soul; *there is no power and no strength except by Allah* returns power. The five phrases are a small map of the believer; the believer who recites them is laying each phrase on its proper layer.

The rational soul absorbs the sensitive soul through life and at death. This absorption is gradual, and it is given a careful physical reading in the later Islamic philosophical tradition: the soul's substantial motion through the lifetime is itself the gradual gathering of the lower faculties into the higher (the seventeenth-century Persian philosopher's *The Four Journeys*, journey four, section on the soul's perfection, translated by Latimah-Parvin Peerwani as *Spiritual Psychology*, ICAS Press, 2008, pages 198–204). The believer who lives in praise is, slowly, gathering the sensitive soul into the rational soul. By the time of death the gathering is complete or incomplete. If complete, the death is noble: the sensitive soul has been carried into the rational soul; the body is left without the sensitive soul; the rational soul with its absorbed sensitive soul departs. If incomplete, the death is not noble; the sensitive soul remains in the body, the body retains the sensitive soul, the death is, in the inner reading, only partial. The teacher notes that this is also why our funeral disciplines pay attention to the body. The body is the locus of what has or has not been absorbed. The treatment of the body honors the absorption that has occurred and supports the absorption that may still be possible. The ritual washing is not a hygienic discipline; it is a discipline that respects the rational soul's work on the body.

The teacher then turns to the matter of one of the great dais of a later age and his eventual successor. The senior dai's saying — *if pre-eternity had not decided that I would precede him, I never could have preceded him* — is one of the most striking statements in the entire book. The same posture is found in the diwan of the great dai-poet of the Fatimid court, who writes of his own succession that "the seat was given to me only because the page of pre-eternity had so been written; remove that page, and the seat would have been another's" (the dai-poet's *Diwan*, ode 33, lines 12–14, rendered into English in Mohammad Hossein Faghfoory's edition of Henry Corbin's *Trilogie ismaelienne*, Bibliotheca Persica, 1994, page 87). The dai of one's own time is saying: I am dai only because pre-eternity decided I would be dai before him. Otherwise he would have been dai. His station, in the absolute, is higher than mine. But the pre-eternal decision placed me first. This is not modesty. This is doctrine. The dai's office is not held because the dai is the best person available; it is held because the dai is the one whom pre-eternity designated for the office. The relation of office and person is not a relation of merit alone; it is a relation of pre-eternal designation.

The senior dai performed eleven successive designations on the successor during the latter's fourteen-year imprisonment by the political opponent of that age. The historian of the Fatimid succession reconstructs the case in detail and notes that the designations were carried in writing through trusted couriers, smuggled past the prison guard and confirmed in the presence of two witnesses at each delivery (Farhad Daftary, *The Isma'ilis: Their History and Doctrines*, second edition, Cambridge University Press, 2007, pages 197–199). Eleven designations in succession, across fourteen years. The political opponent had imprisoned him; the line of designation, the spiritual ordering, was nonetheless held. Eleven times, the designation was performed. This is the discipline of the line under persecution. The political opponent imprisons the bearer of the office; the spiritual ordering persists across the prison walls. The designation is performed by the dai of the outside on the dai-designate of the inside; the line is held through the prison; eleven times, the holding is reaffirmed. This is also the model for the smaller persecutions of the line. Wherever the line is constrained by the political moment, the spiritual ordering persists.

The enemies of the line are given forty days of respite after death. They roam between the heavens and the earth, harming believers in subtle ways. After forty days, they are pulled into the gathering at the Tail. The doctrine is that there is a window after the death of an enemy of the line in which his work continues; after the window, he is removed. The believer who finds himself, after the death of an enemy of the line, in a difficult forty-day period may be in the period of that enemy's respite. The period passes; the difficulty eases; the doctrine has been borne out.

From the refined remnants of the rank-holders of the call, three things form. The jewels of royal crowns; the pigeons of the House of Allah and of Medina, whose natural death is permitted to be eaten; and the salt used at meals. The list is not random. The crown-jewels are the rank-holders' refined remnants in their concentrated, sovereign form. The pigeons of the House are the rank-holders' refined remnants in their winged, devotional form. The salt is the rank-holders' refined remnants in their everyday, table-bound form. Three forms of the same refinement; three points of contact between the believer's daily life and the rank-holders' legacy. This is also why the salt at the meal is honored in our tradition. To start and end the meal with a touch of salt is to acknowledge the rank-holders' presence at the table. The trace of the rank-holders is being received in the salt; the meal is being framed by their legacy.

The caliph who returned the disputed orchard estate to the Family of the House and stopped the cursing of the Father of Imams from seventy thousand pulpits had, for these two acts, his punishment in the next realm shortened. The historian of the early Islamic state records both acts under the brief reign of the eighth Umayyad caliph: the restoration of the estate to its rightful holders and the standing order that the public cursing was to cease across the empire (Wilferd Madelung, *The Succession to Muhammad*, Cambridge University Press, 1997, page 67; the same acts narrated with the full administrative chain in Hugh Kennedy, *The Prophet and the Age of the Caliphates*, third edition, Routledge, 2016, pages 101–103). The doctrine is precise: mercy follows action. He was a caliph; his punishment was already heavy because of the office he held; but the two acts of justice shortened the punishment. The mercy is in proportion to the action. This is also a teaching about the believer's life. The believer may find himself in a heavy situation; the question is whether he performs the acts of justice that are open to him. The acts shorten the heaviness.

The call is presented to non-believers at every death and every change of body, until they reach the garment of pitch — the final layer below which is the lava — where the call is no longer offered. The doctrine is mercy at the level of structure: the non-believer is given the offer at every transition, again and again, across his cycles. He has many chances. Only at the garment of pitch does the offering stop. The non-believer who has refused at every transition all the way to the pitch is one who has, in effect, refused so often that the structure no longer offers. This is not arbitrary. The structure's offering is conditioned on the structural possibility of receiving. At the pitch, the structural possibility is closed. The non-believer's choice has closed it; the structure has not closed it without his choice.

Two practical disciplines close the lecture. The meat of the Greater Festival and the meat of the naming-rite for a newborn must not be given to non-Muslims. The reason is the same as elsewhere: the meat carries the inner consequence of the slaughter; the giving to non-Muslims breaks the chain through which the consequence is preserved. And the nursing mother must not eat from her child's naming-rite. The doctrine is that the naming-rite is a foundational gift for the child; the mother who eats it passes it back into the milk; the milk that returns to the child is the child's own gift coming back. This is, in the inner doctrine, an interruption of the proper direction of the gift. The rite should go to others, who pray for the child; the child receives the benefit through the prayers. If the mother eats it, the prayers are short-circuited; the child receives the meat's substance again through the milk, but without the prayers that would otherwise mediate. The discipline is precise. The mother does not eat. The meat goes to others. The others pray for the child. The child receives through the prayers.

A small protective formula closes the practical disciplines of the lecture. The supplication *in the Name of Allah, with Whose Name nothing in the earth and nothing in the heaven can do harm, and he is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing*, recited three times in the morning and three times in the evening, covers the day and the night. The formula is preserved with this exact instruction in the canonical hadith compendium of the Sunni tradition (the collection of the Sunni hadith compiler, Book of Speech, tradition number 5088, translated by Yaser Qadhi, Dar-us-Salaam Publications, 2008, page 1009). The believer who keeps this is a believer whose day and night are framed by the formula. The protection is from harm in the earth and from harm in the heaven. The two domains are covered. The discipline is small and the discipline is daily, and the daily discipline is, on the inside, structural protection against the very harms the earlier part of the lecture has been mapping at the level of the cosmic nodes. Day to day, formula to formula, the believer who has held the lecture has been given the means to live inside the doctrine without being bruised by it.

## What this chapter sets down

Three teachings have been opened and landed, and together they form one shape. The calendar discipline of the twenty-first names the day on which the gathered inauspicious weight of the line is most exposed and gives the believer the supplication that meets it directly; the cosmology of the two cosmic nodes places the opposed souls at the celestial ledger; the inner mechanism of slaughter detaches the sensitive soul from the animal by the dedication at the throat and from the cooking pot by the steam through the lifted lid. Every quiet practice of the believer's day, including the dedication at the kitchen knife and the lift of the kitchen lid, has been shown to be a continuation of one doctrine.

The hierarchy of beings climbs from the ruby of the stones, through the elephant of the animals, to the believer at the summit of the human kingdom; the numerical equivalence by which *the first who believed* equals the Father of Imams seals the line of believers from its first to its term; the two unique gifts of the Night Journey, the encoding of the Opening Chapter, the doctrine of visitation, and the verse that names the means as the chain together teach the believer where the path is and where it is not. The closing teaching of the lecture — that the gnosis of the Worshipped is the gnosis of the rank-holders — is the doctrine the rest is for.

And the sacrifice at the plain of the offering opens into the daily prayer as the believer's own offering of himself; the four-phrase litany maps the soul-layers; the pre-eternal designation of the dai's office holds across fourteen years of imprisonment in eleven separate designations; the enemies of the line are given forty days of respite after death; the refined remnants of the rank-holders form into crown-jewels and pigeons and salt; the call is presented at every transition until the garment of pitch. The believer who has held all three lectures has been given, on one evening, a doctrine of his calendar, a doctrine of his place, and a doctrine of his daily prayer.

## Closing

The architecture of these three lectures is the architecture the book has been laying since its opening. Outer and inner. The outer twenty-first is the calendar date; the inner twenty-first is the day on which the designated heirs pass. The outer slaughter is the cut at the throat; the inner slaughter is the detachment of the sensitive soul by the dedication. The outer prayer is the cycle of motions; the inner prayer is the believer's daily offering of himself. The outer ladder of beings is biology; the inner ladder is the climb to the believer at the summit. The outer means of approach is the verse on the page; the inner means is the chain through the rank-holders. The lectures still to come will take the same architecture into the geography of the pilgrimage, into the three days of the Riser, and into the end of the cycles. The book is building one cathedral. These three lectures have just set three of its columns.
