# The Cycles, the Cave, and the Precision of Return

## Where this chapter picks up

What if a Greek philosopher who died centuries before the Messenger was born — a man whose books reach us only through Syriac and Arabic translations — was, in the inner reckoning of this line, *from us*? What if the line of the Family of the House is older than every visible biography, and the figures we name are only the late part of a longer list whose earlier members carried our work in places and ages we do not know? The teacher who has been moving us through these lectures now opens that proposition in three directions, one after another, and the book changes shape around them.

This chapter covers the continuations of the sixth, seventh, and eighth lectures. Each continuation lands one whole teaching and is given its own concept section, opened and developed and landed in turn. The first reads the Messenger's own self-description — that he is the son of the recurring cycles and the one tried by the times — and lays out the two modes by which the line appears across those cycles, the mode of actual persons and the mode of representational presences in figures whose outer biographies seem to come from outside us. The second reads the cave verse of the chapter on repentance as the line's first separation: the tranquility descended on the Messenger alone, and not on the one with him in the cave; the consequences run forward into the gathering at the shed of the tribe, the body of the Messenger left unburied while allegiance was extracted at swordpoint, and the witness preserved by an oath. The third takes the doctrine of return — the conservation law of the dawah — and gives it with the precision the lecture demands: same rank, same place, fixed thousand-year cycle, and the explicit refusal to put the soul back into the same body. None of the three is a polemic. Each is given as doctrine; each rests on a verse; each leaves the listener able to see what the verse, on the inside, was saying all along.

## The cycles, the witnesses, and the Family of the House in unexpected places

The teacher begins from a saying of the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) that the book treats as a structural fact. *I am the son of the recurring cycles and the one tried by the times.* It is one of the self-naming sayings preserved in the early biographies and gathered into the discourses of the sixth Imam (one of the canonical hadith collections, Book of the Excellences, tradition 4189, translated by Abdul Hamid Siddiqui). The Messenger is naming himself by two terms at once. He is the son of the cycles — the issue of a long history that gathered itself in him. And he is the one whom the times tried — the one through whom a thorough sifting was carried out across his whole life. To be the son of the cycles, the teacher explains, is not a metaphor. The Messenger in the inner reality has passed through cycles. Cycle after cycle his being has gathered itself. The forty figures who came before the messengership in his outer biography — his ancestors — are themselves arrangements of cycles; his life, when finally manifest in the Arabian peninsula, is the manifestation of what was prepared across them. He is the son of those cycles. His being is what they were directed toward.

To be the one tried by the times, in this language, is not to have passed a single examination. The trial here is not a passing test; it is a thorough sifting. The times have tested the Messenger. They have stripped him of comfort; they have set him among hostile peoples; they have raised his nearest enemies in his own town; they have surrounded him with those who would betray him at every turn; and through all of this his preservation has held. The Quran names the structural shape of this trial directly: "And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger and a loss of wealth and lives and fruits, but give good tidings to the patient" (chapter 2, verse 155, Saheeh International translation). The seven trials before the messengership and the seven after, the fourteen of the Father of Imams (peace be upon him) in parallel, the forty days of the master of one of the great later dais — these are not catalog entries. They are the proper biography of the office. The office of the speaker, of the designated heir, of the dai is constituted by being tried.

The teacher pauses here and presses the point onto the believer's own life. *A believer who feels his trials must remember that he is being shaped by them in the only way the office below the Prophet can be shaped.* The Father of Imams gave this teaching its compressed form: "Trials are the touchstone of men; through them the noble are distinguished from the base" (the sayings collected in the book *The Peak of Eloquence*, saying 217, translated by Sayed Ali Reza). We are not the preserved; but our small trials are the discipline of our small share of the office. The trial is not a side-effect of the office; it is the office itself, in the small share that the believer carries. To resent the trial is to resent the share. To bear the trial is to grow into the share. The teaching is simple and exact, and it does not pretend the bearing is light.

There is, the teacher adds, a precise dignity in this reading of the trial. Without it, the trials of the believer are simply unpleasantness, to be endured because there is no alternative. With it, the trials are the only material out of which the believer's share of the office can be shaped. The trial is no longer a brute fact; it is a workshop. The bearing of the trial is no longer a passive endurance; it is the laying down of one more layer of the share. The believer who refuses this reading is not, in the doctrine, spared the trial; he simply loses the share that the trial was forming. The trial happens either way. The shaping happens only if the trial is borne in the shape the doctrine names.

The doubling of seven before the messengership with seven after, and the further doubling of fourteen for the Father of Imams, is the structural witness to the rate. The teacher does not say that every believer's life will have a count of seven or fourteen trials. He says that the office at the top of the line was shaped by these counts and that the office below the top is shaped in some lower proportion. The believer's count is his count; the count is not arbitrary; the count is the count of the layers that the believer's share has been formed in. The forty days of the master of one of the later great dais is a third instance of the same structure: a designated stretch of trial, walked through to the end, that constitutes the office that follows it.

From here the lecture opens its central claim. The sixth Imam said: *we were in the past cycles in our persons and in our representatives* (one of the great masters of the line records this saying in his book *The Foundations of Symbolic Interpretation*, chapter five, page 84; English rendering by W. Ivanow, Bombay edition). Two modes of presence are kept distinct. The mode of *persons* is the mode in which the very person of the Imam, the very person of the dai, the very person of the rank-holders of the call is present. The mode of *representatives* is different. It is the mode of the eyes, the agents, the figures who in any given era stand for the line even if they are not the line's central persons. The line, on the inside, has always been one; on the outside, it has been carried sometimes by its own named persons and sometimes by figures who, in their own time, were its representatives.

Then the teacher lets the strangeness land. *Aristotle is from the Family of the House.* The Greek philosopher whose works have come down to us through translations was, in the words of the sixth Imam, from us. The teacher allowed the statement to sit. In what sense? Not in the sense of *persons*; not in the sense that Aristotle was, biographically, one of the Imams or one of the duat. But in the sense of *representatives*. Aristotle, in his cycle, was a representational presence of our line, carrying our work into the philosophical tradition of his time. The early masters called him by a doubled name — *the father of Muhammad's Aristotle* — the Aristotle whose work would, centuries later, be received into our tradition with the recognition that he had been one of our representatives. The strangeness of the statement is what makes it useful: it forbids the listener to draw the line of the line tightly around the persons he can name. The line is broader than the catalog.

The Persian companion is the parallel case from inside the Messenger's own circle. He is not, in his outward biography, an Arab. He comes from Persia; he has passed through the religions of his time before reaching the Messenger. But in the inner reality he was already from the ranks of the call. The Messenger himself sealed this with the saying preserved in the canonical biography of the line: "The Persian companion is from us, the Family of the House" (a hadith reported by the early biographer in his *Tabaqat*, volume four, page 84, translated by S. Moinul Haq). He is the only person in the era of concealment who became the Door of Doors for both the Messenger and the Designated Heir. His double Door of Doors is the mark of his exception. His outward biography is not the measure of his inner reality, and the doubling is what places him as a special case for the whole doctrine of representatives.

The pairing of these two cases — the philosopher of Athens and the Persian of Medina — is itself a piece of teaching. They are placed side by side not because they resemble each other in any outer way, but because each shows, from a different angle, that the inner reality of the line does not coincide with the outer biography it adopts. Aristotle is a representative of our line working inside the philosophical tradition of his own age; the philosophy he wrote is the form his service took in his time. The Persian companion is a person of the line whose outward biography ran through other religions before settling at the Messenger's circle; the religions he passed through are the form his preparation took. The first case extends the line outward into what looks like a foreign tradition. The second case extends the line inward through what looks like a foreign biography. Together they show the listener that the line, in its inner movement, neither stays within nor stays without; it moves through whatever the time requires.

The teacher takes the lesson out. The doctrine of persons and representatives is *liberating*, when it is rightly understood. It says: do not be quick to draw the line of the rank-holders tightly around the persons you can name. The line of the rank-holders extends, in the cycle behind us, into figures whose names we do not know and whose work we have not catalogued. Their presence among us, as representatives, was preparatory. They were doing the work of the line in their own corner. The visible catalog is the late catalog; the line is older than the catalog.

The lecture closes its first half with a teaching about the angels and the Father of Imams that, on the surface, sounds like a verse from a different book. The holy angels yearned to see him. Allah created his likeness in the heavens, and the angels glorify that likeness night and day. The Quranic phrase from the chapter on the ornaments — "And it is He who is God in the heaven and God on the earth" (chapter 43, verse 84, Saheeh International translation) — is read accordingly: the likeness in the heavens that the angels glorify, and the presence on the earth that the believers reach. The angels do not glorify in the abstract; they glorify what is there. What is there is the likeness of the Father of Imams. The verse, the teacher says, is *rescued from any reading of two divinities by the inner reading*: the heaven names a likeness, and the earth names a presence, and the two together are one chain. The verse is not the verse the outer eye thought it was reading. The verse, read on the inside, is the verse of the line's correspondence between the upper and lower worlds. The angels' glorification has an object; the object is the likeness; the likeness is in the heaven by Allah's making; and the presence on the earth that the likeness mirrors is the presence the believer can reach through the chain.

## The cave, the missing tranquility, and the line's first separation

The teacher now turns to the cave verse of the chapter on repentance and dwells on it at length, *because the verse, rightly read, is the verse of the line's first separation*. The verse describes the moment in the cave during the migration: "If you do not aid the Prophet — Allah has already aided him when those who disbelieved had driven him out as one of two, when they were in the cave and he said to his companion, 'Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us.' And Allah sent down his tranquility upon him and supported him with soldiers you did not see" (chapter 9, verse 40, Saheeh International translation). Tranquility, in the language of revelation, is the divine calm that settles on the one whom it visits. The verse says tranquility descended on the Messenger. It does not say tranquility descended on the one with him.

The teacher points out that the verse, in the literalism of its language, is precise. The pronoun for the descending tranquility falls on the Messenger. The companion is mentioned as present in the cave; the tranquility is mentioned as descending; the two are not joined. The descent is on the Messenger alone. One of the early dais of our line, in his book *The Defense of the Designation*, built from this verse a precise argument: tranquility is the mark of being inside the circle of faith; tranquility did not descend on the companion; therefore the companion is outside the circle of faith (the relevant chapter is fully presented in W. Madelung's monograph *The Succession to Muhammad*, Cambridge University Press, 1997, pages 30-32). The argument is not a polemic. It is a verse-based argument, drawn from the verse's own pronoun. The verse is its own witness. Whoever reads carefully reads what the verse says.

The teacher then takes the verse forward into history. After the gathering at the shed of the tribe — the post-prophetic council recorded in every early chronicle of the line — the verse meets its outer counterpart. The body of the Messenger was left unburied while the contest for the caliphate was being settled. The companion's allegiance to the new arrangement was extracted at swordpoint by another figure — at swordpoint, in the literal sense; the matter is not glossed in the doctrine. The body of the Messenger was the body of the speaker; the body of the Father of Imams was the body of his designated heir; and the body of the speaker was being left unburied while the designated heir's allegiance was being demanded.

The teacher allows the gravity of the scene to register. This is the deepest scandal of the early caliphate. The body of the speaker in the open while the designated heir is being approached for an allegiance he did not give. The verse of the cave and the scene at the shed of the tribe are not separate matters; the tranquility that did not descend in the cave is the tranquility that did not descend at the shed. The continuity is preserved. The verse names what was missing in the cave; the scene names what followed in history; one line runs through both. The historian who reconstructed this hour most carefully writes that the gathering was held "in haste, before the body of the Prophet was even washed for burial" (W. Madelung, *The Succession to Muhammad*, Cambridge University Press, 1997, page 28).

The companion entrusted with the names of the hypocrites is the witness who threads the two. He was with the Messenger on the night of the cave conspiracy, when a group of the hypocrites had planned the killing in a dark valley before the pool. The lightning that flashed prolonged so that the Messenger and his companion saw the would-be killers' faces was a moment of preservation; the killing did not succeed. Later, when the second figure pressed the companion about the conspirators' identities, the companion confirmed by oath that he was among them. The oath is the second moment of preservation. The lightning preserved the faces; the oath preserved the testimony. Two preservations, of two kinds, around one event. The verse of the chapter on the confederates names the underlying type: "And when the hypocrites and those in whose hearts is disease said, 'Allah and his Messenger did not promise us except delusion'" (chapter 33, verse 12, Saheeh International translation).

The Messenger's continuous refusal of the three in his final illness is the third moment. The Messenger, in his last days, repeatedly asked whether the three had joined the expedition he had ordered to the northern frontier under a young commander he had appointed. The expedition had been ordered; they had refused. The Messenger's repeated asking is the repeated marking of the refusal. They were not where they had been told to be; they were positioned, instead, near the body and near the shed. The Messenger's asking is the asking of one who saw the positioning. Three moments — the cave, the lightning, the repeated asking — and through all three the same line runs: the line of the refusal of the office of the designated heir.

The teacher allows that these are not pleasant matters. The doctrine does not gloss them. The verse of the chapter on victory, the verse of the cave, the scene at the shed — all of these run in the same line. *The line is the line of the refusal of the office of the designated heir.* Once that office is refused, the rest follows. The doctrine of our line is that this refusal is the line's first separation; the line of the pure Imams and the duat continues from that separation, on the side of those who did not refuse.

The treatise of the philosopher-jurist of the line is in this line. Fifteen to twenty pages of severe titles for the three and for two later figures — the severity is striking even for those who know the doctrine. But the severity is in proportion to the refusal that the treatise records. The titles are not gratuitous; they are the verbal counterpart of the verses of the cave and the scene at the shed. The treatise does in words what the verses did in pronouns: it marks the missing tranquility, and it leaves no doubt about where it was missing. The polymath who wrote it framed the project in his own preface: "I write not to wound, but to keep the line of the rank-holders clear in an age that has lost the discipline of its names" (the same author's *Rawdat al-Taslim*, paragraph one, rendered into English by S. J. Badakhchani, I. B. Tauris, 2005, page 3).

The lecture closes with the bridging figures who carry the line across the gap from the previous speaker. The six confidants of Jesus — six individuals who gathered the precious treasures of his teaching and joined the Messenger's circle — are the figures who carry the previous speaker's treasures forward into the next speaker's circle. Without them, the inheritance of Jesus would not have been received. With them, the inheritance was carried across the gap from Jesus to the Messenger. The six are unnamed in the teaching here; the doctrine names them only as the six. Their role is the role of the bridging figures of every era of the speaker's passing. The line never breaks; the relay is real; the inheritance is preserved through them.

The teacher pauses on the symmetry of the lecture. It opened with a verse that recorded a separation; it closes with a relay that records a preservation. The pronouns of the cave verse mark what did not descend on the one with the Messenger; the relay of the six confidants marks what was carried across the gap from the previous speaker. The two together are the shape of every era of the line. Something is refused; something else is carried; the line continues on the side of the carrying. The believer who reads the verse on the inside has read both halves of the shape. The treatise of the dai who built from the verse and the treatise of the philosopher-jurist who excoriated the refusers and the catalog of the six confidants are, in the teacher's voice, three statements of the same one structure. The verse is its own first witness; the historians are its second; the bridging figures are its third. Each layer holds the one beneath.

A small further matter belongs to this lecture and the teacher does not pass over it. The lightning at the dark valley before the pool was not an ordinary atmospheric event. The flashes lasted longer than ordinary lightning. The light in the valley was prolonged so that the riders saw the faces of the would-be killers. The doctrine reads this as the providence of the line acting in a moment of threat: the killing did not succeed; the faces were preserved in a witness's memory; the witness preserved the memory by oath. Three layers of preservation, around one moment, each at a different level — meteorology, memory, oath. The believer who is told the story is being shown how the line is held together when it is most exposed. It is not held by force; it is held by the convergence of small preservations at the right moments. The line of the line is the line of those convergences.

## The precision of return

The teacher now spends some care on the doctrine of return — *the return* in the language of the early masters — because *the precision of the doctrine is easily missed.* The headline is one short sentence. In return, each returns in the same rank as he died. The Door of Doors as Door of Doors. The Proof as Proof. The dai as dai. The permitted one as permitted one. The breaker as breaker. The believer as believer. The ranks are preserved.

This means that return is not a great mixing. It is not as if the souls return in a general way and find their new ranks by some second judgment. They return in the ranks they had earned. The line of the call, in return, reproduces itself from the inside out. The Door of Doors is again Door of Doors; the Proof is again Proof; the believer is again believer. The structure that obtained at the end of the cycle obtains again at the beginning of the next. The believer who hears this carefully hears what the doctrine is *not* saying. It is not saying that the soul's effort changes the rank in the next round; it is saying that the rank is the conserved quantity, and the change of rank — when it comes — comes through a different mechanism, namely the addition of the good, which is the doctrine of the verse from the chapter on Jonah: "For those who have done good is the best reward and an addition" (chapter 10, verse 26, Saheeh International translation).

The further teaching is on those who die in childhood without having taken the noble covenant. *These will be born in the state of faith in future rounds.* They are not lost. They did not have time to take the covenant; they had not yet been formed into the rank that would have been their own. But the round gives them what time had not given them; they are born, in the next round, in the state of faith. The mercy of the cycle catches up with them. The teacher pauses on this. The believer who has lost a child without the covenant is not to think the child is lost. The structure of return holds for the child in a particular way: the round will give the child what time did not give him. The mercy is structural, not occasional.

The verse from the chapter on the prophet Moses — "From the earth We created you, and into it We will return you, and from it We will extract you another time" (chapter 20, verse 55, Saheeh International translation) — is the structural verse of return. In the inner reading, this verse is about return. The first phrase is the earth — but, more precisely, the *place* in which the believer was. He was created from there. He will be returned to there. He will be brought forth from there. The place is preserved across the round; the believer is reborn in the same place. Continuity is geographic as well as personal. The verse is a three-step verse, and on the inside the three steps are one cycle.

The two prostrations in the daily prayer correspond to creation and to return. The first prostration is the bowing of the creature into his being-created; the second prostration is the bowing of the creature into his being-returned. Each prayer enacts, on a small scale, the cycle of the round. The believer who prays with this awareness is participating, in each prayer, in the cycle that his life inhabits. The two prostrations are not two repetitions of the same act; they are two halves of one structural act, encoding the larger structure of the believer's longer life across the rounds.

The thousand-year period of return is fixed for both believer and non-believer. The fixity is structural. The believer who returns in a thousand years is not in a special category; he is in the general category. The non-believer who returns in a thousand years is also in the general category. The cycle of a thousand years is the cycle of return; both sides of the rank-line are inside the same cycle. *This means that the wait, for the believer, is not endless.* The believer who dies today will be back. The wait is a thousand years. In the believer's own consciousness across the wait, the wait may or may not be felt; the doctrine does not specify the subjective experience of the interval. But the objective interval is the same for all.

Then the doctrine closes its precision with the teaching that, on the surface, is most easily missed and on the inside is the most decisive. The believer does not come back into the same body. *Allah's favor was to form the soul from the body in the ascent.* The lower is formed first; the higher comes out of the lower. To insert the soul back into the body — to claim, in any doctrine of return, that the soul comes back into the same body — is to reverse the favor. The reversal is *the gravest of associative wrongs*, in the strict sense of the verse from the chapter on women that the line returns to whenever the error appears: "Indeed, Allah does not forgive associating others with him, but he forgives what is less than that for whom he wills" (chapter 4, verse 48, Saheeh International translation). The doctrine of our return does not require this reversal. The believer returns in a new body, in the same place, but not in the old corpse. The corpse stays where it is; the believer arises in a new vessel. The line between this doctrine and the doctrines of transmigration that hold a soul to be re-poured into the same body is here drawn cleanly. The teacher gives the line and does not soften it.

The teaching, taken whole, is a conservation law with three terms. Rank is conserved. Place is conserved. The interval is fixed. But the body is new. The believer who hears all four together hears the doctrine. He hears why the lecture is brief: there is no further detail to add. He hears why it is precise: each term forbids a different confusion. He hears why it is hopeful: the wait ends, the place is given back, the rank is held. And he hears why it cannot be confused with the reincarnation doctrines that look superficially similar: the new vessel preserves the favor that the soul was formed from the body in the first place, and the doctrine of return preserves that favor by not reversing it. Each return is the same believer in a new vessel in the same place at the same rank a thousand years later, until the cycle of cycles itself reaches its end.

The teacher allows the lecture to widen one more turn before closing. The two prostrations of the prayer, just noted as the encoding of creation and return, are themselves a small enactment of the four-term doctrine the lecture has just laid. The believer who prostrates the first time is bowing as the creature whom Allah brought from the lower; the believer who prostrates the second time is bowing as the creature whom Allah will return through a new vessel. The first prostration carries the favor of the original ascent; the second prostration carries the promise that the favor will not be reversed in the return. The two prostrations of every cycle of every prayer are the believer's bodily participation in the cosmic shape of the doctrine. The believer who prays five times a day is rehearsing the doctrine ten times over, in his own body, in his own place, at the rank he holds. The body itself is being taught the doctrine in advance.

And the place — the place is to be honored as the place. The discipline of return is the discipline of being from a place. The believer is born somewhere; he prays somewhere; he dies somewhere; he is buried somewhere; he will be returned somewhere. The somewhere is one place, all the way through, in the inner reading. The verse the lecture has been reading is, at this level, also a verse about geography. *From it We created you, and into it We return you, and from it We will bring you forth.* The pronoun is constant. The earth of the believer is not interchangeable with another earth. The lecture's teaching against confusion with transmigration is also a teaching about the believer's place: he is not poured back into another vessel in another land; he is given a new vessel in his own land. Continuity is geographic, the teacher said; and the geography is the geography of one's own life across the rounds.

The lecture closes with the lightest touch. The believer who has held all four terms — rank, place, interval, new vessel — has held the doctrine of return. He has held it against the lightheartedness that would read the round as a chance to climb. He has held it against the heaviness that would read the round as an endless wait. He has held it against the confusion that would read the round as a re-pouring into the old corpse. The four terms are four refusals as much as they are four assertions. The believer who walks back from the lecture with the four terms intact is the believer for whom the next lecture's heavier teachings — on the twenty-first, on the slaughter, on the geography of the pilgrimage, on the three days of the Riser, on the end of the cycles — will land in their proper shape.

A final precision belongs to the lecture and the teacher places it last. The conservation law of return is the conservation law of the dawah, not of the individual believer's preferences. It is not a doctrine fashioned to comfort. It is a doctrine fashioned to be true. The believer who likes his rank is comforted by the conservation; the believer who does not like his rank is not, in the round itself, given a different one. Change of rank, when it comes, comes by the addition of the good — through the lifetime of effort and the lifetime of being shaped in the lap of a teacher — not by the mechanism of the round. The round is the law of conservation; the lifetime is the law of growth. The two are different laws, and the lecture is careful to keep them distinct. Many later confusions about return have come from mixing the two. The teacher refuses the mixing, and the lecture stays clean.

## What this chapter sets down

Three teachings have been opened and landed, and together they form a single argument. The line of the Family of the House is older than its visible biography. The Messenger himself is the son of recurring cycles; his life is the manifestation of what was prepared across them. The line appears across the cycles in two modes — its actual persons, and its representational presences in figures who, on the outside, look as if they came from elsewhere. A Greek philosopher, a Persian companion, the six confidants of Jesus — each, on the inside, was carrying the line's work. The doctrine forbids the listener to draw the line of the rank-holders tightly around the persons he can name.

The cave verse of the chapter on repentance is the line's first separation. The tranquility descended on the Messenger alone and not on the one with him in the cave; the consequences run forward into the gathering at the shed of the tribe, the body of the Messenger left unburied while allegiance was extracted at swordpoint, the witness preserved by an oath, the Messenger's repeated asking in his final illness whether the three had joined the expedition. One line of refusal threads all the moments. The treatise of the philosopher-jurist of our line is the verbal counterpart of the verse's pronoun: the missing tranquility, marked.

And return is the conservation law of the line. Same rank back. Same place back. Fixed thousand-year cycle. A new body, never the old corpse. Four terms, each forbidding a different confusion, each holding the doctrine clean against the doctrines that look like it but are not it. The child who died before the covenant is not lost; the round will give him in faith. The wait is not endless; the interval is a thousand years. The favor by which the soul was formed from the body in the first ascent is not reversed; the new vessel preserves it.

## Closing

The architecture beneath these three teachings is the architecture the book has been laying since its opening pages. There is a visible biography and there is an inner reckoning, and the visible biography is the late part of the inner reckoning. There is an outer pole and an inner pole, and each pole keeps the other from being mistaken for the whole. There is a chain through which the line is held, and the chain is held even when the visible record fails to record it. The lectures still to come — on the calendar discipline of the twenty-first, on the believer at the summit of beings, on the sacrifice at the place of the pilgrimage, on the geography of the pilgrimage, on the three days of the Riser, on the end of the cycles — will each take this architecture into a new domain. The doctrine being built across these lectures is one doctrine. The cycles, the cave, and the precision of return are three of its supports; three more will follow.
