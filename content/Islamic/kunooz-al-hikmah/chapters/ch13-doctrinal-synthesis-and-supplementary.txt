# Doctrinal Synthesis, Supplementary Expositions, and the Last Word

## Where the chapter opens

What does it mean to *finish* a book whose treasures are, by the author's own admission, not transferred but only displayed? After fifteen lectures, after twelve prior chapters that opened the family of light, named the duat, traced the cycle, set the disciplines, and distinguished the two Mehdis, what remains for the book to do at the end?

The remaining work is not to add a new doctrine. The remaining work is, first, to see what has been built — to step back from the lectures and let their structure show itself as one. The doctrine of the cycle, the doctrine of concealment, the doctrine of the heart's three cavities, the doctrine of the numerical lattice, the doctrine of the karrah and the mawr — these were given as the lectures permitted. The synthesis takes the lectures together and shows what they together establish. That is the first movement of this final chapter.

Then comes the second movement: a final closing, in the form the author chose for it — invocational, valedictory, a prayer for the Dai and the Imam of his time, a benediction on the line, the recitation of the Fatiha. We will let that closing speak in its own voice rather than summarize it.

And then a third movement: the supplementary expositions, the doctrines that bear further setting out — items the lectures touched but that the author wanted, at the close, to anchor more firmly. The five tones of the shahadah and the place of Jonah. The dark valley before Ghadir and the prolonged lightning. The two brothers of Imam Husayn and the structural priority of Ali Zayn al-Abidin. The five teachers of the Prophet before the call. Salman as the double gate. The noble death and the bad death. The steam from the pot. The mansub of Yemen. The three jazair. A final note on the author and his single book. The book closes with a reflection on how to read this book itself.

Three movements: synthesis, closing, last setting-out. We take them in order.

## The synthesis

The synthesis the author offers is not a recapitulation. He does not, at the end, run through the lectures and number their teachings. He does something subtler. He names six principles that the lectures together establish, and he lets the principles do the seeing-together. The principles are not new content; they are the structural shape that the content has been taking all along. The believer who has followed the lectures with attention finds, when the principles are named, that he had been carrying them implicitly.

The first principle is the principle of the two hierarchies. The book has continually held two parallel hierarchies in view. The first is the hierarchy of the Aimmah: the Pure Imams in whom complete protection from error is realized, descending from Husayn through the Fatimid Imams, culminating in the present Imam and, in the future, in the Qaim. The second is the hierarchy of the duat: the summoners in whom partial protection is realized, connected through Hasan, carrying the call across the eras of concealment, culminating in the Dai of the present time. The two hierarchies are not in competition. They are complementary. The Aimmah carry the Imamate. The duat carry the summoning. The Aimmah are veiled in the era of concealment. The duat are visible. The believer's straight path runs through the duat to the Aimmah; the duat are the visible portion of that chain; the Aimmah are the veiled portion. This is also why the *Kunooz al-Hikmah* is stored *for the duat* — they are the ones in partial protection who need treasures to draw from; the Aimmah do not need stored treasures because their substance is direct. And it is also why the book is written by a son of a Dai, from inside the office of partial protection, for his fellows in that office. The dais' book is the duat's book.

The second principle is the discipline of boundaries. Six boundaries hold the doctrine in place. The boundary of speculation: no inquiry into the *how* of the originating act, because the intellect has no scope there; speculation in that region is assigning a partner and is destruction. The boundary of broadcasting: no carrying of the inner doctrine beyond those prepared to receive it, on pain of the seat in fire. The boundary of giving: no transfer of the *aqiqa* meat or the Eid al-Adha meat to those outside the structural channel. The boundary of slaughter: no consumption of animals that died of themselves in the wild, with the exception of the pigeons of the Sacred House and Medina. The boundary of reading: no opening of this book without the permission of one who has the authority of precedence and postponement. The boundary of imitation: no taking of the Prophet's total charity as personal model — the middle path is for us, the extremes belong to the protected. These are not arbitrary restrictions. They are the structural conditions under which the doctrine can be carried forward across the cycles. Without them, the doctrine dilutes — in the eating, the broadcasting, the reading, the imitating. With them, it is preserved at the depth where it continues to feed the line.

The third principle is the pattern of the structural numbers. The book has traced twenty-eight and twenty-nine at length; the synthesis adds the smaller numbers that run through the body of the work. Three: three veils of the Imam, three jewels of the shahada, three cavities of the heart, three days of the offerings after burial, three days of the Qaim's manifestation, three intermediary ancestors of the Prophet. Four: four ranks of prophethood, mission, Imamate, succession; four sections of the shahada; four pillars of the House; four doors of the journey. Seven: seven Imams of the station of rest, seven sections of the shahada's writing, seven seekings-of-forgiveness after the Throne Verse, seven cycles of concealment, seven verses of the Opening, seven great intellects, seven tests of the Prophet before the mission and seven after. Twelve: twelve islands, twelve duat, twelve hearts, twelve letters of the word of testimony, twelve duat of the presences, twelve duat of the islands, twelve consecutive prayer-cycles of the night-prayer. Twenty-five: twenty-five words of the Opening, twenty-five prophets named in the Quran. Twenty-eight: two plus three plus four plus seven plus twelve equals twenty-eight, the eternal order; twenty-eight letters of the word of testimony; twenty-eight stations of the believer's ascent; twenty-eight letters of the Quranic alphabet; the twenty-eighth in any sequence as gatherer; twenty-eight titles of the first designation of Dawud bin Ajabshah. Twenty-nine: twenty-nine of the abbreviated letter-openings; the twenty-ninth in any sequence as breaker; the phrase *Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah* in the twenty-ninth verse of the chapter of the Opening Victory. Three hundred and sixty: three hundred and sixty manifestations of the integrated child per cycle; three hundred and sixty subtle bodies in the human form. Forty hundred thousand multiplied by forty hundred thousand: that is one great cycle. Fifty thousand: the years of this present era of the Qaim. These numbers are not numerology in the loose sense. They are the structural countings of the doctrine's elements. When the believer encounters one of them, he can usually find the structural element it indexes. The believer who has internalized the lattice has internalized the doctrine at the structural level.

The fourth principle is the triad of disciplines that organizes the believer's life: faith, action, and manners. Faith is the inner orientation toward the chain — the heart set on the Imam, the Dai, the structural ranks. It is not abstract belief; it is structural orientation. Action is the embodied expression of that orientation — the prayer, the fast, the alms, the pilgrimage, the giving of charity, the attending of the gathering, the taking of the covenant. Manners are the small disciplines that maintain the connection — how one stands in the presence of the Dai, how one greets a fellow believer, how one handles the *aqiqa* meat, how one performs the ablution, how one closes the invocation of the Name. The three together constitute the believer's life. Faith without action is empty; action without manners is graceless; manners without faith are theatrical. The triad must hold. This is also why the book carries so many practical rulings alongside its cosmological doctrines. The doctrines are not for cognition alone; they are for the action and the manners. The believer who reads the doctrine of the Imam's three veils is also reading the doctrine of how to perform the praising of the Lord with attention to *God is greater*, *Glory be to God*, and the essence of the Imam. The cognition and the practice run together.

The fifth principle is the continuity of the line. The Imam's soul dissolves, the substance enters the ranks, the ranks return the substance, the next Imam is formed. The mechanism runs across generations. The line, structurally, is never broken. The duat are named in the praise — Sulayman, Jafar the seventh, Muhammad bin Fuad as the Mim, Ali bin Sulayman as the Ayn, Ibrahim the traveler, Muhammad bin Ismail the emigrant — and the names together carry the line. The believer connects through his junior summoner, his breaker, his Dai; the connection is his straight path; the connection is preserved across his life and, through the cyclic return, into the next. The continuity is therefore at every level: in the formation of the Imam's soul, in the naming of the duat, in the believer's chain, in the return. The cycle and the line work together. The standard reference history of the call confirms the structural reading from the outside: across the era of partial protection, the line preserved itself by the mechanism the doctrine describes, with the duat carrying the visible portion and the Imams the veiled portion, and the believer's allegiance to his own rung the active means of preservation. [Farhad Daftary, *The Ismailis: Their History and Doctrines*, 2nd ed. (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007), pp. 128–131.] This is the structural answer to whether the line can be lost. It cannot. The mechanism is too deeply embedded. The duat may face the Pharaoh of the age; the believers may face persecution; the discipline of concealment may be severe — but the line itself, by structural necessity, continues. The believer's role is to participate in the continuity, not to worry about it. The continuity does not depend on his worry; it depends on his participation. His daily prayer, his manners, his action, his faith — these are his participation. The line takes care of itself through the participation of its members.

The sixth and final principle is the doctrine of the end and the return. The end is the great cycle, of which nine remain; at the end of the ninth, all are absorbed into the First Emanator, and only the domain of the First Intellect and the First Emanator remains. The return is the cycle of the thousand years; each believer returns in the same rank, in the same place; the cycle is structurally fair. The two together orient the believer's life. He is not in an endless drift; the great cycle has its end. He is not in a single life; the return gives him return. His effort in this cyclic visit shapes his rank in the next; his ranks across the visits compound; the compound brings him, over the great cycles, toward the final absorption. The believer who has internalized this orientation is the believer whose decisions are made on the right scale. He is not deciding for this lifetime alone; he is deciding for the cyclic returns and the great cycles. The disciplines that feel difficult in the lifetime are easy at the scale of the great cycle. The luxuries that feel necessary in the lifetime are nothing at the scale of the great cycle. The orientation transforms the felt weights. The modern reader of Ismaili cosmology has described this scaling of action to the great cycle as the doctrine in which the believer's measure of his own life is taken not from the bounded interval of one lifetime but from the unbounded horizon of the cyclic return. [Henry Corbin, *Cyclical Time and Ismaili Gnosis*, trans. Ralph Manheim and James W. Morris (London: Kegan Paul International, 1983), pp. 96–99.] And this is also why the line's wisdom is gathered for the duat in the *Stored Treasures of Wisdom*. The treasures are scaled to the great cycle. The duat draw from them at the scale at which they must serve. The believers receive what the duat distribute at the scale appropriate to their position. The whole is scaled to the cycle that contains it.

Six principles. Two hierarchies, the discipline of boundaries, the lattice of numbers, the triad of life, the continuity, the end and the return. This is the synthesis: not a list of doctrines but a structural shape of the whole. The believer who carries the six can read any of the doctrines anew through them.

## The final closing

The book then closes in its own voice. The closing is not commentary; it is itself an act — invocational, valedictory, the duat's manner of closing a final gathering. We let it speak whole, in refined English, as the author wrote it. This is the book's own benediction on itself.

The Stored Treasures of Wisdom have been displayed within the limits of what may be displayed. The treasures themselves remain stored; what has been displayed is a portion that the reader may, with the permission of one who has the authority of precedence and postponement, take into his life and let work in him.

The author has written. The questioner has asked. The Dai has answered. The discipline of concealment has been set. The line of duat has been named. The cosmology of the human realm and the divine realm has been described. The doctrines of the heart, of the House, of the abbreviated letter-openings, of the cyclic return, of the thousand-year cycle, of the great cycle — these have been laid out. The two awaited figures have been distinguished. The believer's straight path has been identified as his chain. The discipline of ablution, of the pre-dawn meal, of the breaking of the fast, of the call to prayer, of slaughter, of the seventh-day offering, of visitation, of charity, of speech — these have been preserved.

The book closes as the duat of the line will close their final gathering: with the prayer for the Dai of today, for the Imam of today, for the protection of both, for the prolongation of both, for the elevation of both.

For the sake of our Master the Absolute Summoner, may Allah prolong his noble life. For the sake of our Master and our Lord the Manifest Imam, our master, may Allah prolong his life and elevate his station. For the sake of the People of the House, the good and the pure, the prayers of Allah and his peace upon them all.

In the Name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Most Compassionate. All praise is due to Allah, Lord of the worlds. The Most Merciful, the Most Compassionate. Master of the Day of Judgment. You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help. Guide us along the straight path — the path of those You have blessed, not of those upon whom is anger, nor of those who go astray. Amen.

*Peace be upon them all, for eternity upon eternity.*

That is the final closing. The author does not add to it. He lets the prayer end the book the way the duat end the gathering.

## The supplementary expositions

After the closing, the author returns. Not to add new doctrine, but to set out more fully a handful of teachings the lectures had touched in passing — items the close of a book is the right place to anchor. This is itself a mode of teaching. The supplementary mode is the mode of returning to what has been said and giving it the small additional turn that lets it settle properly. Six or seven such turns, then a final note on the author himself, then a reflection on how to read this book.

Consider the five tones of the testimony. The word of testimony has appeared in five tones across the Quran: *there is no god but Allah*, *there is no god but He*, *there is no god but I*, *there is no god but the One who*, and *there is no god but You*. Of these, *there is no god but You* has come only once — in the supplication of Jonah, peace be upon him, when he was in the depth of the sea, in the belly of the great fish, in a darkness within darkness within darkness. The verse runs, in the Yusuf Ali rendering at verse eighty-seven of the chapter on the Prophets, *He cried through the depths of darkness: 'There is no god but Thou: glory to Thee: I was indeed wrong!'* [The Holy Quran, ch. 21 verse 87, trans. Abdullah Yusuf Ali (Beltsville: Amana Publications, 1989), p. 240.] *There is no god but You; glory be to You; indeed I was among the wrongdoers.* The teacher would say: this is the formula of the soul in the deepest enclosure. The other tones keep the divine in the third or the first person; *but You* addresses the divine directly, in the second person, in vocative directness. That directness is the mark of the depth. The formula has come once because the situation calling it forth is rare. The believer in his own depths — the darknesses within darknesses — may reach for it. It is not for the ordinary moment; it is for the moment in the belly of the fish. And the deliverance of Jonah from the fish is the consequence: the depth was opened by the wrongs; the depth was closed by the formula; the deliverance was given because the formula was rightly spoken. The depth is not opened by mere endurance; it is opened by the formula spoken from the depth.

Consider the dark valley before Ghadir. A companion of the prophet, in her closeness, was told the secret about the succession of Ali; she did not keep it; the secret reached the hypocrites; they decided to prevent the declaration by killing the Prophet in the dark valley on the road to Ghadir. Allah intervened directly. The lightning that flashed in the valley was not ordinary lightning. It lasted longer than the natural lightning lasts. It lit the dark valley as if it were noon. The Prophet and Hudhayfa al-Yamani saw together, and in the seeing they recognized the faces of those who had gathered. The recognition was the preservation. The conspirators, once their faces were seen, withdrew. The killing did not proceed. The Prophet passed through the valley and arrived at Ghadir; the declaration was made. Hudhayfa's later oath — that he had been among them — is the witness preserved. The structural meaning is precise: at the moment when the line is most threatened, the line is most directly preserved. The believer who finds himself, in his own life, at a moment of crisis around the chain may pray for a similar intervention. It may not come as lightning; it may come in another form. But the principle holds: at the moment of greatest threat, the line is most directly preserved by the divine attention.

Consider the two brothers, Hasan and Husayn. In every Imam, all four ranks are present — prophethood, mission, Imamate, succession. The two higher are Imamate and succession; the two lower are prophethood and mission. In Hasan's time, the two higher were manifest with Hasan as the Imam; the two lower were manifest with Husayn under his brother's authority. In Husayn's time, after Hasan's passing, all four came together with Husayn — but the two lower dominated over the two higher in his own being. Husayn was the Imam, but his outward office — the bearing of the public responsibility, the standing against Yazid, the procession to Karbala, the martyrdom — was a manifestation of the two lower. The two higher were present but veiled by the two lower. The first Imam in whom all four were combined with the two higher *dominating* was Ali Zayn al-Abidin. He is therefore called the Adam of the Family of the Cloak — the first in the family of the Prophet in the fully-combined-with-higher-dominating sense. The foundational Ismaili compendium gathers the hadith of the cloak and the verse of purification as the structural anchor of the household's identification, preserving the corroborating chains across both witnesses. [the book *The Pillars of Islam*, trans. Asaf A. A. Fyzee, rev. Ismail K. Poonawala (New Delhi: Oxford University Press, 2002), vol. 1, pp. 22–25.] This is a precise resolution of what might otherwise be a tension. Husayn is, doctrinally, the Imam; the line of the Aimmah passes through him; but the internal structure of his Imamate had the two lower dominating, which is part of the meaning of his being the one who manifested the occultation through the martyrdom. The martyrdom was the work of the two lower ranks; the two higher remained protected within the two lower. From Ali Zayn al-Abidin forward, the structure is the dominance of the higher over the lower. The pattern thereafter is settled.

Consider the five teachers of the Prophet before the call. He had five teachers in succession. The first was Ubayy ibn Kab, who taught him Adam's laws. The last was Khadijah. The three between taught the laws of Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus — the five speakers of the previous era. The Prophet's full inheritance of the line is therefore constituted by these five. Each teacher, after teaching, became indebted-in-return to the Prophet, because what he taught had been received from the speaker in another order of being; the indebted one is not a debtor in the ordinary sense but an inheritor of the connection. Khadijah, the last teacher before the call, became indebted-in-return to the Prophet and then became the teacher of Ali. This double-relation is what makes her place so unusual. She is teacher and indebted-in-return simultaneously; she carries Ali's instruction; she is the Mary of the inner reading, with Ali as the son-of-Mary in our doctrine. Her exemption from the five prayers follows from this teacher-relation: a teacher of the speaker and of the deputy operates in a different mode from the ordinary ritual obligation. The doctrine of the five teachers also explains why the call was not an interruption from outside the Prophet's preparation but the manifestation of what had been gradually built. The teachers prepared the vessel. The call announced what the vessel already held.

Consider Salman the Persian. He is the only person in the era of concealment who became the supreme gate for both the Prophet and his deputy. There is normally one supreme gate per office. Salman is the exception. The doctrine of the exception is that his inner reality was already constituted in a way that allowed the double office. His outward biography passed through other religions, but his inner reality was already from the ranks of the line. By the time he came to the Prophet, he was prepared to serve at an office that ordinarily would have required a different individual. This is also why, in our community, he is honored as he is. *Salman is from us, the People of the House.* The Prophet's statement is not a courtesy; it is a doctrinal recognition. Salman was already from the People of the House in the inner reading; the Prophet's statement makes the inner reading visible. The lesson is that the line of the ranks extends, in its inner reality, into figures whose outward biographies pass through other communities. The believer should be cautious about drawing the line too tightly around the persons of his own immediate community. Salman, Aristotle, the confidants of Jesus — these are reminders that the essential beings of the line are wider than the individual persons we can see in the present.

Consider the discipline of the body in death. The speaking soul absorbs the sensitive soul through life. The absorption is gradual. By the time of death, the absorption is more or less complete depending on the discipline of the life. If complete, the speaking soul departs with the sensitive soul gathered within it; the body is left without sensation; the corpse does not linger as a sentient thing. This is the noble death. If incomplete, the sensitive soul remains in the body at death; the corpse retains some residue of sensation; the death is not the clean separation it should have been. This is the bad death. The remedy across the lifetime is the discipline of praising — *God is greater*, *All praise belongs to God*, *Glory be to God*, *There is no god but Allah*. These phrases, especially *All praise belongs to God*, work on the sensitive soul; they gradually orient the sensations toward praise; they gradually carry the sensitive soul into the speaking soul's frame. By the end of life, the believer who has practiced the praising has gathered the sensitive soul substantially. This is also why our funeral disciplines pay attention to what is done in the three days. The opening-recitations and the other offerings work on what remains; the family takes up what the deceased could not complete. The doctrine does not condemn; it organizes our response. And there is a kitchen-discipline of the same shape: the steam from the cooked meat must be allowed to escape. The slaughter has separated the sensitive substance from the animal by the *God is greater*, but the separation is not always complete at the moment of slaughter. The cooking opens the body further; the heat releases the remainder; the remainder departs through the steam. If the pot is sealed tightly, the substance may be trapped in the food and carried into the eater. The discipline therefore requires that some opening be left. A small rule of the kitchen carrying the same structural weight as the slaughter itself.

Consider the place of Yemen. The summit-officer of Yemen, of Najran — the one ranked below the Dai but above the breaker and the junior summoner — is uniquely elevated. He is the Dai's successor in potentiality. When the Dai passes, the noble designation may fall on him, elevating him to the Dai. The structural potentiality confers a present elevation: his station today is above the station of the other officers because his future, if the designation falls on him, is to be Dai. The future office, in our doctrine, casts a present shadow. The believers of Najran, in particular, honor him with the depth this dual position requires. And this connects to the three islands — Yemen, India, and Sindh — in which the Dai of Yemen leads prayer; he does not lead prayer in the other islands. The three are connected in a special way through his office; the other islands have their own structures. For the believer in India or Sindh, the connection to Yemen is therefore not merely sentimental. It is structural. He is in the Dai's prayer-circle in a way the believer in another island is not. The exception of the Dai leading prayer outside the three was given only to Sayyidna Muhammad bin Ismail, for Habasha, for the loftiness of his station. The rule is precise.

A final note on the author. Sayyidna Ali bin Hibatullah wrote one book. He wrote it during his father's lifetime, in obedience to the command. He passed away during his father's lifetime, becoming a non-independent Dai. He did not have the time, or the office, to write more. The single book is the *Stored Treasures of Wisdom*. The treasures stored for the masters of partial protection are in this one book. The duat after him have drawn from these treasures; the duat of our time still draw. The author's humility in his introduction is precise. He pleads deficiency of knowledge, distance of nature, weakness of understanding. He attributes all correctness to the Dai who caused his tongue to flow; he attributes all error to himself. This humility is not theatrical. It is structural. The author is at the office of partial protection; he writes from inside that office; the office requires the humility because the office is not complete protection. The humility is the correct epistemic disposition of one who carries partial protection.

And finally a word on reading this book. The reader who has come this far will recognize that the book repays slow reading. The doctrines do not yield to a single pass. The structural numbers come back at different levels. The same teaching is opened from one direction in an early section and from another direction in a later one. The Imam's three veils in the second question return as the three jewels of the testimony, return again as the three cavities of the heart, return again as the three layers of the human realm. The believer who has been through the book once will, on the second reading, see what he could not have seen the first time; on the third, what he could not have seen on the second. The Commander of the Faithful had taught the same posture toward the deep word — that the words have a limit but the meanings have none — and the fifth-century compiler of his aphorisms preserves it among the sayings the rank-holders place behind every commentary on durable speech. [the book *A Treasury of Virtues: Sayings, Sermons, and Teachings of Ali*, ed. and trans. Tahera Qutbuddin (New York: NYU Press, 2013), §28, p. 16.] This is the nature of the truths. They are not propositions to be memorized; they are structures to be inhabited. The believer who has inhabited the structure for a year reads them differently from the believer who is encountering them for the first time. The doctrine is not in the words; it is in what the words point to. The pointing requires the believer to follow with attention. And the book is also one that, the discipline of concealment requires, is not for every reader. The author has bound the reader by covenant. Whoever has come this far without proper permission is, in the strict reading, in violation. Whoever has come this far with the permission of one who has the authority of precedence and postponement is in the proper relation. The covenant should not be set aside. For the reader in the proper relation, the book is a treasure to live with. The doctrines settle in over years. The disciplines of ablution and the call and the pre-dawn meal and the breaking of the fast and slaughter and the seventh-day offering and visitation, taken from the book, shape the believer's days. The cosmology of the cyclic return and the great cycle shapes the believer's sense of time. The doctrine of the straight path as one's own chain shapes the believer's relation to those above him in the hierarchy. The book is, in this sense, a manual for the office of partial protection. The duat who hold the office use it as their structured deposit; the believers receive what the duat distribute; the chain is preserved across the eras.

We close with a small return to the very opening of the book. *All praise is for Allah, the Great, who manifested a special human form from the gardens of the people of specialty.* The phrase is opening; it is also doctrine. We are the gardens. The Prophet ascended through what we, in our small way, contributed to. The line that runs from the Prophet down through the Aimmah and the duat passes also through us. We are not bystanders to the line; we are constituents of its substance. To know this is to receive the book at the right level.

There is one more thing the supplementary mode does that is worth naming. By returning to teachings that the lectures had touched, the author models for the reader what to do with the book after closing it. The reader is being taught not only the doctrines but the *manner* of return — that one comes back to a teaching with a small new turn each time, that one does not read the testimony of Jonah the same way after carrying the doctrine of the noble death, that one does not read the dark valley the same way after carrying the doctrine of structural continuity. The book is closing; the reading is beginning. This is the structural reason for placing the supplementary expositions where they sit. They are not afterthoughts but instruction in how the book, once closed, asks to be carried.

## What the chapter lands

The chapter lands three things. First, the structural shape of the whole book in six principles: two hierarchies, the discipline of boundaries, the lattice of numbers, the triad of faith and action and manners, the continuity of the line, and the end and the return. Second, the author's own final closing — invocational, valedictory, the prayer for the Dai and the Imam of his time, closing as the duat close a gathering, with the recitation of the Opening. Third, the supplementary mode itself: the doctrines that bear further setting out, from the singular tone of Jonah to the dark valley before Ghadir to the structural priority of Ali Zayn al-Abidin to the five teachers to Salman to the noble death to Yemen — and a final reflection on how this book asks to be read. The whole of the book, returned in its own voice and given back to the reader.

## Closing

The treasures will yield what they will yield. The discipline of concealment will hold what it must hold. The line will continue across the cyclic returns and the great cycles. The believer participates in the line by his prayer, his action, his manners, his faith. The Mehdi will come at his time; the Qaim will manifest in the three days of his office; the believers will perform the cyclic return in the thousand-year cycle; at the end of the ninth great cycle remaining, all will be absorbed into the First Emanator. Until then, the believers participate. The duat draw from the treasures. The author's book continues, century after century, to feed the office for which it was written. The chain runs through us, and we through it, and the chain — preserved — will, at its end, bring us to where the chain itself ends. The book closes, as the duat close their gathering, with the prayer that the chain be preserved.
