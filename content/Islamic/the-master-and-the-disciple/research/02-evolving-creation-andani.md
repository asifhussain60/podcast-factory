## (c) Digest

The paper is a constructive work of Shi'i Ismaili Muslim theology arguing that Ismailis can accept Neo-Darwinian evolution "without exceptions" -- including human evolution -- because of four mutually reinforcing features of Ismaili thought. The abstract states the thesis plainly: "By drawing on Ismaili metaphysics and hermeneutics, I argue that Ismailis can affirm evolution without exceptions due to four features of contemporary and historical Ismaili thought" (Andani, abstract; diacritics ASCII-folded).

**Feature 1 -- Living magisterial precedent.** The two most recent hereditary Imams already integrated evolution into their teaching. Aga Khan III (d. 1957), the 48th Imam, synthesized "his faith in the Almighty as well as in Darwin's theory of the origin of the species" (Andani, citing the Imam's memoirs). The present Imam at the time of writing, Aga Khan IV, "teaches that there is no conflict between Islam and scientific truth because God's creative act is eternal and continuous" (abstract). Creation is not a one-off past event but an ongoing divine act, so new scientific accounts of natural history pose no threat.

**Feature 2 -- Neoplatonic metaphysics.** Drawing on classical Ismaili philosopher-theologians such as al-Sijistani and Nasir-i Khusraw, Andani presents a cosmology in which God's creative action is perpetual and unchanging, mediated through the Universal Intellect and Universal Soul. All natural processes are "guided by the Universal Soul without a need for miracles" (abstract); "the sheer existence and law-like behavior of the natural world" is itself the manifestation of divine action (body text as fetched). This absorbs the apparent randomness of mutation: genetic mutations may have "unknowable physical causes" at the empirical level while remaining metaphysically ordered, since the Universal Soul "continuously contemplates the intelligible forms or archetypes" that nature instantiates. Divine action and Darwinian mechanism operate at different levels of description, so neither displaces the other.

**Feature 3 -- Common descent supports the Imamate.** In a distinctive move, Andani argues evolution positively reinforces Ismaili doctrine rather than merely coexisting with it: "the common descent of all life lends further support to the Ismaili belief in an unbroken lineage of hereditary Imams since the origins of life on earth" (abstract). He cites the modern Ismaili scholar Abualy Alibhai Aziz, who proposed that the "fish avatara" figure referenced in Ismaili devotional literature can be read as gesturing toward evolutionary ancestry within a continuous chain of divine guidance reaching back to life's origin.

**Feature 4 -- Esoteric hermeneutics dissolves the Adam problem.** The usual scriptural obstacle for Muslim acceptance of human evolution -- the Quranic account of Adam's special creation from clay -- does not bind Ismaili exegesis, because "Ismaili scholars read the Adam story symbolically through esoteric exegesis (ta'wil)" (abstract). Andani shows this is not a modern apologetic improvisation: the 10th-century Ismaili author Ja'far b. Mansur al-Yaman already rejected literal creation from clay as contradicting "empirical observation," holding instead that Adam was "naturally born from a father and a mother." Adam's "creation from clay" is read as symbolic of spiritual formation and initiation, not biological origination.

**Structure and significance.** The article moves from the modern Imams' statements, to classical Ismaili Neoplatonism, to the Imamate-and-common-descent argument, to ta'wil of the Adam narrative, concluding that Ismailism offers one of the most friction-free Muslim engagements with evolutionary biology -- a notable contrast with creationist and human-exceptionalist positions elsewhere in contemporary Islamic discourse. The paper appeared in Zygon's special section on Islam and evolution alongside other Muslim responses.
