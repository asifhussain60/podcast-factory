## (c) Digest of the closest verifiable scholarship (labeled as such)

This is a summary of the two verified closest-match papers, not of the unlocatable title.

**Taufik (2021), UIN Sunan Kalijaga.** This article treats al-Farabi (d. 950) not only as a political philosopher but as a kind of Qur'anic interpreter. Although al-Farabi never wrote a complete tafsir, the author notes that he "consistently paraphras[ed] verses of the Qur'an into political statements" (abstract, as fetched from the journal page). On that basis the paper explores whether al-Farabi's concept of the ideal society (madinah al-fadhilah, the virtuous city ordered toward happiness, sa'adah) can address the shortcomings of Indonesian democracy in the period after a national election. The author characterizes Indonesia's democratic practice as still "green" -- developmentally immature -- particularly in the post-campaign environment, and proposes al-Farabi's fusion of Platonic-Aristotelian political philosophy with Islamic doctrine as an alternative resource for repairing those deficits. The structure moves from al-Farabi's exegetical-political method, to his typology of states and the ideal city, to an application against observed democratization challenges in Indonesia.

**Fata, Izzuddin, and Fauzan (2025), Journal of Islamic Thought and Civilization.** This more recent paper makes the convergence argument explicitly constitutional. Its stated aim (abstract, verbatim): "The current research aimed to examine the relationship, shared moral and metaphysical foundations between al-Farabi's concept of the ideal state, as presented in Kitab Ara' Ahl al-Madinah al-Fadilah, and Indonesia's national ideology, Pancasila." (Diacritics ASCII-folded.) The authors argue that in al-Farabi's framework metaphysical understanding -- comprehension of divinity and of life's purpose -- is what shapes individual and societal ethics; politics rests on ontology. They then map this onto Pancasila's first principle, Belief in One God, which similarly positions divinity as the moral bedrock of the state. Diagnostically, they observe that despite Pancasila's centrality Indonesia faces persistent corruption and moral decline, and they prescribe al-Farabi's requirement that the state be led by individuals with philosophical insight and moral integrity: leaders must prioritize virtue and collective welfare over personal ambition and power. Search-result summaries of the same paper add that "engaging in metaphysical thought with a sincere internalization of Pancasila's principles enabled the revival of public ethics and political accountability."

**Common thread across the candidate literature.** Both papers (and the adjacent regional-elections study) share one argumentative skeleton that the unlocated title also implies: (1) al-Farabi's ideal state aims at happiness (sa'adah) in this world and the next; (2) the state is a cooperative moral project, so leadership quality is the decisive variable; (3) Indonesia, as a religiously grounded Pancasila democracy, is unusually receptive to this framework; (4) actual Indonesian democratic practice (money politics, identity exploitation, ethical violations in elections) falls short, and al-Farabi supplies the corrective standard.
