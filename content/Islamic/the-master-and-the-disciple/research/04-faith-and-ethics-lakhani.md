## (c) Digest

The book is the first major survey of the thought of Prince Karim Aga Khan IV, the 49th hereditary Ismaili Imam, distilled from roughly six decades of his public speeches, addresses, and interviews. Per the reviewer, it "was developed from his [Lakhani's] essay 'Living the Ethics of One's Faith'" (Sotillos review). Its central claim is that the Imamat's worldly engagement -- the schools, hospitals, and development agencies of the Aga Khan Development Network -- is not philanthropy adjacent to religion but the direct ethical expression of an Islamic metaphysics. The IIS description notes the Imam's initiatives "are aimed at improving the quality of human life among the disadvantaged, regardless of their religion or ethnicity," with "the values of integrity and dignity ... at the forefront of his work."

**Structure.** Ten chapters, per the review: (1) The Ismaili Imam and the Imamat; (2) Ethical Foundations; (3) Tradition and Modernity; (4) The Ethos of Modernism; (5) Pluralism and the Cosmopolitan Ethic; (6) Cohesion Within the Umma; (7) Islam and the West; (8) Cultivating an Enabling Environment; (9) Living the Ethics of Islam; (10) Global Convergence -- plus appendices and indices (272 pp. in the IIS listing).

**Argument.** The ethical vision is rooted in tawhid, the metaphysical oneness of God, from which flow unity of the human family and the obligation of social justice (adl). On this reading, "Islam does not deal in dichotomies but is an all-encompassing unity" (quoted in the Sotillos review) -- there is no wall between faith (din) and world (dunya), so material development, education, healthcare, and culture are religiously serious work. The middle chapters take up the encounter with modernity: the book distinguishes tradition from traditionalism and modernity from modernism, arguing the Imamat models a path that embraces scientific and institutional modernity while refusing secularist reduction. The pluralism chapters present the Aga Khan's signature "cosmopolitan ethic": difference as divinely intended, and a "clash of ignorance" (rather than of civilizations) to be remedied through education and intercultural institutions. Later chapters address intra-Muslim cohesion across the diverse umma, the Islam-West relationship, and the "enabling environment" -- the civil-society, legal, and economic conditions under which human dignity can flourish. The reviewer underscores the corrective intent: "The examination of the Ismaili ethos in this way provides a corrective to false stereotypes of Islam that have come to dominate much of the Western world."

**Key claims as conveyed by the review.** The Imam's values "are universal in their appeal and they exemplify the Muslim spirit while illustrating their relevance to the modern world." Ethics here is not rule-keeping but the lived consequence of faith: "Faith allows one to plumb the spiritual and metaphysical depths of what it means to be human, which is synonymous with living a principled life." The book also relays the Aga Khan's own diagnosis of the contemporary condition: "A deepening sense of spiritual commitment, and the ethical framework that goes with it, will be a central requirement if we are to find our way through the minefields and the quicksands of modern life." Sotillos closes on the book's timeliness: "With so much wisdom being lost and forgotten in the modern world, the Aga Khan's message of hope and voice of tolerance and compassion beckon to be heard."

**Caveat.** Chapter-by-chapter content beyond the table of contents, and any direct quotation of Lakhani's own prose beyond what the review reproduces, could not be verified against the gated full text.
