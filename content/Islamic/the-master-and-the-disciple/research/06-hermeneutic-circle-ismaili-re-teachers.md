## Digest

Dharamsi studies how seven Ismaili Muslim secondary religious-education
teachers in Canada interpret and enact the Institute of Ismaili Studies (IIS)
Secondary Curriculum -- a global curriculum initiative -- inside the lived
reality of Canadian classrooms. Methodologically the study is hermeneutic
phenomenology, drawing on Gadamer (pre-understanding, fusion of horizons,
phronesis) and, as its curricular-theoretical spine, Dwayne Huebner's
reconceptualist scholarship on curriculum and transcendence. Her central
claim, stated in the abstract: "curriculum inquiry is an interpretive quest
for both teachers and students which requires relational, temporal and
hermeneutic ways of knowing."

Chapter 1 frames three challenges facing religious education in the West:
secularization, the tension of tradition and modernity, and perceptions of
Islam. Chapter 2, "The Hermeneutics of a Living Tradition," grounds the study
in Shia and Ismaili intellectual history -- the Quran as "an inexhaustible
wellspring of inspiration," the Alid tradition, the early Ismaili Imams, and
the dialectic of revelation and reason. As she puts it: "The interpretive
quest highlights the esoteric nature of the living tradition, the need for an
authoritative guide to interpret the revelation for the community according to
the time, and the importance of engaging the dialectic of revelation and
reason as a responsibility of the believer." From this she derives the four
interpretive principles that organize the whole dissertation: unity of being;
epistemological humility; community realization; and attunement to beauty and
infinitude.

Chapter 3 connects Huebner's conception of curriculum as "transcendent
possibilities" with the aims of the IIS Secondary Curriculum; Chapter 4 sets
out the research design (seven teacher participants, hermeneutic
phenomenological interviews) within a Canadian Ismaili community itself in
transformation. The three findings chapters then map onto the principles.
Chapter 5 (unity of being) interprets the intentionality of the IIS curriculum
-- what it responds to (secular forces, identity, living tradition) and how
teachers develop "curricular consciousness" and relationality with the text.
Chapter 6 (epistemological humility) dwells in the pedagogical encounter:
teacher aspirations, the "weightiness" and ambiguity of the role, and
dissonances between curricular intent and student receptivity. It presents
seven named portraits of interpretive space-making -- Yasmine (in-betweenness),
Aidan (self-reflexivity), Shakeel (the questions behind the questions), Aryana
(knowledge relationships), Raziq (contemplation and sacredness), Zakir
(divinity within humanity), and Khaliya (co-journeying). The chapter argues
that "a disposition of epistemological humility, that is, seeking knowledge
from a plurality of sources, is central to the interpretive endeavor."
Chapter 7 (community realization) examines teacher-student relationality, the
classroom as community, and the vulnerabilities, tensions, and latent
potential of teacher learning communities.

Chapter 8 closes the circle with the author's own interpretive quest and the
fourth principle. The abstract anticipates the payoff: "Through practicing
epistemological humility in pursuing a plurality of knowledge sources, new
expressions of the tradition and its enduring values can be created that
resonate with modern times." Beauty is the engine of that renewal -- the
conclusion asks whether attunement to beauty is "an abstraction or one that
draws us closer to our worlds by acting as a motive force for the guidance of
conduct," and answers in lived terms: "Beauty releases the softness, the
gentleness, the prayerfulness in our being." The dissertation ends with the
insight "as to how attunement to beauty and infinitude can sustain and give
vitality and possibility to living traditions."

The work's significance: it treats a faith community's religious education not
as transmission of fixed content but as a hermeneutic circle in which
teachers, students, curriculum text, and tradition continuously reinterpret
one another -- making teacher agency the place where a living tradition
actually lives.
