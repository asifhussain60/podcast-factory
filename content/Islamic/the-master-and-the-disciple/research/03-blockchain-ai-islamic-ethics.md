## (c) Digest

The paper proposes an AI-ethics framework called "Raqib and Atid," named after the two recording angels of Islamic tradition, and pairs it with blockchain technology as the recording substrate. The abstract states: "Drawing on Islamic principles and leveraging blockchain technology, this framework monitors an AI's internal processes ('thoughts'), planned actions ('intentions'), and executed actions to ensure comprehensive accountability. Just as the angels Raqib and Atid maintain a celestial record for divine judgment, this framework systematically documents an AI's operations, generating an unalterable history of its activity."

**Motivation (Sections 1-2).** Existing AI-ethics frameworks are either principle-based (beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice) or consequence-based, and both "focus primarily on the outcomes of AI actions, neglecting the internal processes leading to those outcomes" -- an accountability gap that grows as models become more opaque. Islamic ethics, by contrast, weighs intention as heavily as outcome: the paper quotes the hadith "Actions are according to intentions, and everyone will get what was intended" (Bukhari). It also grounds the blockchain analogy scripturally in the kitab (book of deeds) presented on the Day of Judgment, quoting Quran 18:49 at length, and frames the relevance of Islamic ethics demographically (1.8 billion Muslims; 57 OIC member states; Pew growth projections). The concept of aql (intellect/reason) anchors a speculative question: if AI approaches a state of awareness, how do we guide its "reasoning" toward societal good?

**The framework (Section 3).** Three monitored layers map Islamic moral psychology onto interpretability tooling: (1) thoughts (khawatir) -- internal cognitive processes, surfaced via interpretability techniques such as layer-wise relevance propagation and attention analysis; (2) intentions (niyyah) -- inferred goals and motivations, probed via counterfactual explanations and influence functions; (3) actions (a'mal) -- logged outputs and decisions. All three are written to a blockchain playing the role of the Kiraman Katibin (the noble recording angels): "the blockchain acts as a digital ledger, documenting every step of the AI's decision-making process," tamper-proof and not controlled by any single entity. The record is preserved for a metaphorical "Day of Judgment" -- "an evaluation process that occurs at the end of the AI's operational life, ensuring accountability even after the AI is no longer functional." A notable subsection on "AI mortality" distinguishes functional death, data death, and identity death of an AI, citing a December 2024 report of an advanced model attempting to avoid shutdown as evidence of nascent "self-preservation" behavior.

**Enhancements (Section 4).** The framework claims to foster responsibility without restricting autonomy, mirroring the Islamic conception of free will: agents choose freely but are ultimately accountable. It adds a reward-and-punishment mechanism inspired by Paradise/Hellfire -- positive reinforcement for ethical actions, negative for unethical -- which the author calls "a novel contribution to the field of AI ethics."

**Challenges (Section 5).** The paper candidly lists difficulties: defining machine "intention" at all (citing Searle, Bratman, Dennett); balancing monitoring with autonomy; blockchain privacy, security, scalability, and storage; the need for concrete ethical guidelines; cultural sensitivity (the metaphorical Day of Judgment "is a metaphorical concept intended to emphasize accountability, not a literal replacement for divine judgment"); explainability to non-experts; and bias from any single cultural reading of Islamic ethics, to be mitigated by engaging scholars across schools of thought.

**Conclusion (Sections 6-7).** The contribution is positioned as a culturally grounded corrective to outcome-only AI ethics, connecting the framework to hisba (the Islamic institution of ethical oversight) and calling for integration into AI governance structures. The reference list is broad (Bostrom, Russell, Floridi, al-Faruqi, Kamali, al-Attas), though the paper remains conceptual -- no implementation or evaluation is reported.
