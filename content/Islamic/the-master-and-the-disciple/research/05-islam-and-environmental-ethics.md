## Digest

Gada's Element argues that Islamic tradition is not a latecomer to
environmental thought but contains, in its scripture, jurisprudence, and
civilizational practice, a complete environmental-ethical system. The abstract
states the thesis directly: "Islamic culture and civilization are rich in
environmental concerns" and "Protecting and conserving the environment are not
only moral duties but also an obligation in Islam." The system, he claims,
"offers both conceptual paradigms and operational components to realize
environmental justice and sustainable development."

The book is organized into six sections plus an Introduction and Conclusion.
Section 1 surveys the current environmental crisis, which Gada attributes to
"the modern consumerist outlook and uncontrolled economic pursuits that
disregard essential human values and responsibilities toward nature and fellow
human beings." He defines the object of study carefully: "an environmental
problem is characterized as any change in the physical environment's condition
arising from human involvement in the environment" (citing Glasbergen and
Blowers). He names the three master challenges as "climate change,
biodiversity loss, and overuse of critical natural resources," and marshals
statistics: air pollution causes about seven million premature deaths a year
(WHO), land-based sources account for roughly 80 percent of marine pollution
(UNESCO), one in four people lack safe drinking water, and tropical forest
loss runs near 8.3 million acres a year.

Section 2 maps seven predominant secular frameworks in environmental
philosophy -- anthropocentrism, biocentrism, sentiocentrism, ecocentrism, deep
ecology, ecofeminism, and social ecology -- and closes by arguing for "the
necessity of a theocentric approach," whose Islamic version the later sections
develop. This is the book's pivotal move: none of the secular centrisms, in
Gada's view, anchors obligation the way a God-centered cosmology does.

Section 3 turns to premodern Islam: classical works "that contain abundant
references to nature" and the nature-related practices and jurisprudence
developed before modernity. Section 4 introduces the three main approaches to
contemporary Islamic eco-theology and surveys notable modern authors. Section
5, which Gada calls "the major portion of this Element," systematizes the
fundamental principles derived from the Quran and Sunnah: unity (tawhid),
stewardship (khilafah), trusteeship (amanah) with accountability (akhirah),
balance (mizan), and natural disposition (fitrah). From these he derives
practical guidelines: moderation, avoidance of waste and corruption,
preservation of the Earth's beauty and cleanliness, recognition of the rights
of animals and plants, and respect for water.

Section 6 moves from theory to practice, presenting case studies from
Zanzibar, Indonesia, Qatar, Egypt, Africa, the UK, and the US that "illustrate
how Islamic ethics have been harnessed in diverse forms to inspire positive
behavioral changes among Muslims toward nature," with a brief treatment of
interfaith approaches. The Conclusion offers recommendations as practical
guidelines for sustainability.

The overall shape is apologetic-constructive: diagnose the crisis in secular
terms, show the limits of secular ethical frameworks, then present Islamic
theocentrism as both conceptually richer (obligation grounded in divine trust
rather than human preference) and operationally tested (premodern
jurisprudence plus modern grassroots initiatives). Keywords given by the
publisher: Islam, environment, ethics, Tawhid, nature.
