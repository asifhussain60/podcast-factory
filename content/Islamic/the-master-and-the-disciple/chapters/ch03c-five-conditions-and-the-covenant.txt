# Five Conditions and the Covenant

*Episode three of* The Master and the Disciple. *The young man has begged to become the scholar's servant. The instrument of seeking — hearing, sight, and the heart — has been named. But before the scholar agrees to teach a single doctrine, he sets a precondition that the young man does not yet understand: knowledge is of no benefit until it has been put into action, and action is of no benefit until it has been accepted, and the only thing Allah accepts is sincerity. He then imposes five conditions of secrecy that read, on first hearing, as pedagogical fussiness. They are not. They are protocols of survival. The young man's father is a known leader of a rival community that has rejected the Imam. Premature disclosure of the call could destroy the mission. The scholar trains the boy through a period of trial; when the boy's merit and perseverance become clear, the covenant is taken — the pact of Allah, the key the Master names as the difference between sacred and stolen, and the book's title turns out to teach the reader what a disciple actually is.*

## Where this episode picks up

Why would a teacher who has just been begged for knowledge refuse to teach a single doctrine until his student swears to keep it secret? At the close of the last episode, the scholar had named the design of creation, the chosen ranks of provision, and the three-part instrument of hearing, sight, and the heart. The young man begged to become his servant. The scholar answered, before he agreed: "Surely Allah did not create his creatures as fully grown adults or scholars. He created them as infants — small and ignorant — so that they grow big after having been small, and come to know after having been ignorant. Their smallness is a precondition for their greatness, and their ignorance is a sign pointing toward knowledge. The same is true of spiritual knowledge: one can only attain its essence on the basis of preceding, lower forms of knowledge, which the hearts can quickly assimilate. That is Allah's accustomed way regarding His servants."

Notice what the scholar has just done with the phrase Allah's accustomed way. He uses it to mean the recurring divine pattern embedded in creation, not merely prophetic practice or a jurisprudential category. It allows him to speak of the divine custom of pairs in cosmology and the divine custom of sending messengers as one and the same divine habit. Just as one cannot feed solid food to a newborn — for all the newborn can do is suck milk — most of us are spiritual infants who must first learn history, then the outward Law, then the inner interpretation, before reaching the inner realities. The graduated ladder is itself the divine custom.

The young man said: "Indeed I am an infant in relation to what I wish to achieve, so raise and educate me." The scholar replied that compassion alone would not bring him closer without his own working for it: "For just as every word has a proper employment that can only be accepted if it is sincere and true, similarly knowledge is of no benefit until it has been put into action, and action is of no benefit until it is accepted by Allah. And the only thing Allah accepts is sincerity."

That last clause — *the only thing Allah accepts is sincerity* — is the same standard the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) set in the saying that opens the canonical Sunni Prophetic-traditions collection, the chapter on revelation:

> Actions are only by intentions, and every person shall have only what he intended. So whoever's migration was for Allah and His Messenger, his migration was for Allah and His Messenger; and whoever's migration was for some worldly gain or a woman he would marry, his migration was for what he migrated to.
> — Source: the canonical Sunni Prophetic-traditions collection, chapter on revelation, first hadith (the standard English rendering).

The hadith and the scholar's instruction make one structure: the visible act is indifferent; what determines whether Allah accepts it is the intention beneath it. The Persian master is not improvising a private doctrine; he is restating, in the disciple's ear, the gate-keeping principle of the entire tradition. Two strangers can perform the same prayer side by side; only one of the prayers will be received, because only one of the prayers had its origin in the heart.

He then advised on how to acquire knowledge: "Do not seek knowledge impatiently, and do not dismiss it out of negligence. Nor should you accept it out of blind imitation without a convincing argument — for then it would slip out of your heart at the first onset of doubt. But do not deny an argument once it has been firmly established, or you will become the pleader for all that is false. And do not stop inquiring about the knowledge you have attained, or your judgment will become sterile. You must trust me without prejudice, make every effort to understand without confusion, seek the path as you seek the truth, and constantly ask Allah's help in your endeavor."

That instruction is the disposition under which the conditions about to be imposed make sense. The disciple is being told that the asking does not end at the first answer, the accepting does not stop at the first proof, and the trust required is not blind. He is being prepared for an arrangement in which he will be expected to keep questioning while also keeping silent.

## The five conditions and the political stake

When the young man asked about the revealed paths of righteous acts, the scholar imposed five conditions on him before answering anything.

First: do not neglect anything entrusted to you, nor take anything given to you lightly.

Second: do not conceal anything when questioned.

Third: do not come looking for him whenever you have a question.

Fourth: do not ask about anything until he himself brings it up.

Fifth: do not mention him to your father.

Read those five together. The first two are about how the disciple holds the trust internally — nothing trivial in his hands, nothing hidden when he is questioned. The next two are about the rhythm of the relationship between master and disciple — the disciple does not get to set the pace of the teaching; the master does. The fifth is about the world outside the relationship. And of the five, the fifth is by far the heaviest.

The young man's father was a well-known leader of his tribe and a strong supporter of a rival community that had rejected the source of true knowledge, the Imam. This is the political stake of the entire story: the disciple is the son of a sectarian leader who has rejected the Imam, and any premature disclosure of the call could destroy the mission. The five conditions are not pedagogical fussiness. They are protocols of survival in a hostile environment.

The scholar's instruction on how to handle the father is itself doctrinal. He instructed the young man not to move away from his father — for that would invite suspicion — but to stay with him, respect him, please him where possible, while protecting his innermost beliefs and keeping his spiritual concerns secret. "Indeed Allah will protect you from his, and other people's, hostility." The five conditions taken together form a discipline of prudential concealment, in which the disciple maintains every external mark of filial loyalty so that the inner work cannot be detected.

The Quran itself authorizes this discipline. In the chapter of the Family of Imran, verse twenty-eight, in the translation by Marmaduke Pickthall:

> Let not the believers take disbelievers for their friends in preference to believers. Whoever does that has no connection with Allah unless you but guard yourselves against them, taking, as it were, security.
> — Source: Quran, chapter of the Family of Imran, verse 28 (Pickthall).

The phrase rendered "taking security" is the textual root of the doctrine the scholar is enforcing. Concealment under duress is not deceit; it is the believer holding his life and his teacher's life in his own hands. The verse names the same conditions the scholar names: a hostile environment, a believer embedded inside it, and an obligation to *guard*. The disciple keeps every visible filial form so that the invisible bond can survive.

The Commander of the Faithful (peace be upon him) restated the same standard in one of his short sayings, preserved in the canonical collection of his sermons, letters, and aphorisms:

> The tongue of the believer is behind his heart, and the heart of the hypocrite is behind his tongue. For when the believer wishes to speak a word he reflects on it: if it is good he discloses it, and if it is evil he keeps it hidden.
> — Source: the canonical collection of his sermons, letters, and aphorisms, the aphorism section, saying number forty (Sayed Ali Reza translation).

A tongue placed behind a heart is exactly the architecture the five conditions are building inside the disciple. The first two conditions train the heart to receive without dropping or diluting. The next two train the tongue to stay still until the heart is ready. The fifth makes the rule literal under the worst of all listeners — a father who would, in good faith, betray the secret to a community that has refused the Imam.

The scholar and the young man met occasionally over a period of time. The young man kept performing everything assigned to him from the outward Law, though without the underlying knowledge behind those actions, and not even knowing the scholar's residence. This was his period of trial. When his merit and perseverance became clear, a meeting was arranged. The young man prostrated himself in gratitude, and the scholar gently corrected him: gratitude offered before the deed is done gives rise to suspicion of insincerity. Then he prepared to teach.

## The covenant: the pact of Allah and the key of allegiance

"There is a key to religion," the scholar began, "which renders it either sacred or profane, like the difference between marriage and fornication." Hold that comparison. Both are the same act. What differentiates the permitted from the forbidden is the formality of the marriage rite — blessing the act with Allah's name. It is the same animal we eat, but the ritual slaughter blessed with Allah's name makes it permitted. "That key," the scholar said, "is the pact of Allah, which confirms His rights, encompasses His obligations and safeguards His friends. It is the rope of Allah upon His earth and His guarantee of security among His creation."

The image of the rope is taken directly from the chapter of the Family of Imran, verse one hundred and three, in the Pickthall rendering:

> And hold fast, all of you together, to the rope of Allah, and be not divided among yourselves. And remember Allah's favor unto you: how you were enemies and He made friendship between your hearts, so that by His grace you became brethren.
> — Source: Quran, chapter of the Family of Imran, verse 103 (Pickthall).

The rope is not metaphor. It is what binds a fragmented people back into a single body. The scholar names the pact as that very rope, descended into the disciple's hands by means of an oath. To take the pact is to grasp the rope; to grasp the rope is to be reattached to the chain by which Allah preserves His friends.

Then the scholar took the noble oath of allegiance from the young man — for without it all this knowledge of the inner interpretation would be forbidden. This is the mechanism of the bond of allegiance to the Imam. The covenant of allegiance is what makes religious action effective. True knowledge belongs to the Imam; accepting his allegiance is like paying the owner his due, while using that knowledge without the allegiance is theft. And stolen food is forbidden regardless of how permitted the animal is.

It is told of the sixth Imam that a man stole five apples and gave them to charity, calculating that fifty rewards for the charity, minus five sins for the theft, left him forty-five net rewards. The Imam corrected him: "When you stole those apples you earned five sins. And when you gave them to the old lady you gained another five sins" — because he had propagated the forbidden into another person's hand. Total: ten sins, zero rewards. This is why acts of piety performed without allegiance are ineffective. The act without its covenant is stolen, and what is stolen cannot be sanctified.

The Quran itself binds the act of pledging to the act of being received. From the chapter of the Conquest, verse ten, in the Pickthall translation:

> Verily those who swear allegiance unto thee, swear allegiance only unto Allah. The hand of Allah is above their hands. So whoever breaks his oath breaks it only to his soul's hurt; and whoever keeps his covenant with Allah, on him will He bestow immense reward.
> — Source: Quran, chapter of the Conquest, verse 10 (Pickthall).

The verse is the divine signature on the scholar's procedure. The disciple swears allegiance to a Persian stranger in a rented inn, and the swearing reaches further than the stranger; it lands on the hand above his. The act of oath-taking is the moment at which a private exchange between two men becomes a public transaction with the Owner of religion. From that moment the disciple's prayers are no longer stolen offerings; they are paid in to the One Who is owed.

The great early voice of the inward path, the master of Baghdad in the third century of the Muslim calendar, captured the same logic in one of his preserved sayings:

> Sincerity is a secret between the servant and his Lord. The recording angel does not know it so as to write it down; the devil does not know it so as to corrupt it; the passions do not know it so as to bend it. Whoever's act is owned by another cannot make it sincere.
> — Source: the early Sufi master of Baghdad, preserved in the standard biographical compendium of the Sufis (Arberry's English rendering of the *Muslim Saints and Mystics* tradition, the chapter on him).

Sincerity is a secret transaction, and a stolen secret cannot be transacted. The scholar is making the same point in a different vocabulary. The act has no value unless the act is owned by the actor; the act is not owned by the actor unless the actor has paid the rightful Owner His due; the due is paid through the pact of Allah.

Track the structure of the doctrine. Marriage and fornication are the same physical act, distinguished by the formality of the marriage rite. The permitted animal and the forbidden one are the same animal, distinguished by the ritual slaughter. The pious deed and the stolen deed are the same deed, distinguished by the bond of allegiance. In each pair, what makes the act sacred is not the act but the framing that places the act under Allah's name and under the owner's rights. The pact of Allah is the framing that does that for religious action.

## Why the title teaches: discipline, disciple, and the graduated ladder

This is also why the title of the book is itself a teaching. The outward Law is a graduated discipline. Ritual purity and the daily prayer five times every day. The fast one month yearly. The alms once yearly. The pilgrimage once a lifetime. And allegiance to the Imam every breath. The frequencies are stacked from the most punctual ritual at the bottom to the most continuous one at the top — and the most continuous one is the one that makes all the others count.

The Prophet captured the stacking in the foundational saying that opens the section on faith in the canonical Sunni Prophetic-traditions collection:

> Islam is built upon five: that none is worshiped but Allah, that the daily prayer be established, that the alms be paid, that the fast be kept in its month, and that the pilgrimage be performed by whoever is able to find a way.
> — Source: the canonical Sunni Prophetic-traditions collection, the book of faith (the standard English rendering).

Five pillars in the Prophetic saying; five conditions in the master's instruction. The match is not accidental. The five pillars build the discipline at the level of the visible body; the five conditions build the discipline at the level of the invisible heart. The visible discipline trains the limbs into habits of submission. The invisible discipline trains the soul into habits of holding the secret. Both are the outward Law in the broader sense the scholar uses the term — the trained, graduated body the believer becomes.

The outward Law prepares us for knowledge; it creates discipline within us. That is where the word disciple comes from — disciple from discipline, and discipline from the Law. The disciple of this book becomes the disciple precisely by submitting to that graduated discipline under the covenant. The title — *The Book of the Master and the Disciple* — is not a label. It is the doctrine of the book in two words. The disciple is what the seeker becomes when he accepts the discipline; the discipline is what the Law does to the heart; the covenant is what makes the whole arrangement sacred rather than stolen.

So the scholar recited the oath, and the young man repeated it and bound himself to it, his eyes streaming with tears, until he knew with certainty that from that moment on he was a member of the party of Allah and the party of Allah's friends.

That phrase, *the party of Allah*, is the Quran's own designation for the people who have grasped the rope. From the chapter of the Table Spread, verse fifty-six, in the Pickthall rendering:

> And whoever takes Allah and His Messenger and those who believe as his protectors, then verily the party of Allah, they are the victorious.
> — Source: Quran, chapter of the Table Spread, verse 56 (Pickthall).

The verse is the door the disciple walks through at the moment of the oath. He had been one of the townspeople who happened to be inquiring. He becomes, in the same instant the master finishes reciting the formula and he repeats it back, a member of the named party — protected, victorious, accountable, no longer alone.

## Closing

The opening narrative arc of the book has now landed where it was always going to land: on the night of the covenant, with the disciple's tears, with the certainty that he has been accepted into the party of Allah. The cosmology, the hierarchy, the inner interpretation — all of these stand on this opening establishment of why transmission matters and why the covenant makes the difference between sacred and stolen. The disciple has been chosen, tested, conditioned, tried, and bound. The teaching can now begin.
