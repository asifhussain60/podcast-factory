# Five Conditions and the Covenant

*Episode three of* The Master and the Disciple. *The young man has begged to become the scholar's servant. The instrument of seeking — hearing, sight, and the heart — has been named. But before the scholar agrees to teach a single doctrine, he sets a precondition that the young man does not yet understand: knowledge is of no benefit until it has been put into action, and action is of no benefit until it has been accepted, and the only thing Allah accepts is sincerity. He then imposes five conditions of secrecy that read, on first hearing, as pedagogical fussiness. They are not. They are protocols of survival. The young man's father is a known leader of a rival community that has rejected the Imam. Premature disclosure of the da'wa could destroy the mission. The scholar trains the boy through a period of trial; when the boy's merit and perseverance become clear, the covenant is taken — the 'ahd Allah, the key the Master names as the difference between sacred and stolen, and the book's title turns out to teach the reader what a disciple actually is.*

## Where this episode picks up

At the close of the last episode, the scholar had named the design of creation, the chosen ranks of provision, and the three-part instrument of hearing, sight, and the heart. The young man begged to become his servant. The scholar answered, before he agreed: "Surely Allah did not create his creatures as fully grown adults or scholars. He created them as infants — small and ignorant — so that they grow big after having been small, and come to know after having been ignorant. Their smallness is a precondition for their greatness, and their ignorance is a sign pointing toward knowledge. The same is true of spiritual knowledge: one can only attain its essence on the basis of preceding, lower forms of knowledge, which the hearts can quickly assimilate. That is Allah's sunna regarding His servants."

Notice what the scholar has just done with the word sunna. He uses it to mean Allah's accustomed way, the recurring divine pattern embedded in creation, not merely prophetic practice or a jurisprudential category. It allows him to speak of the sunna of pairs in cosmology and the sunna of sending messengers as one and the same divine habit. Just as one cannot feed biryani to a newborn — for all the newborn can do is suck milk — most of us are spiritual infants who must first learn history, then shari'a, then ta'wil, before reaching haqa'iq. The graduated ladder is itself the sunna.

The young man said: "Indeed I am an infant in relation to what I wish to achieve, so raise and educate me." The scholar replied that compassion alone would not bring him closer without his own working for it: "For just as every word has a proper employment, a tasrif, that can only be accepted if it is sincere and true, similarly knowledge is of no benefit until it has been put into action, and action is of no benefit until it is accepted by Allah. And the only thing Allah accepts is sincerity."

He then advised on how to acquire knowledge: "Do not seek knowledge impatiently, and do not dismiss it out of negligence. Nor should you accept it out of blind imitation without a convincing argument — for then it would slip out of your heart at the first onset of doubt. But do not deny an argument once it has been firmly established, or you will become the pleader for all that is false. And do not stop inquiring about the knowledge you have attained, or your judgment will become sterile. You must trust me without prejudice, make every effort to understand without confusion, seek the path as you seek the truth, and constantly ask Allah's help in your endeavor."

That instruction is the disposition under which the conditions about to be imposed make sense. The disciple is being told that the asking does not end at the first answer, the accepting does not stop at the first proof, and the trust required is not blind. He is being prepared for an arrangement in which he will be expected to keep questioning while also keeping silent.

## The five conditions and the political stake

When the young man asked about the revealed paths of righteous acts, the scholar imposed five conditions on him before answering anything.

First: do not neglect anything entrusted to you, nor take anything given to you lightly.

Second: do not conceal anything when questioned.

Third: do not come looking for him whenever you have a question.

Fourth: do not ask about anything until he himself brings it up.

Fifth: do not mention him to your father.

Read those five together. The first two are about how the disciple holds the trust internally — nothing trivial in his hands, nothing hidden when he is questioned. The next two are about the rhythm of the relationship between master and disciple — the disciple does not get to set the pace of the teaching; the master does. The fifth is about the world outside the relationship. And of the five, the fifth is by far the heaviest.

The young man's father was a well-known leader of his tribe and a strong supporter of a rival community that had rejected the source of true knowledge, the Imam. This is the political stake of the entire story: the disciple is the son of a sectarian leader who has rejected the Imam, and any premature disclosure of the da'wa could destroy the mission. The five conditions are not pedagogical fussiness. They are protocols of survival in a hostile environment.

The scholar's instruction on how to handle the father is itself doctrinal. He instructed the young man not to move away from his father — for that would invite suspicion — but to stay with him, respect him, please him where possible, while protecting his innermost beliefs and keeping his spiritual concerns secret. "Indeed Allah will protect you from his, and other people's, hostility." The five conditions taken together form a discipline of taqiyya, of prudential concealment, in which the disciple maintains every external mark of filial loyalty so that the inner work cannot be detected.

The scholar and the young man met occasionally over a period of time. The young man kept performing everything assigned to him from the shari'a, though without the underlying knowledge behind those actions, and not even knowing the scholar's residence. This was his period of trial. When his merit and perseverance became clear, a meeting was arranged. The young man prostrated himself in gratitude, and the scholar gently corrected him: gratitude offered before the deed is done gives rise to suspicion of insincerity. Then he prepared to teach.

## The covenant: 'ahd Allah and the key of walaya

"There is a key to religion," the scholar began, "which renders it either sacred or profane, like the difference between marriage and fornication." Hold that comparison. Both are the same act. What differentiates the halal from the haram is the formality of the nikah — blessing the act with Allah's name. It is the same animal we eat, but the dhabiha — the slaughter blessed with Allah's name — makes it halal. "That key," the scholar said, "is the pact of Allah, the 'ahd Allah, which confirms His rights, encompasses His obligations and safeguards His friends. It is the rope of Allah upon His earth and His guarantee of security among His creation."

Then the scholar took the oath of allegiance, the 'ahd sharif, from the young man — for without it all this knowledge of ta'wil would be haram. This is the mechanism of walaya. The covenant of allegiance is what makes religious action effective. True knowledge belongs to the Imam; accepting his walaya is like paying the owner his due, while using that knowledge without the walaya is theft. And stolen food is haram regardless of how halal the animal is.

It is told of Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq (peace be upon him) that a man stole five apples and gave them to charity, calculating that fifty rewards for the charity, minus five sins for the theft, left him forty-five net rewards. The Imam corrected him: "When you stole those apples you earned five sins. And when you gave them to the old lady you gained another five sins" — because he had propagated the haram to another person. Total: ten sins, zero rewards. This is why acts of piety performed without walaya are ineffective. The act without its covenant is stolen, and what is stolen cannot be sanctified.

Track the structure of the doctrine. Marriage and fornication are the same physical act, distinguished by the formality of nikah. The halal animal and the haram one are the same animal, distinguished by the dhabiha. The pious deed and the stolen deed are the same deed, distinguished by the walaya. In each pair, what makes the act sacred is not the act but the framing that places the act under Allah's name and under the owner's rights. The 'ahd Allah is the framing that does that for religious action.

## Why the title teaches: discipline, disciple, and the graduated ladder

This is also why the title of the book is itself a teaching. Shari'a is a graduated discipline. Tahara and salat five times daily. Sawm one month yearly. Zakat once yearly. Hajj once a lifetime. And walaya every breath. The frequencies are stacked from the most punctual ritual at the bottom to the most continuous one at the top — and the most continuous one is the one that makes all the others count.

Shari'a prepares us for knowledge; it creates discipline within us. That is where the word disciple comes from — disciple from discipline, and discipline from shari'a. The disciple of this book becomes the disciple precisely by submitting to that graduated discipline under the covenant. The title — The Book of the Master and the Disciple — is not a label. It is the doctrine of the book in two words. The disciple is what the seeker becomes when he accepts the discipline; the discipline is what shari'a does to the heart; the covenant is what makes the whole arrangement sacred rather than stolen.

So the scholar recited the oath, and the young man repeated it and bound himself to it, his eyes streaming with tears, until he knew with certainty that from that moment on he was a member of the party of Allah and the party of Allah's friends.

## Closing

The opening narrative arc of the book has now landed where it was always going to land: on the night of the covenant, with the disciple's tears, with the certainty that he has been accepted into the Hizb Allah. Every later move in the book — the cosmology, the hierarchy, the ta'wil — stands on this opening establishment of why transmission matters and why the covenant makes the difference between sacred and stolen. The disciple has been chosen, tested, conditioned, tried, and bound. The teaching can now begin.
