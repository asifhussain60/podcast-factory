# Jewels, the Moon, and the Description of Religion

*Episode sixteen of* The Master and the Disciple. *The cup has been emptied. Now Abu Malik invites Salih to state his arguments — wisdom is like jewels, he says; observed in the sunlight, they sparkle; left alone, they do not lose value. Salih accepts the image but adds the condition that decides everything: jewels are useful only where there is no confusion between a real jewel and forged glass. The episode that follows turns on that condition. Salih names the people who can tell a jewel from glass — those who receive divine knowledge from the heavens — and Abu Malik asks the harder question: who, after the prophets, can that possibly be? Salih's answer reframes religion itself. Worship grounded only in transmitted reports, he says, should not be called religion at all. He gives the moon-sighting controversy as the working example, recovers the true meaning of "follow the moon" as the established symbol of the wasi, and finishes with the description of religion that Abu Malik will confess, by the close of the episode, has the essential meaning the religion of his world has lost.*

## Where this episode opens

The previous episode closed with Salih's caution that the truth confirms itself by its own excellence but that every seed needs its ground prepared and every knowledge needs its heart purified by proper argument. The cup has been emptied; the ground has been broken. Now the seed is offered. Abu Malik gives the opening image. He invites Salih to state his arguments. "Words of wisdom are like jewels," he says. "Observed in the sunlight they sparkle; left alone, they do not lose value." It is a graceful invitation. Wisdom, in this reading, is what does not lose its worth in the dark.

Salih agrees to the image and adds the condition without which the image will not hold. Jewels are useful, he says, only where there is no confusion between a real jewel and forged glass.

## Jewels and the assayers of divine knowledge

Salih then tells Abu Malik a story about his own former faith. The jewel of that faith, presented to the jewelers — to those whose work is the knowledge of gems — had been exposed as a forgery of glass. His teacher, and the people who receive divine knowledge from the heavens, had been the assayers. The verdict had not gone the way the merchant expected. The thing that had looked like a jewel under the sunlight of his community's praise turned out, under the lamps of the assayers, to be glass.

Salih draws the principle that the story carries. Spiritual knowledge, he says, cannot be accepted as genuine until it has been presented to those who receive divine knowledge from the heavens. Only if they accept it can it be deemed genuine. The criterion is not the sparkle in the sunlight of public opinion. The criterion is the verdict of the people whose trade is assaying the divine.

Abu Malik asks the question every reader of the episode has been waiting to hear. Who, after the prophets and messengers, can these people of divine knowledge be? Revelation has ended. The community now worships by transmitted reports. The chain through which divine knowledge once descended is — on the standard reading of his world — closed.

Salih's answer reframes the question. "Every religion in which worship is based solely on transmitted reports should not be named religion at all." That is the load-bearing sentence and it is worth holding a moment. He does not say transmitted reports are worthless. He says a religion that is grounded only in them is not religion. He gives the reason. "Religion is one, purely upholding what is due to Allah without difference; while transmitted reports all differ from and deny one another." The unity of religion and the difference of reports are an incompatibility. A house cannot be built on a foundation whose stones contradict one another.

## Follow the moon

Salih illustrates with the example that the community of Abu Malik's world cannot ignore. The confusion of moon-sighting. There are communities, he says, that eat on the first day of Ramadan — which is forbidden — and fast on the day of Eid — also forbidden — justifying it by the hadith of moon-sighting. The hadith of moon-sighting, he says, has turned into the hadith of moon-fighting. The communities have been so unable to agree on whose moon-sighting counts that they fast on each other's feast days and feast on each other's fast days and find the contradiction in transmitted texts.

Then Salih recovers the meaning the bare text never states outright. When the Messenger of Allah (peace be upon him and his family) was asked, before Ramadan, "who do we follow when you are gone," he answered, "follow the moon." Read with the community's symbols in hand, the answer is not a piece of astronomy. It is a designation of a successor. The moon is the established symbol of the wasi — the trustee, the one to whom authority is left. The sun is the established symbol of the natiq — the one who speaks the revelation. To say "follow the moon" was to designate the wasi the community would follow when the sun of revelation had set. The Messenger used the moon as a generic formula. In all matters of your life, follow Ali. Not limited to Ramadan; not limited to a single visible disc in a single sky. The moon was chosen as the symbol because the moon consistently follows the same path across the sky that the sun follows.

The ancient Arabs, the source records, were capable of understanding this symbolism. They lived in a world of symbols and they could read them. But out of hatred and jealousy for Maulana Ali (peace be upon him) they reduced the saying to the literal physical moon, producing the chaos that followed. The symbol was collapsed into the disc. The wasi was lost into the lunar argument. And once that move was made the community's calendar became the place where its loss was acted out — feast against fast, sect against sect, hadith against hadith.

Salih then cites the Quran's own witness against this confusion. The Quran calls the days of fasting "a fixed number of days" — clearly counted days (chapter 2, verses 183 to 184). And it declares of the heavenly bodies: "The sun and the moon follow precisely calculated paths" (the verse Salih cites uses the word husban, the superlative of hisab — calculation; the sun and the moon never deviate). What never deviates can be calculated. What can be calculated can be put on a fixed calendar. The Quran's own description of the cosmos is the description of a calendar that can be known in advance — exactly what makes a fixed lunar calendar possible. The confusion is not a feature of the source. The confusion is a feature of what the community has done with the source.

Salih closes the example with a small case study that bites. He recalls a man who came to Abu Malik after divorcing his wife once and then breaking his oath. Abu Malik, faced with the contradictory reports the case raised, told the man — "Some say she is divorced; some say she is not; act according to whichever you like" — leaving him not knowing whether his act was permitted or forbidden. Salih puts the question to Abu Malik plainly. "Is that how wise judgment should be in Allah's religion?" The case is small and the implication is large. A religion that cannot tell a man whether he is married or divorced is not a religion. It is a market of reports.

Abu Malik concedes. "By my life — sharia has been corrupted and rendered ineffectual, and we are only clinging to the name of religion. So what is the state of religion in your opinion?"

That concession is the door Salih has been working toward. The greatest scholar of the opposing community has just stated, in his own voice, that his community is clinging to a name whose content has been corrupted. The next move is Salih's description of what religion actually is.

## The description of religion

Salih's craft in answering is to draw out Abu Malik's own definition first. Religion, Abu Malik allows, consists of commands, prohibitions, and prophetic traditions. And sharia, he allows when Salih presses, has the same content. Salih lets the equivalence stand for a beat and then shows what it costs. Abu Malik has placed religion and sharia at the same level, so the corruption of sharia he has just admitted is the corruption of religion itself. He is left with nothing. The equivalence dissolves the ground he had been standing on.

Then Salih gives his own description. It is worth hearing whole.

"Religion is the veil Allah has strengthened against those who sow corruption, its gates opened to those seeking truth. It is a rope extending from the heavens to the earth, continuous and without breakage. The firm handhold of those who adhere to Allah. The ark of the divine presence. The ship of salvation. The light of life. Its knowledge is well-protected, and its jewels have their distinctive signs."

Hold each image. Religion as a veil — strengthened against corruption, opened to seekers. The seeker passes through the veil; the corruptor is held back. Religion as a rope from heaven to earth — continuous and unbroken. The rope is not the report of one human to another; it is the connection between worlds. Religion as the firm handhold — the urwa wuthqa the Quran names — for those who hold fast to Allah. Religion as the ark of the divine presence — the sakina that descends upon those who are inside the ark. Religion as the ship of salvation — the safina that carries those who climb aboard through waters in which all the rest drown. Religion as the light of life. And finally the description that returns the episode to its opening image: the knowledge of religion is well-protected, and its jewels have their distinctive signs. The jewels Abu Malik invoked at the start of the episode have their distinctive signs. The signs are what allow the assayer to tell the jewel from the glass.

Abu Malik replies with the line that opens the next part of the debate. "If Allah has a religion by which He is worshiped, and a day for His rewards and punishments, then indeed this would be its essential meaning. But what about the reality of what you have described?" He has accepted the description as the essential meaning religion would have to have. He is now asking for the reality of which the description is the picture. The veil, the rope, the handhold, the ark, the ship, the light, the protected knowledge, the jewels with their signs — he is willing to grant that this is what religion would have to be. What he is asking is how to find it.

## Closing

The episode opened with Abu Malik's image of jewels in the sunlight and closed with Salih's image of jewels whose signs reveal which is real. In between, Salih has named the assayers of divine knowledge as those who receive it from the heavens; refused the standing of any religion grounded only in transmitted reports; recovered the meaning of "follow the moon" as the designation of the wasi the Messenger was leaving behind; cited the Quran's own description of the sun and moon as following calculated paths against the chaos of competing moon-sightings; pressed the case of a man left not knowing whether he was married or divorced; drawn from Abu Malik the confession that sharia has been corrupted; dissolved the equivalence of religion and sharia by which Abu Malik had been holding what little he had left; and given his own description of religion — veil, rope, handhold, ark, ship, light, protected knowledge, jewels with their signs. Abu Malik has named the description as the essential meaning religion would have to have. The way is now opened, the source tells us, for the final part of the debate — the part in which the description must be turned into a reality the seeker can lay hold of.
