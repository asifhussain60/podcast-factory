# Homecoming, the Father, and the Debate with Abu Malik

The father's anger did not spare him. "O my son," he said, "is this the reward of sons to their fathers? I taught you only the best that a child can be taught by a father. You never denied your own self nor renounced your beliefs until the day I grew old with this strange man. You drew him in upon me, and you crossed over with him in his affair without ever consulting your parents. If what I knew was true, then you have hindered me in my very self, for you concealed the truth from me. And if it is false, then you have disobeyed me in your own ruin, and whatever has reached him has thereby reached me."

The boy answered gently. "O my father, I have shown you nothing but mercy and compassion. You raised me as a mercy from you, and you nourished me, choosing me over family and wealth. I have no wish to be unkind to you on this day of ours. Rather I ask that you grant me, out of your bounty, one of two things."

"Name the two," said the Shaykh. "What are they?"

"Either you allow me to keep to my courtesy and my kindness toward you, for I am your son whom you raised when I was small, and our affair is in the hands of God; or you grant me leave to debate with you, and give me your assurance that I am safe from your anger, so that I may draw upon your mind."

The Shaykh weighed both. "As for courtesy on the outside while our hearts differ within, that is a reproach that would stand between me and you. How long can outward agreement hold when what lies beneath it differs? I do not want that. But your debate and your candor you shall have. So say what seems best to you."

The boy said, "Consider my standing with you for forty years. Can it be anything other than one of two cases? Either you are a scholar, and you have withheld from me the teaching I was owed. If so, do not blame me for seeking my own salvation through another, since you forbade it to me. Or you are ignorant, and then you are excused in my eyes; but in that case you needed the stranger even more than I did. Were I to reach him before you, it is because of your age and the lateness of your birth, for I have heard his words just as you have heard them, and I have seen what you have seen."

The Shaykh's heart was rent by the boy's words. He saw that there was no escape for him from either argument, and his eyes overflowed with tears. "O my son," he said, "the argument you have laid against me falls likewise upon you, if you keep your affair hidden from me. So tell it to me. If it is true, I will accept it from you, as a dignity to myself; and if it is false, I will turn you back from it, as a dignity to you and out of compassion for you."

So they debated at intervals and disclosed things to one another at length, until they were both guided to the right path: acting upon the religion of God, upholding it, holding fast to it, calling others to it, and cooperating in it. Through the two of them God revived many of His creation. The son went out into the world to give the good news of his father's guidance and to call others to come to the teaching, and he returned and became a cause of guidance for them. God blessed the people of that country through them and at their hands. God's command was fulfilled, and slander was disappointed.

The boy's name was Salih, and his father's name was al-Bakhtari. The two of them were honored above their people and raised to a high standing for the excellence of the manners they had acquired. News of them spread through the land, and with it news of their religion, until it reached a group who still held to the old creed. Al-Bakhtari had long carried influence among them, and they feared that his departure from their religion would cut off that influence. So they went out and gathered before a scholar of theirs named Abu Malik, Abd al-Jabbar, the mighty servant, who was called among them, for his gravity, the abundance of his judgments, and his defense of his religion, "the heel of the inks." He was their authority through his knowledge, his grasp of speech, and his discernment of opinions.

When they came in to him they said, "O Abu Malik, do you not see what the boy has gone over to?"

"Which boy?" he asked.

"Salih ibn al-Bakhtari."

"And what has he gone over to?"

"We tell you that a stranger came to the country, with a doctrine and a creed he calls people to, and we do not know what it is. The boy went to him and went out with him without his father's leave, and was absent a while. Then he came back speaking the stranger's words and calling to what the stranger called to. And we are told that his father has now answered him in his opinion and taken his side. This grieves us, and we have held back from them for three reasons. First: al-Bakhtari's household was as our own, for the bond of religion we shared, and we dread estrangement after intimacy and hate division after brotherhood. Second: if these people are upon the truth, then we have failed to follow them and to help them in it. Third: if they have gone astray, then we have failed to turn them back and to support the truth and its people. You are the head of our religion and one of our standards, and we have come to inform you and to consult you, and we will not go against your opinion."

Abu Malik said, "Be gentle in your judgment, for what you say has given me pause. You know that al-Bakhtari and his son were among those we counted as people of gravity and piety, and that they have rights over us and old favors to their credit. You have said of them what you have said, and you are people in whom I trust the truth above all else, so that God has spared you from resorting to anything less. This is news that has come upon a people, and an argument has been shown to them, and they have followed it and been satisfied by it. We cannot rule them astray and so wrong them, nor rule them guided and so wrong ourselves by imitating them, nor say they were deceived and refute them without knowledge of them, for they are nobler with us than that. What we must do is look at this news with a fair eye, by the light of justice and with a steadfast mind. It has been said: he who is steadfast in his affairs keeps his authority secure, and he who is hasty cannot keep his foot from slipping.

"A breach in most things admits repair, and what is spoiled in them can be replaced, as with a flaw in food, whose harm is easily remedied and whose cure is near. But a breach in religion is gravely damaging and greatly ruinous. Its flaw cannot be mended nor exchanged for another, as with a flaw in a medicine, which grants its taker no reprieve and leaves him no way to save himself. We have been taught that the best of provisions are the treasures of sound minds and the acquisition of manners, whose fruits are praiseworthy; and among their fruits is the following of forbearance. Forbearance is the crown of the learned. By it noble resolve lifts them to the highest of glories, their merit is made plain in every place, and they attain the honor of gain with ease of demand. For ourselves we have laid up a measure of that forbearance we borrowed, and something of the manners we acquired in the past of our days amid narrow circumstances and hard striving. We endured that for its worth, and we call upon it against the blows of events and the accidents of the age. We cannot let it go to waste. This is the very hour of our need for it, and of putting it to use in what has come upon us in the matter of our brothers.

"There is no sense in leaving them and what they are upon without laying it open and debating it with them, with the forbearance of the capable and the earnestness of seekers, free of any doubt in what we hold and free of any accusation against these people. They may have turned from the truth in forgetfulness, and they are nobler than that; or they may have gone out into error, and that is beneath them. Falsehood has no cleverness left to it once the truth stands plain. So reflect with me on the affair of these people, for it cannot escape one of three cases. Either they are upon what we know of the religion, and what has happened among them is only some addition of manners, reports, and verses, and the speech of men is much; or an increase has come to them upon the religion they already hold, and they have rejoiced in it, for knowledge is the lost property of every believer, and an increase of knowledge is better than a lack of it; or a guidance has come to them other than what they had, through the prophecy of a prophet brought to them and calling them to it, which they have followed upon clear proof. And this is not to be denied of the command of God. Creation will never reach the limit of God's decree over them or His will for them, however hard they strive, for God is at work every day."

They said, "O Abu Malik, we came to you with hearts full of rage against these people, ready to declare them unbelievers, to refute their opinion, and to make lawful to ourselves their blood and their wealth. And now we find our hearts humbled by your words and by the way you have stood up in argument on their behalf and spoken in their defense."

Abu Malik said, "This argument of mine is not for them. It is for the truth, that I may not betray it in its absence. I have risen for the argument of the truth when the one who would argue for it is not here. Nor is it a defense of them; I have defended myself and you against speaking falsehood and hurling accusations into the unseen about what we do not encompass in knowledge. For otherwise we would be like the tyrants of the nations before us, who were pleased with their own ways of judgment and rejoiced in the knowledge they had, until they imagined they had encompassed all that God commanded and taught and willed for His creation. They said God would send no messengers after their messengers, and they never supposed that beyond the knowledge they had reached there lay any further way. So they ruled that God should cut off His argument from His creation and His deputies in His earth. They made the knowledge of God in His servants, and what He sent His prophets and messengers with, no greater than the reach of their own minds; and whatever passed beyond that was, to them, misguidance. By that opinion they went astray and led many astray. They made lawful by their own ruling what God had forbidden, and by their own opinion they killed the prophets of God. I beg you, by God, do not follow the whims of a people who went astray before you. People of intellect may be swayed by whim toward every notion and deceived away from every path, save the path of religion, for in seeking it there is safety, and its merit is not hidden."

They said, "O Abu Malik, we will not go against your command, nor is your counsel hidden from us. We will not obey you at the beginning of our affair only to disobey you at its end. You have pointed to a course and hinted at a search. If you see fit, take us to these people, that we may have their doctrine laid open to us and look into it. If it is sound guidance, we will follow it, and if it is error, we will avoid it."

Abu Malik said, "This is doubt on your part about what you yourselves hold, and one who doubts neither knows his own right nor the falsehood of another."

They said, "Then we hold firm to our truth and reject whatever else there may be, whatever it be."

Abu Malik said, "That is not how meaning is sought, nor is guidance found by rejection."

They said, "How then is the search to be made, if we neither reject falsehood nor affirm the truth?"

Abu Malik said, "If you already know the truth and accept it, and know falsehood and reject it, then you are not among the seekers at all. You are among those already learned in prophecy and guidance, who judge by revelation over the people of the world."

They said, "Then we are content with the truth we hold and have no doubt in it, and we ask to be excused from the labor of seeking."

Abu Malik said, "Are you not content that they should have merit over you in one respect, that you would grant them merit over you in many? If you will not consent to stand in need among the ranks of the seekers, will you then sit in ignorance among those who hold back?"

They said, "How has this come upon us, and in what have they been given merit over us?"

Abu Malik said, "Their merit over you is that they know what you know and, beyond it, what you do not. This is a matter that has come upon a people. If you do not seek them out, they will seek you out to it; and if you will not listen to them, they will strive against you for it. So they will gain merit over you both by being first and by their striving. As for holding back from the search, that is ignorance entire, and negligence, and a fault: upon the learned until he teaches, and upon the ignorant until he learns."

They said, "O Abu Malik, you have caught us with proof from every side, and your every word is right. Now open for us a door in your counsel by which we may act."

Abu Malik said, "To be dazzled by one's own opinion is blindness, and to neglect the search is error; yet there is no shame in the search upon the seekers. But the seeker must know the doors of seeking before he can rightly seek the truth by that knowledge. Whoever seeks the truth without knowing how to seek is the quicker to deny it, for falsehood shows its first signs in display and in agreement with prevailing opinions, while the truth shows its first signs in hardship and in opposition to whims. And none forsakes the agreement of his whim and bears patiently the onset of hardship except one whose heart is sound."

They said, "Here a door has opened for us, in which you point us toward the knowledge of seeking. Show us, then: what is that knowledge, and what is the sound heart?"

Abu Malik said, "As for the knowledge of seeking, it is that you know yourselves to be in need; and the needy one seeks his need in the humility of want before what he seeks. As for the sound heart, it is a heart that does not persist in denying whatever is brought to it, whatever it be, until the truth is what makes itself plain and shows its merit, and falsehood is what lays bare its own defect. Only then does such a heart discern falsehood by its reason and accept the truth by acceptance."

They said, "But we have been taught that whoever stands in this position, who neither verifies the truth nor refutes falsehood, is ignorant in his conduct: for he does not know the truth, that he might follow it, nor falsehood, that he might avoid it."

They said, "But we have been taught that whoever stands in this position, who neither verifies the truth so as to follow it nor refutes falsehood so as to avoid it, is ignorant in his conduct."

Abu Malik said, "What you have related, you have believed. But has this one not passed beyond the limit of the liars and become, as it were, two ignorant men in one?"

They said, "Yes. With us he is ignorant, not a liar."

Abu Malik said, "The ignorant man is nearer to the good and to seeking it. He seeks knowledge for himself; and if he does not, the learned seek it out for him, because ignorance is the root condition of creation, and there is no shame in it for one who seeks knowledge on account of his ignorance. But lying is the worst of commodities. The liar is not guided by his lying, and he is never believed in what is recorded against him."

They said, "You have made us certain of it. We have known the defect of lying, and so we will never lie about a thing until we know it for what it is. And you have taught us the door of the request, which is reverence in one's need toward what is sought. So is this reverence and humility toward what is sought to be shown after it has made its right plain, or before the statement is given?"

Abu Malik said, "Rather before the statement, so that by your reverence you may grasp the statement itself."

They said, "But if we revere what is sought until it becomes clear what it holds, and then its statement proves to be misleading, what is the standing of our reverence? Has it not been wasted?"

Abu Malik said, "No. Rather it was reverence toward God and humility before His command, and its reward is yours even if the one before whom you humbled yourself did not deserve it. For the seeker has discharged the duty of the request, even if the duty of the truth was not discharged for him. His case is like that of one who stands before some king to ask a favor, and who can ask only with reverence, flattery, and insistent complaint. If the king gives him something of what he asked, the king has met the duty of grace and earns the reward of what he gave. And if he gives nothing, the petitioner has still discharged the duty of asking gently, while stinginess has bound the withholder to his refusal. So too the seeker of knowledge: he must begin with humility, persistence, and patience under trial until he has discharged the duty of seeking, and then time will bring him afterward whatever it brings."

They said, "This is the manifest, evident truth. Will you permit us, then, to go out to the people as you have counseled, and we will not treat what you have commanded as burdensome?"

Abu Malik said, "I would not have counseled you to anything while begrudging myself the same. Were it not for the fear of falling short by turning you away from the path of God, I would have counseled you to hold back while I went ahead of you to the good, keeping that honor for myself and preferring my own soul above every other. But we go together. I comfort you with my own presence, as an honor to you and in thanks for the goodness you have singled me out to share."

They said, "It is as though you were expecting this affair before today."

He said, "This affair in particular, no. But I am always expecting deliverance from God from every side, hoping for good by every road, inclining my ear toward every speaker and seeking an increase from his words, whether he be right or wrong."

They said, "An increase from the words of the truthful, that we understand. But what do you hope to gain from the words of the false?"

Abu Malik said, "An increase of truth upon truth is light, and to recognize falsehood in the false is an increase in certainty; and the greater the certainty, the greater the light of the heart. None are saved with God except the people of certainty. I am rising now to go to the people. If you are resolved to rise with me, then look to yourselves. Whoever knows a sin in himself, let him repent to God; whoever bears a debt, let him repay it to its owner. Wash yourselves clean of every defilement, put on for the request the purest garments of resolve, and bring to what is sought the intention of the needy, for I myself am doing this. Our going out is a going out to God. If He knows truthfulness in us, He will grant us success to His truth; and if He knows in us any mockery of the faith, He will turn us from His signs by the smallest disgrace, keeping His friend pure of the mockers."

They said, "Yes. We will do what you have commanded, within three days."

He said, "No."

They said, "Within two days, then?"

He said, "No, only within this our day. If you do it, well; and if not, then I go on alone."

They said, "Then we repent to God from every sin, and we discharge every debt, and we do what you have commanded. There is no power except in God."

So they did this from that very day, and they all went out together until they came to the gate of al-Bakhtari. When they had greeted him and were seated, Abu Malik said to al-Bakhtari, "O Abu Salih, how is your son Salih, and where is he?"

Al-Bakhtari said, "Today Salih is my father, and I am the son of Salih; and Salih is with his Lord."

Abu Malik supposed that Salih had died, taking his father's words to mean that he was with his Lord, and he said, "إنا لله وإنا إليه راجعون. Has Salih died?"

Al-Bakhtari said, "Salih has not died and will never die. He abides as long as time abides."

Abu Malik said, "Here are three matters whose meanings have been turned about. The child has become the father; the Lord is found in the earth, and the Lord has a servant to whom one is brought through Him; and the one created for perishing has become everlasting. A whiff of perfume for perplexed seekers, and behind this lie further wonders."

Al-Bakhtari said, "O Abu Malik, come to your need, and be steady in your asking, for you are at the very beginning of the trial."

Abu Malik said, "You have spoken truly. The beginning of the truth is a trial. My need is to meet the righteous servant."

Al-Bakhtari said, "There is no way to that. But perhaps you mean my son, Salih?"

He said, "Yes."

He said, "That is by his leave."

So the old man rose in haste until he came in upon his son Salih and told him of Abu Malik and his companions, and Salih was glad at this for what he knew of the excellence of their minds. He said, "O God, open the hearing of Your servants' hearts, guide them by Your mercy to the signs of what is sought, and expand our breasts with the light of Your veiled light." Then he wiped his hand over his face, cleared the assembly for them, and gave his father the word to bring them in.

When they had come in upon him, greeted him, and taken their seats, Abu Malik and those below him being seated by introduction, and Salih drawing Abu Malik near to himself, the first thing Salih said was, "O Abu Malik, you have honored us with your visit and outstripped us to the good by your generosity, though you had the better right that we should visit you, to preserve your affection and to discharge the duty long owed to you."

Abu Malik said, "And when were you ever without excellence, O Abu al-Khair? You were forbearing with us when you were small and generous to us when you had grown, and you became among us a caller and a warner to the good. Your root is wholesome, your branch is high, your work is pure. So joy be to you."

Salih said to him, "Have you come to me as one making an accusation, O Ka'b al-Ahbar, or as one merely imitating? Where is that perfect mind and noble forbearance I have always known in you?"

Abu Malik said to him, "Your rank is too high for accusation, and religion stands too high for imitation."

Salih said to him, "Then how do you name me a warner, when the warner is a prophet, and the prophet is a proof from God between Him and His creation, for them and against them? How can one be a proof for you who is not also a proof against you?"

Abu Malik said, "You have spoken truly. This is the obligation. In this matter we do not act on the ease of a phrase. But we began with you in the fairest way. I saw that truthfulness was more fitting for one who has set himself as a veil for the religion and a champion for the truth and has opened its doors to seekers. We have come to know the excellence of seeking, so we sought you and hoped for you as the one sought. Come, then, to our need."

Salih said, "O Abu Malik, is not the one whose excellence you have come to know also one whose right becomes binding upon you?"

He said, "Yes, it becomes binding."

He said, "And when you came to know the excellence of seeking, did you come to know its right?"

He said, "And what is its right?"

Salih said to him, "To know its faces, so that from among them you may aim at the one in which the right becomes due to you."

Abu Malik said, "And what are its faces, and how many are they?"

Salih said to him, "Seeking has three faces: a seeker who knows, a seeker who is coming to know, and a seeker who knows only that he does not know. Which of the three are you?"

Abu Malik said, "I am more in need of knowing all three than of anything. How could I claim a station I do not know?"

Salih said to him, "Then how did you claim to be a seeker at all, when you do not know the faces of seeking?"

Abu Malik said, "I did not know them. So make them plain to me."

Salih said, "Wealth is the treasure of the people of this world, and knowledge is the treasure of the people of the Hereafter. The seeking of wealth is of three faces: a seeker who asks for what he already owns, a seeker who asks for what is owed to him at a named term, and a seeker who asks for the bounty of alms. So too is the seeking of religion and the treasures of the Hereafter of three faces. The first is the seeker who already knows, and he is the divine scholar, the '`alim rabbani`,' who seeks out the dead in their ignorance in order to revive them by his knowledge. The second is the seeker who is coming to know, and he is the learner who has grasped some of the degrees of knowledge and seeks its farthest end and limit. The third is the seeker who is only becoming acquainted with himself, and he is the ignorant man made known by his ignorance, who knows nothing beyond knowing that he is in need, and so he seeks the scholars that he may learn. These are the three faces, and the descriptions of the seekers."

Abu Malik said, "Yes, these are the faces of seeking, in religion and in the world. And I am the seeker who is only becoming acquainted with himself, who knows nothing yet has come to know that he is in need. So teach me."

He said, "Did you come to know it, or did you come to see it?"

Abu Malik said, "And what is the difference between the two?"

Salih said, "Knowledge is report; direct acquaintance is eyewitness sight."

Abu Malik said, "This too is among the things one needs to know."

Salih said, "As for the way of report, it is the case of an ignorant man who does not know that he is ignorant until he meets a scholar, and the scholar informs him of his ignorance; then he knows, by that scholar's word, that he is an ignorant man in need of knowledge, and his knowledge of his own ignorance has become a matter of report. As for acquaintance with one's need and ignorance by direct sight, it is the man who knows in his own mind that he is asking about something and does not know the face of its answer; his breast is straitened by that ignorance, and he hopes for no relief except by knowing what he was ignorant of, so his ignorance has itself become a knowing by his own mind. Ignorance is want, and want is narrowness, and narrowness is a severe need that compels the creature to seek breadth; and the breadth is the breadth of knowledge, for knowledge is wide and spacious. From this I have come to know that you are in need."

Abu Malik said, "I find within myself everything you have described. I have come to know that I am needy, of severe want. So relieve my want by your excellence."

Salih said, "I see you hastening in the request before it is firmly settled."

Abu Malik said, "Want hastened me by its narrowness, so I hastened; and I came to know the excellence of breadth, so I sought."

Salih said to him, "If want has hastened you and you have come to know the excellence of breadth, then how will you come to knowledge of the one you seek?"

Abu Malik said, "Your excellence has been proven sound to me, so I made for you; and the meaning came near, so I sought you."

Salih said to him, "From where was it proven sound to you that I, and not you, am the one known for breadth, so that you sought me? And from where did you come to know that I am the one sought, so that you made for me? No scholar versed in prophethood pointed you to me, that you might know; nor did you see in me the confirmations of the proofs, that you might recognize. Do you not see that in your seeking you are rising toward the ease of imitation, and so you wrong wisdom and you wrong yourself, like one who measures with the underside of the measure? If he does not strike it level, he wrongs his creditor, and if he strikes it too full, he wrongs himself. So it is with one who takes knowledge by imitation: if he acts on it, he is not thanked, and if he leaves it, it is said that he has disbelieved. And you, in my judgment, are wiser than this."

Abu Malik said, "I truly know that it is as you say. But I hoped that the truth would be that which establishes itself, and that I might spare you the labor of the proof; for disputation breeds contention, and contention breeds rancor, since each adversary seeks his own victory over his fellow. When will he ever reach the truth who persists in defending the false?"

Salih said to him, "How excellent is what you have said. The truth does indeed establish itself by its own excellence and honor, and disputation, when it grows great, only occupies hearts away from what is more worthy than it. But there is no forsaking what the scholars have laid down as a way, for they have said: no crop whose ground has not first been made wholesome for it grows pure, and no knowledge for which the hearts have not been purified by proof increases."

Abu Malik said, "Then recite whatever proofs you wish. For the speech of wisdom is like the jewel: if you turn it about, its light glints; and if you set it aside, its worth is not hidden."

Salih said to him, "You have spoken truly, yet that is so only when there is no counterfeit of coated glass set beside the jewel; then the jewel is under no concealment. But when claims grow many and the tyrants of the world contend over religion until they have clothed it in what is not itself and coated it with a likeness before the seekers, then the seeker is obliged to put the jewel to the test until its worth is made plain and the counterfeit and its people are exposed."

Abu Malik said, "You have dealt justly and discharged the duty of the truth. As for this people's counterfeiting, we have come to know it and its defects, so we rejected it and held fast to this view by which we have come to know you, and we said it is the sound jewel of speech. We stood by it, and you agreed with us upon it, and we all clung together to its rope. If you have found a meaning more precious than it as a jewel, then by my life, jewels do surpass one another. So show me the worth of your jewel by a test through which its worth is known. As for our jewel, we have known it and you have known it, so say of it whatever appears to you."

Salih said to him, "As for your jewel, it was with us noble, of the rank of the sound jewel, until we presented it to the jewelers and the people of the mines, and they judged it counterfeit for us and would not accept it from us as a jewel, and said, this is coated glass. They laid its defects bare for us, and it fell from the bounds of the jewel. For the jewel is raised only by its preciousness, and by the discernment of the people of insight into its worth, and by their vying over it, so that its price climbs to every height. And once the jewelers have cast it down, who else can know it, or who will accept it after that but the people of delusion, who neither sell nor buy? So too is knowledge: it is not knowledge until it is presented to those who know the knowledge of heaven. If they accept it, it is sound knowledge; and if they do not accept it, it is idle prattle, and idle prattle has no place in wisdom and is not accepted by the wise."

Abu Malik said, "And who are these that know the knowledge of heaven after the prophets and the messengers? For we know of no one of them in this age of ours, and the people have had no acquaintance with such since revelation by the scriptures was cut off; and the nations follow nothing now but transmitted reports. So what do you say of this?"

Abu Malik asked, "And who are these that know the science of heaven after the prophets and the messengers? For we know of none of them in our time, nor have the people had any covenant with such men since revelation was cut off by the books, and the nations follow nothing now but transmitted reports. So what do you say of this?"

Salih answered, "Any religion that is established only upon narration is not called a religion, for religion is what is valued by God in its own right and admits no difference within it, whereas the narrations, as you well know, are full of disagreement, each denying the other. And the religion of God is dearer than that."

"Yet when they differ in the narration," said Abu Malik, "they refer back to the books. Whatever agrees with the books they accept, and whatever contradicts them they reject as false."

"But what is found in the books only in general terms," Salih replied, "who then will make it specific for them? And what is found there already specified, who will connect it back for them? Who among them is trusted concerning what God has made lawful and what He has forbidden? I heard you myself on the day of the quarrel, when a distressed man came to you and said, 'O Abu Malik, I swore an oath of divorce and then broke it.' You said to him, 'Some hold that she is now cut off from you and not lawful to you until she marries another husband, and some hold that she is still your wife and is not made forbidden to you by a single pronouncement of divorce.' The man asked you, 'Then which of the two shall I act upon?' and you told him, 'Act upon whichever of the two you wish.' So he rose and left you not knowing whether the home he returns to is lawful or forbidden. Is this how judgment is rendered in the religion of God? Is this how His lawful and His forbidden are determined?"

Abu Malik said, "By my life, the Sharia of this age has been ruined, corrupted, and left idle, and we hold now to nothing but the name of religion. So what is the state of religion with you?"

"And what is religion, O Abu Malik?" Salih asked.

"Command and prohibition," he said, "the lawful and the forbidden, the Sunnah and the obligations."

"And in your view," said Salih, "is Sharia anything other than this?"

"I know of nothing beyond what I have described to you."

"Then you have gathered religion and Sharia onto a single ground?"

"Yes."

"Then religion has been left idle by the suspension of the Sharia," said Salih, "and corrupted by its corruption. What is left with you?"

At this Abu Malik fell silent for a time, his head bowed, turning over in his mind the matter of his religion. Then he raised his head and said, "I do not see that anything is left with me. So what religion do you hold?"

Salih said, "Religion, with me, is that whose veil is made firm against the corruptors and whose doors are opened wide to the seekers, so that the one who brings it is ennobled by his precedence and the one who seeks it attains the good; through its excellence works are honored, and through its justice the sacred laws flourish. It is a cause joining heaven to earth, connected and unbroken, the loop of those who take hold and the rope of those who cling fast, the ark of tranquility, the ship of salvation, and the light of life. Its content is generous, its sciences are safeguarded, its jewels are marked with a sign, and their worth is hidden from no one who looks upon them."

"If God has a religion by which men are to be judged one day," said Abu Malik, "with which He is pleased, and by which He rewards and punishes, then it must be in this sense you describe. And now the excellence of the attribute has become plain, so how then is the One who is thus described?"

"The good comes only from what is good, O Abu Malik," Salih said. "Set your light in the lamp-niche of your mind and grasp what is intended for you. The claims of destiny have pulled you in their contest, the robes of vanity have dazzled you, and you have taken upon yourself a burden that no one else can bear for you, together with the affairs of those who follow you in good faith, who have listened to your speech, accepted your guidance, and trusted your judgment. It is your duty to guard yourself and them from a fire you cannot escape passing near, so seek out the road to deliverance from it. You are the most favored of them in the grace of God and the most bound to give thanks for it, for I have known you to recognize the good and to hope for its reward. As for what you asked, the confirming of that to which I have called you, it is my obligation to establish it for you from whatever aspect you wish."

"All that you have said so far is sound and firmly true," said Abu Malik. "As for the narrations of the common people, religion is not set right by them, nor are they fit for it. There remains for us only to know that to which you call us."

"I call you," said Salih, "to the justice of God and His oneness."

"How excellent is that to which you call," Abu Malik replied. "Indeed, this is the very doctrine we profess, and we know its excellence above all other schools of thought."

"That is not where your thought has gone, O Abu Malik," said Salih. "The doctrine you mean is a description of justice without knowledge of the One so described, and a use of the names of God without knowledge of His oneness, and this I refuse. What I call you to is the knowledge of God's justice and the true landmarks of His unity. If you wish, I will hold you back from the corruption of that saying however you like."

"If the doctrine of the people of justice is corrupt and their profession of oneness is denial," said Abu Malik, "then there is no firm truth left upon the earth, unless the meaning lies outside all the doctrines altogether. So show us the corruption of justice and of oneness, and from where it comes."

"Justice and oneness are among the noblest of all doctrines," said Salih. "Oneness is too great to be corrupted, for there is nothing set against oneness, and justice cannot be corrupted, for it is the judgment that stands between God and His creation. But the doctrines are all spoiled by the way men have corrupted the saying of justice and the rest, for it becomes a mere claim by which no statement is proven, and they do not encompass the knowledge of what they say until they return to the command of God. So take up whichever of the two you wish and defend it, until I strip it away from you by the argument of truth."

"I will not defend a saying I do not hold," said Abu Malik, "nor one whose author never asked this of me, who never appointed me his agent, nor am I a guardian over the masters of these doctrines. I will defend before you only my own saying. I say that God is One, nothing is like Him, just in His judgment, who does not burden His servants with what they have no power to carry. He commanded them and forbade them, yet He did not command by compulsion nor forbid by force; rather His command is a giving of choice and His prohibition is a warning, so the obedient did not obey under coercion, and the disobedient did not disobey as one overpowered. This is my meaning and what I profess."

"Did I not tell you," said Salih, "that this doctrine is among the noblest? But these are names and attributes that profit nothing unless their meaning is present behind them. It is like your saying, 'fire is hot.' You have spoken truly, yet the truth does not profit you, for your saying 'fire is hot' is only the name of an attribute, and to utter it profits nothing if no food is cooked by it and no darkness is lit by it; those who speak it remain in the dark without the meaning of fire being present. Were names and attributes to stand in things in the place of meanings, then the mere mention of fire would burn your tongue, and the mention of food would leave you full."

"As for what you say of these things, you have spoken truly," said Abu Malik, "for their names and attributes profit nothing unless their meaning is present. So show me that my words about justice and oneness are names and attributes that require the knowledge of a named and described reality."

"You say that God is One," said Salih, "and 'One' is four letters. We see two letters joined and two letters separate. So which of them is 'one,' or are they all together 'one'? And you say 'Allah,' and that too is written in letters. Is the alif alone the name, or are all the letters together 'Allah,' or are they signs pointing to God, or do you say something other than this?"

Abu Malik was struck silent for a time. Then he said, "I find no way out of this except that they are signs pointing to God."

"Which of them points to God?" Salih asked. "And by what do they point to Him, by their being apart or by their being joined? Or singly and together, as when you say 'Allah,' where the alif stands alone and the three letters are connected. Which then indicates God, this one standing alone, or these joined together?"

"I do not know what to say to you about this," said Abu Malik. "Take us on to some other door."

"Then the door to the knowledge of oneness has been shut in your face, O Abu Malik," said Salih. "Whoever taught you it in this fashion has closed it against you. Is it not so?"

"Yes," he said.

"Then I ask you about your knowledge of God," said Salih. "From where does it come, and from whom did you take it? Was it from an attribute that led you to One described?"

"No."

"Was it from a name that led you to One named?"

"Yes."

"And what is the name that led you?"

"Allah."

"Is 'Allah' a name," asked Salih, "or the One named?"

"The One named."

"Then what is the name?"

"Allah."

"So 'Allah' is a name for Allah?"

"Yes."

"Then what is the difference between the name and the One named?" asked Salih. "And which of the two do you worship, or both?"

Abu Malik was perplexed for a time, turning over his answer in his mind. Then he said, "I find no way through this except that I have seen that every craft has a maker, and so I judged that this vast creation has a Creator who resembles nothing of what He has made, just as craftsmen do not resemble the things they fashion."

"O Abu Malik," said Salih, "you have strayed from the first meaning. The narrowness you met in the meaning of oneness has driven you to seek Him by analogy and to set up likenesses for Him, and God is dearer than that. For everything made is made from some prior thing, whereas God did not create things from anything that existed before them. As for these craftsmen, you know them because you have watched some of them with your own eyes and seen how they work; so you passed judgment upon a making whose maker was hidden from you, on the strength of an action resembling it that you witnessed among the craftsmen you have seen and known, reasoning by comparison. But when God created, He did not make you a witness to the creation of what He fashioned, nor did He have any likeness that fashions as He fashions. Yet you have testified from a present likeness to an absent one. I never knew you, O Abu Malik, to be content with analogy in your religion, so how can you be content to seek the knowledge of your Lord by measurement? Your very words, 'nothing is like Him,' prove, out of your own mouth, that He has a likeness and that nothing is like that likeness. Had you meant true oneness, you would have said, 'He is not like anything,' and you would not have said, 'nothing is like Him.' Glory be to the One whom no reach of aspiring thought can grasp, and whom no soaring of the pointing mind can attain by perception, exalted far above the description of those who are charged with duty. O Abu Malik, had you turned this search upon one of His servants, or a king from among His angels, you would be left unable to comprehend what is said of that one and how he was made, so how then of his Creator? The lights of the people of the earth fall short of perceiving the light of the sun, and the sun is but one of the lights of this world. How then shall one who falls short before a creature of His perceive the Creator Himself? Say now what seems right to you."

Abu Malik cast his eyes to the ground, bewildered, finding no path to speech.

"O Abu Malik," said Salih, "raise your head and say whatever comes to you."

"Speech is gone, and there is nothing to say," said Abu Malik. "Minds are confounded before the answer, the imaginings are struck dumb before thought, and the faculty is too weak; by it one can reach nothing but the seeking and the asking."

"O Abu Malik," said Salih, "the One who is of such greatness, do the eyes perceive Him?"

"No, He is higher than that."

"Is speech heard from Him?"

"There is no speech except from a speaker," said Abu Malik, "and the speaker is described by speech, and God is too great for such attributes."

"This Vast One of greatness, this Exalted One of power," said Salih, "would He be unjust in His creation, charging them with what they cannot bear and commanding them with what they cannot grasp, and then punishing them if they fail to do it?"

"Whoever charges the obedient servant with what he has no power to bear," said Abu Malik, "has laid an act of disobedience upon him, and to punish him for it would be a wrong, and God is dearer than that."

"O Abu Malik," said Salih, "the One who is thus removed from injustice and thus exalted above wrongdoing, would He be content that ignorance should be a familiar companion to His servants?"

"Ignorance is a sure denial of thanksgiving and a persistence in unbelief," said Abu Malik, "and God does not love the unbelievers."

"Were they then created as knowers," asked Salih, "already aware of what is wanted of them?"

"No," he said. "He created them ignorant, knowing nothing at all."

"Then from where do they come by the knowledge of what He wants of them," asked Salih, "so that it is made binding upon them to please Him, and so that they may be guarded from His wrath?"

They learned this from God, said Abu Malik.

Salih answered: If He is higher than sight can reach, wider than perception can grasp, and greater than the limits of speech, then how does the one whom He commands and forbids come to know what is wanted of him, so as to obey Him rather than disobey, and thank Him rather than disbelieve, unless you accept that there are causes for this knowledge set between Him and His creation? Are there those who deliver His justice and bear witness to His witnessing, for them and against them, men whom He has chosen by His knowledge and selected out of all His creation, whom He has made messengers to them and proofs against them, so that they stand as the just ones between Him and His servants, conveying His command and His prohibition? Obedience to them is then obedience to God and the winning of His pleasure, and disobedience to them is His wrath and His punishment. Otherwise, how could the command come from God to His creation at all?

This, for me, said Abu Malik, is among the foundations of justice. Once creation stands in need of God's justice to teach them what He wants of them, and He teaches them through the hand of the righteous one He has appointed for that, then each shall thank his benefactor, be rewarded as God wills, be reproached, and be punished if God wills.

Salih said to him: Is it permissible to rule with justice in any way other than this?

It is permissible for none but God, he said, and God is the wisest of judges.

Would it be permissible for Him, Salih went on, to entrust the command to some of His creatures, setting them up in justice against their own wrongdoing, and then to lay the very same charge upon others, yet not guide these others to it by a just guide as He guided the first?

The judgment can only be one, said Abu Malik, because justice is one. Were the ruling split into two meanings, one of the two could not escape injustice.

Salih said to him: Has it not been established for you, then, that the judgment of God is one, with no difference in it, and that His way is one, unchanged in His messengers? Were it not for the unbroken succession of the messengers and the firm standing of command and prohibition, the truth would have no growth and creation no meaning.

Yes, he said, this is the truth.

Is not their succession from God to His creation, said Salih, so that He may make obedience binding upon them and set them as an authority between Himself and His creatures, to bear witness for whoever obeys them, and so that their obedience is accepted, and to bear witness against whoever disobeys them, and so that such a one is turned away?

The judgment could be nothing else, said Abu Malik, for otherwise He would have given the lie to His messengers and broken His promise, and God is too generous for that. The affair of the messengers has made plain that they are of His justice, and that creation worships Him by obeying them. But tell me of the ranks of these just ones with God. Are they equal, or have some a merit over others?

Salih said to him: How could they stand level in rank when they are bound to obey one another? Were they equal in rank, none of them would obey another. Rather, by His justice He has preferred some above others, so that obedience passes from the lesser to the greater, and from the greater to one greater still, until obedience and worship reach the utmost of what God wills.

Then the one who obeys the greater among them and the greater one himself, asked Abu Malik, are they equal in reward?

Yes, he said. This belongs to the justice of God, which grants every obedient one his reward as a due, and no one is questioned as to the limit of the greater or the lesser.

Is any one of the excellent ones, asked Abu Malik, matched by his opposite?

No, he said, for they come from the Creator bearing knowledge of the unseen in the Books He has revealed to them, and no one can bring the like of these except from God.

Then can anyone match the lesser among them, he asked, if no book is sent down to him carrying revealed knowledge of the unseen?

No one can lay claim to that station, he said.

And how is that, he asked, when they did not distinguish themselves from creation by the unseen, as the excellent one distinguished himself?

Salih said to him: If the excellent one distinguished himself from others by knowledge of the unseen in the revelation, then the lesser likewise distinguished himself from others by knowledge of the unseen in the interpretation. Both the revelation and its interpretation are from God, and what is with God is attained only through inspiration.

And the lesser is also inspired? asked Abu Malik.

Yes, he said, and for that reason obedience to him became obedience to God.

If you set him level with the excellent one in obedience and in inspiration, asked Abu Malik, why do you call him the lesser?

By his obedience to the excellent one, said Salih, and his need of him.

And what need has he of a creature, asked Abu Malik, when the inspiration comes to him from the Creator?

Salih said to him: Though the inspiration comes to him, it comes bearing the interpretation of the reports and the Books and the rest of what the excellent ones left behind, as it was revealed to Lot at the hand of Abraham, so that Abraham's excellence was made plain, and as it was revealed to Ishmael and to Isaac, so that the goods, the blessings, and the merit were set down to Abraham. The inspiration from God is joined to His just ones in His land, each according to his rank. He does not, in His justice, will to cut off His command and His prohibition and His worship from His servants after He has favored them with it, so that worship would be severed and ignorance made lawful.

The bearer of the revelation, said Abu Malik, has shown his truthfulness by the knowledge of the unseen in his book. By what does the bearer of the interpretation show that he too holds knowledge of the unseen, so that the revelation bears witness for him and makes obedience to him binding as it does for the bearer of the revelation?

Salih said to him: In his interpretation he brings knowledge from heaven and a declaration from the highest assembly, and the Book bears witness to it.

Then I bear witness, said Abu Malik, to what the Books bear witness to: that every interpretation the revelation testifies to is a revelation from God. Otherwise all the Books and the messengers would have to be given the lie, since their meaning is one and the same. And he added: If some of them give the lie to others, all must be reckoned liars; and if some confirm others, all must be confirmed.

Such is justice, Abu Malik said, and by it the truthfulness of those who hold to it is known, and their merit. Obedience is binding to those confirmed among the messengers who are His justice within His creation, whom He charged to convey His message. Yet if obedience is binding through prophethood, revelation, and interpretation, then what of those who sit in the seats of the prophets, resembling them though they are not of them? They have all agreed that after their prophet there is no prophet and no warner in their age, that they themselves are the ones who uphold the command of God and His religion, and that what they say leads to God's reward or His punishment. You say otherwise. So how have the scholars of this community failed to look into this matter?

Salih said to him: They have not wholly left it, but they have not looked into it with a sound looking, and no one has spoken to them of it so that they might incline their minds toward it, for it has been made to soak into their hearts that they are the worthiest of God's command and His religion, and that there is no prophet after their prophet, just as the seducers of the nations before them said. So every nation has claimed that there is no prophet after its own prophet.

What was the beginning of that, asked Abu Malik, and what did they intend by it?

Salih said to him: Its beginning was three in every age: a rebellious devil, a stubborn tyrant, and a wrangling jurist. By this saying they sought to cut the marks of the prophets off from the special gifts of prophethood, and to make it a possession passed among themselves. They could not do so until they had steeped the hearts of the nations in the saying, put to every nation, that God sent none better than your prophet, that there is no prophet after him and no warner. So they whispered to them from where they did not perceive, and turned them aside from where they did not know. Each nation clung to its own prophet by giving the lie to whoever came after him, supposing that this drew them nearer to their prophet. Each nation claimed that every prophet after its own was a liar. Thus the mention of prophethood and the marks of the prophets ceased among all the nations by that saying, and they did not seek what they had lost, nor would they have believed had they sought. So the seducing devils gained power over the seduced and seduced them, and the tyrants gained the power to kill the prophets and killed them, and doubt crept in from the evil jurists, who dressed it up finely, and were destroyed and destroyed others. All of this was so that the hearts of creation should not incline to the prophets when they appeared, nor seek them when they were lost. Despair of the prophets grew upon them, until the prophets called to the truth openly and were given the lie, and were slain among them without stirring their anger.

Here is the nation of the Magi, clinging to its tyranny, worshipping its idols, keeping to its fires, saying, by their own claim, that God will send no messenger after their messenger. Here is the nation of the Torah, clinging to its scripture, bent over its calf, standing by the bounds of its prophets, humble before its Judge, saying, by their claim, that God will raise no messenger after Moses. Here is the nation of the Gospel, speaking by its likening, claiming that its prophet is its God, drawing near by its crosses, exalting its rabbis, saying that God will send no messenger after Jesus. And here is your community, walking the paths of those before it, seduced by what seduced them. It inherited the Book from other than its rightful people, walked with the religion outside its true way, followed its leaders in error, obeyed its masters in straying, and its jurists bowed before the tyrants, so that they set up for the people a religion without the established practice, performing the prayer at other than its time and paying the alms into other than its due. They are patient with these masters, persistent in their need of them, certain of their independence from all others, saying that God will send no messenger and no warner after their Messenger. So you and your like have followed the saying of the great multitude, dazzled by the abundance of consensus and the force of the sultans, and you accepted it from them, and sweetened it with the fairness of your schools, and adorned it with the fineness of your words, and by it you set up an argument of justice where no argument had been raised against you. So you followed those above you among the tyrants for the awe of them, and those below you followed you out of desire, all supposing it to be the truth.

Now if this community must be believed in what it has decreed against God, that His proofs are cut off from His creation by a single strand of consensus, then the earlier nations have the better claim to be believed, by three qualities which oblige an argument for them against you, and in which they are more entitled to truth than you.

And what are those qualities, asked Abu Malik, in which they have merit over us, so that they must be believed if their word is true, or given the lie if their word is crooked?

Salih said to him: Their word is like your word, and your word is of the same kind as theirs. Your hearts resemble one another, and theirs is the merit over you in the argument. As for the first quality, they say they are earlier to the religion of God than you, so theirs is the merit of those who came first. The second is that the consensus, both theirs and yours, confirms what is with them, so theirs is the merit of the truthful. The third is your own testimony that their prophets are truthful and are the messengers of God, while none of them has borne witness for your prophet, neither to prophethood nor to truthfulness. So theirs is the merit over you by your agreement with them and their standing apart from you, and they say that the knowledge you hold is copied from their knowledge, and that theirs is the root of yours. This is their argument against you, clear in word and plain in justice. If the ruling of creatures is binding upon the Creator, then the ruling of these is the more binding. But if the Creator is the ruler over His creation, abrogating what He wills and confirming what He wills, with none to overturn His ruling, then by my life the saying of them all falls away, and the Creator has the first claim over creation and over the command. Every day He is engaged in some affair, and His act is not to be denied, even were He to send a warner every day.

This community, said Abu Malik, has folded itself upon a far-reaching error.

Salih said to him: Most of those before them went astray also. They cast the friends of God out of their right and let the foolish overcome them in their affair, and they made lawful what God had forbidden, deliberately, so as not to resemble prophethood, lest people should long for the prophets and then not kill them. But they distracted them with kingship and largesse and led them to follow their whims. In this they were helped by men known outwardly for truthfulness and piety, who made a show of it before the people, until the very ones who had once commanded the community to good turned it from the good, and the ones who had forbidden it evil pointed it toward the evil. The people of faithfulness to the friends of God grew few, and truth and falsehood became alternating fortunes, like light and darkness in their difference. Were it not for the remnants of the friends of God and His just ones in the earth, and the inspiration that comes to them, the little of the truth would not prevail over the much of the falsehood. Otherwise, by what would God distinguish the corrupt from the good, except by His just ones? And how would the argument of God reach all creation, except through them?

There is no way, said Abu Malik, and no escape for the justice of God and His command, amid the multitude of adversaries, except by the just ones of God. By them the argument of God to His creation is made sound.

Salih said to him: If it is established that the company of the prophets of God are the just ones, is not each single one of them called a just one?

Yes, he said. He is a just one, as it is said, so-and-so bore witness, and he is a just one among the just.

Salih said to him: Then it has been established for you that the just one is the friend of God, and this has passed out of what you called merely a description, and become a thing described.

Yes, he said, this is sound as a saying.

Salih said to him: And one who does not reach the thing described, does the description profit him?

The description does not profit him, he said, except by reaching the thing described.

Salih pressed the point further. "Then whoever does not know the just one of Allah and His guardian, will his speech itself be just?"

At this the narrator tells us that Abu Malik smiled. "You have brought me out of my own chapter on justice and monotheism," he said, "and what has brought me out of it is nothing but the truth itself, argued in the clearest of proofs and spoken in the kindest of words. After this, what chapter is left for me to prove?"

"From here, O Abu Malik," Salih answered, "the camouflaged glass falls away from those who trade in the colors of jewels. They never recognized its worth; but once it is set before one who knows, the essentialists stand exposed, and those they deceived stand exposed with them."

Abu Malik said, "I have believed, and I have acted upon it. But how are we to be safe from the seducers of the nations, and how are we to know the causes by which Allah reaches His servants, or even one such cause among them, when we do not live in their time? In our age there are neither prophets nor messengers, as there were among the nations before us."

"Glory be to Allah, O Abu Malik," said Salih. "For a long stretch of your life you have kept affirming the justice of Allah. Yet the moment we press that justice upon you, in your own presence, where it becomes binding on you, you turn back. You begin to say that you would only be deceived about Allah, and you attribute injustice to Him."

"God forbid," Abu Malik protested. "I say no such thing. Whoever says it has transgressed and lied against Allah. Where have you found me committed to that?"

"From your own words," Salih replied. "You say the age of the prophets has passed, and that in our time there is neither prophet nor messenger. So tell me: when Allah sent those earlier ranks of messengers, was that sending a matter of His justice among His servants, or was it in vain?"

"May Allah forbid that it be anything but justice from Him," he said.

"Then what has shut us out of that justice?" Salih asked. "Are we not His servants as they were? They were created as we are created, and we are bound in His service as they were bound. Has the obligation been lifted from us after it was made binding upon them? Has His giving run dry in our time, so that we cling to Him only by what was granted to them? Has the Sunnah of Allah changed in our case after standing firm in those before us, so that we should now rule that God Himself has changed? Or is justice a duty owed to us and His judgment binding upon us, exactly as it was His way with those before us, so that there is no altering the words of Allah and none to overturn His decree? And who deserves the name just except the guardians of Allah? Justice is not found among any Allah created except in the one He Himself chose, who became just by His command. That is why a judge is called just, for the sake of the One who appointed him, and a true word is called truthful because of the truthfulness of the one who utters it."

"It can only be as you say," Abu Malik admitted. "We are not permitted to say anything about Allah except the truth. Yet if we followed our own opinion in what we said, and judged by it in the absence of the rightly guided among us, we would have abandoned His judgment. This is part of Allah's ruling and His Sunnah with us, just as it was with those before us during the interregna between one messenger and the next. There was the period between the religion of Abraham and the time of Moses and the Torah, and the period between the Torah and the time of Jesus and the Gospel. We have stood upon that same footing, working through the interregnum, holding fast to the traditions and the Sunnah."

"The religion of Allah is far too precious to Him for that," Salih answered. "Was there truly any interregnum after the religion of Abraham, when Allah sent after him Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Jonah, and Shu'ayb, leading on to Moses? He inspired them to do good, to call to the religion of Abraham, to establish prayer according to its Sunnah and to give zakat according to its obligation, until at last the ruling of the Torah was sent down to Moses. And what interregnum stood between Moses and the Gospel, when after Moses Allah sent Joshua son of Nun, and Elias, Talut, David, Solomon, Zechariah, and John, leading on to Jesus? They upheld the ruling of the Torah, holding to its religion, calling others to it, reciting its Psalms, until the ruling of the Gospel came down to Jesus. So it was with those who passed before them and those who came after, and with the many generations between them whose full number none but Allah knows. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs; for each He set forth parables, and each of them called to the truth. Where, then, is this interregnum, and when did it ever occur? Allah said only, 'After a succession of messengers,' and by that He affirmed the messengers themselves. What came after was the rising of the call once fear had settled in, so that they prayed in secret and concealment. Why then should you imagine they were ever cut off from the earth? The earth is never emptied of the justice of Allah for the blink of an eye. He is either openly present, or hidden and veiled in fear."

"This is a statement that cannot be refuted," Abu Malik said, "a settled word in which there is no doubt. As for the standing of Allah's argument through justice and the succession of the messengers, that is now established and made plain. So show me the meaning of their absence in our own time. What is its cause?"

"What was the cause of Moses' own absence," Salih asked in turn, "when he went out of Pharaoh's city and passed on in fear, watching and waiting until he reached the land of Shu'ayb?"

"He fled from Pharaoh's people, hiding himself from them," Abu Malik said, "because he feared for his life once they had agreed to kill him."

"Have you heard in this law of yours," Salih continued, "of any among the just, the just ones of Allah and the sons of His prophets, who was killed while patiently bearing the burdens of the religion?"

"Yes," he said. "Many of them have been killed."

"Then which of the just ones of Allah do you expect to appear to you and to the people of your age? One who was killed, whom Allah sends back to you from death? Or one who fled from you, whom you would drive back to the very killing he escaped, though he has followed Allah's own way in His prophets and messengers, patient under fear and in concealment, until Allah judges between him and us, and He is the best of judges?"

"There is no blame," Abu Malik said, "upon one who follows the Sunnah of the righteous by fearing for his life and fleeing from his people. The blame lies upon the wicked nations and the helpers of the powers of darkness, for killing the prophets of Allah and His just ones, and for their unanimous agreement upon that crime."

"And what do you say of the one who kills the just one of Allah and His proof?" Salih asked. "Where is his end, and where the end of the one who helped him?"

"Both are in the Fire," Abu Malik answered, "and so too is anyone who raised a banner for them or so much as carried an inkpot in their service."

"What do your jurists say about this?"

"They call for mercy upon the slain," he said, "yet they see no disobedience in the one who killed him."

"And whoever obeys the killer of his own accord," Salih pressed, "is he not a partner with him in the blood of the slain?"

"Yes, by Allah," said Abu Malik. "He is his partner, and even more wicked than he."

"His partnership is plain enough through the help he gave," Salih said. "But how has he become worse than the killer himself?"

"Were it not for the narrations of the jurists," Abu Malik answered, "the rule of the tyrants could never have been established through the foolish."

"You have spoken the truth, O Abu Malik," said Salih. "And now I lay this upon you yourself. You have accepted their kingdom as your home, their land as your resting place, their soldiers as your neighbors, and their enforcers as your intimate companions. You serve as their just man, judging on their behalf by your learning and settling your own needs through their power. You have prayed for them when you had a request, and thanked them for what you took from them. You set the crown of your justice upon their tyranny, and they grew proud. You clothed their falsehood in the robe of the sacred law, and they went to further excess. You lit up their falsehood with the idleness of your truth. So the weak were led astray by your conduct, and every scholar like you, and they said, 'These are the jurists of the community; in them we have our example.' They went astray through you, and you went astray through the tyrants, and you supposed that the one held to account was someone other than yourself. You forgot the words of Allah Almighty, 'And do not incline toward those who have done wrong, lest the Fire touch you.' So you severed what Allah commanded to be joined, and you saw yourselves doing it. You spoke of the outward forms of justice while you were lost to its very path. You came upon His saying, 'Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you,' and you made these powers of darkness the ones in authority over you, even when they had disbelieved. And you forgot His saying, 'Fight those of the disbelievers who are near to you.' So you disobeyed the ones you were commanded to obey, and obeyed the ones you were commanded to fight, all the while imagining you were doing good. Your error ran on and your opinion carried you so far that at last you said Allah will send neither messenger nor warner in your age. Is it that the goodness of your deeds has freed you from any need of the guardians of Allah and their proof? Or that the foulness of your deeds has cut off your hope of Allah's accepting repentance? Or is it justice that you should pass judgment on God without justice, and be content on your Messenger's behalf with what none of you would accept for himself in his place? Would any one of you accept that his own son be killed, or that his house and his wealth be seized from him without right?"

"No one would accept that," Abu Malik said. "If a child of ours were killed, or a man among us driven from his property, he would rage, and his people would rage with him, and they would not rest until he was satisfied."

"Then Allah and His Messenger," said Salih, "have a greater right that you should be angry for their sake, when His vicegerent is killed and His guardian is driven out of the land."

"It is bitter to us indeed," Abu Malik replied, "that the guardian of Allah should be killed among us or driven from his land. But they have deliberately withdrawn themselves from us, and the tyrants have risen up over us in their place."

"Had you supported the friends of Allah, they would have appeared," Salih said. "Had you forsaken His enemies, they could never have prevailed. But your standing toward the guardians of Allah has fallen into three ranks. There is the assailant given power over them, who never tires of the hunt and is never satisfied even when he has struck. There is the one who looks on with malicious pleasure, biting his fingertips in spite, sparing no effort to incite others against them and never turning his gaze from them even in their heedlessness. And there is the one who hates what is done to them and holds in his heart a love for the guardians of Allah, yet forsakes them all the same, having no clear insight into faith and no trustworthiness in keeping a secret. For the sake of all this the guardians of Allah have withdrawn themselves from you by Taqiyya, out of caution before you and before this great mass of people who have taken their own desires as their god, fallen greedily upon the goods of this world, and joined together in the killing of the prophets. They were blind to the fault in what they did, so they never held themselves to account for the wrong they committed, and were never humbled into repentance for their deeds. Instead they turned the injustice of their own actions back upon Allah and ascribed it to Him, saying, 'Allah will send no messenger after these messengers, and no warner.' So they made lawful the killing of the guardians of Allah without right. They wronged by their deeds, they turned aside by their words, and they overstepped by their denial."

"Yes," said Abu Malik, "this is the very description of this nation of ours, and this is exactly what they say. So where is the salvation from it, and where is the escape from the punishment of Allah?"

"To Allah," Salih answered. "Seek refuge in Him, and He will grant you refuge. Repent to Him, and He will accept your repentance. Ask Him for the way of His guardians, and by your works He will deliver you from the deeds of those who did wrong. So it is with this mass of people as well: were they to repent to Allah, He would accept their repentance, and then what use would He have for punishing them? For Allah does not wrong them by the weight of an atom, even when they are unjust and lie, and He does not hasten their punishment, even when they hasten to disobey."

"Then I take refuge from the punishment of Allah," Abu Malik said, "by repenting to Him and by seeking the means to what pleases Him. Show your favor, then, to the one who seeks from you and asks to be guided. May your Lord reward you."

"If you do that," Salih said, "you will find Allah nearer to pardoning you than you are to your own repentance, for He loves those who turn to Him in repentance; and you will find me eager to guide you and watchful over you."

Then Salih's eyes filled with tears at the mention of repentance, and he broke off his speech. He gave them heart, and ordered them to go on to his father's house. He himself went in to his father, the scholar who had first sent him out, and consulted him about the affair of Abu Malik and his companions.

The scholar said, "You know your companions best. If you see good in them, guide them with your guidance. And when you warn them, do not let eagerness carry you into inflicting ta'zir upon them. Test them by leaving them to themselves for a while, without harshness, and instruct your elders to treat them well and act kindly toward them in due season. The one who truly desires good cannot stay hidden. Allah will not abandon what you leave behind you. He will open for you, from the light of His enabling grace, that by which the affairs of His creation are ordered, and from the gates of guidance that which will let you see the works of those rightly guided."

So Abu Malik and his companions remained in their trial, wavering, until their affair was settled and they came to know their own guidance, and the guardian of Allah turned toward them in their guidance with tenderness. They gave thanks to Allah for that, and returned to their people as warners. And through them Allah guided a company of His servants to His religion.

The author said: Whatever in this account took the shape of a story was no idle tale invented, but a confirmation of what Allah has commanded, for in it lies the steadfastness of the messengers, the signs of the guardians, and the manners of the seekers.

Praise be to Allah, first and last. May Allah bless His Messenger, our master Muhammad, sent forth from Him to His creation as a bearer of good news and a warner; and His guardian, the Imam of the God-fearing, the radiant leader of the bright-marked, the beloved of the Lord of the Worlds; and the Imams of the people of his house, upon whom Allah has bestowed His favor, from whom He has removed every impurity and whom He has purified completely. Allah is sufficient for us, and how excellent a Guardian; how excellent a Master and how excellent a Helper. There is no power and no strength except by Allah, the Most High, the Most Great.
