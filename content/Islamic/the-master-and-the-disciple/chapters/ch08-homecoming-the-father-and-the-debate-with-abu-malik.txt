# Homecoming, the Father, and the Debate with Abu Malik

His father said: "O my son, is this the reward of sons to their fathers? I have taught you only the best that a child can be taught by a father, and you have not denied your own self nor renounced your beliefs except from the time I grew old, when this strange man came. You drew him in upon me, and you crossed over with him in his affair without consulting your parents. If what I knew was true, then you have wronged me in my very self, because you concealed the truth from me. And if it is false, then you have disobeyed me in your own ruin, and whatever has reached you has thereby reached me."

The boy said: "O my father, I have known you to be nothing but merciful and compassionate toward me. You raised me as a mercy from you, and you nourished me, choosing me over family and wealth. My greatest need of you this day is that you do me the favor of one of two things."

The Shaykh said: "Name the two, what are they?"

The boy said: "Either you leave me to my courtesy and my good conduct toward you, for I am your son whom you raised as a child, and our affair rests with Allah; or you grant me leave to debate with you, and assure me of safety from your anger, and put your mind to use."

The Shaykh said: "As for your courtesy on the outside while hearts differ within, that is a reproach between me and you. How long can outward agreement stand when the inward differs? I do not want that. As for your debate and your candor, that is yours, so say what seems best to you."

The boy said: "Consider my standing with you for forty years upon nothing at all. Does it not come down to one of two things? Either you are a scholar, and you have withheld your knowledge from me, so do not blame me if you forbid me and I seek my own salvation through another. Or you are ignorant, and in that you are excused with me, but then you were the one more in need of this stranger than I was. Had I gone before you to him, it was because of your age and the lateness of your birth, for I have heard his words just as you have heard them, in what I have seen."

The Shaykh's heart was rent by the boy's words, and he knew there was no escape for him from either of the two arguments together, and his eyes overflowed with tears. He said: "O my son, the argument that has fallen upon me for your sake falls upon you for mine in like measure, if you keep your affair hidden from me. So relate it to me. If it is right, I will accept it from you, as an honor to myself; and if it is false, I will turn you back from it, as an honor to you and out of compassion for you."

So they debated for a time and disclosed things to each other at length, and their affair came to rest upon guidance and right direction: to uphold the religion of Allah, to guard it, to hold fast to it, to call others to it, and to help one another in it. Through the two of them Allah gave life to many of His creatures. The boy sent word to the scholar, giving him the glad news of his father's uprightness and asking him to come to them; and he came to them and was a cause of good for them both. Allah caused blessings to flow upon the people of that land through them and at their hands. The command of Allah was fulfilled, and the one who slandered was disappointed.

The boy's name was Salih, and his father's name was al-Bakhtari. The two of them held honor over their people, through favors they had extended and a high name won by the excellence of the manners they had acquired. News of them spread through the land, and of the religion they now held, until it reached a people who clung to their old creed. Al-Bakhtari had a standing bounty among them, and they feared it would be cut off from them by his leaving their religion. So they went out and gathered before a scholar of theirs called Abd al-Jabbar, Abu Malik, who was named among them, for his piety, the abundance of his forbearance, and his defense of his religion, "كعب الأحبار." He had prominence among them for his learning, an understanding of discourse, and a discernment of opinions. When they came in to him, they said: "O Abu Malik, do you not see what the boy has gone over to?"

He said: "Which boy?"

They said: "Salih ibn al-Bakhtari."

He said: "And what has he gone over to?"

They said: "We are telling you that a strange man came to our land, with fine speech and a doctrine he calls people to, and we do not know what it is. The boy inclined to him and went out with him without his father's leave, and was absent from him for a while. Then he came back speaking the strange man's words and calling to what he was calling to. We have heard that his father has answered him to his view and backed him in it. This has grieved us, both what they have done and our holding back from them, for three reasons. The first: a bounty that used to flow to us from his father, along with the brotherhood of religion you know of between us, so that we fear estrangement after intimacy and hate division after brotherhood. The second: if these people are upon the truth, then we have fallen short of following them in it and helping them upon it. The third: if they are upon an error into which they have strayed, then we have fallen short of driving them from it and of aiding the truth and its people. You are a head in our religion and a standard among our standards, and we have come to inform you and consult you, and we will not go beyond your opinion."

Abu Malik said: "Gently now. This word of yours has alarmed me. You know that al-Bakhtari and his son were among those we used to reckon among us as people of forbearance and piety, and that they have rights and old favors over us. You have mentioned about them what you have mentioned, and you are, with me, of those who do not stray from the truth to anything else. This is a report that has come upon a people, and its argument has been made plain to them, so they have followed it and are content with it. We cannot judge them to be in error and so wrong them; nor can we judge them to be upon guidance and so wrong ourselves by imitating them; nor can we say they were deceived and refute them without knowledge, for they are, in our eyes, nobler than that. But we need to look into this report with the eye of fairness, the light of justice, and the firmness of the mind. For it is said: whoever is deliberate in affairs, his authority remains secure, and whoever is hasty in his affair is not safe from the slip of his foot. A rent in all things has its fault remedied and its ruin replaced, like a rent in food, whose harm is easy and whose cure is near at hand. But a rent in religion is of great harm and grievous loss; its fault is not remedied nor is another put in its place, like a rent in the medicine itself, which does not release the one who takes it nor allow him to save himself. We have been taught in this that the best of stores are the treasures of sound minds and the acquiring of good manners, for their fruits are of praiseworthy outcome. And among their fruits is following the way of forbearance, and forbearance is the crown of the learned. By it, noble aspirations raise them to the heights of glory, and their merit is made plain in every place, along with the honor they attain of gainful things with ease of pursuit. We have laid up for ourselves some measure of that: a portion of forbearance we drew in, and something of manners we acquired in the time gone by, amid straitened circumstance and hardness of pursuit. We bore that patiently for the sake of its merit, and we seek its help against the calamities of affairs and the accidents of the ages. We cannot let it go to waste, and this is the hour of our need for it, and of putting it to use in this new matter that has come upon us among our brothers. There is no sense in leaving them and what they are upon without laying it bare with them and debating it with them, with the forbearance of the capable and the understanding of seekers, without doubting what is with us and without accusing the people of having turned from the truth in aversion, for they are, with us, nobler than that; or of having gone out into error, for error is beneath them. Falsehood has no eminence that plain truth should be abandoned for it. But reflect with me on the affair of these people, for it cannot fall outside three cases. Either the people are upon what we know of the religion, and this new thing among them is only an increase in manners, sayings, and poetry, and the speech of people is much. Or a people has come to them with an increase upon the religion they hold, and they have rejoiced in it, for knowledge is the lost property of every believer, and an increase in knowledge is better than a lack of it. Or a guidance other than what they had has come to them, from the prophethood of a prophet who was brought to them and who called them to it, and they followed it upon clear proof, and that is not to be denied of the command of Allah. The creation will not reach the utmost of Allah's decree among them and His will for them, however they strive; and Allah, every day, is upon some task."

They said: "O Abu Malik, we came to you with hearts full of rage against these people, ready to declare them unbelievers, to refute their opinion, and to hold their blood and their wealth lawful for ourselves. But we have found our hearts humbled by your words and by what you have undertaken of their argument and your defense of them."

Abu Malik said: "This argument of mine is not for them; rather my argument is for the truth, that I not betray it in its absence. I have upheld the truth's own argument when the one who would argue for it is away. Nor is it a defense of them; rather I have defended myself and you against the speech of falsehood and the flinging of stones into the unseen, at what we have not encompassed in knowledge. For then we would be like the tyrants of the nations before us, when their doctrines pleased them in their forbearance, and they rejoiced in the knowledge they had, until they supposed they had encompassed all that Allah had commanded and taught, and all that He willed of His creation. So they said: Allah will send no messengers after their messengers, and they did not suppose that beyond the knowledge they had reached lay any doctrine. So they passed judgment upon Allah, that His argument should be cut off from His creation and His successors in His earth. Then they made the knowledge of Allah among His servants, and that with which He sent His prophets and messengers, the measure of their own minds; whatever went beyond that was, to them, error. So they went astray by that opinion and led many astray. By their own judgment they made lawful what Allah had forbidden, and by their own opinion they killed the prophets of Allah. I take refuge for you with Allah, that you should follow the whims of a people who went astray before you. For people of intellect may be inclined by whim toward every meaning and deceived away from every path, except the path of religion, for it is the most forgiving in its pursuit, and its merit is not hidden."

They said: "O Abu Malik, we will not oppose your command, and your counsel is not lost on us. We would not obey you at the beginning of our affair and disobey you at its end. You have pointed to the opinion and hinted at the pursuit. If you see fit, take us to these people so that we may have their doctrine laid bare to us and look into it. If it is right guidance, we will follow it, and if it is misguidance, we will shun it."

Abu Malik said: "This is doubt on your part about what you hold, and one who doubts neither knows his own right nor the falsehood of another."

They said: "Then we hold firm to our truth and give the lie to whatever else there may be, be it what it may."

Abu Malik said: "This is not how meaning is sought, nor is guidance found through giving the lie."

They said: "How then is the seeking done, if we do not give the lie to falsehood and affirm the truth?"

Abu Malik said: "If you already know the truth and its acceptance, and know falsehood and its rejection, then you are not among the number of seekers; you are among the number of those learned in prophethood and guidance, who rule by revelation over the people of the world."

They said: "Then we are content with the truth we hold and do not doubt it, and we excuse ourselves from the burden of seeking."

Abu Malik said: "Were you not willing that they should have the merit over you in one respect, that you now give them the merit over you in many? If you would not consent to stand in want in the stations of the seekers, will you then stand in ignorance in the stations of those who sit back?"

They said: "How has this come to be among us, and in what have they been given merit over us?"

Abu Malik said: "Their merit over you is in knowing what you know, and an increase in what you do not know. This is a matter that has come upon a people. If you do not seek them out for it, they will seek you out to it; and if you do not listen to it, they will strive against you upon it, and so they will attain merit over you by being first, by seeking, and by striving. As for sitting back from the seeking, that is ignorance entire, a squandering and a fault: upon the learned until he teaches, and upon the ignorant until he learns."

They said: "O Abu Malik, you have taken us by the argument from every side, and every word of yours is right. So open for us a door of your opinion by which we may act."

Abu Malik said: "Conceit in one's opinion is blindness, and neglect of the seeking is error, and there is no fault upon seekers in the seeking. But the seeker needs to know the doors of the seeking, so that he seeks the truth with knowledge of how to seek. Whoever seeks the truth without knowing how to seek is the quicker to give it the lie, for falsehood shows its first signs in ostentation and agreement with received opinions, while the truth shows its first signs in trial and in opposition to whims. None forsakes the following of whim and bears patiently the onset of trial except one of sound heart."

They said: "Here a door has opened for us, wherein you point to the knowledge of seeking. Make plain to us what that knowledge is, and what the sound heart is."

Abu Malik said: "As for the knowledge of seeking, it is that you know yourselves to be in need. The one in need seeks his need with the humility of want before the thing sought. As for the sound heart, it is a heart that does not persist in giving the lie to what comes upon it, be it what it may, until it is the truth itself that makes itself plain and shows its merit, and falsehood that lays bare its own defect. Then the sound heart distinguishes the falsehood by its intellect and accepts the truth by acceptance."

They said: "We have been taught that whoever is in this station, who does not affirm the truth nor refute the falsehood, is ignorant in his conduct, since he does not know the truth so as to follow it, nor falsehood so as to shun it."

Abu Malik said: "You have spoken truly in what you have related. But has he not, in your view, passed out of the rank of the liars and come into the rank of the ignorant?"

They said: "Yes, in our view he is ignorant, not one who gives the lie."

Abu Malik said: "The ignorant is the more to be hoped from for good and its seeking. For either he seeks knowledge for himself, or the learned seek him out, since ignorance is the ground of the created, and there is no fault upon one who seeks knowledge out of his ignorance. But giving the lie is the worst of wares; the one who gives the lie attains no guidance by it, and is never made safe in what has been prescribed for him."

They said: "You have spoken truly. You have made known to us the fault of giving the lie, so that we will not give the lie to anything ever until we know it. And you have made known to us the door of seeking, through the humility of want before the thing sought. Is this humility and lowering of oneself before the thing sought after its right is made plain, or before the plain proof?"

Abu Malik said: "No, only before the plain proof, so that by humility you may attain the plain proof."

They said: "Then if we humble ourselves before the thing sought until what it holds is made plain, and its plain proof turns out to be error, what is the standing of that humility? Has it not been wasted?"

Abu Malik said: "No. Rather the humility returns to Allah, and the lowering of oneself is to His command, and its reward is yours even when the one humbled before did not deserve it, because the seeker has fulfilled the duty owed to the seeking, though the truth's due was not fulfilled for him. His likeness in that is the likeness of the petitioner who stands before some of the kings seeking their bounty, and in his seeking nothing suffices him but humility."

Abu Malik said to them: "What you have related, you have believed. But has not this one passed beyond the limit of the liars, and become two ignorant men gathered in one?"

They said: "Yes. With us he is ignorant, not a liar."

Abu Malik said: "The ignorant man hopes for the good and seeks it. He seeks knowledge for himself; and if he does not, the learned seek it out for him, because ignorance is the root condition of creation, and there is no shame in it for one who seeks knowledge on account of his ignorance. But lying is the worst of wares. The liar is not guided by his lying, and he is never believed in what is recorded against him."

They said: "You have made us certain of it. We have come to know the defect of lying, and so we will never lie about anything until we know it for what it is. And you have opened to us the door of the request, which is reverence toward what is sought. Is this reverence and humility for the thing sought to be shown after it has made its right plain, or before the statement is given?"

Abu Malik said: "Rather before the statement, so that by reverence you may reach the statement."

They said: "But if we revere the one sought until what he holds becomes clear, and then his statement turns out to be misleading, what standing has our reverence then? Has it not been wasted?"

Abu Malik said: "No. Rather it was reverence to God and lowliness before His command, and you shall have its reward, even if the one before whom you humbled yourself did not deserve it. For the seeker has discharged the duty of the request, even if he has not discharged the duty of the truth. His likeness in this is the petitioner who stands before some king to ask for his gift, and can ask only with reverence, flattery, and insistent complaint. If the king gives him something of what he asked, the king has fulfilled the duty owed to grace, and has the reward of what he gave. And if he gives nothing, the petitioner has still discharged the duty of asking by the gentleness of his plea, while niggardliness binds the withholder to his refusal. So too the seeker of knowledge: he must begin with reverence, humility, and patience under trial until he fulfills the duty of the request; and then time will bring him afterward whatever it brings."

They said: "This is the clear, manifest truth. Will you permit us, then, to go out to the people upon what you have counseled us, and not to overstep what you have commanded?"

Abu Malik said: "I would not counsel you to a thing and then be sparing of myself in it. Were it not for the fear of falling into fault by turning you back from the path of Allah, I would have counseled you to hold back while I went on ahead of you to the good, as an honor to myself and a preferring of my own soul above every soul. But we go together, and I comfort you with my own self, as an honor to you and in thanks for the good in which you have made me a partner."

They said: "It is as though you were expecting this matter before today."

He said: "This very thing, no. But I have never ceased expecting relief from Allah on every side, hoping for good from every road, inclining my ear toward every speaker, seeking increase from his words, whether he be in the right or in the wrong."

They said: "Increase from the words of the truthful, that we understand. But how do you hope for it from the words of the false?"

Abu Malik said: "Increase of truth upon truth is light; and to know falsehood for what it is in the false one is increase in certainty. And the more certainty grows, the greater the light of the heart grows from it. None are saved with Allah but the people of certainty. I am rising now to go to the people. If you are resolved to rise with me, then look to yourselves: whoever knows a sin in himself, let him repent to Allah; whoever bears a debt, let him pay it back to its owner. Wash yourselves clean of defilement, and put on for the request the purest garments of resolve, and bring to the thing sought the intention of one in need, for this is what I myself am doing. For our going out is a going out to Allah: if He knows truthfulness in us, He will grant us to attain His truth; and if He knows in us a mockery of the faith, He will turn us from His signs by the least humiliation, keeping His friend pure of the mockers."

They said: "Yes. We will do what you have commanded, within three days."

He said: "No."

They said: "Within two days, then?"

He said: "No, only within this our own day. If you will do it, well; and if not, then I am going on."

They said: "Then we repent to Allah from every sin, and we clear away every debt from us, and we do what you have commanded. There is no power except in Allah لا قوة إلا بالله."

So they did this from that same day, and they all went out together until they came to the gate of al-Bakhtari. When they had greeted him and sat down, Abu Malik said to al-Bakhtari: "O Abu Salih, how is your good son Salih, and where is he?"

Al-Bakhtari said: "Today Salih is my father, and I am the son of Salih; and Salih is with his Lord."

Abu Malik, supposing that Salih had died, because his father had said of him that he was with his Lord, said: "إنا لله وإنا إليه راجعون, To Allah we belong and to Him we return. Has Salih died?"

Al-Bakhtari said: "Salih has not died, and will never die. He abides as long as time abides."

Abu Malik said: "These are three matters whose meanings have been overturned. The child has become the father; the Lord is made present in the earth, and the Lord has a servant to whom one comes by way of Him; and the one created for perishing has become everlasting. Alas for seekers, from tangled speech. Behind this there is surely a wonder."

Al-Bakhtari said: "O Abu Malik, come to your need, and be sure in your asking, for you are at the very beginning of the ordeal."

Abu Malik said: "You have spoken truly. The beginning of the truth is an ordeal. And my need is a meeting with the righteous servant."

Al-Bakhtari said: "There is no way to that. Yet perhaps you mean my son Salih?"

He said: "Yes."

He said: "That is by his leave."

So the old man rose in haste and came in upon his son Salih, and told him the tidings of Abu Malik and his companions, rejoicing at it, for he knew the excellence of their minds. And Salih said: "O Allah, open the hearing of the hearts of Your servants, and guide them by Your mercy to the signs of what is sought, and expand our breasts with the light of Your veiled light." Then he wiped his hand over his face, cleared the assembly for them, and gave word to his father to bring them in.

When they entered upon him, greeted him, and sat down, Abu Malik and those below him taking their places in order, Salih drew Abu Malik near and seated him close to himself. The first thing Salih said was: "O Abu Malik, you have favored us with your visit and outstripped us to the good by your generosity; yet you had the greater right that we should visit you, to keep your affection and to pay the duty long owed to you."

Abu Malik said: "And when were you ever wanting in excellence, O Abu al-Khair? You were forbearing among us when we were small, and honored us when we were grown; you became among us a caller and a warner to the good. Your root is sound, your branch is high, your work is pure. So joy be to you."

Salih said to him: "Have you come to me accusing, O Ka'b al-Ahbar, or as one merely following others? Where is that perfect mind and that excellent forbearance I have always known in you?"

Abu Malik said to him: "Your worth is too high for accusation, and religion is too high for blind following."

Salih said to him: "Then how do you name me a warner, when the warner is a prophet, and the prophet is a proof from Allah between Him and His creation, for them and against them? How can he be a proof for you when he is no proof against you?"

Abu Malik said: "You have spoken truly. This is the due. We do not proceed in this on the ease of speech, but we have begun with you by that which is fairer. And I saw that truthfulness is fitter for one who has set himself up as a veil for religion and a chief for the truth, and has opened its doors to seekers. We have come to know the excellence of asking, so we sought you out and set our hope on you as the one sought. Come, then, to our need."

Salih said: "O Abu Malik, is not the one whose excellence you have come to know also one whose right has become owing upon you?"

He said: "Yes, it is owing."

He said: "And when you came to know the excellence of asking, did you come to know its right?"

He said: "And what is its right?"

Salih said to him: "To know its faces, and then to seek from among them that in which the truth becomes owing to you."

Abu Malik said: "And what are its faces, and how many faces are they?"

Salih said to him: "Seeking is of three faces: a seeker who knows, a seeker who does not yet know, and a seeker who knows that he does not know. Which of the three are you?"

Abu Malik said: "I am in more need of knowing the three than anything. How should I lay claim to a station I do not know?"

Salih said to him: "Then how did you claim to be a seeker at all, when you do not know the faces of seeking?"

Abu Malik said: "I did not know them. Make them plain to me."

Salih said: "Wealth is the treasure of the people of this world, and knowledge is the treasure of the people of the Hereafter. The seeking of wealth is of three kinds: a seeker who seeks what he already owns, a seeker who seeks a thing due at an appointed term, and a seeker who seeks the gift of alms. So likewise is the seeking of religion and the treasures of the Hereafter of three faces. The first is the seeker who knows, and he is the divine scholar, the rabbani, who seeks out the dead in their ignorance to revive them by his knowledge. The second is the seeker who is coming to know, and he is the learner who has grasped some of the degrees of knowledge and seeks its farthest end and limit. The third is the seeker who is coming to recognize, and he is the ignorant man known by his ignorance, who knows nothing beyond his knowing that he is in need; so he seeks the scholars in order to learn. These are the three faces, and the descriptions of the seekers."

Abu Malik said: "Yes. These are the faces of seeking, for religion and for the world. And I am the seeker coming to recognize, who knows nothing yet knows that he is in need. So teach me."

He said: "Did you come to know, or did you come to know by sight?"

Abu Malik said: "And what is the difference between the two?"

Salih said: "Knowledge is report; recognition is seeing with the eye."

Abu Malik said: "This too is something one needs to know."

Salih said: "As for report: that is a man who is ignorant and does not know that he is ignorant, until he meets a scholar, and the scholar informs him of his ignorance; then he knows that he is ignorant and in need of knowledge, by the word of that scholar. So his knowing of his ignorance came to him as report. As for recognition of need and ignorance: that is a man who has already known by his own mind that he asks about a thing but does not know the face of its answer, so his breast is straitened by ignorance, and he hopes for no relief except by knowledge of what he was ignorant of; so his ignorance itself became a recognition by his mind. Ignorance is want, and want is narrowness, and narrowness is a severe need that compels the creature to seek breadth; and the breadth is the breadth of knowledge, for knowledge is wide and spacious. From this I have known that you are in need."

Abu Malik said: "I find all that you have described within myself. I have come to know that I am in need, in severe want. So relieve my want by your excellence."

Salih said: "I see you hastening in the request before being sure of it."

Abu Malik said: "Want hastened me by its narrowness, so I hastened; and I knew the excellence of breadth, so I sought it."

Salih said to him: "If want has hastened you and you know the excellence of breadth, then how have you come to knowledge of the one sought?"

Abu Malik said: "Your excellence has been made sure to me, so I turned to you; and I hinted at the meaning, so I sought you."

Salih said to him: "But from where was it made sure to you that I am the one known for breadth, and not you, so that you sought me? And from where did you learn that I am the one sought, so that you came? No scholar of prophecy has pointed you to me, that you might learn; nor have you seen in me the tokens of the proofs, that you might recognize. Do you not see that in your seeking you are rising toward the ease of imitation? In this you wrong wisdom and you wrong yourself, like a man who measures grain by the bottom of the measure: if he does not level it off, he wrongs his creditor, and if he presses it down, he wrongs himself. So it is with one who takes knowledge by imitation: if he acts, he is not thanked; and if he abandons it, he is called an unbeliever. And you, to my mind, are wiser than that."

Abu Malik said: "I know full well that it is so. Yet I hoped that the truth would be that which establishes itself, and I would spare you the labor of establishing it, for dispute breeds contention, and contention breeds rancor, since each of two adversaries seeks the upper hand over his fellow. And when will he ever reach the truth who persists in defending the false?"

Salih said to him: "Excellent is what you have said. The truth does indeed establish itself by its own excellence and honor, and when quarreling grows many it does but distract hearts from that which is worthier than it. Yet there is no forsaking what the scholars have laid down, for they have said: every seed for which the ground has not first been made sweet does not thrive; and every knowledge for which hearts are not made pure by proof does not grow."

Abu Malik said: "Then bring forward whatever proofs you wish. For the words of wisdom are like the jewel: if you turn it over, its light gleams, and if you set it aside, its excellence is not hidden."

Salih said to him: "You speak truly. But that is only when there is no counterfeit of coated glass set beside the jewel; then the jewel is not hidden. But when claims grow many, and the tyrants of the world contend over religion until they dress it in what is not its own and coat it over with a likeness before the seekers, then it becomes the seeker's duty to put the jewel to the test, so that its excellence may become plain and the counterfeit and its people may be exposed."

Abu Malik said: "You have dealt fairly and fulfilled the duty of the truth. As for the counterfeiting of these people, we have come to know it and its defects, and so we have cast it off, and held fast to this opinion by which we have come to know you, and we have called it the sound jewel of speech. We took our stand upon it, and you agreed with us upon it, and we all clung together to its rope. Now if you have found a meaning that is more precious as a jewel than this, then by my life, jewels do surpass one another, some above some. So show me where the excellence of your jewel lies, by a testing through which its excellence is known. As for our jewel, we have known it and made it known; so say of it whatever appears to you."

The talk between them had come round to the testing of true knowledge, which Salih likened to a precious jewel. The duty of the seeker, he had said, is to have the jewel assayed, so that its worth is made plain and the counterfeit, together with those who trade in it, is exposed.

Abu Malik said: "You have dealt fairly and fulfilled the duty of truth. As for the counterfeit of this doctrine, we have come to know it and its defects, and so we rejected it and held fast to the opinion by which we know ourselves, saying, This is the sound jewel of speech. We upheld it, you agreed with us upon it, and together we held to its rope. If you have found a meaning more precious in substance than ours, then, by my life, jewels do indeed surpass one another. So show me the excellence of your jewel by a test through which its worth may be known. As for our own jewel, we have known it and you have known it; say of it what seems right to you."

Salih said to him: "As for your jewel, it stood with us as an honored thing, in the place of the sound jewel, until we presented it to the jewelers and the people of the mines, and they found it false for us and would not accept it from us as a jewel. They said, This is glass in disguise, and they showed us its defects, so that it fell away from the bounds of what a jewel is. For a jewel is raised up only by its own preciousness, and by the knowledge of the people of insight who prize it and vie for it, until its price reaches every sum. When the jewelers who know it reject it, who else recognizes it, and who accepts it afterward save the people of delusion, who neither buy nor sell? So it is with knowledge: it is not knowledge until it is presented to those who know the science of heaven. If they accept it, it is true knowledge; and if they do not accept it, it is idle nonsense. Idle nonsense has no place in wisdom, nor do the wise accept it."

Abu Malik said: "And who are these that know the science of heaven, after the prophets and the messengers? We know of none of them in our age, nor have men had any covenant with such since revelation was cut off with the closing of the books; and the nations are now held to account only by reports. What do you say to this?"

Salih said: "Any religion held to by way of report alone is not called a religion, for religion is one, upright, upheld by God in its truth, admitting no difference within it, whereas the reports, as you well know, are shot through with disagreement, each denying the other. And the religion of God is dearer than that."

Abu Malik said: "But when they differ over a report, they refer it back to the books. What agrees with the books they accept, and what contradicts them they set aside as false."

Salih said to him: "Yet what is found in the books only in general terms, who will make it particular for them? And what is set down in detail, who will join it together for them? Who among them is trusted concerning what God has made lawful and what He has forbidden? I heard you on the day of the dispute, when a distressed man came to you and said, O Abu Malik, I swore an oath by divorce and then broke it. You said to him, Some hold that she is severed from you and not lawful to you until she marries another husband, and some hold that she is still your wife and is not forbidden to you by a single pronouncement. The man said to you, Then which of the two shall I act upon? You said, Act upon whichever of the two you wish. So he rose from your presence not knowing whether he was going home to what is lawful or to what is forbidden. Is this how judgment is given in the religion of God? Is this how His lawful and His forbidden are made known?"

Abu Malik said: "The Sharia of my age has indeed been ruined, suspended, and thrown into disorder, and we hold to nothing now but the name of religion. What, then, is the state of religion with you?"

Salih said to him: "And what is religion, O Abu Malik?"

He said: "Command and prohibition, the lawful and the forbidden, the Sunnah and the obligations."

Salih said to him: "And is Sharia, in your view, anything other than this?"

Abu Malik said: "I know of nothing beyond what I have described to you."

Salih said to him: "Then you have gathered religion and Sharia onto a single ground?"

He said: "Yes."

Salih said to him: "Then religion has been thrown into disorder by the suspension of Sharia and corrupted by its corruption. What is left with you?"

Here Abu Malik held back from answering for a time, his head bowed, turning over the matter of his religion. Then he raised his head and said: "I do not see that anything at all is left with me. So what is religion with you?"

Salih said to him: "Religion, as I hold it, is that whose door is barred against the corrupters and whose gates are thrown open to the seekers, so that whoever brings it is ennobled by being foremost to it, and whoever seeks it attains the good through it. By it works are honored, and by its justice the laws come into flower. It is a cause joining heaven to earth, connected and unbroken: the firm handhold of those who take hold, the rope of those who cling fast, the ark of tranquility, the ship of deliverance, and the light of life; generous in what it holds, guarded in its sciences, marked in its jewels, its excellence hidden from none who look upon it."

Abu Malik said: "If God has a religion by which He will judge on the Day, one with which He is pleased and for which He rewards and punishes, then in this sense it must be. And now that the excellence of the description has been made plain, what of the One described?"

Salih said to him: "The good comes only from the good, O Abu Malik. Set your light in the lamp-niche of your reason and grasp what is meant for you. The claims of what is decreed have contended over you, the robes of delusion have made you heedless, and you have taken upon yourself a charge that none but you can bear, along with the affairs of those who follow you in good faith, who have listened to your word, accepted your guidance, and trusted your judgment. It is your duty to guard yourself and them from a Fire you cannot avoid coming to; so seek the way of escape from it. You are the most favored of them in the grace of God and the most bound to give Him thanks, for I have known you to recognize the good and to hope for its reward. As for what you have asked, the confirming of that to which I call you, it is my duty to establish it for you from whatever side you wish."

Abu Malik said: "All that you have said before is firm and true. As for the reports of the common people, religion is not set right by them, nor are they fit for it. Nothing is left to us but to know that to which you call us."

Salih said to him: "I call you to the justice of God and to His oneness."

Abu Malik said: "How excellent is that to which you call. We ourselves hold this saying and know its excellence above all doctrines."

Salih said to him: "It is not where your fancy has gone, O Abu Malik. That doctrine you have in mind is a describing of justice without knowledge of the One described, and a swerving into the names of God without knowledge of His oneness. What I call you to is the knowledge of God's justice and the landmarks of His unity. If you wish, I will bring you to see the corruption of that saying, as far as you please."

Abu Malik said: "If the saying of the people of justice is corrupt and their monotheism a swerving, then there is no firm truth upon the earth, unless the meaning be something other than all the doctrines. Show us, then, the corruption of justice and monotheism, and from where it comes."

Salih said to him: "Justice and monotheism are among the noblest of all doctrines. Monotheism is too exalted to be corrupted, for monotheism has no opposite; and justice is not corrupted, for it is the judgment between God and His creation. But the doctrines are all corrupted by the way their people have corrupted the saying of justice and the rest, for it is a claim by which no statement is established. They do not encompass the knowledge of what they say until they defer to the command of God. So take up whichever of the two you wish and hold to it, until I strip it from you by the proof of truth."

Abu Malik said: "I would not defend a saying I do not hold, nor one whose author was questioned about it rather than I, and I have not set myself up as anyone's agent, nor am I keeper over the authors of these doctrines. I will defend before you nothing but my own saying. I say that God is One, that nothing is like Him, just in His ruling, who does not burden His servants with what they have no power to bear. He commanded them and forbade them, yet He did not command by absolute decree nor forbid by compulsion; rather His command is a giving of choice and His prohibition a warning, so that none obeyed Him under coercion and none disobeyed Him as one overpowered. This is my meaning and what I hold."

Salih said to him: "Did I not tell you that this doctrine is among the noblest? Yet these are names and attributes that avail nothing unless their meaning is present. It is as when you say, Fire is hot: you have spoken truly, but with a truth that does not profit you, for your saying, Fire is hot, is a name and an attribute whose mention does not profit you if no food is cooked by them and no darkness is lit by them, apart from the meaning that is the very presence of fire. And if names and attributes could stand in things in the place of meanings, then the mere mention of fire would burn your tongue, and the mention of food would fill you."

Abu Malik said: "As for the saying about these things, you have spoken truly, for their names and attributes profit nothing except by the presence of their meaning. So show me that my saying of justice and monotheism are names and attributes that call for the knowledge of a named and described One."

Salih said to him: "Your saying that God is one, and 'one,' واحد, is four letters, of which we see two joined and two apart. Which of them is 'one,' or are all of them together 'one'? And your saying 'Allah,' الله, is four letters. Which of them is Allah, or are all of them together Allah, or are they signs that point to God, or do you say otherwise?"

Then Abu Malik was struck silent for a time. Then he said: "I find no way out of this except that they are signs that point to God."

Salih said to him: "Which of them points to God, and by what do they point to Him: by their being apart or by their being joined? Or singly and jointly, as when you say الله, where the alif stands alone and the three letters are joined together? Which points to God: this one by standing alone, or these by being joined?"

Abu Malik said: "I do not know what to say to you in this. Take us on to some other door."

Salih said to him: "The door to the knowledge of monotheism has been shut in your face by whoever told you this in this way, O Abu Malik."

He said: "Yes."

Salih said to him: "Then I ask you about your knowledge of God: from where is it, and from whom did you take it? Was it from an attribute that pointed you to One described?"

He said: "No."

Salih said: "Then from a name of that One, pointing to a named?"

He said: "Yes."

Salih said: "And what is the name that pointed you?"

He said: "Allah."

Salih said: "Is 'Allah' a name, or the named?"

He said: "The named."

Salih said: "Then what is the name?"

He said: "Allah."

Salih said: "So 'Allah' is a name for Allah?"

He said: "Yes."

Salih said: "Then what is the difference between the name and the named? And which of the two do you worship, or do you worship both?"

Abu Malik was bewildered for a time, turning over his answer. Then he said: "I find no way in this except that I have seen all the crafts, and each of them has a maker; and so I judged that this great creation has a Creator who resembles nothing of His creation, just as the makers do not resemble what they have made."

Salih said to him: "O Abu Malik, you have led us away from the first point, and the narrowness of the meaning of monotheism has driven you to seek Him by analogy and to set up parables for Him. God is dearer than that, for everything made is made from some thing, whereas God did not create things from anything that was before them. These makers you know only by having watched some of them with your own eyes, how each one works; and so you passed judgment on a making whose maker was hidden from you, by what you had observed of a doing that resembled the doings of the makers you had seen and known, which is analogy on your part. But when God created, He did not make you witness the creation of what He made, nor was there for Him a likeness who makes as He makes; yet you have testified by the present likeness to the absent likeness. I have never known you, O Abu Malik, to be content with analogy in your religion, so how are you content to seek the knowledge of your Lord by measurement? And your saying, Nothing is like Him, ليس كمثله شيء, proves, out of your own words, that He has a likeness and that nothing is like that likeness. Had you intended monotheism, you would have said, He is not like anything, ليس كهو شيء, and you would not have said, Nothing is like Him. Glory be to Him whom no far reach of aspiration attains by thought, and whom no soaring of the pointing hand attains by perception; nay, He is exalted high above the description of those who are charged with the law. O Abu Malik, had you aimed this quest at one of His servants, or an angel of His, you would have been left unable to grasp what is said of him and how he was made; how then the One who made him? The lights of the people of the earth fall short of grasping the light of the sun, and the sun is but one of the lights of this world; so how shall the one who falls short of grasping a servant of His grasp the Creator? Say what seems right to you."

Then Abu Malik cast his eyes to the ground in bewilderment and could find no path to speech.

Salih said to him: "O Abu Malik, raise your head and say whatever is present to you."

Abu Malik said: "Speech is gone; there is nothing to say. Minds are bewildered before the answer, imaginations are stunned away from thought, and the instrument is too weak, so that nothing is grasped by it but seeking and asking."

Salih said: "O Abu Malik, the One who is of such greatness, do the eyes grasp Him?"

He said: "No, He is higher than that."

Salih said: "Is speech heard from Him?"

He said: "There is no speech except from a speaker, and the speaker is described by speech, and God is too exalted for attributes."

Salih said to him: "This Vast One of greatness, this High One of power, would He be unjust in His creation, charging them with what they have no power for and commanding them with what they cannot grasp, and then punishing them if they do not do it?"

Abu Malik said: "Whoever charges the obedient one with what he has no power for has charged him with disobedience, and to punish him for it is an outrage; and God is dearer than that."

Salih said: "O Abu Malik, the One who so holds Himself far above injustice and above outrage, would He be content that ignorance should be a companion to His servants?"

Abu Malik said: "Ignorance is a sure denial of thanksgiving and a persisting in disbelief, and God does not love the unbelievers."

Salih said: "Were they, then, created as knowers who already knew what was wanted of them?"

He said: "No, rather He created them ignorant, knowing nothing."

Salih said: "Then from where do they come to know what He wants of them, so that it becomes binding upon them to seek His good pleasure and to guard themselves against His wrath?"

Abu Malik said that men had learned this from God.

Then Salih pressed him. If He is higher than sight can reach, wider than perception can grasp, and greater than the limits of speech, then how does the one whom He commands and forbids come to know His will, so as to obey Him rather than disobey, and thank Him rather than deny, unless there are causes for this knowledge set between Him and His creation? Must there not be those who deliver His justice and bear witness to His witnessing, for the servants and against them, whom He has chosen by His knowledge and singled out from all His creation, and made messengers to them and proofs against them, so that they stand as the just ones between Him and His servants, conveying His command and His prohibition? Obedience to them is then obedience to God and the winning of His pleasure, and disobedience to them is His wrath and His punishment. For otherwise, how could the command come from God to His creation at all?

This, said Abu Malik, belongs to the foundations of justice. Once creation stands in need of God's justice to teach them what He wants of them, and He teaches them through the hands of the righteous one He has appointed, then each shall thank his benefactor, and be rewarded, reproached, or punished by Him as He wills.

Salih asked whether it was permissible to rule with justice in any way other than this. It is permissible for none but God, Abu Malik answered, and God is the wisest of judges.

Then Salih posed the harder case. Would it be permissible for Him to delegate the command to some of His people, setting them up justly to correct their own wrongdoing, and then to assign two others to the very same task, yet without guiding them by an equal measure of His justice, as He had guided the first?

The judgment can only be one, said Abu Malik, because justice is one. If the ruling were split into two meanings, one of the two would inevitably fall into wrong.

Has it not been established for you, Salih said, that the judgment of God is one, with no difference in it, and that His way is a single way, unchanged among His messengers? Were it not for the succession of the messengers, and the standing of command and prohibition, the truth would have no increase and creation no meaning. Yes, said Abu Malik, this is the truth.

And is not this succession of the messengers, sent from God to His creation, so that He may lay obedience upon them as an obligation, and set them as an authority between Himself and His creatures, bearing witness for those who obey them, so that their obedience is accepted, and against those who disobey them, so that they are turned away? The judgment could not have been otherwise, Abu Malik said, for otherwise He would have given the lie to His own messengers and broken His promise, and God is too generous for that. The affair of the messengers has made it plain that they are of His justice, and that people worship Him by obeying them. But tell me of their ranks: are they equal in the sight of God, or do some hold a merit above others?

How could they stand level in rank, Salih answered, when they are bound to obey one another? Were they all on one plane, none would owe obedience to another. Rather, by His justice He has preferred some above others, so that obedience passes from the lesser to the more excellent, and from the excellent to the one above him, until it reaches its utmost, and they obey and worship to the very limit of what God wills.

Then is the one who obeys the excellent among them, Abu Malik asked, equal in reward to the excellent one himself? Yes, said Salih. This is the justice of God, which grants every obedient servant his reward, and one does not ask whether the measure of the superior or of the lesser applies.

Is each of them, then, like his fellow? No, said Salih, for they come from the Creator bearing knowledge of the unseen, the Books He has revealed to them, and no one can produce their like except from God.

Then can anyone imitate the lesser one among them, Abu Malik asked, if no Book is sent down to him carrying knowledge of the unseen? No one, said Salih, can lay claim to that station.

And how can that be, when the lesser ones did not set themselves apart from the rest of creation by the unseen, as the excellent one made himself plain?

If the excellent one, Salih replied, set himself apart from others by knowledge of the unseen in the revelation, the tanzil, then the lesser one likewise sets himself apart from others by knowledge of the unseen in the interpretation, the tawil. Both the revelation and its interpretation are from God, and what is with God is attained only through revelation.

And the lesser one too is inspired? Abu Malik asked. Yes, said Salih, and for that reason obedience to him became obedience to God.

But if you place him level with the excellent one in obedience and in inspiration, why do you name him the lesser? Because of the obedience he owes to the excellent one, Salih said, and his need of him.

And what need has he of a creature, Abu Malik pressed, when the revelation reaches him from the Creator? Though the revelation reaches him, Salih answered, it reaches him as the interpretation of the sayings and the Books, and of the remainder that the excellent ones left behind. So it was revealed to Lot at the hands of Abraham, and Abraham's excellence was made plain; and so it was revealed to Ishmael and to Isaac, while the goodness, the blessings, and the merit were reckoned to Abraham. The revelation from God is joined to His just ones in His earth, each according to his rank. He does not, in His justice, will to cut off His command and His prohibition and His worship from His servants after He has favored them with these, so that worship should be severed and ignorance made lawful.

The bearer of the revelation, Abu Malik said, has shown his truthfulness by the knowledge of the unseen in his Book. By what does the bearer of the interpretation show that the knowledge of the unseen is with him, so that the revelation bears witness for him and lays obedience to him alone as an obligation, as it did for the bearer of the revelation? He comes in his interpretation, Salih said, with knowledge from heaven and a declaration from the highest assembly, al-mala' al-a'la, and the Book bears witness to it.

Then I bear witness, said Abu Malik, to what the Books have testified: that every interpretation to which the revelation itself testifies is a revelation from God. Otherwise all the Books and the messengers would have to be denied, since their meaning is one and the same. If some of them give the lie to others, all must be reckoned liars; and if some confirm others, all must be believed.

Such is justice, Abu Malik went on, and by it the truthfulness of those who hold to it is known, and their merit. Obedience is due to those confirmed to be among the messengers who are His justice in His creation, whom He charged to convey His message. And if obedience is obligatory by prophethood, by revelation, and by interpretation, then what of those who sit in the seats of the messengers, resembling them and yet not of them? They have all agreed that after their prophet there is no prophet and in their time no warner, that they themselves are the ones who uphold the command of God and His religion, and that what they say leads to God's reward or His punishment. Yet you say otherwise. How, then, could the scholars of this community have failed to look into this matter?

They have not left it wholly unexamined, Salih answered, but they have not looked into it with a sound looking, and no one has spoken of it to them so that they might lend it the ear of their minds; for it has been steeped into their hearts that they are worthier of the command of God and His religion, and that there is no prophet after their prophet, just as the misleaders of the nations before them said. Every nation has claimed that there is no prophet after its own.

And what, Abu Malik asked, was the origin of this, and what did they intend by it?

Its originators, Salih said, were three in every age: a rebellious devil, a stubborn tyrant, and a hypocritical jurist. By this saying they sought to cut off the marks of the prophets from the distinctions of prophethood and to make it a possession passed among themselves. They could not do so until they had steeped the hearts of the nations in the saying, addressed to every nation, that God had sent none better than your prophet, and that after him there is no prophet and no warner. So they whispered to them from where they did not perceive it, and drew them away from where they did not know. Every nation clung to its own prophet by calling all who came after him liars, supposing that this drew them nearer to their prophet, and each held that every prophet after its own was false. Thus, on the strength of that saying, the mention of prophethood and the marks of the prophets ceased among all the nations, so that they neither sought what they had lost nor would have believed it had they sought. So the misleading devils gained their power over those they misled, and the tyrants gained their power to kill the prophets and killed them, and the doubt of the corrupt jurists worked its way in, so that they were adorned in their own eyes, and thus they perished and made others perish. All of this because no hearts among creation inclined toward the prophets when they appeared, nor sought them out when they were gone, so that despair of the prophets ran to its end among them, until the prophets called them openly to the truth and were called liars, and were killed in their midst, and no one was moved to anger.

Here is the nation of the Magi, clinging to its tyranny, worshipping its idols upon its fire, saying, as they claim, that God will send no messenger after their messenger. Here is the nation of the Torah, clinging to its scripture, devoted to its calf, standing within the bounds set by its prophets, humbling itself before its Judge, saying that God will raise up no messenger after Moses. Here is the nation of the Gospel, speaking by its likening, claiming that its prophet is its God, drawing near with its crosses, exalting its priests, saying that God will send no messenger after Jesus. And here is your own community, led along the paths of those who went before it, misled by what misled them: it inherited the Book from other than its rightful people, walked the religion by other than its own way, followed its great ones into error, obeyed its masters in misguidance, and its jurists bowed before the tyrants, so that they set up for the people a religion other than the Sunnah, performing the prayer at other than its time, and rendering the alms to other than its rightful due. They are agreed to be patient with these leaders, constant in their need of them, certain of their independence from any other, saying that God will send no messenger and no warner after their Messenger.

So you and those like you followed the saying of the great multitude, and what won you over was the abundance of the consensus and the force of the sultans. You accepted it from them, adorned it with the beauty of your doctrines, gave it preference by the beauty of your words, and raised upon it an argument of justice in a matter where no such argument can be brought against you. You followed those above you among the mighty for the sake of awe, and those below you followed you out of desire, and they supposed it was the truth. Now if this community must be believed in what it has decreed against God, that His proofs are cut off from His creation on the strength of a single strand of consensus, then the nations before it have the better claim to be believed, by three qualities that establish their argument against you, and by which the truth is theirs rather than yours.

And what, Abu Malik asked, are those qualities in which their merit over us is such that they must be believed if their word is true, or held liars if their word is crooked? Their word is like your word, Salih said, and your word is of the same kind as theirs; your hearts resemble one another, and yet the merit in the argument is theirs. The first quality: they say they came earlier to the religion of God than you, and so theirs is the merit of the foremost. The second: the consensus, both among themselves and between them and you, agrees in affirming what they hold, and so theirs is the merit of the truthful. The third: you yourselves testify that their prophets are truthful and are the messengers of God, while not one of them has borne witness to any prophet of yours, neither to prophethood nor to truthfulness. So the merit over you is theirs, by your agreeing with them and their standing apart from you; and they say that the knowledge you possess is copied from their knowledge, and that theirs is the root of yours. This is their argument against you, clear in speech and plain in justice. If the ruling of creation is binding upon the Creator, then the ruling of these is the more binding. But if the Creator is Himself the Ruler over His creation, abrogating what He wills and confirming what He wills, with none to overturn His judgment, then by my life the word of all of them falls away, and the Creator is foremost over the creation and over the command. Every day He is engaged in some affair, "كل يوم هو في شأن", and His act is not to be denied, though He send a warner every day.

This community, said Abu Malik, has fallen into a far straying. Most of those before them strayed as well, Salih answered, for they cast the friends of God, the awliya' Allah, out of their right, and let the foolish overpower them in their affair, and made lawful what God had forbidden, and did so deliberately, that they might not seem to imitate prophethood and so make the people long for the prophets and refrain from killing them. Instead they diverted the people with kingship and largesse, and led them along in the following of their desires. In this they were helped by those known outwardly for truthfulness and piety, who made a show of it before the people, until the very ones who had commanded the community to the good turned it away from the good, and the ones who had forbidden the evil guided it toward the evil, and few remained who kept faith with the friends of God. So truth and falsehood came to alternate like light and darkness in their difference. Were it not for the remnant of God's friends and His just ones in the earth, and the revelation that comes to them, the little that is true would never rise above the much that is false. For by what could God tell the foul from the good, save by His just ones? And how could God's argument reach all creation, save through them?

There is no escape, Abu Malik said, and no evasion of the justice of God and His command; with the multitude of adversaries, none remain but the just servants of God, and by them the argument of God to His creation is upheld.

Then, since it is established that the company of the prophets of God are the just ones, Salih said, is not each single one of them called a just man? Yes, said Abu Malik, he is a just man, as it is said, "So-and-so bore witness, and he was a just man among the just."

So it is now established for you, Salih said, that the just one is the friend of God, and that he has passed beyond the bounds of your talk of a mere attribute and has become one who is himself described. Yes, said Abu Malik, this is a true saying.

And whoever does not attain the one described, Salih asked, does the description profit him? The description does not profit him, Abu Malik answered, except by attaining the one described.

Salih said to him: Then whoever does not know the just one of Allah and His guardian, can his own speech be just?

At this the narrator relates that Abu Malik smiled and said: By Allah, you have drawn me out of my whole position on justice and monotheism, and nothing has drawn me out of it but the truth itself, in the clearest of arguments and the gentlest of speech. After this, what position is left for me to defend?

Salih said: From here, O Abu Malik, the coated glass falls away from the hands of those who trade it as the colored gems it is not. Its worth was never truly known; but when it was set before the jewelers it was exposed, and its deceived owners were exposed along with it.

Abu Malik said: You have spoken truly, and the crookedness of these doctrines and the straying of the nations has now become plain to me. But how are we to find safety from them, and to know the means that lead to Allah, or even one such means, when we did not live in the age of the prophets, and there are in our own time neither prophets nor messengers as there were among the nations before us?

Salih said: Glory be to Allah, O Abu Malik. You have spent a long stretch of your life affirming the justice of Allah, yet the moment we press it upon you where it becomes binding upon you to accept it, you turn back and speak as though Allah would deceive you, and you ascribe injustice to Him.

Abu Malik said: God forbid. I say no such thing, for whoever says it has transgressed and lied against Allah. From where did you bind me to that?

Salih said: From your own words, that the age of the prophets has passed you by, and that in our time there is no prophet and no messenger. Tell me: was His sending of the messengers among the former peoples an act of justice from Him toward His servants, or was it done in vain?

He said: God forbid that it be anything but justice from Him.

Salih said: Then what has cut us off from that justice? Are we not His servants as they were? Were they not created as we are created, and are we not bound in service as they were bound? Has He then wronged us after being just to them, so that we who come after are left excused? Or has His giving run dry in our time, so that we cling only to the bounty He gave to them, and He is content with that from us? Or has the way of Allah changed with us after it stood firm with those before us, so that we should pass judgment upon Allah that He has changed? Or is His justice an obligation owed to us and His ruling in force upon us, just as it was His way with those who came before us, so that there is no altering the words of Allah and none to overturn His judgment? And who deserves the name of the just one except the guardians of Allah? Among all that Allah has created, none is just except the one Allah has chosen, who became just by His command. It is for this reason that a ruling is called just, on account of the justice of the one who decreed it, and a word is called true on account of the truthfulness of the one who spoke it.

Abu Malik said: It can only be as you say, and we are not permitted to say of Allah anything but the truth. Yet we walked the road of our own opinion in speech and judged by the absence of the rightly guided from among us. We held that this too was part of Allah's ruling and His way with us, as it had been with those before us in the interregnums between one messenger and the next, like the interval between the religion of Abraham and the time of Moses and the Torah, and the interval between the Torah and the time of Jesus and the Gospel. On that footing we spoke and acted, working our way through an age of interruption, holding fast to the traditions and the established way.

Salih said to him: The religion of Allah is far too precious to Him for that. What interregnum was there after the religion of Abraham, when Allah sent after him Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Jonah, and Shu'ayb, leading on to Moses? He inspired them to do good works, to call to the religion of Abraham, to establish the prayer according to its way and to render the zakat according to its obligations, until He sent down the ruling of the Torah upon Moses. And what interregnum was there after the religion of Moses, between him and the Gospel, when Allah sent after Moses Joshua son of Nun, and Elias, and Talut, and David, and Solomon, and Zechariah, and John, leading on to Jesus? They upheld the ruling of the Torah, holding to its religion, calling to it, and reciting its Psalms, until the ruling of the Gospel was sent down upon Jesus. So it was with those who went before them and those who came after, and with the many generations between, whom none knows but Allah. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs; for each people Allah set forth His parables, and each was called to the truth. Where, then, is this interregnum among them, and when did it ever fall? Allah said only, "after a succession of messengers," عَلَى فَتْرَةٍ مِنَ الرُّسُل, and by these words He affirmed both the messengers and the pause. When the call arose it arose out of fear, so they called in secret and concealment. The earth was never left empty of the justice of Allah for the blink of an eye, but that His just one was in it, either openly present or hidden away in fear.

Abu Malik said: This is a statement that cannot be turned back, a settled word in which there is no doubt. As for the standing of Allah's proof through justice and the succession of the messengers, that is now established and made clear. So point me now to the meaning of their absence in this age of ours. What is its cause?

Salih said to him: What was the cause of the absence of Moses when he went out from Pharaoh's city and passed beyond it in fear, watchful, until he reached the land of Shu'ayb?

Abu Malik said: He fled from the people of Pharaoh, hiding himself from them, because he feared for his life when they had agreed together to kill him.

Salih said to him: Have you heard, in this law of yours, of any one of the just, the just ones of Allah and the sons of His prophets, who was killed while patiently bearing the burdens of the religion?

Abu Malik said: Yes. A great many of them have been killed.

Salih said to him: Then which just one of the just of Allah do you wish would appear to you and to the people of your age? One who was slain, whom Allah would raise up and send to you after death? Or one in hiding from among you, whom you would drive back to the very killing he fled, when he has only followed the way of Allah in His prophets and messengers, patient under fear and in concealment, until Allah judges between him and us, and He is the best of judges?

Abu Malik said: There is no blame upon one who follows the way of the righteous by fearing for himself and fleeing from his people. The blame lies upon the evil nations and the helpers of the oppressors, for their killing of the prophets of Allah and His just ones, and for their agreeing together upon that deed.

Salih said: Then what do you say of one who kills the just one of Allah and His guide? Where is his end? And what of the one who helped him?

Abu Malik said: Both are bound for the Fire, and so is anyone who raised a banner for them or handed them an inkwell.

Salih said to him: And what do your jurists say of this?

Abu Malik said: They ask mercy for the slain, yet they see no sin in the killer.

Salih said to him: And whoever obeys the killer of his own accord, is he not a partner with him in the blood of the slain?

Abu Malik said: Yes, by Allah. He is his partner, and worse than he is.

Salih said to him: His partnership is plain, from his aiding him. But how has he become worse than the killer himself?

Abu Malik said: Were it not for the reports of the jurists, the rule of the tyrants could never have been established through the fools who follow them.

Salih said to him: You have spoken the truth, O Abu Malik. Have you then accepted for yourself their kingdom as your home, their land as your dwelling place, their armies as your neighbors, and their enforcers as your close companions, while you serve as their just man, judging for them by your knowledge and settling their affairs by their authority? You have prayed for them in your petitions and thanked them for what you sought from them. Upon their tyranny you set the crown of your justice, and they grew proud; over their filth you cast the robe of the sacred law, and they overstepped all bounds; and you lit up their falsehood by leaving your own truth to lie idle. So the weak were deceived by your conduct, and every scholar who was like you said: These are the jurists of the community, we have in them a pattern to follow. They went astray through you, and you went astray through the ways of the tyrants, and you supposed that the one held to account would be someone other than yourselves. You forgot the words of Allah, glory be to Him, "وَلاَ تَرْكَنُوا إِلَى الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا فَتَمَسَّكُمُ النَّارُ," And do not incline toward those who have done wrong, lest the Fire touch you. So you severed what Allah commanded to be joined, and you saw what you were doing; you spoke of the outward forms of justice while wandering lost from its path. You turned away from His saying, "أَطِيعُوا اللهَ وَأَطِيعُوا الرَّسُولَ وَأُولِي الأَمْرِ مِنْكُمْ," Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you, and you claimed that these oppressors were your rulers, even though they had disbelieved. You forgot the words of Allah, exalted is He, "قَاتِلُوا الَّذِينَ يَلُونَكُمْ مِنَ الكُفَّارِ," Fight those of the disbelievers who are near you. So you disobeyed the one you were commanded to obey, and obeyed the one you were commanded to fight, all the while supposing that you were doing good work. Error carried you away, and your own opinion led you on, until you said that Allah would send no messenger and no warner in your age. Is it that the goodness of your deeds has set you above all need of the guardians of Allah for the proof? Or is it that the ugliness of your deeds has cut you off from all hope in Allah's acceptance of repentance? Or is it justice that you should pass judgment upon Allah without justice, and be content, on behalf of your Messenger, with what none of you would be content with for himself in his place? Would any one of you consent that his own son be killed, or that his house and wealth be taken from him without right?

Abu Malik said: No one consents to that. If a child of one of us were killed, or he were driven from his property, he would rage, and his people would rage with him, and they would not be appeased until he was appeased.

Salih said to him: Then Allah and His Messenger have a greater right that you should rage for their sake, when His vicegerent is killed and His guardian is driven from the earth.

Abu Malik said: It is bitter to us that the guardian of Allah should be killed among us or driven from his land. But they themselves chose to withdraw from us, and the tyrants rose to power over us.

Salih said to him: Had you supported the guardians of Allah, they would have appeared; had you forsaken His enemies, those enemies would never have prevailed. But the case has ranged you, before the guardians of Allah, in three ranks. There is the empowered killer, who never tires of the pursuit and is not satisfied even when he has won. There is the gloating approver, who bites his fingertips in rage, sparing no effort to incite others against them, never heedless of them even when they are heedless of him. And there is the one who loathes what is done, who professes love for the guardians of Allah yet forsakes them, having no clear sight in faith and no trust to be placed in him for keeping a secret. For this cause the guardians of Allah have withdrawn from you, in prudent concealment, for your sake and for the sake of this vast multitude, who have taken their own desires as their god, fallen greedily upon the goods of this world, and joined hands over the killing of the prophets. They knew nothing of the fault in what they did, so they never held themselves to account for the wrong they committed, and never humbled themselves to repent of what they had done. Instead they turned the injustice of their own deeds back upon Allah and ascribed it to Him, and said: Allah will send no messenger after their messenger, and no warner. So they made lawful the killing of the guardians of Allah without right. They wronged in deed, deviated in word, and transgressed by their denial.

Abu Malik said: Yes. This is the very description of this community of ours, and this is exactly what they say. Where, then, is the deliverance from it, and where is the escape from the punishment of Allah?

Salih said to him: To Allah. Seek His protection, and He will protect you. Turn to Him in repentance, and He will accept your repentance. Ask Him for the way of His guardians, and by your works He will save you from the deeds of those who did wrong. So it is with this whole multitude: were they to turn to Allah in repentance, He would accept their repentance, and what then would Allah do with punishing them? Allah does not wrong them by the weight of a single atom, though they have wronged and lied, and He does not hasten the punishment upon them, though they hasten to disobey.

Abu Malik said: Then I seek refuge from the punishment of Allah by turning to Him in repentance and seeking the means to what pleases Him. So show your kindness to the one who seeks it of you, and grant guidance to the one who asks to be guided, and your Lord will reward you.

Salih said to him: If you do that, you will find Allah nearer in His pardon of you than your own repentance is to Him, for He loves those who turn to Him; and you will find me eager in guiding you and watchful over you.

Then the eyes of Salih filled with tears at the mention of repentance, and he broke off his speech. He heartened them, and bade them go to his father's house. He himself went in to his father, the learned man who had first sent him out, and consulted him about the affair of Abu Malik and his companions.

The learned man said: You know your companions better than I. If you find good in them, guide them by your guidance; and if you have cause to warn them, do not let eagerness move you to be quick in punishing them. Test them by leaving them to themselves for a time, yet without harshness, and instruct your father to treat them well and to show them kindness in due season. For the one who longs for good cannot stay hidden, and Allah will not abandon you at your back. He will open for you, from the light of His enabling grace, that by which He orders the affairs of His creation, and from the gates of guidance that which will show you the deeds of the rightly guided.

And so, the account continues, Abu Malik and his companions passed through the working out of their trial until their affair came to completion, and they came to know their own guidance, and the guardian of Allah among them turned toward that guidance with tenderness. They gave thanks to Allah for it, and returned to their people as warners, and through them Allah guided many of His servants to His religion.

The author said: Whatever in this took the shape of a story was no idle invention, but a confirmation of what Allah has commanded; for in it lies the steadfastness of the messengers, the signs of the guardians, and the manners of the seekers.

Praise be to Allah, first and last. May Allah bless His Messenger, our master Muhammad, sent forth from Him to His creation as a bearer of good tidings and a warner, and bless his guardian, the Imam of the God-fearing, the radiant, blaze-marked leader (القائد الغرّ المحجّلين), the beloved of the Lord of the Worlds, and the Imams of the people of his house, upon whom Allah has bestowed His favor, from whom He has removed all defilement, and whom He has purified with a complete purifying. Allah is sufficient for us, and how excellent a Guardian; how excellent a Master, and how excellent a Helper. There is no power and no strength except by Allah, the Most High, the Most Great.
