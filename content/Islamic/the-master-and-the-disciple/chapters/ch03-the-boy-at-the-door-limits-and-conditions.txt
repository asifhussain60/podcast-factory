# The Boy at the Door — Limits and Conditions

His eyes overflowed with tears, and he broke off his speech. He took his leave of the people, rose, and set out upon his journey. They wept at the warmth and the eloquence of his sermon and wished to rise and go with him; but out of courtesy they held back, unsure of what accompanying him would require of them, and so they returned to their houses.

One of the group followed him: a youth, the youngest of them in years and the most thoughtful of them in mind, whose training had perfected his intellect and whose reflection had filled his heart. All of this was joined to what he had heard from the Master, to what had been laid before him and granted to him. The boy had a father who was a great sheikh of the Arabs, the most honorable of them in lineage and the most severe of them against the people of religion. So the boy kept the company of the Master, by his leave, until the Master reached the end of his journey. The Master entered his lodging and brought the boy in with him, then called for food. When they had finished and were settled in quiet talk, the boy spoke.

"O wise one," he said, "you have spoken and I have heard; you have preached and I have been informed. You adorned the religion with the finest ornament, made it desired with the best of longing and the fullest of provision, and called to it by the noblest of its trusts. You made minds yearn to question you by the blows your words struck upon the hearts. Such excellence could come only from one whose inward knowledge has been made whole and whose outward forbearance has grown wide, for he is a fountain of wisdom springing from a firm root whose height towers over the worlds, and from whose branches hang fruits for those who seek. I hope that you are one of those blessed branches, for I see in you noble judgment, a sound core, and a perfect intellect. You have said that minds are driven by ignorance to seek knowledge and capacity, and my mind is among the neediest of them and the most destitute. Is there for me a way to life?"

The Master said: "He who created the creatures by His power did not create them in vain, nor did He make them scholars from the outset, nor does He abandon them to wander in ignorance, nor is He content that stupidity be their culture or the garments of ignorance their clothing. He has appointed for them a way out of all of this, by the very faculties with which He composed their frame and completed their creation. He made their hearing, their eyes, and their hearts filled with lights, an honor from Him to them and a proof standing against them. Then He placed the treasures of His wisdom and the marks of His will within the paths that please Him among His chosen creatures. He charged them with religion and called them to it, and laid upon them the duty of answering by the faculties we have named, so that God might sift the corrupt from the pure. Thus the seeker is made able by his seeking, and the ignorant is exposed by his falling behind, so that no argument remains against Allah after His Messengers, and no excuse remains for going astray after the evidence has come."

The boy said: "The absence of any excuse has frightened me, and the presence of the evidence has made me long for it. I beg you for a guide, and I will be your servant."

The Master said: "God did not create men already scholars. He created them as small children, ignorant, knowing nothing. None is great except after being small, and none is a scholar except after ignorance. Smallness is the prelude to greatness, and ignorance is a guide toward knowledge. In the same way, knowledge cannot be borne except by the introduction of a lesser knowledge before it, one swifter to the heart and smoother to the understanding, so that its beginnings may settle in the mind, the limbs may be humbled to its duties, the breast may tremble at its depth, and the intellect may carry its purpose to the end. This is the way of Allah with His servants. Let us not depart from the community by our own opinion, nor follow with our guidance anything other than the established way."

The boy said: "Then I am too small for what I want, so raise me; I am ignorant of what I seek, so teach me; I am bound by your bounty, so have mercy on me. You yourself were once in this very condition, and God led to you His favor by the hands of one whose trial before you was great and whose thanks became due upon you. I know the state of one who suffers the narrowness of ignorance and the need that comes of a feeble resourcefulness."

The Master said: "The state you describe is indeed as you say, yet I will not show you mercy by doing for you what your own effort ought to do. Every claim is accepted only when it is truthful; knowledge does not profit until it is acted upon, and action does not profit until it is accepted. The truthfulness of speech is its justice, and the proof of knowledge lies in the open roads of deeds and the documented examples that have endured across the lands, over which night and day have passed in turn. Do not pursue an argument heedlessly, do not dismiss one heedlessly, and do not accept one upon mere imitation, lest the first doubt that rises against it tear it from your heart. Do not reject a proof once it stands firm, or you will be an advocate for falsehood; and do not abandon inquiry into it and understanding of it, or your judgment will be barren. Think well of me without holding back, strive in your questioning without suspicion, seek the religion as one like you should seek it, and ask God's help in your affair. God does not let the reward of the seeker go to waste, nor does He deny recompense to anyone He sees seeking the good."

The boy said: "You have dealt justly with yourself in a way both fitting and effective, and you have set forth the truth in the best of speech. I ask God's help, and I ask you about this matter to which you call me: what is it, whose is it, and to whom does it belong?"

The Master said: "It is the religion of Allah, which He has approved, and which is from Him, so that none but it is accepted from Him. He delivered it first to the elite among His Messengers, then in general to all His creation. Upon the one specially favored is thanks for it, action upon it, and the call of others to it; upon the one deprived of it is the seeking after it until he knows, and obedience to the one who guides him."

The boy said: "This favor that Allah has singled out for His friends, are all such favors equal in their excellence, or do some surpass others?"

The Master said: "Have we not said that every knowledge has a knowledge before it that taught it, and that each bearer carried his own portion, so that they stand at graded ranks before their Lord, each charged only with what he acquired of knowledge? Then each possessor of merit is given his merit, in justice from God toward His creation and in mercy toward His servants."

The boy said: "This to which you call me, is it that favor itself, or is it something other than it?"

The Master said: "It is neither the whole of it nor wholly other than it. It is drawn from it, as a single cup of water is drawn from the breadth of the Euphrates. I call you to what Allah has approved for His servants and honored on the day He perfected it: precious, guarded, generous, hidden. Its honor, through its reward, is dignity for the one who upholds it; its retribution is debasement for the one who lets it fall. Its outward form is figures, and its inner reality is knowledge. Its ruling is a light, and its justice is plain. This is what I call you to."

The boy said: "What has made you more entitled to this matter than others, and more deserving of it than the rest, when you claim it is from God for all creation, so that everyone lays claim to it against everyone else? What has made you more worthy of the name of truth than they, when this matter reached you only from where it reached them?"

The Master said: "There is no scholar who utters a word of truth but that he has taken it from the very roots from which we ourselves have taken; in this they agree with us, by an acknowledgment from us and from them. What then divided us from them was their departure from those roots while we held fast, our obedience where they disobeyed, our safeguarding of what we were entrusted with where they squandered it, our fulfillment of what we were charged with where they neglected. They agree with us in acknowledgment and differ from us in deed. Their acknowledgment is a witness for us against them, and their loss is the sign of their poverty toward us and their need of what is in our hands.

"They are like brothers who inherited great wealth from their father and divided it equally between them. Some squandered their share until they fell into poverty, while others kept theirs, preserved it, traded with it, and multiplied the wealth in their hands. Then the poor among the brothers turned to the wealthier ones, to serve them and to seek their bounty. So too those who lost the way of their Messengers were driven by their ignorance to seek knowledge from the people of the noble roots. The proof that we are more deserving of the truth than they is their need of us and our sufficiency without them, so that our learning has become the answer to their ignorance, our garment covers their nakedness, and our abundance satisfies their hunger. Thus the truth is binding upon the one who would be virtuous, the credit belongs to the one who confers favor, and thanks is owed to the one who bestows kindness."

The boy said: "As for your superiority over them, that has become clear, and as for the argument of truth, it stands against every honest seeker who lets it slip. But what argument have you against me, when I already agree with what you have said, stand by what you have described, deny what you have denied, and know the truth and its worth as well as falsehood and its people?"

The Master said: "My argument against you is the same as my argument against them, since you differ from them in description but agree with them in meaning."

The boy said: "How is that, when I acknowledge the truth to its owner while they deny it?"

The Master said: "If your acknowledgment sets you apart from them where they deny, you still join them in withholding where they withhold. What use is acknowledgment without a turning to act upon it, and what harm is denial when the setting itself is right?"

The boy said: "Then bring me to the people of truth that I may know them, or to an agent who can establish it for them. Show me which of the two you are able to do."

The Master said: "Do you not see that you have been driven to need the proof of the rightful owner of the truth, or of his agent, just as the common people have been driven to need a proof of the truth itself? Deed has parted you from them, even as ignorance has joined you to them."

The boy said: "You have spoken truly, and the matter has become clear to me."

The Master said: "Have I not firmly established my argument against you and against the common people?"

"Yes," he said.

The Master said: "Can anyone establish for himself a truth that belongs to another?"

"No one is able to do that," he said.

The Master said: "Then the argument stands against you and against the common people, and nothing remains but submission and a turning toward the one whom you cannot do without and who has no need of you."

The boy said: "As for my poverty, I acknowledge it. As for the One who is rich while I am in need, I ask of Him, and I ask Him for mercy."

The Master said: "Have you seen one driven by poverty to a man of bounty obtain anything from him except by earning or by gift?"

The boy said: "Yes. Nothing is obtained from him except by earning or by gift."

The Master said: "And do the people of gift attain what belongs to the people of earning by way of earning?"

"They do not," he said.

The Master said: "Then seek this matter by work, and you will attain it as a thing deserved. Do not seek it by flattery, or you will attain it as a mere gift and so be left deprived."

The boy said: "You are the key of good and its guide. Show me the more meaningful of the two paths and the nearer to guidance. The greatness of my need of you has bound me fast to you."

The Master said: "O my son, there is no distance between us and no blame upon you. What you have hoped from me will reach you. But it has limits that are not to be crossed and conditions that are not to be forgotten."

The boy said: "Set whatever limits you love, and lay down whatever conditions you see fit. I hope you will find me patient under your trial and grateful for your kindness."

The Master said: "The first of the limits is to uphold the outward meaning of the Book and its laws, and to act upon what you know of it. For whoever acts for God according to what he knows, God will guide him to what he does not know."

The boy said: "But the Books are many, and all of them are from Allah. For each Book there is a scholar among the creation who holds fast to it, and all of them are agreed upon safeguarding it and upon the religion it carries."

The Master said: "We have come back to our earlier words, and now we must settle what should never have needed settling."

The boy said: "How is that?"

The Master said: "By your saying that the Books are many, that all of them are from Allah, and that each has a scholar of its own. Do you then suppose that the words of God and His Books cancel one another, that the first of them gives the lie to the last and the last to the first?"

The boy said: "I do not suppose that. But what is the proof?"

The Master said: "Had the creation acted upon what is in the first Book, it would have pointed them to the second; and had they acted upon the second, it would have pointed them to the third, until the work culminates in the last of them. The last is more entitled to be followed than what came before it, even though all of them are from Allah, because the last is nearer to the present age and clearer in its way, since it confirms and supersedes what came before it, and nothing came after it to supersede it."

The boy said: "You have spoken truly and made plain the knowledge of the truth and the mark of truthfulness. I will hold to the limits you have commanded me. So what are your conditions upon me?"

The Master said: "My conditions upon you are five. Do not fail me if I summon you. Do not conceal anything from me if I ask you. Do not seek me until I answer you. Do not ask me for anything until I begin with you. And do not mention my affair to your father."

The boy said: "I will do all that you have named, and it is easy for me set against the greatness of what I hope from you. But how shall I contrive with my father? He is among the heaviest of trials upon me and the sorest that I bear. Will you permit me to remove myself from him?"

The Master said: "Withdrawing from him would be a sign to others. Rather, remain with him, treat him well, and please him with some measure of outward agreement. Keep your secret from him and conceal your affair, for God will suffice us against his evil and the evil of others."

The narrator said: Then the Master and the boy would meet and part for a stretch of their days, the boy perplexed in his affair, knowing nothing but what the law had brought him to stand upon, unable to find where the Master had settled, and unable to seek him out because of the condition laid upon him, while the term lengthened and the Master watched his patience and his endurance of what had been asked of him. At last, on a quiet night when the creation was still and the spies had grown heedless, the two settled together in their assembly. The boy felt his need drawing near and the chance of privacy with his companion at hand, and he fell down before him in prostration. When he raised his head, the Master spoke.

The Master said to him: "I saw you prostrate in thanksgiving. Thanksgiving before the deed breeds suspicion, and thanksgiving cut short after the deed makes the deeds themselves void."

The boy said: "All your words are truth, and all your deeds are mastery. I thanked you only for the kindness that has already come from you, and by that thanks I hoped for more from you."

The Master said: "Thanksgiving is fitting, for gratitude is what makes thanksgiving increase. Your right has been fulfilled. So come to me with your heart, make your understanding present, and listen to what is set before you."

The boy said: "I have brought the instrument of understanding and taken up the tool of hearing. Say what seems good to you."

The Master said: "The religion has a key that opens it and closes it, like that which distinguishes forbidden union from lawful marriage."

The boy said: "This key you have named is great in the sight of Allah, since it is what parts the lawful from the unlawful and truth from falsehood. What is it?"

The Master said: "It is the covenant of Allah, which establishes His rights, gathers His obligations, and makes the Garden binding for His friends. It is the rope of God upon His earth and the safe haven among His servants. I will recite it to you and bind you to it."

The boy said: "Yes. Take from me whatever you love. I do not refuse your opinion, and I do not overstep your command."

The narrator said: Then the Master set about reciting the covenant to the boy, conveying it to him and binding him fast by it, while the boy could not contain himself for awe, his tears streaming from the depth of his instruction, until he reached the end of the covenant. He praised God and glorified Him and thanked Him for what had been granted him, and he knew with certainty that, by accepting their pledge, he had entered the Party of God (حزب الله) and the company of His friends. Then he fell silent in his composure, and the Master began to expound and to make clear.
