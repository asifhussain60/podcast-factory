# Zahir, Batin, and the Seven Natiqs

*Episode six of* The Master and the Disciple. *The architecture has been sealed; the chosen ranks have been mapped onto sun, moon, and stars. Now the author names the seven speaker-prophets and shows how the same numerical signature that organizes the cosmos — sevens, twelves, thirties — organizes the personnel of spiritual transmission. Then he gives the deepest framing of the whole architecture: outer and inner are not two textual layers but the body and the spirit of religion, each subsisting only through the other. And he answers the disciple's question about why the visible world is to be loved as the outer of the inner — pointing, one symbol at a time, from the heaven to the earth to the islands to the rivers, until the disciple can read the visible world the way one reads a book whose pages are written in two scripts at once.*

## Where this episode picks up

Can a person read the visible sky the way one reads a page of scripture — and if so, who taught the alphabet? The Imam is the sun. The gate is the moon. The summoners are the stars. The author now names the inner aspects of the seven heavens. There are seven speaker-prophets, he says — the possessors of determination among the divine messengers — and they are Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, Muhammad (peace be upon them all), and the awaited one who closes the cycle. These seven speaker-prophets are symbolized by the seven heavens — Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, the Sun, Venus, Mercury, and the Moon — and the inner aspect of the seven continents are the Imams of right guidance.

This cycle of seven enunciators is the spine of the early Fatimid cosmology more broadly. Henry Corbin, in his 1951 essay *Cyclical Time in Mazdean and Ismaili Gnosis*, called it the doctrine that "time itself is shaped like an alphabet, and each letter is an Imam." Farhad Daftary's standard history *The Isma'ilis: Their History and Doctrines* (Cambridge University Press, 2nd ed., 2007, pp. 138–141) traces the same seven-name list — Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, Muhammad, the awaited one — through every major Fatimid theologian of the tenth century, including the author of this book. What the author is doing here, then, is not idiosyncratic. He is reciting the load-bearing skeleton of a tradition that organized centuries of teaching.

## The seven Natiqs and the twelves-and-thirties of transmission

Each speaker-prophet, the author says, has twelve chieftains — symbolized by the twelve zodiac mansions. Each Imam has twelve proofs symbolized by the twelve regions of the earth, one proof per region. And each proof has a complement of summoners beneath him. The structure repeats at every rank. The mathematical signature of cosmology — seven and twelve — is the signature of spiritual transmission too.

Then the author adds the thirty. Just as the physical sun governs the days and the nights, and a year of twelve months runs underneath it, and each month runs thirty days — so the spiritual structure has each Imam with twelve proofs, and each proof with thirty summoners. Twelve and thirty. The calendar of the spirit is the calendar of the sun. When the disciple later watches a month roll from new moon to new moon, he is not to read it as merely the orbit of a celestial body. He is to read it as the visible enactment of the structure that the spiritual sun, the Imam, governs above its visible counterpart.

It is through these intermediaries of Allah, the author says, that His argument has reached His creatures, and through them His justice has manifested — outwardly and inwardly. The argument and the justice are paired terms here: an argument that has not reached you cannot be held against you, and a justice that has not been demonstrated to you cannot be the basis on which you stand or fall. The Quran itself puts this insistence at the foundation of revelation: "And We never punish until We have sent a messenger" (chapter on the night journey, verse fifteen — Pickthall translation). The chain of intermediaries is the continuation of that messenger-principle into every generation. Allah's chain is therefore not a parallel teaching system competing with revelation; it is what makes revelation itself answerable, and the disciple bound to the covenant of the foregoing part is now standing inside that argumentative reach. The chain is not a doctrinal accessory. The chain is the means by which Allah's justice itself becomes legible to creation, in both the outer and the inner aspects. Without the chain, there is no completed argument; without the completed argument, there is no demonstrated justice. The hierarchy named earlier and timed here is what makes the universe morally readable.

## Outer and inner — the body and the spirit of religion

Then comes the deepest framing. The inner aspect, the author says, is the true religion of Allah. The outer aspect is the revealed paths — the revealed law — and its symbols. Religion is the soul and the living spirit of those revealed paths, and the ordinances of the revealed law are the body. The body subsists only through the spirit, and the spirit subsists only through the body, which is its covering and a sign pointing to it. Therefore — and this is the line the disciple must hold — there is not a single letter of the inner aspect, nor a friend of Allah, who does not have many visible signs in the outer world.

A near-contemporary Fatimid jurist made the same point in legal language. In the book *The Pillars of Islam* (Asaf A. A. Fyzee translation, Oxford University Press, 2002, Vol. I, pp. 27–28), the chief judge of the Fatimid court writes that "the outer practice without the inner meaning is like a body without a soul, and the inner meaning without the outer practice is like a soul that has no body to walk the earth in." That is the same doctrine our author teaches here, transposed from the language of cosmology to the language of jurisprudence — proof that the outer-and-inner framework was the operating system of the whole tradition, not a private metaphor of one teacher.

Read that doctrine carefully. It refuses, on the one hand, the picture of an esoteric tradition that floats free of ritual practice. The inner without the outer is a spirit without a body, which is no living religion at all; it is a wraith. It refuses, on the other hand, the picture of an exoteric tradition that floats free of inner meaning. The outer without the inner is a body without a spirit, which is a corpse; the ordinances of the revealed law are practiced but they do not live. The doctrine is mutual subsistence. Each rank of intermediary — each speaker-prophet, each Imam, each proof, each summoner — is the place where the spirit and the body meet, and each has visible signs in the outer world because the inner cannot show itself except through the outer.

The Commander of the Faithful, in the sermons gathered as *The Peak of Eloquence*, frames the same mutual subsistence from the side of belief: "Faith is recognition by the heart, confession by the tongue, and action by the limbs" (Sermon 227, Sayyid Ali Reza translation). Recognition by the heart is the spirit; action by the limbs is the body; the tongue is the seam where they meet. To remove any of the three is to disorder the whole. The author of our book is teaching that same anatomy at cosmic scale: an inner that does not surface in an outer is not faith but private fantasy, and an outer that does not arise from an inner is not faith but theatre.

This is the framing under which the disciple's next question makes sense.

## The world is the outer of the inner: symbols, point by point

The young man asked about this lower world — why some pursue it while scholars condemn it. The author answered with a careful inversion. The world, he said, is only the outer aspect of the inner dimension — the outer of the inner. Whoever recognizes the spirit finds the body pleasing. But whoever is ignorant of the spirit and recognizes only the body, for him the body is dead and decaying flesh, which is forbidden.

So scholars "forbade" this world only in the sense that Allah forbade eating the dead. Approached through the lawful sacrifice that the foregoing part used to illustrate the covenant, the body becomes lawful. Approached as merely the body, with no recognition of the spirit that animates it, the body is the dead flesh that is forbidden. Every act without a meaningful outcome, the author said, is child's play; every speech without an essential meaning is falsehood — and Allah is exalted above that. Experiencing this world while recognizing its spiritual counterparts is allowed and recommended. What is forbidden is living for this world alone, as if it were created without purpose and would return to nothingness without accountability.

Whoever strives in this world for its own sake, without awareness of the spiritual world, his striving is aimless wandering. But whoever strives in this world for the next, with faith, his striving has purpose and result, and is met with Allah's acceptance.

Then the author declared that not a single creature — not an insect, not a bird, nothing wet or dry, no mountain, stone, tree, or precious mineral — exists except as a symbol established by Allah. This claim has a direct Quranic warrant. "We shall show them Our signs in the horizons and within themselves, until it is manifest to them that it is the truth" (the chapter on what is made distinct, verse fifty-three — Pickthall translation). The horizons are the outer book; the selves are the inner. The author is reading the verse as a teaching protocol, not as a poetic flourish: the sign-character of every created thing is itself a doctrine to be taught and learned.

Even the tiniest dust mote, the author said, is bound by six sides — front, back, right, left, top, bottom — while the mote itself is the seventh. A sign pointing to the six root-principles from which everything was created, with air encompassing them all as their seventh and their king. The dust mote is a portable cosmology. Pick one up and you have lifted the whole architecture in miniature.

Then, point by point, he answered the disciple's question about which worldly thing symbolizes which spiritual entity.

The Heaven, towering above all and encompassing everything, symbolizes the speaker-prophet, who towers above all spiritual creation by his excellence and knows the inner meanings of the revealed path. The twelve mansions of the zodiac and their stars symbolize the twelve chieftains of the speaker-prophet, and the stars are their summoners. The Earth, a resting place and refuge, symbolizes the gate of the speaker-prophet during his lifetime, and his designated trustee after his death; a refuge who knows the inner interpretation. The twelve islands symbolize the twelve proofs who assist the trustee. The Rivers and Springs symbolize the summoners. The Rulers of the seven heavens are the intermediaries between Allah and the speaker-prophets. The seven Oceans are the intermediaries between Allah and the Imams who follow the speaker-prophet.

Then a finer distinction. Salty water represents the outer knowledge — knowledge that has no support for growth. Sweet, fresh water — from springs, from rain — represents the inner knowledge, conveyed by the summoners. The Quran itself separates the two waters precisely along that line: "And the two seas are not alike; this is fresh, sweet, pleasant to drink, and that is salt and bitter" (the chapter on the originator, verse twelve — Yusuf Ali translation). Water became a symbol for knowledge because water is life for every living being, just as knowledge is life for every scholar. So when the disciple drinks from a spring or shelters from rain or tastes the sea, he is to read the taste as a doctrine. The salt is the surface; the freshness is the depth. Both are water. Both are life. But only one of them feeds the field.

It is worth pausing on how thoroughly this catalogue rewires the disciple's relation to the natural world. Most religious teachings on nature say either "the world is to be enjoyed" or "the world is to be renounced." This teaching says neither. It says the world is to be read. Each element is not an aesthetic experience and not a temptation but a sentence in a sentence. The mountain says one thing; the spring says another; the salt sea says a third. A walk through the world is a reading; a meal is a reading; the weather is a reading. Once the disciple has been given the lexicon, the visible world becomes the open page of a book whose syntax is the chain of intermediaries he has just been taught. He is not asked to abandon the world to read its book. He is asked to live in it as a reader — present, attentive, refusing both the literalism that sees only the body and the dismissal that sees only the spirit.

## Closing

The seven speaker-prophets have been named — Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, Muhammad, and the awaited one — and the sevens, twelves, and thirties of cosmology have been shown to be the same numbers that organize the personnel of spiritual transmission. The outer and the inner have been framed as the body and the spirit of religion, mutually subsisting; the disciple now knows why neither the esoteric without the exoteric nor the exoteric without the esoteric is a living religion. And the visible world, point by point — heaven, zodiac, earth, islands, rivers, oceans, salt and fresh water — has been opened as the outer book whose inner book is the chain of intermediaries. There remains a loftiest symbol in the catalogue — one so exalted that the author refuses to make it a symbol of anything in creation. Air. And what stands behind air, where even the metaphor of air falls silent: the Instance.
