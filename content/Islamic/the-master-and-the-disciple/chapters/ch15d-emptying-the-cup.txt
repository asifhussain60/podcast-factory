# Emptying the Cup

*Episode fifteen of* The Master and the Disciple. *Abu Malik arrives at al-Bakhtari's door with his companions. He is the greatest scholar of the opposing community and he has come on the standard of justice. The riddle that meets him at the threshold prepares him for what is about to happen. Al-Bakhtari calls his son his father; he calls himself the son; he says Salih is with his Lord. Abu Malik thinks Salih has died. He has not. He has been re-born. The episode that follows is the opening of a debate Salih conducts not by overwhelming his opponent but by emptying him — the technical term is kasrah, demolition; the rank in the spiritual hierarchy that performs it is the mukasir, the Demolisher. Salih asks Abu Malik which of three kinds of seeker he is. Abu Malik places himself in the third. Then Salih draws the distinction that will govern everything that follows: between alim and arif, knowledge and gnosis, report and vision.*

## Where this episode picks up

How do you win a debate against the greatest scholar of the opposing community — by filling him with your arguments, or by first emptying his cup? Just before this, Abu Malik and his companions set out for al-Bakhtari's residence the same day. They have repented of their sins, repaid their debts, purified themselves, and ridden out under the saying that there is no power except through Allah. They arrive at the house. They ask after Salih. And the riddle the father gives them at the threshold is the first move of the debate before Salih has opened his mouth.

## The riddle at the door

When Abu Malik asks after Salih, al-Bakhtari does not give a straight answer. He says: "Today Salih is my father, and I am his son, and Salih is with his Lord."

Three reversals in one sentence. The son has become the father. The father has become the son. And Salih, who is alive in the next room, is said to be with his Lord — words a Muslim ear hears as words about a man who has died.

Abu Malik takes the natural reading. Assuming Salih has died, he recites the verse Muslims recite when news of death reaches them — "Indeed we belong to Allah and to Him we shall return" (chapter 2, verse 156). The father's reply gathers the riddle and refuses it. "No. Salih is not dead, nor will he ever die; indeed he abides with Allah for all eternity."

Abu Malik notes the three perplexities. The son has become the father. The Lord exists on earth with a servant. The mortal lives forever. He asks to meet the righteous slave. The father — now, as he has just said, subservient to his own son — goes to ask Salih's permission. Abu Malik has not yet stepped through the inner door and he has already been told, by the structure of his host's first sentence, that he is entering a house where the hierarchies of the world have been re-arranged from inside. The greatest scholar of his community is being asked to meet a young man who, to the household that produced him, is the father; the Lord is present in this house in the company of His servant; and death does not apply to the one Abu Malik has come to debate. The riddle has prepared him for what is about to happen. He does not yet know how completely.

Salih receives them with perfect adab — the manners owed to a guest by a host — and prays that Allah will open the ears of their hearts. He has already named the only opening the debate can use. Then the debate begins.

## Three kinds of seeker and the demolisher's art

Salih's first move is not a thesis. It is a question. Which of three kinds of seeker, he asks Abu Malik, are you? Are you the one who already knows? The one beginning to know? Or the one who desires to know but does not yet?

Abu Malik confesses he does not know the modes of seeking at all. He has been a scholar all his life and he has not asked which kind of seeking he was doing. Salih teaches them to him. Among the treasures of the other world there is the arif — the sanctified scholar, al-alim al-rabbani, the one who seeks the dead to give them life. Then there is the mutalim — the student who has some knowledge already and seeks its final stage. And then there is the one just beginning — the ignorant who recognizes only his own need.

Abu Malik places himself in the third. He has read more books than his entire community combined and he has just told Salih he is the beginner. That placement is the move that opens the door.

The structure of these three categories was not invented by Salih. It echoes a saying recorded in *Nahj al-Balagha* (The Peak of Eloquence), the canonical compilation of the sermons and sayings of the Commander of the Faithful (peace be upon him), edited by al-Sharif al-Radi in the late tenth century. In a conversation with his close companion Kumayl ibn Ziyad, the Commander of the Faithful divides humanity into three: "People are of three kinds — a divine scholar, a learner upon the path of salvation, and the riffraff and rabble who follow every screamer and bend with every wind" (Saying 147, translated by Sayyid Ali Reza, World Organization for Islamic Services, Tehran, p. 533). The first two — the divine scholar and the learner on the path of salvation — track the first two seekers exactly. The third in the saying is the one who has never entered the search at all; Salih's third is the one who has at last recognized his own need. The match is close enough to suggest the categories of seeking were a shared inheritance the early community had been carrying for some time, and that Salih's pedagogical use of them is a way of placing Abu Malik inside a hierarchy Abu Malik himself, as a scholar of the wider community, would have already recognized.

The source steps back and names what Salih is doing. He is applying a basic principle of debate. The principle is called emptying the cup. Salih is not breaking Abu Malik down to humiliate him; he is making him realize that he truly does not know, creating an opening, a vastness, into which what is true can be poured. The technical term for the move is kasrah — demolition. And there is a formal rank in the spiritual hierarchy whose function is defined by this word: the mukasir, the Demolisher. His main function is to demolish existing ideas so that they can be replaced with new ones.

This is, the source says, why Salih spends so much of the debate breaking down Abu Malik's prior beliefs before offering alternatives. It is not intellectual cruelty. It is a recognized spiritual pedagogy. The cup that has been full of one liquid cannot receive another until it has been emptied. The ground that has been hardened around old assumptions cannot receive a new seed until it has been broken open. The student who has not been demolished cannot be rebuilt.

The image rewards careful holding. A scholar of Abu Malik's standing has been polished by years of being right. The polish is the obstacle. He cannot receive the next thing because the surface he has built has nowhere left for it to land. Salih's first three questions have already opened a small hole in the polish. He has not asked Abu Malik what he knows; he has asked him what kind of knower he is. And Abu Malik has answered by placing himself in the beginner's seat. The opening is small but it is real. The rest of the episode will widen it.

## Knowledge and gnosis — alim and arif, report and vision

Then Salih draws the distinction on which the entire debate will turn. He draws it slowly, in the source's own vocabulary, and it is worth following slowly.

There is, he says, a difference between merely knowing and truly understanding. The first is ilm — knowledge. The second is marifa — gnosis. The first belongs to the alim, the knower. The second belongs to the arif, the gnostic.

What is the difference?

"Knowledge is simply a report," Salih says. "True understanding is a vision." Ilm is khabar — the report of something one has heard. Marifa is iyan — direct seeing.

He gives the working example. The report — the case of the alim — is the case of an ignorant person who only learns he is ignorant when a scholar informs him. The scholar arrives, says something, and the ignorant man absorbs the news that he had not known. He has gained information. He has not gained anything else.

True understanding — the case of the arif — is different. True understanding is when a man knows by his own intellect that he cannot find the answers himself; his heart becomes constricted by his ignorance; and his only hope of freedom is to learn. He has not waited to be told. He has felt the ignorance in his own bones. The constriction of his heart is the entry into the search.

The example Salih builds on this is the most pointed in the episode. The alim, he says, knows that heaven and hell exist. The knowledge stays at the level of "good to know." It can be cited in conversation; it can be deployed in argument; it can be turned into a treatise. But the arif experiences a desperation to act. He has a deep realization of what heaven means and a powerful longing to reach it and avoid hell at all cost. The same two words — heaven and hell — have generated two different lives. In one, the words are information stored in the head. In the other, the words have generated a burning in the chest that will not let the body sit still.

The source records the working principle. Many operate at the level of ilm their whole lives. Few develop the desperation of irfan. The scarcity is not because the words are different; the words are the same. The scarcity is because the words have been received differently. The alim received heaven and hell as a report and stored them. The arif received heaven and hell as a vision and was changed.

The distinction has classical roots that long pre-date this debate. The chapter on the throngs (chapter 39, verse 9) asks pointedly: "Say: Are those who know equal to those who do not know? Only those possessed of intellect take admonition" (translated by Ali Quli Qarai, *The Quran with Phrase-by-Phrase English Translation*, ICAS Press, London, 2004, p. 537). The verse opens the gap between two states of the soul — knowing and not-knowing — and treats the difference as the difference between two unequal weights, not merely between fuller and emptier minds. The sixth Imam is reported to have sharpened the same point in a saying preserved in the book *Misbah al-Shari'a* (The Lamp of the Sacred Law), traditionally attributed to him: "Knowledge is not by much narration. Knowledge is a light that Allah casts into the heart of whomever He wills" (chapter 32, "On the Reality of Knowledge"). The first half of the saying refuses to identify knowledge with the recitation of reports; the second half identifies it with an act of God on the heart of the seeker. The alim of Salih's distinction has stayed inside the first half. The arif has been touched by the second.

The same line is drawn three centuries later in the book *Ihya Ulum al-Din* (The Revival of the Religious Sciences) by al-Ghazali, who uses it to warn the student. The scholar who has only accumulated reports is, he says, a danger to himself; the one who has been transformed by what he reports is the only one safe to imitate (Book One, "The Book of Knowledge," ch. 2, translated by Nabih Amin Faris, Sh. Muhammad Ashraf, Lahore, 1962, pp. 33–38). The chest-burning Salih has just named in front of Abu Malik is the mark al-Ghazali will later use to tell the two apart from the outside, when only their work and their lives are left to be read.

Abu Malik confesses he found that intense neediness in his own soul. He has been demolished one step further. The cup has been emptied one step further. He has just admitted — in front of his companions, who have come with him on the standard of justice — that the words his community has been carrying for centuries can be carried in two ways, and that he is now ready to ask which way he has been carrying them.

Salih cautions him against rushing the rebuilding. "Indeed the truth confirms itself by its own excellence and nobility," he says — Salih is praising the hope Abu Malik has just expressed, that disputation might be avoided if truth simply shows itself. But Salih adds the qualification that the source records carefully. "Every seed for which the ground has not been prepared will not grow, and every knowledge for which the hearts of students have not first been purified by proper argument will not flourish." The argument is the preparing. The demolition has been the digging. The seed cannot be planted before the ground is ready. Abu Malik is being told that the demolition is the work.

## Closing

Abu Malik came to al-Bakhtari's door as the greatest scholar of the opposing community. He was met at the threshold by a riddle that re-arranged every hierarchy he carried with him. He was admitted to a room where the young man he had been told to handle prayed first that Allah would open the ears of his heart. He was asked which of three kinds of seeker he was and he placed himself among the beginners. He was given the distinction between knowledge and gnosis, between report and vision, and confessed that the intense neediness of the gnostic was the longing he himself had been carrying without knowing its name. He was cautioned against rushing the rebuilding because the ground had to be prepared before the seed could be planted. The cup has been emptied. The seeker has been named. The distinction has been drawn. The ground has been broken so that a seed can at last be received.
