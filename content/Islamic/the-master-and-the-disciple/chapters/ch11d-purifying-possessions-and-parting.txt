# Purifying Possessions and Parting from the Master

*Episode eleven of* The Master and the Disciple. *The doctrine has been laid down — three dimensions of knowledge, three levels of creatures, the formula opened, the dreams read, the union of inner and outer secured. Now the dialogue turns from doctrine to the most concrete question a disciple can ask: what do I do with what I own? The scholar gives him a rule. Take only what was lawfully acquired; pay zakat from the whole; take a fifth from the finest portion and give it to those most deserving. And the disciple — who has not been a passive student — surprises him by having already divided his possessions into five portions, each with its own pedagogical weight. The scholar refuses his own portion. He instructs the disciple to keep the purification portion as a trust for the Imam. He leaves to consult his own master. And when he returns he finds the disciple changed — pale and weak — and the cause of the change is itself the doctrine the book has been preparing all along. The episode ends with the journey beginning.*

## Where this episode picks up

The disciple has been carried through the union of inner and outer and the doctrine of divine justice. He asks the next question with the practical bluntness of someone who has heard enough doctrine to want to act on it. What of his possessions? What are his obligations regarding what he owns? The scholar's answer comes in steps. Take only what was lawfully acquired, he says, in which no one was wronged. From the whole, pay out zakat — the obligatory purification the disciple already knows. Then a second purification: take one-fifth, khums, from the finest portion that you love most, and spend it on those most deserving. The scholar names the principle that holds the two together. "You purify your possessions through charity just as you purify your body through righteous acts. Righteous acts are a protection; charity is a purification." The remainder the disciple should spend on himself and his family in the accustomed manner, and give the surplus in charity to the needy. It is a complete economy, given in three sentences, and the disciple is ready for it because the inner of the inner has prepared him to hear it.

## The disciple's five portions

The disciple's reply opens what the scholar did not expect. He has already done it. Not the rule as the scholar just gave it, but a more elaborate version that the disciple has worked out — a five-portion spiritual economy, each portion with its own pedagogical weight. He counts them out for the scholar.

One portion he has set aside as zakat — the purification the scholar just named.

One portion he has set aside as kafara, atonement — for the harm he had done his own soul before this dialogue began. And this portion, he tells the scholar, he keeps so that he keeps remembering his sins and avoids the spiritual complacency that comes when atonement is paid once and forgotten.

One portion he calls the shukr portion — the portion of gratitude for the knowledge he has acquired. This one he intends to hold as a trust, untouched, until he reaches spiritual maturity and knows where it should be spent.

One portion he has set aside for the needy among his brothers — and these, he says, he will give by his own hand, so they recognize his generosity rather than receiving anonymously. The detail matters. The disciple has thought about what charity costs the giver and what it gives the receiver, and he has chosen the form that costs the giver the awkwardness of being seen and gives the receiver the dignity of a face.

And one portion he has set aside to support the scholar — the alim rabbani — in his travels. He has saved the smallest and most personal portion for the man who has carried him to the lofty edge.

It is a small map of how a disciple has learned to think. The portions are not equal in amount but in pedagogical role. Purification of the visible. Remembrance of sin so atonement does not become complacency. Gratitude held in trust against the day the gratitude can be properly paid. Generosity that does not strip the receiver of being seen. And love for the teacher. Five portions to do five separable kinds of inner work.

## The scholar's refusal and the trust for the Imam

The scholar is moved to tears. And his answer is the second move of this episode — a refusal, layered with instruction, that quietly relocates the portions toward the figure who has stood behind the dialogue from the beginning.

He refuses his own portion. "I have no need of it. I have left behind worldly possessions and fled from the conflicts and temptations of wealth." The portion the disciple set aside for the man in front of him is not where it belongs.

Then the scholar redirects each of the other portions. The brothers' portion — give it with your own hands, he confirms. Hand to hand, face to face, so the disciple's brothers see him as the disciple intended. The atonement portion — keep it, the scholar agrees. Keep it as the remembrance of your sins that you intended it to be. The gratitude portion — hold it as a trust until maturity, as you said. Continue.

And the purification portion — which the disciple thought he would dispense to the most deserving — the scholar redirects to a recipient the disciple has not yet named. "Keep the purification portion entrusted to him by its rightful master — until you meet him. For he is the most rightful judge, for you and against you."

The disciple asks who this noble person is. And the scholar speaks of the Imam — the one whose right Allah has made obligatory upon every believer, in whose hands are the keys of the heavenly gardens, the guide of angels — ma'alim al-malakut — whose palms reveal the light of the mountain of Sinai, the tur. "I am his spiritual son," the scholar says of himself, "and one of his beautiful works — hasana min hasanatihi — constantly seeking his satisfaction and fearing his wrath." The disciple's purification portion is not the scholar's to receive and not the disciple's to dispense to ordinary recipients. It is a trust for the rightful master, the Imam, in whose name the entire chain of intermediaries the disciple has been hearing about has been transmitting divine speech to him. The five-portion economy the disciple worked out under his own discipline is reframed by the scholar as a five-portion economy that has its terminus in the figure who has been the implied recipient of every doctrine the dialogue has carried.

## Reunion, longing, and the journey ahead

Then the scholar departs. He travels until he reaches his own spiritual father — walid al-akbar — the Sheikh. He narrates the disciple's story. The Sheikh recognizes the disciple's qualities. He is pleased. And he gives the scholar his instruction. "Hurry back with this young man, for I desire for him to become a gateway of loving kindness that Allah may open for the people of his time." The disciple is to become a Bab — the very figure of the architecture, in his time, for his people. The Sheikh has named the trajectory.

When the scholar returns he finds the disciple in a very different state. His body is weak. His complexion is pale. The pallor the previous episode named in the true scholars now appears in the disciple — but here the cause is not vigil over the next world. It is the absence of the scholar in this one.

"That is from the desolation and grief that overcame me after you left," the disciple says. "Your separation made me lonely; and the fear of not being able to meet you again made me sick." The source records the meaning the dialogue has been building toward. These descriptions are descriptions of extreme love. This is ishq, the word for what love does to a lover parted from the beloved. The physical deterioration is not illness. It is spiritual longing in the body — the body recording the soul's grief at the absence of the one through whom the inner has reached it.

The scholar's reply gathers the whole dialogue's tenderness into a single short speech. "Allah knew what was in your heart, so He made your trial easy and placed a special loving mercy in the heart of His friend for you." The Sheikh's instruction is implicitly named here — the friend of Allah is pleased; the trial of waiting is shortened; the disciple's longing has been read and answered. "Start preparing for your journey," the scholar says, "for I am taking you to the doorstep of your ultimate goal — and there is no power but through Allah." The formula opened in the last episode has become the closing benediction of this one. The Imam through whom power flows, the Bab through whom strength is distributed, the rope of Allah held tightly through these — all carried in one familiar line, which means at the end of this dialogue what it has come to mean by the work of the dialogue itself.

Hearing this, the source records, the disciple sighed so heavily with joy and awe that his spirit almost left his body. The episode ends with the breath of a man who is about to be carried toward what he has been seeking the entire book.

There is a small detail in the disciple's pallor worth holding next to the previous episode's reading of the pale scholars. There, the body recorded the soul's vigil over the next world. Here, the body records the soul's grief at the absence of the one who carries the next world into this one. Two pallors, both from concern for what cannot be touched, both legible to the eye that knows how to read bodies. The disciple has now been on both sides of this reading. He has heard the scholar weep over the question of why true scholars look pale; he is now himself the disciple whose paleness has its own cause and its own name. And the name the source gives that cause — ishq — is precise. It is not infatuation with a person; it is the soul's grief at the absence of the channel through which the inner has reached it. The next stage of the journey will reverse that grief by reversing the absence. The scholar is here, again. The Sheikh waits. The doorstep approaches.

## Closing

The dialogue between the scholar and the disciple has reached its closing movement. The five-portion economy has been received and redirected. The Imam has been named as the rightful judge for whom the purification portion is a trust. The Sheikh has authorized the journey and named the disciple's trajectory: a gateway of loving kindness to be opened in his own time. The pallor of the true scholars has appeared in the disciple — and the source has given it a name, ishq, the extreme love that the body cannot hide. The next part of the book turns from the dialogue between scholar and disciple to the journey of disciple and scholar toward the Sheikh, and from the Sheikh toward whatever the doorstep of the ultimate goal will turn out to be. The disciple has been brought to the brink of the meeting that the entire dialogue has been preparing him to bear.
