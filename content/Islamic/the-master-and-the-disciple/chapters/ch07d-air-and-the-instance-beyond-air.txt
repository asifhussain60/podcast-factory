# Air and the Instance Beyond Air

*Episode seven of* The Master and the Disciple. *The architecture of creation has been laid down, sealed into the Bismillah, populated with the chosen ranks, mirrored in sun and moon and stars, framed as the mutual subsistence of zahir and batin, and read out into a catalog of worldly symbols pointing one by one to spiritual entities. There is only one element left to address — the one that has been quietly waiting since the very first concept of this teaching: Air. The first of the seven; the singular and unpaired; the principle that mixes with everything and is encompassed by nothing. The disciple asks about it, and the scholar's answer takes the architecture to its lofty edge. Then the disciple raises a Quranic objection that seems to pull the entire teaching down — and the scholar's answer turns the objection into the doctrine's strongest pillar. And then, the disciple pushes one rung further, and the scholar names what stands behind even air: al-Awan, the Instance of His divine will. There the metaphor breaks. There the dialogue, on this side of the book, ends.*

## Where this episode picks up

The catalog of worldly symbols has finished. The heaven points to the Natiq. The earth points to the Bab. The islands point to the Hujaj. The rivers point to the Du'at. The salty sea points to outward knowledge. The sweet spring points to inner knowledge. One element in the cosmology has been conspicuously absent from the catalog. The disciple notices. He asks about the Air which encompasses everything.

## Air, the loftiest symbol

The scholar replies with a refusal that is itself the doctrine. "The Air," he says, "is too exalted to be a symbol for any creation."

The disciple, working through what he has been told, asks whether Air or the heavens and the earth are greater. The scholar answers without hesitation: "Indeed, Air is greater than both." And then he reaches for a scale of comparisons that any listener can hold in the imagination. The extent of the earth in relation to the Air, he says, is like a seal-ring in relation to a great plain. The atmosphere in relation to the heavens is like a seal-ring in a vast desert. And the heaven in comparison to the full extent of the Air is like an egg in relation to an immense plain. Three magnitudes stacked, each itself dwarfed by the next, and Air containing them all.

"What, then, could symbolize Air?" the scholar asks. "There is none like Air." Its inner meaning is that it is "a sign pointing to the One who has no likeness — neither in the outward aspects nor the inner." Notice the careful logic. Every other element of cosmology has been made to symbolize a rank of intermediary because every other element has a peer. Heaven has a counterpart in earth; sun a counterpart in moon; salty water a counterpart in sweet. The disciple can hold two terms side by side and let the difference between them carry meaning. Air refuses that. Air is alone among the seven. And precisely because it is alone, it cannot be made to point downward — to any rank within creation. It can only point upward. It cannot symbolize anyone in creation, because nothing in creation matches it. The only thing it can point to is the One who has no like.

This is the move the broader Quranic tradition recognizes in a second, more absolute formulation — "There is nothing whatsoever like Him, and He is the All-Hearing, the All-Seeing" (the chapter on consultation, verse eleven; Arberry's rendering). That verse is the apophatic anchor of classical Muslim theology, the line every later school cites when it argues that no created predicate transfers cleanly to the Creator. The scholar's argument with Air does not violate it. He is not making Air into a likeness of God; he is using Air as the sign that nothing in creation can stand level with it, and therefore as the sign whose very incomparability among created things points beyond the order of comparison entirely.

## The disciple's Quranic objection — and the loftiest similitude

The young man raises an objection. He cites the verse — "So do not strike likenesses for Allah, for He knows and you do not" (chapter 16, verse 74). If the Quran forbids making likenesses for Allah, then how can the scholar make Air into a sign for Him?

The scholar answers by citing another verse from the same chapter — "And Allah's is the loftiest similitude" (chapter 16, verse 60). Both verses are true. The doctrine is in the distinction. He says it carefully. Every creation is a symbol with a meaning; the external meaning of symbols is a sign pointing to their inner meaning. Allah forbade that He be compared to anything among His creatures — forbade saying "this is a symbol for Allah" the way one might say "this is a symbol for the Imam," or "for his Hujja," or "for a Da'i." Because their symbols become signs pointing to them and to their respective dignities, and to do that with Allah is to demote Him to a rung in the same series.

But — the scholar continues — symbols are understood according to their meanings. And the meanings have an internal ladder. The lesser symbols point to the greater. The greater point to the highest. The highest point to the Most Exalted. So when the Quran says "And Allah's is the loftiest similitude," it is naming a sign that stands at the top of the ladder of signs — a sign that points to the One above all signs without being a peer of any sign below it.

And that loftiest similitude, the scholar says, is Air. Higher than heaven and earth. Encompassing everything and encompassed by nothing. It is the loftiest similitude, pointing to the One who has no likeness. He did not say "nothing is like Him," the scholar adds, because He has no likeness; rather He meant that the sign pointing to Him is not like other signs. None of the signs pointing to His creations is like His sign. So blessed be Allah, Master of the majestic throne.

Hear the careful point. The scholar is not saying that Air is "like" Allah. He is saying that nothing in creation is like Air — and then that nothing, including Air, is like Allah. The comparison demonstrates incomparability through scale. The argument is: here is the one element of the cosmos so vast that nothing in the cosmos can be compared to it. Now extend that ratio one more step. The One to whom even this incomparable element points stands in a relation to Air that is not even a ratio. The function of the loftiest similitude is precisely to be the sign that announces the failure of signs.

The early Muslim tradition has long held this paradox in tension. A widely transmitted sacred saying — quoted by both Sunni and Shi'i mystical commentators, and given its most influential English airing by William Chittick in *The Sufi Path of Knowledge* (State University of New York Press, 1989, page 391) — reports that God said, "I was a hidden treasure and I loved to be known, so I created creation in order to be known." The created order, in that saying, exists as a sign-system addressed to a knower; and yet the One signed remains hidden. The loftiest similitude is what holds both halves of that saying together. It is the only sign at the top of the cosmos whose very form is to insist that the signified is not on the same scale as the sign — known only as the One who cannot be placed inside the catalog that announces Him.

Consider what this resolves for the disciple. Earlier in the architecture he was taught to read the heaven as Natiq, the earth as Bab, the islands as Hujaj, the rivers as Du'at. Each of those is a peer-to-peer pointing — a visible thing standing for an invisible person, where both stand inside the same series of created things. Allah, the doctrine has insisted from the Originator declarations, does not stand inside the series. So the question naturally arises: must Allah then be insulated from all pointing whatsoever? The verse "do not strike likenesses for Allah" sounds as though the answer is yes. But the same chapter says "Allah's is the loftiest similitude," which sounds as though the answer is no. The scholar's resolution is that both are true and they are about different kinds of signs. Lesser signs — the peer-to-peer signs of the catalog — are forbidden as comparisons for Allah because they would slot Him into a peer relation with His creatures. The loftiest similitude is licensed because it is the only sign in the catalog that is incomparable to its own peers; it cannot slot Allah into a peer relation because it has no peer relations of its own.

## The Instance beyond Air: al-Awan, and the limit of the dialogue

The disciple, climbing further, asks whether anything existed before the Air, between it and Allah. "Yes," the scholar says. "The Instance — al-Awan — the instance of His divine will, through which He created what He willed and commanded what He wished." This is the ultimate limit of the dialogue, the point where even the metaphor of Air breaks down. Air is the loftiest similitude in creation; the Instance is what stands behind Air — the moment, the divine instance in which the will became willed and the command commanded. It is the doorway of irada itself, the place where the three primordial words begin to issue. The scholar names it but does not unfold it.

The Fatimid theological tradition of the tenth and eleventh centuries — the milieu in which this book was composed and circulated — held with unusual rigor that creation issues from a single divine instance which is neither inside time as ordinary moments are, nor outside time as eternity is imagined to be, but is the originating moment of time itself. Paul Walker, in his study *Early Philosophical Shiism* (Cambridge University Press, 1993, pages 81 through 93), describes this strand as a deliberate philosophical discipline: the Instance is named, but not explained, because to explain it in the categories of before-and-after would already be to fold it back into the created order it is supposed to precede. The scholar's reluctance to unfold the Instance further is consistent with that whole tradition's discipline of silence at the highest rung.

And the disciple, hearing the name, pleads. "Now you've carried me into the depths of the ocean of this loftiest assembly. Please return with me to the lower physical world... for I fear for my soul, that my intellect may have led me toward something whose essence I cannot grasp." There is something moving in the disciple's plea. He has climbed the ladder he begged to climb. He has asked for the lofty teaching. And at the very edge he asks to be brought back down — because he can feel that one rung further would not be intellectual ascent but intellectual undoing.

The scholar's reply matches the disciple's honesty. "You climbed high in your questioning, and my answers climbed with you to the very peak of these inner aspects. Therein you lost your vision and your intellect was perplexed. So what state would you have been in if I started unveiling the inner meanings of these inner aspects — batin al-batin — for you? You and I would have been like Moses and the scholar." The reference is to the story of Moses and the figure traditionally identified as Khidr in the chapter of the cave, verses sixty through eighty-two — the story in which Moses asks to follow the scholar and is told plainly, "Truly you will not be able to bear with me patiently" (chapter of the cave, verse sixty-seven; Arberry's rendering), and indeed cannot bear it past the third sign. The scholar is invoking it as a self-aware closure. The teaching has reached its lofty edge. To press further would be to ask the disciple to bear what he is not yet able to bear. The architecture must be allowed to settle in the disciple's heart before the inner of the inner can be opened.

## Closing

This is where Part Two lands. Air, the loftiest similitude, points upward to the One who has no like. The Quranic objection has been answered: lesser signs are forbidden as comparisons; the loftiest similitude is licensed as a pointer that announces its own incommensurability. The Instance — al-Awan — has been named as the doorway of will itself, behind Air, behind even the seven, at the very threshold where the dialogue must fall silent. The disciple has climbed to the height his asking opened, and the scholar has returned with him before vertigo became damage. The architecture of creation has been completed at the height of which any teaching of architecture can be completed in words. The next part of the book turns from cosmology to anthropology — from how the design was made to who can climb back through it, and how. The disciple has been brought to the lofty edge. The descent into the human, and into the spiritual economy of knowledge versus action, begins next.
