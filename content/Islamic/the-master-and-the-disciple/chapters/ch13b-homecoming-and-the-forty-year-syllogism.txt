# Homecoming and the Forty-Year Syllogism

*Episode thirteen of* The Master and the Disciple. *The disciple has been named in the orbit of the Sheikh. Now the Sheikh sends him home. The reasons are three — the Sheikh is about to travel, the disciple has been long away from his father, and what the disciple has received now obligates him to give. The scholar is permitted to accompany him. As they reach the outskirts of the city, the scholar gives his own parting counsel — six brief lines that read like an operating manual for one who calls others to the truth. Then the disciple thanks him for three things, kisses his feet, and goes home to a father who confronts him with the most painful charge a son can be given. The disciple answers with a forty-year syllogism that closes every escape route — and the father, whose heart had been waiting for it, weeps and enters the path. Through father and son, the book records, Allah gives life to a great number of people in that country.*

## Where this chapter picks up

What argument can a son offer a father so complete that forty years of paternal authority dissolve into tears — and the father follows the son onto his path? The young man has been re-born to the Sheikh, named after the seven-day wait, taught the realities pens cannot write, and brought into the circling the source reads as the love of the planets for their sun. He visits the Sheikh regularly with the scholar to study. And the Sheikh, watching, sees that the disciple's roots have been well watered and his branches have grown tall. The student is ready to be sent. We come in at the moment the Sheikh decides to release him — and gives the three reasons that release is now correct.

## The Sheikh releases the disciple

The Sheikh's release has three reasons. First, the Sheikh himself is about to travel; the regular study sessions are about to end on his side as well. Second, the disciple has been long separated from his father, and the father's sorrow is a real and growing thing. The disciple does not get to pretend that doctrine is the only obligation in the world; his father is alive, and the disciple owes him a son's presence. Third — and this is the load-bearing reason — it has now become the disciple's obligation to pass on what this divine favor required of him. What has been received must be transmitted.

The Quran turns that transmission into a covenant, not a courtesy. In the chapter on the family of Imran, verse 187, Allah recalls the pledge taken from those given the scripture: that they would make it clear to the people and not conceal it (Yusuf Ali translation, *The Holy Qur'an*, 1934, p. 173). What is hidden from those who need it is owed back to them. The seven-day rebirth was not for the disciple's private satisfaction; it has put him under a new debt to whatever community his return puts in his path.

The Sheikh adds four counsels of his own. Be mindful of Allah. Uphold the oath by which you have been bound. Safeguard and assist your spiritual father — the scholar at your side. And keep your identity concealed. That last word is not a small one. The disciple is being sent into the world he came from, and the world he came from does not yet have the eyes to see what he has become. He is not to advertise the rank he has been given. The concealment is not shame; it is the protection of a delicate thing from the hands of the careless.

The first counsel is the keystone of the other three. The chapter on the family of Imran, verse 102, gives it the widest possible frame: "O you who believe, fear Allah as He should be feared, and die not except in a state of submission" (Yusuf Ali, p. 152). The book of eloquence collected from the Father of Imams (may God be pleased with him) reads the same instruction as the provision that lightens every other burden — "Whoever is mindful of God, God prepares a way out for him from every difficulty" (Nahj al-Balagha, sermon 157, Reza translation, p. 322). The other three counsels — the oath, the spiritual father, the concealed identity — are arrangements of that one mindfulness in three different situations.

The Sheikh then gives the scholar permission to accompany him. As they reach the outskirts of the city the scholar names his own residence so the disciple will know where to find him. And then the scholar gives his own parting counsel — six lines that read, in the source's own framing, like an operating manual for one who calls others to the truth.

Once you have resolved upon something, entrust yourself completely to Allah.

Speak with words of wisdom when you call others to good.

Do not become angry when you are wronged.

Do not become upset when you are called a liar.

Remain patient with anyone who responds to your call.

Make sure you become the best example of that to which you are calling others.

Each line is a working rule. Resolution entrusts the outcome to Allah and frees the worker from carrying the result; calling speaks wisdom, not noise; the wronged worker does not return anger; the slandered worker does not break under accusation; the responder is met with patience, not impatience at his pace; and the worker himself is the best example of what he is asking others to be.

This is not a manual invented from nowhere. The chapter on the bee, verse 125, gives the template the six lines unpack: "Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and beautiful preaching, and argue with them in ways that are best and most gracious" (Yusuf Ali, p. 686). Wisdom in the speech, beauty in the manner, graciousness in the argument — the scholar has decomposed that single verse into six operating conditions, each one a moment of the call where temper or vanity could break it. The sixth condition closes the ethic. The chapter on signs distinguished, verse 33, asks: "Who is better in speech than one who calls to Allah, works righteousness, and says, 'I am of those who submit'?" (Yusuf Ali, p. 1287). The caller must already be inside what he is calling to. The book of eloquence puts the same standard in plainer language — "The teacher of others is more in need of teaching than they are" (Nahj al-Balagha, saying 73).

The disciple's thanks come in three measures. He thanks the scholar for three things. For the Imam whose argument you have upheld. For the trust-bearer whose pact you have fulfilled. And for this corpse, that you have so graciously given life to. The order is the order of the architecture the dialogue has built: the Imam above, the trust-bearer as the bond by which the Imam's argument reaches creation, and the disciple himself as the dead body the dialogue has revived. The image of life given through right knowledge is itself Quranic. The chapter on cattle, verse 122, asks: "Can he who was dead, to whom We gave life, and a light by which he can walk among men, be like him who is in the depths of darkness from which he can never come out?" (Yusuf Ali, p. 322). The disciple's thanks quote that verse without naming it. At their separation he sighs as though his soul has taken flight, kisses the scholar's feet, and returns home. There his grief is so great that his neighbors grow annoyed by it. The body of a man whose spiritual father has just left him is not a quiet thing.

## The father's confrontation

Then his father confronts him. The confrontation is not gentle and the source does not pretend it is. The charge is the most painful one a father can lay on a son.

"Is this how sons repay their fathers? You chose that stranger over me and supported his cause over the path of your forefathers. If what you found in him is truth, you betrayed me by concealing it. And if it is false, you destroyed yourself."

There are two horns in that charge, and both are sharp. If the son has found the truth, he should not have hidden it from the father who raised him; concealment of the truth from one's own parent is itself a betrayal of the bond by which the parent and child are tied. And if the son has been deceived, then everything the father invested in him — forty years of formation, generosity, expectation — has been thrown away in a moment by a stranger. Either way the son owes an account. The father is not refusing the conversation; he is naming the conversation that must happen.

The Quran has its own model for this kind of conversation between son and father, and the disciple's reply walks its line. In the chapter on Mary, verses 41 through 50, Abraham (peace be upon him) confronts his own father about the worship of objects that hear nothing and see nothing — "O my father, why do you worship that which hears not and sees not and cannot benefit you in anything?" He presses with respect, names the father at every turn, and offers guidance rather than verdict. The disciple here is in the same posture: the bond is honored even as the bond's assumptions are challenged.

He answers with gentleness. He asks for one of two things. Either to be left to his beliefs, remaining an obedient son — the silent option, in which the household keeps its peace and the disagreement is contained. Or to be allowed to debate openly, with the assurance that his father will not become angry. The disciple is offering the father a choice between two ways of carrying their disagreement: hidden, or open. He is refusing to choose for the father.

The father refuses the silence. He will not have the hypocrisy of outward agreement amid inner difference. He chooses the debate. And in choosing the debate, he has handed the disciple the opening the disciple has been waiting for.

## The forty-year syllogism

The disciple does not multiply arguments. He presses one. He calls on the forty years of the father's life that have passed before this day.

Over the past forty years, he says, one of two things must have been true. Either you were a scholar of the truth who withheld my share of your knowledge from me — in which case you have no standing to criticize me for seeking it elsewhere. Or you were ignorant of the truth I have now found — in which case you are even more in need of that stranger than I am, for you should have preceded me in learning from him.

That is a syllogism whose two horns close every door. If the father was a scholar and withheld, the father is at fault. If the father was ignorant, the father is the one who should be running first to the stranger. There is no third position from which the father can stand and condemn.

The shape of this move has a name in the classical tradition. It is a disjunctive syllogism — what later logicians will call a constructive dilemma. Aristotle's *Prior Analytics*, book one, chapter twenty-nine, lays out the same architecture: when two exhaustive alternatives both lead to the conclusion the questioner wants, the answer is forced regardless of which branch the opponent chooses (Hugh Tredennick translation, Loeb Classical Library, 1938, p. 339). The disciple has not invented a new logic; he has applied the oldest move in argument to the specific case of a father who has had forty years to decide which branch he stood on. The argument is not designed to humiliate; it is designed to remove the ground from beneath the position the father has tried to take. It works the way the disciple's own examination at the door of the Sheikh worked. It exposes. It returns the questioner to the bottom of his own cup.

The source records what happens next without sentimentality. The old man's heart was touched. His eyes overflowed with tears at the truth. He too entered the path. And then the sentence that the whole movement has been building toward: through father and son, Allah gave life to a great number of people in that country. The closing sentence has a Quranic echo behind it. The chapter on the table, verse 32, declares: "Whoever saves a life, it shall be as if he had saved the life of all humanity" (Yusuf Ali, p. 254). Two lives saved — father and son — become the seed of a great number in their country, because the seed is the right kind of seed.

It is worth noting what the source does not record. It does not record that the father became a student in the same sense the son had been a student. It does not give the father a long doctrinal apprenticeship. It does not put the father through seven days of waiting for a name. The father enters the path on the strength of the son's argument and on the weight of his own forty years of waiting for something the syllogism finally allowed him to admit he had been missing. The conversion is told briefly because the conversion was brief. The argument did its work because the ground had already been prepared by a son's gentleness, by a father's honesty in refusing the hypocrisy of outward agreement, and by the years of love between them that gave the son's pressing its standing to land. The forty-year syllogism is the visible move; the invisible move is the bond inside which the pressing was allowed.

The disciple has carried home what he was sent home with. The scholar's six counsels are not abstract; the disciple has just used the first four of them in a single conversation. He entrusted his resolution to Allah; he spoke words of wisdom in his calling; he did not become angry when his father charged him with betrayal; he was not upset at being called destroyed by deception. The fifth and sixth — patience with those who respond, and being himself the best example — will be the work of the rest of his life. The forty-year syllogism was the disciple's first test of the manual and it has passed.

## Closing

The Sheikh has released the disciple on three reasons — the Sheikh's travel, the father's sorrow, the disciple's obligation to give what he has received — and bound him with four counsels: be mindful of Allah, uphold the oath, safeguard the spiritual father at his side, keep his identity concealed. The scholar has accompanied him to the outskirts of the city, named his own residence, and given his six counsels — the manual of the one who calls others to the truth. The disciple has thanked him for three: the Imam whose argument was upheld; the pact whose trust-bearer was kept; the corpse to which life has been given. He has come home to a confrontation no son can avoid, offered his father the choice between silence and open debate, and pressed the forty-year syllogism whose two horns close every door. The father has wept and entered the path. The book records what follows in a single sentence: through father and son, Allah gave life to a great number of people in that country.
