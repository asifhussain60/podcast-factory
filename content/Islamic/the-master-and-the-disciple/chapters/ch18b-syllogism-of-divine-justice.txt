# The Syllogism of Divine Justice

*Episode eighteen of* The Master and the Disciple. *Standing on the disciple's confession that intellects are bewildered before Allah, the master now builds a formal argument from divine justice. Three premises, Allah cannot be seen, cannot be heard directly, and cannot be unjust, yield the necessity of just witnesses between Him and His creatures. The master names the rank-order of these witnesses, the equality of their reward, the subordinate's share in the unseen through esoteric interpretation, and the wisdom that does not let the divine command be cut off from the servants. The disciple affirms it as the very foundation of justice.*

## Where the argument stands

The preceding stretch of the dialogue left the disciple in honest bewilderment. His words had left him. His intellect could not weigh Allah. His imagination could not picture Him. The standard moves of his school, names, attributes, the analogy to a craftsman, had all been shown to forge a likeness or to settle for words instead of realities. The position is not nothing. It is the position from which the next stage of the argument can be built. A man who has dropped his own resources is in a position to be led step by step. The master now leads him.

The argument that follows is the backbone of the book's call. It is not given as a hammered conclusion. It is given as a chain of questions, each of which the disciple must answer for himself. The master is not trying to defeat him with a proof. He is trying to walk him through the proof so that the proof's conclusion is something the disciple says, not something the disciple concedes.

## The three premises

The master begins. "Can the One of unimaginable immensity be perceived by our vision?" "No," the disciple answers. "He is far above that." Then can we hear His speech? "No," the disciple says, "for speech comes from a speaker described by his speech, and Allah is beyond such attributes." Two of the three premises are now in place. The senses cannot reach Him. The voice of His speech is not a voice we can directly hear, because a voice belongs to a speaker we could describe, and Allah is beyond every such description.

Then comes the third premise, the one the whole argument turns on. Would this All-Embracing, Exalted One be unjust to His creatures, imposing on them what they cannot bear, commanding what they cannot perceive, then punishing them for failing to do what they had no way of doing? The disciple gives the only honest answer his own creed permits. "Anyone who imposes such an impossible obligation cannot be just," he says. "And Allah is far exalted above that."

The three premises are now in his mouth. He cannot see Allah. He cannot hear Allah. Allah is not unjust. The conjunction is the lever. If the first two are true and the third is true, then there is something the third requires. The very justice that the disciple has just affirmed forbids the situation the disciple has been describing. A community that cannot see or hear Allah, and yet is held to His command, must be reached by Him through something else. The justice of Allah is the premise that makes the intermediary necessary. The intermediary is not a luxury that piety adds; the intermediary is what the justice itself requires.

The Quran states this lever in one sentence. In the chapter on the night journey, verse fifteen reads, in the standard English rendering, "We do not punish until We have sent a messenger" (Quran 17:15, Abdullah Yusuf Ali translation, in *The Holy Quran: Text, Translation and Commentary*, p. 698). The verse is the third premise stated as a law. Allah does not levy a punishment on a creature who had no way of learning the command. The whole argument the master is building has its anchor in that single Quranic phrase. The chapter on women makes the same point from the other side, verse one hundred sixty-five names the function of the messengers as the closing of every excuse, so that "mankind would have no argument against Allah after the messengers" (Quran 4:165, Yusuf Ali translation, p. 234). The justice the disciple has just affirmed is the same justice the revealed book names. The two are not different doctrines; they are the same doctrine arrived at from two directions, the master's by argument from premise, the verse by direct statement.

## The necessity of intermediaries

The master now draws the consequence, slowly. "Would the One removed from injustice be pleased with ignorance as a companion for His servants? Did He create them as perfect scholars, or as ignorant?" The senses point one way. Children are born knowing nothing. Communities have to be taught what they know. The starting condition of the human creature is ignorance. So the master's question answers itself. Allah did not create them as perfect scholars. They were created not-knowing.

Then how, the master asks, did they receive the knowledge of the gratitude desired from them, so that they could earn His pleasure and amnesty from His wrath? They are ignorant by creation. He is the standard. He cannot be directly seen or heard. If they were left to themselves, they would be punished for not doing what they had no way of learning. That would be the injustice the disciple has just denied.

So the master names the only path that preserves the justice. "Such knowledge could not be perceived except through intermediaries between Him and His creatures. Their existence is His act of justice, for and against His creation. He selected them from His knowledge, purified them above the rest, sent them as messengers and arguments, and made them just witnesses between Him and His servants. Willing obedience to them becomes part of obeying Allah; disobeying them entails His wrath. Otherwise, how else would the divine command extend from Allah to His creatures?"

The closing sentence is the test. If no intermediary, then no extension of the command. If no extension of the command, then either no command or no creature held to it. The first denies Allah's lordship; the second denies the obligation the disciple has been defending all his life. The intermediary is what saves both lordship and obligation from collapsing into each other.

The same lesson is carried forward in a famous saying of the Commander of the Faithful preserved in *The Peak of Eloquence* (Saying One Hundred and Forty-Seven in the standard English rendering, pp. 504-505). Pressed by a student about who in the present world stands between Allah and His servants, he answers that the earth is never left void of a standing argument for Allah, whether seen by people or hidden from them, lest the divine proofs and clarifications be nullified. The argument the master is making is the same argument in another voice. To say the earth is empty of a witness is to say the proofs have been nullified, and the justice the disciple has just affirmed will not let that be said.

The disciple affirms what he has been brought to. "This is the very foundation of justice." The phrase is not a debating concession. It is a recognition. He has been talking about justice his whole career; he has not, until this moment, seen that the very thing he has been talking about requires what the master has now made him say.

## Unbroken judgment, ranked witnesses, and esoteric interpretation

The master now extends the principle into time. Allah's wise judgment is one and unchanging. He would not point out His command to one group through a just witness and impose the same command on another without one, for justice is unified by its nature. If a judgment had two meanings, present here, absent there, one of the two meanings would be unjust. So the principle that produces the witness in one age produces a witness in every age. The continuous succession of messengers necessarily imposes on every age the duty of willing obedience. There is no period of history in which the obligation operates without the means of meeting it.

Within that succession the master names a rank-order. The witnesses are not all equal in the same way. They have ranks, one subordinate to another. Yet, and this is the move the school will not have heard, all are equal in reward. The hierarchy is in function, not in dignity. The superior messenger is distinguished by his knowledge of the unseen in the revealed book. The subordinate, the one entrusted with the book after its bringer, is distinguished by his knowledge of the unseen in his esoteric interpretation of the book. Both the revealed word and its interpretation come from Allah through revealed inspiration. The interpretation is not human invention added to the divine word; it is a second channel of the same divine knowledge, given to a second class of witness.

The master illustrates with names from the chain of the prophets. Allah's inspiration came to Lot by means of Abraham, and to Ishmael and Isaac by means of the same chain, while the blessing and the excellence were assigned to Abraham. The subordinate is called subordinate only because of his obedience to, and need for, his superior. It is not that the subordinate is less filled with the divine; it is that the subordinate is filled by way of the superior. The order is functional. The reward is equal. The chain is one.

It is worth dwelling on what the rank-and-reward distinction does to the school the disciple comes from. The school is used to thinking of a single prophet at the top, and of everyone else as standing at a flat distance from him. The master's picture is different. It is a chain in which every link is divine, and in which the divine reaches the lower link by way of the upper. The picture explains a feature of the prophetic histories the school has had to flatten. Lot is a prophet, but the source of his guidance is Abraham. Ishmael and Isaac are prophets, but the channel runs through the household of Abraham. The blessing and excellence are assigned to the head; the function of the limbs is real; the reward is whole. This is not a demotion of the lesser figures. It is a recognition that the divine economy works through a chain, not a broadcast, and that the chain has shape.

The same picture explains why the school's most acute argument against the master's tradition, that the role of esoteric interpretation reads novelty into the text, misses what the master is actually claiming. The master is not saying that the one entrusted adds a meaning to the book from outside the book. He is saying that the meaning the entrusted one carries was always in the book, and that the divine inspiration which placed the meaning in the book also produced a class of witness whose function is to carry that meaning forward. Outer and inner are two sides of one divine deposit. The superior carries the outer. The subordinate carries the inner. The inner is not the superior's leftover. It is its own discipline, given by its own inspiration, balanced against the outer in the divine plan from the start.

Modern scholarship on the tradition this book speaks for has stated the same shape with unusual clarity. Henry Corbin, in *Cyclical Time and Ismaili Gnosis* (translated from the French by Ralph Manheim and James Morris, Kegan Paul International, 1983, pp. 84-90), describes the tradition's prophetology as a doctrine in which each speaker-prophet brings the outer word and an entrusted successor receives the inner sense at the same act of inspiration, so that the pair, not the single bringer, is what the divine economy actually deploys. Farhad Daftary, in *The Ismailis: Their History and Doctrines* (Cambridge University Press, second edition, 2007, pp. 91-93), describes the same pairing as the architecture by which the community has always read the revealed text: an outer transmitter and an inner interpreter, working in lockstep, each indispensable to the other. The picture the master is laying out here is the picture the tradition's modern interpreters describe as its center. It is not a peripheral devotional move. It is the doctrine of how the divine command actually reaches the world.

Then comes the closing principle, which sets up everything that depends on it. "In His justice, He does not like His commands to be cut off from His servants after His blessing to them, for that would stop their worship and legitimize ignorance." The line forecloses the move every late community wants to make. The move is to say: yes, there was a chain; yes, it produced witnesses; but it has stopped. The master says the very justice that produced the chain forbids the chain from being cut. To say the chain has stopped is to say that Allah's blessing has become a withdrawal of the means of worship; that He has built up His creatures' duty and then removed the only way they could discharge it. The justice that the disciple has just affirmed does not permit that picture. The chain continues, or the justice fails. There is no third option.

## Closing

What has happened in this stretch of the dialogue is something the school the disciple comes from did not see coming. The vocabulary it shared with the master, Allah is one, Allah is just, has been used to derive a conclusion it had not been willing to draw. From its own premises, the conclusion is forced. Allah cannot be sensed; Allah cannot be unjust; therefore Allah reaches creatures through chosen witnesses; therefore those witnesses are ranked but equal in reward; therefore the superior carries the revealed text and the subordinate carries the esoteric interpretation; therefore the chain does not stop, because if it stopped the justice that began it would have collapsed in the very act of completing it. The disciple has called this the very foundation of justice. The Quranic anchor, that Allah does not punish before sending a messenger, and the saying of the Commander of the Faithful that the earth is never void of an argument for Allah, both stand behind what the disciple has just affirmed. The argument is no longer the master's argument. It is the disciple's argument, in the disciple's mouth, drawn from the very premises the disciple came in defending.
