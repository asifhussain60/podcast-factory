# Hawl and Quwwa, and the Dream of Joseph

*Episode nine of* The Master and the Disciple. *The disciple has just said the line that every Muslim says: there is no power and no strength except through Allah. The scholar takes the line apart. First he surfaces the common interpretations of the two words — hawl as "a year," quwwa as "mules" — and shows how unbearable the formula becomes if the common readings are taken seriously. Then he gives the inner meaning: hawl is the Imam who transforms divine speech into human words; quwwa is the Bab who bears those words and disseminates them. And then he reads the dream of Joseph — eleven stars, the sun, and the moon — and the king's dream of the seven cows and seven ears, as the same architecture in another register. By the end of the episode the disciple has heard the formula he has been reciting all his life turned into a doctrine of the chain through which knowledge reaches him at all.*

## Where this episode picks up

The disciple has just spoken the formula. There is no power and no strength except through Allah. The scholar takes the words seriously enough to ask the disciple what he thinks they mean. "What are that power and that strength which exist only through Allah?" The disciple, who has been a careful student, offers the common interpretations he has heard. He says that hawl means "a year," and cites the Quran's reference to mothers suckling their children for two complete hawls — two complete years. He says that quwwa refers to mules, citing the verse about the treasures of Qarun where the heavy keys required a band of strong men, and where "band" was said to mean forty men and "strength" to mean forty mules. He has reported the common readings honestly. The scholar lets the readings stand for one breath and then makes them absurd. "Then 'there is no power and no strength except through Allah' would mean 'there is no year and no mule except through Allah.'" The disciple smiles inwardly at the absurdity and admits the readings are weak. He asks for the true inner meaning. And the scholar gives him the principle that authorizes the whole inquiry. "People do not utter a single word of truth without its having a source in the inner dimension. Nor is there any falsehood that did not once have a real meaning — only those words were turned away from their original meaning and so became absurd."

## Hawl and quwwa as Imam and Bab

The scholar then takes the formula apart in the same way he took the verse of the garment apart in the last episode. Hawl, he says, refers to the Natiq of Allah, or to his Imam — the master of the twelve chiefs, the Nuqaba, just as the year as hawl is completed only by twelve months. The numeric signature gives the doctrinal identification. The Imam is called hawl because he transforms and conveys — that is the literal sense of the Arabic root, tahwil, conversion — the Creator's speech. He converts the subtle essential meanings of divine speech into human language. The words of wisdom that human beings can hear and recite are the body and form; the Creator's speech itself is their true spirit. The Imam is the converter between spirit and body, between subtle essence and human word.

The scholar then walks the communications model of the whole hierarchy. The Creator's speech is pure spirit. The Imam, as the hawl, converts it into the body and form of words of wisdom. The Bab then condenses that body and distributes it to the Du'at in the detailed form suited to each Da'i's level. It is the structure of a general giving orders to captains, who pass them on to soldiers. Each rung receives speech in the form its level can bear and passes it to the next rung in the form that rung can bear.

Quwwa, the scholar continues, is the Bab. The Bab is the gateway to whom Allah has given strength to bear His mighty words. The Bab takes the divine speech from the Imam in its condensed, all-inclusive form — its mujmal form — and disseminates it to the Du'at in more detailed and particular form. So the Hujja is called strength, but has no strength except through Allah. The formula reveals itself. "There is no power and no strength except through Allah" means there is no Imam and no Hujja except through Allah. The everyday line said in difficulty has been the architecture of the chain of intermediaries the entire time.

The scholar offers one more refinement that locks the formula into the cosmology already taught. Hawl has to do with scheming and strategy — a function of the spirit and the mind. Quwwa has to do with physical capability. The mind devises a strategy; the body executes it with strength. There are two contexts in which the formula reads, the scholar says, and both are true. In the first context, the Natiq is the hawl who provides the strategy by transforming divine speech, and his Wasi is the quwwa who bears and disseminates it. In the second context, the Imam is the hawl and his Hujja is the quwwa. So the line la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah means that neither the Natiq nor the Wasi — and neither the Imam nor the Hujja — has the capacity to provide or to disseminate revelation except as Allah enables it. The formula is a confession that the entire chain of chosen ranks is itself dependent at every link.

## Joseph's dream and the king's vision

This, the scholar says, is why Joseph spoke of his dream to his father as he did. "Indeed I have seen eleven stars and the sun and the moon prostrating to me." Chapter twelve, verse four. By the sun, Joseph meant the Imam. By the moon, he meant his Hujja and Bab. By the eleven stars, he meant the eleven Nuqaba. And when Joseph's brothers said, "Joseph and his brother are more beloved to our father than we, while we are a clan" — chapter twelve, verse eight — they were not children jealous over their father's affection. They were referring to themselves as the twelve Nuqaba.

The doctrinal point the scholar then makes is precise and easily missed in the literal reading. The twelve brothers of Joseph were not his biological brothers. They were the twelve members of the cabinet of Isaac, the prophet of the time. The sun of Joseph's dream was Isaac himself. The moon was Joseph's own Bab. And the eleven stars were the remaining cabinet members beside Joseph. Joseph's dream therefore revealed that his rank would be elevated above all twelve members of Isaac's cabinet, including Isaac himself. The jealousy of the brothers was the jealousy of cabinet members over rank — not a family quarrel — and Allah's affair was fulfilled, ending in their remorse and their repentance. A dream of stars and luminaries has become a dream of the chosen ranks the architecture of the book has been naming all along.

The scholar then turns to the second great dream of the same surah — the dream of the king. Seven fat cows eaten by seven lean ones; seven green ears of wheat alongside seven dry ones. The vision, the scholar says, is like the outer dimension of religious action — a mirage that yields nothing when you reach it. Then he reads it. The seven fat cows are Allah's seven heavenly intermediaries between Him and the Natiq. The seven lean cows are the seven Natiqs themselves, hungry for what comes to them from Allah through those intermediaries. The seven green ears are the seven intermediaries between Allah and the Wasi — fresh, full of their healthy ingredient. The seven dry ears are the seven Wasis themselves, who would dry up without their sustenance from above. And with respect to the foundation of Islam, these symbols are like a seed grown into seven ears of grain — from whose seed have come seven Imams.

The shape the disciple is meant to catch is the shape the whole episode has been teaching. A formula recited in difficulty is the chain of intermediaries. A boy's dream of luminaries is the chain of intermediaries. A king's dream of cattle and grain is the chain of intermediaries. The cosmology of Part Two has not been left behind in Part Three; it has been carried into the very grammar of how Allah's speech is heard, and into the dreams through which He has always been making the same single architecture visible.

The disciple, by the close of the king's-dream reading, has been given a small interpretive technique he can carry into the rest of the book and the rest of his life. Numbers in Quranic narratives are not decoration. When a number signals the architecture — seven, twelve — the narrative is naming the chain. Joseph's eleven stars plus a sun and a moon do not add up to the twelve members of a literal family; they add up to the twelve Nuqaba plus the figure above them. The king's seven fat and seven lean cows, with seven green and seven dry ears beside them, are not a riddle about a coming famine alone; the famine is the outer of the inner, and the inner is the seven-and-seven structure of the cosmology already named. The scholar is teaching the disciple to read the visible content of a vision as the body of a meaning, in just the way the verse of the garment in the previous episode taught him to read clothing as a body of meaning. The same hermeneutic. The same architecture. The same Allah giving the same shape to His messages in every register. The disciple has been taught to read once and given keys to keep reading. Every later verse, every later dream, every later formula recited in difficulty can be opened in the same way; the doorway the scholar has opened in this episode is the doorway through which the rest of the listener's own life can be read. The formula la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah will now be heard, every time the disciple hears it, as the confession of an entire chain — and as the gentle daily reminder that the chain itself is held only by Allah.

## Closing

The episode lands here. The formula la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah is no longer about years and mules. It is the confession that the Imam, who transforms divine speech, and the Bab, who bears and disseminates it, have no strength of their own; the entire chain is dependent at every link. Joseph's dream and the king's dream are read into the same architecture. And the next episode will turn from the inner of the inner that has been opened here to the urgent practical question that follows: if the inner is everything, what then of the outer? Of prayer? Of fasting? Of the shari'a the disciple has been observing all his life?
