# The Conspiracy Formula

*Episode nineteen of* The Master and the Disciple. *If divine justice requires a living witness in every age, why do the learned of this community sit in their assemblies as if there were none? The disciple asks the decisive sociological question. Salih answers with a formula — three actors in every period, a rebellious devil, a willing oppressor, and a hypocritical religious scholar — whose collaboration severs the outward traces of prophecy from its living continuation. He traces the formula across communities, names this community as the latest case, and then turns the consensus argument back: by that very logic, the earlier communities have the greater claim.*

## Where the argument stands

The preceding stretch of the dialogue had put the disciple on the foundation of justice. He had said it himself: a chain of just witnesses in every age is what divine justice requires. The chain, Salih had added, must not be cut, because cutting it would legitimize the very ignorance the chain exists to remove. Standing on that, the disciple asks the decisive question of the whole debate. It is the question every honest person asks at this point, and the source records it word for word.

"What is the matter with these people who sit in their assemblies pretending to resemble the prophets of resolution, agreeing that they alone uphold Allah's command? How is it that the learned scholars of this community have abandoned inquiry?"

The question is not theoretical. It is about a community the disciple can name, in cities he has walked through, with scholars he has debated. He is asking why what he can see does not match what he has just admitted must be the case. Salih's reply does not retreat into theology. It is a piece of social analysis.

## The formula — three actors in every period

"They no longer inquire as they should," Salih says, "because it has been imbued in their hearts that they are the true possessors of Allah's command, and that there will be no prophet after their prophet. This is exactly what was said by those who strayed before them, for every community claims there is no prophet after its own."

The diagnosis names a belief, not a doctrine. The belief that no prophet will come after ours is not held because anyone has shown it must be true; it is held because the community has been settled in it. Settledness is the engine. Once a community is settled, inquiry becomes redundant; redundancy becomes suspicion; suspicion becomes prohibition. The scholars who once would have asked, stop asking; the scholars who never asked, are confirmed in their non-asking; and the surface of the community closes.

Then Salih names the mechanism that produces the settledness. The formula is precise.

"Every period has started with three types — a rebellious devil, a willing oppressor, and a hypocritical religious scholar."

The three are distinct functions. The rebellious devil produces the temptation. The willing oppressor produces the force. The hypocritical scholar produces the legitimation. None of the three could do the work alone. The temptation without the force does not last; the force without the legitimation does not hold; the legitimation without the temptation has nothing to dignify. Together they form a closed system. The temptation makes the falsehood attractive. The force makes opposition costly. The legitimation makes acquiescence look like virtue.

The Quran names this triangular pressure on the masses directly. In the chapter on the confederates, verse sixty-seven puts the complaint into the mouths of the followers themselves on the day of accounting — "Our Lord, we obeyed our chiefs and our great ones, and they misled us from the way" (Quran 33:67, Abdullah Yusuf Ali translation, in *The Holy Quran: Text, Translation and Commentary*, p. 1135). The verse names two of the three actors with audible precision — the chiefs are the oppressors, the great ones are the scholars whose authority dignified the chiefs — and reports the followers complaining of a misleading they did not analyze at the time. Salih's formula is the same triangle viewed from above rather than from below. The chapter on the table spread sharpens the indictment one notch further. Verses seventy-eight and seventy-nine warn that a prior community was cursed not because every member transgressed but because the scholars "did not forbid one another from wrongdoing they used to commit; evil indeed was the thing they did" (Quran 5:79, Yusuf Ali, p. 269). The silence of the learned, not only the action of the wicked, is what brings the curse. The hypocritical scholar of Salih's formula is the same figure the verse condemns — a man who could have spoken and chose not to.

The Prophetic tradition extends the diagnosis to a recognizable type. The well-attested hadith of "the learned of corruption" warns that "there will come upon people a time when nothing remains of Islam except its name and nothing remains of the Quran except its script; their mosques will be full of buildings but empty of guidance; their learned ones will be the worst people under the sky — from them strife will go out and to them it will return" (reported in al-Bayhaqi, *Shu'ab al-Iman*, vol. 2, no. 1763, ed. Abu Hajar Muhammad Sa'id Zaghlul, Beirut: Dar al-Kutub al-'Ilmiyya, 1990, p. 312, on the authority of the Prophet, peace be upon him). The hadith is the type-portrait of the third actor in Salih's formula. The buildings remain; the guidance is gone; the surface of religion is intact; the learned are the very conduit through which strife circulates. Salih's analysis is not a novelty. It is a reading of a warning that the tradition itself had placed at the center.

The work the three do is a single act of severance. They cut the outward traces of prophecy from its living reality so that they can exploit those traces as a commodity. The traces remain — the books, the rituals, the names of the prophets, the inherited prestige of a sacred history — and they are now controlled by the three. The living reality, the function the chain of just witnesses was supposed to perform, has been removed. The community is left with the surface and not the source. The community thinks it has the religion. It has the artifact of the religion.

The propagation runs through one slogan. "No prophet after our prophet." Salih names exactly what that slogan does inside a community. It insinuates that no one superior to their own prophet will ever come. It does not need to be argued. It needs to be repeated. Repeated long enough, it removes from the community even the appetite to seek. People abandon seeking true religion without realizing that they have abandoned anything. They grow attached to their own prophet, deny those who come later, and imagine that the denial itself brings them closer to him. The denial of further prophecy becomes a form of loyalty. So the devils who lead astray become established, the oppressors gain the power to kill new prophets, and the evil scholars dignify the killing as zeal.

## The historical roll call

Salih now traces the formula across communities — not as a digression, but as proof that the formula is not a slander against one community but a description of a recurring social fact.

The Zoroastrians cling to their fire-temples, declaring that Allah will never send a messenger after Zoroaster. The community of the Torah tends to their calf and submits to their judges, declaring that Allah will never send a messenger after Moses. The community of the Gospels venerates its priests and worships its prophet as God, declaring that Allah will never send a messenger after Jesus. The list is patterned. Each community has its own version of the artifact — fire-temple, calf, priesthood — and each has its own version of the slogan. The artifacts differ. The structure is the same.

"And now your community has followed in the ways of those who preceded them." The line is the heart of the indictment. They have inherited the book from those unworthy of it. They have followed the seductions of their leaders. They have established prayers at the wrong times and paid alms to the undeserving — all while proclaiming that Allah will never send a messenger after their messenger. The same three actors are at work. The same slogan is being repeated. The same outward traces have been severed from the same living reality. The community thinks it is exceptional. The pattern says it is the latest case.

Salih closes the diagnosis with the social verdict. "That is how you and those like you followed the great majority, terrified by scholarly consensus and the tyranny of rulers." The two pressures are the same two functions named above — the legitimation of the hypocritical scholar and the force of the willing oppressor — now experienced as consensus and as tyranny. The community is not following its scholars because the scholars have shown them anything. It is following its scholars because dissent from consensus is socially expensive, and because the rulers will make it more expensive still.

The Commander of the Faithful had already named this pair of pressures as the engine of religious decay. In the collected sermons attributed to him, he warns that "the ruin of the religion comes about by two — a corrupt scholar and an ignorant worshipper; the worshipper deceives the people with his worship, and the scholar deceives them with his corruption" (attributed to the Commander of the Faithful in al-Sharif al-Radi's *Nahj al-Balagha*, short sayings, no. 110; cited from the William Chittick translation, *A Shi'ite Anthology*, Albany: SUNY Press, 1981, p. 75). The saying isolates the same third actor Salih has named — the corrupt scholar — and adds that even the surface of piety, the worship of the ignorant, can collaborate in the same severance. A community can be visibly religious in two registers — sermons in its mosques and prostrations in its homes — and yet be drained of inquiry. The Commander of the Faithful is naming the social condition Salih is naming.

## Turning consensus back

Salih now performs a move that is rare in a sectarian debate. He takes the very logic the disciple's community uses to certify itself and runs it against itself.

"If we must acknowledge this community on the basis of its consensus that Allah has cut off His proofs, then the earlier communities are even more deserving of acknowledgment — on three points. They preceded you in adhering to Allah's religion. You confirm the truth of what their prophets brought, but they confirmed it before you did. And you all attest that they followed the true messengers, while none of them attests to your prophet. So they hold precedence through your mutual consensus, while you stand alone."

The argument is brutal because it is honest. The argument from consensus does not in fact favor the latest community. It favors the earliest. The earlier communities are confirmed by all later ones — Salih's community confirms their prophets, the Zoroastrians do not bend on theirs, the people of the book do not bend on theirs — but no earlier community confirms the latest. By the very metric the disciple's school appeals to, the latest community has the weakest case.

Then Salih removes the metric itself. "However, if the Creator is the One who judges wisely, abrogating and confirming as He wills, then everything these groups say collapses. For He has more right to create and command, and is engaged in a new work every day — so His actions could not be denied even if He sent a warner every day."

The collapse is total. If you appeal to community consensus, the earlier communities beat you. If you appeal to divine sovereignty, no community can close the chain. There is no logical seat left in which "no prophet after ours" can be defended. The consensus argument is either turned against the user or rendered void by the Creator's freedom.

It is worth pausing to mark the form of this move, because it is not the form a polemicist usually takes. A polemicist who wants to win would have stopped at the historical roll call. The roll call already does most of the rhetorical work: it lines up the audience's neighbors as foils and lets the audience feel the comfort of being the latest in a chain of failures. Salih does not take that comfort. He uses the audience's own logic against itself, and then he removes the logic as a logic. The audience is left without an exit. They cannot say their community is the exception, because their own consensus argument no longer favors them. They cannot say the question is settled by divine decree, because divine decree, if it is to mean anything, is not a closure but an openness to whatever new work Allah wills next. The two doors are shut. The only door left is the one Salih has been opening since the beginning of the debate: the door to the living witness whose chain was never cut, and whose absence from the surface of the community is not absence from the world.

There is also a quiet pastoral observation buried in this stretch of the dialogue that is easy to miss in the speed of the formula. Salih has not said that the average member of these communities is wicked. He has named three actors at the top — devil, oppressor, scholar — and described what happens to the community downstream. The members are described as terrified by consensus and tyranny. Terror is not the same as conviction. The community is not following because it believes. It is following because it is afraid. The diagnosis is harsher on the three actors than on the followers, and softer on the followers than the followers' own scholars are on each other. That asymmetry is itself part of why the disciple can hear it. The diagnosis is not asking him to despise his community. It is asking him to see clearly where the pressure on his community is coming from, and where the way out is.

## Closing

The conspiracy formula is one of the book's most distinctive contributions, and the order in which the source unfolds it has been preserved here. The three actors. Their joint product. The severance of the outward from the living. The slogan that holds the severance in place. The historical roll call that shows the formula is not directed at one group but describes a pattern of severance any community can fall into. The consensus argument turned around. The collapse of every appeal except the Creator's freedom to abrogate and confirm. The disciple, who has been on the foundation of justice since that earlier admission, is now standing on a diagnosis that names his own community as the latest case.

It is worth registering how unusual this formula is in the surrounding tenth-century literature. Modern scholarship on the early call has noted that the author of this book belongs to a generation of teachers who were already systematizing what they had inherited from the chain of speaker-prophets into a recognizable philosophy of history. Henry Corbin observed that the author's circle treated the recurring failure of communities as a structural feature of the cycle of prophecy, not a contingent misfortune — the surface always closes, the inner reality is always carried by the smaller chain (Henry Corbin, *Cyclical Time and Ismaili Gnosis*, trans. Ralph Manheim and James Morris, London: Kegan Paul International, 1983, pp. 84–87). Farhad Daftary, in his standard history, locates the same period as the moment when the call's teachers were articulating the cosmological and social roles of their hierarchy with the precision a literate audience now demanded (Farhad Daftary, *The Isma'ilis: Their History and Doctrines*, 2nd ed., Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2007, pp. 161–164). Salih's three-actor formula is the social face of that articulation. The closure of the surface and the survival of the chain are the same event seen from opposite sides — what the formula explains as a conspiracy of three, the doctrine of the cycle explains as the function of the witness who is never absent.

What follows in the book picks up the consequence — that the absence of the witness in his own time is not the silence of heaven but the concealment of a chain that was never broken.
