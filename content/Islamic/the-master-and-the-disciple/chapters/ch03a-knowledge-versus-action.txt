# Knowledge versus Action

*Episode three of* The Master and the Disciple. *The disciple has taken the covenant and learned the architecture of creation. The dialogue now turns inward. The author moves from the cosmology to the spiritual economy of the seeker: the three levels of knowledge mapped onto the three orders of creature; the inner reading of the formula every Muslim recites — there is no power nor strength except through Allah; the dreams of Joseph and the king as readings of the one chain; the necessary union of outer and inner so that neither the rotter nor the burier station claims him; the equality of inner faculties by divine justice; and a five-part purification of his own possessions before he parts from the author and is drawn toward his rebirth. The episode lands where the disciple's body itself stops being able to bear the separation — his complexion pale, his heart undone by the love of a lover for the beloved.*

## Inner aspect within the inner

The young man asked whether there is an inner aspect to the inner dimension. The author answered: *by my life, indeed there is. It holds the highest of spiritual stations.* And so the young man grasped that there are three levels of knowledge — not two. There is an outer dimension. There is an inner dimension. And there is an *innermost* dimension of that inner aspect.

The author surfaced the proof from the Book of Allah, in the chapter on the heights: *O children of Adam, We have sent down to you clothing that conceals your private parts, and as adornment. But the clothing of righteousness — that is best* (Quran 7:26). The clothing itself is the outer aspect. Its *purpose* — to cover the private parts — is the inner. And there is an innermost beyond both: the garment of piety, toward which the symbol of adornment points. One verse, three levels, in the same order.

Then the intellectual proof. Everything is understood through three handles: its name, its attributes, and its meaning. The meaning is the real essence, the thing itself. The name belongs to it. The attribute points to it. *The moon rose*, the author said: the disk is the meaning; *moon* is the name; *rose* is the attribute. None alone has meaning; combined they reveal it.

So Allah's religion has its outer aspect, which is its name; its inner aspect, which is its attributes; and its innermost level, which is its true meaning. The symbol he gave was the egg. The shell is the outer aspect. The white is the inner. The yolk is the innermost — its true substance, the only part that holds life. The shell is real, but it is not the egg. The white is real, but it is not the egg. The egg is the yolk.

The classical commentators on the Quran preserve a saying attributed to the Prophet (peace be upon him) that runs along the same grain: *every verse has an outer and an inner, and every inner has its own inner, up to seven inner dimensions.* The threefold structure the author teaches the disciple here is not a private doctrine. It is the architecture by which the prophetic tradition itself instructed its first listeners to read every line of revelation. The author is simply naming what was already given.

## The mapping onto the three creatures

Then the crucial mapping — the one the rest of this episode rests on.

*Creatures exist in three levels: the level of Angels; the level of human beings; and the level of the beasts.* Knowledge of the outer aspect of this physical world is at the level of the beasts. Whoever knows the outer alone, without the inner, operates at the level of such animals. Knowledge of the inner aspect is the knowledge of the true descendants of Adam. The one who knows it has true faith and operates at the level of a human being. But knowledge of the inner dimension of that inner aspect is the knowledge of the angels: whoever knows that becomes purely spiritual in his knowledge, *resembling the angels*, yet remaining physical with a body resembling humans. *Such a person is a prophet, whom Allah places as His vicegerent on earth.* He becomes the veil of the angels, the exemplar and interpreter of divine revelation, holding the keys to the gardens of Paradise and authority over Hellfire.

Hold the verdict the mapping carries. It is not a flattering ladder. It is a verdict on the listener's *current* rank. Whoever lives entirely in the outer — knowing the names of things, the surfaces of things, the appearances of things — has been classified by this teaching as operating at the level of the beasts. Not because he is wicked. Because he has not used the instrument by which the human is distinguished from the animal. The mapping is unsparing. The author does not soften it.

The Commander of the Faithful, in a saying preserved in the collected sermons known to every student of his discourse, draws the same line: *people are of three kinds — a divine sage, a seeker on the path of salvation, and the swarming riffraff who follow every screaming voice, who bend with every wind, who have neither sought the light of knowledge nor taken refuge in a firm support* (a paraphrase of his discourse to his companion Kumayl, preserved in the collected sermons). The author repeats it almost word for word: *in essence, there are only two types of human beings — the divine scholar, who understands the purpose of all spiritual sciences and whose spirit is connected to the spirit of certainty — sanctified through his actions as well as his knowledge — and all others, who are still seeking along the path of salvation.* And then the third class, the *non*-seekers, named exactly: *the rest of mankind are riffraff and rabble who don't really know anything: followers of every screaming voice, deluded by error, yet believing they do good.* Three classes, named without hedging.

## The angelic level cannot be climbed

The young man asked the obvious question. Could that lofty level be reached by climbing through the levels below it — beast to human, human to angel?

The author's answer is the chapter's first major correction. *The angelic level is specially honored by Allah and can only be attained through the most excellent and meritorious action, with the most sincere certainty and a pure attentive heart. Surely their Allah is also your Allah, and there is no undeserved bond of nearness between Allah and his creation. Every creature comes close to Allah only by his spiritual mindfulness and righteous action.*

There is no kinship between the angels and Allah that the human does not have access to. The angels are *near* by virtue of what they do, not by birthright. And the human can come equally near, also by virtue of what he does. But the doing has to be of a specific kind. Sincere certainty. Pure attentive heart. Most excellent and meritorious action. The author will not let the disciple imagine that proximity is a matter of accumulated study.

The principle is anchored in the Quran itself. *Indeed the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most mindful of you* (the chapter on the inner chambers, verse 13). Lineage is not currency in this economy. Action is. The Prophet, in a saying preserved across the canonical hadith collections, sharpened it further: *whoever is slowed by his deeds will not be sped up by his lineage*. The angelic level is real. It is not, however, inherited.

And the author gives the agricultural image that locks the doctrine in. The scholar can teach but cannot *create* the harvest. Like a farmer who fertilizes, plants, and waters but cannot make the plant grow, the scholar imparts the seeds but the growth is Allah's. *Hold tightly to the rope of Allah, through which you have been called and guided.* The disciple asked the precise follow-up: *is Allah's rope the Imam?* The author's answer: *he is an outward aspect of that rope — your strongest support, reached through the intermediaries of his proofs and his gateway.*

The young man, hearing this, gave the chapter its hinge sentence: *so there is no power and no strength except through Allah. Surely whoever is without a true spiritual guide would drown in the floods of ignorance, just as anyone without a ship would drown in the oceans of this physical world.* He has uttered the daily formula. The author is about to unfold it.

## The strategy and the strength — the inner names

*But what are that power and that strength which exist only through Allah?* The young man first offered the common literal interpretations. He offered that the word for *strength* refers, in one verse on suckling, to *a year* — *mothers shall suckle their children for two complete years* (Quran 2:233). And he offered that *strength* refers, in the verse on the treasures of the rebel against Moses, to *mules* — those of strength (Quran 28:76) — where, by the literal community's reading, the relevant word meant forty men and *strength* meant forty mules.

The author pointed out the absurdity directly. *Then there is no power and no strength except through Allah would mean there is no year and no mule except through Allah.* The reduction is its own refutation. The disciple admitted the gloss was weak and asked for the true inner meaning.

And the author laid down the principle that licenses every inner reading in the book: *people do not utter a single word of truth without its having a source in the inner dimension. Nor is there any falsehood that did not once have a real meaning — only those words were turned away from their original meaning and so became absurd.* Falsehood is not the absence of meaning. It is meaning *redirected* — a real word with its referent severed.

Then the unfolding. The first term refers to Allah's speaker-prophet, or his Imam — the master of the twelve chieftains — just as a year is completed only by twelve months. The Imam is called by this name because he *transforms* and *conveys* the Creator's speech, converting its subtle essential meanings into human language. The words of wisdom become the body and form; the Creator's speech is their true spirit. The Imam is the place at which pure spiritual speech is rendered into communicable form.

This is the communications model of the hierarchy. The Creator's speech is pure spirit. The Imam, as the transformer, converts it into the body and form of the words of wisdom. The gateway then condenses and distributes it to the summoners in detailed form, suited to their level. The image the source gives is the chain of command: a general gives orders to captains, who pass them to soldiers. Each rank steps the message down through a denser register of address.

And *strength* refers to the Imam's gateway, *to whom Allah has given strength to bear His mighty words*. The gateway takes divine speech from the Imam *in its condensed, all-inclusive form* and disseminates it to the summoners in more detailed and particular form. The proof of the Imam is therefore called *strength*, but has no strength except through Allah. *There is no power and no strength except through Allah* therefore means, in its inner dimension, *there is no Imam and no proof of the Imam except through Allah.*

There is a finer distinction layered on top of this. The first term has to do with scheming or strategy — a function of the spirit and mind. The second has to do with physical capability. The mind devises a strategy; the body executes it with strength. So in two ranked contexts the pairing maps cleanly. In the first context, the speaker-prophet is the strategy-bearer, and his appointed successor is the bearer of execution. In the second, the Imam is the strategy-bearer and his proof is the bearer of execution. *The formula means that neither the speaker-prophet nor his successor — and neither the Imam nor his proof — has the capacity to provide or disseminate revelation except that Allah enables it.* The most familiar phrase in Islamic speech is, on this reading, a confession of dependence on the chain.

## The dream of Joseph

This is why Joseph said to his father, describing his dream: *indeed I have seen eleven stars and the sun and the moon prostrating to me* (Quran 12:4). By the *sun* he meant the Imam of his time. By the *moon*, his proof and his gateway. And by the *stars*, the eleven chieftains.

Likewise his brothers said: *Joseph and his brother are more beloved to our father than we, while we are a clan* (Quran 12:8). The word for *clan* carries the technical sense of a band of twelve. The brothers referred to themselves as *the twelve chieftains*.

The doctrinal point follows. *The twelve brothers of Joseph were not his biological brothers.* They were the twelve members of the cabinet of the prophet Isaac — the speaker-prophet of that time. The sun in the dream was Isaac himself. The moon was Joseph's own gateway. And the eleven stars were the remaining cabinet members. The dream revealed that Joseph's rank would be elevated above all twelve members of Isaac's cabinet, *including Isaac himself*. The brothers' jealousy was the jealousy of cabinet members over rank, not a family quarrel. Allah's affair was fulfilled, and it ended in their remorse and repentance.

## The king's vision

The king's dream is read by the same key. *Seven fat cows eaten by seven lean, and seven green ears of wheat and dry ones* (Quran 12:43). The vision is like the outer aspect of religious action — a mirage that yields nothing when reached. Inwardly, the seven fat cows are Allah's seven heavenly intermediaries between Him and the speaker-prophet. The seven lean cows are the seven speaker-prophets themselves, hungry for what comes from Allah through those intermediaries. The seven green ears are the seven heavenly intermediaries between Allah and the appointed successor — fresh, full of healthy ingredient. The seven dry ears are the successors themselves, who would dry up without their sustenance. And with respect to the foundation of Islam itself, these symbols are like a seed grown into seven ears of grain — *from whose seed have come seven Imams.* Every recurring seven in the Quran is a re-signaling of the one architecture episode two named.

The author's method here repays attention. He has not introduced new doctrine. He has shown the disciple how to *read* the doctrine that is already on the page of every Quran in the world. The chain of speaker-prophet, successor, Imam, proof, gateway, chieftains, summoners — the chain laid out cosmologically in episode two — is the same chain encrypted, in episode three, into the formula every Muslim recites at the smallest setback, into the dream of a prophet's son, into the cows and ears of a king's vision. The Quran is not silent about the chain. The Quran signals the chain everywhere, in every register — formulaic, narrative, visionary. What the disciple has now been given is the key by which the signals become readable. He cannot any longer hear *there is no power and no strength except through Allah* and think of a year and a mule. He cannot any longer read the eleven stars and think of eleven brothers. The verses have been *re-meaned* in his ear.

## The union of inner and outer

The young man asked about the verse *do not let the life of this physical world deceive you*. The author noted what Allah did *not* say. He did not say *do not let this physical world deceive you*. He said *do not let the* life *of this physical world deceive you*. Life has four meanings: physical life in this inferior world and its passing away; spiritual life in the superior world and its lasting eternally. On the Day of Judgment man will regret: *I wish I had prepared for my eternal life* (Quran 89:24).

There is life through knowledge of the world's outer aspect — *the lowest form* — and life through knowledge of its inner aspect, *which is the basis for true eternal life*. Physical knowledge is forgotten at death; the soul takes with it only the spiritual understanding of physical realities. Whatever is known only in the outer is dropped when the outer drops. Whatever has been understood at the inner travels with the soul into what comes next.

Then the author ranked the cases — and the ranking is the chapter's strongest practical verdict.

The one who knows the inner aspect but *neglects* the outer obligations of prayer and fasting is in a terrible state. The inner cannot subsist when the protective outer is abandoned. *Like fruit whose covering is peeled before ripening, it rots; like a body whose limbs are cut, the spirit cannot remain.* Such a person is a *rotter* — the word the Arabs use when something rots and turns ugly. He is *an enemy of Allah's friends who can never attain their company, for he has severed what Allah commanded to be joined*: *and they sever what Allah has commanded to be joined* (Quran 13:25). The verdict is exact: the one who has the inner but not the outer is not a higher rank than the law-keeping believer. He is a *rotter*.

And the one who knows and upholds the outer aspect but remains ignorant of the inner is in *the worst station of all*. Like a body with no spirit breathed into it, numbered among the dead. The Arabic term used here — the one most familiar to English readers in its theological sense as *disbeliever* — comes from a root meaning *to cover* or *to conceal*. The farmer who hides the seed under the ground is the same root. The covering is the literal action; the disbelief is the metaphorical extension. The one who keeps the outer without the inner is not opposing the truth so much as *burying* it — preserving the form while the substance is interred beneath.

Only through the union of both — the inner spiritual dimension and the outer obligations of the sacred law — can one succeed in the afterlife and be a person of faith, a true believer. For such a person, this physical world becomes licit; *and they are most deserving of it*. The world is not earned by abandoning it. It is earned by knowing what it is.

The vocabulary the author uses is worth pausing on. The first term names a person whose substance has spoiled — the same word the language already uses for spoiled meat, for a date past its shelf life. The inner without the outer does not preserve the inner; it ferments it. The author is giving the disciple a moral grammar by which both failure-modes have proper names that say what they are. The Commander of the Faithful captured the same diagnosis in a single sentence preserved in his collected discourses: *knowledge without action is a tree without fruit, and action without knowledge is a journey without a road.* The author has expanded that one sentence into the architecture of the chapter.

## Why the true scholars look pale and weak

The young man asked why the true scholars look pale and weak, rejecting this world though they are not sick. The author wept before answering. Then: *those people reflected on their souls with the true light of life; certain of the divine promise and threat, they hastened to act while they had time.* Their bodies grew weak from fasting. Their complexions paled from vigilant nights of prayer. They were distracted from luxury by reflection — and engrossed in the sweetness of the remembrance of Allah. *They rejected this physical world only because they were distracted from it by their concern for the next — not because they considered it forbidden.*

The pallor is a side-effect of where their attention has gone. They are not punishing the body. They are not feeding the body, because they are too occupied to remember to. This is the difference between renunciation as discipline and renunciation as distraction. The first is a project the renunciant is conscious of. The second is what happens when the next world has, in fact, become more present than this one.

Hold the small but exact distinction the author is making. He is not saying the true scholars *despise* this world. He is saying their attention is so consumed by what is to come that the goods of this world have stopped registering as goods worth pursuing. The body's request for food and rest and warmth is not refused on principle. It is simply forgotten, because something more pressing is in front of the soul. Sweetness — the sweetness of the remembrance — is the word the author chooses for what has captured them. The pallor is the body's record of where the sweetness was found. The Commander of the Faithful had described the same condition in his discourse on the godly: *their hearts are grieved, their evils are guarded against, their bodies are lean, their needs are slight, their souls are chaste.* The disciple is being told that the pallor is not a symptom of weakness but a signature of orientation. And this is why, when the disciple's own complexion drains later in the episode, the author will know what to read in it: not illness, but a soul that has located something it cannot bear to be apart from.

## Divine justice in the inner instruments

Asked about the origin of creatures and the difference in their opinions, the author laid down a small theology of equality. Bodies vary in language and color as a sign of Allah's power, so people may recognize one another. *But their internal and external instruments and faculties are created identically.* No scholar has three eyes. No ignorant person has one eye. There is only equality in the creation of the physical instruments — and from that equality of design, an identical equality of *inner* instruments must follow. The inner faculties of will, intellect, and reasoning are identical too, by the pattern of divine justice.

*For Allah would not treat His creatures justly in their outer aspect and unjustly in their inner aspects. He has not placed two hearts in the scholar's chest, nor forbidden any means of understanding for the ignorant. The instrument of knowledge is one.* It is by this instrument that Allah established His argument against them — *He provided what they failed to employ* — as in the verse on those who do not use the faculties given them: *they have hearts but do not understand with them; eyes but do not see; ears but do not hear* (Quran 7:179).

The difference in passions and factions, then, came not from the instrument but from the root being corrupted. *All came from Adam, and Adam from dust — but through marriage they left behind the divine way; their progeny abandoned the divine creed and followed paths that led them astray. Once the root is corrupted, the branches are soon ruined.* All were alike in seeking ease for their bodies and respect for their souls, but they differed in reaching the unified goal — like people shooting arrows at one target, alike in intention and instrument, yet differing in actually hitting it. One group accepted the messengers' promise of Allah's eternal recompense, though it was rare in this world. The other chose this world's fleeting but readily available pleasures. *Each one receives from Him what he seeks.*

## Ranks of religion and the duty of capacity

As people of this world have ranks — kings and those who serve them — so the people of religion have ranks. Allah, in His mercy, accepts from the weak whatever they can perform in good faith. *He imposed the duty of knowledge and action on every person according to their capability.* He then joins them, in His justice, with the people of the most perfect faith. *Whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be with the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous* (Quran 4:69).

The required effort is *acquiring knowledge and acting upon it to the furthest extent of your capabilities*. Sincerity and faithfulness are Allah's two gardens. *None will be saved except the sincere and faithful.* The foundation of action is obedience — carrying out all obligations and abstaining from all that is prohibited. This is *the alms of the body*, purifying the rest. Staying in gratitude. Treating people as you would want them to treat you — a teaching the Prophet himself made the kernel of belief in a saying preserved across the canonical collections: *none of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself*.

There is a structural point hidden in this teaching that should not be missed. The earlier doctrine — angelic level reached only by the most excellent and meritorious action — could be heard as crushing. If the angelic level requires the *most* excellent, what is the use of the merely sincere effort of the ordinary believer? The author answers it here. *He imposed the duty of knowledge and action on every person according to their capability.* What is asked of the weak is not what is asked of the strong. What is accepted from the seeker is calibrated to what the seeker can bring. The angelic level is real and is honored separately. But the joining promised in the verse on the company of the prophets — with the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, the righteous — is open to anyone who fulfills, to the furthest extent of his capability, the obedience that lies within his reach. The doctrine is exact. The ladder is steep, but it does not require the same step from every climber.

When the young man asked about allowance under necessity, the author cited *whoever is forced by necessity, neither desiring the forbidden nor willingly disobeying, there is no sin upon him* (Quran 2:173). And he recalled the case from the time of the second successor to the Prophet: a woman, thirsting for three days and fearing for her children, complied with a man's condition for water. The second successor ordered her stoned. The Father of Imams declared her free of sin by this verse. The judgment is not soft. It is exact: necessity opens, intention preserves.

## Purifying possessions — the five-portion division

The young man then asked about his obligations regarding his possessions. The author instructed him in three steps. First: take only what was lawfully acquired, in which no one was wronged. Second: take out the obligatory alms from the whole. Third: purify it by taking one-fifth from its finest portion that you love most, and spend it on those most deserving. *You purify your possessions through charity just as you purify your body through righteous acts. Righteous acts are a protection; charity is a purification.* The remainder he should spend on himself and family in the accustomed manner, and give the surplus in charity to the needy.

The young man then revealed that he had — independently — divided all his possessions into five portions. The division is a small pedagogical masterpiece, and each portion has its own moral weight.

One portion for its purification — the obligatory alms. The compulsory cleansing of the whole.

One for atonement — for the harm he had done his soul. This portion was *to be kept*, not spent. The reason he gave is exact: *so I keep remembering my sins and avoid complacency*. The portion does its work by remaining in his sight, a perpetual marker of what he owes.

One in gratitude — the gratitude portion for the knowledge acquired. To be held as a trust until he reached spiritual maturity. The gratitude is not yet ripe; the portion holds its position until the gratitude has somewhere worthy to be spent.

One for the needy among his brothers, to be given *by his own hand*. The condition is precise. He did not delegate the giving. *So they recognize his generosity* — not as vanity, but so the relationship between giver and receiver is not anonymized into a transaction. Generosity that hides its source severs the human bond it is meant to confirm. The Prophet had taught the same: *the best charity is what the giver places himself in the hand of the receiver*, preserved in the prophetic tradition on giving.

And one to support the author himself in his travels.

The author, moved to tears, refused his own portion. *I have no need of it; I have left behind worldly possessions and fled from the conflicts and temptations of wealth.* And he reassigned the others as trusts. He instructed the young man to give the brothers' portion with his own hands. To keep the atonement portion as a remembrance of his sins. To hold the gratitude portion as a trust until maturity. And to keep the purification portion *entrusted to him by its rightful master* — *until you meet him; for he is the most rightful judge, for you and against you*.

Asked who that noble person was, the author spoke of the Imam. *The one whose right Allah has made obligatory upon every believer, in whose hands are the keys of the heavenly gardens, the guide of the angelic kingdom, whose palms reveal the light of the mountain of Sinai.* And he placed himself in relation to him: *I am his spiritual son, and one of his beautiful works, constantly seeking his satisfaction and fearing his wrath.*

The whole five-portion division is not bookkeeping. It is a moral architecture by which every kind of wealth has been bound to a different organ of the spiritual life. Purification, atonement, gratitude, brotherhood, support of the chain. The disciple, before the author has even instructed him, has *already* arrived at the form. The author only adjusts the destinations.

## Parting

Then the author departed and traveled until he reached his own spiritual father — the greater father, the elder teacher. He narrated the young man's story. The elder recognized the disciple's qualities, was pleased, and said: *hurry back with this young man, for I desire for him to become a gateway of loving kindness that Allah may open for the people of his time*.

When the author returned, he found the young man in a very different state. His body weak. His complexion pale. *That is from the desolation and grief that overcame me after you left*, the young man said. *Your separation made me lonely; and the fear of not being able to meet you again made me sick.*

These are descriptions of extreme love. This is what love does to a lover parted from his beloved. The physical deterioration is not illness but spiritual longing. The body, deprived of the object toward which the soul has been oriented, has begun to fail under the weight of the orientation it cannot fulfill. The pallor is the same pallor of the *true scholars* the disciple asked about earlier — though for a different reason. The distraction is not voluntary fasting and night prayer. The distraction is grief at absence. But the *form* the body takes is the same: the body emptied of its claim on the soul, because the soul has gone elsewhere.

The author reassured him: *Allah knew what was in your heart, so He made your trial easy and placed a special loving mercy in the heart of His friend for you. Start preparing for your journey, for I am taking you to the doorstep of your ultimate goal — and there is no power but through Allah.*

Hearing this, the young man sighed so heavily with joy and awe that his spirit almost left his body.

## What this episode lands

Three levels of knowledge, mapped onto three creatural ranks. The divine scholar and the seeker on the path of salvation, and beneath them the swarming riffraff who follow every screaming voice. The angelic level can be reached, but only through the most excellent action with sincere certainty — never by ladder, never by birthright. Allah's rope is approached through the Imam, who is its outward aspect, reached through the proof and the gateway. The formula *there is no power and no strength except through Allah* names that same chain in disguise: the speaker-prophet and the Imam are the strategy-bearers, the successor and the proof are the strength-bearers, and the formula confesses that neither has the capacity to provide or disseminate revelation except that Allah enables it. The dreams of Joseph and the king are read as re-signalings of the seven-and-twelve architecture of the chain. The one who keeps the inner without the outer rots; the one who keeps the outer without the inner buries; only the believer who joins both walks the path. Inner faculties are equal by divine justice — no scholar has three eyes — and the differences among people come not from instrument but from the corruption of the root. Necessity is met with mercy, by the Father of Imams' ruling on the thirsting woman. And the disciple's own five-part purification — the obligatory alms, the atonement kept as a memento of his sins, the gratitude held in trust until maturity, the brothers' portion given by his own hand, and the portion offered to the author — is reassigned by the author as a moral architecture pointing forward to the Imam.

The episode lands on the disciple's collapse into the longing of a lover for the beloved — the body pale, the heart sick, the spirit nearly leaving as the author promises the journey to the doorstep of his ultimate goal. The grief is exactly the emptying-out that the next episode's rebirth ritual will require. The disciple cannot be named while he is still the man he was. He has to lose his shape before he can be given his name. The pallor the author finds him in is the first sign that the losing has begun.

The next episode opens on the journey to the elder — the seven days of waiting, the naming ritual, the inner circumambulation, the meeting with the Imam, and the disciple's return home with the six counsels and the conversion of his own father. *Start preparing for your journey, for I am taking you to the doorstep of your ultimate goal — and there is no power but through Allah.* Hold the closing image. The disciple's spirit has almost left his body. The next episode begins where it has gone.
