# Three Levels of Knowledge — Angels, Humans, and Beasts

*Episode eight of* The Master and the Disciple. *Part Two ended at the lofty edge of cosmology, where Air pointed to the One who has no like and al-Awan named the doorway of will. Part Three turns from cosmology to anthropology. The architecture has been laid down; now the disciple asks who can climb back through it. The scholar's answer maps three dimensions of knowledge onto three levels of creatures — beasts, humans, angels — and uses the verse of the garment, the rising of the moon, and the egg as nested proofs of the same single structure. Then the disciple asks the question that follows necessarily: can a person climb from the lowest rung to the highest? And the scholar answers with the metaphor of the farmer, the rope of Allah, and the gateway of the proofs.*

## Where this episode opens

The disciple has been carried to the lofty edge of cosmology and brought back. The architecture of creation has been completed. Now he reaches for one more rung — not in the structure of the cosmos but in the structure of knowledge itself. He asks whether there is an inner aspect to the inner dimension. The scholar answers without hesitation. "By my life, indeed there is. It holds the highest of spiritual stations." From that one answer the disciple grasps the entire shape of knowledge. There is an outer dimension; there is an inner dimension; and there is an innermost dimension of that inner aspect. Three layers, not two. And the rest of this episode is the scholar showing him, from three different angles, that this triple structure is everywhere in Allah's book and in the very grammar of how anything is understood.

## Three dimensions: zahir, batin, and the inner of the inner

The scholar opens the Book of Allah to the verse of the garment, in chapter seven, verse twenty-six. He reads it in three layers. The clothing itself is the outer aspect — the zahir, the visible covering. The purpose of the clothing, to cover what is private, is the inner aspect — the batin, the function for which the visible exists. And the innermost layer, the inner of the inner, is the garment of piety — the deepest reason the body is to be covered at all, toward which the symbol of "adornment" only points. One verse, one image of clothing, three nested meanings. The structure of the verse is the structure of all knowledge.

Then he gives the intellectual proof. Everything in the world, he says, is understood through three things: its name, its attributes, and its meaning. The meaning is the real essence. The name belongs to that essence. The attribute points to it. Take the simplest sentence: the moon rose. The actual disk of the moon, the body in the sky, is the meaning. The word "moon" is the name. The action "rose" is the attribute. None of the three alone tells you anything. The disk without the name is something seen but not named. The name without the disk is a sound without reference. The attribute without either is a verb hanging in air. Only the three combined reveal what happened. And so it is with Allah's religion. The outer aspect of the religion is its name — what is recited, what is signed, what is called by its title. The inner aspect is its attributes — what those names point to in the inward life of the believer. And the innermost level is the true meaning — the essence the names and attributes both serve.

He gives one more image, smaller than a sentence and yet equally exact. Picture an egg. The shell is the outer aspect — what you would see if it sat on the table. The white is the inner aspect — what the shell protects, the layer between cover and core. And the yolk is the innermost — the true substance, the part from which life would come. Three together make an egg; remove any one and it ceases to be an egg. The disciple now has the structure given to him in three different registers — a verse from the Quran, the grammar of a simple sentence, and the architecture of the simplest organic object. Outer, inner, innermost. Name, attribute, meaning. Shell, white, yolk. The same three everywhere.

## Three levels of creatures: beasts, humans, angels

Then comes the crucial mapping, and the structure of knowledge becomes the structure of who knows. Creatures, the scholar says, exist at three levels. There is the level of the beasts. There is the level of human beings. And there is the level of the angels. And to each of these three levels a kind of knowledge corresponds.

Knowledge of the outer aspect of the physical world, taken alone with no inner, is at the level of the beasts. The scholar is precise here. He is not insulting whoever knows the outward; he is saying that whoever knows only the outward, who can recite the names and observe the rituals but who never asks what they point to, operates at the level of an animal that registers the world without understanding it. Knowledge of the inner aspect of the physical world is the knowledge of the true descendants of Adam — what the disciple is hearing right now is the human level of knowing. The one who carries that knowledge, the scholar says, has true faith and operates at the level of a human being. And then the third. Knowledge of the inner dimension of that inner aspect — the inner of the inner — is the knowledge of the angels. Whoever attains it becomes purely spiritual in his knowledge while remaining physical in his body. Such a person, the scholar says, is a prophet, whom Allah places as His vicegerent on earth. The veil of the angels. The exemplar and interpreter of revelation. The one who holds the keys of the gardens of paradise and the authority over hellfire.

The disciple has been given a ladder, and the angelic rung at the top has been named for what it is — the rung of the prophets, the chosen ranks the architecture of Part Two named one by one. But the scholar refuses to let the ladder become a sorting of mankind into thirds. There are only two kinds of human beings, he says. There is the sanctified scholar — the alim rabbani — whose spirit is connected to the spirit of certainty, sanctified through his actions as much as through his knowledge. And there is everyone else who is still seeking along the path of salvation. The rest of mankind, those who never seek at all, the scholar describes in a bracing phrase taken from the source: riffraff and rabble. Followers of every screaming voice. Deluded by error and yet certain they do good. The hierarchy of knowledge is not a caste system; it is the geometry that any seeker walks. The two real categories are the arrived and the seeking. The unsought is not even on the map.

## Climbing the ladder — and what cannot simply be given

The disciple asks the obvious question. Can that lofty angelic level be reached by climbing through the levels below it? The scholar's answer is at once tender and exact. The angelic level is specially honored by Allah. It can only be attained through the most excellent and meritorious action, with the most sincere certainty, and a pure attentive heart. There is no shortcut. There is no birth right. And there is no undeserved bond of nearness between Allah and any creature. The Allah of the prophets, the scholar says, is the disciple's own Allah; every creature comes close to Allah only by spiritual mindfulness and good actions.

Then comes the metaphor that the dialogue has been waiting for. The scholar, the alim rabbani, is like a farmer. He fertilizes. He plants. He waters. He weeds. And then he waits. He cannot make the plant grow. Growth is not in his hand. The disciple can be given the seed and the field and the water and even the instruction in how to tend the field; the actual germination, the actual rising into a stalk, the actual production of grain, is not anywhere the farmer can reach. So with the inner of the inner. The scholar can teach. He cannot create the harvest. And so he gives the disciple the imperative that the metaphor demands. "Hold tightly to the rope of Allah, through which you have been called and guided." The disciple, who has been listening with the practiced sharpness of a student inside a doctrine, asks the precise question: is the rope of Allah the Imam? And the scholar's answer is one more small refinement that the architecture has prepared him to hear. The Imam, he says, is an outer aspect of that rope — the strongest support, reached through the intermediaries of his proofs, the Hujja, and his gateway, the Bab.

The disciple completes the thought himself. "So there is no power and no strength except through Allah." Without a true spiritual guide, he says, one would drown in the floods of ignorance just as one without a ship would drown in the oceans of this physical world. The line is a quotation of a familiar formula and at the same time a quiet acknowledgment that the dialogue is about to turn. The next episode will take that formula — la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah — and show that its inner meaning has been waiting on the disciple's tongue the entire time.

There is one more small move the scholar makes before the disciple speaks the formula. He gives the disciple the verse to which all of this is anchored. "Surely their Allah is also your Allah, and there is no undeserved bond of nearness between Allah and his creation. Every creature comes close to Allah only by his spiritual mindfulness — taqwa — and good actions — al-amal al-salih." The line is doing two pieces of work at once. It is closing the door on any thought that the chosen ranks are chosen by lineage alone — they are reached and they are reached by work. And it is opening the door to what Part Three will spend the rest of its time elaborating: the precise content of that mindfulness and those actions, how possessions are arranged inside them, how the outer and the inner of religious life relate, how Allah's justice in receiving the work of unequal souls operates. The disciple has been given the ladder; he has been told the rungs cannot be transferred but must be climbed; he has been pointed to the rope held tightly through the Imam, the proofs, and the gateway; and he has been told that the climbing is by the small daily currencies of mindfulness and right action. The architecture of the cosmos has become the architecture of a life.

## Closing

Part Three has opened, and it has opened at the place where cosmology becomes anthropology. The three levels of knowledge — outer, inner, inner of the inner — map onto the three levels of creatures — beasts, humans, angels. The verse of the garment carries the structure. The rising of the moon carries it again. The egg carries it once more. And the disciple has been given both the ladder and the warning that walking it is not a transfer of cargo from one head to another but a tending of soil that may or may not produce its harvest. The rope of Allah is held tightly through the Imam, and reached through the proofs and the gateway. And the disciple has spoken the formula whose inner meaning the next episode will unfold — that neither power nor strength belongs to anyone except through Allah.
